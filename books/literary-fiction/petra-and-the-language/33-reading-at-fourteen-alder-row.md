## 33. Reading at Fourteen Alder Row

### Xanthe

The sky over Alder Row was the color of wet ash when I arrived, and I thought, absurdly, that it had dressed for the occasion.

I had not slept. The ledger sat in my bag against my hip like a second heartbeat, and every few steps I pressed my hand against it to be sure — as if forty years in a tin might have taught it to wander. The fence around the lot still stood, but the city notice had been torn down sometime in the night, all except one corner, which flapped in the wind like a small stubborn flag. Someone had chalked something on the plywood hoarding. I stopped to read it.

*They were here.*

Not a slogan. Not a demand. Just that. Three words in a child's uneven hand, or an old person's, it was impossible to tell. I put my fingers on the chalk and it came off on my skin, white dust on my fingertips, and I did not wipe it away.

The lot itself was smaller than memory insisted. That is the first lie a burned house tells you — that it was vast, that its rooms were countries, that the distance from the kitchen door to the stairs was a journey you could get lost in. Standing at the fence gap now, I could see the whole of it in one glance: cracked foundation slab, weeds gone silver with frost, the blackened stub of what had been the chimney standing at the back like a man who stayed behind after everyone else left. My father's house fit inside a breath. It had only ever been enormous because of who had lived in it.

Tomas was already there. Of course he was. He stood near the chimney stub with his coat collar up and his hands clasped behind his back, and when he saw me he didn't wave or call out. He simply turned, the way a lighthouse turns, and let me come to him.

"You slept," I said.

"Badly."

"Good." I meant it unkindly and then didn't. "Ilse?"

"Here since five. She brought the thermoses." He nodded toward the fence where Ilse Brandt stood in a coat too formal for the hour, arranging paper cups on an overturned crate with the precision of a woman building a case out of crockery. "She says if anyone films this, let them. She wants witnesses. She wants *records*."

"Ilse always wants records."

"That's why she's still standing," Tomas said quietly, "and so many others aren't."

Cars were beginning to arrive. Slowly, the way people arrive at hospitals and funerals — headlights sweeping the row, engines cutting off one by one, doors opening with that particular hesitation, as though the morning might still be refused. A woman got out of the first car holding a photograph in a frame pressed to her chest like a shield. An old man came on a cane, each step negotiated separately with the ground. A young couple I didn't recognize carried folding chairs, of all things, as if they'd come for a concert, and maybe they had; maybe that's exactly what this was.

"They came," I said. I hadn't realized until I said it aloud how much I had doubted.

"You wrote to them," Tomas said. "People come when someone finally writes back."

### Marta

Marta Ferenc had not been inside a church in thirty years, but she recognized a congregation when she stood in one.

She had brought her father's railway cap. It sat on her gray head slightly too large, the way it always had, and under it she felt the way she had felt at nine years old, carrying a sealed tin through the harbor fog because a dying man had gripped her wrist and said: *A Varga will come. You keep it till then. Promise me.* She had promised. Promises made to the dead are the only ones with no expiration date.

The Delacroix woman — Varga, she corrected herself, the girl was a Varga, whatever name she'd hidden behind — stood at the front of the lot with a heavy book open in her hands. Marta had carried that book half across the city yesterday in a taxi whose driver kept looking at her in the mirror, and she had felt its weight the whole way, the weight of other people's suffering, which is heavier than lead because lead doesn't have names in it.

Around her the crowd arranged itself without instruction. Nobody organized this. There had been no announcement beyond Xanthe Varga's letters, sent out like seeds on the wind to every address the ledger contained, and yet here were perhaps sixty people, seventy, standing in the frost-breath dawn among the weeds of a demolition site, waiting. Marta understood why. For forty years these families had known — the way you know a bone is broken even through the cast — that something had been done to their people, and for forty years the world had declined to say the word for it. This morning the word would be spoken. All the words would be spoken. Every single name.

The girl raised her voice. It carried better than Marta expected from someone so slight.

"My name is Xanthe Varga. When I was a child I lived in this house. Last night was the last night this ground belonged to silence." She looked down at the book. "My father kept accounts. Not of money. Of people. What was taken from them, by whom, and when. He died for keeping it. Most of you never learned how your people died, or why, or that anyone else shared it. This morning you will hear it. Not from a court. Not from a newspaper. From each other."

She opened the ledger.

"The first entry is dated the eleventh of March, forty-one years ago." Her voice caught, steadied. "*Emil Sando. Cartwright's Yard. Taken from his workshop at night. Extorted twice before, four hundred and sixty, then seven hundred. Never returned.*"

A sound went up from the crowd — not a gasp, something older than a gasp, a sound like a held breath finally released after decades. Somewhere to Marta's left a woman began to weep without any attempt at quiet, and no one asked her to stop, because this was the place for it, this was the hour, this was the ground that had swallowed grief for forty years and was being asked, at last, to give some of it back.

"*Emil Sando,*" the girl read again, slower. "And then she waited. And into that waiting, from the crowd, a voice — an old man's voice, cracked and certain — said:

"He was my brother."

And the girl said, "Tell us about him."

### Tomas

This was the thing Tomas had not anticipated, standing in the cold with his interpreter's habits humming uselessly in his ears: that the reading would become a liturgy none of them had designed.

Xanthe read a name. The name found its family in the crowd. The family answered — a word, a story, a date, sometimes only a sob — and Xanthe would lower the ledger and listen, truly listen, the way her father must once have listened across a shop counter, and then she would say, *we have him now, we have her now*, and write nothing down, because the point was not the record anymore. The record existed. The point was the mouths.

An hour passed. The sun cleared the rooftops of Alder Row and laid long gold bars across the foundation slab, and the frost began to smoke off the weeds, and the names kept coming. Tomas knew some of them. God help him, he knew some of them — he had typed them himself, once, into sanitized summaries, had rendered *taken from his bed* as *departed residence under escort*, had translated terror into paperwork until the paperwork told no story at all. Now the stories were being told properly, in the original language of grief, and there was no clean version, and that was the entire point.

When Xanthe reached the entry for the Halberstam sisters, two women from the tannery district, an elderly man stepped forward whom Tomas recognized from the photographs Ilse had assembled — Josef Halberstam, the brother, eighty-one years old, who had spent three decades telling anyone who'd listen that his sisters hadn't run away, that they'd been taken, and who had been politely pitied for it in every room in the city.

Josef stood before the crowd and did not raise his voice.

"They took them on a Tuesday," he said. "I know because the washing was out. Forty years I've said it and forty years men in good suits have smiled at me the way you smile at a man who talks to his dead wife. Well." He turned, slowly, taking in the faces, the cameras Ilse had invited, the journalist from the evening paper standing at the fence with her recorder held out like a candle. "Look around you. Am I alone in the room now?"

"No," said the crowd, sixty voices strong, and Josef Halberstam sat down on one of the folding chairs the young couple had brought, and wept like a man set free from prison, which is what he was.

Tomas found Xanthe's eyes across the lot. She was holding the ledger against her chest. Her face was wrecked and radiant at once, and he understood that she was watching her father's work finish itself — that the ledger had never really been accounts at all. It had been a promise, kept in secret for forty years, waiting for exactly this morning, exactly these voices, exactly this ground.

There was a rustle at the fence line. Tomas turned.

A black car idled at the mouth of Alder Row. It had not parked. It was simply sitting there, engine running, and behind its glass Tomas could see the shape of a broad-shouldered man in a good coat, watching.

Dorn.

Tomas's stomach dropped through the frozen earth. But beside him, Ilse Brandt lifted her thermos cup in a small, deliberate salute toward the car, and the journalist raised her camera, and the crowd — sensing nothing yet, or sensing everything — turned back to the ledger, to the next name, and Xanthe's voice rose again over the smoking weeds:

"*Anna Varga. My mother. Died in the fire, the same night my father was taken. The report lists no occupants.*"

Her voice broke on the last word, and sixty strangers caught it and carried it for her, saying her mother's name back to her, over and over, until the name filled the lot the way the house once had — until 14 Alder Row, for one more hour, was full of people after all.

And the black car's door opened.
