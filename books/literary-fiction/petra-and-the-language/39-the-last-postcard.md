## 39. The Last Postcard

The kiosk on Kestrel Street had changed hands twice since I started coming here. The old man who used to sell me postcards — the one with the hearing aid and the habit of undercharging me because he thought I was buying them for a school project — was long gone. The new owner was a woman in her thirties with a sleeve of tattoos and a radio playing something with horns behind the counter, and she didn't know me at all, which meant she looked at me the way strangers look at anyone: without history, without weight.

I stood at the rack for a long time anyway.

It was a ritual by now, three years of it, and rituals deserve a certain ceremony even when no one is watching. Especially then. I turned the carousel slowly, reading the captions on the backs the way other people read horoscopes. *Greetings from the Old Harbor.* *Spring in the Botanical Gardens.* *The Cathedral at Dusk.* The same images rotated through every season — the city photographing itself from its most flattering angles, pretending nothing ever burned.

I chose the harbor. Not the postcard version of it — the real one, where Marta's father had worked the freight lines for thirty years and where his daughter still lived above a chandlery that smelled of rope and tar. The card showed cranes against a gray sky, gulls frozen mid-wheel, water the color of an old coin. It wasn't beautiful. That was why I took it.

"Nice one," the woman said, ringing me up. "Nobody buys that one."

"It's honest," I said, and she laughed like I'd made a joke, and maybe I had.

I paid with exact change. Outside, the afternoon had gone soft and gold, the light slanting between buildings the way it does in late autumn when the sun gives up early and everything looks briefly forgiven. I walked home the long way, past the bakery, past the shuttered cinema, past the corner where Ilse had once waited for me under an awning with rain dripping off her umbrella and the worst news of my life folded into her briefcase. The city kept doing this — offering up its intersections like evidence. Every street was a witness now. I had stopped resenting it.

My flat was cold when I let myself in. I lit the small burner under the kettle and stood with my hands out over the flame, watching the blue tongues bend toward my fingers. Three years ago I would have called this room *temporary*. I had called everything temporary — the mattress, the curtains, the chipped bowl I ate from, the life itself. Temporary was a spell I cast to keep loss from sticking to things. If nothing belonged to me, nothing could be taken.

The lease on the table said otherwise now. My name sat at the bottom of it in ink: **Xanthe Delacroix Varga**. Both names, one line, signed six weeks ago in a rental office that smelled of toner and coffee while a bored clerk watched me write the word *Varga* and did not flinch, did not look up, did not care. That was the miracle of it. All those years I'd treated the name like a live wire, and the world received it as nothing more than letters.

The kettle sang. I made tea I wouldn't finish and carried it to the desk by the window.

* * *

The desk was the first thing I'd bought after signing the lease. Secondhand, oak, someone else's rings worn into the wood where their elbows had rested for decades. I liked that. I liked inheriting the ghost of a stranger's patience.

On the desk, in a shallow wooden tray, lived the tools of my old ritual: the pen I always used, a small brass ruler for straightening my handwriting, and a stack of blank postcards bought in bulk during a month when I'd been afraid the kiosk might close. Beside the tray, weighted down with a river stone Tomas had given me — he'd picked it up off the ground at Fourteen Alder Row the day we found Marta, saying only, *you should have something from there that isn't ash* — sat the tin.

No. Not the tin anymore. The tin had gone to the inquiry, catalogued, photographed, opened under fluorescent lights by people in gloves who handled my father's ledger like scripture or bomb. What remained on my desk was the ring box, returned to me after the archivists were done, empty now except for what I'd put back inside it: a curl of paper bearing the first line my father ever wrote about me, cut carefully from the ledger's first page with a scalpel borrowed from Ilse. *Born at dawn, Tuesday, the third of March. She has her mother's hands. God keep them both.*

I had read that line so many times the paper had gone soft as cloth.

But the drawer — the second drawer, the deep one, the one that stuck unless you lifted the handle as you pulled — that was where the important things lived. I set down my tea and opened it now, the way I had opened it every evening since the demolition, since the morning I stood on bare earth where the kitchen had been and spoke to my father in full sentences for the first time in twenty years.

Inside: the wedding ring, in its velvet groove, catching the lamplight. A photograph of my mother at nineteen, laughing at something outside the frame. And beneath them, a thin folder of papers I had not yet been able to throw away — the fire report with its tidy lie, the demolition notice, the copy of Tomas's testimony, forty pages of a man dismantling his own silence sentence by sentence.

And at the very back, face down, the last undelivered letter. The one I'd written to the families. No — not that one; that one had gone out, all sixty-three of them, and the answers were still arriving, some grateful, some furious, some just confused, one written in a shaking hand that simply said, *So that's what happened to him. Thank you. Thank you. Thank you.*

At the very back was something else. Something I hadn't decided about yet.

I took it out and turned it over.

* * *

It was a postcard, of course. Everything in my life for three years had been postcards — the smallest possible vessel for the largest possible feelings, a form that forced you to say your grief in public, in the open, where any postal worker, any stranger, any dead letter clerk could read it. I had chosen the form deliberately, though it took me until this year to admit that. A letter could be private. A postcard was a confession you mailed to yourself.

This one showed the botanical gardens in spring, all pink and white blossom, absurdly cheerful. I had bought it eleven months ago, the week after the inquiry opened, and I had started writing on it four separate times. Each attempt was still visible on the card, crossed out, abandoned, layered over like geological strata. Anyone holding it up to the light could excavate my hesitations.

I sat with it for a while. Then I got a fresh card instead — the harbor, the honest one — and uncapped the pen.

The ritual began the way it always began. Address first. **14 Alder Row.** The numbers came out of my hand without consulting me, the 4 with its open top the way I'd learned to write it at the kitchen table in that same house, my father's hand over mine, guiding the pen. *Numbers are honest,* he used to say. *They go where you send them.* He'd been wrong about that, or the world had been wrong about him, but the sentence survived anyway, the way sentences do, orphaned and true.

Below the address, no name. There had never needed to be a name. The house knew who the letters were for.

Then I sat for a long time with the blank half of the card, and the tea went cold beside me, and the light moved across the floorboards, and somewhere below a neighbor's television murmured its evening dramas to an audience of one.

What do you write to a house?

Not to the people in it — they were beyond mail, all of them, my father under the ground he'd been denied for twenty years, my mother beside him now, my childhood self scattered through the rooms like dust that never settled. But the house itself. The walls that heard everything and told no one. The kitchen where the ledger was written, line by line, in a hand that recorded other people's suffering because no one official would. The stairs I slid down on a tea tray when I was seven. The door with the scratch at knee height where our dog, whose name I can finally say again — Bors, his name was Bors — had begged to be let in every night of his short loud life.

The house had held my words. That was the thing I had never thanked it for. For three years I had written to a burned address knowing — half-knowing, then fully knowing — that the letters were being redirected, forwarded, read. I had told myself I was writing into the void, practicing grief in a safe direction. But some part of me had known the truth all along: that my father had arranged it before he died, that he had known his daughter, that he had trusted her to keep writing to the place where she learned to speak. The postcards were never lost. They were *kept*. Someone was minding them the way you mind a candle flame cupped in your palms against the wind.

And the house — the idea of the house, the address as a fixed point in a drifting world — had held me too. Every time I wrote *14 Alder Row*, I was writing myself back into existence.

I put pen to paper.

*Dear House,*

*I have written to you ninety-one times. I counted this morning. Ninety-one postcards, and you kept every one of them, or someone kept them for you, and either way I am grateful in a way I don't have clean words for. So I will use the crooked ones.*

*Thank you for the kitchen, where my father wrote the truth down when telling it would have killed him faster. Thank you for the window over the sink, where my mother stood when she wanted to be alone, and where I learned that wanting to be alone is a kind of prayer. Thank you for the stairs and the door and the scratch at knee height. Thank you for burning, if that's what it took, rather than letting them find what you were hiding. I don't know if houses get a choice. I choose to believe you did.*

*You're gone now. They took you down in an hour and a half on a Tuesday, and the machines were louder than I expected, and I felt lighter afterward, which I'm still learning not to feel guilty about. Tomas says grief and relief can live in the same body. He should know. He carried both for thirty years.*

*I have your name again. Our name. Varga. I sign it now on leases and envelopes and once, memorably, on a complaint form at the records office, where the clerk typed it into a government database without blinking, and I walked out into the street and laughed until a woman asked me if I was all right. I was. That was the strange part. I was all right.*

*This is the last one. Not because I'm done with you — I don't think you're ever done with the place that made you — but because you've done your work. You held my words while I couldn't say them out loud. Now I can. I say them in the daylight, to lawyers and journalists and neighbors and a child downstairs named Petra who watches me from the stairwell like I'm a program on television. Last week I taught her four letters of the old alphabet. She drew them on the pavement in chalk, big as dinner plates, and jumped from one to another shouting their sounds, and the whole street heard, and nobody came to stop us. Do you understand what that means? Do you understand how long I waited for nobody to come?*

*So this is goodbye, but the good kind. The kind where you thank the thing before you set it down. I'm keeping this card. I'm not mailing it. Some letters, it turns out, are for the living — and I am, against all odds and all reports, alive.*

*With love, from your daughter,*

*Xanthe Delacroix Varga*

*P.S. — Bors sends his regards. Wherever dogs go.*

I read it through twice. My eyes snagged on the P.S., and I almost crossed it out — sentiment, indulgence — and then I left it, because my father had signed his ledger entries with small flourishes no accountant would approve of, and because a book this long deserved one unnecessary kindness near the end.

Then I did the thing the ritual demanded and the ritual forbade.

For three years, the final act had always been the same: stamp, drop, walk away. The mailbox on the corner of Kestrel Street, its slot like a mouth that swallowed everything and answered nothing. I had stood in front of it hundreds of times. I knew the exact shade of its rust.

Instead, I laid the card flat on the desk and let it dry, and I did not reach for a stamp, and the absence of that motion felt enormous, like setting down luggage I'd forgotten I was carrying.

* * *

Evening came on. I ate standing at the counter — rice and the last of Riva's stew, which she still sent over in jars despite insisting she was too old to cook, the jar lids sealed so tight I needed a towel to open them, her love expressed exclusively in torque. I washed the bowl. I dried it. I put it away in the cupboard that was mine, in the kitchen that was mine, in the flat that was mine, each possessive landing a little more solidly than the last, the way calluses form.

Then I went back to the desk and opened the deep drawer.

The ring went in first, in its box, in its corner. Then the photograph of my mother. Then the folder of documents — I'd stopped needing them daily, but I wasn't ready to shelve them; some evidence you keep close the way soldiers keep helmets. And last, face up this time, where I could see the harbor cranes and the gray coin-colored water whenever I opened the drawer, the final postcard.

I rested my hand on it for a moment. Ninety-one cards sent into the dark, and one kept back, and somehow that arithmetic balanced. The ones I'd mailed had traveled — through the dead letter office, through my father's arrangement, through Tomas's careful keeping, through the inquiry's evidence bags — and every one of them had ended up exactly where it needed to be. This one would end up here, in a drawer, next to a ring that proved my father had loved us to the end and past it. Some words don't need distance. Some words just need a safe place to wait.

I closed the drawer. It stuck, then gave, the way it always did — lift the handle, pull gently, trust the mechanism. Things opened easier when you stopped fighting them.

The lamp clicked off. In the dark, the city went on making its small noises: a tram sighing around the corner, a bottle rolling along the gutter, footsteps on the stairs two floors down, unhurried, familiar. Mine now, all of it. Not owned, exactly — I was still learning the difference between owning and belonging — but inhabited. Signed for.

Before bed, I stood at the window with the last of the cold tea and looked down at the pavement below my building, where the chalk letters had faded to ghosts after the rain but not disappeared entirely. Tomorrow was Saturday. Petra would come knocking at nine sharp, the way she did, dragging her alphabet book and her hundred questions, and I would teach her the next four letters, and she would shout them into the morning air where anyone could hear.

I found I was already deciding which ones.

* * *

The knock came at five past nine, earlier than usual, urgent as a fire alarm.

"Xanthe! Xanthe, wake up, you have to see—" Petra's voice, breathless, pitched high with something between panic and delight. "Somebody chalked the whole wall by the school. All of it. Every letter. And underneath, in big letters—"

She was pounding on the door now, and beyond her voice I could hear the street waking up, doors opening, people talking all at once, and I was already reaching for my coat, already knowing, already afraid and glad in the same breath, what the wall must say.
