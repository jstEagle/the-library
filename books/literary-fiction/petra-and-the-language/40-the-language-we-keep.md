## 40. The Language We Keep

The inquiry had been running for eleven months when the first settlement letters went out.

I know because Ilse called me on a Tuesday evening, her voice careful in the way it always was when she was about to hand me something heavy and ask me to hold it gently. "Three families," she said. "The Ostrics, the Hallers, and the widow of Emil Brandt. Compensation for wrongful detention and death. Modest amounts. No admission of criminal conduct — the state never admits anything, you know that — but the language of the letter uses the word *unlawful*. I made sure of that. Unlawful. It's in writing now."

I sat on the floor of my flat with the phone against my ear and looked at the pot on the windowsill where a fig sapling stood, thin as a pencil, three leaves unfurled toward the glass.

"Unlawful," I repeated.

"It took forty years to get one adjective," Ilse said. "But adjectives are how everything starts. Nouns come later. Then sentences. Then verdicts, if we're lucky and we live long enough." She paused. "Dorn's resignation is official as of Friday. Quiet terms. He keeps his pension from the years before the permits office; he loses the rest. There are people who will call that a scandal and people who will call it a mercy, and both of them will be right."

"And Tomas?"

"Tomas testifies again next month. The fourth time. They keep asking him the same questions hoping he'll soften a sentence somewhere, make it easier to bury. He won't." A dry sound down the line that might have been a laugh. "He lost his pension, you know. All of it. Thirty-one years of service, gone because he told the truth in a room full of people paid to forget it. When I told him what it would cost, he said—" she stopped, and when she resumed her voice had changed, gone softer at its edges, "—he said he'd spent thirty-one years renting out his mouth. It was time to live in it himself."

After she hung up I sat for a long while in the blue hour before the streetlamps came on, and I thought about what the truth had actually bought. Not justice — not the kind with scales, not the kind they print in schoolbooks. Some families got money. Some got only a letter with the word *unlawful* in it, and a name spoken aloud in a hearing room by a stranger with a stenographer's machine clicking under his fingers. Some got nothing at all, because their dead were too far back, the paper trail too burned, the witnesses too scattered or too buried. Marta's father got a posthumous commendation that his daughter accepted on his behalf with a face like closed shutters, and then went home to the harbor district and made coffee the way he used to make it, and told no one, and slept well for the first time in decades.

And I thought: this is what it looks like. Not a door swinging open. A crack. Light through a crack is still light.

* * *

The child's name is Lena and she lives two floors below me, in the flat with the blue door that sticks in winter. Her mother, Denisa, works doubles at the hospital and pays me twenty crowns an hour to sit with her on Thursday evenings, though I would do it free and have told her so, and she has refused, and we have reached a diplomatic stalemate in which she leaves coins on my windowsill and I pretend not to see them.

Lena is seven. She has her mother's dark hair and her father's stubborn chin, and she asks questions the way children do — without any of the machinery adults build around asking, all the padding and apology. She just asks.

"Why does your alphabet go the other way?"

We were sitting cross-legged on my floor, the old primer open between us — Riva's primer, actually, the one she pressed into my hands the day after the reading at Alder Row, saying only, *It should be with someone who'll use it.* Its spine is cracked and its pages smell of cardamom and decades. On the page in front of us, the first letter of the old alphabet curved like a bird mid-turn.

"Because it comes from a place where they wrote from right to left," I said. "Like Hebrew. Like Arabic. It remembers where it came from."

"Where did it come from?"

"A long time ago. Before the borders moved. Before your street had its name."

She frowned at the letter, then at me. "Is it a secret language?"

That question landed in my chest like a stone dropped down a well. I waited for the splash.

"No," I said. "It used to be. Or — not a secret exactly. A private thing. Something we kept inside the house." I turned the primer toward her. "My father spoke it. He kept accounts in it. Numbers and names, written in these letters, so that anyone who didn't know would just see squiggles and move along."

"What happened to him?"

Children. No padding, no apology. I had rehearsed answers to this question for months — short ones, deflecting ones — and found, sitting there on the floor with the afternoon going gold outside, that I didn't want any of them. Not for her. She deserved the version I'd learned to say out loud, the one that cost something to say and therefore meant something once said.

"He was taken by bad men a long time ago," I said. "Before you were born. And the people who should have stopped them wrote it down wrong on purpose, so it looked like nothing happened. For a very long time, everyone agreed to pretend. But some people kept the truth anyway, in hidden places, waiting. And last year we found it. And now everyone knows."

"Did telling the truth fix it?"

I almost laughed. Out of the mouths of babes, as my mother never said, my mother who never said much of anything in either language near the end.

"Some of it," I said. "Not all of it. Maybe not most of it. But it fixed more than pretending did."

She considered this with the gravity of a supreme court, then pointed at the bird-shaped letter. "Teach me that one."

So I taught her. That was eleven months ago, and now she knows the whole alphabet and can read slow words and write her own name in two scripts, and last week she wrote it on the fogged-up window of the stairwell — LENA in the old letters, backwards and beautiful — and the downstairs neighbor, Mr. Fabian, who is eighty and grew up four streets from where Alder Row used to be, saw it and stood very still for a long moment and then asked her, in the old language, whether she wanted a sweet from his pocket. She understood him. Seven years old, and she understood him, and answered him, and he cried a little and pretended it was the wind.

* * *

Riva came to stay for a week in the spring. She slept on my sofa and complained about the mattress and cooked as though feeding an army, which is how she has always loved people — through pots and ladles, through the imperative mood: *Eat. More. You're thin.* One evening I found her standing at my window looking at the fig sapling with an expression I couldn't read.

"You planted it," she said.

"In a pot. Not in the ground."

"I heard." She touched a leaf with one finger, the way you touch something you're not sure will let you. "Your father had a fig tree. Did I ever tell you? In the courtyard behind the shop. He talked to it. Your mother teased him mercilessly." She snorted, half a laugh, half something older. "He said a tree you plant yourself teaches you patience. He said everything else just teaches you waiting."

"Was there a difference?" I asked. "Between patience and waiting?"

Riva turned from the window and looked at me for a long moment, and I saw her deciding something, the way she had decided things all her life — quickly, privately, bearing the weight alone. "Yes," she said finally. "Waiting is what you do when you think someone else holds the answer. Patience is what you do when you've decided to keep living while you look." She tapped the pot once, briskly, a benediction. "You waited long enough, child. I'm glad to see you planting."

She left on a Sunday morning, kissed me on both cheeks, and at the station platform turned back suddenly and said, in the old language, words I hadn't heard directed at me since I was small enough to be carried: *"You are the daughter of Istvan Varga, and you speak like him now."*

Istvan. His name, in the open air, between strangers with suitcases, and the sky did not fall, and the trains ran on time, and I stood there on the platform with my heart hammering like something newly alive and understood that this was what she had been saving up to give me all week.

* * *

Tomas visits on the first Thursday of every month. We have never discussed the schedule; it simply established itself, the way rivers establish banks. He brings pastries from the bakery on Kossuth Street — the good ones, poppy seed — and we drink tea and talk about the inquiry, or the weather, or nothing, and there is a peace between us now that I could not have imagined on the night I burned his card in my sink and watched his name curl into ash.

I forgave him. I want to write that plainly, because it wasn't plain at the time and it still isn't simple. Forgiveness, I learned, is not a feeling. It is a decision you make and then remake, daily, sometimes hourly, the way you take a medicine. Some days the anger comes back with its teeth out, toothless but loud, and I let it shout itself hoarse and then I choose again. He translated my father's suffering into clean sentences. He also carried those sentences for forty years like stones in his shoes until he could carry them no further, and then he set them down in public, in front of cameras, knowing what it would cost. Both things are true. I have stopped trying to make one cancel the other.

Last month he brought something besides pastries: a photograph, creased into quarters, soft at the folds from handling. My father, younger than I am now, standing in the doorway of his shop, one hand on the frame, squinting into the sun. Behind him, barely visible, the corner of a sign in the old script.

"Where did you—"

"The city archive. They digitized the commercial registers. I requested everything with the name Varga in it." He set it on the table between us, carefully, the way you set down something breakable that isn't yours. "There were forty-three documents. Ledgers, licenses, a petition he filed in '61 about the water rates. He fought the water rates for six years and lost." A pause. "I thought you should have them. All of them. He shouldn't just be the man in the fire report anymore. He should be the man who fought the water rates."

I laughed, and then I was crying, and Tomas sat across from me with his big clumsy hands folded and let me, because he understands better than anyone alive that grief and gratitude come from the same well, and that sometimes the bucket comes up holding both.

* * *

Here is what the year has taught me, and I set it down plainly because someone should:

The truth was not nothing.

It was not enough — God knows it was not enough. Dorn walks free with a trimmed pension and a house in the hills. Two of the men named in the ledger died before any of this began, warm in their beds, honored in their obituaries. The inquiry will grind on for another year, maybe two, and it will end in a report nobody reads and recommendations nobody enforces, and Ilse will stand in her office surrounded by boxes of paper and call it, in her dry way, "a foundation, at least. Foundations don't look like much. Neither do foundations of houses."

But Mrs. Halder, whose grandfather's name is on page twelve of the ledger, wrote to tell me that her family said his name aloud at dinner for the first time in her life, and her father — who had never been told why his own father went silent whenever certain streets were mentioned — wept into his soup, and afterward the silence in that family was different. Smaller. A silence with a door in it instead of a wall.

And the Brandt widow got her word. *Unlawful.* In writing. With a stamp.

And Mr. Fabian speaks the old language with a child on the stairs now, and Lena writes her name in two alphabets, and Riva says my father's name on train platforms, and Tomas lives in his own mouth at last, and I —

I signed a lease. Eleven months ago, but I still touch the signature sometimes, run my finger over the ink like a blind woman reading. Xanthe Delacroix Varga. Both names, one line. The landlord thought nothing of it; names are just names to people who never had to lose one. But I knew what I was doing when I wrote it. I was picking up what I put down. I was walking back into the house I burned behind me and finding, impossibly, that some things had survived the fire after all — packed away in tins and primers and old men's pockets, waiting in the dark with the patience of seeds.

* * *

Thursday evening. Lena sits cross-legged on my floor, tongue between her teeth, copying the alphabet into a notebook Denisa bought her, pink with a cartoon cat on the cover, the old letters marching across pages designed for multiplication tables. Outside, October is putting on its show — copper and rust, the light going long and low.

"Marta sent this," I tell her, and hand her the envelope that came yesterday, harbor-district postmark, Marta's spidery hand. Inside is a photograph of the tin — the famous tin, biscuit-brand, dented, forty years in a cupboard above her stove — and beneath it, in the old script, a single sentence. "Read it to me."

Lena bends over it, sounding it out, her finger moving under each letter like a small boat feeling its way along a shore. "*The... sea... keeps...*" She frowns. "*What's this one?*"

"That's *what it's given*," I say. "It means the same thing here as everywhere. The sea keeps what it's given."

"*The sea keeps what it's given,*" she reads, triumphant, and looks up at me for approval, and gets it, all of it, the whole ridiculous overflowing amount.

Later, after her mother collects her and the coins appear on my windowsill and I pretend not to see them, I stand at the window with the lamp off and watch the street. The lot on Alder Row is bare earth now, fenced, weed-ragged, waiting for whatever the city decides to build there — flats, probably, or a car park, something with no memory. But there's a bench now, at the edge of it. The families paid for it together, out of settlements and savings, a plain wooden bench with a small brass plate: FOR THOSE WHO WERE COUNTED HERE. People sit on it. Strangers sit on it, eating lunch, checking phones, having no idea. And sometimes someone who knows sits down beside them and says nothing, and that is enough, because presence is its own language and it needs no alphabet.

In the drawer by my bed, the ring's box and the unmailed postcard sleep side by side. On the windowsill, the fig has five leaves now. In the notebook on my table, under a list of groceries, I have started — hesitantly, badly, the grammar rusted — writing a few lines each night in the old script. What happened that day. What the light was like. What I'm afraid of. What I planted. A ledger of my own, you might say, except that everything in it is given freely, and someday — not soon, but someday — I mean to leave it where someone will find it.

Because that is the whole lesson, the one that took me forty years and a burned house and a tin in a harbor cupboard to learn: languages don't die when their speakers die. They die when their speakers fall silent while still breathing. And they live — shabby, stubborn, unglamorous, alive — every single time someone opens their mouth and lets the old words out into the new air.

A child is learning the alphabet two floors below me. An old man keeps sweets in his pocket for her. A woman on a train platform speaks a dead man's name to strangers. A tin in a cupboard held a truth for forty years because someone was trusted to keep it, and kept it.

The language survives because someone spoke it out loud.

Tonight, it's me.
