## 1. The Address That Burned

The laundromat on Alberta Street smelled of wet cotton and quarters, and Sable Brightwater had come to think of it as a kind of church.

It was not a beautiful church. The fluorescent tube above the folding table flickered at intervals like a nervous tic, and the linoleum was cracked in a spiderweb pattern that had been there longer than he had been alive, probably. A man two machines down was washing what appeared to be an entire set of hotel curtains, and a woman with a sleeping toddler strapped to her chest fed dollar bills into the change machine with the weary precision of someone performing surgery on herself. Nobody looked at anybody. That was the liturgy of the place: everyone together, everyone alone.

Sable sat at the folding table with his back to the dryers, which roared in a warm chorus behind him like a tide that never went out. In front of him: a stack of postcards, a felt-tip pen whose ink had gone slightly brown with age, and his coffee, going cold in a paper cup he'd wedged between a bottle of fabric softener and the table's one wobbly leg.

Postcard number 2,557.

He knew the number the way other people knew their own birthdays. He had started keeping count around card forty, when it stopped being a gesture and became a practice, and now the count lived in him somewhere below language, the way a sailor knows the sea without describing it. Two thousand five hundred and fifty-six cards before this one, each addressed to the same place:

*Juniper Brightwater*
*417 Gullwing Road*
*Manzanita, Oregon*

The house at 417 Gullwing Road did not exist. It had not existed for seven years. On a night in late October — Sable could summon the date the way you'd touch a scar, absently, to check it was still there — the house had burned down to its foundation, taking with it the kitchen where their mother had taught them all to gut a crab, the hallway with the pencil marks measuring four children against the doorframe, and, according to every official record and every family mouth that would open on the subject, Juniper himself.

Sable wrote anyway.

*Dear J,* he began, because even in writing he observed the rule. The rule was unwritten, unspoken, enforced by nothing so crude as punishment — only by the particular quality of silence that fell over a Brightwater dinner table when anyone came close to the name. Seven years ago Marisol had said it once, at the memorial, her voice cracking on the second syllable like ice on a pond, and then she had never said it again, and neither had anyone else. Ezra had made sure of that, though Ezra's methods were always atmospheric rather than explicit. You learned the shape of what could not be said by watching your father's face go still, the way water goes still before it freezes.

So: *Dear J.* It was a code the whole family understood and no one acknowledged, which was, Sable thought now, pen hovering, essentially the Brightwater family religion in miniature.

*Dear J. There's a woman at this laundromat washing hotel curtains. I don't know how she got them or where they're going but there are eleven of them, I counted. The dryers sound like the ocean tonight, which is either a coincidence or the coast missing us both.*

He paused. Outside the plate-glass window, Portland was doing its October thing — rain arriving sideways, neon from the taqueria across the street smearing red and green across the wet asphalt like a painting left out in weather. A bus sighed past, lit and empty except for one figure near the back reading something under a small yellow light. Sable watched until the bus turned the corner and the figure became just a person again, anonymous, going home to some address that actually received mail.

That was the part he never got used to. Not the grief itself — grief he had made a life around, the way you build a house around a tree, leaving the trunk standing in the living room. What he never got used to was the banality of the machinery that had declared his brother dead. A certificate. A fire report with a section marked *REMAINS RECOVERED* that said, in the flat bureaucratic dialect of such documents, *insufficient*. Insufficient remains. As if Juniper had been a recipe missing an ingredient. As if the fire had simply run short.

Marisol had wept beautifully at the memorial. Odile, fourteen years old and shell-shocked, had told three different versions of that night to three different relatives before the week was out, and Ezra had gently, firmly, retired her from storytelling altogether. And Sable — Sable had stood at the edge of the scorched foundation in his funeral suit, looking down into the black square where the kitchen used to be, and had felt absolutely nothing arrive. No confirmation. No closure, that word everyone kept handing him like a casserole. Just a strange, ringing absence, the feeling of a phone line open to a room where nobody was speaking.

Everyone else had heard a death in that silence. Sable had heard a held breath.

*The laundromat is my favorite church,* he wrote. *You'd hate it and love it equally. The fluorescents flicker like Marisol's temper. There's a boy here doing his laundry in a band t-shirt that says something obscene in a font shaped like flames, and I thought of you, because you would have designed that font. Remember the flyers for the harvest show? You lettered the whole thing by hand and Dad said it looked amateur and you said good, that was the point, and then you played the best set anyone ever played in that barn and Dad clapped last and loudest, like always, like applause was a thing he was granting rather than feeling.*

He stopped. His hand had gone tight around the pen. Behind him a dryer buzzed its finished chime, bright and idiotic, and no one moved to claim it.

That was the trouble with these cards. They were supposed to be small — that had been the original idea, postcards being physically incapable of holding anything large. You got maybe fifty words. You wrote about weather, about the heron on the dock, about the terrible coffee at the diner on Highway 101. You wrote the way you'd leave a note on a brother's pillow: light, quick, affectionate, gone. But seven years is a long time to write light notes into a void, and lately the void had begun writing back in its way — lately the cards came out heavier, the ink sinking deeper, Sable unable to stop himself from saying the things that needed a whole sky of paper.

*I miss your handwriting most,* he wrote. *Isn't that stupid? Everyone would say I should miss your voice, your laugh, whatever. But you had this way of slanting your letters like they were all leaning toward something off the page. Like every sentence you wrote was eavesdropping on a better one. I keep your grocery list from the last week. Oat milk, batteries, "the good bread." I can't throw it out. It's the most honest document our family ever produced.*

The woman with the curtains began pulling them from the washer, dripping, and the smell of detergent rose through the room, clean and chemical, almost like the coast. Almost like the compound in Manzanita after a storm, salt and soap and cedar smoke, the whole sensory signature of a childhood Sable had fled at nineteen and spent his entire adult life failing to escape in any way that mattered. He had moved to Portland. He had gotten an apartment, a job doing layout for a small press, a life measured in city blocks. And every Sunday for seven years he had driven or walked to wherever he happened to be living and written to a burned house, because the alternative was to admit what he actually believed, and what he actually believed was unsayable at any family table in America.

He believed his brother was alive.

Not hoped. Believed. The distinction mattered, and Sable had spent seven years learning exactly why. Hope was a soft thing, a wish, the stuff of candles and vigils. Belief was load-bearing. Belief was the beam he had built his whole interior architecture around, and if it ever gave way — if someone put a body in front of him, a photograph, a dental record — the entire structure would come down and bury him under it. So he did not hope. He wrote postcards. Devotion, dressed up as denial, mailed weekly to a lot of blackened earth.

Because here was the thing Sable had never told anyone, the thing that lived in him like a splinter too deep for tweezers: insufficient remains meant no remains. He had read the fire report eleven times. He knew the burn patterns, knew the origin point in the orchard-side wall, knew that the fire had burned hot and strange and fast, faster than a house that size should have gone. And he knew Juniper. Knew that his brother had been sneaking out windows since he was nine, knew he could move through dark terrain like a rumor, knew that on the night of the fire Juniper had been carrying the only copy of everything he'd ever written — songs, notebooks, a demo tape Ezra had forbidden him to record properly — in a canvas bag he took everywhere like a third arm.

A boy like that did not stay in a burning house. A boy like that was already halfway out the window when the smoke came.

But the family had closed around the death like a fist, and Sable — twenty-three then, grieving, outnumbered, out-argued by a father who could make silence feel like law — had let the fist close. Had let them hold the memorial. Had let Ezra say *we lost him* in that voice that ended conversations the way gavels end trials. And then, alone in Portland, he had begun the only rebellion available to a man who couldn't yet name his heresy: he wrote to the dead man every week, care of a house that wasn't there, and trusted the mail to do what families cannot, which is deliver things honestly.

*Two thousand five hundred and fifty-seven,* he wrote now, in the corner of the card, small, the way he always did. A private census. Proof of attendance.

He signed it — *Love, Sable* — and capped the pen, and sat for a moment with the card face-down on the table, letting the dryer-warmth soak into his shoulders. Around him the laundromat continued its indifferent grace: the curtain woman folding, the toddler waking with a small confused cry and being hushed, the rain writing its long illegible sentence down the window. Sable thought, not for the first time, that if Juniper were dead — truly, verifiably, completely dead — then these cards were insane, and he was a man who had spent seven years mailing letters to ash. And if Juniper were alive, then someone in his family knew it, and had known it, and had let Sable stand at a memorial service and fold into himself like paper while they watched.

Both possibilities were unbearable. He had built a life precisely between them, narrow as a window ledge.

He gathered his laundry — warm, smelling of the cheap cedar detergent he used because it was the closest thing Portland sold to home — and carried the postcard out into the rain. There was a blue collection box on the corner by the taqueria, dented, its paint gone chalky, and Sable fed the card into the slot the way he had done 2,556 times before: with two fingers, slowly, a small ceremony, listening for the soft slap of cardboard landing on cardboard. Somewhere in the belly of that box, his card joined the week's mail, bound for a sorting facility, bound for a coastal route, bound for an address where no mailbox stood — where, in fact, the county had long since removed even the charred stub of the old one.

The mail carrier who worked that route would look at the card tomorrow morning, shake her head with the tired tenderness people reserve for the harmless mad, and set it aside. Undeliverable. Return to sender, eventually, if return-to-sender addresses behaved the way they were supposed to.

They didn't. Not in Sable's experience. Nothing in his family returned; things just went silent and stayed silent, and everyone agreed to call the silence peace.

He walked home through the rain without hurrying, hands in his pockets, and if a passerby had looked closely they might have seen that his lips were moving, forming words he would never say aloud in any room containing his blood relations — the name, the actual name, the one that had been buried more thoroughly than any body ever was, spoken softly to the wet Portland night like a password to a door that hadn't opened in seven years.

Three days later, in a diner outside Tillamook, a radio would play a song that had never been recorded, and everything Sable believed would stop being a private madness and become a hunt.

But that was three days away. Tonight there was only the rain, the walk, the warm weight of clean laundry under his arm, and the small, stubborn, ridiculous faith of a man who had just mailed his heart to a hole in the ground — for the 2,557th time, and counting.
