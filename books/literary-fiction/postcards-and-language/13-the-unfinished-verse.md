## 13. The Unfinished Verse

The morning it happened, I was salting a pot of beans and the salt would not come out of the shaker. That's how these things begin — not with trumpets, but with a shaker turned upside down over a pot, nothing falling, and a woman standing in her own kitchen feeling the whole day tilt.

I set the shaker down. I picked it up. I shook it again over the sink, and the salt came out fine, a little white rope of it, and I stood there with my hand trembling and understood that the day had been waiting for me the way a dog waits at the door of a room where something is dying.

I had known, of course. You know. The body sends its letters for months and you read only the envelopes. The tiredness I had been calling age. the weight I'd been losing and calling the heat. The ache under my ribs I'd been calling the damp off the sea, which in Punta Clara is a diagnosis for everything and a cure for nothing. I had known the way you know a song is about to end — not because anyone tells you, but because you can feel the last verse gathering itself somewhere below the melody, patient, inevitable.

But knowing a thing and being asked to hold it in your two hands are different animals.

I finished the beans. That is the first thing you should know about me: I finished the beans. I tasted the broth off the wooden spoon — too much oregano, not enough salt, my hands had gone shy — and I corrected it, and I set the pot to simmer low, and I wiped down the counter that Chuy had re-tiled the year Luz arrived, and I did all of it at the speed of a woman who is not thinking about anything, which is the fastest speed there is.

Then I sat down at the table by the window with my coffee going cold, and I let it come.

Thirty years ago I had made a promise to a dead man. Not out loud — Teo and I were not the kind of lovers who made promises out loud; we made them in chords, in the way he'd leave his notebook open on my pillow so I'd know he'd be back, in the way I'd leave the porch lamp burning so the road home would have one lit inch of it. But there had been a promise anyway, the kind you sign with your whole life: *we will finish it together.* The song. *La Canción del Farol* — the song of the porch lamp, though Teo hated when I translated it, said translation was like photographing a fire. It had three verses and a bridge and a melody that climbed at the end like a person standing up from a table, and it was missing the last verse, and he had laughed about that, the last time. He'd said, *I'll write it on the road, Mari. The last verse always comes from the road.*

He drowned on the road. The river took him between the ferry crossing and the bend where the cypress trees lean, and the last verse never came, and for thirty years I had lived in a house built on that absence the way a barn is built on its posts.

I had tried. God knows I had tried. In the early years I tried every night, sitting where he used to sit with the guitar, and everything I wrote came out either too bitter or too sweet, grief with no craft in it, or craft with no grief. A last verse, I learned, is not just more words. It is the room the whole song has been walking toward. It has to be earned by everything that came before it, and I could not earn it because I had not been allowed to keep anything that came before it — not the name, not the town, not the truth of how he died, not even my own version of the story. Aurelio Villalobos had taken the story out of my mouth and replaced it with money and a bus ticket, and a woman without her story cannot finish a verse. A verse is a story with music in its pockets.

So I had done the next thing. I had cooked. I had fed a town for thirty years, and I had raised a family out of the strays that drifted in — Chuy with his radios and his ruined hearing, Beto who was born into silence and found the drums like a man finding water in a desert, Luz who came to us with her mother's bruises still mapping her arms. I had built the house that music built, and I had never played the song in it, because a house should not stand on a verse that isn't finished. That was my superstition. You don't sing a song with a hole in it inside your own walls. The hole lets the weather in.

And now the weather was in me.

I sat with my cold coffee and I did what women in my family have always done with unbearable news: I made a list. Not on paper. In the body. *The beans are done. The fish order comes at seven. Beto's birthday is in three weeks — no, I will not think about three weeks. Luz needs new school shoes. The awning over the patio is loose. Cyrus Villalobos has been washing my dishes for eleven days and I have not decided what he is.*

That last one. That was the list item that would not stay in its place.

The boy — I still called him the boy in my head, though he was fifty if he was a day, with his mother's long hands and his father's jaw, that jaw, the whole Villalobos jaw like a doorframe, like something built to hold a house up whether the house deserved it or not — the boy had come into my cantina on a Tuesday and ordered everything and finished nothing, and I had known him before the door closed behind him. Not his name. His *face*. Thirty years, and grief keeps better records than any registry. He had Teo's way of sitting slightly sideways at a table, as if his body were always half-turned toward an exit or a melody. He had the Villalobos hands. And when I set the fish in front of him — the fish, I will admit it, the fish I had cooked for Teo the last night, the snapper with the green sauce, I had cooked it for him as a test and a weapon both — he had looked at it the way you look at a photograph of someone you loved.

He had wept into it. He had confessed, in a voice cracked down the middle, that he could not taste a single thing he ate, that he was a restaurant critic, of all the absurd professions grief could hand a man, and that he had been writing about flavor for years with a tongue like a stone.

And I had not forgiven him. Let the record show that. Whatever the town says about me, whatever Luz tells the tourists, I did not forgive him that night and I have not forgiven him now. Forgiveness is a door and I am not obliged to open mine just because a Villalobos is standing in the rain outside it. His family put me on a bus with an envelope of money and a story in my mouth that wasn't mine, and thirty years is thirty years, and the dead do not come back because their brother's brother cries into a fish.

But I had let him wash dishes. Because his hands looked like they needed work, and because a man who cannot taste should at least learn what salt feels like between his fingers, and because — I will be honest, here, in the privacy of my own skull, where I keep the only honest room I have — because when he stood at my sink with his sleeves pushed up, he stood the way Teo used to stand at the sink of his mother's kitchen, hip cocked, humming, and for eleven days I had been letting that ghost wash my plates and I had not decided whether that was cruelty or company.

* * *

He came at four, the way he always came now, through the kitchen door instead of the front, which was its own small surrender. The lunch rush was over. Luz was at school. Beto was at the shop with Chuy, learning for the hundredth time how a radio works, which is to say how everything works if you're patient enough to take it apart. The kitchen was mine and the light was coming in low and gold through the west window, the light I have cooked in for thirty years, the light that makes even the dented pots look like they were chosen.

He tied the apron without being asked. He had learned that much. He rolled his sleeves and stood at the sink and began on the lunch pots, and I let him work for a while because there is a language in work and I wanted us both to be fluent in it before we tried words.

Then I said, "Sit down, Cyrus."

He looked up. I almost never used his name. He knew what it meant, that I used it. He dried his hands on the towel with the care of a man defusing something and sat at the table by the window, and I put the pot of coffee between us because some conversations need a third thing on the table or they tip over.

"I'm going to tell you something," I said. "And then I'm going to ask you something. And you're not going to answer right away, because the answer you'd give right away would be the wrong one. The wrong one is the polite one. I don't want the polite one. I've had thirty years of the polite one."

His hands went flat on the table. The Villalobos in him wanted to brace; I watched him decide not to.

"Play it for me," I said. "In your head. The song from the radio. The one that made you drive off the road like a drunk man."

Color went up his neck. "You know about that."

"I know everything that happens in this kitchen, and half of what happens in this town. Chuy heard the same broadcast. He told me a stranger nearly put his truck in the ditch on the coast road listening to a song by M. Reyes." I poured the coffee. "That was the first time it had been played. Did you know that? Thirty years I have kept that song in this house like a coal in ash, and one afternoon I was cleaning Chuy's back room and there was a cassette in a shoebox with a date on it, and I thought, well. The dead don't need it. And I gave it to him. And he sent it to the station in the city, because Chuy believes music that isn't being heard is just furniture."

Cyrus's eyes had gone bright and wet and fixed on me. "It was his," he said. "I know it was his. Every note. He used to hum it in the shower. He used to—" He stopped. Swallowed. "How do you have it?"

"Because I was there when he wrote it," I said. "Verse by verse. On that porch, in the town you've never seen, in the summer the whole world still had its skin on." I wrapped my hands around my cup. "And I'm going to tell you the thing I have never told anyone, not Chuy, not the priest, not the sea. And then I'm going to ask."

The kitchen was very quiet. The beans ticked on the stove. Somewhere down the street a dog was losing an argument with a gull.

"The song is not finished," I said. "You heard three verses and a bridge and then it stops — it doesn't end, it *stops*, like a man walking out of a room mid-sentence. There's a fourth verse. There was always going to be a fourth verse. Teo said the last verse comes from the road." My voice did something I didn't authorize. I let it. "And then the road took him, and for thirty years I have not been able to finish it. Not because I lack words. I have words. I have thirty years of words stacked to the ceiling. But a last verse isn't words, Cyrus. It's what the whole song was *for*. And I can't see what it was for anymore. I could see it at twenty-two. I can't see it at fifty-two. Grief doesn't take your hearing first. It takes your *hearing-toward*."

He didn't speak. Good. He was learning.

"So here is my question." I looked at him straight, the way I look at a fish to judge its freshness, the way I looked at his father once across a desk while he told me what my life was going to be. "You knew him. Not the way I knew him — I'm not asking you to compete with the dead, God, no one wins that — but you knew him the way a brother knows him. The part of him that was only for family. So you tell me. What would Teo have said? For the last verse. What would he have written? What was he walking toward, all that summer, every time he played it and stopped and smiled and said *not yet, Mari, it's not ripe*?"

Cyrus's breath went out of him slowly, like air from a tire. He looked at his hands. He turned them over, palms up, as if the answer might be written there, and I thought: he is going to say *I don't know*, and I will understand, and something in this kitchen will close like a shop at the end of a bad day.

But he didn't say it.

"He'd have written about the lamp," Cyrus said.

I went still.

"The porch lamp. That's what the song is — I only understood it later, I was fifteen, I didn't understand anything then, but I understand it now because I've spent thirty years—" He stopped, corrected himself. "Eleven days. I've spent eleven days in a house where a lamp is always burning, and I finally understand what he was writing. It's a song about waiting for someone to come home. The first verse is the person leaving. The second is the road. The third is the waiting — that's the one that climbs, right? That's the one that stands up out of the chair? Because the waiting gets tired of being patient. And then—" His voice caught. He pushed through it, and I let him, because a man should have to push through his own voice at least once in his life. "And then the last verse would be the choice. Whether to keep the lamp lit. Whether the person at the window decides — after all the waiting, after the road does what roads do — whether they decide to keep loving anyway. Not because the person came back. Because the lamp is *theirs*. The waiting is theirs. It's the only thing nobody can take from you, not even—" and here his voice did break, clean through, "—not even the people who take everything else."

The beans ticked. The dog outside had won or lost, I don't know. I sat with my cold coffee and looked at this man — this boy, this Villalobos, this stranger with his brother's sideways way of sitting — and I felt something I had not felt in thirty years, which was the song moving. Not finished. Not even close. But *moving*, the way a heavy thing moves when a second pair of hands gets under it, and I hated him a little for it, and I was grateful to him a little for it, and both of those feelings fit inside me at once the way two people fit in a kitchen.

"The lamp," I said. My voice was gravel and water. "He never told me what the fourth verse was. He said it wasn't ripe. I used to think he was stalling because he was lazy — that boy could stall like a mule." I laughed, one syllable, surprised out of me. "He wasn't stalling. He was waiting for it to be true. That's what he'd say. *It's not true yet, Mari. You can't write a verse that hasn't happened.*"

Cyrus was crying without noise, the way men cry when they learned young that noise costs too much. I did not hand him a towel. Some things you have to sit through to the end.

"Here's what I know," I said, and I stood, because I am a woman who thinks better on her feet, and I went to the shelf above the stove where I keep the things that aren't for sale — the clay whistles, the saint with the chipped face, the tin box — and I did not open the tin box, but I put my hand on it, so he could see that it existed, so that he would understand there was a locked room in this conversation and I knew where the key was. "I know the song is not finished. I know it has waited thirty years, and I know that I am not going to be the one to finish it alone, because I have tried alone and alone is a country I'm done living in. And I know that you—" I pointed at him, flour still on my finger from the morning's bread, "—you are going to help me whether you want to or not. Not because I forgive you. Don't confuse the two. We are going to finish Teo's song the way a wound closes: from the inside, slowly, and it's going to hurt the whole time."

He wiped his face with the heel of his hand. He laughed, wet and cracked. "And if I get it wrong?"

"You'll get it wrong," I said. "The first ten times. Teo got everything wrong ten times. He wrote a verse about my mother's chickens once that was so bad I made him sleep outside." And there it was again, the laugh, his and mine, meeting in the middle of the kitchen like two people who'd been sent to the same errand.

I took down two cups. I poured the coffee that had gone cold and decided not to care, and we drank it cold, together, at the table by the window, and outside the light went on getting lower and gold, and inside me the song turned over in its sleep like something that has decided, after thirty years, to wake up.

That night, after close, after Luz's homework and Beto's drumming through the floorboards and Chuy's radios murmuring their stations to each other down the hall, I took the tin box down from the shelf. I didn't open it. I held it. It was heavier than tin should be, the way a thing gets heavy when it's been waiting.

Because there was something in that box I had never shown anyone. Something of Teo's. And in the morning, when the boy came through the kitchen door and tied the apron without being asked, I set the box on the table between the coffee cups, and I said, "Before we write one word, there's something you need to read."
