## 17. The Question

The question had been living in me for weeks before I said it aloud. It nested somewhere behind my sternum, in the same hollow place where taste used to be, and it grew the way things grow in the dark — quietly, without permission, fed by everything I refused to look at directly.

I had come to Punta Clara to find a song. That was the story I told myself on the drive down, the one I'd rehearsed for Rosa and for Priya and for the mirror in my apartment that I no longer stood in front of. A song on the radio, a name credited to M. Reyes, a brother's melody rising out of static after thirty years. Any reasonable person would call that a mystery worth solving. But mysteries are comfortable. Mysteries let you circle the thing instead of touching it.

The truth was uglier and simpler: I had come to ask whether my family had destroyed an innocent woman.

I knew this the way you know weather is coming — not by seeing the storm but by the ache in old scars, by the way dogs go silent, by the particular pressure behind the eyes. I knew it every time I watched Marisol move through her cantina kitchen with that economy of motion that comes from decades of cooking for people who might leave at any moment. I knew it when Beto flinched at raised voices he couldn't even hear but could feel through the floorboards. I knew it when Luz asked me, once, casually, over dishes, "So what did she actually do?" and I opened my mouth and found nothing there — nothing, thirty years of family silence, and not one verifiable fact.

*What did she do.* The question of a child. And I was a child when they answered it, weren't I? Seventeen, hollowed out by Teo's drowning, standing in the marble foyer while Aurelio explained — not to me, never to me, but to the room, to the lawyer, to the air — that the girl had been reckless, that she had kept him out late, that the boat should never have gone out, that the Villalobos name would survive this if everyone held the line. I had signed the silence the way children sign things: by not objecting. By letting my grief be organized for me. By eating the food my mother put in front of me at the funeral and tasting nothing, which everyone mistook for sorrow and which was, perhaps, sorrow — or perhaps the first symptom of a body refusing to participate in a lie.

Now I stood in Marisol's kitchen at six in the morning, before the cantina opened, chopping onions because she had handed me the knife without comment three weeks ago and had never taken it back.

She worked beside me in the blue-gray light, her hands moving faster than mine, correcting my cuts with a flick of her wrist rather than a word. She had stopped calling me *the boy* some time ago, though she hadn't yet settled on anything else. Mostly she called me nothing. She communicated in imperatives: *More heat. Less salt — no, wait, more. Turn it. Hold this.* It was the closest thing to tenderness I'd received from a Villalobos-adjacent adult in my adult life, and I treasured it the way you treasure a fire in wet wood — knowing how improbable it was, how easily extinguished.

That morning she was making stock. Chicken backs, charred onion, a fistful of epazote from the pot by the door. The smell filled the kitchen like a congregation filling a church, and I stood inside it unable to feel a thing except the cold of the cutting board under my palms.

"Marisol." My voice came out wrong. Too formal. I tried again. "I need to ask you something."

She didn't stop working. Her knife kept its rhythm against the board — *tak, tak, tak* — steady as a metronome, and I understood suddenly that she had been waiting for this the way a coastal town waits for hurricane season. Not hoping. Just knowing.

"You can ask," she said. "Asking costs nothing here."

"Teo's death." The words felt enormous in my mouth, too large to swallow. "What actually happened. On the water. What did you see, what did Aurelio—"

"I wasn't there."

"I know. I know you weren't there. That's—" I set down the knife because my hands had begun to shake, and I didn't want to lose any fingers to this conversation, though part of me thought it might be fair payment. "That's what I'm asking about. Because you weren't there, and somehow that became the whole point. Somehow *you* became the point. And nobody — not my mother, not my aunt, not me — nobody ever once said what you were supposed to have done. There was no *what*. There was only *who*. You were the who. And we all just..."

I trailed off. Outside, the town was waking up: Chuy's radio repair shop rattling open its shutter, gulls arguing over something at the pier, Luz's footsteps on the stairs above us as she got ready for school. A whole small world assembling itself around this woman, this kitchen, this morning — a world my father had tried to erase with money and silence and had failed, because you cannot fail harder than trying to erase Marisol Reyes.

She turned off the burner under the stock. The hiss died. In the sudden quiet I could hear my own breathing, ragged and loud.

"Sit down," she said.

"I'd rather—"

"Sit down, Cyrus."

My name. She used it rarely enough that it landed like a hand on the chest. I sat at the little table by the window where Luz did her homework, where Beto ate his breakfast with his palms flat on the wood so he could feel the morning traffic through the floor. Marisol poured two cups of coffee from the pot that was always on, always slightly burnt, always exactly right, and sat across from me. She wrapped both hands around her cup. Her knuckles were enormous, ruined-looking, beautiful — a working woman's hands, a lifetime of them.

"You want to know if your father invented my guilt," she said.

It wasn't a question, so I didn't answer it. But my face must have done something, because she nodded slowly, as if I'd confirmed a diagnosis she'd made long ago.

"For thirty years I waited for someone from that house to sit where you're sitting and ask me that. Thirty years. Your mother came closest. Did you know that?"

"No." My mother. Dead eleven years now, buried under a stone I visited twice a year and felt nothing at. "No, I didn't know that."

"She wrote to me. Twice. The first letter I burned without opening — I was angry, and anger makes you stupid. The second one I kept." She looked out the window at the gray water. "In it she said, *I don't believe it, but I don't know how to be against him alone.* That was your mother. She could see the shape of the lie perfectly well. She just couldn't stand up inside it."

The coffee trembled in my hands. "Why didn't you fight? You could have — lawyers, the truth, anything. He paid you off and you *took it*, and I've hated you a little for that my whole life, if I'm honest, and I don't understand—"

"You don't hate me." She said it mildly, without offense, the way she'd say *the stock needs skimming*. "You hate the version of me your family built so they wouldn't have to look at themselves. That's different. That girl doesn't exist. She never did."

"Then help me bury her. Tell me what happened."

Marisol was quiet for a long time. Through the window the light was changing, gray going to gold, the way it does on the coast in the hour before the fishing boats come back. Somewhere below us, Beto was sweeping the dining room, and I could feel through my own feet the rhythm of the broom, patient and even.

"Do you know why I cook without salt?" she said finally.

The non sequitur knocked me sideways. "You—what?"

"The dish I served you, the first night. The one you cried over." She wasn't looking at me; she was looking at her hands. "Teo's dish. I made it for him the night he died. And I've made it ten thousand times since, and I have never once been able to put salt in it. Because the last thing I ever cooked for him was perfect, and then he went out on that water, and somewhere in my head, if the dish stays unfinished — unsalted, incomplete — then the night stays unfinished too. And a night that isn't finished can't end badly. Do you understand? It's madness. I know it's madness. But grief doesn't negotiate."

"Luz told me," I said quietly. "About the salt. She thinks it's a recipe secret."

"It's a wound secret." She smiled, and it was terrible and lovely at once. "There are two kinds of secrets in this world, Cyrus. The kind you keep to protect yourself, and the kind other people keep to protect themselves *from* you. Your family kept the second kind. For thirty years, every Villalobos at that dinner table was keeping a secret from me, and the effort of it — do you know what that does to a family? It's like carrying water in your hands. Everyone has to walk slowly. Nobody can reach for anything. Everyone resents everyone else for the slowness, and no one will say why."

"That's exactly what it was." My voice cracked on it. "That's — God, that's exactly it. We walked slowly for thirty years. My father's whole life was walking slowly with his hands full."

"And yours?"

"Mine?"

"You're a critic." She said the word without contempt, which somehow made it worse. "A man whose entire profession is telling the truth about other people's tables. Don't you think that's a strange job for a boy who grew up in a house where the truth about his own table was forbidden? You didn't choose criticism, mijo. Criticism chose you. It's the only honest language anyone taught you."

I stared at her. The onions sat forgotten on the board, their sharpness stinging my eyes, and I let myself cry a little from them, using the vegetables as cover the way men of my family had always used work and wine and eloquence as cover.

"Tell me," I said. "Please. Whatever you know. Even if it's nothing. Even if the answer is that you don't know either. I need to hear it from you and not from the silence."

Marisol rose. She moved to the pantry — the deep one, the old one, with shelves that sagged under jars of dried chiles and hoarded sugar — and I assumed she was getting more coffee or another jar of something, and I looked back out at the water, and it took me a moment to realize she was crying.

Not loudly. Not dramatically. She stood with her back to me in front of the pantry and her shoulders shook, once, twice, and then went still, and when she turned around her face was composed again, that guarded hospitality locked back into place, except her eyes were wet and furious and afraid all at the same time.

"He wrote to me," she said. "Before. Before the boat. A letter. He was coming to tell the family — about us, about the music, about leaving the restaurant. He wanted to do it properly, face to face, but he wrote it down first because Teo always needed to write a thing down before he could say it. You know that about him?"

I nodded. I couldn't speak.

"After the funeral, after your father's lawyer came with the check and the threats — and they were threats, Cyrus, dressed up as generosity, but threats — I kept the letter. I was supposed to burn everything. His letters, our songs, the photographs. I burned most of it. I sat right there—" she pointed at the stove, at the burner she used for everything, births and deaths and daily bread— "and I burned four years of my life in a metal bucket. But I couldn't burn that one. Because it wasn't about me. It was about him. It was his voice. And your family had already taken his voice away — they'd made him into a poor drowned boy led astray by a bad girl, they'd made him into a *story* — and that letter was the last place Teo still sounded like Teo."

"So you've had it. All this time. Thirty years, you've had—"

"In a tin. Behind the dried chiles. Where I could see it every day and touch it every day and never read it again, because reading it meant the night was finished." She laughed, short and broken. "Grief doesn't negotiate. I told you."

"Why now?" I whispered. "Why show me now?"

"Because you asked the question." She crossed the kitchen and stood over me, and I saw that she was older than I'd been letting myself see — that the months had been taking something from her that she hid well, that there was a fragility in her wrists I'd been refusing to notice. "Because you sat in my kitchen and asked it out loud, which means the water is carrying it now, which means it can't be put back. And because—" she stopped, and pressed her lips together, and started again— "because I am not going to be here forever, and that letter deserves to be read by someone who loved him. Read *aloud*. He wrote it to be heard. He was a musician. Everything he wrote was written to be heard."

She went back to the pantry. I heard the tin — a small metallic scrape, a lid resisting, giving. When she turned around she was holding an envelope gone soft and yellow with age, soft as cloth from thirty years of being touched, and I recognized the handwriting on it before I recognized anything else, because I had spent my childhood watching that handwriting appear across the tops of my school papers, on birthday cards, on the score paper he propped on the piano:

*Para Marisol* — and beneath it, in smaller letters, the way he signed everything, half joke and half prayer: *the last verse is coming.*

My hands went out before I decided anything. She placed the letter in them like handing over a child, or a flame.

"Read it," Marisol said, and sat down across from me, and folded her ruined hands, and waited — the way she must have waited for thirty years, the way the whole coast waits for weather — "read it to me. Out loud. Every word. Let him finish talking."

I broke the seal my brother had sealed the summer the water took him, and unfolded the pages, and began.

Outside, the boats were coming home.
