## 20. The Last Verse

**Marisol**

The song had been sitting in my chest for thirty years like a bone that never set right.

I knew every note of it the way you know a scar — not because you study it, but because it's yours. Teo had written the melody on the back of an order pad from his father's restaurant, in pencil, the graphite already smudging when he sang it to me that last summer. He had two verses and a chorus and a bridge that climbed like stairs toward a window, and then he had nothing, because he had run out of time before he ran out of song.

*Y la última estrofa te la debo a ti,* he'd said, laughing, his head in my lap on the beach at Punta Clara while the tide came for our shoes. *I owe you the last verse.*

He never paid that debt. And I had spent three decades refusing to pay it for him, because finishing it felt like closing a door, and I was not ready to live in a house where that door stayed shut.

But Cyrus Villalobos sat at my kitchen table now with a pencil of his own, and outside, Beto was sweeping the cantina floor with the broom he held like a drumstick, tapping out time against the tiles, and Luz was arguing with Chuy about whether a radio could love someone back, and I thought: *the door has been open for weeks. You just haven't looked at it.*

"Play it once," I said. "Before we touch anything. Play it the way you heard it on the radio."

Cyrus looked up. He had his mother's eyes — I'd known that since the first night, though I'd said nothing then. The jaw was Aurelio's, that hard square line, but the eyes were hers, soft and startled, always a half-second behind the world.

"You remember how it goes?"

"I remember everything about that summer except how to stop it." I wiped my hands on my apron, a habit, not a need; they were clean. "Go on."

He hummed it first, uncertain, the way a man tests ice. Then he found the words — Teo's words, the ones from the order pad, which I had never let myself forget either — and his voice cracked on the second verse, and I let it crack. A voice that doesn't crack is a voice that isn't telling the truth.

When he finished, the kitchen was very quiet. From the cantina came the small sound of Beto's broom stopping.

"That's where it stops," Cyrus said. "That's where it always stopped. On the radio too — the DJ faded it out right there, like even she didn't know what came next."

"Nobody knows what comes next. That's what makes it the last verse." I pulled out the chair across from him and sat, and put both my hands flat on the table between us, palms down, the way I did when I needed the wood to hold me steady. "Teo wrote melodies the way water finds its way downhill. He'd have wanted the ending to fall. To land somewhere low and warm. Not up high, not triumphant. He didn't trust triumph."

Cyrus turned the pencil over in his fingers. "What did he trust?"

"The tide coming back." I almost smiled. "You wouldn't know it from how your family treated the sea. Aurelio built that restaurant facing away from the water, did you ever notice? All those years, all that money, and he built a wall with tables behind it."

"He built a lot of walls."

"Yes. And you've been living inside one of them so long you call it an apartment." I said it without cruelty — or with only the cruelty that truth requires, which is different. "So. Words are yours. That's the bargain. I'll carry the melody; you tell me what happened after. What the silence was like from the inside. Because that's the verse he couldn't write, mijo. The one where the singer is gone and everyone else has to keep singing anyway."

He went pale. I watched the color leave his face like a tide going out, and I understood that no one had ever asked him to describe the silence — that his whole family had agreed to pretend there wasn't one, that his whole profession was a way of filling pages without saying anything at all.

"All right," he said finally. His voice was rough. "All right. But it might take more than one night."

"It took me thirty years," I said. "You have until dinner."

* * *

**Cyrus**

Here is what I learned about writing with Marisol Reyes: she did not believe in inspiration. She believed in work.

We sat across from each other at the kitchen table for four evenings while the cantina emptied and Beto locked the doors and Luz did her homework in the corner booth with her earbuds in — or pretending to, one earbud always loose, listening to us the way she listened to everything, sideways and hungry. Marisol would hum a phrase and stop. She'd tap the table twice. She'd say *no, lower* or *again, but sadder this time*, and I would scrape back whatever line I'd offered and try another.

My first attempts were terrible. I know that now. They were the kind of lines I'd been writing for years in my column — polished, weightless, food-magazine beautiful. *The night remembers what the morning forgives.* Marisol read that one off my notebook page and looked at me with such flat disappointment that I felt eight years old.

"That's a postcard," she said. "Not a verse. A postcard says *wish you were here*. A verse says why you're not."

So I stopped performing. It was the same muscle Priya had caught me failing to use at that press dinner, months ago now, a lifetime ago — the muscle of actually swallowing, of letting something get all the way inside you. I started telling the truth into the notebook, ugly and plain. About the year after Teo drowned. About how my mother stopped cooking and started ordering catering for family dinners, and how we all ate beautiful food in silence like mourners at a catered wake. About how I learned that if you described a dish precisely enough, no one would ever ask whether you'd tasted it.

Marisol listened to all of it without interrupting. Once, when I told her about the review I filed the week of my mother's funeral — the glowing one, the fraud one — she reached across the table and closed my notebook gently, the way you close the eyes of the dead.

"Now you're in the song," she said.

On the fifth evening, the verse arrived. Not all at once — it came the way she said Teo's melodies came, finding its way downhill. I wrote a line about eating without hunger, and she shook her head and hummed the bridge again, that staircase climbing toward the window, and I understood suddenly that the bridge wasn't climbing toward anything. It was climbing *away*. It was Teo leaving. The last verse had to be the view from the window he never got to — the life he saw from the top of the stairs, the restaurant by the sea with music in it, the family that wasn't built on silence.

I wrote:

*Build the house with open doors,*
*let the salt come in with the tide,*
*and if I'm not there when the music starts,*
*sing it loud enough for two —*
*I'll be in the floor, I'll be in the wave,*
*I'll be the taste you can't describe.*

Marisol read it twice. Her lips moved on the second pass. Then she got up, went to the shelf above the stove where she kept the good bottle she never opened, and poured two small glasses of mezcal.

"To the debt," she said, and touched her glass to mine. "Paid."

* * *

**Marisol**

I did not sleep that night. Not from grief — from a strange, humming alertness, like the hour before guests arrive when the pots are all clean and the tables are set and the whole building seems to hold its breath.

At dawn I stood in the empty cantina and decided things.

If we were going to sing it, we would sing it properly. Once. Here. For the people in this room, who had become — I could say the word now, I had earned the word — my family. Not for the radio. Not for any archive. Not for Aurelio Villalobos, whose name I would not speak in this building, and whose anniversary tribute I had read online with a cold, quiet fury that Cyrus still didn't know about.

Beto arrived at seven, as he always did, letting himself in with his own key, and found me moving tables.

"What are we doing?" he signed, then, catching my face, answered himself: "A concert?"

"One song."

His whole body changed. My nephew, who had never heard a note of music in his life and felt more of it than anyone I knew, set down his bag and signed, fast and bright: *Whose song?*

"Your uncle Teo's."

I watched him take that in. He had grown up on stories of Teo the way other children grow up on saints — the man who died young, who loved music, whose name we kept like a candle in a back room. Beto pressed his palm flat against the floorboards, the way he did when Luz played her terrible pop songs too loud, feeling for the shape of what was coming.

*Then we practice,* he signed. *The floor has to learn it too.*

By noon, Luz knew. She took the news the way she took everything — first with armor (*fine, whatever, I don't even like singing in front of people*) and then, ten minutes later, standing beside me at the stove, quietly: "Is it sad?"

"It's true," I said. "It's both."

"Okay." She chopped onions with fierce concentration. "I can do both."

Chuy came in at four with a radio under his arm — the old one, the 1962 Philips he'd restored twice, the one that had started all of this, playing Teo's song into a coastal night and pulling a grieving man off the side of a highway. He set it on the bar like an offering.

"For luck," he said. "And because a song should be born near a radio. That's how songs travel now. Like seeds on the wind."

* * *

**Cyrus**

On the sixth evening, we performed it.

There was no announcement. Marisol simply cooked — a long, slow dinner, the kind she made when something mattered, courses arriving like chapters — and when the plates were cleared she nodded to Beto, and Beto sat down on the floor of the cantina, cross-legged, barefoot, his palms flat against the boards, and Luz took the stool beside the microphone-less microphone, the space where a microphone would be if we were the kind of place that had one, and I stood next to Marisol in the middle of the room feeling like a man about to step off a pier.

I hadn't sung in front of anyone since I was nine years old, at a school recital my mother filmed and my father critiqued. But this wasn't performance. I understood that as soon as Marisol began to play — she'd brought out a guitar I'd never seen, old and small-bodied, kept in a case under her bed, and when I asked her earlier she'd said only, *it waited long enough.*

Her voice was lower than it had been at twenty-two. Time had taken the brightness out of it and left something better: grain, weight, the sound of a woman who had carried a song for thirty years and was setting it down at last. She sang the first verse and I felt the room change temperature. Chuy took off his cap. Luz's hands went still in her lap.

I came in on the second verse, my voice cracking exactly where it had cracked in the kitchen, and this time the crack sounded right, sounded like the song had been built to hold it. Luz lifted her voice under ours on the chorus — a clear, untrained harmony, a third above the melody, the kind of thing nobody teaches and some people simply know — and the cantina filled up with all three of us, and underneath us, through the floor, Beto.

I watched him while we sang. He had his eyes closed and his hands spread wide on the boards, and his body moved — not dancing, something older than dancing, something tidal. He was conducting us. When the bridge climbed, he leaned forward; when we landed in the last verse, low and warm, the way Teo would have wanted, he pressed his palms down hard and nodded, nodding, nodding, keeping time with the tide.

*Build the house with open doors,*
*let the salt come in with the tide...*

I sang *I'll be the taste you can't describe* and my throat closed and I kept singing anyway, wet-eyed, off-pitch, honest, and across the room Marisol met my gaze and did not look away, and neither did I. We finished together. The last chord hung in the air of the cantina like dust in light.

Nobody clapped. That was the miracle of it. Chuy put his cap back on. Luz wiped her face with her sleeve and pretended she hadn't. Beto opened his eyes and signed one word at the floor, at the room, at his dead-before-he-was-born uncle:

*Beautiful.*

Then Luz ruined everything, the way teenagers do, the way they save you: "Okay, but that was a rehearsal, right? Because we're doing it again at — " and she stopped herself, glancing at Marisol, and the whole room went careful and quiet, because everyone knew about the doctor's visit now, or thought they did, or had guessed, the way families guess.

Marisol smiled at her, slow and sure. "Again whenever you want, niña. It's not going anywhere anymore." She rested her hand on the guitar's neck. "Neither am I. Not really. Songs don't die. They just wait for someone to finish them."

Later, after the dishes, after Luz had gone up to bed and Beto had wheeled his drum kit home and Chuy had locked the doors, Marisol and I sat in the kitchen with the last of the mezcal and the order-pad page — the original, thirty years soft at the folds — lying between us next to my notebook page, pencil and pencil.

"There's one thing left," she said.

I waited.

"Aurelio's anniversary is Saturday. Your tribute piece runs Sunday." She turned her glass slowly on the table. "I've read what you wrote about him, Cyrus. It's generous. It's well-made. And it's a lie built out of true sentences, which is the most expensive kind." She looked up at me, and her eyes were my mother's eyes looking back at me from another face, another life, another family — the family I had chosen, the one that had chosen me back. "You can't finish his song and keep singing theirs. Not both. The house with open doors doesn't have room for walls."

I looked down at the two pages side by side — Teo's handwriting fading, mine fresh — and I thought about the column waiting in my drafts folder in the city, polished and weightless, a postcard that said *wish you were here* to a father who had never once wondered where I was.

"I know," I said. "I've known since the bridge."

"Then tomorrow," Marisol said, "you write the real one."
