## 7. Tomas Explains Nothing

### Oscar

Finding Tomas took me four days and cost me the last of my dignity at a bowling alley.

I want to be clear that I did not expect my mother's past to have a bowling alley in it. I had imagined, if I imagined anything, something more literary — a shuttered bookshop, a pier, a church with bad acoustics. But the address Tomas had left scrawled on the back of a sympathy card turned out to be the Fenwick Lanes, a building shaped like a shoebox with ambition, its sign missing the *e* so it read FENWICK LAN S, which felt less like vandalism than prophecy. The whole neighborhood had that quality — things missing their final letters, their final residents, their final reasons to stand.

The man behind the counter said Tomas came in Tuesdays and Thursdays, league nights, always lane seven, always alone since Marta died. He said this the way people in Fenwick said everything: as though privacy were a currency that had been devalued long ago and nobody was pretending otherwise.

So on Thursday I sat in the plastic chairs by the ball return and watched a seventy-something man in a cardigan the color of strong tea bowl a 147 game against nobody. His form was textbook. His face was granite. When he finally noticed me — I'd been staring like a private detective in a bad film, which is to say conspicuously — he didn't startle. He just set his ball down, wiped his hands on a towel he produced from nowhere, and said:

"You're Marion's boy."

Not a question. That was the thing about Tomas, I would learn. He didn't ask questions because he'd already done the asking, decades ago, and been given answers he hadn't liked.

"Oscar," I said.

"I know." He nodded at the seat beside him. "Sit. I'm between frames, which is the only time I'm good for conversation."

I sat. The vinyl was cold through my trousers. Somewhere down the lanes a child was celebrating a strike with the unselfconscious shriek of someone who has never once considered what it costs to be happy.

"You bowl well," I offered, because I am my mother's son and therefore filled silences with pleasantries even when the silence is load-bearing.

"I bowl the way old men do everything," Tomas said. "Correctly and without joy." He looked at me then, really looked, and whatever he saw made him exhale through his nose. "You have her way of sitting. Like you're apologizing for taking up space."

"She taught me that."

"No," he said quietly. "She taught you that by example."

I had rehearsed questions on the bus over — I'd written them in a notebook like an investigative journalist, which is what grief had apparently made of me: *Who was Amber Vale? Why did she leave? Who is V. Lindqvist? What happened in 1989?* But sitting next to this man, smelling the wax and the old coffee, every question felt obscene, like asking a surgeon to describe his first mistake over dinner.

Tomas saved me from myself. He always would, I suspect. That was his function in other people's stories.

"You've got the cards," he said.

It wasn't a question either. "Forty-one," I managed.

"Forty-one." He closed his eyes briefly, the way you do when a number confirms a wound. "She never sent them. I used to tell her — Vale, I told her, a stamp costs less than a confession and weighs nothing at all. And she'd say, *that's exactly the problem, Tommo.*" He opened his eyes. "You called her Marion your whole life."

"Yes."

"And they buried her under that name too. I checked. I went." He picked up his towel, folded it, unfolded it. "Stood at the back like a burglar at a funeral. Which is roughly what I was. A thief of attendance."

"Why 'Vale'?" I asked. It wasn't even one of my notebook questions; it simply fell out of me. "Amber Vale. Was it — a joke? Amber, Vale?"

"It was a door," Tomas said. "Names are doors. Some people are born in a house with only one, and some people build extra ones so they can leave without anyone hearing the latch." He stood, retrieved his ball, and rolled a strike while barely looking. Then he sat back down as if the ball were incidental. "Marion Amberdale was the daughter who stayed. Amber Vale was the woman who almost didn't."

"*Almost.*"

"There's a story in that word and I'm not the one who should tell it." He held up a hand before I could protest. "No. Hear me out, boy, because I'm going to give you something better than the story. I'm going to give you the shape of it, so when somebody else tells you the story, you'll know if they're lying."

The child down the lanes shrieked again. Tomas waited out the noise with the patience of a man who had spent a lifetime waiting out noises.

"I loved her," he said. Flatly, the way you report weather. "From the summer we were nineteen until roughly last Tuesday. I loved her the way you love a window in a house you can't afford — you press your face to it, you see the life inside, and eventually you go home." He smiled, and for a second the granite cracked and I saw the nineteen-year-old underneath, all elbows and certainty. "But I wasn't the one she wrote forty-one postcards to. I wasn't the one whose name she couldn't finish writing."

"Vera Lindqvist."

"Vera." He said it the way you set something fragile down on a high shelf. "They met at the textile mill — no, don't write that down, it's not important how, it's important *that*. Vera Lindqvist worked the looms and sang while she did it, badly, on purpose, because the foreman hated singing. Your mother — Vale — was there doing the family's books, slumming it in her father's opinion, though she'd have fought you over that word. And one day Vera sang something filthy in Swedish, assuming nobody understood, and Vale laughed out loud across the floor, and that was that." He shrugged. "Thirty years of history, and it starts with a dirty song. Most things do, if you trace them back far enough."

The ball return clunked. My hands were shaking slightly, and I pressed them flat against my knees like a schoolboy keeping his place.

"They were together," I said. Not a question. I was learning.

"For six years. Openly, in the way that two women can be openly together in a neighborhood where everyone knows everyone and no one tells — which is to say, openly to us and invisibly to the world. Everyone in Fenwick knew. The baker knew. The priest pretended not to know, which in that parish was a sacrament." Tomas's voice hardened by degrees, like cooling metal. "And then the family found out. Properly found out. Not suspected — *found*, with documents, with leverage. And they gave her a choice dressed up as a gift: come home, take up the estate, be Marion again, full name restored, or stay here and be cut off so thoroughly that even her name would be taken away. Not disowned, you understand. *Erased.* There's a difference. Disowning leaves a scar. Erasing leaves a smooth wall, and everyone pretends the wall was always there."

"Did she—"

"She chose the estate." He said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse. "Don't judge her yet. You haven't seen what they held over her. I haven't either, not all of it, but I saw enough to know the choice wasn't free. Choices made under duress aren't choices, they're sentences being served." He looked at me. "But she chose it, and she left, and Vera stayed, and I stayed, and the neighborhood stayed, and now the neighborhood's being demolished, which feels about right. Everything that remembers her is coming down. Convenient, isn't it, how redevelopment rhymes with forgetting."

I thought of the burned corner in the family photo album. I thought of Uncle Gerald's gentle voice saying *some things are filed for a reason, Oscar.* I said none of this. Tomas watched me not say it, and nodded slowly, as if I'd passed a test.

"You're going to find Vera," he said.

"I need to."

"Do you?" He leaned forward. "Genuine question, boy, and think before you answer, because I'm an old man and I haven't got time to watch you make the fashionable mistakes. Do you *need* to, or do you need to feel like someone who needs to? Because those are different hungers and they eat different things."

I thought about it honestly, which is the hardest kind of thinking. "Both," I admitted. "Is that disqualifying?"

"No. It's human. Disqualifying would be if you said *only*." He sat back. "Then I'll tell you where she is. Above the bakery on Corven Street — Kowalczyk's, the blue awning. She's seventy-eight and her hands hurt and she will pretend she doesn't know who you are for at least ten minutes, and you will let her, because that's the toll." He paused. "There's something else you should know before you knock on that door."

"Whatever it is, I've probably already guessed wrong versions of it."

"Good instinct." He rubbed his jaw. "Vera didn't just lose your mother. She lost—" He stopped. Started again. "There was more in that house than two women and a cat. There was a future. Futures are the expensive part. People think the tragedy is the love ending. The tragedy is everything the love was *going to become* that never got the chance to disappoint anybody." He stood, collected his ball, and this time held it, turning it in his spotted hands like a planet. "Some doors open outward onto drops, Oscar. Your mother spent forty years standing at one, holding it shut with her body. She mailed nothing because mailing something means the drop gets a voice. Understand that before you go kicking doors open. Whatever's on the other side falls *out*. Onto you. Onto Vera. Onto whoever else is standing in the room."

"Whoever else," I repeated. "Meaning there is someone else."

Tomas rolled the ball. Seven pins fell. He watched the remaining three with visible contempt.

"Meaning," he said, "that Fenwick is small, and memory is smaller, and you should talk to Vera before you go poking around the community center on Delancey, where people who ask questions get answers they haven't earned." He glanced at me sideways. "That's not a warning. That's a map with one road blacked out. Figure out why."

We sat in silence for a while after that, watching the child bowl gutter balls with undiminished joy. Eventually Tomas spoke again, softer.

"I'll tell you one true thing about her, since you'll be needing true things and won't know where to shop. Your mother — Vale — used to say that silence wasn't empty. She said it was a letter still being written, and that some letters take longer than others, and that the shame isn't in the length, it's in pretending you never picked up the pen." He shook his head. "I thought it was nonsense when I was twenty. Poetry as anesthesia. Now I'm seventy-four and I think it might be the truest thing anyone ever said to me, and she said it about herself, about what she was doing to Vera, and she *still* didn't mail the cards. That's the size of what they held over her, boy. Big enough to beat poetry."

He walked me to the door. Outside, Fenwick was doing its evening trick — half the shops dark, the other half lit defiantly, murals staring down from brick walls like witnesses who refused to leave the stand.

"Come back Thursdays," Tomas said. "League night. I bowl against nobody, but nobody cheats, so it's fair."

"Thank you," I said. "For the map."

"Don't thank me." He zipped the cardigan to his chin against the wind. "Thank her. Forty-one postcards means she was trying to pick up the pen for thirty years. All you're doing is finishing her sentence." He paused on the threshold. "Just make sure you finish it *hers* and not yours. There's a difference, and Vera will know."

### Tomas

The boy walked off toward Corven Street with the postcards in a canvas bag slung across his chest like a bandolier, and I stood in the doorway of the Lanes and let myself have the feeling I'd been refusing all evening, which was this: Marion's chin, Marion's apologetic way of occupying a chair, Marion's habit of thanking people for things that weren't kindnesses but debts repaid.

I went back inside and bowled the rest of my game. Nobody cheated.

Here is what I didn't tell him, and why.

I didn't tell him that in 1989, the week she left, I drove her to the station myself, because Vera couldn't — Vera had said she wouldn't stand at a platform and watch, and I respected it then and respect it now, though I have wondered for thirty-five years whether it was dignity or whether it was the other thing, the thing that makes a person unable to witness their own amputation. I put Vale's two suitcases in the boot of my brother's borrowed car and she sat with her hands in her lap like a passenger being transported somewhere against her will, which technically she was.

At the platform she said, "Tommo, if she ever comes looking—"

"She won't," I said. "You know she won't. They'll see to that."

"But if she does. If someday there's a knock." Her hands twisted in her lap. "Tell her I didn't stop writing. Tell her the letters kept — tell her I—"

And then the train came in, because trains have no respect for unfinished sentences, and she kissed my cheek and got on, and the last I saw of Amber Vale was her hand raised at the window, and the last thing she ever asked of me was a message I was never able to deliver, because Vera Lindqvist does not take messages about Marion Amberdale, and I understood that too.

Until now. Until a boy with her chin sat down next to me holding forty-one unsent postcards like evidence at a trial nobody ever convened.

I told him some doors open outward onto drops. True. But I didn't tell him the rest of it, the part that's mine to carry: that I've spent thirty-five years standing at that same door, holding it shut from the other side, because I promised her. *If she ever comes looking, Tommo — don't make it easy. Make sure it's real. Make sure whoever's knocking wants the truth more than they want the story.*

The boy wants the truth. I think he does. But wanting it and surviving it are different sports, and there's a piece he doesn't have yet, a piece that isn't mine to give, a piece that lives at the community center on Delancey Street with a stethoscope around her neck and her mother's stubbornness and — though she doesn't know it, though *nobody* ever told her — Marion's laugh.

Nell.

I said nothing. I gave him a map with one road blacked out, and I watched him walk toward the bakery, and I picked up my ball, and I told myself the old lie, the comfortable one: *not my story to tell.*

But I'm seventy-four years old, and my arm is getting worse, and the truth is I'm tired of holding doors.

Thursday, I thought. Maybe Thursday I'll tell him about Nell myself. Before he finds her the wrong way. Before he introduces himself as a researcher, God help us, and loses her before he ever had her.

Maybe Thursday.

Nobody cheats.
