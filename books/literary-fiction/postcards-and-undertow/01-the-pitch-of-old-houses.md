## 1. The Pitch of Old Houses

Every house has a pitch. Not the note it plays when you strike something—the accidental music of doors and banisters—but a fundamental tone underneath all that, the frequency at which its bones prefer to hum. Most people never hear it because most people never shut up long enough to listen. I do shut up. It's my profession, more or less. People think piano tuning is about pianos. It isn't. It's about listening to everything else first, so you know what you're tuning against.

The Vasseur house sat on the salt side of the dune road like an old woman who refuses to admit she's cold. Gray shingles gone silver, white trim gone gray, a wraparound porch with railings that had been repainted exactly often enough to avoid condemnation. Ten years I'd been coming here, and in ten years the house had dropped perhaps a quarter-tone overall—settling into itself the way old voices settle, lower and less willing.

I parked my van where the gravel always swallowed my tires to the hubcap and sat for a moment, as I always did, with my hands on the wheel, listening through the closed windows. This was not superstition. Well. It was not *only* superstition. A house tells you what kind of day it's going to be before you reach the door. Today it told me: wind from the northeast, radiators already complaining, and—one voice short. I couldn't have said which one. That's the trouble with counting by ear. You only notice the missing voice after you've noticed the silence around it.

I got out. The gravel announced me, which seemed fair; the house would announce itself back.

Céleste Vasseur opened the door before I knocked, which was her way. She had a talent for arriving at thresholds at precisely the moment you did, as though she'd been standing behind them all morning waiting for the world to require her. She was seventy-one and wore her age the way some women wear scarves—deliberately, beautifully, and as a kind of armor.

"Orsen." She said my name the way she said everything, with a faint French rounding of the vowels she'd never quite surrendered and never quite claimed. "You're punctual. Édouard says tuners are either punctual or deaf."

"Édouard is right," I said. "Both, in my case."

She laughed the laugh she'd been laughing for ten years, warm on the surface, and stepped aside. "The parlor piano. It's been sulking since October. And Odile wants the whole house done before—" She stopped herself on the word the way you stop yourself on a stair that isn't there anymore. "Before winter."

Before winter. Not: before the sale. Not yet. In October the signs hadn't gone up. But houses hear things before their owners say them, and I'd been hearing something in this one for weeks—a new hollowness in the hallways, a change in how sound died in the rooms. Furniture being moved does that. Furniture being *removed* does something else entirely.

"Parlor first," I said, and followed her in.

* * *

Let me explain how I read a room, since apparently I'm the sort of man who explains things now, alone, to no one, in his own head.

A furnished room has a certain acoustic density. Sofas eat high frequencies. Curtains soften the attack of every footstep. Bookshelves scatter sound into a pleasant fuzz. When you walk into a lived-in room, your ears register *fullness* before your eyes register anything at all. It's why hotel rooms feel like lies even when they're beautiful. They're pitched correctly but they hold no memory; sound goes into them and comes back out unchanged, unembarrassed, having learned nothing.

The Vasseur front hall had always been dense with memory. Coat hooks that rattled in the northeast wind. An umbrella stand that rang a low B whenever the door swung hard. A grandfather clock in the study whose tick I could feel through the floorboards of the parlor, eleven feet away, like a second pulse under the house's first.

Today the umbrella stand was silent. Not gone—I could see it, still standing its post by the door—but muffled, wrapped in newspaper, the way you pack things you intend to ship. The coat hooks were bare. The Persian runner in the hall had been rolled back from the stairs, exposing pine boards that threw my footsteps back at me sharp and naked, a stranger's footsteps, though they were mine.

I said nothing. Saying nothing is also my profession.

"You'll forgive the state of things," Céleste said, gliding ahead of me. "We're doing some reorganizing. Odile insists."

Odile. The younger daughter, the brisk one, the one who'd arrived sometime in the last few years as the family's spine and knew it and resented knowing it. Odile organized grief the way other people organize closets: ruthlessly, with labels. I liked her and feared her in roughly equal measure, which I suspect is how everyone in that family felt about everyone else.

"The parlor," Céleste repeated, and opened the double doors with both hands, a small ceremony she performed every time, as if the room were a theater and the piano a diva who required proper introduction.

The parlor piano was a 1912 Pleyel, upright, walnut, older than anyone currently living in the house except possibly Céleste, and better preserved than all of them. I had tuned it forty-some times over ten years. I knew its sins intimately: the F above middle C ran sharp when the humidity dropped, the bass bridge had a hairline whisper I could hear but three technicians had failed to find, and the damper felts on the top octave were tired, letting notes bloom a half-second too long, like guests who don't take the hint.

But before I ever opened my case, I stood in the doorway and listened to the room.

Emptier than furnished. That was the phrase my mind handed me, and it's an odd phrase, I'll grant you, because the parlor looked full enough—the Pleyel, the two settees, the marble-topped table with its bowl of (artificial, always artificial; fresh flowers were for funerals in that house, though nobody said so) hydrangeas. But the sound was wrong. Sound went into that room and didn't come back right. There was a dead patch along the east wall where the echo used to live, and when I clapped once, softly, experimentally, the clap fell into the floor and didn't get up.

Something had been taken out of that corner. Something heavy, or something that had been there so long the room had grown around it.

"Radiators are loud today," Céleste observed, following my silence rather than my gaze. She was good at that. Too good.

"They're always loud in November."

"They're louder than usual."

They were. The cast-iron radiators in that house had a vocabulary I'd catalogued over a decade: the knock in the entry radiator was air in the line, harmless; the hiss in the library was a valve that wanted replacing and would never get it; but the parlor radiator made a sound today I hadn't heard before—a slow, irregular ticking, like something cooling down, or counting down. I filed it away. My head is full of such files. It's a crowded attic up there and nothing gets thrown out.

I opened my case. Tuning fork, felt strips, mutes, the little rubber wedges like a surgeon's candy dish. The ritual of laying them out calms people, I've found. It says: *nothing is wrong that order cannot address.* Which is a lie, of course, but a useful one, and I've built a career on useful lies told with felt strips.

"Will you stay?" I asked Céleste, as I asked every time. Some owners stayed. Édouard used to stay, in the years when he could sit for an hour, saying nothing, watching my hands with an attention that unnerved the younger me. Now he stayed mostly in his chair by the study window, humming.

"I have calls to make." She paused at the threshold, hand on the doorframe, and for a moment—half a second, no longer—she looked at the east wall. The dead-patch wall. Then she looked at me, and whatever had crossed her face was folded up and put away so fast I could almost believe I'd imagined it. Almost. "Odile will be in and out. Don't let her rush you. She rushes everything now."

"She's efficient."

"She's frightened," Céleste said pleasantly, and left.

That was the thing about Céleste Vasseur. She said true things in the tone of false ones, so you could never quote her, never accuse her, never quite be sure. Ten years of practice had taught me that her charm was load-bearing. Remove it and who knew what the family's architecture would do.

* * *

I worked. You want the mechanics? Fine, briefly: felt strip across the middle section, mute the outer strings of each unison, strike the fork against my knee—not the tuning pin, amateurs crack forks that way—and set the temperament octave, then work outward, octaves and fifths, stretching the treble sharp and the bass flat because strings aren't mathematicians and neither, thank God, are ears. Forty minutes for the rough pass, another hour for fine voicing if the piano deserves it. The Pleyel deserved it. The Pleyel always deserved it.

And while I worked, the house talked to me, the way it always did.

Houses talk through their plumbing first. The Vasseurs' pipes were lead and galvanized steel, a century of mineral throat-clearing, and each fixture had its own voice. The kitchen tap groaned in thirds when it shut off. The upstairs bath dripped at a tempo I could have notated: dotted rhythm, slightly dragging, like a waltz played by someone thinking about something else. On my first visits I'd found the arrhythmia maddening. Now I found its absence maddening. Houses, like people, become themselves through their flaws.

Then the floorboards. Every third board on the stair to the second floor spoke in G, a soft woody G, and the fourth held its tongue until you put your weight fully on it and then confessed in a creak like a question. Marguerite used to jump the fourth step. I know this because I heard it—or rather, I heard it stop happening.

Marguerite. I'm going to have to talk about her eventually, so I may as well start badly, the way all beginnings in this story are bad.

Marguerite Vasseur was the elder daughter, the pianist, the one the Pleyel belonged to in every way that mattered, ownership papers notwithstanding. She drowned the summer she was twenty-two, off the point past the boathouse, on a night in late August when the water was warm and the sky was doing that thing coastal skies do, going gold and bruised at once. The police report said she swam alone. The town said it gently, the way towns say terrible things, wrapping each word in wool. The family said nothing at all, which is its own kind of sentence, and I have listened to that silence for ten years the way you listen to a cracked bell—knowing the flaw is there, hearing it every time the thing is struck, unable to locate it precisely, unable to stop listening.

I tuned the piano the week after the funeral. Nobody asked me to. Céleste called and said, "The piano will need it, won't it, after all the doors being open and shut," and I said yes, ma'am, and came, and tuned it, and the whole time the house was so quiet I could hear the tide two hundred yards off, and the piano was a half-step flat, every string, as if it had leaned toward the ground in mourning and decided to stay there.

I brought it back up to pitch. It took three visits. Some part of me has always wondered whether I should have let it stay flat.

* * *

The parlor door opened at four o'clock without a knock, which meant Odile.

Odile Vasseur—she'd kept the name through a marriage that lasted four years and produced a divorce and, as far as I could tell, no regrets worth mentioning—was fifty-three and dressed like a woman who had personally appointed herself executor of reality. Clipboard. Reading glasses on a chain. The particular posture of someone carrying a house's entire future in a folder.

"Don't stop," she said, when my hands paused over the keys. "I just need to measure the window alcove. The buyer's surveyor wants dimensions."

"The buyer's surveyor," I repeated, keeping my eyes on the pins. "So it's real, then."

"It's been real since August, Orsen. Where have you been?"

Here, I almost said. Here the whole time, the way I've been here the whole time, ten years of Thursdays and emergency calls and Christmases when the family needed the piano presentable for carols nobody sang with any conviction. But I said, "August was busy," which was true in the way most of my sentences were true: technically, hollowly.

She measured the alcove. Tape measure singing out, snapping back. She wrote numbers on the clipboard with the finality of a judge signing warrants. Then, instead of leaving, she drifted—there's no other word for it—toward the east wall. Toward the wardrobe.

It was a large piece, that wardrobe, walnut and mirrored, older than the piano, older probably than the house's paint. It stood against the east wall like a sentry nobody remembered posting. I had never once heard it open. In ten years. Think about that. A wardrobe in a family home, and in ten years of Thursdays I had never heard its hinges speak. Children had grown up in this house. Seasons had cycled. Wardrobes get opened. Unless they're not wardrobes anymore. Unless they've been promoted to something else—monument, seal, headstone with doors.

Odile put her palm flat against its side panel, the way you'd check a horse for fever, and stood there a moment. I watched her in the mirror, which gave me her face at an angle she didn't know I could see. What was on it wasn't efficiency. Efficiency was the costume. Underneath was something rawer and much quieter, the look of a woman standing next to a locked thing she had agreed, long ago, never to ask about.

"Orsen." Still facing the wardrobe. "When you're done with the parlor, the music room piano needs doing too. Marguerite's. We haven't had it touched since—" The pause again, the missing stair. "In a while."

The music room piano. The grand. The good one. Untouched since the summer Marguerite died, which meant ten years of drift, ten years of that instrument sliding slowly, gracefully, catastrophically out of true, and a family that walked past its silence every day and called the walking something else.

"I'll need most of a day for it," I said. "Maybe two. Ten years is ten years."

"Two days." She wrote it on the clipboard. As if scheduling grief were the same as solving it. Maybe, in her ledger, it was. "Thursday, then. Come early. Before the movers come for the east parlor things."

"Movers."

"The buyer wants the property clear by month's end." She said it briskly, and then, catching something in my face—whatever my face was doing, I've never had full command of it—she added, softer, "It's just furniture, Orsen."

Just furniture. Just a wardrobe that never opens. Just a piano that hasn't spoken in ten years. Just a house emptied of its voices one Thursday at a time, pitched lower and lower, settling into a silence so complete that pretty soon the only person who'd remember what the house sounded like when it was whole would be the man they paid to keep one small part of it honest.

"Just furniture," I agreed, and struck the fork against my knee, and went back to work.

* * *

I finished the parlor Pleyel at dusk. Played it through—Chopin, something safe, something that wouldn't raise ghosts the way Marguerite's repertoire would have—and the piano answered beautifully, the F holding its temper, the tired felts still blooming their half-second too long. I'd fix those next visit. There would be a next visit. There had to be. The alternative was a world in which the Vasseur house went silent entirely, and I found I couldn't afford that world. Not yet. Possibly not ever.

Packing up, coiling the cord, latching the case—I lingered. Call it professionalism. Call it the thing I actually did in that house, which was belong to it in a way no one had invited and no one had forbidden, a belonging conducted entirely in frequencies, invisible, deniable, safe.

On my way out I passed the east wall one last time. The radiator ticked its countdown. The wardrobe stood its watch. And beneath the floor, faint but unmistakable to anyone who knew how to listen, the house held a note it had been holding for ten years—a low, strained, unresolved tone, the sound of something waiting to be found.

I let myself out. The gravel took my tires. Behind me the windows went dark one by one, and the house, emptier than furnished, kept its pitch.
