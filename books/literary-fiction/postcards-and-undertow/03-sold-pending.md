## 3. Sold, Pending

The signs went up on a Tuesday, which struck me as wrong. Houses like this one should be sold on Sundays—days of obligation, of half-mourning, when the family can tell itself the transaction is somehow devotional. Tuesday is a day for dentists and oil changes. But the estate agent was a Tuesday sort of man, all cufflinks and brisk handshakes, and by noon there were two of his signs leaning against the gateposts like hired mourners who'd arrived early and didn't know the deceased.

I saw them from the road before I saw anything else. SOLD, PENDING, in letters the size of grief itself, with a smaller line beneath: *Do not remove by order of Harrow & Finch Properties.* As if anyone in this town had ever removed anything from that property. The Vasseurs' whole history was built on not removing things.

My van knew the way up the drive without my help. Ten years of Tuesdays—or Thursdays, or whenever Céleste telephoned in that voice like warm honey poured over an instruction—had worn a groove into both of us. The gravel still did its little applause under the tires. The house still stood at the top of it doing its impression of a grand old lady pretending not to be listening.

But something was off. I noticed it before I'd even cut the engine, the way you notice a wrong note held too long in a hymn. The house sounded *emptier than furnished*. That's a real thing, by the way—not a poet's invention. A furnished room has a certain density to its silence. Upholstery drinks sound. Curtains soften it. Books on shelves break it into small pieces and scatter them like bread for birds. An empty room returns everything you give it, flat and immediate, like a mirror.

This house was giving back too much.

* * *

Odile met me at the door, which was new. For ten years the door had been answered by Marta the housekeeper, then by nobody, then by whatever draft happened to be moving through the hall. Odile answering doors was a sign of the times.

"Orsen." She said my name the way she said everything now—efficiently, with a small nod attached, like a signature on a document. "You got my message."

"I got your message."

"One last tuning. The buyer wants the piano assessed separately—it's apparently worth something." She stepped back to let me in, and I carried my case across a threshold I'd crossed perhaps two hundred times, and this time the house flinched under my feet. Or I flinched. Hard to say where one of us ended.

The hall was a war zone of cardboard. Not chaos—Odile doesn't permit chaos—but the orderly devastation of a woman who has turned bereavement into logistics. Boxes labeled in her narrow handwriting: HALL. BOOKS—KEEP. BOOKS—SELL. CHINA (MOTHER'S—DO NOT TOUCH UNTIL I SAY). There was even a box labeled MISC, which in Odile's world was less a category than a confession of failure, and I noticed she'd written it small, almost apologetically, as if hoping no one would see it.

"How's your mother?" I asked, because it seemed like the thing to ask.

"Packing," Odile said. "Which for her means supervising me packing while telling me I'm doing it wrong." A pause, and then, with the ghost of the old dry humor that used to make Sunday lunches bearable: "She's arranged the grief very neatly, though. You should see the linen closet."

I laughed, because she wanted me to, and because ten years ago I would have laughed anyway. That's the thing about being the piano tuner in a family like this—you become furniture that occasionally makes jokes. Furniture is allowed a certain license. Furniture also hears everything, but nobody minds, because furniture isn't real.

"The parlor's clear," Odile said. "Except the piano, obviously, and—" she gestured vaguely toward the corner where the great walnut wardrobe stood, draped now in a dust sheet like something in mourning of itself, "—that thing won't fit through the door. Buyer says leave it. Says it's 'period.' Everything else goes."

"Everything else," I repeated.

"Everything else," she confirmed, and there was something underneath the words, some sediment of feeling that had settled out of her voice over the years the way silt settles in a harbor. Odile Vasseur at thirty-four was a different creature from the girl who used to practice violin badly in the mornings while I tuned downstairs and pretended not to wince. This one had a ledger behind her eyes. I could practically hear the columns adding themselves up as she looked at me: *piano tuner, forty-one years old, ten years' service, reliable, unremarkable, safe.*

"I'll need about three hours," I said.

"Take four. The agent comes Friday." She turned to go, then stopped, and for a moment something moved behind her face like a fish under ice. "Orsen. Do you remember—" She stopped again. Recalibrated. "Never mind. Three hours is fine."

I have thought about that unfinished sentence more times than I'd like to admit. *Do you remember.* Do you remember what, Odile? The summer? The music? Your sister? The night the sea came into our lives and rearranged the furniture?

Furniture. Everything in this family comes back to furniture eventually.

* * *

The parlor piano was a Bechstein, 1908, and it had been complaining of a cold for three weeks. I could hear it from the doorway—the F above middle C had gone sour, and the whole treble had that thin, shivering quality of an instrument left alone in a damp house through a dying autumn. Pianos are honest that way. They record everything. Humidity, neglect, argument, absence—all of it gets pressed into the wood and the felt, and a tuner with his ear trained can read a household's history off the strings like tree rings.

This piano's rings told a story I already knew by heart. The bass end still carried the ghost of Édouard's heavy hands from the years when he played after dinner, before the stroke slowed him to humming. The middle register held decades of Marguerite—I could feel her in the wear patterns of the hammers, the particular way she'd leaned into the fourth octave, always slightly too much weight on the left side, always slightly too much heart. And the top notes were nearly virgin territory, untouched, because nobody in that family ever went that high except her, near the end, reaching for sounds that hadn't been invented yet.

I opened the case and began.

There's a trance state that comes over me when I work. The tuning fork against my knee, the felt strip laid across the strings, the mutes going in like small gray headstones row by row. The world narrows to intervals. Perfect fifths, tempered just so. The beat frequencies between strings slowing, slowing, until they merge into a single clean tone—and each time one does, it's a tiny resurrection. A dead note brought back to life. I've spent twenty-five years raising the dead two cents at a time, and I've never once gotten tired of it, though I've gotten tired of plenty of other things.

Outside, the packing continued. I could hear Odile directing the movers in her clipped way—*no, the other end, lift with your knees, that box is books not bricks*—and beneath her voice, the house itself was talking. Old houses talk constantly; you just have to know the language. The radiators were filing their usual grievances, a knocking complaint in the west wall that meant air in the pipes, which meant the boiler was failing, which meant money, which explained a great deal about the sale. The floorboards in the hall creaked their familiar sentence—*someone is walking past the sealed room, someone is walking past the sealed room*—and above it all, faint but persistent, the sea kept up its long gray argument with the shore.

And then, somewhere between the third octave and the fourth, I heard the wrongness.

Not in the piano. Behind me.

It's difficult to describe how a wall can sound wrong, but walls have voices the same as everything else. Plaster on lath has a certain thickness to its silence—a solid, dumb, honest quiet. When I struck the A and let it ring, the room returned the tone to me, and the room's return had a flaw in it. A hollowness. Somewhere behind that draped wardrobe, the wall was answering in two voices—one solid, one empty—and the empty one had a resonance to it, a cavity-note, the sound of a space that shouldn't be there.

I stopped tuning. Sat very still. Struck the A again.

There it was. Faint. Like hearing your own name called from another room—too soft to be sure, too shaped to ignore.

I told myself it was settling. Old houses settle; that's what they do instead of crying. I told myself it was a pipe, a chase, a cavity left by some Victorian builder who'd had other things on his mind that day. I tuned the next string. My hands were steady. My hands are always steady; it's the rest of me that shakes.

But the sound stayed in my ear the way a splinter stays in a thumb, working deeper every time I touched the keys, and by the time I reached the treble I wasn't really tuning anymore. I was listening past the piano, past the movers' footsteps overhead, past the sea, into that hollow behind the wardrobe, and the hollow was listening back.

Dread is a strange animal. It arrives ahead of its own explanation, the way thunder arrives ahead of the storm's worst weather, and you stand there in the green light knowing something is coming without knowing what. That's what I felt, sitting at the Bechstein with my tuning hammer idle in my lap: the dread first, the discovery still to come, and between them a corridor of seconds in which I could still have packed up my tools, invoiced Odile, driven away, and lived the rest of my life as furniture.

I want it noted, for the record, that I considered it.

Then I set down the hammer, wiped my hands on my trousers, and crossed the parlor floor—which creaked its warning, *someone is walking toward the wardrobe, someone is walking toward the wardrobe*—and pulled the dust sheet off in a single motion, like a magician revealing the trick.

The wardrobe loomed, walnut and dark, taller than me by a head. It stood six inches off the wall—just enough gap for a man to sidle into sideways, which I did, with my cheek nearly against the cool varnished wood and my breath fogging it faintly. Behind me the parlor went on existing. In front of me, the wall.

I put my palm flat against the plaster.

Cold. Then, beneath the cold, when I pressed—nothing. A give. A hollowness the size of a shoebox, exactly where the room's echo had said it would be, hidden behind the wardrobe where only a piano tuner's ear—or a person who had spent ten years learning to listen to this house instead of speaking in it—could ever have found it.

Someone had built a false patch of wall. Someone had hidden something inside my house—inside *this* house, the house whose silences I knew better than my own heartbeat—and the family was selling it, wardrobe and all, to a stranger who wanted everything gone by month's end.

Somewhere upstairs, Odile's voice rose and fell, tallying. The sea argued on. And my hand, flat against that thin lying skin of plaster, began—for the first time in ten years—to shake.
