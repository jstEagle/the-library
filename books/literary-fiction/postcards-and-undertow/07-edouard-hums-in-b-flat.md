## 7. Édouard Hums in B Flat

Édouard Vasseur lived now in the garden flat of a care residence that smelled of lavender and institutional optimism. The brochure in the lobby called it "a community." The radiator in the corridor called it something else—a low, arrhythmic knocking, like a man tapping on the inside of a coffin lid to ask for more time.

I had been dreading this visit for three days, which is its own kind of appointment-keeping. I'd told myself I was coming because Édouard's piano needed attention—the upright in his room had gone sour at the top of the keyboard, Odile said, and nobody else would notice or care. That was true as far as it went. But I have learned over ten years that the reasons I give myself are usually the tuning pegs holding up some other string entirely.

The truth was simpler and worse: after finding those letters, after reading the first three by lamplight with my hands not entirely steady, I needed to hear Marguerite's music from a living mouth. I needed to know whether what she'd written on paper matched what her father still carried in his broken head. If it did, then the letters were real—not a dead girl's fantasy, not some posthumous performance—and I would have to decide what to do with that.

If it didn't match, I could go back to being a piano tuner. There are worse lives. I've nearly had one.

* * *

The nurse let me in with the practiced warmth of someone who has learned that families visit less often than they intend to. "He's having a good morning," she said, which I understood to be a clinical term, like *stable* or *responsive*. Good mornings were rationed here, dispensed like the little paper cups of pills.

Édouard sat in an armchair angled toward the window, where the sea did its grey arithmetic against the seawall. He had shrunk since I'd last seen him—not just thinner but *condensed*, the way a house settles, everything still there but closer together. His left hand lay in his lap like a sleeping bird. His right hand, though. His right hand was moving.

Not playing. Not quite. The fingers rose and fell against the arm of the chair in small patient arcs, pressing keys that weren't there, and I recognized the gesture before I recognized the music because I'd spent a decade watching hands do it—pianists who can't sleep, pianists waiting in stations, pianists at funerals. The body keeps the instrument even when everything else is repossessed.

"Orsen," he said, without turning around.

I hadn't made a sound. The floorboards under me were the quiet kind; I know them all in that building. But Édouard had always heard people the way I heard houses—not arriving, but *arrived*, a change in the pressure of a room.

"Monsieur Vasseur."

"You're standing there deciding whether to call me Édouard." He turned his head with the slow deliberation of a lighthouse. "You've been deciding for ten years. Take your time. It's the only thing we have plenty of here."

The stroke had taken most of his speech's right side—words came out levelled, slightly flattened, a landscape after a flood—but it had apparently taken nothing from his aim.

"I brought tools," I said, lifting the case like a peace offering.

"For the piano." He looked at the upright against the wall, a modest Zimmermann that had once lived in the Vasseur parlor until Céleste decided the parlor should hold only furniture that couldn't make accusations. "It hasn't been played since they moved me here. You'll find it's gone strange. Pianos go strange when no one argues with them."

I opened the case anyway. It gave me somewhere to put my hands.

* * *

I tuned while he watched, which is a particular intimacy. Most clients leave the room or bury themselves in a phone or a newspaper, granting the tuner the fiction of privacy. Édouard watched every movement of my arms with the concentration of a man memorizing a route home.

"The A is lying," he said at one point.

"It's telling the truth slowly," I said. "Old strings do that. They get to the pitch eventually, but they want you to wait for it."

"Like everyone in my family."

I let that sit. There was a time—I could date it almost to the season—when a remark like that would have sent me into my professional crouch, all deflection and felt buttons. But the letters had changed the acoustics between us. I kept working, and he kept watching, and the piano came back to itself note by note, and neither of us said anything true for eleven minutes, which for the Vasseur family constituted open conversation.

When I finished, I played a scale, top to bottom, and the Zimmermann answered cleanly, gratefully, like a dog remembering its name.

"There," I said. "She'll hold."

"She." He smiled with half his face; the other half held still, which made the smile look earned rather than given. "You always named them. Céleste hated that. She said instruments don't have souls, they have maintenance schedules."

"Céleste is entitled to her theory."

"And you're entitled to yours." He shifted in the chair, and his right hand went back to the armrest, to those small arcs, those absent keys. "Play something. Anything. My hands remember more than I do these days, and it's humiliating to be outlived by your own fingers."

So I played. I don't perform—I tune, which is a different profession wearing similar clothes—but alone with a man who was mostly listening with his hand, I could risk a Chopin mazurka I half knew, and then something of Satie's, and then, because the room was warm and the sea outside had gone quiet and I am weak in ways I will be paying for shortly, I improvised.

That was the mistake. Or the door. Depending on how you count.

Because improvising, my left hand wandered into B flat, and B flat has a gravity in that family's history—it was Marguerite's key, the key she thought in, the key of the sonata she never finished—and without deciding to, I found myself sketching the opening phrase of it. Four bars. Maybe five. Just the shape of it, the way you might hum a dead friend's laugh without meaning to.

The sound Édouard made stopped my hands.

It wasn't a word. It was lower than words, a hum that started in his chest and climbed out through the flattened side of his mouth, and it found the melody exactly where mine had left off and carried it forward—into the second phrase, the one Marguerite had never written down, the one that existed nowhere except in that room on a storm-night ten years ago when she played it for an audience of one piano tuner and asked him a question he still hadn't answered.

He hummed it perfectly. Every interval. The little hesitation before the turn, the way she used to lean into the dissonance like leaning into wind.

My ears did what they always do, which is betray me with precision. And what they reported was this: the phrase in letter nine—the one I had read four times the night before, the one she described as *"the part Papa whistles without knowing, the part that proves he listened even when he pretended the music was wallpaper"*—that phrase and the one coming out of Édouard now were the same phrase, note for note, hesitation for hesitation.

She had written it down. In a letter addressed to no one. To *the one who heard me.*

And he had kept it. In a head the stroke had gutted like a flooded house, through two years of losing nouns and faces and the names of his own grandchildren—he had kept eight bars of his drowned daughter's unfinished sonata the way other men keep a photograph in a wallet.

* * *

I must have made some sound of my own, because the humming stopped and Édouard looked at me, and for a moment—one long, terrible, luminous moment—I saw the man he had been before the flood: sharp, dry, watchful, the father who communicated primarily through silence and secondarily through raising one eyebrow at Céleste's arrangements.

"You know it," he said.

"It's familiar," I managed.

"No." He shook his head, slow, immovable. "Familiar is what the nurse is when she comes in. Familiar is the smell of Tuesday. You *know* it. You know where the phrase goes after the turn. Your face went there just now—to the place after the turn—and there is no place after the turn, because she never finished it. She died before the turn resolved." His right hand pressed down on the armrest, hard, all five fingers, a chord on wood. "Unless you've heard it resolve."

The sea ticked against the seawall. Somewhere down the corridor a trolley rattled, and a woman laughed at something a visitor said, and the radiator knocked its soft coffin-lid knock, and I stood in the middle of all that ordinary sound knowing that whatever I said next would either close a door I'd waited ten years to open or blow the whole wall out.

"Marguerite played it for me once," I said.

There. Said. Badly, quietly, without ceremony—the way a first crack appears in plaster, hairline, deniable.

Édouard didn't move. Then his eyes filled, not dramatically, not the way grief looks in films, but the way water rises in a lock—level, unstoppable, hydraulic. "The storm night," he said. "October. She came home with wet cuffs and wouldn't say where she'd been, and for a week afterward she hummed that phrase like it was a secret she was keeping from herself." He looked at his right hand, the one that remembered. "I used to listen outside the music room door. She'd stop if she heard the floor. Everyone thinks I never listened. Céleste arranged it so that listening looked like intruding, and intruding looked like betrayal, and after a while none of us went near any doors at all."

"Monsieur Vasseur—"

"Édouard." The correction came fast, fierce, from the undamaged side of him. "Say it. You've earned the name. Ten years of tuning my pianos and holding my family's silences like a man carrying a tray through an earthquake—you've earned the name, Orsen. Use it."

No one had called me anything but Delacroix or *the tuner* in that family's presence for a decade. Hearing my own first name in Édouard's flattened voice did something I wasn't prepared for. It un-levelled me. I sat down on the piano bench because my legs filed a complaint.

"Édouard," I said.

"Yes." He closed his eyes briefly, like a man tasting something he'd been denied on doctor's orders. "Now. Tell me about the storm night. Tell me what she played you, and tell me why, when she came home that night, she looked like a girl who had finally been *heard*—and tell me why, in ten years, no one in my family has ever mentioned that she was here at all that autumn, that she was writing things, that she was *becoming* someone—" His voice caught on the flattening, snagged, pushed through. "Tell me why I had to lose most of my mind before anyone would stand in a room with me and say her name out loud."

Because your wife built a museum of silence and hired me as its caretaker. Because the truth has a name attached to it that would burn this family down to its foundations. Because I was a coward with excellent hearing.

What I said was: "She played me the sonata. What there was of it. And she asked me something, and I didn't answer, and then the sea—" My throat closed around the rest. Some sentences are load-bearing; remove them and the whole structure of your composure comes down.

Édouard watched me fail with a patience that was worse than anger. Then he said, very quietly, "She asked you to tell the truth about something. Didn't she."

It wasn't a question. It was a father's ear, tuned across years of listening at doors, catching the one frequency I'd tried to damp.

"How could you possibly—"

"Because she asked me too." His right hand lifted from the armrest, trembled, landed again. "Three days before. She sat where you're sitting and she said, *Papa, if I tell you something, will you do the right thing with it?* And I said yes, of course, and she looked at me for a long time and then she said, *No. You'd arrange it. You'd put it in a drawer.* And she was right." The water rose in his eyes again. "I've had ten years to wonder what was in that drawer. And now you walk in carrying the same expression she had, and I think—I think you know what's in it."

I thought of the tin box behind the wardrobe. Forty-one envelopes. Three in my coat pocket at that very moment, six miles away, weighing approximately as much as a verdict.

"I need time," I said. It was the truest sentence available to me and it tasted like every other sentence I'd ever given this family: accurate, incomplete.

"We don't have time." For a moment the stroke receded entirely and Édouard Vasseur spoke with the full authority of the man who had once, I'd gathered, been formidable. "They're selling the house, Orsen. They're selling the house where my daughter wrote whatever she wrote, and packing her life into boxes marked *miscellaneous*, and signing papers in a lawyer's office with a buyer who wants everything *gone*. Time is the one thing this family has never had enough of and has always wasted beautifully." He leaned forward, and his good hand caught my wrist—grip surprisingly strong, a pianist's grip, fingers that had once held octaves. "Whatever she gave you. Whatever you're carrying. Don't let them pack it in a box marked miscellaneous."

I looked at his hand on my wrist. I thought about lying. I have a professional relationship with lying—we're friendly, we nod at each other across rooms—but looking at that ruined, faithful face, I found I couldn't produce one.

"I found something," I said. "Behind the wardrobe in the music room. Letters. Hers. Forty-one of them."

His grip tightened. Outside, the sea kept its own counsel, and the radiator knocked, and Édouard Vasseur began, very softly, to cry—not the flattened half-cry the stroke permitted, but something older, whole-bodied, the sound of a man whose drawer had finally been opened.

"Read them," he whispered. "All of them. And then, Orsen—whatever they say—"

But he never finished the instruction. The door opened behind us, and the nurse's cheerful voice announced his afternoon medication, and the moment folded itself away like sheet music—and I sat there on the bench of a tuned piano, holding three letters in my coat pocket and one unfinished sentence in the air, understanding that I had just run out of almosts.
