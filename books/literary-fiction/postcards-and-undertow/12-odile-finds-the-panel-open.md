## 12. Odile Finds the Panel Open

I want to say, for the record, that I was not snooping.

There is a difference between snooping and cataloguing, and the difference is a spreadsheet. Snooping is what my mother does when she opens a drawer looking for scissors and comes out forty minutes later holding someone's old love letters and a grudge. Cataloguing is what I do, which is opening every drawer in this house on purpose, with a clipboard, because a buyer's surveyor is coming Thursday and I intend to hand him an inventory so complete he'll weep into his laser measure.

Item one hundred and six: mahogany wardrobe, master bedroom, circa 1890s, mirror foxed at lower left corner, one door hinge loose, contents to be assessed separately.

Except the wardrobe had moved.

Not far. Three inches, maybe four — enough that the baseboard scuff behind it showed, enough that the panel of the wall it had been hiding sat proud of its frame like a tooth that hadn't finished coming out. And the panel itself was ajar. Not open. Ajar. The way a mouth goes before it decides whether to speak.

I stood there with my clipboard against my chest the way you'd hold a shield, and I did the arithmetic that Odile Vasseur does instead of crying, which is faster and cheaper.

The wardrobe weighs as much as a dead horse. Nobody moves it by accident. Nobody moves it at all unless they're looking for something, or hiding something, or both. And of everyone who has walked through this house in the last month — the agent with her voice like a radio jingle, the buyer's people with their little clicky devices, the movers who broke the third-floor sash window and lied about it with such fluency I almost applauded — only one person has a reason to be near that wall, a decade of reasons, and a set of hands that knows exactly how much noise a house can absorb.

Orsen Delacroix.

I put down the clipboard. That was the strange part, how calm my hands were. I'd expected, if I ever found evidence of something rotten in this family, to feel vindicated. Instead I felt the way you feel when the doctor confirms what you already knew: relief first, then the bill.

The box was gone. I could tell from the dust. There was a rectangle of clean wood inside the cavity, sharp-edged, the size of a biscuit tin, sitting in ten years of grey like a photograph left face-down on a table. Whatever had been there had been there a long time, and now it wasn't, and the only person who tuned pianos in this house had found it before I did.

Ten years. Let me tell you about ten years, since apparently we're doing inventory.

* * *

When Marguerite died, I was twenty-six and working in Lyon at a firm where I learned that grief is a scheduling problem. I came home for the funeral and stayed for the wreckage, and somewhere in the second year I stopped being Édouard and Céleste's daughter and became the family's operating system. Céleste arranged; I executed. She decided which rooms stayed shut; I made sure they stayed shut. She said "we don't discuss it"; I enforced the policy without ever having been given the title.

And through all of it, twice a year, sometimes four times, came Orsen Delacroix with his black case and his soft shoes and his way of standing in a room like a man listening to something underneath the room.

Here is what nobody in my family understands about me: I am not cold. I am a ledger. Every person I love is a column, and every debt between us gets entered, dated, and carried forward. It's not that I don't forgive. It's that I don't forget to *bill*.

And Orsen Delacroix has been running a tab for ten years.

Entry one: the night after the funeral, he came to tune the parlor piano — unasked, unpaid — and when I found him there at eleven at night, his hands on the keys not playing but pressing, one note at a time, like a man taking a pulse, he looked at me and said, "She kept it in tune herself, you know. Between visits." As if that were comfort. As if that weren't a knife wrapped in a dishcloth.

Entry two: every year after, some small observation delivered at the door, hat already on. "The boathouse roof will go this winter." "Your mother keeps the music room at a different temperature than the rest of the house." "Marguerite would have hated these radiators." Always her name, dropped into conversation like a stone into a well we'd all agreed to cap. My mother built a beautiful silence around my sister's death — a silence with crown molding, a silence you could live in — and Orsen knocked on it twice a year and called it maintenance.

Entry three, and this is the one I've never entered anywhere aloud: he was here. The week she died. He always claimed it was a scheduled tuning, and maybe it was, but he was in this house in the last weeks of her life, hearing things, seeing things, being the person she talked to while the rest of us were busy being a family in the official sense. When the police asked their questions, we answered. What did Orsen answer? Did anyone ask? No. Because he was the piano man. He was furniture with opinions. He witnessed everything and reported nothing, and then he came back every spring and autumn and let us pour him coffee and pay him for the privilege of his silence.

You can see why the ledger runs long.

So when I stood in front of that open panel with the dust-shadow of a missing box printed on the wall, I didn't think *burglar*. Burglars don't move wardrobes back into place. Burglars don't leave the hinge oiled — and it was oiled, I noticed, the hinge, someone had oiled it recently, which meant this wasn't the first visit. I thought: *of course*. Of course it's him. Ten years of almost-saying-things, and finally he's taken something.

The question — and I am very good at questions, it's most of what I do — was what.

* * *

He came on Tuesday, as scheduled. Final tuning before clearance. I heard his car in the lane at nine sharp, heard him sit in it for a full two minutes before getting out — he does that, he always does that, sits in the car like a diver checking his gauges — and I met him at the door with tea already made, because ambushes go better with civility. My mother taught me that. Everything goes better with civility, including war.

"Odile." He said it the way he says everything, quietly, like the word might wake someone.

"Orsen. Parlor piano. Then the upright in the music room, if you have the stomach for it."

"I always have the stomach for it."

We went through the hall. I watched him pass the stairs. I watched him not look at the stairs, which is its own kind of looking. In the parlor he opened the case and began, and I sat in the chair by the window with my tea and my clipboard and let him work, because there is no point confronting a man mid-aria. You wait for the movement to end.

He tuned the way he always tunes: fastidious, unhurried, humming under his breath in a register just below hearing. And I sat there studying the side of his face and thinking about the rectangle of clean wood, and about my sister, and about how the two of them had shared something in this house that none of the rest of us had been invited into, and how the invitation had apparently never existed, how you can't resent not being invited to something that was never offered, and yet.

And yet.

"Orsen," I said, during the silence between movements, the engineered silence, the one he builds so the piano can settle. "Behind the wardrobe in the master bedroom. There's a cavity in the wall."

His hands stopped. Not dramatically. They simply arrived at rest, the way water arrives at level. If I hadn't spent ten years reading this man I'd have missed it entirely.

"Old houses have those," he said. "Services chases. Pipe runs."

"Mm. This one had a box in it."

The clock on the mantel did its work for him. Twenty seconds, maybe. Long enough for me to count three debts and part of a fourth.

"Did it," he said.

"It's gone now."

"Is it." He turned on the bench then, and looked at me, and I'll give him this: he didn't perform confusion. Most people, caught, would reach for the wide eyes, the furrowed brow, the whole amateur theatre of innocence. Orsen just looked at me with his tired grey face and let the silence do what silences do in his hands, which is grow until you're the one who wants to fill it.

I didn't fill it. I'm my mother's daughter, whatever else that means.

"What was in the box, Odile?" he asked finally.

That was good. I'll admit it was good. Turn the interrogation around, make the questioner the questioned. But I've sat across from lawyers and buyers' agents and one truly memorable insurance investigator, and I know a deflection when it's handed to me gift-wrapped.

"You tell me," I said. "You're the one who found it."

The pause again. Shorter this time. Then, mildly, terribly mildly: "Why would you think I found it?"

"Because the hinge was oiled. Because the wardrobe sits four inches off its mark and you're the only person in this house who notices four inches — you've corrected me on less. Because the dust shadow is fresh. And because—" I put down my cup, deliberately, so the saucer clicked, punctuation, "—because you are the only person alive who has ever wanted anything from inside our walls."

Something moved through his face then. Not guilt. Something older and more tired, the look of a man who has been waiting ten years for a knock and is almost disappointed it took this shape.

"Odile," he said, "what do you think happened to your sister?"

And there it was. Ten years of tab, presented at once, no itemization. I felt my ears go hot, which they do when I'm furious, a childish inheritance I've never managed to audit out of myself.

"Don't," I said. "Don't you dare turn this into one of your door-hat observations. You opened a wall in a dying woman's house and took something that belonged to her, and you will tell me what it was, or I will call the police, and unlike ten years ago, Orsen, the police in this town still take my calls."

"The police." He said it softly. Almost sadly. "Yes. They took them before, too, didn't they. Your mother's especially."

The room tilted — not visibly, nothing so useful as that, but internally, the way a floor tilts when you realize the joists are wrong. He wasn't deflecting. He was aiming. At what, I couldn't see yet, but the trajectory ran straight through my mother and I felt it pass close enough to raise the hair on my arms.

"My mother," I said carefully, "has done nothing but hold this family together since Marguerite died."

"Yes," he said. "That's exactly what she's done. Held it together. Like a hand over a wound." He stood up from the piano bench, slowly, joints announcing themselves. "Ask her what she burned, Odile. Ask her what was in the grate the spring after the funeral, when she told the neighbors the chimney drew badly. Ask her who wrote the account the police accepted, and why it needed writing at all, if your sister simply swam alone."

"Stop."

"She kept letters once. Did you know that? A bundle of them. Your father told me—"

"*Stop.*"

I heard my own voice crack off the high windows and come back at me, and I hated it, and I hated him for causing it, and beneath the hatred — I'll be honest, since we're keeping books — beneath it something else was moving, something with teeth, gnawing at a memory I've never let myself examine: my mother in the garden that April, sleeves rolled, ash on her knuckles, telling me the chimney flue had cracked and she'd handled it. Telling me not to come inside. My mother, who arranges everything. Who arranged the funeral in forty-eight hours flat. Who wrote the statement to the police herself, in her own hand, because — she said — the children were too distraught.

I was too distraught. Wasn't I. I remember being distraught. I remember it the way you remember a photograph of a thing rather than the thing.

Orsen was watching me watch the memory, and I swear to God he had the decency to look sorry about it.

"The box," I said, when I could trust my voice. "What was in the box."

"Letters," he said. "Forty-one of them. In her hand."

The clipboard slid off my knee. I caught it without looking, some clerk's reflex deep in the spine, and held it against me like a plank in water.

"To whom?" I asked. And then, because I already knew, because some part of me had known since entry one, since the pulse-taking at the piano the night after the funeral: "Who were they to?"

He picked up his tuning hammer from the top of the piano. Set it down again. Looked at the keys as if asking their permission.

"They were addressed," he said, "'to the one who heard me.'"

The clock ticked. Somewhere upstairs a radiator knocked twice and went quiet, the way this house has always gone quiet at exactly the wrong moments, as if it too were keeping a ledger and choosing what not to enter.

"And you took them," I said. "From her house. From her wall. Without telling anyone."

"I resealed the wall."

"Oh, well then. *Martyrdom.*"

"Odile." He said my name again, and this time there was no softness in it at all, and I understood suddenly that the man I had spent ten years billing as a coward, a bystander, a furniture-with-opinions, had been carrying something the whole time that made my ledger look like a shopping list. "Before you decide what I am, ask yourself why a girl with a living family — a mother, a father, a sister — writes forty-one letters and hides them in a wall addressed to no one in it."

"That's not an answer."

"No," he agreed. "It's a question. I've had ten years of mine. Now you have yours."

He closed the piano case. Latched it. Picked up his coat, and at the door he stopped, and for one moment I thought — hoped — feared — he would say it, whatever *it* was, the whole of it, right there in the hall among the packed crates.

Instead he said: "Thursday, when the surveyor comes, check the inventory against the music room. Count the sheet music. Count what's missing from the sonata."

Then he was gone, and his car sat in the lane for two full minutes before it started, and I stood in the parlor with my clipboard and my cold tea and the beginning of a number in my head that I did not want to finish calculating.

Because I remembered the sonata. Everyone remembers the sonata; it was the only piece she never finished, the one Édouard hums now in his broken way, the one Céleste keeps shut in the music room along with everything else. And I remembered — I was remembering it even as I climbed the stairs, even as I stood outside the sealed room with my hand on the handle I hadn't touched in years — I remembered that the last time I saw the manuscript pages, there had been more of them.

I opened the door. Dust, sheeted furniture, the upright piano under its shroud like a body under a sheet.

And on the music desk, where a stack of manuscript should have been, a single page lay squared to the edge, placed there recently, deliberately — placed there, I understood with a cold spreading certainty, by a man with soft shoes who notices four inches.

At the top of the page, in my sister's handwriting, a date. The date of the week she died.

And below it, the first line of a letter I had never seen:

*I saw what you did at the boathouse, Maman.*
