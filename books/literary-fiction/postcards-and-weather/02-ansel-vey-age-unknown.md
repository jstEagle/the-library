## 2. Ansel Vey, Age Unknown

The annex had a smell the main building had lost. That was the first thing Rosalind noticed, back when he was still learning the building's geography as a stranger—the faint mineral tang of old plaster and the ghost of rosin baked into the walls, something the renovated concert halls had been scrubbed of years ago in favor of whatever neutral scent institutions buy in bulk. The rehearsal annex sat behind the east wing like a shed behind a manor house, connected by a corridor nobody repainted, and it held the practice rooms that had been deemed too small, too out-of-date, too honest for the conservatory's public face. Room 4 had a piano with one dead key, an E above middle C that had gone silent sometime in the previous decade and never been mourned. Rosalind had taken to ending his rounds there, sitting on the floor with his back against the radiator, listening to the building settle.

He had memorized the acoustics the way other men memorized racing forms or the backs of playing cards. The stairwell by the loading door turned a footstep into a drumroll. The corridor outside the faculty offices had a standing wave at shoulder height, so that voices seemed to travel slightly above the heads of the people speaking them, a procession of sound always leaving the room before the speakers did. The annex was the opposite—dead, padded, forgiving. Sound went in and did not come out. It was the kind of room where you could say something true and the walls would keep it.

He had not, in seven years, said anything true in it. But he liked knowing he could.

It was 11:40 on a Thursday night, the hour when his mop cart's wheels squeaked in a particular way on the annex tile that he had never managed to fix and had, in a private and unexamined way, come to enjoy. He pushed the cart through the corridor, past the notice board with its curling flyers for student recitals, past the window where October was pressing its face against the glass, and stopped.

The door to Room 4 was open. He always closed it. It was the last thing he did every night, a small liturgy: lights, cart, door. The door was open now, and from inside came a sound he could not immediately classify, and classification was, in Rosalind's private taxonomy of terrors, the thing he needed most.

It was humming. But not the humming of a man passing time. It had architecture. It rose, held, turned a corner he could not have predicted, and descended by a route that made his trained ear flinch—not because it was wrong, but because it refused to be right in any system he knew. The intervals were not major, not minor, not modal in any of the church modes he could name. They were intervals from some other mathematics entirely, a scale that had evolved in isolation, the way an island bird evolves a beak no mainland bird would recognize. It was beautiful the way weather is beautiful: indifferent to his opinion of it.

Rosalind stood in the corridor with his hand on the cart's handle and did not breathe.

The annex was locked at ten. He locked it himself. Which meant either the building had grown a door, or someone had a key, or someone had been inside since before ten, waiting through the hours in the dark of a practice room the way other people waited for trains.

He told himself it was a student. Students broke in all the time—well, not all the time, but often enough; the annex was where they came to cry or practice pieces they were ashamed of, and he had learned to mop around grief without commenting on it. He told himself this, and then he looked through the doorway.

An old man was asleep in the chair by the piano. Not slumped—*arranged*, the way a man arranges himself when sleep is a decision rather than an accident: coat folded over the armrest, hands loose in his lap, chin lifted as though listening even in sleep. He was small, the kind of small that happens to men who were once tall, folded down by decades into something dense and economical. His hair was white in the way of paper left in sun. His shoes were leather, old, mended at the heel with a care that suggested either poverty or principle, and in Rosalind's experience the two were often the same thing.

The humming went on. That was the impossible part. The man was asleep and the humming went on, low and continuous, threading through the dead air of Room 4 like water finding a channel. Rosalind had heard singers practice in their sleep—conservatories were full of people whose bodies would not stop working even when their minds surrendered—but this was not practice. There was no correction in it, no striving. It was the melody itself, unattended, the way a river runs when no one is watching it.

Rosalind's ear, that traitor, that ruined instrument, began unprompted to transcribe. He stood there in the doorway of his own quiet life and felt the notes arrange themselves in his head, felt his fingers move faintly against the mop handle, and hated himself with the easy fluency of long practice.

He must have made a sound. He did not think he did—the cart was still, his breath had stopped—but the old man's eyes opened. Not gradually. All at once, the way a bird's eyes open, as though sleep had been a posture rather than a state.

They looked at each other across the small dead room.

"You're the one who locks the door at ten," the old man said. His voice was rough, unpracticed in the way of voices used mostly to silence, but the consonants were exact, placed with a precision that Rosalind's ear catalogued before he could stop it: someone who had once spoken a great deal, aloud, in company, and had recently fallen out of the habit.

"I'm the janitor," Rosalind said. It was his sentence. He had said it so many times it had worn smooth as a river stone, and it slid out of him now without friction, without meaning, without him.

"Yes," the old man said. "You're the janitor." And then, mildly, the way a man remarks on the weather: "Hello, Roszie."

* * *

The world did not stop. That was the strange part, the part Rosalind would return to later, turning it over like a stone with something underneath it. The world continued exactly as it had been. The radiator ticked. The dead E key sat in its silence. October pressed at the window. Somewhere in the main building a furnace cycled on with a sound like a held note. Nothing stopped, because nothing had been asked to stop, and yet Rosalind felt the floor of his life tilt—not dramatically, not the way a building falls, but the way a boat tilts when a single passenger shifts their weight, so that every object in it slides a few inches and will never quite slide back.

*Roszie.* No one had called him that in seven years. No one had called him that in longer than seven years, because the person who invented it had stopped using it around the time Rosalind stopped being worth nicknames, and the person who inherited it had let it die of neglect like a houseplant in a room no one entered. It had been his grandmother's word, then his sister's, then—briefly, in the years when his hands still worked—his father's, used only in public, as a kind of ownership stamp. *Roszie played beautifully tonight.* *Roszie has a competition in March.* A name like a ribbon on a prize.

He had spent seven years becoming a man no one could call anything. *The janitor.* *Hey, you.* *Sir.* He had built the namelessness plank by plank, and it had held, it had held beautifully, it was the only structure he had ever finished—and now an old man with mended shoes had walked through it as though it were fog.

"Who told you that name?" Rosalind heard himself ask. His voice came out lower than he intended, rougher. The janitor's voice, which he wore like a coat, had slipped.

"No one told me." The old man unfolded himself from the chair with the unhurried economy of someone who had decided that rushing was for people with less time, not more. He stood, and he was taller standing than sitting, though not by much, and he looked at Rosalind the way Rosalind had seen conductors look at a section that was playing the right notes in the wrong spirit. "I listened. Names are all over you. You walk past the photographs in the east corridor every night at 11:15 and slow down at the one from the spring gala, 2016, and your breathing changes. You mop Room 9 last on Tuesdays because the acoustics there are best at that hour and you like to hear the wind ensemble through the wall, and you stand with your mop still for exactly as long as the oboe solo lasts. You hum when you think no one is here. You hum in a minor third below whatever the students were playing that afternoon, always below, never above, as if you're holding the floor under their music." He paused. "And you close the door of this room every night like you're tucking it in. So. Either you are a janitor who loves this building, or you are something else who is hiding in a janitor, and I am eighty-four years old and have no time for the first one."

The silence afterward was enormous. It filled Room 4 the way water fills a glass, completely, to the meniscus, trembling at the edge.

Rosalind's first coherent thought was not fear. It was a kind of horrified, vertiginous relief, and he recognized it the way you recognize a smell from childhood—sudden, total, unwelcome. *Someone has been watching me.* For seven years he had performed invisibility to empty houses. The mop cart, the schedule, the lowered eyes, the janitor's flat voice—all of it had been a recital given to no one, and there is no lonelier art than that. And now: an audience. One seat filled. An old man who had done him the one thing he could not forgive and could not live without, which was to pay attention.

"I don't know what you're talking about," he said, and heard how dead the sentence was, how it arrived already embalmed.

"No," the old man agreed pleasantly. "You wouldn't." He picked up his folded coat. Under it on the chair had been a notebook, cloth-bound, swollen with paperclips and loose pages, and he gathered it against his chest the way a man holds a bird he does not entirely trust himself not to crush. "I'll come back Thursday. You lock up at ten; I'll be here before. You don't have to speak to me. You don't even have to look at me. But you'll hear it, and you already heard it, and I watched your hands on that mop handle, Roszie, so we both know what your hands do when they hear something they've never heard before."

"What is it?" The question escaped him before he could weigh it, before he could remind himself that curiosity was a door he had nailed shut with his own hands. "The melody. What scale is it? It's not pentatonic, it's not any mode I—"

He stopped. Too late. The old man was smiling, and it was not a triumphant smile, which would have been bearable. It was a tired one, a smile like a lamp coming on in a window at the end of a long street, meant for someone else, lit anyway.

"Thursday," he said, and walked past Rosalind into the corridor, small and dense and certain, his mended heels clicking a rhythm Rosalind's ear transcribed before he could stop it. At the corner he paused, without turning. "Ansel Vey," he said, as if answering a question that had not been asked aloud. "You'll want the name for the notebook. You'll start the notebook tonight. I know because I did the same thing at your age, when I heard my first word that had no translation, and I didn't sleep, and I wrote everything down, and I have never once regretted it. Not even now. *Especially* not now."

Then he was gone, and the annex was quiet, and Rosalind stood in Room 4 with his hand still resting on the mop handle where his fingers had, without permission, without mercy, been playing the old man's melody against the wood—those impossible intervals, that island-bird scale, that water finding its channel.

* * *

He did not start the notebook. He wanted that on the record, whatever record existed for a life like his. He went home to the rented room above the dry cleaner's where he lived as R. Coldwell, tenant, quiet, pays on time, and he did not write anything down. He boiled water for tea he did not drink. He lay on the bed with his coat still on and stared at the ceiling, where a water stain had spent the autumn growing into the shape of a country no cartographer would claim.

But his hands would not stop. He held them up against the ceiling light and watched them move—the fingering of a scale that did not exist, that he had heard exactly once, that his traitor body was already building a house for. He had spent seven years teaching his hands to be janitors' hands. They had learned. They were good students. And it had taken one old man, asleep, humming, eleven minutes of it, to undo the whole curriculum.

*Roszie.* He said it to himself in the dark, testing it, the way you test a floorboard you suspect has rotted. It held his weight. That was the terrible thing. It held.

He thought: *someone has been watching me for seven years.* He thought: *he knew about the photograph, about the oboe solo, about the door.* He thought: *I am not invisible. I never was. I was only unwatched by the people I was hiding from, and the watching I was actually receiving was worse, was better, was—*

He did not finish the thought. He slept, eventually, the way you fall rather than the way you arrive, and he dreamed of a melody in no scale he knew, and in the dream he could play it, and in the dream no one in the third row was watching, and the dream was so kind that he woke from it angry.

Thursday came the way Thursdays do, without permission. At 9:40 he unlocked the annex, swept the corridor, checked Room 9's window latch, and stood for a long moment in front of Room 4 with his hand on the light switch. The room was empty. The chair sat by the piano with the particular innocence of furniture that has never held anything.

He locked the door at ten. He stood in the corridor afterward, in the dark, listening to his own breathing change.

At 11:15 he passed the photograph from the spring gala, 2016, and slowed down, because he always slowed down, because a man cannot unlearn the route to his own wound. In the photograph he was twenty-four and radiant in the way that only the doomed are radiant, hands lifted over the keys, and in the third row a face he had spent seven years not looking at was looking at him with an expression the program notes had called *pride* and the family had called *pride* and Rosalind had privately, in the seven years since, called nothing at all, because it had no name in any language he spoke.

Perhaps, he thought, in some language it had a name. Perhaps there was a language somewhere with a word for that exact thing—a father's face at the exact moment before—and perhaps there was an old man who spoke it, and perhaps that was why, at 11:40, when he opened the annex door and heard the humming begin again in Room 4, low and continuous and utterly indifferent to him, Rosalind did not reach for his cart.

He reached for the light switch instead.

"Thursday," said Ansel Vey, without opening his eyes. "You're early. Sit down. I've been thinking about what to teach you first, and I've decided it's the word for rain that falls while the sun is still out, because it's the only word I know that means two opposite things at once, and you strike me, Roszie, as a man who is currently two opposite things at once, and it's rude to begin a student's education with something he isn't."
