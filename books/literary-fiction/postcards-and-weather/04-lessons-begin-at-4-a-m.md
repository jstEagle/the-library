## 4. Lessons Begin at 4 A.M.

The annex had a clock that lied. It hung above the door to the storage room, its hands frozen at some hour no one had bothered to correct, and every night when Rosalind came through with his cart he looked at it the way you look at a photograph of someone who has since died—fondly, briefly, without expectation. Then he checked his own watch, because the truth of the hour mattered now in a way it hadn't for seven years.

At 3:40 a.m., the conservatory belonged to the pipes. Water moved through the building like a second circulation system, a slow arterial murmur under the floors, and if you stood in the annex with the lights off you could hear it thinking. Rosalind had spent months learning this building's body—the way sound pooled in the stairwell by the east windows, the way the rehearsal hall swallowed a note and gave it back an eighth of a second late, wearing it like borrowed clothing. He had told himself, in the beginning, that he memorized the acoustics out of professional habit, the way a blind man learns a room. But habit was a lie he'd kept in a jar, and lately the lid was loose.

At 3:55 he unlocked the annex side door and left it open a hand's width. At 4:00 exactly, Ansel Vey arrived, smelling of woodsmoke and wet wool regardless of the weather, carrying a canvas bag that clinked.

"You're early," Rosalind said, though Ansel was never early or late; he simply arrived, as weather arrives, on a schedule only he subscribed to.

"I'm old," Ansel said. "Old men are always early. We're practicing."

* * *

There was no notation. That was the first thing Rosalind had to surrender, and it went harder than he expected—a prodigy does not give up the staff easily; the five lines were the scaffolding of everything he'd ever been praised for. On the first morning Ansel had watched him reach for a pencil and pushed the pencil off the table with one finger, deliberate as a man closing a door.

"No," he said. "Paper is where sounds go to be embalmed. In here." He tapped his own temple, then, unexpectedly, Rosalind's sternum. "And in here. If it can't live there, it doesn't deserve paper."

So they worked by ear and mouth alone, in the blue dark before the radiators woke, and Rosalind discovered that his memory—which he had assumed was ruined along with everything else, pickled in seven years of bleach and floor wax—was not ruined at all. It was starving. It took the sounds Ansel made and held them whole, greedy, undamaged, and came back for more.

Ansel's language had no name for itself, or rather its name for itself meant *the speech*, the way a river's name means *the river*. It had eleven speakers left in the world, then ten, and now—one old man and one janitor, which was either two or one and a half depending on how you counted. It had words Rosalind had no shelf for. The first week, Ansel taught him only rain.

"Your language," Ansel said—he always said *your language* with a faint apology, as if English were a coat Rosalind had been forced to wear to school—"has one word for rain falling, one word for the season of rain, maybe a few pretty ones from poetry. Listen. This—" and he made a sound low in his throat, a syllable like a stone dropped into deep water, *tlum* but softer, the l almost swallowed— "this is rain that falls while the sun is still out. Rain that lies about the sky. Your poets call it sunshower. We say—" and here he smiled, the first time Rosalind had seen him do it, a crooked rearrangement of the whole face— "we say the sky is arguing with itself."

"And that?"

"That is rain that comes sideways, riding wind, so that it seems to fall upward. Fishermen used it. You would say, *the sea is throwing its water back.*"

"And that one?" A dry clicking syllable, like a beetle on a windowpane.

"Rain that begins and stops, begins and stops, all night, so that no one sleeps and no one can say it rained. The liars' rain." Ansel looked at him a long moment. "You will use that one someday. Everyone uses that one eventually."

Rosalind wrote none of it down. He carried it home at five-thirty in the dark, across the empty quad, past the statue of some benefactor with a bronze violin, and lay in his narrow bed above the laundromat with the words turning over in him like coins in a pocket, and slept better than he had in years, which frightened him.

* * *

The disguise was the schedule. This was the elegant part, the part Rosalind appreciated with the cold admiration of one craftsman for another: nobody questions a janitor's hours. When the day staff arrived at seven, Rosalind was mopping the corridor outside the practice rooms, face blank, cart squeaking, a man so thoroughly invisible that Dr. Halloway from the theory department nodded at him daily without ever once seeing him. The same colleagues who had once fought over seats at his recitals now stepped around his wet floor signs. There was a symmetry in it that might have been funny if he'd been the sort of person who laughed.

But between four and six-thirty, the mop leaned against the wall and Rosalind Coldharbour sat cross-legged on the annex floor with an eighty-something-year-old man and learned to make sounds his mouth had never made.

Some of them hurt. There was a consonant—an ejective, Ansel called it, though he refused all such terminology, calling it instead "the sound of a door slamming in a small room"—that required muscles at the back of Rosalind's throat he didn't know he owned, and for three mornings it came out wrong, flaccid, apologetic, and Ansel would shake his head and say, "Again," in the tone of a man watching rain fail to fall. On the fourth morning it cracked out of Rosalind sharp and true, a small percussive miracle, and Ansel closed his eyes and said something in *the speech* that Rosalind didn't yet understand, and would not understand until weeks later, when he finally asked, and Ansel translated with studied casualness: *"It says, the well was deeper than we thought."*

Rosalind turned away and pretended to check the mop bucket. His eyes were doing something he did not permit them to do in daylight.

Because that was the dangerous part—not the secrecy, not even the grief threaded through everything Ansel taught, each word a survivor of a village, a generation, a way of drying fish and naming children and apologizing to rivers. The dangerous part was the joy. It came up in him like groundwater, unbidden, rising through cracks he'd sealed. He would get a phrase right—all of it, the pitch contour and the breath and the little slammed-door consonant—and something in his chest would light up like a practice room at dusk, warm and gold and utterly undefended, and he would think: *oh no.* Because he remembered what being good at something cost. He remembered where excellence led: to a stage, to a father's face in the third row, to a night with rain on the conservatory roof and applause that sounded like weather, like something falling that could not be caught.

"Stop," Ansel said one morning, apropos of nothing. Rosalind had just repeated a phrase perfectly, twice, and was reaching greedily for a third.

"What?"

"You do that. When it goes well, you flinch. Like touching a stove." Ansel's eyes were pale and unhurried, the color of weak tea. "Who taught you to apologize for being good?"

Rosalind said nothing. Outside, the sky was going from black to the gray of dishwater, the hour when the building began to stir.

"We'll work on that too," Ansel said, and picked up his bag, and left.

* * *

The lessons developed a liturgy. Ansel arrived at four; they made tea on a hot plate Ansel produced from his canvas bag the second morning, refusing to explain where he'd gotten it or why he'd assumed permission. They drank from mismatched cups found in the annex cupboard. Ansel taught in spirals, not lines—returning to a word from three days ago and unfolding another layer of it, so that learning felt less like climbing than like weather systems moving over fixed ground, the same field seen under different skies.

He taught Rosalind the word for the silence between two people who have agreed not to speak of something. It was a long word, built of smaller words, and when Rosalind finally parsed its roots—*the roofed-over fire*—he had to set down his cup.

"There's a word for that," he said. "In your language. For exactly that."

"There are words for most things people actually suffer," Ansel said. "That's what languages are for. Yours has words for kinds of snow because your ancestors froze. Mine has words for silences because mine survived by keeping quiet about things." He sipped his tea. "A language is a map of what a people couldn't afford to forget."

"And yours is dying because—"

"Because everyone who needed the map is dead, mostly. And the country changed." Ansel said it the way he said everything about himself: flatly, completely, without invitation to pity. "Ten speakers last spring. Nine now. I am nine." He held up his hand, fingers spread, then folded one down. "I don't say this to make you sad. Sadness is cheap. I say it so you understand the schedule. I am not teaching you a hobby."

That night Rosalind stood in the shower for forty minutes, letting the water hit the back of his neck, saying the word for *roofed-over fire* over and over until it stopped feeling like a word and started feeling like a diagnosis.

* * *

He began arriving earlier. Three-forty became three-twenty became three. He told himself it was to prepare the room, heat the tea water, review—but preparation was a cover story, and both of them knew it. He wanted to be there when Ansel came through the side door. He wanted the extra minutes the way a hungry man wants the edge pieces of bread. And in those stolen minutes, Ansel talked—or rather, he let things fall out of him the way an overloaded truck sheds cargo on rough roads: names of dead cousins, the recipe for a soup whose name meant *what the pot remembers*, the melody of a lullaby sung to children during a flood year, when the whole village slept on rooftops.

It was the lullaby that broke something open. Ansel hummed it once, absently, waiting for the kettle, and Rosalind—without deciding to, without any thought at all—hummed it back, harmonized, a third above, the way you'd harmonize anything, the way his hands used to find chords before his mind got involved. The annex filled with two voices in the dark, an old man's threadbare line and a hidden one braided above it, and when they stopped, neither spoke for a long time. The kettle ticked as it cooled.

"You hear it," Ansel said finally. Not a question. "Underneath. You hear the architecture."

"It's pentatonic," Rosalind said, and hated himself for the reflex, the analyst's tic, reducing a flood-year lullaby to a scale degree.

"Yes," Ansel said mildly. "And your mother's face is meat and water, and you love her anyway. Description isn't betrayal, boy. Only substitution is." He poured the tea. "You may analyze. Just don't mistake it for knowing."

Rosalind drank his tea and looked at the lying clock and understood that he was in trouble. Not the external kind—though that was coming, he could feel it assembling itself somewhere beyond the walls of the annex, patient as winter. The internal kind. The kind where a life built on almosts develops a taste for wholes. He had constructed his invisibility with real craftsmanship: the neutral clothes, the lowered gaze, the name tag reading R. COLDHARBOUR in letters too small to read, the practiced shuffle that made people's eyes slide off him like rain off glass. Seven years of maintenance. And in eleven days of dawn lessons, one old man with a hot plate had loosened every joint of it, simply by insisting that Rosalind's ears were worth feeding.

At 6:15 he wheeled out the cart. At 7:00 he was mopping the east corridor, and Dr. Halloway nodded at him without seeing him, and Rosalind nodded back and thought: *the liars' rain. Begins and stops, begins and stops, all night, so that no one can say it rained.*

Except it was raining now. It was raining in him, steadily, and getting harder to deny.

* * *

On the twelfth morning, Ansel brought a small tape recorder.

Rosalind saw it in the canvas bag and felt something cold move through him—not quite betrayal, not yet, but its cousin. "What's that for?"

"For later." Ansel set it on the table between them, unapologetic, a black box with a red eye. "Not today. Today we learn the future tense."

"The future tense."

"In my language, you cannot say *it will rain*. There is no will. The grammar refuses it." Ansel drew a slow circle on the table with one finger, like a man smoothing flour. "Instead you say: *the ground is being prepared for rain.* Or for anything. To speak of what a person will do, you say their ground is being prepared. You do not predict. You prepare." He looked up. "Do you understand what that builds into a people? You cannot threaten in the future tense. You cannot promise cheaply. Every sentence about tomorrow is already a sentence about work."

Rosalind sat with that. Through the high annex windows, the sky was doing its slow theater, black to iron to the bruised silver that precedes a gray dawn. Somewhere below, the first bus of the morning sighed at the corner.

"So if I wanted to say," Rosalind said carefully, "that I will play again—someday—I couldn't. I'd have to say my ground is being prepared for it."

Ansel's tea-weak eyes held him, and there was something in them Rosalind hadn't seen before—not the teacher's patience, not the archivist's hunger, but a kind of fierce, specific hope, aimed at him like a lamp.

"Say it," Ansel said quietly. "In the speech. My ground is being prepared. Say it and mean it, and I'll record that, and only that, and keep it until you're ready to ask me what it's for."

The recorder sat between them with its red eye dark. Rosalind's throat worked. Seven years of sealed rooms, of the mop and the alias and the postcard in his locker going soft with damp, and here was an old man asking him to commit grammar.

Outside, the first rain of the morning began—light, intermittent, the kind his new language had a word for, the kind that couldn't decide.

"My ground," Rosalind said, in *the speech*, slowly, each syllable placed like a foot on ice, "is being prepared."

Ansel reached over and pressed record.

And at that exact moment—Rosalind would remember the timing forever, the way you remember where you were standing when bad news arrives—there came a knock at the annex door. Three knocks, crisp, unhurried, entirely wrong for 6:20 a.m. A knock that belonged to daylight and good shoes and family.

Through the frosted pane of the side door, a silhouette. Slim. Standing very straight. Waiting.

Ansel clicked off the recorder and raised an eyebrow. "Expecting someone?"

Rosalind stared at the shape in the glass, and his stomach dropped the way it drops on stairs in the dark, one step more than your leg expected.

"No," he said. "But I know who it is."
