## 22. Weather, Continuing

The rain came in off the harbor the way Ansel said it always would, sideways and without apology, and Rosalind had learned—finally, at thirty-four, a decade later than any prodigy should have learned anything—to stop fighting it.

He stood at the window of the rehearsal annex with his coat still on, watching the water bead and run down the glass in long diagonal stitches. A year ago this window had been painted shut; he'd spent an entire Tuesday afternoon with a putty knife and a can of penetrating oil freeing it, working by the light of his own mop bucket, humming one of the eleven songs under his breath because no one was listening. Or so he'd thought. Later, Priya told him she'd heard him through the wall and had stood in the corridor for twenty minutes, afraid to move, the way you stand still when a deer crosses the road in front of your car.

Now the window opened on oiled hinges with a sound like a held note finally released.

"Rosalind." Priya's voice from the doorway. "The radiator's doing the thing again."

"The thing where it sounds like it's dying?"

"The thing where it sounds like it's dying *in E minor.*"

"That's not a thing," said Tomas from behind her, shouldering past with two folding chairs under each arm. "E minor is a thing that happens to radiators. Not the other way around."

Rosalind smiled and let them argue. It was six-forty in the evening, and in twenty minutes his class would begin—not a conservatory class, nothing with credit attached or a dean's signature required, but a class nonetheless, listed on a photocopied flyer taped to the annex door in five languages, four of them dead and one of them dying until recently. *Lessons in the Vey tongue. All welcome. Bring nothing.*

Five students tonight. There had been as many as nine once, in the spring, when the video of the funeral performance made its second slow circuit of the internet and a documentary crew came sniffing around the annex with boom microphones and earnest questions about "cultural preservation." Nine had been too many. Nine turned the language into a spectacle, and Ansel had warned him about spectacles with the last of his breath almost: *a language survives only if someone is willing to be embarrassing with it, and nobody is embarrassed in front of a camera. They're only embarrassed in front of each other.*

So now there were five, and five was right. Five was a kitchen table. Five was the size of the room where Ansel had first hummed to him in the dark of the annex, three years ago now, or two—the arithmetic of grief refused to stay tidy—and said, in a language with no word for *hello* but eleven words for kinds of rain, the sentence that had unmade and remade him:

*I know who you were. Come be what you are instead.*

* * *

The class itself was small and strange and the best hour of his day.

They began, as they always began, with weather. It was not sentimentality—or it was, but it was also pedagogy, because in the Vey tongue weather was grammar. The future tense was built from sky. You did not say *it will rain*; you said, roughly, *the ground is being prepared for rain*, and the same construction carried every intention, every promise, every fear. To say *I will love you* was to say *I am preparing the ground for loving you*, which meant that in Ansel's language no one could promise anything lightly. Every vow was a farmer's vow. Every commitment admitted its own conditions.

Tomas struggled with it, as he struggled with everything—he was a percussionist with hands like hammers and a soul that wanted the language to behave like rhythm, regular, countable—but tonight something landed. Rosalind gave them the construction for *next year*—literally, *the winter after the winter that is coming*—and Tomas repeated it, frowning, then frowned harder, then looked up with the expression of a man who has been handed a key to a door he'd assumed was painted on.

"It doesn't predict," he said. "It prepares."

"Yes."

"So you can't say *it will rain*. You can only say *we're getting ready for it*."

"And if it doesn't rain," said Priya, catching it now too, her eyes going bright behind her glasses, "you weren't wrong. You were just... ready for something else."

"Ansel used to say his people never wasted time being certain," Rosalind said. "Certainty was a luxury of languages that could afford to lie."

He said it evenly. He had practiced saying it evenly, in the first months, when Ansel's name still caught in his throat like a fishbone. Now it went down smooth, and what remained was not pain exactly but a kind of weight, the way a stone you carry long enough stops being a burden and becomes a way of knowing where your hand is.

Marguerite arrived at seven-fifteen, during the tea interval, shaking rain from her hair in the doorway with the exasperated tenderness that was, he had come to understand, the truest form of her love. She had stopped managing him somewhere around month four after the funeral. It hadn't happened all at once. It had happened the way ice goes out of a river—not cracking, not dramatic, just one morning the current running clear where the white had been.

She brought pastries from the place near the station and refused to sit through the second half of class, preferring instead to stand at the back with her arms folded, listening to five strangers mangle the vowel that didn't exist in any alphabet she knew, wearing the expression of a woman auditing a miracle.

"You've got them saying it wrong," she whispered afterward, while the students packed up and the annex filled with the scrape of chairs and the smell of wet wool.

"I know."

"You let them say it wrong?"

"Ansel said the accent is the fingerprint. If they sounded like him, they'd be impersonating him. They need to sound like themselves saying his words. That's how it lives."

Marguerite considered this. Outside, the rain thickened against the glass, and Rosalind found himself naming it automatically, in the old tongue—*the rain that comes before the rain that means it*, the preliminary rain, the overture—a word Ansel had taught him in the fourth week of lessons, laughing so hard at Rosalind's butchered pronunciation that he'd had to sit down on the piano bench.

"You're different," Marguerite said. It wasn't an accusation anymore. Somewhere in the last year it had stopped being one. "I keep waiting for it to wear off."

"What?"

"The—" She gestured at him, at all of him, the mop cart long gone but some residue of it still in the set of his shoulders, or maybe that was new too, maybe that was the posture of a man carrying something he'd chosen. "The peace. I keep thinking it's a phase. Like the janitor thing was a phase. Like you'll wake up one morning and want the old life back."

"Do you want me to?"

"No." She said it fast, and then slower, looking out at the rain. "No. I want to understand it, though. I'm working on it. Dad sent his lawyer's letter, and I framed it—no, I didn't frame it, but I thought about framing it. He cut us off, Roz. Both of us. And I keep waiting to feel like an orphan and instead I feel like—" She searched. "Like a house after you take down a load-bearing wall and discover it stands anyway. Better, even. More windows."

Rosalind laughed, and the laugh surprised them both, and Marguerite swatted his arm with the pastry bag, and for a moment they were children again in the back seat of their father's car, except that now the car was theirs, and the road went wherever they pointed it.

* * *

The postcard came on a Thursday.

He had kept the locker—kept the job, technically, though the dean had restructured it into something with a title, *Facilities Steward, Annex Programs*, a bureaucratic fig leaf Rosalind accepted because it let him keep his keys and his early mornings and his anonymity among the pipes and radiators. The locker still smelled faintly of pine solvent and old paper. The postcard from his mother was still there, unopened, weather-stained at the corners, seven years of salt air seeping through the slot. He had not thrown it away. He had not opened it either. Some questions you answer by living forward; others you keep in a locker like a coat you might need if the season turns.

But this postcard was new. This one arrived in the mail slot of the annex itself, which meant someone had walked here, through the October drizzle, and stood in front of the door with the photocopied flyer, and pushed it through.

No return address. No message. Just the front: a photograph of the harbor in fog, the kind of tourist card sold in racks at the ferry terminal, mass-produced and anonymous.

And on the back, in handwriting he would have known blindfolded, in the dark, underwater, across the width of a continent and the length of a silence—

*Rozzy.*

One word. His childhood nickname. The name his mother had used, the name his father had used in the years before the third row and the trembling hands, the name that belonged to a boy who had never yet failed at anything and therefore had no idea what failure would cost, or whom it would cost it to.

Rosalind stood in the annex doorway holding it for a long time. The radiator ticked. Down the hall, someone's scales rose and fell, patient as tide.

His father did not write. His father cut contact the way he did everything—cleanly, expensively, with documentation. So this was not his father's hand, even if the hand resembled it; grief and rage had both lived in that penmanship once, and neither lived there now. This was something else. This was a man standing at a ferry terminal rack, choosing a card of fog because fog was honest—it showed you nothing and promised nothing—and writing one word because one word was all he could afford, or all he could bear, or perhaps all he had understood, finally, of what his son had been trying to tell him for seven years: that the truth does not arrive in paragraphs. It arrives in weather. It arrives as a change in pressure you feel in your teeth before you can name it.

*Rozzy.*

Not forgiveness. Not apology. Not even contact, really—an antenna raised, a signal sent up into cloud cover, awaiting confirmation of receipt.

Rosalind thought of Ansel's future tense. *You do not predict. You prepare the ground.* His father had not said *I will come around*. He had said, in the only grammar available to him, *the ground is being prepared*. For what, he might not know himself. Perhaps for nothing. Perhaps for a phone call in another year, another postcard, another single word. Perhaps the old man would die with the signal unanswered, and perhaps that would be its own kind of justice, the kind outside courtrooms, the kind Marguerite had once described to him with terrible clarity: *not punishment, Roz. Just consequence, allowed to finish its sentence.*

Or perhaps not. Perhaps the ground was being prepared for something.

Rosalind pinned the postcard to the corkboard above the annex piano, next to the photograph of Ansel—he was mid-laugh in it, caught by Priya's phone during a lesson, one hand raised as if conducting the rain itself—and beside the flyer, and beside the list of the eleven songs in Rosalind's own careful notation, the notation that had taken him eight months to invent because the language had never had one and he'd had to build it from scratch, borrowing from everywhere, a bridge made of salvaged parts.

Then he crossed the room and opened the window.

* * *

It was, he would think later, such a small rebellion that it barely deserved the name. The conservatory's facilities manual—of which he was now, absurdly, a custodian—specified that annex windows remain closed during heating season. The radiators fought the open window valiantly, ticking and hissing, burning fuel to warm a room that was steadily filling with October. The sheet music on the piano stand lifted its corners and fluttered. The smell of wet leaves and harbor salt moved through the space like a visitor taking off its coat.

Ansel had died at dawn in a rain his language named specifically, precisely, tenderly—the rain that falls when something ends and something else is already beginning, the transition rain, the hinge of the sky. Rosalind had been holding his hand. There had been no deathbed revelation, no final secret. There had been a grip, a long exhale, and outside the annex window a sound on the roof that Rosalind had identified aloud, voice breaking, using the correct word, and Priya, asleep in the chair by the door, had woken at the sound of a language she didn't know and wept anyway, because grief, like music, requires no translation.

*A language survives only if someone is willing to be embarrassing with it.*

So he let the rain in. It spotted the floorboards. It curled the edges of the flyer taped inside the door. It dampened the corner of the corkboard where the postcard hung, so that the fog on the harbor photograph bloomed slightly, softened, became less a picture and more a memory of weather—which was, Rosalind supposed, what all photographs become eventually, and what all families become too, if you're lucky: not the thing itself, but the trace of it, the stain the season left.

He sat down at the piano, which was out of tune in the way he had stopped correcting, because Ansel had loved it out of tune—*a piano in perfect tune is telling you it has nothing to say*—and he played the opening figure of the first song, the leaving song, the one about a traveler who packs light because everything heavy was already given away.

Tomorrow there would be class again. Tomorrow Priya would argue with Tomas about keys and Marguerite would bring pastries and pretend not to listen, and the five of them—they called themselves, half-joking, *the weather bureau*—would mangle vowels and build futures out of prepared ground. Tomorrow he would teach them the word for *goodbye that expects return*, the last word Ansel ever gave him, the one Rosalind had promised to sing publicly and had sung, and sang still, every week, to a room of strangers-becoming-students-becoming-speakers.

And tomorrow, or the day after, or in another year of Thursdays, he would buy a postcard at the ferry terminal rack—fog, probably, or rain, something honest—and write a single word on the back, and send it south with no return address and no demand, because the question had been answered, and an answered question needs no audience, only an address.

For now, the rain came through the open window and touched everything he had built, and none of it washed away.

He played on into the evening, out of tune, unembarrassed, the annex glowing yellow against the gray like a lamp left in a window for someone walking home through weather that had not yet invented itself—and somewhere between the fifth song and the sixth, without deciding to, he began to sing.
