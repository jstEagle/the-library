## 5. What the Neighbor Saw

Dolores Vann had lived at 12 Alder Lane for fifty-one years, and in that time she had learned that houses kept their mouths shut but neighbors did not — eventually, one way or another, everything came out over a fence or across a porch rail, and the trick was to be patient enough to hear it. She was eighty-one now, her knees announcing weather like barometers, her hands spotted and strong from sixty years of gardening, and she had outlived nearly everyone who could have told her what happened on Alder Lane in the autumn of 1979. Mrs. Halloway had gone last spring, quiet as a lamp going off. Which meant that when the young man with the careful face came walking up the street on a Thursday morning in October, Dolores recognized him before he reached the third mailbox.

Rafael Dunmore. Grown into those long bones, hair cut short and silvering early, dressed like someone who had never once been caught in the rain without wanting to be. But it was the walk she knew — that walk of a child trying to pass through a room without disturbing the air.

She was on her porch with her coffee when he stopped in front of the Halloway house, and she watched him stand there the way people stand in front of graves they aren't sure are occupied. He didn't go up the steps. He just looked. And then he took out a small notebook — an actual paper notebook, which pleased her enormously; she'd thought nobody under sixty wrote anything down anymore — and wrote something in it, standing there on the sidewalk like a man taking dictation from the house itself.

Dolores set down her cup. Some things you waited for, and some things walked right up your street and stood in front of you.

"Rafael Dunmore," she called, not as a question. "You get any taller, you'll have to duck my eaves."

* * *

He turned slowly, and she saw the machinery start up behind his eyes — the little pause, the sorting of faces, the calculation of who this old woman might be and what she might cost him. She'd seen that same look on his father's face thirty years ago, and something in her chest went hard and soft at once.

"Mrs. Vann," he said, and got it right on the first try, which told her he'd been rehearsing names in the car. "You look wonderful."

"I look old," she said. "But I'm still quicker than you are nervous. Come sit down. That coffee's been ready since seven and I don't drink a second pot for my health."

He hesitated — she counted it, two full seconds, a man weighing a porch swing against an exit — and then he climbed her steps and sat where she pointed, knees angled politely, notebook closed on his thigh like a hand folded in prayer.

"You're here about the house," she said.

"The estate. Yes. I'm named in Mrs. Halloway's will." He said it lightly, administratively, a man reading from a letter. "I'm still not entirely sure why."

"Oh, I expect you know why." Dolores let that sit in the air between them like smoke. "Edith Halloway didn't do a thing in her life without a reason, and most of her reasons took decades to ripen. She was patient the way winter is patient."

"She was kind to me," Rafael said. "When we were kids. When—" A small stop, a stone skipped over water. "She let us use her garage for our club meetings. Emeka Okonkwo and me. We had a whole civilization in there. Chalk on the floorboards, a constitution written on butcher paper."

"A republic of two," Dolores said, and watched his face do something complicated. "I used to see you both through the gap in my hedge. Two boys sitting on paint cans arguing about whether a country needed a flag if nobody else knew it existed."

"We decided it did."

"And then?" she asked, gently, the way you lift a bandage at the corner.

Rafael smiled. It was a beautiful smile, she'd give him that — practiced, warm at the edges, a smile built like a good fence: attractive, and designed to keep things out. "And then childhood ended, the way it does. Families move. You know how it is."

"I know exactly how it is," Dolores said. "I was here."

The smile held. But she saw his thumb press flat against the notebook cover, pressing hard enough that the cardboard creaked, and she understood that underneath the polish there was a boy still standing in front of a house he couldn't enter.

"Your mother sent over a pie, the week before you all left," Dolores said, changing the angle, because you didn't dig straight down, you tunneled. "Lemon meringue. Store-bought crust, but she'd have denied it under oath. She left it on my step with a card that said *thank you for everything.*" She paused. "We hadn't done anything. That always struck me. A thank-you for nothing, attached to a pie, three days before your family vanished out of that house at six in the morning with the lamps still hanging in the windows."

Rafael looked at her then, really looked, and for a moment the fence came down and she saw the eleven-year-old underneath, the one who had stood in her yard once asking if he could pick her raspberries because his own house felt wrong to him that summer — his word, *wrong*, delivered with terrible gravity by a boy who didn't yet know what the word would come to mean.

"I don't remember much about leaving," he said quietly.

"No," Dolores agreed. "Children rarely remember what they're helped not to."

* * *

The porch swing chain ticked against its hook in the wind. Down the street, the Halloway house sat with its plywood window boards gone gray, its gutters holding a decade of maple seedlings, and Rafael found himself unable to stop looking at it while Mrs. Vann talked, the way you can't stop probing a tooth.

He had prepared for this trip the way he prepared for everything: research, angles, a folder of sentences. He had a version of himself ready for the neighbors — the successful son returning dutifully, wistful, unburdened. He had deployed it twice already at the diner, where the waitress had hugged him and called him *honey* and asked after his parents with such naked hunger for news that he'd given her nothing and she'd thanked him anyway.

But Dolores Vann was not eating from his hand. She was watching him eat, and noting.

"Tell me about the town now," he said, steering. "It looks smaller."

"Towns don't shrink. We do." She refilled his cup without asking. "Kettleman's closed. The Okonkwos' old place sold twice and fell apart twice. The Hendersons' boy died in Fallujah and the Hendersons moved to be near the grave. Otherwise it's all here." She gestured with her chin at the whole visible world — the lane, the hedge, the hill, the water tower. "You were saying you don't remember much."

"I was eleven."

"Mm. I was forty-eight, and I remember every minute of that October." She said it pleasantly, stirring her coffee, and Rafael felt the temperature of the conversation change the way you feel a storm front arrive — no rain yet, just the light going strange. "I remember the night before you left, there were cars in your driveway until two in the morning. I remember your father carrying boxes out to a station wagon in the dark, and I remember thinking it was odd, because who packs a car at midnight for a move they've known about for weeks?"

Rafael's cup stopped halfway to his mouth.

"I remember," Dolores continued, in the same mild voice, "that the boxes went into the Halloways' garage. Not your garage. Edith's. I saw it plain as Sunday from my kitchen window, because your father's headlights swept my curtains every trip. And I remember that the next evening the fire trucks came, and by Friday the Okonkwo family was gone too, and nobody ever put those two facts next to each other out loud. Not once. In fifty years." She looked at him. "Isn't that something? A whole town keeping two facts apart, like children who won't let two magnets touch."

"Mrs. Vann—"

"Dolores."

"Dolores." He set the cup down. His hands were perfectly steady; she noticed that, and noted it too, the steadiness itself a kind of telling. "I was a child. Whatever you think you saw—"

"I don't think. I saw." No heat in it. That was the unnerving thing — she spoke the way his editor spoke about a bad sentence, with the calm of someone simply reading what was already on the page. "I saw your father moving boxes at midnight. I saw the fire the next day. I saw the Okonkwos' furniture on the curb a week later, and Adaeze Okonkwo standing over it in her church coat while people walked past and didn't look at her. I was afraid, Rafael. That's my confession, since we're trading them. I was afraid of your father, same as half the town, and so I kept my eyes down like everybody else, and a family lost everything while I watered my tomatoes."

The wind moved through the hedge. Somewhere a dog was barking at nothing, patiently, the way dogs do.

"I don't remember testifying to anything," Rafael said at last, and heard how the sentence arrived already broken — *testifying*, a word he hadn't planned to say, surfacing from some flooded room in him. "I don't remember the fire. I don't remember leaving. There's a—" He stopped. Started again, more carefully, rebuilding the fence plank by plank. "There's very little from that period. Childhood amnesia, probably. The doctors say—"

"Boy." Dolores leaned forward and took his wrist, her grip dry and astonishingly strong, and the touch undid something in him that none of his sentences had ever reached. "You just said *testifying*. Nobody said testify. I said boxes and fire and furniture. Where'd that word come from?"

Rafael opened his mouth.

Nothing came out.

It was, he would think later, the first honest silence of his adult life — not the performed pause of a critic savoring, not the strategic silence of a man letting someone else fill the air, but a true and total vacancy, a hole in the page where a paragraph should have been. Eleven years old, a kitchen lit yellow, his mother's hands on his shoulders, his father's voice saying *this is what happened, say it back* — the memory rose like a body surfacing and he shoved it down and it went, but it left ripples all through him, and Dolores Vann watched them cross his face one by one.

"There it is," she said softly. "Whatever it is. It's still in you."

"I should go." He stood, too fast; the swing protested. "Thank you for the coffee. Thank you for—"

"Sit down, Rafael Dunmore." Not loud. Just final, the voice of a woman who had raised four children and buried two. "I'm not finished, and neither are you. Sit."

He sat. That surprised them both.

"I'm not your enemy," she said. "Hear that plainly. I fed your friend Emeka's mother casseroles for a year after they landed in the apartment over the hardware store, and I was the only one who did, and I have been ashamed of that arithmetic for fifty years — one casserole against a whole town's silence. I'm not looking to hurt you. But I'm eighty-one, and I'm the last one on this street who saw anything, and when I die, the seeing dies with me. So I'm going to ask you one question, and you can lie to me if you need to, God knows lying has been the local industry around here long enough. But I'd take it kindly if you didn't."

The Halloway house stared at them both down the length of the lane.

"What was in the boxes, Rafael?"

And Rafael — celebrated, precise, fluent in the borrowed language of other people's pleasures, a man who could make a stranger taste a peach in a single sentence — looked at this old woman with her garden hands and her fifty-one years of patience, and reached for the beautiful deflection waiting in his throat, the charming turn, the graceful pivot he had used on editors and exiles and his own mother —

— and found, instead, that he wanted to answer her.

That was the frightening part. Not that he couldn't lie. That some starved thing in him *wanted* to tell her, wanted it the way a man dying of thirst wants water, and that want terrified him more than any question she could ask.

"I don't know," he said. And then, because it was the truest thing he'd said in thirty years and it frightened him how easily it came: "I think maybe I did know. Once."

Dolores nodded slowly, as if he'd confirmed a delivery she'd been expecting for decades.

"Well," she said. "Then I expect you'll want to talk to the person who'd know what was in them besides your father. And before you drive back to whatever city you're living in and lose your nerve —" she leaned over the arm of the swing, and her voice dropped to almost nothing, "— you should know the Okonkwo boy never left. He's two towns over, past Millbrook. Bakes bread. Has done for years. Folks say he's the best within a hundred miles, and folks say he tastes everything like it's the last time." Her eyes held his. "Emeka. You remember that name, don't you?"

The chalk dust ghosted up through him — a garage, a constitution on butcher paper, a republic of two.

"Yes," Rafael said. "I remember Emeka."
