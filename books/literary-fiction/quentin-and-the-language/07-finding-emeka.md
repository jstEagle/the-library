## 7. Finding Emeka

The oven woke him before the alarm did, the way it had for thirty-one years — a low animal heat breathing through the kitchen wall, patient as tide. Emeka Okonkwo lay in the dark of his apartment above the bakery and listened to it, and as always on the first morning of the week he said the same silent thing he had said every morning since he was old enough to understand what his mother had lost: *I will taste all of it today. Every crumb. I will taste it for you too.*

Then he got up and made bread.

The bakery was called The Second Proof, which people assumed was a baking joke and it was, but not only that. His daughter had named it when she was nineteen and full of ideas about resurrection. He had let her, because the name was true in ways she didn't know. Everything in this town two hours from Alder Lane was a second proofing — the whole life was. You took the dough you'd been given, the one that had collapsed, and you folded it again and set it somewhere warm and waited to see if it would rise.

It had risen. That was the plain miracle of it, the one he never got tired of: flour, water, salt, time, and a man's hands, and out of that came something people drove across the county for. He had built the place with a loan he'd been afraid to ask for and a work ethic his father would have recognized and a stubbornness that was purely his mother's — Adaeze Okonkwo, who had stood in a courtroom with her chin up while a child repeated words an adult had put in his mouth, and who had never once, in all the years after, let Emeka see her cry about it.

She had cried anyway. He'd heard her through the wall of whatever cheap rental they'd landed in after the bank took everything, a sound like weather. He was nine years old and he had pressed his palm flat against the plaster and promised the wall things no nine-year-old can keep.

He kept most of them, though. That was the thing nobody understood about him — the ones who knew the story at all, which by now was almost nobody. They expected bitterness, or they expected forgiveness, some clean single note they could file him under. But Emeka had learned early that a man is not one note. A man is a loaf: crust and crumb, char and softness, and if you only ever bite the outside you will swear you know him and be wrong.

* * *

He tasted everything deliberately. This was the discipline, the quiet defiance, though defiance against whom he could no longer quite say — against the world that had tried to make his family into a cautionary tale, maybe; against forgetting itself, which was the more patient enemy. When the first batch came out at five-forty he broke a heel off the sourdough and ate it standing at the steel counter, steam still rising off the torn interior, and he did not eat it the way hungry men eat, fast and without witness. He ate it slowly. He let the crust crackle against his teeth and listened to the crackle. He held the crumb on his tongue until the wheat turned sweet, because wheat does turn sweet, if you give it long enough, and so many people never give anything long enough.

*Salt,* he thought, cataloguing. *Good salt. Kosher, coarse, my own hand.* Then the acid tang of the starter — three days fed, slightly warmer than usual because the radiator in the back ran hot — then underneath it all the deep brown bass note of the Maillard, the browning, the transformation. He had read once that you couldn't smell your own house. That familiarity burned the receptors out. He had decided, at some point in his forties, that this would never happen to him. He would keep his own house strange. He would walk into his own life every morning like a stranger taking notes.

His staff arrived at six. Marisol first, always, tying her apron with the efficiency of someone who had better places to be eventually and was choosing, daily, to be here. Then Dev, the counter kid, twenty-two and earnest, who called the regulars by their orders instead of their names — *large black, oat milk no sugar, the croissant lady* — and whom Emeka corrected gently every week and would correct gently every week until one of them died.

"You've got a look," Marisol said, weighing out butter. "You've got your Monday look."

"I have a face."

"You've got a look on the face." She snapped the scale shut. "Something coming?"

Emeka considered telling her nothing, which was his habit, and then considered why the habit existed, which was rarer. "Somebody's coming," he said. "I don't know who yet. Just a feeling. Like weather."

"Bad weather?"

"No." He turned a tray of proofing loaves, checking the rise with two fingers, the way his mother had taught him before everything — before the fire, before the accusation, before the name Okonkwo became something whispered at the PTA meeting and the loan office. "Just weather. The kind that changes things."

* * *

The bell over the door rang at ten past eight, and Emeka looked up from scoring a batard, and the knife stopped in his hand.

He had imagined this moment, over the years, more often than he admitted even to himself. In the imagining it went different ways. Sometimes he was cold — turned his back, let Dev handle it, made the man wait like everyone else, made him wait longer. Sometimes he rehearsed speeches, forty years of them compressed into a paragraph that would land like a verdict. Once or twice, in the honest dark, he had imagined simply weeping, because Rafael Dunmore had been his best friend and there was a boy-shaped hole in his childhood shaped exactly like that face, and grief and grievance had been braided together in him so long he couldn't tell which strand was holding the knot.

None of the imaginings survived contact. What actually happened was simpler and stranger: he looked up, and he knew him instantly — the long face, the careful eyes, the way Rafael stood slightly sideways to rooms as if always about to leave them — and the knowing arrived not as anger but as a kind of vertigo, the floor of the present tilting down into the past.

Rafael Dunmore had gotten old the way elegant people get old, which is to say deliberately. Silver at the temples, arranged. A coat worth more than Emeka's oven. But the eyes were eleven years old and standing in the Halloways' driveway, and they were frightened, and they were looking at Emeka as if Emeka were the ghost.

Emeka put down the knife.

He did not speak. This was not strategy, or not only strategy. It was also that his voice, in that first second, was not reliably his own. So he did the thing his hands knew how to do instead. He pulled a loaf from the morning rack — the sourdough, the good one, the one he tasted most carefully — and he cut a slice, thick, and set it on a plate, and set the plate on the counter in front of the register, directly in Rafael Dunmore's path.

Then he stepped back and waited.

Dev glanced between them, sensing voltage he couldn't source. "Sir? Can I — we've got the—"

"It's fine," Emeka said quietly. "I've got this one."

Rafael approached the counter the way a man approaches a dog that might bite. Up close the resemblance to the boy was almost unbearable — the same hesitancy at thresholds, the same habit of looking at Emeka's hands rather than his face. Rafael looked at the bread. At the plate. At Emeka's hands.

"Hello, Emeka," Rafael said finally, and the voice hadn't changed either, not really. It had just learned to modulate itself, the way voices do when their owners spend decades saying things they don't mean.

Emeka nodded once. He tapped the plate with one finger. *Eat,* the gesture said. Or perhaps: *This is what I do now. This is what they couldn't take.* Or perhaps both.

Rafael sat at the little counter bar — four stools, worn maple, the closest thing the bakery had to a table for one — and picked up the slice, and Emeka watched him with an attention that felt, from the inside, like standing very still in tall grass.

He watched Rafael taste the bread.

And here was the thing, the thing Emeka would turn over that night and the next and for weeks after: Rafael tasted it wrong. Not badly — the man was famous, presumably he knew the vocabulary — but wrong in a way Emeka recognized the way you recognize a limp. Rafael chewed, and paused, and his lips moved faintly, retrieving words, and then he said, "Beautiful crumb. The hydration's high — sixty-eight, seventy percent? And there's a rye in the levain, a small percentage, for the sourness." All of it accurate. All of it correct. And none of it alive. It was a coroner's report delivered about a living child. Rafael described the bread the way you describe a photograph of bread, and Emeka felt something in his chest go tight and cold, because he understood — not fully, not yet, but he understood — that he was watching a man read a language he could no longer hear.

"You remembered me," Rafael said. It wasn't a question, exactly, but it wasn't brave enough to be a statement.

Emeka wiped the counter where no crumbs were. He let the silence sit. Let it do its work. Silence was a tool in a bakery; dough rose in it, flavors married in it, and customers confessed in it — they always confessed, given enough quiet, told him about divorces and diagnoses and dead brothers over the croissant case. He had learned that most people cannot bear a held silence and will pay almost anything to end it, and that what they pay with is usually the truth.

Rafael lasted nearly a full minute, which was longer than most.

"I'm here because—" A breath. "The house on Alder Lane. Mrs. Halloway left it to me, or left me involved in it, and I've been going through—" The sentence collapsed and rebuilt itself along safer lines. "I found your family's name. In the records. About the house. About what happened."

*What happened.* As if it were weather. As if it were a storm that had blown through and scattered everyone and no one had built it, stoked it, signed their names to its cause.

Emeka set down the cloth. He looked at Rafael Dunmore — at the famous critic, the celebrated man, whose column his mother had sometimes read aloud in her later years with a laugh that had glass in it, *listen to this one, he writes so pretty about food, pretty as a lawyer* — and he felt the braided thing in his chest pull tight, grief and grievance turning around each other like rope under load.

"My mother died believing she was guilty," Emeka said. He hadn't planned to say it. It came up from somewhere below craft or strategy. "That's the part nobody tells you about being accused. You can survive the losing. She never survived the *believing*. Forty years, and every doctor, every neighbor, every kindness — she'd take it and turn it over and find the accusation inside it. Somebody brings you a casserole, you wonder if they think you're a thief. Somebody's nice to your son, you wonder what it cost them." He picked up the cloth again, folded it, unfolded it. "She died in March. Quietly. Believing it."

Rafael had gone very still. "Emeka, I—"

"Don't." The word came out harder than he intended, and he watched it land, watched Rafael flinch like a struck horse, and something in Emeka ached at causing it and something older and deeper refused to take it back. "Not yet. Whatever you came here to say — apology, explanation, whatever speech you built in the car — don't say it yet. Because I've imagined those speeches for forty years and every version lets somebody off. And I want to know, before you say anything at all, whether you even know what happened. Whether you remember. Or whether—" he hesitated, because the next part was cruel, and he was not a cruel man, but the truth has edges and someone has to hold it by them — "or whether there's nothing there to remember."

Rafael's face did something complicated. Something Emeka had seen before on customers holding bad news they hadn't decided to share: the flicker behind the composure, the inventory being taken, the question *how much can I afford to lose by telling the truth* being calculated at speed.

"I don't know," Rafael said at last, barely above the hum of the proofer. "That's the truth. I don't know what I remember."

Emeka nodded slowly. It was, strangely, the best answer available. A lie would have ended it — a smooth, polished, beautifully written lie, the kind Rafael Dunmore was famous for. But this, this cracked and honest nothing, this was a door.

He reached under the counter and brought out a second plate. On it he placed the loaf he saved for himself, the ugly one, the experimental bake — this week it was a country loaf with toasted barley and a long cold ferment, misshapen, deeply colored, the best thing he'd made in a month. He cut it in half and set half in front of Rafael.

"Eat that," he said. "Slowly. Don't tell me what's in it. Don't tell me percentages. Just tell me what it tastes like. To you. Honestly."

Rafael looked down at the bread, and Emeka saw the fear arrive — real fear, the fear of a man asked to perform, live and unrevised, the one act he could no longer do.

"And Rafael." Emeka held his oldest friend's — his oldest enemy's, the categories had never sorted cleanly — gaze across the flour-dusted steel. "I'll know if you're writing."

* * *

Outside, the morning had turned properly bright, and Dev was restocking the window, and the ovens breathed their low animal heat, and Emeka stood behind his counter watching a celebrated man hold a piece of bread like it was evidence, like it was a live wire, like it was the last exam of a life — and waited, with his mother's patience and his father's stubbornness and forty years of salt, to see whether the tongue would come home or whether it had been gone too long, whether some things, once given away by children to protect the adults who asked, could ever be taken back.
