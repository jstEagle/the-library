## 11. Sister Agnes's Records

The town archive lived where it had always lived, which was to say nowhere in particular and everywhere at once—in the basement of St. Brigid's, in a room that had been a boiler room, then a classroom, then a boiler room again, and now was neither, exactly. Jonah remembered it from a school field trip in the sixth grade: the smell of cold concrete and old paper, the fluorescent hum, Sister Agnes's small hands moving over card catalogs like a pianist's. She had not thought about that room in twenty years. Now she stood outside the side door of St. Brigid's with rain beading on her jacket, and found she had to make herself knock.

The notice had been taped inside the door of the hardware store's back office, she'd learned from the woman at the diner—a yellowed sheet listing hours that were more suggestion than schedule. *Tuesdays and Thursdays, 10–2, or ring twice.* It was Wednesday. She rang twice anyway.

For a long moment there was nothing, just the rain working its way under her collar, and then footsteps, unhurried, and the door opened onto a nun who was not Sister Agnes. This one was young—younger than Jonah, certainly—with wire-rimmed glasses and a cardigan the color of oatmeal, and she looked at Jonah the way people in this town had begun looking at her since she'd come back: with the patient, faintly amused attention of someone cataloguing a return.

"You're ringing for the archive," the nun said. Not a question.

"I'm sorry—I know it's not—" Jonah gestured at the sky, which was doing its gray, noncommittal drizzle. "I heard Sister Agnes might still keep the records."

"Sister Agnes retired to Duluth eleven years ago." The nun stepped back to let her in. "But she never trusted anyone else with the files, so they stayed. I'm Sister Miriam. I mostly just try to keep the pipes from freezing." A pause, a small assessing smile. "You're the Nystrom girl. Jonah."

The name landed strangely—not unkindly, but with weight, like a stone set down on a table. *The Nystrom girl.* As if she were still sixteen, still someone's daughter, still a fixed point on the town's map rather than a person who had spent half her life being nobody in particular three hundred miles away.

"Jonah Nystrom," she confirmed. Her voice came out flatter than she intended.

"Weather Lane," Sister Miriam said, and Jonah understood that the whole town had already done this arithmetic—the auction notice, the lot number stenciled on the boarded windows, the prodigal walking past it twice in two days. There was no privacy here. There never had been. That had been the point of leaving.

"I'm actually here about something else," Jonah said. "The fire marshal's report. From 2009. The Halvorsen house fire."

Something moved behind the young nun's glasses—not surprise exactly, but a recalibration, the way a person shifts weight when a conversation turns out to be about more than it appeared. "That would be in the municipal copies," she said slowly. "We hold what the parish held. Correspondence, mostly. Some of Sister Agnes's own notes." She considered Jonah for a moment longer than was comfortable. "Come down. I'll put the kettle on. Nobody reads fire reports standing up."

* * *

The stairs went down into the same cold-smelling room Jonah half-remembered, though the card catalogs were gone now, replaced by metal shelving and banker's boxes labeled in a hand so precise it looked printed. Sister Miriam moved through the aisles with the ease of long tenancy, pulling boxes, sliding them back, until she arrived at one marked *FIRE — 2009 — PARISH RELIEF* and set it on a table beneath the humming light.

"The marshal's report itself you'd get from the county," she said. "But the parish kept everything around it. Who we housed, what we collected, statements people gave us for the relief fund." She hesitated, her hand resting on the box lid. "You should know that some of what's in here is hard. People wrote things to us in the weeks after that they wouldn't have said sober, or in daylight, or to each other. Sister Agnes believed those papers mattered. She said an archive isn't a warehouse of facts, it's a hospital for them." She lifted the lid. "I'll be upstairs if you need me."

And then Jonah was alone with the box, and the kettle sound came faintly through the floor, and she sat down on the metal folding chair and did not move for what felt like a full minute.

She knew what she was looking for. That was the strange part—she had walked down those stairs knowing, in the way you know a thing you have refused to know for fifteen years. She had come to confirm. She had told herself it was diligence, thoroughness, the letter-writer's instinct for documentation. But underneath the tidy story was the truth, which was that a crack had opened in her memory and she needed to know how deep it went.

Because here was the thing she had not said aloud to anyone, including the shoebox, including the postcards, including the version of herself that rehearsed confessions on four-by-six cards: the fire had started in the wrong place.

In her memory—and God, her memory was a cathedral, vaulted and lit and tended daily—the fire began on the porch. It began with the argument. It began with *her sentence*, the one she had never repeated aloud, the words she'd thrown at Quentin like a struck match while his mother stood in the doorway behind him with a dish towel in her hands. In her memory the porch light flickered even as she spoke, as if the house itself were listening, and then the smell of smoke, and Mrs. Halvorsen's voice cracking across the yard, and the whole night tilting into orange. Fifteen years of postcards rested on that sequence. *Words, then flame.* Cause and consequence, clean as a receipt. If the sequence broke, everything she'd been confessing to was—

She pulled the first folder toward her and made herself read.

Most of it was logistics. The parish relief fund ledger, columns of names and amounts, $40 from the Altar Society, $100 anonymous, $25 from *Nystrom, D.*—her father, she realized, with a small jolt; she'd forgotten he'd given anything, forgotten he'd still been in town that autumn at all. Carbon copies of letters thanking donors. A list of families who'd taken in the displaced—*Halvorsen, Q., age 16 — Kowalczyk residence, Elm St.* Quentin had stayed with the Kowalczyks. She hadn't known that either. She had been gone by then, packed off to her aunt in Cedar Falls before the ash was cold, and whatever happened to Quentin in the months after had happened without her, which had been the entire design.

Then, near the bottom of the folder, a mimeographed page, the purple ink faded to the color of a bruise: *SUMMARY OF FINDINGS — OFFICE OF THE FIRE MARSHAL — distributed to cooperating agencies.* And below it, in the flat bureaucratic language that somehow made everything worse:

> Origin of fire determined to be the northeast wall cavity, ground floor, adjacent to the kitchen chimney chase. Ignition source consistent with electrical fault—degraded insulation on branch circuit wiring, arcing within concealed space. No evidence of open flame, smoking materials, accelerants, or occupant activity at point of origin. Estimated time of ignition: between 6:40 and 7:05 p.m.

Jonah read it twice. Three times. The words did not rearrange themselves. *Concealed space. Electrical fault. Between 6:40 and 7:05.*

The argument on the porch had started after seven. She knew that the way she knew her own birthday—by the church bells, by the fact that the streetlights had just come on, by the specific quality of blue-dark that fell at that hour in October. She had stood on those steps and said what she said somewhere around quarter past, maybe twenty past. She had carried the arithmetic of it for fifteen years: *my words, then the fire, minutes apart, close enough to be cause.*

But the fire had already been burning when she spoke. It had been burning inside the wall, silently, invisibly, eating through sixty years of dried-out lumber while two teenagers fought about nothing on the steps above it. The house was dying while she was being cruel. Her sentence had not lit anything. It had fallen, like everything else that night, into a fire that was already going.

She sat very still. The fluorescent light buzzed. Somewhere overhead the kettle began its thin whistle and was taken off the heat.

Here was the vertigo, then, arriving exactly as promised, rising through her like water filling a lock: *if her memory was wrong, what exactly had she been confessing to for fifteen years?*

Two hundred and six postcards. She knew the number because she'd counted them last week, kneeling on her apartment floor with the shoebox between her knees, each returned card a small stamped refusal. Two hundred and six rehearsals of a crime she had not committed—or not that crime, not in that way. She had built a whole penitential architecture on a false foundation. *I burned it down,* she had written, in code, in ellipsis, in the careful incomplete sentences of someone protecting the guilty party from the truth. But she hadn't burned it down. Wiring had burned it down. A circuit installed before either of them was born, degrading year by year inside the walls while they grew up playing Monopoly on the living room floor not four feet from the failing line.

And yet—

Her eye caught on the next item in the folder, a handwritten statement on lined paper torn from a spiral notebook, signed at the bottom in a hand Jonah recognized instantly, because she had seen it on lunch bags and permission slips and birthday cards for the whole of her childhood: *Ruth Halvorsen.*

> To whom it may concern. I want to state clearly that the fire was my responsibility. I had been putting off having the wiring looked at. I noticed the lights dimming on that circuit for months and told myself it could wait. My son and his friend were on the porch when it started and they could have been killed. Whatever anyone says about that evening, the fault is mine and mine alone. Please don't trouble the children with any of it.

Dated October 19, 2009. Nine days after the fire. Nine days after the night, and Ruth Halvorsen had already gone to the parish, to the insurance people, to whoever would listen, and nailed the blame to herself with both hands.

*Please don't trouble the children with any of it.*

Jonah read the line and felt the ground go soft under the vertigo, because now there were two false stories stacked on top of each other like collapsed floors. Hers: *my words started it.* Ruth's: *my neglect started it.* And beneath both, at the bottom of the rubble, the marshal's dry finding: *no one started it. It started itself, the way old houses do, the way old wounds do, quietly and from the inside.*

Except that wasn't quite true either, was it. Because something had happened on that porch. Something had been said—she had said it—and whatever it had done, it had done its work in the days and years after, in the silence between two families, in the way Ruth Halvorsen had spent nine days constructing a confession to bury whatever else that night contained. What had Ruth known? What had she seen from the doorway, dish towel in her hands, that made her decide the fire was the least of what needed covering?

Jonah pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until she saw constellations. The room smelled of concrete and cold paper and, faintly, of tea. On the table the marshal's summary lay face-up, its purple ink patient and indifferent, and she understood suddenly why Sister Agnes had called this place a hospital for facts. The facts lay here, bandaged and waiting, all these years, for someone to come sit with them.

She had confessed to arson for fifteen years. The house had died of natural causes. And the only person who had ever taken the blame—truly taken it, in ink, in duplicate, distributed to cooperating agencies—was dead, and had died believing a lie that protected everyone except herself.

There was a third story under the second one. There had to be. People did not write statements like Ruth's out of simple guilt; people wrote them out of purpose. *Whatever anyone says about that evening.* That evening had contained more than wiring. It had contained a porch, and a doorway, and a sentence, and a woman deciding what her town would be allowed to believe.

Jonah copied the date of the statement into her notebook, and the file reference, and the phrase *northeast wall cavity*, her handwriting steady in the way of someone holding themselves together with procedure. Then she turned back through the folder one more time, slower, hunting—and near the bottom, folded in quarters and tucked inside the relief ledger, she found a note in Sister Agnes's own small precise hand, pinned to a follow-up letter from the county:

*R.H. came to see me again in March. Insists on the same account. Asked me whether a person can be responsible for a thing she failed to prevent. I told her the distinction matters less to God than she thinks. She did not seem comforted. —A.*

*A thing she failed to prevent.*

Jonah sat in the humming cold with the note in her hands and felt fifteen years of postcards turn over inside her like a deck being reshuffled. Upstairs, faintly, Sister Miriam was moving furniture, or setting out a second cup. Outside, the rain had thickened against the high window, running down the glass in long silver threads, and Jonah thought: *the storm is coming tonight, the real one, the kind this town gets in October—and I am not finished here, and neither is he.*

She gathered the papers back into their box with great care, the way you handle evidence, the way you handle a wound, and climbed the stairs toward the tea she suddenly badly needed—because tomorrow she was going to find Ruth Halvorsen's sister, and ask her what Ruth had failed to prevent.
