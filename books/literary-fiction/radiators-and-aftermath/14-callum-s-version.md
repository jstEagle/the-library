## 14. Callum's Version

The chandlery smelled of tar and hemp and something older underneath, something like the inside of a church. Theo stood in the doorway for a full minute before the bell above him stopped swinging, and in that minute he had time to rehearse three openings and abandon all of them.

The man behind the counter was smaller than Theo had imagined, though Theo had no basis for imagining anything. He was seventy at least, maybe more, with a face weathered to the color of old rope and hands that looked like they had spent sixty years arguing with salt water. He wore a canvas apron over a flannel shirt and reading glasses pushed up into white hair that needed cutting. He was doing something patient and precise with a coil of line, feeding it through his fingers loop by loop, and he did not look up when the bell rang, which meant either he hadn't heard it or he had heard it and decided the visitor could wait.

Theo waited. It seemed like the correct posture for this place, this errand. You waited on boats. You waited on weather. You waited forty years, if that was what love required of you.

"Looking for something or browsing?" the man said, without looking up. His voice was flat coastal vowels, unhurried. "Because if you're browsing, mind the varnish. People lean."

"I'm—" Theo began, and then stopped, because his mouth had produced a stranger's voice, a customer's voice, and he didn't want to be a customer. He wanted to be what he actually was, which was a son standing in the wreckage of his mother's secret, holding a shoebox he'd left in the car because carrying it in felt like an accusation. "I'm looking for Callum Reeve."

The hands stopped. The line went still between the fingers, mid-loop, and for a moment the whole shop seemed to hold its breath with them—the hanging oilskins, the brass fittings catching the grey window light, the tide chart curling off its nail.

Then the man looked up, and Theo saw that his eyes were pale blue and very steady, and that there was no surprise in them at all. That was the first shock. Not recognition—something quieter than recognition. The look of a man who has been expecting a letter for decades and has finally heard the postman's footsteps.

"You'll be Eleanor's boy," Callum Reeve said.

Not a question. Theo felt the floor tilt slightly, the way a deck tilts.

"Theo," he managed.

"I know." Callum set down the line with great care, as if it were a living thing he didn't want to startle. He took off his glasses and folded them into his apron pocket. "You have her way of standing in a doorway. Like you're not sure yet whether the room deserves you."

* * *

He made tea on a camp stove behind the counter, in two enamel mugs that had seen better centuries, and Theo sat on a stool among the coils and cleats and did not drink, because his hands were not steady enough to be trusted with hot liquid. Through the back window he could see a strip of grey harbor and one boat moored with the patience of a dog tied outside a shop.

"You knew," Theo said. "You knew about the letters."

Callum lowered himself into a chair that had been repaired more than once, and nodded, and said nothing, and Theo understood that the silence was not evasion. It was courtesy. The man was letting him set the pace.

"She wrote to you for forty years," Theo said. "Forty years, Mr. Reeve. And you never wrote back. Not once. There isn't a single reply in that box. Do you know what that does to a person, reading four hundred letters and finding no answer? I thought—I thought she was writing into a void. I thought you'd died, or forgotten her, or never existed at all. I stood across the street from this shop for twenty minutes yesterday trying to decide if you were worth the walk."

"And decided I wasn't," Callum said, "and came in anyway. That's her too."

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"Make me into her. I'm not her. I'm the thing she chose instead of you. I'm the family. I'm the hotel and the myth and the silence she kept so well that her own children grew up inside it and called it home." Theo heard his own voice rising and could not stop it. "She loved you. She wrote it down, hundreds of times, in handwriting I recognized before I even read a word. And you let her sit in that house writing letters to a man who never answered. Do you know what that is? That's not romance. That's cruelty with good manners."

Callum took this the way a seawall takes water. He waited until the wave had gone out again, and then he reached under the counter and brought up a tin—a biscuit tin, rusted at the corners—and set it on the counter between them without opening it.

"Before you finish sentencing me," he said, "you should know what's in the tin."

* * *

There were forty-one letters inside. Theo counted them without meaning to; counting was what he did now, an inheritance from the shoebox, everything in his life reduced to inventory. Forty-one envelopes, each addressed in a hand Theo knew intimately—his mother's hand, younger in these, rounder, the ink different—and each one unopened.

His mother's handwriting. Addressed to Callum Reeve. Sealed. Never opened by the man they were sent to.

"That can't be right," Theo said. "She got replies. Some of the later letters reference—there are answers in there, things she says that only make sense if—"

"She referenced my silences," Callum said. "That's not the same as replies. Go on. Open one. They're yours as much as mine, I expect. More, probably. I'm just the address."

Theo picked the top envelope with fingers that suddenly felt enormous and clumsy. He broke the seal—he remembered this seal, the same pressed-flower wax she'd used on Christmas cards—and unfolded a single page dated June 1979, two years before Theo was born.

*My dear C. This is the ninth I've written and the ninth you haven't answered, and I've decided that's your answer, and I'm going to keep writing anyway, because the writing is the loving and the posting is the hoping, and I refuse to give up either. Your silence is the most eloquent thing anyone has ever said to me. I've read it a hundred ways. Today I read it as: you will not make me choose, because you know what choosing would cost, and you love me too much to charge me.*

Theo put the page down on the counter very carefully, as if it might bruise.

"You never opened them," he said. "Any of them."

"Not one." Callum poured himself tea he also wasn't drinking. "First one came in the spring of '77. I knew her handwriting before I saw the name—we'd been, oh, we'd been young together, before Winterbourne, before the hotel, before any of it. One summer on the quay when we were nineteen and foolish. Then she married him, and that was that, or I thought it was. Twenty years later a letter arrives with no return address and a seal on it like a promise."

"So you opened it."

"No." Callum said it simply, without drama. "I held it. Sat with it all evening. And I understood that she hadn't sent me a letter. She'd sent me a choice. If I opened it, I was in her life. If I answered, I was in her marriage. Either way I was asking her to carry something she'd already decided to carry alone. So I made her a promise she never knew about: she could write to me for the rest of her life, and I would receive every word, and I would never once make her pay for it."

"That's insane," Theo said. "That's—you let her believe—"

"I let her believe exactly what she needed to believe, which was that her love had somewhere to go." The old man's voice stayed level, but something moved behind it, deep water moving under ice. "You think I didn't want to write back? I wrote back. Every letter, I wrote an answer the same night. Forty years of answers. They're in that drawer." He nodded at a low cabinet under the window, and Theo saw, absurdly, that it was a filing cabinet, an ordinary office filing cabinet in a chandlery, and that its drawers were full. "Never posted a single one. Because she told me, in the only instruction she ever gave me—first letter, last paragraph—that answering would undo her. She'd built a life on the arrangement. A husband she respected, sons she adored, a hotel she ran like a captain runs a ship. All she asked of the world was one sealed door she could slip words under. And I was the floor on the other side of the door. That was my job. Receive. Don't knock."

Theo sat with this. Outside, the harbor light shifted, and somewhere a gull made its one argument.

"She asked whoever found the letters," he said finally, "to decide with kindness, not loyalty. Those are almost her exact words. How did you know she'd say that?"

"Because kindness was her native language," Callum said. "Loyalty she learned later, from your father, and she learned it the way you learn a foreign tongue—fluently, and always aware it wasn't hers." He turned the biscuit tin slowly on the counter, around and around, a small orbit. "You came here expecting grievance. I can see it. You thought you'd find a bitter old man, someone cheated, someone who'd want the story, want the money, want the scandal. Someone to fight."

"Yes," Theo admitted. "I don't know what to do with grace. It doesn't fit anywhere in my family's architecture."

"Then build a new room." Callum looked at him directly then, and Theo saw his mother in the look—not the features, which were nothing alike, but the quality of attention, the way of receiving a person whole. "Tell me why you really came, Theo. Not for her. For yourself. What did you come to ask permission for?"

And Theo, who had interviewed chefs and liars and men with Michelin stars and men with secrets, who had made a career of getting people to say the unsaid thing on the record, found that the question went straight through every professional defense he owned and landed in the soft place.

"My sister wants to sell Saltmere," he said. "A developer's offered a fortune, contingent on discretion. No history published. No fuss. Sign, and the letters stay buried forever along with everything else. Imogen thinks some silences are load-bearing. She might be right. And I'm sitting on a memoir I promised myself fifteen years ago, and a fiancée I haven't been honest with, and a eulogy I gave my father that was full of beautiful lies, and I lost my sense of taste the week after I spoke them, and I have spent four months telling myself it's grief when I know—it's the lie. It's the taste of the lie. I came here to find out if the truth about my mother would set me free or just blow up whatever's left of my family." He laughed, and it came out broken. "And instead I find out the villain of the piece is the best man in it."

"I'm nobody's villain," Callum agreed mildly. "I'm a shopkeeper who kept a promise for forty-four years. But I'll tell you what I know about load-bearing silences, since your sister raised it." He tapped the biscuit tin. "This silence nearly killed her. I watched it happen from forty miles away, one letter at a time. The early ones sing. The middle ones ache. The last ones—" he stopped, and for the first time his composure showed a seam. "The last ones are the reason I'm still alive, if you want the truth. I read the dates on the envelopes, Theo. I never opened them, but I read the dates, and I knew when she was failing, and I did nothing, because doing nothing was the promise. Don't you dare tell me silences hold buildings up. Silences are what the building falls into."

* * *

They drank the tea eventually, both of them, cold. Callum told him about the summer on the quay—Eleanor at nineteen, laughing at his terrible sailing, teaching him to eat oysters—and Theo listened the way you listen to a song you've known all your life and only now hear the words to. His mother had been young. His mother had been happy in a way that had nothing to do with the family myth. It was disorienting, like seeing a photograph of your house before it was your house.

At the door, Theo paused.

"What will you do with them?" Callum asked, nodding toward the car, where the shoebox sat. "Her letters. When the sale goes through—if it goes through."

"I don't know yet. She said whoever finds them decides with kindness, not loyalty. I keep turning that over. Kindness to whom? Her memory? Imogen? The truth?"

"Kindness," Callum said, "isn't a direction. It's a temperature. Whatever you decide, decide warm." He held out his hand, and Theo shook it, and the old rope-colored grip was stronger than expected. "Come back after. Whichever way it breaks. I'll want to know how the story ends—and Theo." The pale eyes held him. "Your mother wrote four hundred letters and never once asked me to save her. But the last one, the date on it—I checked it against the obituary. Three days before she died. Whatever's in that final letter of yours, she wrote it knowing she was running out of time to be understood. Don't let her run out."

Theo drove back along the coast road with the windows down and the cold coming in, and the shoebox on the passenger seat riding the curves like a sleeping animal. Forty miles of grey water on his left. At the top of the hill above Saltmere Bay he pulled over, because the hotel was visible below him—all its windows dark except one, where Imogen's lamp burned over her ledgers—and he understood, with the sudden clarity of altitude, that tomorrow the buyer's solicitors would call, and Imogen would slide the contract across a table, and everyone would watch his pen.

He had expected grievance and found grace, and grace was heavier. Grievance you could fight. Grace you had to answer.

He picked up his phone. Margot had texted twice that day, both messages polite as diplomatic cables, and he typed and deleted three replies before settling on the truest sentence he'd written in months:

*I found my mother's letters' recipient. He never opened one. I need to tell you everything, and I need to do it before Friday.*

He hit send before he could soften it, put the car in gear, and drove down toward the lit window and the waiting signature—while in his pocket, unnoticed, the phone buzzed once with her reply, and buzzed again, and then went quiet.
