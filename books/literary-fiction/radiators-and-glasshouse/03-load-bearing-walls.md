## 3. Load-Bearing Walls

The gutter over the kitchen window had been dripping for eleven days.

Teo knew this because he counted. Not the way Paloma counted—she measured things, wrote numbers in her little green book, spoke of millimeters of wear as if they were weather—but he counted in his own way, the way a man counts when he is waiting for something to happen and suspects it never will. Eleven days of drip. On the twelfth day he got the ladder out of the garage, wiped it down with a rag even though it didn't need wiping, and carried it around the side of the house while the morning was still gray and undecided about itself.

The gutter was full of gravel from the roof shingles and a single maple key, its wing curled like something that had tried to fly and given up halfway. He scooped it out with his bare hands because he'd forgotten gloves, and the water was cold enough to make his knuckles ache, and he thought: this is fine. This is good work. A man should fix what drips.

He did not think, until later, that the drip had been the only sound in the house some nights besides breathing.

* * *

Paloma had been somewhere else for three weeks now. Not gone—she still came home at six-forty, still hung her lanyard on the hook by the door, still ate what he cooked and said thank you and asked about his day and listened to the answer with an attention so complete it felt like being set down gently on a shelf. But she was elsewhere. He could not have said where. It was like living with someone who had left a room without turning off the light; everything still worked, but the current was going somewhere he couldn't see.

He'd tried asking. That was the thing people wouldn't believe about him if he ever told them—that he had tried. Tuesday night, maybe, or Wednesday, standing in the kitchen doorway while she rinsed her plate. "You okay?" Two words, casual, angled to look like nothing. She'd said, "Tired. Big inspection coming up." And she'd smiled at him, and the smile was real, technically, the way a load report is real—it contained accurate information and no feeling whatsoever.

So he fixed things.

The gutter first. Then the garage door, which had been sticking at the bottom on the left side; he adjusted the track and oiled the rollers until it ran smooth as a drawer. Then the car—the Corolla with a hundred and ninety thousand miles on it, whose front-left wheel had developed a faint tick at low speeds that only he could hear. He jacked it up on a Saturday morning in the driveway and pulled the wheel and found the CV boot split and weeping grease, and he spent four hours and sixty dollars repacking it, lying on cardboard on the concrete, his father's socket set spread out beside him like surgical instruments.

His neighbor, Marisol, came by with coffee around noon and stood watching him from the edge of the driveway. "You two fighting?" she asked.

"No," Teo said, and meant it. Fighting would have been something. Fighting would have been contact.

"You're under the car on a Saturday."

"I'm maintaining the car."

She looked at him the way women in his life had always looked at him eventually—with a kind of fond, exhausted recognition, as if he were a machine himself, well-built, running fine, impossible to open. "Sure," she said, and went home.

Under the car, looking up into the dark undercarriage where everything was bolted to everything else exactly as designed, Teo felt more at peace than he had all week. There was no ambiguity under a car. Things either held or they didn't. You could see the rust starting before it became a problem. Nothing under there was pretending.

* * *

His mother had kept a house like a held breath.

That was the phrase that came to him sometimes, unbidden, usually when he was doing dishes or driving home in the dark—his mother's house, the one in Fresno, the one where nothing was ever wrong out loud. Elena Vasquez had run that household the way a dam operator runs a reservoir: constant, invisible vigilance, everything measured against the possibility of failure. His father's moods were the flood season. When Ramón's voice rose—and it rose about money, about the truck, about Teo's grades, about anything that could be made to bear weight—Elena would begin, very quietly, to do things. Refill his glass. Turn down the radio. Start a small repair in another room, something with a screwdriver, something that required her to be elsewhere.

Teo learned to read her repairs like other children learn to read faces. The year he turned twelve, she regrouted the entire bathroom tile over four weekends, and it wasn't until years later that he understood those were the weekends his father had threatened to leave. The grout was beautiful. The grout was perfect. Nobody ever mentioned why.

When his mother died—eight years ago now, quick and unfair, a stroke at fifty-nine—Teo had gone through her house with his sister and found, in the drawer under the sewing machine, a spiral notebook where she had written lists. Not letters. Lists. *Fix screen door hinge. Ask Ramón about the insurance paper. Ask Ramón.* The same line, *ask Ramón*, written eleven times across six pages, each time in slightly smaller handwriting, as though the sentence itself were shrinking, trying to take up less room in the world.

She had never asked him anything. Teo had sat on the floor of that sewing room holding the notebook and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that his mother had spent thirty-one years translating love into maintenance because maintenance was the only language the house permitted. And then he had driven back to his own house, where his wife thanked him for dinner every evening in a voice like a well-kept bridge, and he had not changed a single thing.

Because here was what nobody told you about inheriting silence: it doesn't feel like inheritance. It feels like competence. It feels like being good at your life. You oil the hinges and you pack the bearings and you refill the glass, and you tell yourself this is what a strong man does, and the alternative—sitting down across from someone and saying *I am afraid, are you afraid, what are we doing*—that alternative looks like weakness, looks like flooding the valley, looks like the thing his father did with his voice instead of his hands, which was worse.

So Teo maintained. It was the family trade.

* * *

The following week he cleaned out the gutters along the whole north face, replaced the wax ring on the hall toilet, bled the radiators in the bedrooms because they'd started knocking at night—a metallic tick-tick-tick through the walls, arrhythmic, like something tapping to be let out. He flushed the water heater, which hadn't been done since they moved in, and was quietly appalled by what came out of it, a gray mineral sludge, years of accumulation, the house's own swallowed sediment.

"Did you do something to the heat?" Paloma asked on Thursday. She was standing in the hallway in her coat, not yet out of it, listening to the radiator in their bedroom tick.

"Bled it," he said. "They knock when air gets in the lines."

"It sounds better." She touched the radiator fin lightly, the way she touched things—two fingers, precise, testing. Then she looked at him, and for a moment he thought she was going to say something else, something from wherever she'd been these three weeks. Her mouth opened. Closed. "I'm going to be late tomorrow," she said instead. "Grandmother's house. The estate stuff."

"Okay." He nodded. "You want company?"

"No—I mean, it's boring. Paperwork." She took off her coat finally, hung it on the hook, smoothed it flat with both hands. "Thanks for the radiator."

"Sure," he said. "It's what it needed."

And she smiled at him, that accurate, empty smile, and went upstairs, and Teo stood in the hallway listening to the radiator tick, warm now, steady, and thought: I fixed it. Whatever it was, whatever was knocking—I fixed it.

He almost believed it.

* * *

Saturday he washed both cars and sealed the driveway cracks with tar filler, working the caulk gun along the hairline fractures in the concrete, pressing black beads into every split. Hairline, the inspectors called them. Under a certain width, cosmetic. Watch and monitor. He'd heard Paloma use the word a hundred times on the phone, in bed, at dinner—*it's just a hairline fracture, we'll monitor it*—and she always said it with such calm that it sounded like reassurance, though lately he wondered if it might be the opposite. A hairline fracture was not a repaired fracture. It was a crack you had agreed, together, not to look at too closely.

He pressed the tar into the last crack and straightened up, and his lower back announced itself the way it did now, a dull complaint, forty-three years of maintenance taking its toll on the maintainer. From inside the house he could hear Paloma on the phone—he caught only fragments through the screen door, her voice low and careful. Something about a wardrobe. Something about boxes. Something about *no, I haven't told anyone yet.*

He stood on the freshly sealed driveway with the caulk gun in his hand and did not go inside and ask who she hadn't told anyone about. Because that was the deal, wasn't it? That was the architecture. He kept the gutters clear; she kept her own counsel; the house stood; nobody flooded the valley.

But that night, lying awake beside her at 1 a.m., listening to her breathe the way she breathed when she wasn't asleep either—too controlled, too even, sleep performed rather than inhabited—Teo stared at the ceiling and admitted something to the dark, in the wordless way men like him admitted things:

There was a crack in the load-bearing wall. He could feel it the way he felt the wheel ticking, the way he felt the gutter drip—something small and rhythmic and wrong, something that would hold for now, that would probably hold for years, that would absolutely fail someday, catastrophically, without warning, unless somebody opened their mouth and said the thing.

He reached over in the dark and found her shoulder. Just rested his hand there. She didn't stir, or pretended not to.

Tomorrow, he thought. Tomorrow I'll ask her what's wrong, and this time I won't accept *tired.*

In the morning he re-caulked the bathroom tub instead, and Paloma drove to her grandmother's empty house with a box of unsent letters hidden under a blanket on the back seat, and neither of them said a word about any of it.
