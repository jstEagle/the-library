## 9. Cousins

### Paloma

The invitation went out the way her family always issued invitations: as a fait accompli wrapped in a courtesy. *Sunday, one o'clock, Tía Rosa's. Your uncle is making the ribs.* No question mark anywhere in it. Paloma had replied *we'll be there* and then spent three days deciding what she would and would not say, drafting and revising the way she drafted inspection reports—every sentence load-tested before it was allowed to bear weight.

She dressed carefully, which was itself a tell. She chose the gray blouse with no jewelry, armor for a civilian occasion. In the car Teo drove with one hand on the wheel and the radio low, and she watched the neighborhoods slide past and thought: *I have carried a box in my trunk for two weeks and told no one but Rosa, and Rosa told me to burn it, and I did not burn it, and now I am going to lay it on a table where people are eating ribs.*

"You're doing the thing," Teo said.

"What thing."

"Where you rehearse. Your jaw moves a little. Like you're chewing words before you swallow them."

She almost laughed. "That's a new one. Usually you notice the gutters."

"I notice everything," he said, not bitterly, not warmly, just as a fact, the way he'd say *the brake pads are at three millimeters.* "I just don't always know what to do with it."

She looked at his profile against the passing storefronts—the same jaw his father had, the same careful hands—and felt the strange tenderness that had been arriving lately without warning, like weather. "Today," she said, "just stay near me. That's all I need."

He glanced over. "Near you. I can do that."

* * *

Rosa's house smelled of cumin and cut grass and the particular lemon polish she had used on every surface of every home she'd kept since 1981. The backyard table was already set when they arrived—twelve places, cloth napkins folded into fan shapes, the good glasses out because the cousins' generation had graduated, years ago, from paper cups to *the good glasses* without anyone marking the occasion. Rosa moved among the chairs adjusting them by centimeters, and Paloma watched her aunt's hands and thought: *she has been arranging things her whole life. She arranged my grandmother's life. She arranged it out of love, and love did not make it less of an arrangement.*

"Paloma." Rosa kissed her cheek, held her shoulders a half-second longer than necessary. Her eyes did the thing they'd been doing since the wardrobe—searching, warning, pleading all at once. *Not today,* the eyes said. Paloma smiled and said nothing with her mouth, which in this family was a full sentence.

The cousins arrived in their usual order. Marisol first, always first, with her deviled eggs and her two boys who went straight for the hose. Then Beto and his new wife, who was still in the phase of laughing at everything, bless her. Then Dani.

Daniela Sandoval-Reyes—no relation, as far as anyone knew, to any Reyes worth mentioning, though the coincidence had made Paloma's skin prickle the first time she'd read the name Aurelio Reyes in the county archive—came through the gate the way she came through everything, slightly late, slightly rumpled, carrying a bag of ice she'd clearly bought on the way because she'd forgotten she'd volunteered to bring it. She was thirty-nine, a hospice nurse, divorced, funny in the dry register that Paloma trusted more than any other register in the family. She kissed Paloma's cheek and said, quietly, against her ear: "You look like you're about to give a deposition."

"Maybe I am."

Dani pulled back and studied her. "Huh," she said, and got a chair.

* * *

The lunch went the way lunches went. Marisol's boys soaked each other and were made to apologize and soaked each other again. Beto told the story of his transmission, which had become an epic with installments. Rosa brought out the ribs and the rice and the ensalada rusa she made from a recipe written in her mother's hand, and everyone said it was the best one yet, and it always was, because it was always the same.

Paloma let it run for an hour. She ate. She passed things. She laughed at Beto's transmission. And all the while she was watching the table the way she watched a deck under load—noticing where the weight went, where the structure flexed, where someone had shimmed something years ago and the shim had become part of the building.

Here was the thing she saw, and had always seen, and had never once said: there were subjects that moved through this family like water around stones. Nobody forbade them. Nobody had to. Grandfather Ernesto's temper—around it. The year Tío Chuy's first marriage ended—around it. Money—around it. And threaded through all of it, underneath all of it, the glasshouse itself, which sat on the last parcel of family land like a patient in a coma, spoken of only in the future tense: *when they tear it down, when the county takes it, when we finally sell.* Never the past tense. Never *when Abuela used to grow things there.* Never *who worked there.*

Paloma set down her fork.

"Can I ask something?" she said. "Kind of a strange question."

The table did the thing it always did when someone opened a door that was supposed to be painted shut: it went attentive and wary at once, twelve faces turning with the synchronized caution of people who had learned that questions in this family were rarely just questions.

"Ask," Rosa said, in the tone of a woman granting permission she could not refuse to give.

"When you were kids," Paloma said, "did any of you ever get the feeling there was something we didn't talk about? Not a secret exactly. More like—shape. Like you could see where something used to be because everything else bent around it."

Silence, but a specific silence. Not the silence of confusion. The silence of recognition.

Marisol laughed, one note, automatic. "Paloma, it's Sunday, don't get philosophical—"

"I mean it," Paloma said, gently, and looked at Dani. "Dani. You were the one who was always at Abuela's. You spent summers in that house. Did you ever feel it?"

Dani had gone still. She was turning her good glass slowly by its base, a quarter-turn at a time, and Paloma watched her decide something. Dani had spent twenty years in rooms where people were dying, Paloma remembered. She knew what it looked like when a family arranged itself around a truth nobody would name. She'd sat with dozens of families doing exactly that in exactly those chairs.

"Yeah," Dani said. "Yeah, I did."

"Dani," Rosa said.

"I did," Dani said again, and her voice was steady, and she did not look at Rosa, which was its own kind of answer. "There was a whole part of the yard we didn't go in. There was a door in the glasshouse that was kept locked even when everything else was falling apart. And Abuela—" she stopped, and her jaw worked, and Paloma saw her choose the words with care, laying each one down like a plank over a gap. "Abuela used to sit on the back steps in the evening and look at it. Not sad, exactly. More like—like she was waiting for something that had stopped coming. And when any of us asked what she was looking at, she'd say *nothing, mija, come inside,* and we'd come inside, and that was that. I was maybe ten and I already knew that *nothing* was a load-bearing word in that house."

"*Load-bearing*," Beto repeated, half-laughing, uncomfortable. "It's a garden shed, Dani."

"It's not a garden shed," Paloma said. "It's a glasshouse. It was built to grow things."

"Then why's it full of nothing?" Dani said, and the table went quiet in a different way, because the question had landed somewhere none of them had braced for.

* * *

### Dani

Dani had known this lunch would come, or one like it, ever since Rosa called her three weeks ago in a voice Dani had never heard from her—flattened, careful, a voice reading from a script it hadn't written. *Paloma found some things at your grandmother's house. I'd rather you heard from me that there are things.* Not what things. Not whose things. Just *things*, the noun doing all the work a noun could be made to do.

She'd thought about it for three weeks, the way she thought about everything, which was to say she'd thought about it while driving, while charting, while sitting with the dying and the families of the dying, and what she kept coming back to was a hospice truth she'd never said out loud at a family table: *the secret always comes out at the end, and the only question is whether anyone's left who remembers it kindly.*

Her father had been Ernesto's brother. She'd grown up half in this family and half outside it, close enough to see the machinery, far enough not to be load-bearing in it herself. That was a gift, she understood now. She was one of the few people at this table who could afford to tell the truth, because almost nothing would be taken from her.

She looked at Rosa. Rosa, who had raised half of them in one way or another. Rosa, whose hands were folded on the tablecloth in front of her like a closed book.

"Aunt Rosa," Dani said, "I don't want to blow anything up. I want you to know that. But Paloma asked a real question and I'm not going to pretend I don't have a real answer."

"You don't know what you're—"

"I know I was ten years old and your mother looked at that glasshouse the way my patients look at photographs of people nobody in the room will mention. And I know that when I was fifteen I found a photograph in the drawer of the hall table—everyone in the family in front of the glasshouse, 1968, and there was a man in it. Standing apart. Not cut out, not torn, just—standing apart. And I asked whose friend that was, and my father said *nobody,* and the speed of it, Rosa—the speed of *nobody*—that's how I knew it was somebody."

Beto's wife had stopped laughing at everything. Marisol's boys, mercifully, were at the far end of the yard with the hose. The table had become the thing Dani had sensed her whole childhood: a surface arranged around an absence, everyone's elbows unconsciously avoiding the empty place.

"Paloma," Marisol said slowly, "what did you find? Rosa said you found *things.* What things?"

Paloma looked at Rosa first. Dani watched that look pass between them—watched Rosa's face do the arithmetic of damage, watched Paloma's do something else, something Dani could only call *decision.*

"Letters," Paloma said. "Forty years of letters. Written by Abuela Mercedes. Never sent. They were behind her wardrobe, in a box, and they were addressed to a man named Aurelio."

The name went through the table like a crack through ice. Dani heard Beto's breath catch. She saw Marisol's hand go to her mouth, not in shock exactly—in *recognition*, the face of a woman hearing a word she'd heard once, decades ago, through a door.

"Aurelio," Marisol whispered. "The gardener. Mama said—the gardener who—"

"Who what," Dani said. "Who what, Marisol?"

"Who left. Who was let go. I was little. I remember a man with dirt under his nails who taught me the names of the flowers, and then one day there was no man, and when I asked, Mama said *he went back where he came from,* and I thought that meant he died. I thought that for years. I was afraid of the glasshouse because I thought there was a ghost in it." She laughed, wet and awful. "I wasn't wrong, was I. There was a ghost in it. We were all just standing in it."

"That's enough," Rosa said, and stood, and her chair scraped, and for a moment the whole table looked up at her—five feet two inches of a woman who had held this family together with her hands for fifty years—and Dani saw that Rosa was not angry. Rosa was *afraid.* It was the first time Dani had ever seen fear on her aunt's face, and it was so naked, so sudden, that it did what no argument could have done: it made the secret visible. You could see its shape now, in the outline of Rosa's fear, the way you can see a body under a sheet.

"Rosa," Dani said quietly. "Sit down. Please. Nobody's attacking you."

"I know what this looks like—"

"It looks like love," Dani said. "That's the hell of it. It looks like it was love the whole time, and it still hurt everyone, and nobody got to say so. That's what it looks like to me. I sit with dying people, Aunt Rosa. I know what an unspoken thing costs. I've watched it cost everything, at the end, when there's no time left to say it. Abuela Mercedes had time. She had forty years of time, and she wrote it all down, and she never sent it, and now she's dead and we're the ones holding it." She spread her hands. "I don't want to punish anyone. I want to not do this at *my* table, with *my* kids, if I ever get any. I want it to stop being inherited."

The word landed. Dani saw it land—in Paloma, especially, whose face went through something fast and private, the look of a woman hearing her own thought in someone else's mouth.

* * *

### Teo

Teo had stayed near her. That had been the assignment, and he had done it: seated at her right hand, one elbow's length away all afternoon, saying little, watching everything, the way he watched everything. He had watched Paloma open the door she'd been building toward all week. He had watched the family flinch, and rally, and flinch again. And now, as the lunch broke apart into the smaller weathers of a family afternoon—Marisol collecting her boys, Beto and his wife making their early exit with the too-quick warmth of people escaping, Rosa standing at her kitchen sink with the water running over dishes that didn't need washing yet—Teo stood beside his wife at the edge of the yard and understood, finally, the shape of what she'd been carrying.

"You knew," he said. "Before today. You've known for weeks."

"Two weeks and three days," Paloma said.

He almost smiled. "You counted."

"I count everything. You knew that when you married me."

"I did." He looked out at the yard, at the cousins' cars, at Rosa's rigid back at the sink. "My mother did this," he said, and heard his own voice come out lower than he intended. "Not letters. But there was a room in our house in Fresno I wasn't allowed in after I was eight. My father's things. And she'd stand in the hall outside it sometimes, just—looking at the door. And I'd ask what's in there, and she'd say *boxes,* and I'd ask what's in the boxes, and she'd say *nothing that concerns you.* And I believed her for thirty years. It was only after she died, when I had to clear the house, that I opened them." He paused. "Photographs of a sister nobody ever mentioned. A whole sister. With a whole life. My father had a sister who died young and my mother kept her like a wound and never once said her name to me."

Paloma turned to look at him fully. "You never told me that."

"You never asked what was in the room I don't go in either." He said it without accusation—he'd rehearsed it without accusation on the drive over, and was surprised to find it came out that way anyway. "I'm not blaming you, Paloma. I'm saying I recognize the architecture. Silence is a thing people build on purpose and then their kids grow up living in it, thinking it's just—how a house is."

Dani came across the grass toward them then, keys in hand, and stopped close, and looked at Paloma with an expression Teo could only describe as *co-conspiratorial.*

"Eleven weeks," Dani said. "That's what the county notice says. Beto was talking about it at the far end of the table before you two got here—demolition's confirmed, they're taking the parcel for the drainage project. Eleven weeks, and then that's it. Whatever's in that glasshouse gets bulldozed with it."

"I know," Paloma said. "I've read the notice."

"Have you done anything about it?"

"I'm going to."

Dani held her eyes a moment, then nodded, once, like a nurse confirming a dose. "Good. Because I'll help. Whatever you're going to do—call the county, make noise, whatever it is—I'll help. And Marisol will too. She'll pretend she won't, but she will. She's been afraid of a ghost since she was six, Paloma. She'd like to meet the man."

She squeezed Paloma's arm and left. The gate clicked shut behind her.

Teo and Paloma stood alone in the shrinking afternoon, the ribs smoke still hanging in the air, and through the kitchen window he could see Rosa at her sink, hands still, water running.

"Eleven weeks," Teo said.

"Eleven weeks," Paloma said. "I've spent my whole career certifying structures. Telling people what's safe and what has to come down. I think it's my turn to file something."

"What something?"

She looked at him, and there it was—the thing he'd been watching come back to her face over these weeks, the thing that had been missing so long he'd stopped noticing its absence until it returned: *purpose,* plain as daylight.

"A delay," she said. "An official one. In writing. With my name on it."
