## 20. Panels

The glasshouse at seven in the morning looked like something the sea had tried to swallow and given up on. Fog still clung to the lower panes, and the light came through the intact sections in long amber blades that fell across the gravel path and lay there, patient, as if they had been waiting decades for someone to walk through them.

Paloma stood at the door with a clipboard she had promised herself she wouldn't use, and used anyway.

She had spent her professional life walking into structures that other people had abandoned, and she knew the ritual: stand at the threshold, let the building tell you what it was before you told it what you wanted it to be. Most structures lied. They held their posture and concealed their rot. The glasshouse was not lying. It showed her everything—the rust blooming orange along the lower frame rails, the two panels near the south wall gone entirely, the sag in the ridge beam that she had already measured twice and would measure again because the number mattered, because numbers were how she had loved things her whole life.

But this morning she left the clipboard hanging from her hand and just looked.

Her grandmother had stood here. That was the sentence she was still learning to carry, six days after reading the last letter, six days after Rosa's voice had cracked apart in front of the whole family and put the words down on the gravel like something heavy being set at last into a proper resting place. *Let someone know I was here.* Mercedes Sandoval had stood in this exact doorway, probably, watching Aurelio Reyes move among the benches, and had said nothing, and had gone home, and had written it all down instead, forty years of it, into a box that no one was meant to find.

*Well,* Paloma thought. *We found you.*

The crunch of tires on the drive. Teo's truck, and behind it Dani's hatchback with Lucía's borrowed ladder strapped to the roof at an angle that made Paloma's professional soul flinch. She watched her husband get out, coffee thermos in one hand, work gloves already on, and she felt the thing in her chest that was still new, still unpracticed: not the ache of measuring distance between them, but the simpler warmth of watching someone arrive when you'd asked them to.

Teo had not asked her if she was sure. That was the gift of it. Last week, when he'd slid the heritage preservation filing across the kitchen table with his own signature already on it, he had said only, "You shouldn't have to spend yours alone." She had cried, briefly, undramatically, and then they had eaten dinner like two people who had recently remembered how.

"Ridge beam's worse than I thought," she said, by way of good morning.

"Morning to you too," Teo said, and kissed her cheek, and looked up at the sag with the expression of a man who had spent thirty years fixing things and had finally been handed one worth fixing.

* * *

They worked in the order Lucía proposed, because Lucía had spent two years running her father's nursery and understood plants and structures the way Paloma understood bridges: as things that told the truth about how they were held up.

"Glass first," Lucía said, unloading panes from the hatchback, each one wrapped in moving blankets. "My father always said you don't repair a frame you can't see through. You fix the glass, you find out what the frame is really doing."

Dani, kneeling to unwrap the first pane, laughed. "That's either very wise or something he said to avoid paperwork."

"Both. He was my father."

Paloma watched Lucía say it. Two weeks ago, Lucía Reyes had opened her door to a stranger holding a box of her father's unsent letters—the letters he had received, the ones Mercedes had written that he had answered and never mailed—and had stood in her kitchen with her arms crossed like a woman deciding whether to let weather in. Now she was here, in work boots and her father's old canvas jacket, and when she touched the glasshouse frame her hand lingered on the welds, checking them, and Paloma understood that Lucía was touching the place where her father had worked for eleven years, the place from which he had been erased. She was reading it the way Paloma read steel. Looking for the point of failure. Finding, maybe, the point of holding.

"Careful with the corner panes," Lucía called. "Those are the original glass. You can tell by the ripple—old glass was poured, not floated. Look." She held a salvaged pane up to the light and the morning bent through it in slow waves, like water remembering itself. "Everything that ever grew in here saw the sun through ripples like that."

Dani stood beside her, and the two women held the pane between them for a moment, and Paloma felt something turn over in her chest. Dani, who at the family lunch had said the silences were *arranged around the table like furniture*—Dani, who had spent her whole life sensing the shape of the thing no one would name—was now holding a literal piece of the arrangement in her hands and choosing, deliberately, to put it back where it belonged.

Not to restore the silence. To restore the structure that had held a life the silence had tried to erase.

There was a difference. Paloma was beginning to understand it was the whole difference.

* * *

By noon the fog had burned off and the work had found its rhythm, and Teo, who had spent his marriage expressing love through maintenance and been misread for it, discovered that maintenance performed in company was something else entirely. It was conversation. He and Dani scraped rust from the lower rails while talking about nothing—her kids, his mother's silences, the terrible coffee from the thermos—and the talking was easy because their hands were busy, and Teo thought: *this is how men like me were built to communicate, and no one ever gave us something worth building together.*

Until now.

He was fitting a new pane into the south wall—the second replacement, the first one Paloma had seated that morning with a suction cup and a surgeon's patience—when Lucía came to stand beside him.

"My father talked about this place," she said, not looking at him. "At the end. He had dementia, the last year, and he'd forget the present but the past got very loud. He'd tell me about the benches, the heating pipes, the misting system he designed. He never said your family's name. He'd just say *the glasshouse* like it was a person he'd lost."

Teo set the pane down carefully. "What did he say about leaving?"

Lucía was quiet long enough that the sound of Dani's wire brush filled the space. "He said he was asked to go and he went. That's all he ever said. But he kept writing to her for forty years, Teo. Forty years of letters he never mailed, sitting in a shoebox in his closet, and I found them after the funeral and I didn't understand a single one of them until your wife knocked on my door." She turned and looked at him, and her eyes were Aurelio's eyes—Paloma had the photographs now, she knew—and there was no accusation in them, only the tired clarity of someone who had done her own accounting. "I used to think he was a coward. Now I think everyone in this story was brave in the only direction they were allowed to be brave, which was inward. I'm done being brave inward. I'm here with a ladder and a truck. That's outward."

Teo nodded, because there was nothing adequate to say, and then he said the inadequate thing anyway: "I'm glad you're here."

"So am I," Lucía said, and picked up the other end of the pane.

* * *

They seated the south wall's last new pane at golden hour, all four of them, two on ladders and two below, guiding it into the frame with the slow collective breath-hold that Paloma knew from crane lifts and bridge segment placements—the moment when the load transfers and everyone finds out if the math was honest.

The pane slid home. The clips took it. Dani whooped, actually whooped, a sound that would have been unimaginable at any family gathering Paloma had ever attended, and Lucía laughed, and Teo's hand found the small of Paloma's back, and Paloma looked through the new pane at the old glass opposite, ripple against ripple, new light bending through old waves, and thought: *this is what belonging is. It isn't granted. It's fitted. You cut it to size and you seat it and you press until it holds.*

She had spent her life believing she stood outside the family's architecture, inspecting it. She had been wrong. She had been a load-bearing element all along, carrying silence the way her grandmother had, the way Rosa had, the way Teo's mother had. The difference was that now she was carrying something else, something chosen, and her hands were on the glass.

They sat on the gravel at dusk, eating the sandwiches Dani had brought, the glasshouse behind them glowing faintly with the last light, more than half its panes whole now, the ridge beam shored with the temporary bracing Teo had rigged.

"Demolition crew comes Monday," Dani said. "Eight days."

"Seven and a half," said Paloma, who couldn't help herself, and everyone laughed.

"They'll see the filing went through," Teo said. "Reclassification. They'll stand around, confused, and then they'll leave."

"And then?" Lucía asked.

Paloma looked at the glasshouse. Somewhere behind those panels, scratched into a frame, two sets of initials still held their small claim against erasure. Somewhere in a box in her closet, forty years of a woman's voice waited to be read aloud.

"And then," Paloma said, "we finish it. All of it. Every pane."

It was Dani who went quiet first, her sandwich halfway to her mouth, her eyes on the drive behind them, where a car Paloma didn't recognize had stopped—where a woman in her sixties was sitting behind the wheel, not getting out, just looking at the glasshouse with an expression Paloma had last seen on her own face in a mirror, the look of a person arriving at a place they had only ever known secondhand.

"Paloma," Dani said quietly. "Who is that?"

The woman opened the car door and stood, and the evening light caught her face, and Paloma felt the gravel tilt beneath her, because she knew those features too—she had studied them in a photograph for an hour last week, a young man in a gardener's apron, and she had wondered, with the particular ache of the too-late, whether anyone on earth still carried his face.

Someone did.

"Lucía," Paloma said, and her voice came out strange. "Is that—do you know her?"

Lucía had gone very still. "No," she said. "But she's looking at me like she does."
