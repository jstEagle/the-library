## 1. Expansion Joints

The overpass above Avenida Sete breathed all night, and Ulyana Almeida was the only one who listened to it.

She knelt at the joint on the north span with a feeler gauge in her gloved hand, sliding the thin steel blades into the gap between the approach slab and the deck the way a dentist probes a tooth she already knows is failing. Four millimeters. Last March it had been three and a half. The concrete around the joint had begun to spall in pale crescents, exposing rebar that wept orange stains down the face of the pier like old rust-colored tears. She wrote the number in her field book — 4.0 mm, N-3, monitor quarterly — and pressed hard enough that the next three pages would carry its ghost.

Traffic passed beneath her in a long gray river. From up here the cars made no individual sound, only a continuous hush, like wind through wheat, like the ocean Tomás kept saying they should visit and never did. She had been measuring bridges for twelve years. She knew their languages: the tick of cooling steel at dusk, the groan of a bearing taking a load it was designed for, the higher, thinner complaint of one that wasn't. Bridges said everything out loud. That was the whole discipline, really — learning to hear what structures confessed, because they could not lie. Concrete cracked where it was hurt. Steel stretched where it was asked to bear too much. Nothing in a bridge had ever learned to keep quiet for the sake of someone else's comfort.

Ulyana closed the gauge, stood, and felt her left knee announce itself, forty-one years old and honest about it.

"Senhora Almeida!" The city's junior engineer, Rafael, was climbing the ladder from the maintenance walkway, clipboard against his chest like a shield. He was twenty-six and still believed problems were things you solved rather than things you documented. "The joint on the south side too? Or just north?"

"Just north. South is holding." She handed him the field book to initial, watching his pen hover over her figures as though numbers might be negotiable. They were not. Numbers were the only part of her life that behaved.

They descended together through the scaffolding smell of tar and pigeon droppings, and by the time her boots hit the pavement she had already stopped thinking about the bridge and started thinking about dinner, which meant thinking about everything she would not say at dinner.

* * *

Their apartment held the particular silence of two people who had gotten very good at not colliding. Tomás cooked on Tuesdays and Thursdays; she cooked the other nights or they ate late and separately, a choreography so refined it no longer required discussion. That evening he had made moqueca, the fish falling apart in coconut and dendê, and he set her bowl down across from his without ceremony, the way you'd set down mail.

"How was the overpass," he said. Not quite a question. Twelve years had worn the question marks off most of his sentences.

"Failing. Slowly." She broke off a piece of fish. "Four millimeters of movement at the north joint. It'll need monitoring."

"You always find something."

"That's the job."

He nodded and ate. The ceiling fan turned. On the wall behind him hung the photograph of his mother's farm, and beside it, face-down since some year neither of them named aloud, the frame they had never taken down. Ulyana knew what was in it without looking. You always know what's behind you in your own kitchen.

There were sentences she kept the way inspectors keep files — catalogued, dated, reviewed but never closed. *I miss you even when you're sitting right there.* *I don't blame you.* *I don't know how to be near you anymore.* *Do you remember what we were going to name her.* Each one had a millimeter measurement attached, some increment of distance she could have reported if anyone had hired her to inspect her own marriage. Quarterly monitoring. Movement noted. No intervention recommended.

Tomás looked up. For a moment — half a second, the duration of an expansion joint opening under load — something moved across his face, some pressure looking for a gap to escape through. Then it sealed again.

"The salt," he said. "Did you—"

"It's fine."

He reached for the salt anyway, and she watched his hand, scarred from the bakery ovens he'd left ten years ago for the desk job, and thought: *he is also measuring something. He is also writing numbers in a book I will never see.*

They finished the meal. He washed the dishes; she dried. Their elbows negotiated the small territory of the sink without touching. Outside, the city went on breathing its traffic-breath, and somewhere above Avenida Sete a four-millimeter gap opened and closed, opened and closed, doing honestly what they had learned to do silently.

* * *

The letter came on a Thursday, which was Tomás's night to cook, which meant it arrived while the kitchen smelled of garlic and he was out buying bread.

It was a heavy envelope, cream-colored, addressed by hand in ink gone slightly brown — the handwriting of someone who had learned penmanship before ballpoints. Inside, two pages and a check stub that made her sit down at the table before she'd read a word.

*Consórcio Hidrelétrico Vale Escuro*, the letterhead said. The dam consortium. The project she'd been reading about for years in the papers everyone else skimmed past: the great wall going up in the interior, the valley that would become a lake, the towns that would go under. The second page was a contract offer, and the language of it was so plain it took her a moment to understand why her hands had gone cold.

They wanted an independent structural assessment of the Viaduto do Rio Escuro — the old railway viaduct spanning the valley below the town of Santa Cruz do Rio Escuro — before the reservoir closed. Condemned, the file said. Unsafe since 1979. They needed a licensed engineer to certify its final condition for the record, for insurance, for the tidy paperwork of drowning a piece of history. Ninety days. Generous per diem. A signature line waiting at the bottom like an open mouth.

She should have shown Tomás. That was the thought that arrived first, arriving ahead of every other thought the way a foreman arrives ahead of the work. *We should talk about this. It's three weeks inland. It's the kind of thing spouses decide together.*

But underneath that thought, quieter, in the voice she used only with herself: *three weeks of hotel rooms and bridge decks and no one asking how my day was in a tone that means anything. Three weeks of gaps I'm allowed to measure out loud.*

She got up and found the atlas on the shelf between the dictionaries, the one they'd bought for a honeymoon they'd taken a different version of. The interior spread itself across two pages: brown and green, roads thinning to dotted lines, the river running south to north like a vein. Rio Escuro. Dark River. She put her finger on the town and held it there, and felt something in her chest shift by a fraction of a millimeter — movement noted, cause unknown, monitor closely.

From the kitchen window she could see the street, and Tomás coming up it with bread under his arm, and she had time — a full ten seconds — to call out to him, to hold up the envelope, to begin any of the sentences in her catalogue.

Instead she slid the letter back inside and set a cookbook on top of it, and when he came in smelling of evening air and yeast she said, "You're late," in the mild voice they had agreed on years ago without ever agreeing, and he said, "The line at the bakery," and the gap closed over them both, smooth as water.

That night she lay awake listening to the building settle — steel and concrete ticking as the temperature dropped, the whole structure confessing its small daily stresses to anyone who knew how to listen — and Tomás slept beside her with his back turned, a man-shaped landmass she had charted so thoroughly she no longer needed to look at it.

In the morning, before he woke, she signed the contract at the kitchen table. Her pen pressed hard enough that the ghost of her name would show through three pages.

She mailed it on her way to work. She did not tell him for eleven days, and by then she was already packed, and the lie of omission had hardened into something load-bearing, something she could stand on.

The consortium sent a driver. The road went west, then northwest, then into country where the dotted lines began, and Ulyana watched the city dissolve behind them in the mirror and felt the strangest sensation: not guilt, exactly. More like the moment a joint opens — a gap where there had been compression, admitting light, admitting cold, admitting whatever the structure had been holding apart.

Three hundred kilometers inland, a river waited to be drowned, and a viaduct waited to be measured, and neither of them knew yet what they were going to say.
