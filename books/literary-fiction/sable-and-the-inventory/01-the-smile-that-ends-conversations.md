## 1. The Smile That Ends Conversations

The piano was a Steinway B, seven years old, and it had been crying for months.

Cyrus Thackeray heard it before they were through the front door—the flat fifth in the midregister, the dampness swelling the soundboard, a string here and there that had given up its pitch like a person letting go of an argument. Most people heard a piano as one instrument, one voice. Cyrus heard it as forty-some individuals who had been asked to live together in a wooden apartment, and this one's tenor section was not getting along with anybody.

"Mr.—Thackeray, is it?" The woman of the house stood in the parlor doorway wearing the particular smile of someone who has hired help and wants to be seen hiring it. "We're so glad you could come. I'm Bethany Voss. My husband is upstairs on a call, but he'll want to say hello."

"Cyrus is fine," they said, and gave her the smile.

It was a good smile. Cyrus had been building it since they were nine years old, in kitchens that weren't theirs, across tables set by people whose last names changed every eighteen months. It wasn't warm, exactly. Warmth invited questions. It was something better: a smile that closed conversations the way a well-fitted lid closes a piano. *I am pleasant. I am fine. You needn't wonder about me.* People received it and moved along, relieved, the way you're relieved when an elevator door shuts on a stranger before small talk can begin.

Bethany Voss received it now. "Can I get you anything? Coffee? We have—"

"Just a glass of water, when you think of it. I'll be a couple of hours."

"A couple of hours! For tuning?"

"For listening." They said it mildly, already crouching at the keyboard, lifting the fallboard. "The piano tells me most of it. I just take dictation."

She laughed, a bright hostess laugh, and left them alone.

Alone was where Cyrus did their work. Alone, and inside the sound of things.

They began the way they always began: by sitting still on the bench with their hands in their lap, doing nothing that looked like work, while the house came forward to introduce itself. Every house did this, if you let it. A house was never quiet. It was an orchestra of small complaints—pipes contracting, joists settling, ductwork breathing—and each complaint carried information, the way a doctor reads a pulse. Most people lived inside these sounds the way fish live inside water, which is to say without ever once noticing the water. Cyrus had learned, early and out of necessity, to notice everything. In foster houses, noticing was survival. You learned to hear the argument starting two rooms away so you could be elsewhere when it arrived. You learned the difference between a door closing and a door being shut. You learned that silence has textures, and some of them are dangerous.

The Voss house spoke in a low, moneyed murmur. Radiant heat under the marble floors, so no radiators to read down here—that was a loss; radiators were the gossips of a house—but the pipes ran up through the walls like veins, and Cyrus could feel them through the parlor floor if they pressed their palm flat. Warm water moving. Somewhere above, a faucet ran and stopped. A dishwasher hummed its one long note. And underneath all of it, faint but unmistakable, from the floor directly overhead: footsteps. Two sets. Crossing and recrossing, never crossing toward each other.

Cyrus noted it the way they noted everything, filed it under *not mine*, and turned to the piano.

* * *

The work itself was simple and total. Raise the lid. Strip the felt off the pins. Mute the unisons with a felt strip, then test each string against the tuning fork—a small steel A, 440 vibrations a second, the one fixed point in a world of drift—and bring the wandering strings home one by one. It was meticulous, repetitive, holy. Four hours of it could pass like four minutes. There was no self in tuning, or rather there was nothing left of the self but attention, and attention was the closest thing Cyrus had ever found to peace.

The trouble was that houses kept talking while you worked.

By the second hour, Cyrus had the piano's whole biography. Bought new, played seriously for about a year—there were fingernail marks of enthusiasm on the keys' ivorine, a teacher's pencil annotations inside the lid. Then children, probably; the bench had been re-glued after a break. Then neglect, gentle but complete. The piano hadn't been opened in three years. Dust lay on the strings in an even gray fleece, except in one spot near the treble end where a hand had reached in recently—to retrieve a toy, maybe, or a dropped earring. Someone still touched this piano. Just nobody played it.

Upstairs, the footsteps continued their separate orbits.

At eleven-forty, the husband appeared. Cyrus heard him on the stairs—heavy heels, a man who walked like he was arriving somewhere even when he wasn't—and looked up to find a broad, ruddy face over an open collar, wearing a version of the same smile Bethany had worn. The Vosses matched. Couples usually did, eventually; they tuned themselves to each other like strings, or they went flat together.

"The piano man." He extended a hand. "Daniel Voss. Bethany said you'd want to talk shop, but I told her you'd probably rather work."

"I can do both," Cyrus said. The smile. The lid coming down, gently, on its own hinges.

"My father had this piano tuned twice a year, religiously. Man named Kowalczyk. Smoked these little cigars—" Daniel gestured vaguely at his own mouth, remembering smoke. "You don't hear of people keeping a piano like that anymore."

"No," Cyrus agreed, and turned back to the pins.

It was a deflection dressed as agreement, and it worked, because it was designed to work. Twenty-five years of practice. But Daniel lingered, hands in his pockets, and Cyrus felt the linger the way they felt a false beat in a waltz. Men like this didn't linger unless they wanted something, and what men like this usually wanted was not conversation but audience.

"You know what's funny," Daniel said. "We bought this house for the acoustics. This room especially. Bethany used to play—she was good, actually, conservatory good—and I thought, a room like this deserves music in it." He laughed, short and strange. "Now look at us. The thing's basically furniture."

*There it is,* Cyrus thought. Not a confession, exactly. An advertisement of a wound, held up the way people hold up a broken appliance: *does anyone know what to do with this?*

Cyrus knew exactly what to do with it. That was the terrible gift of it. Through the soles of their feet, through the parlor floor, through the warm veins of the house, they could feel the second heartbeat upstairs—Bethany, pacing a room she didn't leave much, judging by the wear pattern of the footsteps, a circuit roughly twelve feet by fourteen. And they could feel the absence around it: the way Daniel's steps avoided that quadrant of the floor entirely, the way the master bedroom door had developed a hinge-squeak from disuse, the way the plumbing told its own story—two showers run at opposite ends of the morning, never overlapping, two towels, two schedules, a marriage conducted through the building's infrastructure like prisoners passing notes.

The couple upstairs hadn't spoken—not really spoken—in weeks. Maybe months. The house knew it the way a body knows a tumor before the diagnosis. Every pipe in the place was bent slightly around the silence.

"They say a piano should be tuned when the seasons change," Cyrus said, which was true, and safe, and ended nothing and began nothing.

"Is that right." Daniel nodded at the window, at the October light going amber over the garden. "Well. Seasons change." He said it like a man testing a rope bridge with one foot, and then, mercifully, his phone buzzed and he took the exit it offered, climbing back upstairs to his call, his separate orbit, his own room.

Cyrus sat with the felt strip half-laid across the strings and listened to the house resettle around his departure.

This was the part nobody understood about the gift—or the affliction, Cyrus had never settled on which—which was that it demanded nothing and gave nothing back. They could read any room they entered. They could tell you which marriages were ending, which children were afraid of which parents, which grandmother's clock had stopped because the family couldn't bear to wind it. They carried other people's secrets around like a delivery driver carries packages: hundreds of them, addressed to other people, undeliverable. And the rule—the rule that had kept them alive through six foster placements and twenty-five years of adult employment—was that you never, ever opened the packages.

You smiled. You said *just a glass of water.* You tuned the piano and left the marriage exactly as structurally unsound as you found it.

It was a good system. It had worked for decades.

So why, Cyrus wondered, setting the fork against the pinblock and striking it, letting the clean A ring out into the amber room—why did the note sound so lonely?

* * *

They finished the treble section just past noon. Bethany brought the promised water—actually a glass of ice water with lemon, an upgrade, an apology maybe—and stood watching them work with her arms crossed, in the posture of a woman visiting a grave.

"It sounds different already," she said. "Even the notes I don't play."

"That's how pianos are. Everything's connected. You tune one string, the whole instrument shifts a little to make room for it."

Something moved across her face—quick, quickly suppressed, a fish under ice. "My mother used to say something like that. About families." She laughed. "Sorry. You didn't ask for that."

"I didn't mind."

But the smile was already up, the lid already descending, and she took the cue like everyone took the cue, retreating into hostess mode, asking whether he'd like lunch, no thank you, thank you, the check can go to the agency, lovely meeting you, lovely meeting you.

Cyrus packed the kit. Felt strips coiled, mutes racked, tuning hammer wrapped in its cloth like a sleeping animal. They closed the lid over the strings—gently; you never let a lid fall—and stood a moment in the restored, resonant quiet of the parlor, listening to the instrument settle into its new agreement with itself.

From upstairs, faintly, the two sets of footsteps continued their orbits. Never touching. A duet written for two players who refused to share a page.

Cyrus thought, not for the first time: *somebody should say something.*

And then, as always, as faithfully as sunrise: *not me. Not my house.*

They let themselves out. On the front step, in the cold bright afternoon, they checked their phone out of habit more than hope. One new message, from a number they didn't know:

*Mr. Thackeray — Halloway Estate Auctions. We have an upright piano in a house we're preparing for sale, older instrument, estate of a Mrs. Marlowe. Neighbors say it was well maintained. Can you come assess it Thursday? Address below.*

Cyrus read the name twice.

Marlowe.

The word landed in them like a stone dropped down a well—they heard it falling for a long time before it hit bottom. Their hands, professional hands, steady hands, hands that had not shaken in twenty-five years, went very still around the phone.

The street address beneath the message meant nothing to them. Addresses never did; Cyrus had lived in too many. But names stayed. Names were the hooks the world left in you.

Thursday. Three days. They typed *Happy to help* — the smile, rendered in text — and sent it before the smarter part of themselves could object.

Then they stood on the Vosses' cold front step for a long moment, listening to the house behind them hold its breath, and wondered why, of all the thousands of names that had passed through their ears, this one had come back.
