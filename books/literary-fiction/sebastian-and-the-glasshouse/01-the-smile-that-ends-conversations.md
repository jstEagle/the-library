## 1. The Smile That Ends Conversations

The word Vesper Crane was wrestling with at half past four on a Tuesday afternoon was *homesickness*, and the problem was that the language he was translating into had no word for it—not the clean ache of the German *Heimweh*, not even the serviceable English compound, but something older, something his mother had once called by a name that lived only in the valley where she was born and in the mouths of perhaps four hundred remaining speakers, most of them dead or nearly so. He sat in his office at the translation bureau on the fourth floor of a building that smelled permanently of toner and rain-damp carpet, and he turned the phrase over the way a jeweler turns a flawed stone, looking for the facet that would let light through.

*The letter speaks of homesickness*, the source text said, in the careful, stilted German of a woman writing to her sister in 1961. *But it is not the sickness of home. It is the sickness of a home that no longer exists to be sick for.*

Vesper wrote: *She was not homesick. She was homesick for a place that had already stopped existing.*

Then he crossed it out. Then he wrote it again. Then he underlined *already* and hated the underline, and hated himself a little for hating it, and reached for his pen—a fountain pen, an affectation his colleagues teased him about, though gently, the way one teases a man who is known to be harmless—and made a small notation in the margin in a language no one else in the building could read. His mother's language. His private shorthand, the last room in the house where he still let himself be a boy.

That was the thing about Vesper Crane, though no one at the bureau would have put it that way: he was a man who had made an entire career of standing in doorways between languages, holding them open for other people, and never once stepping through himself. Thirty years. He had translated novels and obituaries, corporate apologies and love letters, the testimony of witnesses and the last letters of the dying, and in all that time he had developed a reputation for a particular kind of excellence—the kind that leaves no fingerprints. *Invisible*, the senior editor had once said at a retirement dinner, raising a glass, and Vesper had smiled and understood it as the truest compliment anyone had ever paid him.

He heard Daniel coming before Daniel spoke. Daniel had a walk like an apology—quick, shuffling, slightly sideways, as though he were perpetually trying to get past someone in a narrow corridor. Vesper did not look up. He knew what was coming the way farmers know weather: Daniel asked him to dinner on the first Thursday of every month, and every month Vesper declined, and every month Daniel asked again as though the previous asking had been a dream they had both agreed not to discuss.

"Vesper. So—Thursday. There's this place on Kellerman Street, does this braised thing, lamb, you'd like it, everyone likes it—"

Vesper looked up.

He had spent decades perfecting the smile. It was not a warm smile, exactly, though it could pass for warmth in bad lighting. It was a smile with a hinge in it, a smile that opened just far enough to show goodwill and no farther, a smile that said *I see you, I appreciate you, and this conversation is now complete*. Other people had handshakes that ended conversations, or glances at their watches. Vesper had the smile. He deployed it now, holding it for precisely two and a half seconds—long enough to register as kindness, short enough not to invite response—and then returned his eyes to the page with the gentle finality of a man closing a gate.

"Right," Daniel said. "Thursday. I'll—well. If you change your mind."

"I appreciate it," Vesper said, and his voice did what his voice always did: it performed pleasantness without committing to anything. It was a voice like the smile. He had built it the way a mason builds a wall, one careful stone at a time, until one day he looked around and realized he had walled himself into a life with no doors, only windows, and even the windows were frosted.

Daniel retreated. In the corridor, Vesper heard him ask someone else—Priya, probably—and heard Priya say yes with the unguarded ease of a person who had never once translated her own personality before offering it to the world. Vesper felt nothing about this, he told himself. He felt the small, dry nothing that he had long ago learned to mistake for peace.

He finished the sentence. *She was homesick for a place that had already stopped existing.* He read it back and felt, briefly, without knowing why, as though the sentence were about him. This happened sometimes—words slipping their leashes and turning on their keeper—and he had learned to ignore it. He initialed the page. He capped his pen. Outside the window, the city was going amber at the edges, and somewhere below a bus sighed at its stop, and the day ended the way all his days ended: completely, without incident, sealed.

* * *

His apartment was on the top floor of a narrow building on a street where the trees had been planted by someone with more hope than planning, so that their roots now buckled the pavement like knuckles under skin. Three rooms, high ceilings, a radiator that knocked at night like a polite intruder. He had lived there for nineteen years and had unpacked perhaps sixty percent of what he owned. The remaining boxes sat in the corner of the bedroom with their tape gone brittle, labeled in his younger handwriting—*books, misc.*—and he had stopped seeing them the way one stops seeing a scar.

He worked in the evenings the way other men watched television: not because he had to, but because the work kept the silence at a manageable depth. The radio was part of the ritual. He kept it tuned to the late-night classical and spoken-word station out of habit inherited from his mother, who had believed that a house without a radio was a house where the walls could hear you think. The announcer's voice came and went like weather. Vesper barely heard it. He translated a chapter of someone's memoir about a childhood in a fishing village—more water, more leaving, more homesickness—and the pen moved, and the radiator knocked, and the city hummed its one long note outside the glass.

At 11:40, the announcer said something about listener contributions. *Unreleased recordings, home tapes, private archives—this is your music, sent in by you.* Vesper registered the words the way one registers a neighbor's cough through a wall: present, meaningless, filed.

Then the song began.

It started with a hummed phrase—just a voice, unaccompanied, a boy's voice or a young man's, slightly flat on the third note, and the recording was rough, tape hiss wrapped around it like gauze—and Vesper's pen stopped.

Not slowed. Stopped. Mid-word, the nib pressed into the paper, a small bloom of ink spreading out from the letter he would never finish, and Vesper Crane, who had spent thirty years translating everything, who had never in his adult life been at a loss for a word in any of his four working languages, sat in his kitchen with his mouth open and his heart going like a fist against a locked door.

He knew the melody.

That was not the remarkable part. Thousands of melodies existed; memory was a vast and undiscriminating attic. What was remarkable—what made the room tilt slightly on its axis, what made the radiator's knocking suddenly sound like knocking *for* him—was that he knew the melody had never been recorded. He knew it the way you know your own name. Because the boy who wrote it had written it in a glasshouse in the summer of a year Vesper did not let himself name, had hummed it into the wet green air between the tomato plants and the fogged panes, had said, *I'm going to call it something nobody's ever called a song before*, and had laughed, and the glass had steamed with July, and the light had come down through the roof in long yellow columns like the light in churches, only better, only truer—

Julian.

The name arrived in him like a stone dropped down a well. He did not say it aloud. He had not said it aloud in thirty years; it was the one word in any language he refused to translate. But it arrived, all the same, whole and heavy, and landed at the bottom of him with a sound he felt in his teeth.

On the radio, the voice hummed the phrase again and then began, tentatively, to add words—words Vesper couldn't quite catch under the hiss, words that might have been about a river or a road or a door—and the announcer came back over the top of it, bright and oblivious: *—found on an unlabeled cassette, sent in by a listener, and if anyone knows anything about this piece, we'd love to hear from you—*

Vesper reached over and turned the radio off.

The silence afterward was enormous. It had walls. He sat inside it, his hand still on the dial, and watched the ink bloom dry on the page, a small black planet with a pen for a moon.

It wasn't possible. The song existed only in one place in the world: in a glasshouse that had stood empty for decades, and in the skull of a man who had never sung it to anyone, never hummed it, never once—not to a lover, not to a colleague, not to himself in the shower—because to let it out was to let out everything it was buried under. He had kept it sealed the way you keep a room sealed. He had assumed that when he died, the song would die with him, the last speaker of a language of one.

And yet it had been on the radio. On a cassette. Unlabeled. *Sent in by a listener.*

He got up and stood at the window. The street below was empty except for a cat moving along a wall with the confidence of a landlord. His reflection looked back at him—gray at the temples, a face built for doorways—and for a moment he did not recognize it, or rather he recognized it too well: the face of a man who had arranged his entire life around the principle that some things are better left unsaid, and had called the arrangement professionalism, and privacy, and loyalty, and peace.

*If anyone knows anything about this piece, we'd love to hear from you.*

He went back to the table. He did not pick up the pen. He sat in the dark kitchen with the dead radio in front of him, and somewhere far down inside, under thirty years of careful sediment, the boy in the glasshouse turned over in his sleep and began, very softly, to hum.
