## 5. The Family Arrangement

Ines

The invitation came by phone on Thursday, in Marisol's voice, which was the family's voice, the voice that had called them to every birthday and funeral since Rosa died: warm at the surface, load-bearing underneath. *Sunday dinner, mija. Everyone's coming. You'll come too.* Not a question. It never was.

Ines said yes because saying no had a cost they'd never been able to calculate precisely but knew was ruinous. They spent Saturday doing what they always did before a family gathering — walking the house room by room with a tuning fork, listening for what had shifted. The parlor piano had drifted a quarter-tone flat in the bass; the upstairs bathroom pipe had developed a tick that sounded like a small animal tapping from inside the wall. And the trunk — the trunk sat where it had sat all week, at the foot of Rosa's bed, its lock hanging open like a jaw that couldn't close.

They had not opened it again. That was its own kind of discipline. They had lifted the lid once, seen the manuscript paper, the name *Silas* signed in a hand that slanted leftward like a man writing against a wind, and closed it. But the house had changed around the knowledge. Ines could feel it the way you feel a new weight distribution in an old floor — nothing visible, everything audible. The radiators knocked their pattern at night with what seemed now less like repetition than insistence. *You know. You know. You know.*

Sunday morning, Ines dressed carefully: the good shirt, the one without paint or felt dust, the shoes that didn't announce their arrival across hardwood. They were going into a room full of people who owned the house collectively and owned Ines's presence in it only by tolerance, and they wanted, childishly, to look like someone who belonged to the furniture rather than to the dust.

Marisol arrived first, as she always did, two hours early to commandeer the kitchen. By four o'clock the driveway held three cars and the smell of sofrito and roasting pork filled the hall, layering itself over the house's own smells of radiator dust and old varnish. Ines stood in the doorway of the kitchen watching Marisol work, this woman in her sixties who stirred with her left hand and salted with her right and never once looked at the stove, as if the food were merely an excuse for the standing there.

"You're thin," Marisol said, without turning. "Are you eating?"

"I'm eating."

"You look like your grandmother did, near the end. All ears and elbows." She said it with affection, and with something else beneath the affection, a measuring. "Sit. Peel."

Ines peeled potatoes at the counter while Marisol moved around them, and the domesticity of it was so complete, so practiced, that Ines almost forgot the trunk existed. Almost. Then Marisol paused in her circuit, just behind Ines's shoulder, and said, quietly, in the tone she used for telling people their parking had expired:

"Your tía saw the trunk open. When she brought the linens Tuesday."

The peeler stopped against the potato. Ines kept their eyes on their hands.

"It was open," Ines said. "The lock's broken. It's been broken for years."

"Mm." Marisol resumed stirring. "Well. Some things are better closed even when they're broken. You understand me."

It wasn't a question either. Nothing Marisol said was ever a question.

* * *

Marisol

Marisol had been cooking for this family for forty years and she knew, the way she knew when the pernil was done by smell alone, exactly how much truth a table could hold. The answer was: very little, served cold, in portions no larger than a compliment.

She watched Ines peel potatoes and thought: *Rosa's hands.* The same long fingers, the same careful way of holding a thing as if it might be asked back. It unnerved her, the resemblance. Rosa had been the keeper of silences the way other women kept recipes — memorized, guarded, passed down only in fragments. And now here was the grandchild, silent in the same key, and the trunk open at the foot of the dead woman's bed like a mouth.

Emilio would arrive at five. Emilio, who was ninety-one now and whose hands shook unless he pretended they didn't. Emilio, who paid for everyone's teeth and roofs and funerals and had never once, in Marisol's memory, explained why. She had stopped asking why decades ago. That was the arrangement — not written, never spoken whole, but understood by everyone who sat at this table: *the past is paid for, therefore the past is closed.* Emilio's generosity was the mortgage on all their tongues, and everyone had signed without reading.

Except, apparently, the piano tuner.

Marisol had known about the radio program before Ines had. Her daughter heard it driving home from work and called her, laughing — *Tía, they played some old song on the late show, the man said it was by somebody named Silas Reyes, can you believe it, same name as—* and then the laugh had died in her daughter's throat, because her daughter had been raised in this family too, and knew which names went back in the drawer.

Marisol had listened to the rebroadcast online at two in the morning, alone in her kitchen, headphones on so her husband wouldn't wake. The song was beautiful. That was the unbearable part. It was beautiful the way Silas had been beautiful — quick, aching, a little too much, always a little too much. She had sat with her hands flat on the counter and wept without making any sound, because sound carried in her house, and in her family, and in her life.

Then she had called everyone. Not to discuss it — God, no — but to establish the perimeter. *If it comes up Sunday, we say we don't know anything about it. There are a lot of Silases in the world.* And everyone had agreed in the particular relieved tone of people being handed permission to keep doing what they were already doing.

But Ines had the trunk. Ines lived in the house. Ines heard things in pipes, Rosa used to say — half joke, half warning. *That child hears the walls talk.* And Marisol had laughed then, forty years ago, at the dinner table, while Silas sat across from her with his head down over his plate, smiling at his plate as if it had said something funny.

She remembered that smile now, stirring the pot, and had to grip the spoon handle harder.

* * *

Ines

Dinner was served at five-thirty in the dining room, under the fixture with two dead bulbs nobody had replaced since Rosa died, because replacing them felt like an edit to a document. Eleven relatives. Ines counted them the way they counted beats — automatically, structurally. Tío Emilio at the head, propped in the chair with arms like a throne, ninety-one years old and still arriving last, still seated first. His sons flanking him. The cousins and their children distributed outward in descending order of age, and Ines at the far end, nearest the kitchen door, which was where they had always sat: close enough to serve, far enough not to be central.

Conversation ran on its usual rails: a cousin's promotion, a leaky roof, the indignities of a nephew's landlord. Ines ate and answered when addressed and let the talk wash past them, and the whole time they were aware of the house around them the way a musician is aware of a room's acoustics. Eleven bodies made the floorboards murmur. The radiators, off for the season, ticked occasionally as the afternoon cooled — random ticks, mechanical ticks, nothing like the pattern. But Ines found themselves listening anyway, translating, waiting.

It was Emilio who raised the subject, which surprised everyone, including, visibly, Marisol.

"The radio," he said. He said it mildly, dabbing his mouth with the napkin, a man commenting on weather. "Someone tells me there was a program. Late night. Playing old manuscripts. Unpublished things." He looked down the length of the table, unhurried, letting his gaze touch each face. "A lot of junk gets aired these days. Anybody can mail anything to a station. No verification. No standards." A pause, delicate as a knife laid beside a plate. "I turned it off after ten minutes. Sentimental nonsense."

Silence took the table in sections, like a cloth being drawn. The cousins studied their food. One of the sons refilled his water glass though it was full.

"Was it good?" asked the youngest cousin, Dani, nineteen, who had not yet learned the family grammar. "The sentimental nonsense?"

"Dani," her mother said.

"I just think it's interesting, old music nobody ever recorded. Like finding letters." Dani shrugged. "Like finding a diary."

"Dani." Sharper now.

Ines kept their eyes on their plate and felt the attention move through the room like a current seeking ground. It found them. Of course it did. Ines was the one who lived among the dead woman's things; Ines was the one with the trunk.

"Ines," Emilio said, and his voice was kind, genuinely kind, which was the worst register he owned. "How is the house treating you? Everything working? The plumbing, the heat?" He smiled. "Old houses talk. You have to know which conversations to join and which to let pass. Your grandmother understood that. She was very good at letting things pass."

"I've noticed," Ines said.

The words came out level, and Ines was proud of that, and terrified of it. Around the table, forks resumed, cautiously, the way birds return to a feeder after a noise.

* * *

Marisol

Marisol watched Emilio work the table and marveled, as she had marveled for fifty years, at how completely he controlled rooms without appearing to raise his voice. He had said *sentimental nonsense* and *letting things pass*, and every adult at that table had heard the full sentence underneath: *we know about the broadcast, we have decided it means nothing, and if any of you disagree you will be deciding it alone.*

But the girl — Dani — had said *letters*. *Diary.* Young mouths. The arrangement had always depended on everyone being old enough to remember why silence was necessary, and every year there were more people at the table who remembered nothing, who only inherited the habit of not asking. Habits wore out. Marisol could see the arrangement aging the way she could see a recipe failing — not in any single step but in the texture of the whole.

And Ines. Ines sitting at the end of the table with that stillness, answering Emilio in one flat line — *I've noticed* — and meaning, unmistakably, *I hear everything you're not saying.*

After dessert, Marisol cornered them in the kitchen, where Ines had gone to carry plates. She shut the door. The party noise dimmed to a murmur through the wood.

"Whatever you think you found," she said, keeping her voice low and pleasant, the voice for discussing someone's health at church, "you should put it back."

"The trunk was already open, Tía."

"I know what state the trunk was in. I'm talking about what you do next." She took a dish towel and began drying a plate that was already dry. "You live in that house because this family allows it. I fought for that, mija. Me. When they wanted to clear it out after Rosa died, I said, leave it, the girl needs time. So don't mistake a kindness for a lease."

"I'm not mistaking anything."

"Aren't you?" Marisol set the plate down harder than she meant to. "You go poking in trunks, calling radio stations—" She stopped herself. Too much. She had revealed she knew about the call, or anticipated it, and Ines's eyes changed — sharpened, the way Rosa's eyes used to sharpen, right before she said something devastating in the gentlest possible voice.

"Tía," Ines said quietly, "who was Silas to you?"

And there it was. The question, bare, in the kitchen, with pork grease cooling in the pan and the family murmuring twelve feet away. Marisol felt her whole body organize itself around the answer she had given for fifty years, which was *nothing, no one, a name from before,* and felt, simultaneously, the answer rotting in her throat like a bad tooth.

"He was my brother," she said finally. "And he is dead. And the dead are at rest, and the living are eating dinner in the next room, and that is the whole of it." She hung the towel on its bar with great precision. "Some songs aren't finished because finishing them hurts people. Did you ever think of that? A composer leaves a thing incomplete on purpose. Out of mercy."

"That's not how music works," Ines said. "Or houses."

"No," Marisol agreed, and her voice cracked, just slightly, on the second word, and she hated herself for it. "No, I suppose you'd know."

She opened the kitchen door and returned to her guests wearing her company face, and behind her she heard Ines stand motionless for a long moment among the dirty dishes, and then begin, softly, to hum — three notes, rising, the opening phrase of a song Marisol had heard only once in her life, played badly on a parlor piano by a boy who was dead, and which had followed her for fifty years like a debt.

* * *

Ines

Ines washed dishes because washing dishes was a way to exist at a family event without being in it. Through the window over the sink they watched the light go amber in the yard, and behind them the dining room murmured, and the house held all of it — eleven relatives, one secret, one open trunk upstairs — the way a bell holds a struck note, everything vibrating together whether or not anyone chose to listen.

Dani appeared at their elbow, drying towels, having escaped her mother's jurisdiction.

"What was that song?" she asked. "At dinner. Nobody was humming it, but everybody was thinking it. I could tell. It was like the room had a song stuck in it."

Ines looked at her — nineteen, unguarded, the only person at the table who had said the word *diary* out loud.

"Do you know who Silas Reyes was?" Ines asked.

Dani frowned. "The radio guy mentioned him. My mom got weird about it." She lowered her voice. "Is he, like, related to us?"

Before Ines could answer, the dining room quieted in a specific way — the quiet of a decision being announced. Emilio's voice carried through the doorway, mild and final:

"—so the realtor comes Wednesday to walk the property. Just preliminary. But it's time. The house shouldn't sit empty on one person's goodwill." A pause, and then, warmly, distinctly, pitched to carry into the kitchen: "No one needs to worry about anything. Everything will be handled. It always has been."

Ines dried their hands slowly. Upstairs, faint through the ceiling, a pipe ticked — once, twice — and then settled into the rhythm Ines had been hearing all week, patient as a metronome, unfinished as a held breath.

Wednesday. Two days.

Ines reached for the phone in their pocket and thought of the only person outside the family who had ever treated the mystery as worth solving — the man at the radio station, Tomás Vega, who had promised, in a voicemail Ines hadn't yet answered, that the anonymous manuscript had come with a return address.

Two days. Ines pressed play on the voicemail and stepped out the back door into the cooling yard, where no one in the dining room could see their face.
