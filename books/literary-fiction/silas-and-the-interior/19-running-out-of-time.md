## 19. Running Out of Time

The phone rang at 4:17 in the morning, and Ines knew before answering that the house had already told them.

It had told them at midnight, when the kitchen pipe gave a single low knock — not the rhythmic speech they had learned to read over these weeks, but a blunt percussive syllable, like a fist against a door. It had told them again at two, when the boiler cycled and cut off mid-cycle, something it had never done in all the years Ines had kept its rhythms the way other people kept calendars. And it had told them at three, in the long silence afterward, a silence with a shape to it, the shape of held breath.

So when the screen lit with Marisol's name, Ines sat up in the dark of Rosa's old room and let it ring twice, gathering themselves around the knowledge like a coat.

"Tío Emilio." Marisol's voice was stripped down to wire. "He collapsed at dinner. They're saying stroke. Left side. He's — he's at Mercy, he's stable, but he can't speak. The doctors say he may not—" She stopped. Ines heard her swallow, heard the particular acoustics of Marisol crying while trying not to, a sound Ines had catalogued across thirty years of Sunday dinners: a small wet click, high in the throat, quickly suppressed. "The closing is Friday. His lawyer says everything is signed except his signature, and now—"

"Marisol."

"Don't say anything about the house. Don't. Not today."

"I was going to ask if you've eaten."

A long silence on the line. Somewhere in Marisol's house a clock ticked, or maybe it was a pipe; Ines could not have said which, and the not-knowing felt like a small infidelity.

"No," Marisol said finally. "No, I haven't eaten." And then, quieter, almost to herself: "He was going to sign it Thursday. He had the pen in his jacket pocket. I saw him put it there."

* * *

Mercy Hospital smelled the way hospitals always did — antiseptic laid over something older and sweeter, the smell of many bodies and many fears — but Ines, walking the fourth-floor corridor toward Emilio's room, found themselves reading the building the way they read houses. The radiators along the corridor wall were original 1960s fin-tube, undersized for the renovated wing, running hot and hissing to compensate. The ventilation made a faint arrhythmic flutter above the ceiling tiles, a loose damper somewhere, opening and closing on its own schedule. A building working too hard to keep its promises. Ines thought: *this is what he sounds like now.* Then hated themselves for the thought, and kept walking.

Emilio lay small in the bed. That was the first shock — not the paralysis, not the tube beneath his nose, but the sheer reduction of him. All Ines's life, Emilio Reyes had occupied space the way furniture does, solidly, with an implied right to be where he stood. At family gatherings his chair had been the one at the head of the table even when no one assigned it; his voice had been the one that ended discussions not by volume but by a kind of gravitational finality. Now he was a shrunken man under a pale blue blanket, his left hand curled on the sheet like something washed ashore, his right — the good hand — lying open, palm up, as if waiting to receive.

His eyes tracked Ines from the doorway. There was recognition in them, and beneath the recognition something else that Ines had never seen there before and would spend the rest of their life trying to name: fear. Not of Ines. Of being *seen*, perhaps, at this scale. Or of what he still needed to say and no longer had the mouth to say it.

"Inés." Marisol rose from the vinyl chair by the window. Her eyes were swollen. Beside her sat her son Rafael, thumbing his phone with the grim diligence of a man managing logistics, and behind them, filling the corner, Emilio's eldest, Cousin Daniel, arms crossed, who nodded at Ines the way one nods at a contractor.

"The doctors are optimistic about rehab," Daniel said. "Speech therapy. Some patients come back from this."

Ines looked at Emilio. The old man's gaze had not left them. His right hand twitched on the sheet — once, twice — and then lifted, trembling, and made a gesture so small and so deliberate that Ines felt it land in their chest like a struck tuning fork: two fingers, tapping the blanket. Tap. Tap-tap. Pause. Tap.

The rhythm of it went through Ines like cold water. It was nothing. It was an old man's restless hand. And it was — almost exactly, though slower, degraded, as if played through a failing speaker — the opening figure of Silas's song. The four-note phrase the pipes had carried through the walls for weeks, the phrase that had started everything.

"Did anyone else see that?" Ines asked.

"He's been doing it all night," Rafael said wearily. "Tapping. The nurses say it's common after this kind of event. Motor stuff."

Tap. Tap-tap. Pause. Tap.

Emilio's eyes were wet. Whether from the stroke, from age, from something the body could no longer govern, Ines could not tell. But the fingers kept their time, patient as plumbing, insistent as a conscience, and Ines stood in the hum of the undersized radiators and understood that whatever confession Emilio had failed to make to his grandchildren, whatever partial justice he had offered the family council and then tried to buy back with a deed, some part of him was still keeping Silas's time. Had perhaps always been keeping it. Restitution in rhythm, if in nothing else.

"I'll come back tomorrow," Ines said, and meant it in a way they could not explain to the room.

* * *

The closing papers arrived Wednesday by courier, because the estate's attorney, ever efficient, had arranged a workaround: a bedside signing, a notary dispatched to Mercy Hospital, witnesses assembled. Marisol called to tell Ines this in a voice sanded flat by three days of hospital corridors.

"They need the house vacated by the end of the month," she said. "The buyers want to start renovations immediately. I know. I know what you're going to say."

"What am I going to say?"

"You're going to say the house isn't finished telling you things."

Ines stood in the parlor, phone to ear, looking at the wall behind which they had found the sealed room — the cot, the second manuscript, the letters addressed to no one. The plaster patch they'd cut was still visible, a pale rectangle like a scar, like a page torn out.

"Marisol, has anyone talked to the buyers about the wall? About what's behind it?"

"There's nothing behind it. We sealed it back up. It's plaster and studs and forty years of dust." A pause. "That's what the disclosure will say."

"And what will your disclosure say?"

Marisol exhaled, and Ines heard in it the whole architecture of the family arrangement — the love enforced as silence, the silence enforced as love, load-bearing for fifty years and now, suddenly, audibly, cracking. "Inés. Tío Emilio is lying in a bed that costs four thousand dollars a day. The sale pays for his care. Whatever you think you're doing — the letters, the radio man, the records office — understand what it lands on. It doesn't land on him anymore. It lands on us. On me. On Rafael, who just got laid off. On Daniel's kids' tuition. You want to burn down the story we all live inside, fine. But look around first and count who's standing in it."

"I've counted," Ines said. "I've been counting my whole life. I'm the one who was never counted."

"That's not fair."

"No," Ines agreed. "It isn't. That was rather the point of the arrangement."

They hung up gently, which cost more than shouting would have.

* * *

Tomás Vega came to the house Thursday evening, carrying a canvas bag and the particular energy of a man who has been awake for days on something between conviction and caffeine. He had brought printouts — court records, microfilm scans, the 1975 newspaper coverage of Silas's trial, grainy photographs of three young men outside a courthouse, one of them Silas, one of them unmistakably Emilio at twenty-six, handsome and unafraid.

"I've been sitting on this for a week," Tomás said, spreading the pages across the parlor piano's closed lid. "And I can't sit on it much longer. My producer wants it. The station wants it. And honestly? The story wants it. An anonymous manuscript, a hidden room, a wrongful conviction, a patriarch's deathbed — Inés, this is not a segment, this is a *series*. We air it Sunday. Prime slot. By Monday every person in this city who ever ate a tamale at an Emilio Reyes family reunion is going to know what he did."

"And what happens to the family?"

Tomás paused. He was a careful man underneath the momentum; Ines had liked that about him from the first meeting at the station, the way he handled the manuscript with white gloves and asked permission before photographing it. Now he took off his glasses and rubbed his eyes.

"The family takes the hit. Yes. Marisol, the cousins, the grandkids — they didn't do it, but they maintained it, and publicly that distinction gets lost. The buyers might walk. The sale might collapse. Your aunt will never forgive you." He put the glasses back on. "And Emilio — if he recovers enough to understand what's happening — gets to watch his name get dismantled from a hospital bed, without a voice to defend or confess or explain. Is that justice? I genuinely don't know. I've been asking myself all week."

Ines walked to the radiator under the front window and laid a palm flat against its fins. Warm. Steady. The house breathing evenly, for once, as if listening.

"When I testified at the county office," Ines said slowly, "when I filed the statement to reopen the record — I did it because the record was wrong. Because a dead man carried a crime that wasn't his, and the living built a comfortable life on top of his grave and called it gratitude. That hasn't changed."

"But?"

"But I keep thinking about what testimony *is*. I stood in a records office and swore to what the house told me, and it cost me — it may yet cost me this roof. And it mattered. It's in the file now. Anyone who looks, finds it. But almost no one looks. The record was corrected in a drawer." Ines turned. "Your broadcast would correct it in the air. In everyone's lungs at once. Those aren't the same thing, and I don't know which one Silas would have wanted."

"Can I tell you what I think?" Tomás gathered the photographs, squared their edges against the piano lid, a fidgeting, thoughtful motion. "I think Silas wrote that song knowing it would outlive him. He hid it in the walls. He mailed a copy to a stranger. A man who spends his last free months making sure his music escapes the house — he wanted it *heard*, Inés. Not filed. Heard."

Outside, dusk was coming down, and the boiler kicked on with its familiar ascending groan, and through the floorboards Ines felt the first faint knock of the evening cycle begin its rounds — tap, tap-tap, pause, tap — the house keeping Silas's time, as it had for fifty years, as it would until someone tore the pipes out or someone finally listened all the way through.

"Friday," Ines said. "The sale closes Friday. Give me until then."

"To decide?"

"To finish tuning the house. There's a resonance in the parlor wall I haven't traced yet — a sympathetic vibration I've heard twice and couldn't source. If Silas hid a room, he hid other things. And if I'm going to choose between a drawer and the air—" Ines pressed their palm harder against the warm fins, feeling the pulse come up through the metal like a second heartbeat. "I want to hear everything he left before I speak for him."

Tomás looked at the patched wall, at the piano, at Ines's hand on the radiator, and — to his credit, Ines would remember this — asked no questions. "Sunday," he said. "That's my deadline. After that it leaves my hands."

After he left, Ines sat alone in the darkening parlor with the lamp off, letting the house sound around them: the boiler's climb, the pipe-song rising through the joists, the four-note phrase repeating in the west wall like a man pacing a cell. Two days. One wall. One choice between the quiet correction and the public one, between protecting the living and restoring the dead — the same choice Silas had faced in this room half a century ago, and Ines knew now, with the certainty of water finding its level, that Silas had chosen wrong, or had been made to, and that the whole house had been ringing with the error ever since.

Ines opened the piano lid, set the metronome ticking at Silas's marked tempo, and began, note by note, to tune the instrument to the frequency of the wall.

Somewhere in the middle register, where the strings crossed into sympathy with the pipes, the wall answered — and this time the answer was not the song. It was something new. Three slow knocks, spaced wide, deliberate, from a spot low in the plaster where no pipe ran.

Like knocking. Like someone, at last, asking to be let out.
