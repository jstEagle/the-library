## 4. The Shoebox Under the Stairs

The attic smelled the way attics are supposed to smell — dust and cedar and something faintly sweet, like apples that had given up being apples years ago. Felix climbed the ladder with a flashlight in her teeth and a garbage bag over her shoulder, prepared to be efficient about it. That was the plan for the whole house: three days, four bags, one signature. Grief as logistics.

The attic defeated her immediately by being smaller than she remembered. As a child it had been an entire country — the territory above the world where Sila kept winter things and Felix was allowed to read anything, even the almanacs from before the war. Now it was a low room with a slanted roof she could touch standing up, and her adult shoulders knocked against the rafters like an apology.

She started at the far wall, because that was what you did. You started far from the stairs so you'd have somewhere to go.

There were boxes labeled in Sila's hand — that upright, patient script that had filled every margin of Felix's childhood homework. *WINTER COATS.* *JARS (EMPTY).* *FELIX'S SCHOOL.* The last one stopped her for a full minute, flashlight trembling slightly, until she made herself open it and find only spelling tests and a drawing of a horse that looked like a table. She laughed once, alone, the sound strange in the small space. The horse-table went into the keep pile. It was the first item in the keep pile. She hadn't known there would be a keep pile.

* * *

By the second hour she had established a system: trash, donate, keep, and a fourth category she refused to name, which was growing faster than the others. A hatbox of buttons sorted by color. A tin of foreign coins from countries that no longer existed. A photograph of Sila at maybe thirty, standing in the orchard with her sleeves rolled, squinting at the camera as if it had interrupted her mid-thought — which, knowing Sila, it probably had.

The light through the round window went from white to honey while she worked. Somewhere below, a board creaked — the house settling, or Marta checking on her again, or nothing. Felix had learned to stop flinching at sounds in this house. It had taken her twelve years to unlearn them in the city, and apparently only two days to relearn them here.

It was under the stairs — technically not under the stairs but in the knee-wall cubby beside the chimney, behind a panel Sila had always claimed was load-bearing and therefore off-limits, a claim Felix now understood to have been a lie of magnificent duration — that she found the shoebox.

It was a good shoebox, sturdy, the kind from the cobbler on Kossuth Street who had charged extra for sturdiness and been worth it. Her name was written across the lid in Sila's script: *FELIX VOSS,* and beneath it, smaller, as if the writer had hesitated: *NOT YET.*

Felix sat down on a trunk. The dust rose around her and hung there, gold in the window light, and she did not move for a long time.

Then she opened it.

Postcards. Stacked face-down, bound with string, and beneath the string a rubber band, and beneath the rubber band another rubber band, perished to powder, as if Sila had sealed them twice, in different decades, changing her mind and unchanging it. Felix lifted the top one.

Her own address. Her city address — the first one, the studio on Wessel Street with the radiator that sang. Written in full, apartment number and all, which meant Sila had asked someone for it, or written away for it, or memorized it from a letter Felix barely remembered sending. The postmark box was empty. Of course it was empty. None of these had ever traveled anywhere.

She turned it over. The picture side showed the harbor, the tourist harbor, gulls wheeling over water the color of hammered tin. And in Sila's hand:

*My Felix. The plums came early this year. I stood under the tree this morning and ate three before breakfast like a thief, and I thought, if she were here she would say —*

That was all. No signature, no date, just the sentence stopping as if the pen had been called away, or as if Sila had looked at the words *if she were here* and found she couldn't go on past them.

Felix's hands were shaking. She noticed this the way you notice rain starting — distantly, then all at once.

She counted. She had to count; her mind demanded arithmetic the way a drowning man demands driftwood. She dealt the postcards out on the trunk lid in stacks of ten, and when she finished there were fourteen stacks and four, and the number sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

One hundred forty-four. Twelve years. Twelve a year. One a month.

One a month, for twelve years, Sila had sat down — probably at the kitchen table, probably after lunch, in the hour Felix could almost see if she let herself — and written to her daughter-granddaughter across the distance, and then had not sent it. Had put it in a shoebox instead. Had written *NOT YET* on the lid and hidden it behind a panel she'd spent decades insisting was structural.

"You impossible woman," Felix said aloud, and her voice cracked in the middle of *impossible*, and she said it again anyway, in their language, the old word, the kitchen word, the one that didn't exist in any dictionary she'd ever translated from.

* * *

She read them in order, because order felt like the only respectful thing left.

March of her first year away: *The neighbor's dog got into the yard again. I told him off properly. He looked sorry. I thought you would laugh, because you always said —*

June: *Marta brought me tomatoes and gossip in equal measure. The gossip was better. She says the Varga boy is engaged, and I said, our Felix could have —*

October: *First frost. I covered the trees like babies. Your mother called, don't ask me why, and we spoke for nine minutes exactly, and afterward I sat in your room and —*

Felix stopped. *Your room.* She looked across the attic as if the house itself might explain. Sila had gone into her room. After phone calls. After frost. After plums. For twelve years, one afternoon a month, this woman had picked up a postcard of the harbor or the church or the orchard in bloom — Felix checked; yes, there was the orchard in bloom, twice, three times, five — and begun a sentence to her, and stopped it, and saved the stopping.

Because a goodbye mailed was a goodbye finished. The thought arrived whole, unbidden, in Marta's voice though Marta hadn't said it yet, and Felix pushed it away hard. No. This wasn't goodbye. This was the opposite of goodbye. This was —

This was love with the postage withheld. Love kept in escrow. Love that believed, insanely, stubbornly, against all evidence, that the recipient would come home and collect it in person.

*Not yet.*

Felix pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes until she saw constellations. She was thirty-four years old and she was crying in an attic over stationery, and some cold professional corner of her mind observed that grief, like translation, was mostly a matter of timing — of knowing which word to release and which to hold back, and Sila had held back everything, for twelve years, and now the sender was dead and the mail was undeliverable except by hand, by her, here, now, too late by exactly the width of a lifetime.

* * *

The light had gone amber to rust by the time she reached the bottom of the box. Her knees ached. She didn't care. There were eleven cards left and she dealt them like a losing hand.

The later ones were different. She saw it before she understood it. The handwriting had loosened, the upright patience sagging into something more tired, more human. And the sentences — the sentences were shorter. Not because Sila had less to say. Because the words were harder to find.

*The doctor is a fool with his machines. I told him what my own grandmother told me, that the body keeps its own —*

*I dreamed of the river. In the dream I could not remember the word for —*

*Marta says I should rest. Rest is a word for people who have forgotten how to —*

Felix read that one three times. *Forgotten how to.* Forgotten how to what? Live? Sit still? Speak? The sentence broke off and left a doorway with nothing behind it, and she sat in the dusty gold light feeling vertigo rise through her like water in a well — because she recognized this. She knew this decay from the inside. She had spent twelve years translating away from this language, letting it erode in her mouth word by word, substituting the convenient term for the true one, circling meanings she could no longer land on directly. And all that time, three hundred kilometers away, Sila had been going silent in parallel — losing words month by month, writing around the holes, breaking off where the floor gave way.

They had been doing the same thing. They had been losing the same language at the same rate, separately, like two people draining the same lake from opposite shores.

"No," Felix said softly, to the attic, to the rafters, to nobody. "No, you should have sent them. You should have sent every single one, you stubborn, ridiculous —"

But even as she said it she heard how it wasn't true, or wasn't only true, and the not-true part frightened her more.

Four cards left. Three. Two.

The last one was dated — she turned it toward the window, tilting it to catch the light — dated the third of March. Sila had died on the sixth. Felix knew the date the way you know a scar's shape: without looking.

The picture side was the orchard in bloom. The oldest tree, the one at the fence line, white with blossom, photographed by someone who knew what they were looking at.

*My Felix. If you are reading this, then you came home, and I am either very happy or very surprised, and knowing you, both. The tree is blooming early again. I sat under it yesterday and thought about the day you were born, how the blossoms came down in the wind like snow that wanted to stay, and I held you and I thought, whatever else happens, whatever she does, wherever she goes, I will leave the door —*

And there it ended. Mid-word, almost — the final stroke of the final letter trailing off as if the pen had simply been set down, gently, the way Sila set down everything, as if she'd intended to finish after lunch, after the frost, after next month, after *not yet* became *now.*

Felix sat in the darkening attic with the unfinished sentence in her lap and the whole weight of twelve years pressing down like snow on a roof, and downstairs, unmistakably this time, she heard the front door open and close, and Marta's voice calling up the stairwell — calling her name, and then, softer, in the old language, asking if she was all right.

Felix looked at the postcard. At the door in the sentence that never got built.

"Marta," she called back, and her voice came out strange, thickened, half in one language and half in the other. "Come up. There's something you need to see."
