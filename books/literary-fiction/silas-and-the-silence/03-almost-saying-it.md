## 3. Almost Saying It

There is a particular grammar to not asking a question. I know it the way I know English and Yoruba and the half-remembered French of my secondary school years — fluently, without thinking, in my body before it reaches my mouth. The grammar goes like this: you approach the subject sideways. You begin with something small and safe — the price of tomatoes, the leaking gutter, whether Baba has been taking his blood pressure tablets — and then, at the exact moment the real question rises in your throat like a bird startled off a wire, you change direction. You ask about the tablets again. You comment on the soup. You become, for the fortieth or four-hundredth time, a woman discussing soup.

I had been performing this grammar since I was eleven years old.

* * *

The first time was 1994, a Sunday, the smell of fried plantain moving through the house like weather. I had found a photograph that afternoon while hunting for my school sandals in my parents' wardrobe — a photograph of three young people standing in front of a building I did not recognize, a building with a signboard too blurred by sun damage to read. Two of them were my parents, but younger than I had ever seen them, younger than their own wedding pictures somehow, wearing clothes that belonged to no decade I could name. And between them stood a young woman with high cheekbones and a gap in her teeth when she smiled, her arm slung across my mother's shoulder with an ease that made my chest ache in a way I had no word for yet.

At dinner I put the photograph on the table between the rice and the stew pot.

"Baba, who is this?"

The silence lasted perhaps two seconds. Two seconds is nothing; two seconds is the pause before anyone answers any question. But I have thought about those two seconds so many times in the thirty years since that they have expanded inside me like a folded map, and now I can walk around inside them. In those two seconds my father's hand stopped halfway to his mouth. My mother's serving spoon went still above the pot. Tunde, seven years old and oblivious, asked for more meat.

"An old friend," my father said, and took the photograph, and looked at it for exactly as long as a man looks at a photograph of an old friend, and handed it back. "Where did you find this?"

"Your wardrobe."

"Ah." He nodded. "It has been there long."

And that was all. That was the whole of it. My mother resumed serving; the stew made its soft sound against the plate; Tunde demanded meat again with the moral urgency only a seven-year-old can summon. The photograph went back into the wardrobe, into the dark, and I learned — not consciously, not as a lesson anyone taught, but the way you learn the layout of your own house in darkness — that this was a door that did not open. That some questions in our family were not forbidden, which would at least have been dramatic, but simply *unavailable*, like rooms sealed during renovations, like words removed from a dictionary so thoroughly that even the space where they used to be is smoothed over.

I was eleven. I put the question away. I told myself I would ask again when I was older, when I was cleverer, when the moment was right.

The moment was never right. The moment is a country I have applied to visit my whole life and never been granted entry.

* * *

Now I stood in my childhood kitchen on the fourth day after my mother's funeral, rehearsing.

This is what grief does not tell you: it makes cowards of us precisely when we can least afford cowardice. You would think death would loosen tongues. You would think that standing in the house of a woman who had just been buried, surrounded by the archaeology of her life — her reading glasses on the windowsill, her wrapper folded over the chair back, the faint ghost of her perfume still living in the curtains — you would think all of that would make asking easier. Instead it made everything heavier. Every word I might say to my father now had to pass through the fact of her absence, and she had been, all these years, the one who managed the silence. She was the air traffic controller of our family's avoidance. Without her, I did not know if the planes could land.

Baba sat at the kitchen table doing what he always did at that hour: reading the newspaper he had already read, slowly, as though the news might have changed since morning out of respect for him. He was smaller than I remembered, or I had grown larger around the memory of him. His reading glasses sat low on his nose. His hands — my God, his hands had aged. When did hands do that? They lay on either side of the paper like two tired animals.

"Tunde is coming for dinner," I said.

"Mm." He turned a page.

"I'm making efo riro. The way Mama used to."

Another page. "She used too much pepper."

"She used exactly enough pepper. You are the one who drinks water like a goat."

He laughed — a short, surprised bark of a laugh, the kind that escaped him before he could decide whether he wanted it to. This was good. This was the safe ground, the familiar field: we could joke about pepper. We could joke about anything except the thing.

*Say it*, said a voice in me that sounded suspiciously like my mother. *August, say it. What are you waiting for? A written invitation?*

*I'm waiting*, I told the voice, *for courage.*

*Courage is not delivered*, the voice said, in her dry way, her way that I would never hear again except in my own head. *Courage is cooked. Like soup. With what you have.*

* * *

Tunde arrived at six-thirty with his usual entrance, which was less an arrival than a small weather event. He came through the front door talking — about traffic on Third Mainland Bridge, about a client who wanted a logo "that says innovation but also tradition but also youth but also gravitas," about the price of diesel — and by the time he reached the kitchen he had embraced Baba, criticized my chopping, stolen a piece of fried plantain from the cooling rack, and been slapped on the arm for it, all without appearing to take a breath.

"You've lost weight," he told me, looking me up and down. "Grief diet. Very effective. Very expensive."

"I haven't lost weight."

"Your face is thinner."

"My face is the same face."

"It's a thinner face. It's saying things your mouth isn't saying." He stole another plantain. "Anyway. How is the great cataloguing going? Have you finished indexing Mama's shoes? Please tell me there's a spreadsheet. Tell me my sister made a spreadsheet for shoes."

"There's a spreadsheet," I admitted.

"For shoes."

"The shoes are organized by decade and condition."

"By *decade*." He turned to Baba with the delight of a man presenting evidence in court. "You see what I grew up with? This woman alphabetized her teddy bears. By name. There was a filing system. I once moved Mr. Bubbles from B to T because I felt he identified as a tiger and she didn't speak to me for two days."

"He was a bear," I said. "He knew what he was."

We laughed, all three of us, and the laughter was warm and real and it was also, I understood with sudden terrible clarity, a wall. We built it together, brick by brick, joke by joke, the same wall we had been building for thirty years, and it was such fine workmanship that from the outside you would never know there was anything behind it at all.

Dinner was served. Efo riro, rice, the fried plantain Tunde had already partially consumed. Baba ate with the careful appetite of a man managing his blood pressure and his dignity in equal measure. Tunde talked — Tunde always talked; talking was his way of holding a room the way I held rooms by cataloguing them — and I sat with the question in my lap like a hot bowl I kept almost passing and then pulling back.

*Baba. Who was Adaeze?*

No. Too direct. He would close like a shop at closing time.

*Baba, I found some letters—*

Worse. Letters meant the study, and the study was locked, and mentioning the study was mentioning the whole architecture of the unsaid at once.

*Baba, at the wake, an old man—*

My hand actually opened toward my bag, where the memory of Mr. Okonkwo's grip still lived, where his words still sat folded like a note passed in class: *Tell Silas I saw him.* I had turned that sentence over for days now, the way you turn a stone in a stream. Silas. Nobody in my family was named Silas. My father was Babatunde Oyelaran — no, wait, that was Tunde's trick, we'd named the boy after him and then spent twenty years clarifying which Tunde was being shouted for. My father was Michael Oyelaran. Michael. A name as plain as bread. And yet the old man at the wake had gripped my hand with those papery fingers and looked at me with eyes that had been waiting a long time, and said *tell Silas I saw him*, and he had not been confused. Confusion does not look like that. Confusion does not hold on.

"—and I told him, I said, sir, if you want the logo to say 'disruption,' you cannot also want it to say 'trustworthy,' these are different—" Tunde paused mid-flow, fork in the air. "What?"

"Nothing."

"You're staring at Baba."

"I'm looking at Baba. There's a difference."

"There isn't, actually. Looking-at versus staring-at, in this family, is a very short distance." He pointed the fork at me. "You have the face. The almost-saying face. You've had it since you were small. Mama used to say—" and he stopped.

The stop was tiny, a hairline crack in a sentence, but in our family we heard hairline cracks the way dogs hear frequencies. *Mama used to say.* Used to. The past tense landed on the table among the plates and nobody picked it up. Baba's newspaper was folded now, set aside, and he was looking at his food with the concentration of a man reading it.

"What did Mama used to say?" I asked quietly.

"That you had the face." Tunde's voice had come down a register, the humor draining out of it the way water drains from a sink, leaving the shape of what it carried. "The face of a person carrying a question around like luggage."

"And what did she say I should do with it?"

He looked at me. For a moment — one clean, unguarded moment — my brother looked at me the way we used to look at each other as children during the long silences after the photograph questions, the silences with weight and temperature, and I saw that behind his jokes all these years there had been a door too, and that he had been keeping it shut from his side just as I kept mine shut from mine, and that we had each believed we were protecting the other.

"Leave it," he said softly. "She said leave it, August. Some luggage isn't yours."

"But whose is it?"

"AUGUST." Baba's voice. Not loud — Baba was never loud — but it crossed the table like a line drawn in ink. He had not raised his voice at me in twenty years. He set down his spoon with exaggerated care, the care itself a kind of alarm. "The food is getting cold."

The food was not cold. The food was steaming. But we all understood, the three of us, fluent in our native grammar, that the meal was over, that the window through which anything true might have passed had swung shut on its old hinges, and that we would now discuss the generator, or the funeral costs, or whether Tunde's client should choose blue.

* * *

Later — Tunde gone home, dishes done, the house ticking through its nighttime sounds — I stood in the corridor outside my parents' bedroom, holding the photograph.

I had gone back for it that afternoon, digging past my mother's clothes, which still held her smell and which I touched the way you touch a wound to check if it still hurts (it did). The photograph was where it had always been, under the false bottom of the wardrobe box, beneath a layer of cloth. Three young people. My parents, unrecognizably young. And the woman with the gap-toothed smile and the arm around my mother's shoulders, looking at the camera — no. I had said that wrong for thirty years, even in my own head. She wasn't looking at the camera. I saw it now, finally, the way you finally see the hidden figure in an optical illusion. She was looking slightly off, at someone outside the frame, laughing at something that had just been said, and the someone outside the frame was whoever held the camera, and the camera was held by—

I turned the photograph over. On the back, in pencil, in handwriting I did not know — not my mother's neat teacher's script, not my father's engineer's block letters — someone had written a date and a place:

*Enugu, 1981. Before.*

Before. Before what? Before was a door. Before was the locked study at the end of the corridor, the one room in this house I had not entered, the one key nobody had produced, the room my father walked past every day of my childhood with his eyes fixed forward like a man passing a grave he does not acknowledge.

From the sitting room came the sound of the television, low, and beneath it my father's cough, and beneath that the silence that had raised us all.

I stood in the corridor with the photograph in my hand, and I did not knock on the study door, because I did not have a key, and I did not ask my father, because I had tried and failed at dinner the way I had failed for thirty years.

But tomorrow, I promised myself — and for the first time the promise did not dissolve in my mouth like sugar — tomorrow I would find the key.

Somewhere in this house, my mother had left it. My mother, who managed the silence, who controlled the doors. She had died knowing how to lock them. Women like my mother always knew where the keys were.

I just had to learn to look the way she hid.
