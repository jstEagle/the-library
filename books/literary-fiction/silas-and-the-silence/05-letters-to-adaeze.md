## 5. Letters to Adaeze

The study smelled of dust and camphor and something older underneath both — the smell of a room that had been holding its breath since before I was born.

I sat on the floor with my back against the desk because the chair felt like furniture that belonged to someone else, and in a way it did. It belonged to the version of my mother who had come in here and closed the door for forty years. The morning light came through the louvres in thin stripes and lay across the boxes like bars, and I thought: even her secrets were kept in order. Even her silence was tidy.

There were eleven boxes. I counted them twice, the way she had taught me to count everything twice — *if you count once you are guessing, August; if you count twice you are knowing* — and each box was labeled in her small, upright hand. Not *Letters*. Not *Personal*. The labels said things like *Correspondence, A., 1979–1984* and *Correspondence, A., 1985–1991*, as if she had been running an archive of one person. As if somewhere in her head there had always been a card catalogue, and this room was where the cards lived.

I opened the first box the way you open anything you already regret opening: slowly, with an apology prepared.

Inside, bundled with rubber bands gone brittle and pale, were letters. Dozens of them. All addressed to the same name.

*Adaeze.*

I said it once, under my breath, just to hear what my mouth did with it. Three syllables that fit together strangely after a lifetime of Oyelaran vowels. Ada-eze. Daughter of the king. My mother's people would have known it instantly; my father's people, less so. It was an Igbo name sitting in a Yoruba house, and it had been sitting here, apparently, since before I existed.

The earliest letter was dated March 1979. I knew before I unfolded it that I shouldn't read it. I also knew, with the same certainty, that I would. Grief does strange arithmetic. It subtracts your mother from the world and then demands you find her somewhere else, anywhere else, even in paper, even in paper that was never meant for you.

*My dear sister,* it began.

My mother had no sisters. This was a fact of our family the way the blue gate was a fact of the house — unquestioned, structural. One brother, lost to the war years, she'd say when pressed, and then change the subject with the smoothness of a woman who had practiced. But here was *my dear sister*, written in handwriting I knew better than my own face, the handwriting that had labeled my lunchboxes and corrected my essays and signed, every year of my childhood, the birthday cards that always said exactly the right thing.

*I have received your letter and read it four times. I am glad you are well. I am glad the work is good. You ask if it is safe to write to this address and I will tell you honestly: I do not know. But I have decided that not writing is also not safe, only in a slower way, and I would rather be afraid in ink than afraid in nothing.*

I stopped. Read it again. *Afraid in ink.* My mother, who told me fear was a visitor you fed tea so it would leave sooner. My mother, who never in my life used the word *afraid* about herself.

The letter went on in that vein — careful, warm, and full of holes. She wrote about the weather in Lagos. She wrote about a man she called "the friend I told you about," whom she had met at a wedding in Ibadan, who was "serious in a way that made me laugh." She asked questions and answered none. And at the bottom, beneath her signature — which was not Funmilayo Oyelaran but simply *F.*, just the initial, as if even her own name on paper was a door she preferred to keep half-closed — there was a postscript:

*P.S. I have used the name you gave me. It fits like borrowed shoes but I am walking anyway.*

I set that letter down on the floor beside me very carefully, the way you set down something that might still be alive.

* * *

By the fourth letter I had moved from the floor to the window seat, mostly because my legs had gone numb, partly because I wanted the light. The bundles were chronological — of course they were, she was my mother, chaos offended her — and reading them in order was like watching someone learn to swim in water that kept getting deeper.

1979 was all caution and tenderness. She wrote about learning to cook egusi badly and being teased for it. She wrote about the friend from the wedding — *he has asked me a question I have not yet answered, not because I do not know the answer but because answering it means becoming someone new, and I want to be sure the new person is allowed to keep the old one's friends.* She asked after Adaeze's health, Adaeze's students (so Adaeze taught), Adaeze's town, which was never named. Every letter danced around a center it refused to touch. They were letters written in the shape of a secret, the way a hole in the ground is shaped by what was removed from it.

And every year, without fail, the same line appeared somewhere in the body of the text, like a refrain:

*I have not forgotten. I will not forget. Tell me if there is ever anything more I can carry.*

*Anything more I can carry.* As though the two of them were porters sharing a load too heavy for one back, and my mother's whole married life had been the art of walking steadily while holding up her end.

I laughed at that — actually laughed, alone in a dead woman's study, which is the kind of laughter that frightens the neighbors — because I suddenly saw her at the kitchen table, supervising my homework, carrying everything: the school fees, the church committee, Tunde's asthma, my sulks, my father's long quiet moods that we all pretended were weather. She carried so much so gracefully that I had genuinely believed carrying cost her nothing. That was her genius and, I understood now, sitting among her paper ghosts, possibly her tragedy. She made the load invisible. She made it look like love, and maybe it was love, but it was also labor, and some of the labor was ours to share and she never handed us our end of it.

Or couldn't. There was a difference, and the letters were starting to teach me the difference.

* * *

The 1982 bundle was thinner. The rubber band around it had perished entirely and the letters were loose, and one of them had a brown stain across the corner that might have been tea or might have been time.

Her handwriting had changed.

Not dramatically — my mother would never permit drama in her penmanship — but the upright strokes leaned now, fractionally, like fence posts after a storm. The letters got longer and stranger. In June of '82 she wrote:

*You did not answer my last letter. I have written it again in my head many times wondering which sentence frightened you. If it was the sentence about the photograph, forgive me. I burned it. I should have burned it years ago. I am learning slowly that keeping things can be a kind of danger, and I am a keeper by nature, which may be the most dangerous thing about me.*

A keeper by nature. I looked down at the boxes around me, at the archive of a single name, and felt the sentence land somewhere below my ribs.

In October of '82:

*The children are well. The girl is sharp — sharper than is comfortable, if I am honest. She asks questions the way her father used to, before he learned to stop. I pray she takes longer to learn. I pray she never learns.*

I put that letter face-down on my knee for a full minute. *The girl.* Me. Somewhere in this room was evidence that my mother had watched me become curious and had prayed against it, gently, the way you pray for rain to hold off during a party. I didn't know whether to feel betrayed or held. Both, probably. Both was going to be the flavor of everything I found in this room; I was beginning to accept that.

The 1984 letters were shorter. Her sentences had developed a new habit: they started and then doubled back, crossed out, restarted. Whole paragraphs surrendered mid-thought. Once she wrote *I saw a woman at the market who walked like* and then nothing, just the sentence lying there amputated, and the next line began *How is the harmattan where you are?*

She was editing herself in real time. Or someone had taught her to. I found myself reading faster, hungrily, looking for the letter where the dam broke, where she finally said the thing plainly — *Adaeze, here is what happened, here is who we were, here is why my husband flinches at soldiers on the television* — but the dam never broke. Instead the letters grew more coded, more oblique, more desperate in their politeness. Desperation, I learned from my mother's correspondence, doesn't always shout. Sometimes it sounds like a woman asking, for the ninth year running, after the harmattan.

* * *

It was the 1991 bundle that undid me.

By then I was eight years old in the world outside these letters, losing teeth, convinced my mother knew everything and feared nothing. Inside the bundle, her hand had begun to shake. Not constantly — it came and went, like weather — but on certain pages the letters wobbled visibly, the way an old radio signal wobbles, and once, horribly, a whole line slid downhill off the ruled margin as though the pen had been moving while her attention was elsewhere, or while her hand simply refused.

*My sister,* that letter began, and then, unusually, immediately: *Forgive the state of this page. My hands have their own opinions lately. The doctor says it is nothing. The doctor says many things.*

Then, three paragraphs later, in the middle of news about Tunde's school prize:

*Sometimes I wake at night and think I hear the compound gate, and my heart does the old thing, the 1981 thing, and I lie there listening until the generator comes on and reminds me what decade it is. Forty minutes, some nights. I have gotten good at forty minutes. You would laugh at how good I have gotten at it.*

The 1981 thing. She named it like a diagnosis. Like a condition she'd been living with for ten years and expected to live with forever, and had told absolutely no one in the house — not Tunde, not me, not, I suspected, the man she'd married, because if he'd known, wouldn't he have—

Wouldn't he have what? Held her? Talked? Opened this study and let the air in?

I thought about my father in his armchair, evening after evening, doing his crossword with the television murmuring, a man whose silences I had always filed under *personality*. And a cold little thought walked into me and sat down: what if the silence wasn't personality? What if it was policy? What if two people had agreed, sometime before I was born, on a system — she carries this, he carries that, neither speaks, and the children grow up in the clean quiet between them?

Because here was the thing I could no longer unsee: these letters weren't just my mother's secret. They were addressed to someone. Someone real, someone alive in 1991, someone who received them and — I checked, riffling forward through the bundles with increasing urgency — apparently never once wrote back. Eleven boxes. Twenty-plus years. Not one reply. Either Adaeze had stopped answering, or her answers had never been kept, or —

Or my mother had been writing yearly, faithfully, shakily, to a woman who could not safely answer. To a woman whose existence was itself the secret. To a sister who was not a sister, or who was a sister in the older sense, the sense where the word means *we survived the same thing*.

I sat with that. Outside, the generator next door coughed awake and the afternoon took on its usual Lagos hum, and somewhere down the street a woman was calling a child home for food in a voice that carried three compounds. Ordinary sounds. The sounds my mother must have heard through this same window while she wrote *my heart does the old thing* and sealed the envelope and walked it to the post office and came home and made dinner and never said one word, not one word in twenty years, to either of her children.

I had spent the week cataloguing her belongings like an archivist because it hurt less than grieving. Now the catalogue had turned on me. Every neat label, every ordered bundle, was testimony. My mother hadn't just kept a secret. She had *maintained* one. She had performed maintenance on it annually, in ink, with a steady hand for as long as her hand would cooperate, and then with a shaking one, and she had done it all while packing my lunches and praising my essays and laughing at Tunde's jokes, and I had never once seen the weight move under her clothes.

*A keeper by nature.* Yes. And I was her daughter, and I had inherited the nature without the cargo, and standing in this room I finally understood that the cargo had names.

One name, at least. Adaeze.

The last letter in the final bundle was dated December 2003 — six months before I left for university, a season I remembered as ordinary, Sunday rice and arguments about my course of study. Her handwriting in it was almost steady again, as if the shaking had been a phase, as if she'd decided to be well. It was short. Shorter than any of the others. It said:

*My sister. I am old enough now to say things plainly, so I will say one. Whatever became of you, whatever name you wear, wherever they buried the girl we both were — I hope she knows she was loved by two families, and mourned by one, and that I never once regretted the price. Write if you can. Forgive me if you cannot. I have kept everything. F.*

*I have kept everything.*

Eleven boxes said so. And the twelfth thing — the thing I noticed only now, sitting in the striped light with paper scattered around me like shed skin — was that the boxes were not the whole room. Behind the desk, half-hidden by the curtain, stood a filing cabinet. Small, green, older than me. And unlike every other surface in this meticulously ordered study, its top drawer was locked.

My mother had kept everything. Except, apparently, one thing — the thing she had kept *from herself*, or from the house, or from whoever she'd imagined might someday stand exactly where I was standing.

I don't remember deciding to look for the key. I remember only that my hands were already moving, lifting the hymnal on the desk — her old Methodist hymnal, spine cracked white, the one she'd carried to church every Sunday of my childhood — and that it fell open, the way well-loved books do, to a page near the middle. And there, wedged into the gutter of the binding between two hymns, flat and cold and patient, was a small brass key.

Hymn number 381. I glanced down at it without meaning to. *Beneath the cross of Jesus / I fain would take my stand.*

"Oh, Mama," I said out loud, to nobody, to her. "You hid it in *church*."

The lock was stiff. It fought me for a moment, then gave with a click that sounded, in the quiet study, enormous.

I pulled the drawer open, and inside — not letters this time, not paper at all — was a single photograph, face-down, and beneath it a slim brown envelope with my father's name written on it in my mother's hand.

The photograph, when I turned it over, showed two young women with their arms around each other, squinting into a sun that vanished decades ago. One of them was my mother, twenty-five years old and laughing at something outside the frame.

The other was a stranger.

And she had my face.
