## 13. Adaeze

The morning after the fire, Mama Ngozi swept her compound the way she always did, beginning at the gate and moving inward, as though order could be imposed on the world if only one started at its edges. The ash had come down in the night like grey snow, settling on the hibiscus, on the washing line, on the little stool where Adaeze used to sit and shell beans while complaining about every man in Enugu.

She did not sweep toward the road. She did not look at the road. Looking at the road was a thing she would not permit herself until she had finished with the compound, because a woman who looked too soon at an empty road might never finish anything again.

Inside, Chidi's room was exactly as he had left it: bed made with military corners he'd learned from no army at all, just his own particular madness for tidiness; his school books stacked by size; a half-finished letter to his friend Emeka about football, ending mid-sentence the way all his letters did, because Chidi believed a sentence should be abandoned before it could disappoint you. She sat on his bed and held that letter for a long time. Then she folded it into her wrapper, next to her heart, which was where she kept everything she could not yet afford to feel.

Adaeze's room she did not enter. Not that day. Not for many days.

* * *

People will tell you that grief is a flood, but Mama Ngozi knew better. Grief is a landlord who does not evict you, who simply moves into your back room and pays no rent and makes noise at night, and you learn to walk softly past his door for the rest of your life.

In those first weeks, the whole compound walked softly. Her husband, God rest him, took to fixing things that were not broken — the hinge of the back door, the leg of a table nobody used — because a man's hands must do something when his mouth cannot. The neighbors brought food and lowered their voices, and lowered them further when they saw her coming, so that she moved through the streets inside a bubble of hush, like a woman underwater.

And through it all, the question arrived daily, wearing different clothes.

From her sister-in-law, over bitterleaf soup: "Have you heard anything? Any word at all?"

From the pastor, gently, with the Bible already open to the appropriate page: "Perhaps it is time we held a service. For closure."

From the market women, who meant well the way a machete means well: "Ah-ah, Ngozi, three months now. A person cannot just vanish like smoke. Somebody must have seen something."

To all of them she gave the same answer, delivered with the flat serenity of a woman discussing the weather: "When there is news, you will hear it." And underneath, in the voice only she could hear: *I am not ready to bury my daughter, and I will not bury her without a body, and I will not bury her at all, do you hear me, because Adaeze Okafor does not lose.*

Because that was the thing about Adaeze that people forgot, once she was gone. They remembered her prettiness, her sharp tongue, the way she laughed with her whole head thrown back. But Adaeze was the brave one. Everyone assumed it was Chidi, the boy with the dangerous pamphlets and the dangerous friends, the boy whose name was being read out on lists. But Chidi was brave the way fire is brave — loudly, and with a great deal of damage. Adaeze was brave the way water is brave. Quietly. Persistently. Finding every crack.

It was Adaeze who had noticed, weeks before anyone else, that the men selling roasted plantain near the university had changed, and that the new ones asked questions instead of taking money. It was Adaeze who burned the pamphlets in the kitchen stove one night while Chidi argued with her, saying they were evidence of nothing, and Adaeze telling him, "Evidence is exactly what they are, and evidence is what they will hang you with." It was Adaeze who had said, two days before the soldiers came, standing in this very compound with her arms folded: "Mama, if they come for him, I want you to remember one thing. Whatever happens, whatever you see or hear afterward — you let me handle it. I am faster than him and I lie better than him, and God has given me the face of an innocent woman, which I intend to use."

Mama Ngozi had laughed then. She had actually laughed, and told her daughter that pride comes before a fall, and Adaeze had said, "Then I will fall forward, Mama, and land on somebody."

* * *

The name came to them through a cousin of a cousin, the way such things traveled in those days — whispered, deniable, wrapped in three layers of maybe. A man who worked in records, in one of the ministries, who owed a debt to Mama Ngozi's husband from the old trading days. He came at dusk, would not sit, would not take water, stood in the parlor sweating as though the harmattan were July.

"There was a girl," he said. "Taken up the same night, near the river quarter. Young. They put her in with the others from the lists." He wiped his face. "But there is a record — incomplete, thank God for sloppy clerks — a transfer. To a holding place outside the city. After that—" He spread his hands, and his hands said *nothing*, and his hands also said *do not ask me more*.

"Alive?" Mama Ngozi asked. "Is my daughter alive?"

The man looked at her with the terrible kindness of messengers everywhere. "The record does not say dead," he said.

She seized on it the way a drowning woman seizes on driftwood. For years afterward, she would catch herself doing it — building small altars to that sentence. *The record does not say dead.* Neither does it say alive, her husband would say gently, and she would turn on him with a fury that surprised them both. Hope, she learned, is not a soft thing. Hope has teeth. Hope will bite the hand that feeds it and the hand that tries to take it away.

* * *

It was Mama Ngozi's husband who found the family called Oyelaran.

She never knew all the details — he kept them the way he kept everything, in the locked cabinet of himself — but the shape of it she understood. There was a civil servant in Lagos, a decent, frightened man whose own brother had been taken in the purges, and who had grieved in the manner of grieving men, by wanting to strike back at the darkness in some practical way. Papers could be made. Names could be bought. A young man with a dead man's history could be issued a new life the way you issue a new passport, stamped and sealed and true in every particular except the one that mattered.

"But why Lagos?" Mama Ngozi had asked. "Why so far?"

"Because far is the point," her husband said. "Near, they find you. Far, you have a chance of merely being lonely instead of dead."

"And the name? This Oyelaran?"

"It belongs to a family that ended," he said. "There is a kind of ending that leaves a door open." He would say no more than that, and she learned not to ask, because in those days asking questions aloud was how people disappeared, and she had lost enough to disappearing.

The hardest part — harder than the money, harder than the forged papers, harder than putting her surviving child on a bus to Lagos with a new name sewn into his pocket like a talisman — the hardest part was the rule.

They sat across from each other at the table the night before he left, mother and son, and she laid down the law the way she laid down everything, plainly and without decoration.

"You will not write," she said. "You will not call. You will not send messages through friends, through cousins, through priests, through anybody. You are Silas Oyelaran. You have always been Silas Oyelaran. Your father and mother died in the trouble, God rest them, and you were raised by an uncle in Ibadan. If you ever speak our name, if you ever speak *her* name, you will not only kill yourself. You will kill whatever chance she has. Do you understand me?"

"Mama—"

"Do you understand me, Chidi?"

He was crying. Her son, who had faced soldiers and fire, cried like a boy of six, and she held his face in her hands and made herself look at him, memorizing him, because she understood — with the cold clarity that mothers are granted at the worst moments of their lives — that this might be the last time she saw the face she had made.

"Say it," she whispered. "Say the new name, so I know you can carry it."

"Silas," he said. It came out broken. "Silas Oyelaran."

"Again."

"Silas Oyelaran."

"Good boy," she said, and kissed his forehead, and turned away before he could see that she was crying too, because a general does not weep in front of the troops, and someone in that family had to be a general.

* * *

Years passed the way years do in a house of silence: slowly, and then all at once.

They heard of him the way you hear of the moon — indirectly, by light reflected. A trader from Lagos mentioned a young accountant, very correct, very quiet, doing well. A photograph arrived once, no note, showing a street scene in which a tall man stood with his back half-turned, and Mama Ngozi knew his shoulders the way she knew her own hands. She kept the photograph under the lining of her sewing box. When her husband died, she added his memory to the box. When the political wind changed and the men who had made the lists fell from power and were themselves listed, she allowed herself one small, dry laugh — justice in her country arriving late and drunk, recognizing nobody — and then went back to waiting.

For waiting was the work now. Waiting for Adaeze. Because *the record does not say dead*, and Mama Ngozi had built her whole remaining life on the grammar of that sentence.

She waited through the eighties. Through the nineties, when the country changed its uniforms and its lies. Through the years when waiting began to feel less like hope and more like a profession she had never applied for. She grew old in the doorway of that hope, and the doorway grew comfortable, and that frightened her most of all — that one morning she might realize she had furnished it.

And then, one ordinary Tuesday, a letter came.

Not from Adaeze. From a stranger. A woman's careful hand, a Lagos postmark, a name she did not know: *Oyelaran*. Inside, a single page, written in the voice of someone trying very hard to sound calm.

*I believe you may be the grandmother of my children. My husband's mother, I think, though he will not say so. His name is not his name. I found letters. I am writing to ask you — please tell me what happened to her. Please tell me what happened to Adaeze.*

Mama Ngozi read it four times. Then she put on her good wrapper, sat very straight on her chair, and did what she had done every day for thirty years.

She began, at last, to answer.

* * *

Now — here, in this hot Enugu afternoon, with the ceiling fan turning its slow, faithful circles and the young woman sitting across from her with her mother's forehead and her uncle's stubborn chin — now the answering is nearly done, and Mama Ngozi finds herself strangely reluctant to finish it.

August. What a name to give a child born in that heat. But the mother had been a reader, Tunde had explained, grinning, as though apologizing for his whole family at once, and August it was, and the girl wore the name the way some people wear borrowed clothes — carefully, as if checking the fit.

She has the resemblance. That is what unsettles Mama Ngozi, looking at her. Not the forehead, not the chin. The way she sits with her hands folded, patient and coiled at once, listening the way Adaeze used to listen — as though the other person's words were goods on display and she was deciding which to buy.

"The soldiers came at night," Mama Ngozi says, and her voice comes out steadier than she expected. "They always come at night. Cowards keep night hours."

August leans forward. "My mother wrote that my aunt stayed behind. That she drew them away."

"She did." Mama Ngozi closes her eyes, and the compound of 1981 rises around her: the shouting, the boots, the smell of petrol already soaking into the records office two streets away, the fire that was meant to eat paper and ate more than paper. "Your grandfather tried to stop her. I tried to stop her. She looked at us both — your aunt, my daughter — and she said, 'One of us goes with him and one of us stays with you, and I am faster.' And then she walked out of this house singing. Do you hear me, child? Singing. Some nonsense highlife song about a woman who outwits a chief. Loud enough for the whole street to hear where she was going."

The fan turns. Somewhere outside, a generator coughs to life, dies, thinks better of it.

"They followed her," August says quietly. It is not a question.

"They followed her." Mama Ngozi opens her eyes. "And that is the last certain thing I know, and I have spent forty years refusing to make it the last thing. So tomorrow, when I am rested, I will tell you the rest — what became of the search, and the money, and the man in Accra who claimed he had seen a woman with her laugh. All of it. Every scrap. I am an old woman and my memory is like my eyesight — unreliable at a distance but very sharp up close, and you, my dear, are standing very close."

August smiles — a small, cracked smile, Adaeze's smile — and Mama Ngozi's chest aches with a joy so mixed with sorrow she could not separate them with a knife.

"One thing tonight," the girl says. "Just one. Was she afraid?"

Mama Ngozi considers lying, out of tenderness. Then she remembers who she is talking to, and whose blood is in the room.

"No," she says. "That was the terrible miracle of her. She was not afraid. She was *furious*. At the injustice, at the waste, at the smallness of the men with the guns. Fear is for people who believe the world should be otherwise. Your aunt simply intended to correct it." She reaches across and takes August's hand, and the girl's fingers are cold. "Sleep now. Tomorrow I will give you the rest. And tomorrow, child, you must be prepared — because the story does not end where you think it ends. There is a part of it that involves a letter, and a ship, and a name written in a Bible in Accra, and I have carried it alone long enough."

August's breath catches. "A letter?"

"Tomorrow," Mama Ngozi says, and pats her hand, and adds, because she is old and entitled to her pleasures, "I have waited forty years to make somebody wait one night. Consider it inherited."
