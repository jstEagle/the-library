## 14. Father, Unmasked

The hotel in Enugu had a generator that coughed awake every time NEPA blinked, which was roughly every forty minutes, so that the whole building lived in a rhythm of dying light and sudden resurrection. August had begun to think of it as the city breathing. Tunde said it was the city having a nervous breakdown, and that they should get a room farther from the generator, and then he fell asleep on top of the bedspread with his shoes on, one arm flung over his eyes like a man shielding himself from a sun that wasn't there.

August sat by the window with the phone in her lap.

It was a cheap thing she'd bought at the motor park in Onitsha, a small brick of a handset with a cracked screen guard and a wallpaper of a waterfall that moved when you touched it, which Tunde had declared the single most unnecessary feature ever engineered. She liked it anyway. It was anonymous. It belonged to no house, no history, no archive of silence. If her father's number was saved anywhere, it was saved here under *Dad*, nothing else, as if even the phone knew better than to write down what he'd been called before.

Mama Ngozi slept. The nurse had told them, in the gentle administrative voice nurses use for terrible news, that the lucid intervals were gifts and not to be counted on. *Come back tomorrow,* Mama Ngozi herself had said, gripping August's wrist with a strength that seemed borrowed from somewhere, some younger self still in storage. *Tomorrow I will give you the whole night. You will not like it. But you will have it.* Then she had looked at August for a long moment and said, softly, in Igbo, *You have her chin. Adaeze's chin.* And turned her face to the wall.

Tunde hadn't heard it. He'd gone to find water that didn't taste of chlorine.

August had been carrying those two words around all evening — *Adaeze's chin* — the way you carry a hot pot without a cloth, shifting it from hand to hand, unable to set it down. She kept catching her reflection in the dark window and touching her own jaw as though she might feel someone else's bone beneath the skin.

She thought about calling home in the morning. She rehearsed it in the morning voice, the reasonable daughter voice: *Daddy, something happened at the wake, an old man came, he said a name.* She rehearsed the version where she asked a question instead of saying the name itself, because there were versions of this conversation that left everyone a door to walk through. She had been building doors her whole life. She was, she realized, sitting in a hotel four hundred miles from the house that held its breath, still holding doors open for a man who had never once walked through any of them.

The generator coughed. The lights died. In the blue emergency glow of the exit sign, Tunde shifted and murmured something about jollof rice.

When the power surged back, the television in the lobby downstairs cheered briefly, as if it too had been holding its breath.

August dialed before she could stop being brave.

* * *

He picked up on the third ring, which meant he'd been waiting by the phone. Her father did not wait by phones; he waited by nothing. He was a man who had arranged his entire existence to appear as though he had never anticipated anything, never hoped, never feared — a man who answered the door as though visitors were weather. Three rings meant he had been sitting beside it since the sun went down, and hated himself a little for it.

"August." Not a question. Her name in his mouth always sounded slightly formal, slightly foreign, as though he were pronouncing it carefully to avoid tripping on something buried inside it. "Are you eating?"

"Daddy."

"You sound tired. Is the hotel—"

"I'm fine. We're fine. Tunde is asleep with his shoes on, which is very him."

A pause. Almost a laugh. "That boy has slept fully dressed since he was four. I used to think if we ever had a fire he'd be the only casualty, lying there in his agbada."

And there it was — the joke, the deflection, the family choreography, performed now across four hundred kilometres of telephone line. August felt the old pull of it, the warm gravity of avoidance, how easy it would be to orbit with him another hour, another year, another twenty. Talk about Tunde's shoes. Talk about the house plants, whether Mrs. Adeyemi had been by to water them, whether the neighbors had finally stopped bringing food that nobody ate. There was a whole language they shared, a beautiful elaborate language made entirely of not-saying, and she was fluent in it, native speaker, first tongue.

"Daddy," she said again, and heard her own voice change, and knew he heard it too, because the line went very quiet in the particular way of a man going still. "An old man came to the wake. The last day, just before everyone left. He held my hand very hard, hard enough to hurt, and he said—" She stopped. Started again. "He said, *Tell Silas I saw him.*"

Nothing. Not even breathing. The line held a silence so complete it had texture, like velvet, like the inside of a locked drawer.

"And Daddy," she said, and her heart was going so fast she could hear it in her ears, could feel it in her teeth, "I've been thinking about that word. *Silas.* Because I went through Mummy's things. I found the key. I opened the study." She was speaking quickly now, the words tumbling the way they do when you've spent decades stacking them behind a dam. "There are boxes of letters. Letters to a woman named Adaeze. From 1979. Every year, Daddy. Mummy wrote to her every year like clockwork, and the handwriting gets worse, and the last one stops in the middle of a sentence, and none of them were ever sent—"

"August."

One word. His voice had changed. It wasn't the warning voice, the *enough-of-this* voice she'd known since childhood, the voice that ended conversations about the past the way a hand ends a conversation about a hot stove. It was smaller than that. It was a voice coming from further away than Lagos.

"She had a sister," August said. "Not a sister. A sister. And there's a woman in this town who is dying, and she looked at my face today and said I have Adaeze's chin, and Daddy, I'm standing in Enugu and I can't breathe properly and I need you to tell me—"

"Don't," he said.

"—I need you to tell me who Chidi is."

The silence afterward was not like the other silences. August had catalogued her father's silences the way she catalogued everything — the silence of dinner-table deflection, brief and polished; the silence of the study door, thick and deliberate; the great annual silence that descended each October, which she had never once remarked upon and which now, suddenly, had a date attached to it, a night attached to it, fire attached to it. All her life she had assumed his silences were walls. This one was not a wall. This one was the sound of a wall coming down, and it was much, much louder.

"Daddy?" she whispered. "Papa?"

And then she heard it.

At first she thought it was the line, the crackle of a bad connection, the ghost in the cheap handset. Then she understood, and understanding it felt like the floor tilting. Her father was crying. Not sobbing — she could not have survived sobbing, she thought later; sobbing would have broken something in her that she needed — but weeping, quietly, terribly, the way men weep who have had forty years of practice doing it silently into pillows, into steering wheels, into the dark of unlocked-at-last studies. Small wet sounds. Breath dragged in through the nose, held, released shaking. A man alone in a house in Ikeja at nine o'clock at night, holding a telephone, undone.

In thirty-one years she had never heard him cry. She had seen him at her mother's graveside dry-eyed and upright, receiving condolences like a head of state, and she had hated him a little for it, and admired him, and both of those things now curdled into shame, because she understood at last that he had not been unmoved at the grave. He had been performing the only role he had left. He had spent so long being Silas Oyelaran, a man with no history, that grief itself had to be smuggled out of him in disguise.

"Papa," she said, and the word came out in her mother's inflection without her permission, soft, Yoruba-soft, the word her mother had used for him across thirty years of marriage. "I'm here. I'm not angry. I'm here."

"I killed her," he said.

The words dropped into the line like stones into a well. August gripped the windowsill. Down in the street a taxi horn complained; somewhere a dog gave its opinion of the generator; the ordinary world went on being ordinary, obscenely indifferent.

"No," she said. "Whatever happened—"

"You don't know what happened." His voice was wrecked, unrecognizable, a stranger's voice wearing her father's cadence. "You don't know. Nobody knows. That was the whole point of me. I made sure nobody would ever know, and then I made sure *I* wouldn't know, do you understand? Do you know what it is to erase yourself so thoroughly that even you cannot find the edges? Your mother wrote those letters because somebody in that house had to remember. I couldn't let myself be the one. If I remembered, I would speak. If I spoke—" A breath, ragged. "They took names off doors, August. They read them out loud in the street. And a girl stood up in a compound in 1981 and said *my brother is not here* with such a straight face that soldiers believed her, and I ran, and she—" The sentence stopped. It stopped the way her mother's letter had stopped, mid-air, mid-word, and August felt the two unfinished sentences reach across the years and touch.

"Tunde doesn't know," she said. It wasn't a question. "You never told him either."

"He knows pieces. He's known since he was nineteen. He came to me once, after university, after he'd heard something from someone — I never learned who — and he asked me one question, and I looked at him, and he said, *Never mind, Daddy,* and he never asked again." A sound that was half laugh, entirely grief. "My children. My brilliant, careful children. You both learned mercy from watching me hide. God forgive me, I taught you your silences the way other fathers teach football."

August pressed her forehead against the cool glass of the window. Below, Enugu lay in its orange scatter of lights, hills black against a bruised sky. Somewhere across this city an old woman slept with her face to the wall, holding a night in reserve, rationing it out like the last water in a calabash.

"Mama Ngozi is dying," August said gently. "She's lucid in the mornings. She promised us tomorrow. She promised the whole night, Papa. Everything that happened. What Adaeze did. Where—" She hesitated on the edge of it. "Whether."

Another silence, but a different species now — not a wall, not a collapse. A held breath. A man deciding.

"If I come," he said at last, and his voice had steadied into something raw and new, a voice with no practice in it, "if I come east, I will be walking into a room where people remember me. Do you understand what that means? For forty years I have been safe because everyone who knew is scattered or dead or silent. I am about to sit at the bedside of the last living witness and let her say my name — my *other* name — in front of my children." A long exhale. "Your mother begged me for years. Every October. Every anniversary of nothing, because officially there was no anniversary. She'd stand in the kitchen and say, *Chidi, one day you will wish you had spoken while there was still someone to speak to.* And I would say, *Woman, don't call me that in this house.*" His breath caught. "And she would say, *This house? Which house? Whose house is this, if not the house where our whole life is hidden?*"

August closed her eyes. She could see it — her mother at the sink, hands in grey water, back straight, voice level, waging the only war available to her, one October at a time.

"Papa," she said. "Come east."

The line hummed. The generator downstairs coughed, considered dying, reconsidered.

"There's something else," he said. "Something I have carried since 1981 that I have never shown anyone, not even your mother. Especially not your mother. She knew it existed. She never asked to see it. We had an agreement, your mother and I — the only honest agreement of our marriage, maybe." A pause, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped, gone careful, the voice of a man handling something fragile in the dark. "If I come, I'm bringing it. And August — when you see it, you'll understand why I couldn't open it. Why I couldn't even look at it. Forty years, and I have never once let myself read what she wrote to me."

August's throat tightened. "*She* wrote to *you*?"

"The last thing," he said. "The last thing my sister ever sent me. It came three months after the fire, through three pairs of hands, wrapped in oilcloth, with no return address. And I have carried it sealed through every house, every border, every year, because I was afraid—" The weeping threatened again; she heard him master it, the old terrible discipline. "Because I was afraid of what she would have had to become, to survive, in order to write it. And because as long as it stayed closed, some part of her was still — still possible. Still on her way. Do you understand? As long as the letter stayed shut, my sister was still coming."

Outside, far off toward the hills, thunder muttered its one long syllable.

"Come east, Papa," August said again, barely above a whisper. "Read it with me. Whatever it says — we'll read it together. Nobody reads it alone anymore. That's the whole point. That's what I've decided. Nobody in this family reads anything alone ever again."

Down the corridor, a door opened and closed. Tunde's bare feet slapped tile, heading, no doubt, for the vending machine that dispensed only warm Fanta and disappointment. August listened to her father breathe across the distance — a man mid-transformation, mid-undoing, Silas dissolving and something older surfacing through him like a name rising through water.

"I'll drive," he said. "I'll leave before dawn. I'll be there by afternoon." A pause, and then, haltingly, like a man testing ice: "August. When I arrive — you may call me whatever you need to call me. I think... I think I would like to hear it. Once. Out loud. From one of you."

The line clicked dead in her hand, or he hung up, or the network did what networks do; she never learned which. She stood a moment in the blue dark with the phone against her chest, feeling her own heartbeat slow, feeling the strange lightness of a question finally asked and a wall finally down.

Tunde appeared in the doorway with two warm Fantas, took one look at her face, and set them down on the table with exaggerated care, like a man disarming something.

"So," he said. "He's coming, isn't he."

August turned from the window. "How did you—"

"Because you're smiling," Tunde said, "and you've got that look. The look you had when you found the key." He handed her the Fanta, warm as bathwater, and sat down heavily on the end of the bed, shoes still somehow on. "Okay. Tomorrow, then. Tomorrow we meet the last witness." He rubbed his face with both hands, and when he spoke again his voice was quieter, stripped of jokes. "And Augie — whatever she tells us. However bad it is. We hold onto each other, yeah? That's the deal. That's the only deal."

August looked at her brother — her funny, deflecting, shoe-sleeping brother, keeper of his own fragments, carrier of his own unanswered question from a night when he was nineteen — and reached out and took his hand.

"Yes," she said. "That's the deal."

But even as she said it, she was already elsewhere — already morning, already the hospital corridor, already a door opening onto a room where a dying woman sat up against her pillows with the whole of 1981 folded inside her like a letter waiting forty years to be opened — and August understood, with a clarity that felt almost like fear, that by tomorrow night, nothing in her life would be unnamed.
