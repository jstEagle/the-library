## 15. He Comes East

### Silas

The bus from Lagos took fourteen hours, which was nothing. He had once taken a night that lasted forty years.

Silas Oyelaran—Chidi, he corrected himself, feeling the old name settle over his shoulders like a coat taken down from a high shelf, still smelling faintly of camphor—sat by the window and watched the country unspool eastward. The Niger at Onitsha was brown and muscular as always. The bridge had been rebuilt since the war, then repaired again, then patched again; the whole country was a bridge that kept being crossed and kept being mended, and somehow it held.

His daughter had called him from Enugu. That was the miracle he kept turning over in his hands like a stone too smooth to be believed. August had said *Chidi* into the telephone the way you might set a live coal on a table between you, and then she had wept—not him, her—and said, *Come. She's dying, Papa. Come while there's still someone left who knew you.*

He had not argued. That surprised him most of all. For forty years he had rehearsed arguments the way other men rehearsed golf swings: in the shower, on the drive to work, in the blue hour before sleep. *I did what any father would do. I did what kept you alive. You cannot judge a man for surviving.* But when the moment came, all the arguments lay down like tired dogs and would not rise.

"I'm coming," he had said instead.

Tunde had driven him to the park at dawn, hugging him too hard at the gate and saying nothing useful, which was Tunde's way of saying everything. "Tell Mama Ngozi—" his son began, and stopped, and started again. "Tell her the jollof rice at the funeral was an insult to the nation. She'll know what I mean."

"She will," Silas said, because Mama Ngozi had once thrown a pot of stew at a soldier's head and missed on purpose, so that he would know she *could* have hit him. "She'll understand completely."

Now, in Enugu, the heat came through the bus window like a hand pressed gently against his face. The city had grown upward and outward, new banks where old houses used to be, but the hills were unchanged—those gentle, patient hills, like sleeping animals—and when he saw them something in his chest unlocked that he had not known was locked.

The taxi driver talked the whole way. They always did. "You are visiting family, sir?"

"Yes," Silas said. It did not even feel like a lie. It felt like the first true sentence he'd spoken in decades.

"Mama Ngozi?" the driver guessed, because in that neighborhood everyone knew everyone's business the way rain knows the roof. "Ah-ah! The whole street is talking about it. Two visitors in one week, and both of them looking like they're carrying luggage inside their chest." He laughed at his own observation, delighted with himself. "Her grandson says she has been sitting up every evening asking about tomorrow. An old woman excited about tomorrow! Is that not something?"

"It is something," Silas agreed quietly.

At the house—a low bungalow behind a wall gone soft with bougainvillea—the grandson met him at the gate, a lanky boy of maybe nineteen with his grandmother's unimpressed eyebrows. He looked Silas up and down.

"You're the one from Lagos."

"I'm the one from Lagos."

"Grandmother said you would be smaller."

Silas laughed—an actual laugh, startled out of him, rusty as an unused hinge. "She would say that."

"She also said to tell you"—the boy consulted some inner transcript with great seriousness—"'Wash your hands before you enter my house, Chidi Okonkwo, some things do not change.'"

And there it was again, the name, spoken aloud in daylight by a stranger, and the sky did not fall. The ground did not open. A gecko ran along the wall, entirely indifferent. Forty years of terror, and the world's response was a gecko.

"Tell her," Silas said, "that I washed my hands at the hotel twice."

### Mama Ngozi

Ngozi had been dying for three years now and found it, on the whole, less dramatic than advertised. There was pain, yes, in the mornings especially, sitting in her bones like an unpaid debt. But mostly it was waiting, and she had been good at waiting since 1981, when waiting became the whole of her profession.

They told her the man from Lagos had arrived. Her grandson Emeka delivered the news with the gravity of a radio announcer, then ruined it by asking if he could watch the meeting. She boxed his ear lightly, which is how love is expressed among people who have run out of time for indirection.

"Bring me my wrapper," she said. "The good one. If I am going to receive a ghost, I will receive him properly dressed."

When they brought him into the parlor—the fan turning its slow, useless circles, the afternoon light lying across the cement floor in long gold bars—she looked at him and felt something she had not permitted herself in forty years, which was fury, and underneath the fury, which was worse, relief.

He had become small. That was her first thought, shamefully petty and entirely honest. The young man who had run through her compound with fire behind him had been tall and quick, all elbows and urgency. This one was folded into himself, a cardigan in Enugu heat because old men feel cold the way they feel regret: constantly, and out of season.

"You look terrible," she announced.

"You look," he said, "exactly the same."

"Liar. Sit down before you fall down. Emeka, bring water. Not for him—for me. Watching him makes my throat dry."

Emeka went, grinning. Ngozi studied the man while he lowered himself into the chair opposite hers, the way you study a house you once lived in and find everything shifted two inches to the left.

"So," she said. "Forty years."

"Forty-one," he said. "It was March."

"Don't correct me, Chidi. I carried that month longer than you did. I get to round it up."

He flinched at the name, and she watched the flinch with clinical interest. Still, after all this time. Still the little death each time the syllables landed. She had spent decades imagining this meeting—in the hospital, in the long white afternoons—and in her imaginings she had been magnificent. She had told him exactly what he owed. She had made him kneel. In the actual parlor, with the fan turning and the light going amber, all she felt was the terrible tenderness of seeing another survivor, and how shabby survival looks up close, how it fits nobody well.

"They told me you were dying," he said.

"Everyone is dying. Some of us have simply been given better information." She waved this away. "August came. Did you know? Your daughter. She sat in that chair and looked at me with Adaeze's face, and I nearly passed into the next world early just from the shock of it."

"Is she—"

"She is fine. She is more than fine. She is doing what none of us could do." Ngozi leaned forward, and the effort of it cost her, and she paid without complaint. "She is asking questions and refusing to be answered with silence. Where did that child come from, Chidi? You and your wife built a house with no windows and a girl grew up in it who wants light."

"My wife wrote letters," he said, and his voice cracked on *letters* like dry wood. "For thirty years. To Adaeze. I never—I couldn't read them. Blessing kept them anyway. Kept writing them even though—" He stopped. Started again. "She never got an answer. Not one."

"No," Ngozi said. "Adaeze could not answer. Answering is dangerous when you are dead."

The words fell into the room and neither of them moved to pick them up. Outside, a generator coughed to life somewhere, and a woman called a child home for evening food, the same call, the same words, that mothers had been calling since before the war and would call after everyone in this room was dust.

"You knew," he said finally. "All these years, you knew where she went."

"I knew she lived. I knew she reached Accra. A cousin of a cousin confirmed it in '84, and after that, silence—her choice, not mine. She asked for it, Chidi. Do you understand? She asked that no one follow her. She said—" and here Ngozi's voice, which had narrated massacres to priests and policemen without wavering, wavered— "she said, *if they come looking for me, let them find nothing. Let me be the one thing they never find.*"

"And you honored it."

"I honored it." She fixed him with a look. "You buried yourself alive instead. We each chose our tomb. Mine had visitors."

He put his face in his hands. She watched him weep the way old women watch rain after drought: without gloating, without mercy, counting every drop.

### Silas

They spoke until dark, and then past dark, with only a kerosene lamp between them because NEPA had done what NEPA does, and somehow the failure of the electricity felt right—as if the grid itself understood that some conversations should happen by flame, the way the oldest ones did.

They slipped into Igbo without deciding to. That was the strangest grace of the evening: the language rose up in him intact, unsuppressed, the one part of himself he had never managed to kill. English had been his armor for four decades—careful, fluent, deniable—but under Mama Ngozi's questioning his mother tongue came back like blood to a numb limb, painful and unmistakably alive.

"Do you remember," she said, "the night before? You ate at my table. You complained about the pepper."

"I remember the pepper."

"You said my pepper would send you to an early grave." She cackled, then coughed, then continued as though neither had happened. "And God, in His humor, arranged things differently. The pepper was innocent. It was the names that killed."

"The list," he said. The word still tasted of smoke. "They read the names off paper. Like attendance at school. *Present,* we were supposed to say. Present for our own destruction."

"You were not on the list."

"No."

"But Adaeze put you on it."

He looked up sharply. "What?"

Mama Ngozi smiled, and in the lamplight her face was suddenly the face of the woman who had once stood between soldiers and a compound full of children, arms folded, daring them. "You thought you escaped by luck. By the back fence, by the dark, by God's grace. Ah-ah, Chidi. Nothing in that compound happened by luck that night. Your sister went to the officer herself. Gave him a name. Said the man they wanted was seen running toward the river quarter—you, described to them, pointed away from you. She bought you twenty minutes with a lie and a pointing finger, and she knew—she *knew*, because she was the smartest person in that compound, smarter than you, smarter than me—that twenty minutes was all she was buying, and that the price was herself."

The lamp flickered. Somewhere far off, Enugu hummed its enormous indifferent hum.

"She never told me," he whispered.

"Of course she didn't tell you. What kind of sacrifice announces itself? Then it isn't sacrifice, it's a transaction, and she wasn't buying anything for herself." Ngozi settled back into her chair, exhausted now; he could see the day's spending showing in her face. "Your daughter asked me the same question, in almost the same words. You Oyelarans—or whatever you are—all arrive here thinking you are the keepers of the secret. And every time I must inform you: you were not the keeper. You were the *kept*. Adaeze kept you. Your wife kept you. I kept you. Even your silence kept you, in its stupid, faithful way. You have spent forty years guarding a treasure that was you."

He could not speak. He nodded, because nodding was all the body could manage when the mind has been turned inside out and hung in the sun.

Later—much later, when Emeka had brought food they barely touched and the lamp had burned low—she took his hand. Her grip was still strong. Dying had apparently decided not to press its luck with her hands.

"I don't forgive you," she said conversationally. "There is nothing to forgive. You didn't abandon her; you abandoned *yourself*, which is sadder and harder to pardon, but not mine to pardon either. But hear me, Chidi, because I am old and licensed to say anything: the silence was never loyalty. Loyalty would have been one letter. One letter in forty years, to Accra, to say *I live, I married, I had children, thank you.* She waited for that letter the way the earth waits for rain. Don't tell me she didn't. I knew her."

"I know," he said. "I know she waited."

"Then tomorrow," Mama Ngozi said, "you will help me do the thing I have been saving my strength for. Because your children found something in those letters, and I want to see it before I go, and I want you standing beside me when I do. There is a name we are going to say out loud, in full daylight, in front of God and this useless fan. And afterward—" she squeezed his hand, and her eyes glittered with something that was half mischief and wholly serious— "afterward, you are going to tell your daughter why you never opened the last letter. She thinks she is ready. Let us find out together whether any of us ever were."

Outside, the night stood close and warm around the little house. Inside, two old survivors sat holding the same night between them at last, forty years wide and finally, finally shared—and in the morning, Mama Ngozi promised, the story would finish telling itself.
