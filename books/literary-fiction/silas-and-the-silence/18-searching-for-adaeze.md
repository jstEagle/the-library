## 18. Searching for Adaeze

**August**

The National Archives in Enugu smelled of dust and diesel and the particular patience of paper — that smell of things waiting a long time to be needed. Tunde held the door for me with a flourish he had clearly been saving since 1998.

"After you, madam archivist," he said. "I assume there's a form for this. There's always a form."

"There's always a form," I agreed, "and it will be in triplicate, and the third copy will be for an office that closed in 2004."

He laughed, and I felt something loosen in my chest that had been tight since Mama Ngozi's door closed behind us two days ago. This was the thing about my brother that I kept forgetting and rediscovering like a coin between sofa cushions: under all the jokes, he showed up. He had shown up on the road east when Papa's voice cracked down the phone line like old plaster, and he was showing up now, at nine in the morning, in a building where hope went to be laminated.

The archives were run by a man named Mr. Eze, who wore reading glasses on a chain and had the unhurried dignity of someone who knew exactly which drawer held what and saw no reason to hurry on your account. When I explained — carefully, incompletely, the way we now spoke of everything — that we were looking for records of a woman named Adaeze Okonkwo, last seen in Enugu in 1981, he looked at us over his glasses for a long moment.

"1981," he repeated. "You know what year that was."

"We're learning," Tunde said quietly.

Mr. Eze nodded as if that were the correct answer to every question, which perhaps it was. "Displacement files are in the east wing. Some burned. Some water-damaged. Some—" he paused, choosing the word with care, "—some were never filed at all, because filing requires that somebody official admit something happened." He rose from his desk. "Come. We will see what survived."

* * *

What survived was three metal cabinets and a great deal of patience. The displacement files were organized by local government area, then by year, then by some logic known only to God and Mr. Eze, who consulted both. For two days we sat at a scarred wooden table under a ceiling fan that turned with the weary resolve of a man near retirement, going through folders.

Tunde was better at this than I expected. He read fast and remembered everything, and he had developed a system of colored sticky notes that he defended with surprising passion.

"The green ones are 'possibly relevant,'" he explained. "Yellow is 'definitely relevant.' Pink is 'tragic but not our tragedy.'"

"You've made grief into a filing system."

"Somebody has to. You'd drown otherwise." He said it lightly, but I heard the weight underneath. He had been carrying this longer than I had — those fragments he'd gathered years ago and folded away like letters he couldn't bear to open. Now he opened them one folder at a time, and I watched him do it, and understood that his jokes had always been a kind of sticky note: *this is too much, deal with it later.*

Later had arrived for both of us.

On the third morning, Tunde made a sound I had never heard him make — not a laugh, not a gasp, something between a cough and a prayer.

"August." He didn't look up. "Green note. Come make it yellow."

* * *

**Tunde**

The file was thin. That was the first thing I noticed — how little paper it took to erase a person, or almost-erase her, or whatever it was that had happened to our aunt. A carbon copy of an intake record from a relief center in Onitsha, dated October 1981. A name: *Adaeze Nwosu* — Nwosu, not Okonkwo, and I watched my sister's face as she registered that our aunt had already learned the first lesson of survival, the one our father had mastered: change the name before the name changes you.

And beneath the intake record, a transfer slip. Relief resettlement program. Destination: **Accra**.

"Accra," August said, testing it. She said it the way she said everything important — twice, softly, as if the second saying might confirm the first.

"Ghana," I said, because apparently I was the kind of person who stated the obvious when terrified. "She went west. Everyone assumed—" I stopped. Everyone assumed she was dead; that was the assumption that had built our whole family, load-bearing silence, and here was a slip of onionskin paper pulling it out from under the house.

"Everyone assumed," August agreed. Her voice was steady but her hands weren't; she pressed them flat against the table like a woman steadying herself on a boat. "Tunde. If she got on that bus—"

"Then she lived." I said it because she couldn't yet. "Our aunt lived. She walked out of 1981 and got on a bus and went to Accra and — and bought bread, and argued about prices, and had bad days and good ones. Forty years of ordinary life that nobody in our family knew about."

August laughed, or cried, or did that thing she does where both happen at once and cancel out into a strange bright stillness. Then she did the other thing she does, the archival thing, the thing that used to drive me mad and now looked to me like courage wearing glasses: she took out her notebook and began, methodically, to write it all down.

* * *

**August**

Ghana required passports, and passports required explaining ourselves to immigration officers, and explaining ourselves was becoming its own discipline. *Family research,* we wrote on the forms. It was even true. It simply left out that the family we were researching had been a fiction maintained for forty years by two people who loved each other so carefully they nearly disappeared inside it.

Accra in the rainy season was green and loud and smelled of the sea and frying plantain, and I loved it immediately in the guilty way you love places that aren't yours while you're hunting through them for a ghost. Tunde negotiated with a taxi driver for twenty minutes over a fare difference that came to less than a dollar, both men enjoying themselves enormously, and I stood on the curb thinking: *Adaeze walked streets like these. Did she haggle? Did she laugh? Was there a version of Tunde's charm in her?*

We started where all searches start now: the registry office, the churches, the old neighborhoods. We had one more gift from Mr. Eze, who had turned out to be an archivist of the old school, the kind who believed that files were only half the work and memory the other half. Before we left Enugu he had given us a name — a Ghanaian civil servant, retired now, who had worked refugee resettlement in the eighties. "Madam Comfort Mensah," he said. "If anyone remembers your Adaeze, it will be someone like her. The people who processed the paperwork remember what the paperwork forgets."

Madam Comfort received us in her sitting room in Dansoman, surrounded by photographs of grandchildren and a television playing with the sound off. She was small and sharp-eyed and served us tea before she would hear a word, and when we finally told her what we were looking for — a Nigerian woman, mid-twenties, arrived 1981, name possibly Adaeze, possibly Nwosu, possibly anything — she set down her cup and looked at us for a long time.

"Nwosu," she said. "There was a Nwosu. Quiet woman. Very neat handwriting — she wrote her own forms, wouldn't let me fill them in for her. Said—" Madam Comfort smiled at some forty-year-old memory. "Said she wanted her own hand on her own story. I liked her for that."

My skin prickled. *Her own hand on her own story.* Mama's letters. The confession that stopped mid-sentence. It ran in the family, this need to write things down, this need to be the author of oneself.

"She settled where?" I asked, leaning forward.

"Osu, at first. Then she married — a tailor, a good man, Ghanaian. Changed her name again, of course. They always did." Madam Comfort waved a hand. "Akosua. She took the day-name. Akosua Mensah — no relation to me, half the country is called Mensah. And they moved, let me think — later years, Tema. Near the harbor. She worked at the community school. Taught the little ones their letters."

*Tema.* Tunde wrote it down. His hand was shaking slightly and he pretended it wasn't, and I loved him so much in that moment I could hardly breathe.

"Is she—" I began, and couldn't finish the question, because I already knew how to ask it and was afraid of both answers.

Madam Comfort's face did what faces do when they have delivered hard news many times and never gotten used to it. "Ah. My dear. Akosua passed. Some years back now — 2011? 2012? There was a funeral. Big one. Her daughter organized it — lovely girl, very capable. Efua." She pronounced it with care. "Efua Mensah. She teaches, I believe. Secondary school, English literature." A dry little smile. "Like mother, like mother, if you understand me. The woman who wanted her own hand on her own story raised a girl who hands stories to children for a living."

* * *

**Tunde**

Efua Mensah. A cousin. I had a cousin who taught English literature in Tema, and until four weeks ago I hadn't known I had an aunt, and until four months ago I hadn't known my father had ever been anyone else.

You think you know the shape of your life, and then a retired civil servant pours you tea and redraws the borders.

We sat in the car outside Madam Comfort's gate afterward and neither of us spoke for a while. The rain had started again, soft and businesslike, drumming on the roof. August had the notebook open on her knees but wasn't writing. She was staring at the word *Efua* like it might stare back.

"A whole life," she finally said. "A husband. A job. A funeral with a big turnout — you don't get a big turnout unless people loved you, Tunde. She was loved. Somewhere in this city people still remember her." Her voice caught. "And we weren't there. We didn't know to mourn her. She died in 2011 and our father was — what, fixing the generator? Arguing with the DSTV man? Living his whole careful life next door to a grave he didn't know he had?"

"He knew he had it," I said. "That's the thing. He just didn't know it had a body attached. He thought she was dead, August. He grieved her for thirty years. He just grieved the wrong ending."

August put her hand over her eyes. Through her fingers I could see she was doing the arithmetic she couldn't stop doing — 2011, subtracting backward, all the Christmases, all the birthdays, all the Sunday rice, the whole parallel life running alongside ours like a train on the next track, close enough to wave to, never once flagged down.

"Do you think she looked for us?" she asked. "Do you think she wrote letters the way Mama did, to somebody who never answered?"

"I think," I said slowly, "that we're about to find out. Because there's a daughter. And daughters keep things. Look at us."

That startled a wet laugh out of her. "Look at us. Two professional keepers of things we didn't ask to keep."

"The family business," I said. "Silence & Sons. Except now with a branch office in Accra, apparently, and a staff of one English teacher who doesn't know we exist."

August lowered her hand. And I saw it happen — the moment the grief reorganized itself into purpose, the same shift I'd watched in her since the study, since the letters, since Mama Ngozi gripped her hand and promised tomorrow. She picked up the pen.

"Tomorrow," she said, "we find Efua Mensah."

"Today it's four o'clock and raining and you haven't eaten since the airport."

"Tunde."

"August." I turned the key in the ignition. "She's taught literature in Tema for probably fifteen years. She has a school schedule, a favorite chop bar, a church, a life. She'll be there Monday. She'll be there Tuesday. What she won't be is easier to talk to if we show up starving and soaked and half-mad with hope. We eat. We sleep. Tomorrow we knock on a stranger's door and tell her that her dead mother had a brother, a name, a whole country of people who thought she died in a fire." I pulled out into the rain. "You want to get that right. Trust me. I'm the one who's good with words out loud. You're the one who's good with words on paper. Between us we might manage one honest sentence."

She almost smiled. Then she looked out the window at the shining streets, the umbrellas blooming like dark flowers, the whole unknowable city where our aunt had bought plantain and taught children their letters and died with our name folded away inside another one.

"Tunde," she said quietly. "What if she doesn't want to know? What if her silence was a choice too?"

I drove for a while before I answered, because it deserved an honest answer and honest answers cost me more than jokes do.

"Then we'll knock anyway," I said, "and let her choose. That's the whole point of everything Mama and Papa got wrong, isn't it? Somebody should have knocked. Somebody should have let them choose." I glanced at her. "Besides — a woman whose mother insisted on writing her own forms, raising a daughter who teaches books for a living? August. That girl has been waiting her whole life for chapter nineteen."

August looked at me. "Did you just refer to our lives as chapters?"

"I've been living with a novelist for a month. It's contagious. There's no cure."

But she was smiling now, really smiling, and she opened the notebook to a fresh page, and in her neat, certain, unmistakably Oyelaran hand — Adaeze's hand, Chidi's stubbornness, Mama's ink — she wrote:

*Efua Mensah. Tema. Tomorrow.*

Outside, the rain kept falling on a city full of names, and somewhere in it, unknown to herself, our cousin was grading papers.
