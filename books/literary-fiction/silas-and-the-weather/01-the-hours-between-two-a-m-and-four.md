## 1. The Hours Between Two A.M. and Four

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in a library between two and four in the morning, and I have come to know it the way a farmer knows his soil — by its moods, its seasons, its occasional violence. It is not the silence of absence. It is the silence of held breath. Of books waiting to be chosen the way strangers at a bus stop wait for a bus they have already decided not to board.

I was shelving when the thought arrived, though "shelving" is a generous word for what I do at that hour. I was returning books to their places with the tenderness other men reserve for sleeping children, sliding each one home against its neighbors until the spines sat flush, and thinking, as I always think, that this is the only honest work left in the world: putting things back where they belong.

My name is Isandro Oyelaran, and I am the night librarian at the Marisol Branch, which sits on a corner in a part of the city where the ocean is more rumor than view — you smell it before you ever see it, salt and diesel and kelp, a perfume the tourists never write home about. The Marisol stays open all night because eleven years ago a city councilwoman whose brother couldn't sleep decided insomnia was a public health crisis, and for once the government did something strange and beautiful about it. Now we are one of four libraries in the country that never close, and every night between two and four, the hours I privately call the trough, the building fills with people who cannot be alone with themselves.

I catalog them. Not officially. Officially I shelve, I answer questions, I unlock the bathroom for the man who comes in every Tuesday convinced someone has stolen his shoes. But unofficially, in the ledger I keep in my head, I have developed a taxonomy of the sleepless, and I track them like weather.

There are the Front-Loaders, who arrive at closing time of the bars and sit very still, hands flat on tables, as if the building were moving and they were afraid of falling off. There are the Drifters, who wander the stacks touching spines without reading titles, looking not for a book but for the sensation of being looked after by something that doesn't ask questions. There are the Students, who are actually just lonely people who have discovered that a textbook is an acceptable thing to cry over in public. And there are the Regulars, my barometric pressure, the ones whose absence I notice before their presence: Mr. Adeyemi, who reads naval history and falls asleep sitting up with the dignity of a retired admiral; the twins from the laundromat on Corvus Street, who do their accounting in the periodicals section and argue in whispers so fierce you'd think the numbers had insulted their mother; and the woman I think of as the Grey Coat, who arrives most nights around two-fifteen, orders nothing, borrows nothing, and reads poetry anthologies from back to front, last poem first, as if she were walking downstream toward the source.

Tonight the weather was unsettled. Rain coming off the water in long grey sheets, the kind that makes the whole city sound like it's being slowly erased. We had a Drifter in biography, a Student crying quietly into an anatomy text — pre-med, third year, I'd guessed; grief has a syllabus too — and the Grey Coat at her usual table by the window, a volume of Szymborska open in front of her, reading, I suspected, nothing at all, watching the rain instead, which is also a form of reading.

I pushed my cart down the 700s. Art. Photography. A book on lighthouses that had been checked out forty-one times this year, mostly, I believed, by people who wanted to look at pictures of buildings whose entire purpose is to stand still and shine at something far away. I understood those people. I was one of them, some nights, after close, when I stayed an extra twenty minutes with the lighthouse book open on the reference desk like a devotional object.

"Excuse me." The Student had materialized beside my cart, wet-haired, red-eyed, holding the anatomy book like a shield. "Do you have — I don't know what I'm looking for."

"Nobody does," I said. "That's why we organize it by subject."

She almost smiled. Almost. "Something about — how to know if you made the right choice. About a person."

Ah. The 306s would have the sociology of it, but sociology would only tell her she was normal, and that wasn't what she wanted. What she wanted was permission, or absolution, or a fire to burn something down with. "Poetry," I said. "Second floor, but I'll bring it down. Poetry is better for this than advice. Advice assumes you're asking a question you can survive the answer to."

She looked at me the way people look at librarians at three in the morning — as if I were either a prophet or a hallucination — and went back to her table, and I went and got her Neruda, because Neruda understands that love is a weather system and not a decision, and carried it down and set it on her table without a word, and she looked at the cover and then at me and then at the cover again, and I returned to my cart, and that was the whole conversation, and it was enough. That is the shape of intimacy available to a man like me: correct book, correct moment, no names exchanged. I have been doing it for six years and I could not tell you whether it adds up to a life.

* * *

My room is on Vesper Street, fourth floor of a house that used to be a single family's home and is now five families' worth of walls and doors, a building that has been subdivided so many times it hums like a struck tuning fork whenever anyone above me turns over in bed. I have lived there three years. My suitcase is unpacked in the sense that its contents have migrated to various surfaces, but the suitcase itself remains open under the bed, empty, mouth agape, ready. I keep meaning to put it in the closet. I keep meaning to hang the second curtain rod. I keep meaning to buy a lamp that isn't borrowed from the library's discard pile, a green-shaded banker's lamp with a cracked base that I told myself was temporary and have now owned longer than I've owned any opinion about anything.

The landlady, Mrs. Ferreira, says I live like a man waiting for a train. She says it kindly, the way you'd tell someone they have spinach in their teeth. She is eighty-one and grows tomatoes on the fire escape in coffee cans and leaves them on my landing in paper bags with no note, because a note would make it a transaction and Mrs. Ferreira does not believe in transactions between neighbors, only in obligations, which are different and quieter.

I tell her I like being able to leave quickly. This is a lie I maintain the way other men maintain cars — regularly, without much thought, because stopping would mean confronting what it's for.

The truth, if I let it out of its room, is simpler and worse: I don't leave quickly. I haven't gone anywhere in three years. The suitcase is not for leaving. The suitcase is for the possibility of leaving, which is a different substance entirely, the way a lottery ticket is different from money. I am not a traveler. I am a man who has preserved the option of travel the way some people preserve the option of faith — never practicing, never renouncing, keeping the door unlocked in case God decides to visit.

Three years ago I stopped going somewhere I used to go every Sunday. Three years ago there was a funeral, and after the funeral there was a house, and after the house there was paperwork I did not file, mail I did not open, a key I did not return and did not use. The house is fourteen miles north of here, in a neighborhood called Alder Row, and I could tell you the exact number of times I have driven past it since — twice, both times at night, both times slowing down without slowing enough to stop — but I will not tell you that yet, because tonight is not that night. Tonight is a Tuesday in October and it is raining and I am shelving the 700s and my life is fine.

It is fine. I want to be clear about that. I work. I eat. I have learned to cook exactly four dishes well, which is three more than survival requires. I read sixty-two books last year and remembered perhaps nine of them, which is a better ratio than most. On Thursdays I play chess with Mr. Adeyemi before he falls asleep, and I let him win approximately half the time, and neither of us acknowledges the mathematics of this. I am not unhappy. Unhappiness requires a direction, a place you're falling away from. What I have is more like weatherlessness — no storm, no sun, just a low grey ceiling of days, each one bearing down on the last with the gentle, total weight of a snowfall that never accumulates.

Loneliness, I have found, is easiest to mistake for peace when you schedule it. If your solitude has rituals — the same walk, the same counter seat at the dumpling place on Corvus, the same corner of the same library at the same hour — it stops feeling like exile and starts feeling like practice. Practice for what, I could not say. Perhaps for nothing. Perhaps solitude is simply a skill some of us develop in place of the one we failed at.

* * *

At three-forty the rain eased and the Grey Coat stood, returned Szymborska to the cart I'd left by the window — spine out, correctly, though I'd never shown her how — and paused beside me at the desk.

"You're here every night," she said. It was not a question, but it waited like one.

"I work nights," I said.

"That's not the same thing." She pulled her coat tighter. Under it she was small, bird-boned, maybe seventy, maybe fifty, age going ambiguous in people who live at night. "Being here and working here. One of those is a job."

"And which do you think it is?"

She smiled then, briefly, a crack of light under a door. "I think you'll find out." And she left, the door sighing shut behind her, the rain taking her footsteps.

I stood there holding a copy of *Moby-Dick* that someone had shelved in the 900s, presumably as a joke or a cry for help, and I felt the sentence settle over me like the damp. You're here every night. Being here and working here. One of those is a job.

I reshelved the whale where it belonged. I wiped down the tables. I turned off the reading lamps one by one, west wall first, then east, a ritual so old my hands did it while I thought about nothing, which is the highest achievement of ritual: thought performed elsewhere. At four the early shift would arrive — Devi, with her thermos and her terrible pop music and her relentless morning-person cheer, a woman who has never once asked me a personal question, which is why I would take a bullet for her.

In my head, the ledger updated itself. Student: departed at three-fifty, carrying Neruda, prognosis uncertain but improved. Drifter: still in biography, may have actually fallen asleep among the Kennedys, would need waking gently. Grey Coat: departed, cryptic, recurring. Weather clearing by dawn.

And then, because the mind is a shelf that files things where you least want them, another entry surfaced, unbidden, the way a name surfaces when you're falling asleep:

Three years. The mail has been piling up at a house fourteen miles north of here, in a green metal box on a post by a dead garden, and I have not opened it, and the county does not care that I am grieving, the county only knows that no one has answered.

I did not know yet, that night, what was in the pile. I knew only that the pile existed, the way you know a tooth is bad before it hurts — a low atmospheric pressure, a heaviness in the jaw of my life, some storm assembling itself offshore in the dark.

Devi arrived at four with her thermos and said, "Quiet night?"

"Unsettled," I said. "Clearing by morning."

She looked at me a little too long, the way people do when you've answered a question they didn't ask. Then she hung up her coat and turned on the terrible music, and the day began, and I walked out into the wet grey light with my collar up, past the dumpling place, home to Vesper Street, up four floors, past Mrs. Ferreira's tomatoes in their coffee cans, into my room with its open-mouthed suitcase under the bed, where I slept through the afternoon like something buried and dreaming, and did not dream of Alder Row, because I never do, because I have arranged my sleep the way I arrange everything else — carefully, ritually, around the one thing in the middle of the room.

The letter would arrive Thursday. But that, as I say, is tomorrow's weather.
