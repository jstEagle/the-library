## 12. The Year He Stopped Speaking

The winter his mother died, Tomás Ferreiro learned that grief has a sound.

It was not the sound he expected — not the keening of the aunts in the kitchen, not the rain on the tin roof of the workshop where she had spent forty years sanding wood beside him. It was smaller than that. It was the sound of her radio going quiet. She had kept it tuned to a station that played boleros after midnight, and for three days after the funeral the workshop held nothing but the tick of cooling glue pots and the small animal noises of his own breathing, which seemed suddenly loud, suddenly mortal, as if hearing itself were something that could be used up.

He did not know yet how right that fear would prove to be.

* * *

The diagnosis came six weeks later, on a Tuesday, from an audiologist with cold hands and a wall of framed certificates. Tomás sat in the padded booth with the headphones pressing his ears flat and listened to the beeps descend like birds leaving a tree, one by one, until there were none. The audiologist turned a dial. A frequency appeared and vanished. Appeared and vanished.

"High-frequency loss," she said, when he came out. "Moderate now. Progressive, most likely." She said it the way people announce weather in another country — factual, distant, nothing to be done about it here. "You'll want to think about hearing aids within the year. And you should learn some sign language. It's easier to start before you have to."

He thanked her. He paid at the desk. He walked out into the February light and stood on the clinic steps and realized that what frightened him was not deafness itself but the arithmetic of it: every song he loved had a number of remaining listenings, and the number was shrinking, and no one could tell him the rate.

His mother had gone deaf too, at the end. He remembered now — remembered what he had filed away as eccentricity — how she had stopped singing words in her last years and sung only shapes, her hands rising off the table when she talked, tracing curves in the air as if the melody lived there and she were pointing it out to someone standing behind him. He had thought it was age. He had thought it was memory loosening its grip.

It was neither. It was a language. He understood this all at once, standing on the steps, with the traffic roaring past him in a way he noticed, for the first time, sounded slightly wrong — slightly flattened, like music heard through a wall.

The language of the hands. Her language. The one she had half taught him at the kitchen table when he was small, before she decided a boy needed Spanish more than secrets, before the words thinned out and only the singing remained.

* * *

That spring he began losing words the way a man loses keys — one at a time, in ordinary places. The word for *hinge*. The word for *sparrow*. He would reach for them and find smooth absence, and then they would surface hours later, unbidden, while he planed a board. His doctor said stress. His brother said tiredness. Tomás said nothing, because saying nothing was becoming a skill, and because the person he would have told was the one person who could not hear him say it without laughing at him first, and he could not bear her laughter, kind as it was, aimed at this.

Because there was Ines.

He had known her five years by then — five years of Tuesday mornings in the reading room of the Biblioteca del Alba, of coffee he never finished because finishing it would end the visit, of jokes worn smooth as river stones that they told anyway, on purpose, the way you touch a scar to confirm it's still yours. Five years of the sentence assembling itself inside him, plank by plank, joint by joint, the way a luthier builds a body around a hollow: *I love you.* Simple. Two syllables in Spanish. He could carve it into walnut in under a minute. He could not, apparently, say it aloud in fifteen hundred days.

He knew why, or he believed he knew why. Saying it made it real, and real things could be lost. His mother had taught him that too, though not on purpose — she had loved a man once, before his father, a man who left for the north and never wrote, and she had kept the letter unsent that she'd written him, kept it sixty years in a biscuit tin, and when Tomás found the tin after her death the letter was there, yellowed, folded into eighths, addressed to a name he'd never heard her speak. Love, in his family, was a thing you built and sealed. A thing you made with your hands so your mouth wouldn't have to betray you.

So in April, in the year he stopped speaking — though he did not know yet that this is what history would call it, since history was only him, alone, at a workbench — he began to build.

* * *

It started as humming. He hummed while he worked; he had always hummed; the workshop ran on it the way clocks run on springs. But now the humming had a direction. It went toward a woman cataloguing books across town, and it carried, in its rises and falls, everything his throat refused. He noticed one night that the tune had settled, had stopped changing, had become a fixed thing with verses — and that the verses wanted words, and that the words, if they came in Spanish, would be the sentence, and he was not ready, might never be ready, and the deafness was coming down the road toward him like weather.

So he reached for the other language.

It rose out of him slowly, awkwardly, the way a limb wakes from numbness. His mother's hands-language — the workers' language, the one Dr. Anwari would one day call *the language of the displaced*, though he would not learn that term for years. She had sung in it when she thought he was asleep: hand-shapes riding on top of melody, meaning braided into pitch. It had been built, she told him once, the single time she spoke of it plainly, by people who were forbidden to speak together — field workers, mill workers, migrants moved from valley to valley — who needed to say true things in front of overseers and so learned to put their truths where authority doesn't look: in the hands, in the tune, in the space between the two. *A language for saying things you can't be caught saying,* she said. Then she laughed and ruffled his hair and never mentioned it again, and he had spent fifty years pretending he'd forgotten.

He had not forgotten. His hands remembered. That was the miracle and the terror of it: he would shape a phrase clumsily, testing, and feel it click into place like a tenon into a mortise, and understand, with a lurch, that he was saying something true.

So he built the song the way he built chairs. In secret. At night. Out of materials that would hold weight.

* * *

The first verse took him a month. It was a question — the language demanded questions open things; statements, his mother had shown him, merely closed them — and the question was the whole of his fear laid out in pitch and palm:

*Will you remain when I cannot hear you remain?*

He worked it and reworked it at the bench, humming the melody against the grain of the wood, watching sawdust shiver on the benchtop in time. He sang it with his hands over the unfinished chair backs, alone, at two in the morning, and the workshop felt less empty than it had since her funeral. For the first time in months he slept.

The second verse was harder. It was meant to be the answer — hers, imagined, hoped-for — and he caught himself writing what he wanted her to say rather than leaving room for what she actually might, and he tore up three drafts of notation before he understood the grammar properly: the answer verse in this language is not written by the asker. It is left open. A door frame without a door. You build the hinge side and wait.

So he left it open. It nearly killed him, that openness. Every night the empty verse sat on the page like a chair with no sitter, and every morning he resisted the urge to fill it, to script her, to make her easy. *This is what love is,* he told himself, surprising himself with the plainness of the word. *Leaving the second verse blank.*

The third verse he gave to his mother. Hand-shapes for flour and for river water, for the smell of cedar, for the radio playing boleros at midnight — a verse of gratitude to the dead woman whose tongue he was borrowing, tucked inside the song like a lining. He sang it once, complete, hands moving in the lamplight, and wept the way he had not wept at the funeral, privately, productively, the way you weep when a joint finally seats true.

And then the final verse. The confession. The sentence.

He wrote it in a single night in November, working until dawn, and when it was done he read it back — played it back, rather, hands and voice together, the full architecture of it — and understood what he had made. It was not a letter. Letters can be ignored, mislaid, answered politely. This was a vow, in the old workers' form, the form his mother had described only glancingly: *I give you my remaining hearing days.* All of them. Whatever number the audiologist's dial left him. Given over, entire, to her voice, her laughter, her Tuesday-morning coffee — so that even as the world went quiet, he would be spending his last currency on her, deliberately, joyfully, the way a man spends his last coins on bread he means to share.

No one says that alone. That was the rule underneath the grammar. The vow required the answerer to give something back in kind — her voice, her years, her own remaining whatever — and he could not ask that of her. Could not. Asking was the one verb the language forbade. You could offer a door; you could not push anyone through it.

So he did what his family did with love. He built it, and he sealed it.

* * *

The sealing took a week and felt like a burial. He copied the notation onto good paper — the invented hybrid script, part staff, part hand-diagram, the system his mother had scratched into flour on the kitchen table — unsigned, undated, wrapped in oilcloth against damp. He recorded himself performing it once, badly, on an old cassette machine, because some instinct told him paper alone wasn't enough, that a language lives in bodies and must be preserved in one. His voice cracked on the high phrases. His hands fumbled twice. He almost erased the tape and didn't, telling himself that imperfection was honest, that a luthier knows the difference between flawed and false.

Then he hid the score in the bottom of his tool chest, beneath the planes his grandfather had brought over the mountains, and mailed the tape — this took him four attempts and three returned envelopes — to an address he chose the way you throw a bottle into the sea: a pirate radio frequency he'd heard mentioned on a late-night program, a station that existed, as far as he could tell, somewhere above the city, in a rooftop, in rumor. He included instructions in block capitals, because instructions felt safer than explanations: PLAY IT IN OCTOBER. EVERY YEAR. UNTIL SOMEONE COMES ASKING.

He never signed it. He told himself the anonymity was prudence. He knew, in the honest hour of three a.m., that it was cowardice wearing prudence's coat — that he had built a confession designed to be received only if he failed to deliver it in person, a message in a bottle launched by a man still standing on the shore. If he ever found his courage, the tape would simply play to strangers, anonymous, harmless, a beautiful thing belonging to no one. If he never did — if the silence in his ears arrived before the sentence left his mouth — then October would come, some year, and the song would go out over the sleeping city, and she would hear it, and know.

Either way, he would not have to watch her face while he asked.

That was the reason. He would spend years calling it protection, consideration, patience. It was fear. Fear with excellent joinery.

* * *

The following Tuesday he came to the library as always, tools in their canvas roll, coffee cooling untouched beside the reading-room radiator, and Ines looked up from her cataloguing and smiled at him — that smile, the one that had been assembling itself across five years into something neither of them named — and said, "You look tired, Tomás. Are you sleeping?"

"I sleep like a cabinet," he said. "Closed and full of shelves."

She laughed. He drank his cold coffee to the dregs for the first time ever, just to have something to do with his mouth, and inside his chest the sealed song sat in its oilcloth like a heart in a drawer, beating anyway, beating regardless, because he had made it too well. Because that was the trouble with building things honestly: they outlast your reasons for hiding them.

Across the room, a patron dropped a book. The sound reached Tomás late and thin, arriving through cotton, and he smiled and nodded as if he'd heard it fully, and no one — least of all the woman watching him with those patient, cataloguing eyes — knew that the year he stopped speaking had already begun, and that somewhere above the city, in a rooftop shed he had never seen, a cassette was waiting for October.
