## 13. Coffee Going Cold

The first Tuesday of November arrived the way most Tuesdays did at the Biblioteca del Alba: without announcement, without mercy, smelling faintly of radiator dust and the lemon polish Marisol swore by and used only on the front desk, where it mattered, where the public could see.

Ines had been awake since four. Not because of the song — though the song was always there now, folded into the back of her skull like a letter tucked into a book she couldn't stop reopening — but because of a smaller thing, a domestic thing, the kind of thing that grows teeth overnight. She had been thinking about the way Tomás had laughed on Thursday. Not the laugh itself; the laugh had been fine, warm, correctly timed. It was the half-second before it. The pause where a laugh should have begun immediately, the small dead air between her joke and his response, as if the joke had to travel farther than jokes usually travel, as if it had crossed a border and been inspected.

She had told herself it was nothing. She was very good at telling herself things were nothing. She had five years of practice telling herself that the coffee he never finished was about the coffee, that the sentence they both carried around like contraband was about timing, about propriety, about the library's rules against distraction — anything, anything except what it was actually about.

But then there was Thursday's pause. And Wednesday's, when he'd answered a question she hadn't asked — or rather, had answered the question he thought she'd asked, which was not the one she'd asked at all, and she'd let it go because correcting him would have meant admitting she noticed. And Monday, when the chair leg cracked in the reading room like a pistol shot and every head turned except his.

Every head turned except his.

She stood at the staff entrance at 6:40 in the morning, keys in hand, breath fogging, and admitted it to the dark street in a whisper so quiet not even she could properly hear it:

*He's losing his hearing.*

The words didn't echo. The street took them and gave nothing back, which felt right. Some sentences are spoken only so the air has a record of them.

* * *

Tomás came at nine, as he always did on repair days, carrying his canvas roll of tools and a paper bag of media lunas from the panadería on Calle Juramento, still warm, their sugar already migrating through the wax paper. He nodded at Marisol, nodded at the security guard, nodded at the portrait of the founder that hung in the lobby looking perpetually disappointed by overdue fines. He did not nod at the clock, because Tomás had declared war on clocks years ago — *a man who watches time age gets old twice as fast,* he liked to say, one of their too-many-times jokes, worn smooth as a river stone from handling.

He set up in the reading room, at the long table by the north windows, where the light came in slanted and gold and made even his sawdust look ceremonial. He was re-gluing the joints of chair number eleven, the one with the loose arm that three generations of students had used as a chin rest while pretending to study. He worked the way he always worked: humming.

That was the thing. That was the needle under her skin all morning. He was humming the song.

Not the whole song — never the whole song, just fragments, the opening phrase and then a leap into the middle section, the part that rose like someone climbing stairs two at a time toward something they weren't sure waited at the top. He hummed it low, almost subvocal, more vibration than melody, the way a man hums who does not know he is doing it. His hands moved in the glue and the clamps and the fine sandpaper, and his mouth made the music, and between the two of them — the hands and the mouth — Ines understood that this was how Tomás Ferreiro thought. This was the sound of his interior.

And if the interior was going quiet, note by note, frequency by frequency —

She set down her catalog cards. She picked up two coffees from the break room instead, because ritual was armor and she needed hers.

"Coffee," she said, arriving at his elbow, at full volume, the way she always did.

His head came up. "You're an angel with a caffeine problem," he said, wiping his hands on his apron. "One day they'll build you a statue. Small. Out of sugar."

"Out of cold coffee," she said. "More honest."

He laughed — immediately this time, no border crossing, no customs inspection — and something in her chest unclenched and then clenched again, tighter, because of course today the near distances worked. Of course today he heard her coming. The universe was not going to make this easy, and the universe had never once made anything easy for Ines Villalobos, who had learned to read at two and lie to herself at six and love a man in installments for five years without ever once demanding the balance.

She put the cup at his elbow. He said thank you. She went back to the desk.

And then, at 11:20, when the reading room emptied for the lunch hour and the radiators ticked and the light went thin and gray over the north windows, she did the thing she had been rehearsing since four in the morning.

She tested him.

* * *

It began innocently, the way crimes do. She shelved her way along the 800s, working closer and closer to the reading room, and when she reached the doorway she stopped, out of his line of sight, behind the broad shoulder of the atlas case, and she spoke.

"Tomás."

Normal volume. Conversational. The volume of a woman standing beside him.

The sandpaper kept moving. The humming continued, mid-phrase, unbroken. Chair eleven received another pass of grit-220 and Tomás Ferreiro received no message from the world at all.

Her heart performed a maneuver she did not authorize.

She tried again, softer. A test within the test. "Tomás, the glue is on fire."

Nothing. The sandpaper whispered. He tilted his head slightly — and she saw it, she *saw* it, the small unconscious cant of his ear toward the table, toward the vibration of the wood, the way a man leans into a radio when the signal frays. He wasn't ignoring her. He was listening with everything he had left, and everything he had left was not enough to catch a woman's voice across twelve feet of quiet room.

She should have stopped there. Any decent person would have stopped there. But grief makes researchers of us all, and Ines had spent her whole life believing that knowledge was a form of care — that to understand a thing fully was to love it properly — and so she crossed the room, quietly, on the balls of her feet, until she stood directly behind his chair, close enough to see the sawdust caught in the curls at the nape of his neck, close enough to smell the hide glue and the cedar and, beneath it, the particular clean warmth of him that she had been cataloguing for years without ever entering it into the record.

She leaned down, lips near his ear, and said his name the way she had said it ten thousand times in the privacy of her own skull, with all the weight it carried, with the sentence hidden inside it like the flower pressed inside the returned book.

"Tomás."

Nothing.

Not a flinch. Not a turn. Not the smallest hitch in the humming, which went on and on, ascending its little staircase, climbing toward whatever waited at the top, while the man making it sat inside a silence so complete that her voice, her actual voice, her voice saying his name with five years of unsaid everything packed behind it, simply did not exist for him. She could have said anything. She could have said *I love you* — the sentence itself, the contraband, the whole smuggled cargo — and it would have fallen on him like snow falling on a window from the wrong side. Visible, perhaps, if he'd been looking. Felt, never.

She straightened up slowly. Her eyes were burning. She looked at the back of his bent head and thought: *he is building a life for me out of wood and melody, and he cannot hear the melody anymore, and I am standing here conducting experiments on his grief like it's a specimen.*

Then Tomás turned around.

Later she would reconstruct the sequence — how he must have caught the movement of her shadow across the table, the change in the light, some last channel of information still open to him — but in the moment it felt like being caught at something, because she was caught at something, standing too close behind a man who hadn't heard her come, her face no doubt arranged into an expression she hadn't approved.

"Ines." He blinked up at her, and the smile arrived a beat late, and she watched him assemble it, plank by plank, the way he assembled a chair. "How long have you been standing there?"

"Not long."

"You move like a librarian," he said. "Like a rumor. One day I'm going to turn around and you'll be a legend the patrons tell each other. *They say she still shelves the 900s. They say she's never once sneezed.*"

"That's true, actually. About the sneezing."

"It's the most frightening thing about you." He held up the chair arm, examining the joint. "Did you say something? When you came in?"

The question hung there. Honest, casual, fatal. An open door in a house of locked ones.

And here was the moment. Here was the fork in the road, lit plainly, no fog, no ambiguity: she could say *yes*. She could say *I called your name three times and you didn't turn*, and the whole architecture of pretense would come down in one afternoon, and he would have to look at her and know that she knew, and he would have to be known, fully, in the middle of a library, in the middle of his shrinking world, with nowhere to hide his hands.

Or she could say no.

She thought about the humming. She thought about how a man protects himself — how he builds a room around the wound and furnishes it with jokes and glue and unfinished coffee, and calls the room a workshop, and calls the wound a schedule conflict. She thought about how she would feel if someone walked into her room — the room she'd built around the sentence she'd never said — and switched on all the lights at once, without asking, without knocking, without letting her choose the hour of her own exposure.

"I asked if you wanted the second coffee," she said. "You were miles away."

"Miles," he agreed, and something in his shoulders came down half an inch, some tension releasing that she hadn't seen him raise. "I was in 1974. My mother had a kitchen radio that only got one station and got it badly, and she'd sing anyway, over the static, filling in the parts the signal ate." He set the chair arm down. "Best singer I ever heard. Half her notes were invented."

"She sounds like she'd have gotten along with the DJ in our break room."

"Nobody gets along with that DJ. That DJ is at war with music." He picked up his coffee — the first one, gone cold hours ago — and drank it anyway, all of it, which he never did, and Ines watched him do it and understood that he was thirsty in some way that had nothing to do with coffee, and said nothing about that either.

Complicity, she was learning, tastes exactly like cold coffee. Bitter, familiar, swallowed without comment.

* * *

The afternoon passed the way afternoons do when you have committed a small betrayal and named it kindness. She catalogued. He repaired. At three o'clock the obituary man came in — Señor Aramburu, who read the death notices aloud every day in a voice like a court clerk announcing verdicts, and who had once told Ines that he read them *so somebody hears the names twice, once in print and once in air, and a person isn't really gone until the second hearing* — and settled into his corner with the newspaper and began his liturgy of the departed.

*"Ramírez, Elda, aged eighty-one, beloved mother—"*

Tomás, packing his tools, glanced up. "Who died?"

"Elda Ramírez," Ines said. "Eighty-one."

"Ah." He shook his head gently. "My mother knew a Ramírez. Sewed buttons on in the textile hall. Hands like a sewing machine, sang like one too." He hoisted his canvas roll onto his shoulder. "Same family, maybe. Everything here is the same family, if you go back far enough."

He said goodbye. She said see you next month, and he said wouldn't miss it, and the door closed behind him with its pneumatic sigh, and the reading room held the shape of his absence the way a chair holds the shape of the person who just left it — invisibly, completely, for a while.

Marisol appeared at the desk with the radar she apparently aimed at Ines during all significant silences. "You've got the face."

"What face?"

"The face you make when you've decided something on someone else's behalf." Marisol squared a stack of returns that did not need squaring. "Spit it out or don't, but don't stand there cataloguing your own guilt. It doesn't fit the Dewey system."

Ines looked at her. Marisol had been the day librarian for nineteen years; she had buried a husband, raised a nephew, and survived three directors, and she could read Ines the way Ines read spines — instantly, by touch, in the dark.

"He didn't hear me," Ines said finally. Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "Today. I said his name from across the room and then — closer. Much closer. And he didn't hear any of it. And then he made a joke, and I let him, and now I'm — " She pressed her palms flat on the desk. "I don't know what I am. Accomplice? Coward? Both feel accurate."

Marisol was quiet for a moment. Outside, the traffic on the avenue made its ocean sound, the sound the city made all night and all day, the sound Ines had stopped hearing years ago and only noticed when it stopped, once, during the blackout, and the silence had been so enormous she'd had to sit down.

"You let him keep his secret," Marisol said. "That's not nothing. That's not cowardice, necessarily. But Ines — listen to me, because I'm older and I've wasted more time than you, which makes me an expert." She came around the desk. "There are two kinds of keeping quiet. There's the kind where you hold someone's secret like a bowl of water, careful, both hands, because they're not ready to spill it. And there's the kind where you hold it so long your arms go numb and you forget whose water it is and you never put it down and eventually you're just a woman standing in a library holding a bowl." She tapped the desk once, softly. "Which kind is this going to be?"

Ines didn't answer. She looked out at the reading room, at the empty chairs, at chair eleven with its fresh glue drying pale along the joint, holding together, needing time, needing pressure applied evenly and then left alone.

"I recorded the broadcast again last night," she said, which was not an answer, or was the only real one. "Off the break-room radio. Fourth time. Marisol, the lyrics aren't Spanish. They aren't anything. And the melody is his — it's *his*, note for note, the exact cadence of the way he hums when he sands. Someone recorded a song that Tomás Ferreiro has only ever sung inside this library, alone, to furniture, and sent it to a radio station, and I need to know how that's possible, and I don't even know where to start."

Marisol studied her for a long moment. Then she reached into the drawer where the day shift kept the things the night shift was supposed to handle and produced, of all things, a slip of paper — a return slip, a patron's request form, filled out in ballpoint.

"Start there," she said. "Kid came in Saturday. Sixteen, maybe seventeen. Wanted everything we had on radio transmission and shortwave bands. Checked out three books on antenna theory." She slid the slip across the desk. "Name's Kofi. Left a phone number in case we found anything else. Said — and I wrote this down because it was strange — he said, *tell the night lady I think someone's listening to her station.*"

Ines took the slip. The handwriting was young and urgent, letters leaning forward like runners at a block.

"His station," she repeated.

"That's what he said." Marisol shrugged, but her eyes stayed serious. "Strange kid. Or maybe not strange. Maybe everybody's running a station these days and just calling it something else."

That night, long after midnight, with the obituary man gone home and the insomniacs dispersed and the building breathing its slow building-breath around her, Ines sat at her desk with the return slip in one hand and her phone in the other, and the recording of the song playing soft enough that only she could hear it — which felt suddenly, horribly, like the whole condition of her life.

She dialed the number.

It rang once. Twice. On the third ring, a boy's voice answered, wide awake, as if he'd been sitting in the dark waiting for exactly this call, and he said:

"Biblioteca del Alba? Is this the night lady?"
