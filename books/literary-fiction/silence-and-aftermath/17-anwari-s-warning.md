## 17. Anwari's Warning

The lamp on Dr. Yasmin Anwari's desk had a habit of buzzing at a frequency only she seemed able to hear, a thin insectile whine she had long ago stopped trying to fix and had instead made into a kind of colleague. It buzzed now, past midnight in her office on the fourth floor of the linguistics building, where the radiators knocked like polite ghosts and the window held nothing but the orange smear of a streetlight and, beyond it, the city going about its insomniac business.

She had been looking at the photographs for six days.

Ines Villalobos had brought them to her in a manila folder, slightly damp at one corner as though it had been carried through rain clutched against a coat, and had laid them out with the careful hands of someone who handled fragile things for a living. Photographs of sheet music — if it could be called that. Five staves per page, but the notes were not notes. They were small drawings: open hands, closed hands, hands with fingers bent at angles no human joint would forgive, arranged along the lines like birds on wires. Between the staves ran a second script, denser, made of arcs and hooks that might have been punctuation in some alphabet that had never been printed anywhere.

Anwari had seen invented scripts before. Students brought them to her every few years, certain they had discovered a lost civilization when what they had discovered was their own boredom and a fountain pen. She knew how to be kind about it. She knew how to be kind even when she was not convinced.

But this was not that. This was not the fevered product of one mind. One mind, however brilliant, does not produce *consistency* like this — the same forty-odd hand-shapes recurring across pages with the discipline of an actual phonology, the same arc-script marking the same grammatical boundaries, the same small crescent drawn under certain figures again and again like a breath mark, like a held breath. This was a language that had been used by many people who needed to be understood quickly and could not afford ambiguity. Languages like that are not invented. They are *survived*.

She poured herself the dregs of the coffee she'd made at nine and drank it cold, because cold coffee was the honest taste of work, and turned back to the first photograph.

The song. The girl — Ines, she kept thinking of her as *the girl*, though she was clearly thirty-something and had the tired authority of someone who had worked nights for years — had played her a recording on a phone held between them like a candle. A man's voice, low and roughened, singing syllables that belonged to no language Anwari spoke, and she spoke seven. And beneath the voice, a melody so plain and patient it had gone through her like water through cloth.

She had listened twice in the office, then asked Ines to leave the recording with her, and Ines had hesitated — actually hesitated, physically, her thumb hovering over the phone — before letting it go. That hesitation told Anwari more than the folder did. Whatever this song was, it was not evidence. It was a wound someone else was asking her to touch.

Six days. She had transcribed what she could, cross-referenced the hand-shapes against everything in her memory and then against everything in the university's collections, and on the fifth day, near dawn, sitting in this same chair with her reading glasses pushed up into her hair, she had found it.

Not in a book. In a footnote.

A 1968 paper on gesture systems among agricultural laborers in the river valleys — dry, obscure, cited eleven times in fifty years — mentioning in passing that workers in certain orchards and packing houses, forbidden by landowners from speaking aloud during the long shifts, had developed *manual registers*: structured systems of hand-signs layered over whistled pitch, by which two people standing twenty rows apart could hold a conversation about anything from wages to weddings without a single audible word. The paper called these systems *ephemeral*. It assumed they had died with the fields.

They had not all died. One of them was lying on her desk in photograph form, and someone had written a love song in it.

Anwari took off her glasses and pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until the dark bloomed red. She was sixty-one years old and she had spent her life taking languages apart to see how they held grief, and still, every time, it undid her a little: the fact that when you forbid people to speak, they do not fall silent. They move the silence into their hands and keep talking.

* * *

The problem — and she had been circling it for two days, the way you circle a tooth that hurts — was the structure.

Songs have structures the way buildings do, and most of them are simple: verse, verse, chorus, out. But this one was built wrong, or built wonderfully, depending on how much mercy you had in you that day. She had mapped it on a legal pad, and the map looked less like a song than like a machine with a missing part.

The first section — four verses, in the arc-script densest with markers she now understood to be interrogative — was a question. Not a question *about* something. It was itself the question, the way a knock is itself a request. Her working translation of the opening line, scrawled and rescrawled in the margin of the legal pad, read: *I am making a door of my hands — will you come to it?* The grammar was strange, beautiful, untranslatable in any clean way: the verb for *make* and the verb for *remain* shared a root, so that every act of building was also an act of staying, and the question was not *will you come* so much as *will you stay where I have stayed.*

The middle section was longer, and it was here that the language showed its scars. Verses that circled and returned, that said things and unsaid them, that used the crescent breath-mark under nearly every figure — a whole passage of held breaths. She believed — tentatively, professionally, wanting very much to be wrong — that this section was an answer. An answer composed and then withheld. The singer had the words and would not give them; the melody kept approaching the cadence and turning away, six times, seven times, like a man walking to a door and finding reasons to be elsewhere. In the seventh verse the withheld answer collapsed into something she could only render as: *I practiced saying it until the saying wore the words away.* Whoever wrote this had loved someone for years without telling them, and had done it so carefully, so *methodically*, that the not-telling had become its own composition.

And then the final verse.

She had saved it. She had done everything short of locking it in a drawer. For two evenings she had refused herself, translating the easier material, re-checking the interrogatives, drinking tea she did not want — and tonight, finally, with the lamp buzzing and the radiator knocking its midnight knock, she had let herself look.

It was short. Three lines. And it broke her heart in the specific way that old things break hearts, which is quietly and with enormous weight.

Because the final verse was not complete. Not damaged — *complete-incompleteness* was its design. The last line ended mid-clause, on a hand-shape that functioned grammatically as an offering case — the marker you used when handing something over — followed by a blank space where the object should be, and then the closing figure, which was not a period but a shape she recognized from the footnote's examples: the sign that meant, roughly, *your turn.* The verse was built to be finished by someone else. It was structurally incapable of standing alone. Sing it to an empty room and it simply stopped, hanging, like a hand extended into air.

She sat back. The lamp buzzed. Somewhere below, a night bus sighed at a stop.

"It is not a letter," she said aloud to the empty office, because she had been alone long enough that speaking had become how she thought. "It is a door that needs two hands."

And that was the warning she owed the girl. Because Ines Villalobos had come to her as a scholar comes — bring me the meaning, decode it, hand it back to me finished — and Anwari could not do that, not honestly, not without committing a kind of violence. If she simply *translated* the final verse and gave it to Ines on a page, she would be handing her a door with one hand already on it, and the girl would walk through alone, carrying words that were never meant to be carried by one person. The song's own architecture forbade it. The answerer had to answer. Had to give something back — not sympathy, not gratitude, but a gift of equal weight, in the same language, from her own hands. That was not sentimentality. That was the grammar. The offering-case demanded an object; the closing sign demanded a speaker. The language itself had been built by people who understood that some things cannot survive being received passively.

She thought about the man. The luthier — Ines had mentioned him only glancingly, the way you mention weather, but the glance had lasted too long. He sang in a language he half remembered from his mother, and he was losing his hearing — Anwari had caught it in the recording, the way the vowels went slightly wide, the consonants over-articulated, the self-monitoring of a singer who can no longer fully trust his own ears — and he had written his confession in a tongue designed for hands, for sight, for *remaining*, and then he had sent it out onto the radio like a bottle into water, because apparently neither of them could say a plain sentence in a plain room.

Two people fluent in everyone's needs but their own. She had known such couples. She had *been* half of such a couple, once, in another country, in another life, before the sea and the years took their tithe. She knew exactly how those stories went when no one intervened. They went the way of the ephemeral languages: beautifully, thoroughly dead.

* * *

Near three in the morning she made fresh coffee and spread everything out again on the big table by the window — photographs, legal pad, the photocopy of the 1968 footnote, her transcription sheets fanned like a losing hand. She worked the final verse once more, slowly, testing each hand-shape against the lexicon she was building, and it was during this second pass that she noticed what exhaustion had hidden from her the first time.

Under the blank space — the space reserved for the second speaker's gift — there was a mark. Faint, in different ink, older ink, brown gone almost to rust. Not part of the notation. A word, in ordinary cursive Spanish, written small and slanted, and then scrubbed at, half erased, as though the writer had written it and immediately regretted it.

She got the magnifying lamp. She tilted the photograph through every angle the window light and the desk lamp together could offer.

Three letters survived the erasure. Possibly four. A capital that might have been an *I* or a fragment of an ornament, then what was certainly an *n*, then a letter that could be *e* or *c*, and then a ghost of a descender that might have been *s* or might have been nothing at all, just a fiber in the paper, just a wish.

*Ine—*

Or maybe not. Maybe her own tired eyes were doing what tired eyes do, which is find the face of the person you were just thinking about in every crowd.

But her pulse had picked up, and she made herself be rigorous, the way she taught her students to be rigorous, which is to say she failed completely. Because if it was that name — if the composer of this song had written the name of the woman it was for into the very space where the answer belonged, and then erased it, *couldn't wait and erased it* — then the song was not a message cast at random. It was addressed. It had always been addressed. The radio, the anonymity, the yearly October broadcast — all of it was a letter trying desperately to reach one specific house while pretending to be mail for everyone.

She wrote the fragment in her notebook, dated it, noted her uncertainty in proper scholarly fashion, and then sat looking at it for a long time.

There was something else. She had been assuming the manuscript Ines described — the folder in the library's uncatalogued donations, the librarian's note from 1971 calling the language "the speech of the displaced" — was a copy, a fair version made for the archive. But the erasure bothered her. Archives don't erase. Archivists preserve mistakes lovingly. Someone had erased this name in a moment of private cowardice, which meant this photograph showed a *working* document, a living draft, a page that had been in the composer's hands. Which meant the composer had access to the 1971 donation — or had *made* the donation. Or had learned the language from whoever did.

The language of displaced orchard workers, preserved in a city library's basement, surfacing in a love song sung by a chair-mender whose mother, Ines had said in passing — in passing, the way you say everything important — had spent years borrowing books from that same library.

Anwari wrote one more line in the notebook: *Ask the girl about the mother.*

Then she closed the notebook, and the lamp buzzed, and she understood that her role in this story was not translator after all. Translators finish things. What this needed was a witness — someone to stand at the door and refuse to open it for either of them, and to say, in the flat merciless accent of scholarship: *This cannot be done alone. That is not a flaw. That is the entire point.*

Tomorrow — today, technically; the sky beyond the streetlight was going the color of dishwater — she would call Ines Villalobos in. She would show her the structure: question, withheld answer, unfinished vow. She would watch the girl's face when she understood that the last verse was a hand extended across fifty years and one city street, waiting. And she would tell her the thing scholars are not supposed to tell anyone:

That some sentences are doors, and doors are not read. Doors are opened. From both sides. Or not at all.

She rinsed the cold coffee down the sink and washed the cup, and in the quiet of the washing she heard herself humming — the melody, the patient, turning-away melody — and stopped, startled, her wet hands dripping over the basin.

It was already in her. That was the other thing she would have to warn the girl about, the thing no one warned anyone about anymore: songs written in languages of the displaced do not stay where you put them. They get into the hands of everyone who listens. Somewhere across the city right now, she would have bet her career on it, a man was packing up his workshop or sanding a chair or lying awake beside his hearing aids, and the song was in him too, hanging unfinished, a door with one hand on the handle —

—and tomorrow she would have to tell Ines that the clock inside the music was real, that the final verse was dying to be completed, and that no dictionary on earth could do it for her.
