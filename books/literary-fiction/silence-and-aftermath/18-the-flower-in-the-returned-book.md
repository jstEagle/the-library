## 18. The Flower in the Returned Book

The book had been sitting on my desk for eleven days.

I want to say I hadn't noticed it, but that would be a lie of the comfortable kind, the kind librarians tell themselves about the things they keep within arm's reach. It sat at the edge of my blotter where the lamplight pooled thinnest, a slim volume in a cracked green cloth binding, *Poems of the Interior Rivers*, and pressed between its pages like a held breath was a flower I could not name — five white petals gone the color of weak tea, a center the shade of dried saffron.

It had come back through the return slot on the first of October, the same night the radio played Tomás's song to an empty city, though I wouldn't understand until much later how those two arrivals were braided together, how nothing that night came back alone.

The book had no due date slip. That was the first strangeness. Our older volumes carried the ghosts of their lending lives pasted inside the front cover — date cards, browned and curling, stamped with the dates of every hand that had borrowed them. This one's pocket was empty, the card missing, as if someone had once lifted it out deliberately, or as if the library itself had decided this particular book should travel without a record.

But libraries forget nothing. That is our one superstition and our one truth. We only misplace.

I turned to the catalog. The old system, the paper system, still lived in the sub-basement in rows of gray cabinets that smelled of cold dust and pencil shavings, and on nights when the computers sulked — which in October they did nightly, some argument between the building's ancient wiring and the season's damp — I worked from the cards the way my predecessors had, with a lamp pulled close and a magnifying glass Marisol called "the relic."

The card for *Poems of the Interior Rivers* was thick. Thicker than any card I'd seen outside the reference folios. When I drew it from the drawer it made a sound like a shuffled deck, and I understood before I read a single line that this book had been borrowed the way some people are loved: repeatedly, quietly, over a very long time.

I spread the borrowing slips across my desk under the lamp.

They went back decades. The earliest transaction recorded in the ledger copies — because the slips themselves had been replaced at least twice, the library's slow metabolism of paper — was 1968. Then 1971. 1974, twice in one year. A gap through the late seventies, then again in 1983, 1986, 1990. The handwriting changed from slip to slip, ink giving way to ballpoint, ballpoint to the librarian's stamp, but the pattern never varied: borrowed, returned, borrowed again. And beside nearly every return date, in the margin column where we noted condition — *water damage*, *binding loose*, *pages annotated* — the same notation appeared over and over, in different hands across thirty years:

*Flower enclosed.*

*Flower enclosed.*

*Returned with flower.*

No notes. No names beyond the borrower numbers, which in the oldest entries were written as bare sequences — B-1147, B-2210 — anonymous as coordinates. Whoever this reader was, they had taken the same book of poems home again and again across a lifetime and returned it each time with a flower tucked inside, saying nothing, explaining nothing, leaving the gesture to speak in a language the library had no grammar for.

I sat with that for a long while. The night shift has a way of making small mysteries enormous; without daylight to scale them down, a pressed flower can become a cathedral door. Around me the library performed its two a.m. liturgy — the radiator ticking, the obituary man in his corner chair turning pages with the deliberation of a man feeding birds, someone in the stacks coughing once, softly, like a question asked into a pillow.

I did what I always do with a mystery too large for my desk. I took it downstairs.

* * *

The sub-basement at three a.m. is the closest thing this city has to a held breath. The fluorescent tubes down there hum at a pitch just below hearing, felt more than heard, a vibration in the molars. The donor archives live along the east wall: uncatalogued bequests, boxes of unclaimed property, the sediment the library accumulates when people die and their families don't know what to do with what remains.

I wasn't looking for anything specific. I told myself I was cross-referencing borrower B-1147 against the old registration ledgers, which was true, but the deeper honesty is that I had begun to feel followed by coincidences, and when a night librarian starts feeling followed, the only cure is to turn around and face whatever it is.

The registration ledgers lived in four bound volumes the size of tombstones. B-1147 fell in the 1967–69 volume, in a register kept in a beautiful, severe hand, each entry ruled and numbered like a hymnal.

*Borrower 1147: Ferreiro, Elena. Calle de los Almendros, 14. Occupation: seamstress.*

I read the name twice. Three times. The fluorescents hummed their sub-audible note and the basement held its breath around me and the name sat on the page doing absolutely nothing, being absolutely ordinary, while everything in my body rearranged itself around it.

Ferreiro.

Tomás had spoken of his mother exactly twice in five years, both times in the register he used for the dead and the distant — brief, warm, closed. She had been a seamstress. She had sung while she worked, songs in a language he'd never learned properly, melodies that got into his hands instead of his memory. He'd said that once, sanding a chair leg, not looking at me: *My mother sang in a language she didn't teach me. It got into my hands anyway.* I had thought it was a figure of speech. I had thought most of what Tomás said was figure of speech, which is perhaps the cruelest thing you can decide about a person who means every word.

Elena Ferreiro. Borrower 1147. The woman who had borrowed *Poems of the Interior Rivers* eleven times across twenty-two years and returned it each time with a flower and no explanation.

My hands were not steady. I noticed them the way you notice weather — from the inside.

I pulled the ledger closer and read her full registration. Date joined: March 1963. There was a second column I'd never seen used in the modern system, headed *Referrer*, and in it, for Elena Ferreiro, someone had written: *Sociedad de Trabajadores del Alba — sala comunitaria.* The workers' society. The community hall.

The hall whose address Kofi had given me off a forty-year-old envelope. The hall scheduled for demolition. The hall with the carved wall of hands I had not yet seen but already dreamed about, its refrain repeating behind my eyelids like a song stuck at the threshold.

I stood very still in the humming dark and let the threads find each other. The song on the radio, composed in a language built by migrant workers forbidden to speak aloud. The manuscript in the uncatalogued donations, unsigned, in invented notation, flagged by a librarian in 1971 as *the language of the displaced*. The community hall where that language had lived in hands and walls. And now this: a seamstress named Elena Ferreiro, referred by the society itself, who for two decades carried the same book of poems home and back like a letter she was both sending and receiving, marking each passage with a flower because — because what? Because flowers were her punctuation. Because the language she thought in had no words for what the book gave her, so she answered in the only dialect she had left.

She hadn't been leaving notes. She'd been having a conversation.

And the library — this building, these shelves, this desk where I sat every night cataloguing other people's returns — had been holding both ends of it all along, patient as stone, waiting for someone to read the correspondence.

* * *

I brought the book up to the reading room and set it under the good lamp, and for the first time since it came back through the slot I opened it slowly, the way you open something you now understand to be addressed.

The poems were Spanish translations of work originally written in a river dialect, the kind of regional verse that gets anthologized and forgotten. But Elena Ferreiro had not been reading the poems. I saw that within minutes. Her traces were elsewhere — in the margins, faint as breath on glass. Not words. Marks. Small drawn symbols in pencil, so light I had to angle the pages against the lamp to catch them: a curve like a cupped palm. Two short strokes like fingers folding. A spiral that might have been an ear, or a shell, or a coil of thread — she had been a seamstress; everything in her hand probably looked like thread eventually.

Hand-shapes. Pitch-marks. I recognized the family resemblance immediately, the way you recognize a stranger's nose in a child's face. These were the same shapes Dr. Anwari had circled in red on the manuscript score, the same notation logic — meaning mapped onto gesture and tone — that she said belonged to the workers' language, the language of the displaced, the language Tomás's mother sang in and never taught him because there was no one left to teach it *to*.

Except there had been. There was a boy on Calle de los Almendros absorbing it through his skin while she sewed, storing it somewhere below memory, below hearing, in the place where the hands keep what the mind releases.

Elena Ferreiro had been writing in the margins of a library book in a dying language, and no one had stopped her, because no one could read her, and the book had circulated for fifty years carrying her marginalia like a message in a bottle that kept getting refilled and re-thrown.

I checked the last slips again. The final recorded borrowing was 1990. After that, nothing — the card system phased out, the book shelved, the conversation suspended mid-sentence for thirty-four years. Until October the first, when it came back through my slot with a fresh flower inside and no borrower number anyone could find.

That was the part I kept circling back to, the part that made the hair on my arms rise despite the radiator's warmth. Someone had returned that book three weeks ago. Recently enough that the petals still held a ghost of softness. Which meant either the ledger was incomplete —

— or Elena Ferreiro's habit had outlived Elena Ferreiro, passed down like the songs, like the hand-shapes, like everything else that gets into the hands whether or not the mind consents.

I thought of Tomás at his workbench, humming a song no one had ever recorded, sanding wood in time to a melody his mother sang before he could remember hearing it. I thought of him arriving monthly to fix our chairs, always choosing the ones nearest the poetry shelves, though he claimed it was because the light was better there.

The light was not better there. I had worked nights in that room for six years. I knew exactly where the light fell.

* * *

Marisol arrived at seven, as she always did, bringing coffee and the day's weather in her voice. I was still at my desk. The book was still open under the lamp. I had not moved in what the clock claimed was an hour and my body claimed was a minute.

"You look like you found something," she said, setting the cup down. Marisol could read me the way I read catalog cards — fast, accurately, with a certain professional pity.

"I found who the flower book belongs to."

"Belonged to."

"No." I turned the book toward her, careful of the petals. "Borrowed eleven times. Returned with a flower every time. Borrower registered in 1963 — Elena Ferreiro." I watched the name land. Marisol had worked here longer than I had; she had known the old patrons, the neighborhood, the families. Her face did the thing faces do when a long-ignored drawer slides open: recognition arriving ahead of comprehension.

"Ferreiro," she repeated. "The luthier's mother?"

"The luthier's mother."

Marisol sat down slowly across from me. For a moment neither of us spoke, and the reading room filled the silence with its morning sounds — chairs shifting, the first insomniacs yielding the territory to the first commuters, the obituary man folding his paper with the crisp finality of a man closing a small, well-kept account.

"She used to come in on Thursdays," Marisol said at last, quietly. "Before your time. Small woman, silver braid, gloves even in summer. She'd sit right there—" she pointed at the table by the poetry shelves, the table with the good light, and I almost laughed, almost cried, at the confirmation of it— "and read that same green book. She never talked. But she'd leave a flower in it when she returned it, and old Amparo, the head librarian then, told us never to remove it. Said the book was *corresponding.* Those were her exact words. We thought it was sentiment."

"It wasn't sentiment."

"No." Marisol looked at the pale petals, the ghost-marks in the margins, and her voice dropped further. "Ines. If Elena Ferreiro was writing in the workers' language — if that's what these marks are — then the song on the radio, the manuscript downstairs, the hall, all of it—"

"Was hers first." I said it and felt the floor of the story shift beneath me, the way floors shift when you realize the building has more rooms than the blueprint showed. "Or hers before it was anyone's. Tomás didn't compose that song out of nothing, Marisol. He transcribed it. Or remembered it. Or finished it. His mother's language, his mother's book, his mother's flowers — he's been surrounded by this his whole life and doesn't know the half of it. Maybe doesn't know any of it. Maybe that's why the melody comes out of him like breathing. He thinks he made it up. He inherited it."

"And the book coming back now?" Marisol asked. "Three weeks ago? With a fresh flower?"

"That," I said, "is the part I intend to find out."

Because there was one more thing, one detail I hadn't yet said aloud, the detail that had kept me at this desk past sunrise: the return slip from October the first, the one with no borrower number, had not been blank. At the bottom, in pencil, in a hand I had spent five years watching sign repair invoices and joke-laden notes left on my desk — *the third chair from the window lies about its wobble* — there was a single mark.

A small drawn symbol. A curve like a cupped palm.

The same shape Elena Ferreiro had penciled into the margins fifty years ago. The shape Anwari's lexicon glossed, provisionally, tentatively, with a word the old language used the way we use *stay* — except larger, older, meaning something closer to *remain*, to *keep what cannot be kept*, to hold a thing past the natural end of its holding.

Tomás had returned the book. Tomás, who never borrowed books, who claimed print made him sleepy, had taken his dead mother's green volume home sometime in September and brought it back on the first of October with a fresh flower and his mother's hand-sign penciled at the bottom of the slip — and had said nothing, explained nothing, the way she had, the way the whole family apparently conducted its deepest business: silently, beautifully, in a language the rest of us were only beginning to learn existed.

He knew. He didn't know that he knew. He was losing his hearing and answering his mother's fifty-year-old letters with her own alphabet and calling it coincidence to my face.

Outside, the October sky had gone the color of wet slate, and the first wind of the storm season was worrying the trees along the avenue, shaking down the year's leaves early. Marisol noticed me noticing it.

"Storm tonight," she said. "They're saying it'll be bad."

I looked at the book, at the flower, at the small cupped hand at the bottom of the slip, and felt the whole night tilting toward something — a convergence, a gathering of weather.

"Good," I said, though I couldn't have told you yet why.
