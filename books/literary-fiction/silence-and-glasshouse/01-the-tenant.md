## 1. The Tenant

Nine days after the funeral, August Joubert woke in his mother's house at six-forty in the morning, as he had every morning since, and lay still in the narrow bed of his boyhood, listening to the pipes knock twice in the wall the way they always had, and thought: *I should go home.*

He did not go home. Home, at the moment, was a flat in Bristol with a dying fern and a lease he'd renewed in February with the cheerful confidence of a man who had no idea that in April his mother's heart would simply, without drama, decide it had finished. He had said as much to the GP — *no drama, just finished* — and the GP had nodded with the professional patience of someone who hears this formulation weekly and knows it to be both true and useless.

So August got up, made coffee in the dented aluminium percolator that had outlived three kettles and one marriage, and walked down the gravel path to the glasshouse nursery that had been in the Joubert family since 1951, carrying a legal pad and a pencil, because that was the only thing he knew how to do anymore: make lists.

* * *

The nursery occupied two acres behind the house: four glasshouses of diminishing grandeur, a propagation shed, a potting yard, and a sales bench that had not sold anything to the general public since Odile's hands had gotten too stiff for the work, roughly two years ago, though she had never said so and had continued to open the gate at nine each morning out of what August now suspected was pure defiance of the obvious.

He started, as a tenant would, with the front door.

The first glasshouse — his grandfather had called it the Cathedral, on account of the ridge height and the light — smelled the way it had always smelled: wet loam, cut stems, the faint mineral sweetness of capillary matting. August stood in the doorway with his pencil poised and found he could not move. Not from grief, particularly. From the peculiar paralysis of a man who has entered a room where he has no function. He had been coming here his whole life, and he had never once, he realised, been *needed* here. His mother had run the place the way other people breathe. August had been permitted to help, occasionally, the way a child is permitted to hold the torch.

He wrote on the pad: *Glasshouse 1 — orchids, approx. 200 pots, various.* Then he crossed out *approx.* because his mother would have known the exact number and he felt the imprecision as a kind of disrespect, and then he wrote *approx.* again because he did not know it and lying on a legal pad seemed worse.

He counted instead. It took forty minutes. Two hundred and seventeen, not counting the eleven trays of seedlings on the propagation bench, which he decided were not pots and therefore exempt.

* * *

By ten he had moved to the office, a lean-to room off the potting yard that smelled of compost and rubber bands. Here the inventory changed character entirely, because here the nursery stopped being plants and started being paper.

There was a biscuit tin — a real one, McVitie's, the lid painted with a Scottish loch that had never existed — full of invoices. August sat on the swivel chair, which complained under him in a note he recognised from childhood, and began sorting.

Unpaid: the water board, naturally. The electricity, naturally. A supplier of orchid bark in the Netherlands who had been writing increasingly wounded letters since October, each one more politely devastated than the last, the final one simply reading, in full, *Dear Madame Joubert, we remain your devoted servants.* August felt a pang of genuine affection for the Dutch and put the tin aside to deal with later.

Paid: everything else, it seemed. His mother had paid the seed supplier, the pot supplier, the insurance, the rates, the man who serviced the boiler, and — August paused here — a piano tuner in Exeter, twice a year, every year, going back decades. This puzzled him mildly. There was a piano in the house, an upright in the back parlour, and he had no memory of anyone playing it. His mother had hummed, endlessly — while grafting, while potting, while telling him off — but he could not recall a single occasion of her actually sitting at the keys. And yet she had paid to keep it in tune as faithfully as she'd paid the water board, and the water board she had actually used.

He wrote on the pad: *Piano tuner — ask Colette.*

Under the tin of invoices there was a ledger, and in the ledger, in his mother's small upright hand — a hand that slanted forward like someone perpetually about to set off — the sales records of forty years. August turned the pages slowly. The handwriting changed over the decades: looser in the seventies, cramped in the eighties, and then, after a gap he couldn't account for in the winter of 1990, resuming in a hand that was slightly larger, as though she had decided to stop apologising for taking up space on the line.

He caught himself reading the ledger not as an inventory but as a diary. *3 Cypripedium, Mrs. Tregose, on account.* *1 tray violas, donated, church fete.* *Orchid, no charge, for the boy at the garage whose mother is poorly.*

*The boy at the garage whose mother is poorly.* August put the ledger down. His throat did something complicated and produced nothing. He had been trying, for nine days, to cry, in the way one tries to remember a word — probing around the place where the feeling should be and finding only a smooth blank. He was not, he told himself, in denial. He had organised the funeral. He had chosen the hymns. He had thanked the vicar, the neighbours, the forty-odd people who came, several of whom had said *she was one of a kind* in a tone that suggested they meant it as praise and were not entirely sure.

He simply could not feel it where he was standing. It was like being locked out of his own house and watching the lights go on and off in the rooms inside.

* * *

At noon, his aunt Colette arrived, as she arrived every day, with a Tupperware of something and an opinion.

Colette was his mother's younger sister by four years and her senior by several leagues in the matter of certainty. She was small and square and wore her white hair in a bun that had the structural integrity of masonry. She had helped raise August after his father Bram died — quietly, in a deckchair, when August was nine — and she had never once in thirty years asked him how he was in a tone that permitted an honest answer.

"Cassoulet," she announced, setting the Tupperware on the office desk among the invoices. "You're too thin. Grief burns you up like a fever."

"I'm eating."

"Eating *cassoulet*, or eating?"

"Eating."

She looked at the legal pad, the biscuit tin, the ledger. She looked, for a moment, at August, and something moved behind her eyes and was firmly shut away, like a drawer.

"Your mother kept accounts like a nun," Colette said. "Everything written down. Everything except the things that mattered."

"What things?"

"The things that don't go in ledgers." She straightened a pot of cuttings on the windowsill that did not need straightening. "Have you been up to her room yet?"

August had not. He had slept in his own narrow bed down the hall, walked past her door forty times a day, and not opened it. The door was not locked. That was the thing. Nothing was locked; the house simply waited, politely, for him to be ready, and he was not ready, and the house went on being patient, which was worse than pressure.

"No," he said.

Colette nodded, as if this were correct. "Well. When you do — " she began, and then stopped, which was unlike her. Colette did not stop sentences. She finished them and then had seconds.

"When I do?"

"When you do," she said, "you'll find it's just a room. That's all I'll say. It's just a room, and you shouldn't let it tell you otherwise."

"Aunt Colette, that's not — that doesn't mean anything."

"Neither does cassoulet," she said, "but it does you good." And she went out to inspect the glasshouses, walking the gravel with the flat decisive tread of a woman who had been walking this nursery since she was six and considered it, in some legal sense that no solicitor would uphold, half hers.

* * *

The afternoon he spent in Glasshouse 2, which the family had always called the Long House, and which held the stock plants: the fuchsias his grandfather had bred, the geraniums that came back from cuttings like bad news, and along the whole north wall, in rows on raised benches, the orchids.

Not the show orchids of Glasshouse 1 — those were the money, the *Cymbidiums* and the slipper orchids that Mrs. Tregose and her kind had collected for decades. The north wall of the Long House held something else. A single variety, propagated over and over for as long as August could remember: a small, unassuming *Odontoglossum*-type thing with a white flower freckled at the lip with a violet so deep it looked bruised. His mother had called it — he searched for the name and found it, absurdly, immediately, as though it had been waiting — *her signature.* She had given cuttings to every customer she liked, every neighbour who had a bereavement, every vicar, every teacher. Half the village, August suspected, had this orchid on a windowsill and didn't know they were holding a piece of his mother.

He took down a pot and looked at it properly for the first time in years. The plant was rootbound, the compost tired, the leaves a little yellow at the tips. It had been waiting, too.

He should repot it. He knew, distantly, that he knew how — he had been made to learn, one bored summer, and had resented every minute — but his hands hovered over the root ball without committing. This was the whole of him at present, he thought: a man hovering over a root ball. Competent in theory. Unable to begin.

He put the pot back on the bench, exactly where it had been, and wrote on the pad: *Orchids (north wall) — repot. Ask Colette about compost order.*

He had written *ask Colette* four times now. It was becoming a refrain. He was forty-six years old and his entire plan for the nursery consisted of asking his aunt.

* * *

At four o'clock he did the thing he had been avoiding, which was walk the perimeter.

The nursery was bounded on two sides by hedgerow, on one by the lane, and on the fourth by the field that had belonged, until his father's death, to the Vos farm — though the Vos name had left the district twenty years ago and the field belonged now to a horse sanctuary run by a woman named Denise who waved at August with genuine warmth and no idea who he was.

He walked it slowly. The gravel, the grass paths, the cold frames with their lights half off their hinges. At the back of the propagation shed he found the heap — his mother's compost heap, turned weekly for fifty years, a black sweet-smelling cathedral of its own. Nothing wasted. Everything that died here went into the heap and came back as something living. She had said this to him once, when he was small and had cried over a frost-killed seedling: *Nothing in a nursery is lost, August. It's just waiting to be used again.*

He stood at the heap for a long time. The wind moved the glass above the Long House with a sound like slow breathing.

He could not cry and he could not leave. He understood this now as a single condition, not two. The grief was in there — he could feel its mass, the way you feel furniture in a dark room — but it would not come to him, and he would not go to it, and so they stood in the dark together, politely, like strangers at a wake.

* * *

Colette had left, leaving instructions about the cassoulet (oven, thirty minutes, *do not* microwave) and a look at the office door that August chose not to interpret.

In the evening he went back to the ledger, because the ledger was safe. He was halfway through 1987 when he found the entry that stopped him.

It was small. It was, on its face, nothing. A line in the sales column: *Piano tuner, Exeter — paid, half-yearly, standing.*

He had seen it already. But this time, below it, in the margin, in his mother's hand, there was a note — the only marginal note in forty years of ledger, as far as he'd seen. Not an account. Just four words, slanting forward, about to set off:

*Tune it for him.*

August sat with that for a long while. The pipes knocked in the wall. Outside, a car went down the lane with its radio going, some pop thing fading in and out, and the last light came through the office window and lay across the ledger like a hand.

*Him.* His father had died in 1994, and had never played the piano, as far as August knew. Had never done anything louder than mending a fence and reading the paper. And the tuner entries went back before the marriage — he'd have to check, but he was nearly sure — back into the seventies, back before August, possibly back before Bram.

He wrote on the pad, under *Piano tuner — ask Colette*: *Tune it for him — who is him?*

Then he sat in the swivel chair, which complained, and looked at his list. *Glasshouse 1 — 217 pots. Glasshouse 2 — repot. Invoices — tin. Ledger. Piano — him.* It was a good list. It was a tenant's list. A man cataloguing a property he did not own, itemising a life he had walked past every day of his childhood without once opening the door.

Nine days, and he had not opened her door.

He got up, walked down the gravel in the blue dark, let himself into the house, and stood in the corridor. Her door at the end, ajar an inch, the way she'd always left it. The house patient around him.

He did not open it tonight. But he stood there long enough that he could no longer pretend he was merely passing, and he understood — with the eerie, weightless clarity of a man who has been feeling nothing very efficiently for over a week — that the inventory was wrong. He had been listing the wrong things. The plants and the invoices were the nursery's surface, and his mother had never once, in forty years of ledgers, written down the things that mattered.

Tomorrow, he thought. Tomorrow he would open the door.

He slept badly, and in the morning the door stood ajar an inch, and behind it, in the corner of a room he had never entered as an adult, something wooden waited under the window — a cedar chest with a small brass lock — and it was, he would discover within the hour, the only thing in the house that was locked at all.
