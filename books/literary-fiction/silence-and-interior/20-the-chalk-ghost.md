## 20. The Chalk Ghost

The gray sedan was not outside the library that night, and somehow that was worse.

Three nights it had sat at the curb, patient as a heron. Tonight the curb was empty, the street washed clean by an early rain, and Rafael kept checking the mirrors of the bus anyway, cataloguing every pair of headlights behind them, every man in a coat. Paranoia, she told herself, is just attention with nowhere to go. She had attention to spare. She had been spending it for weeks, on microfilm and margin initials and the particular way Aldous Brandt said *Vesper Lane* as though the words tasted of money.

She got off two stops early. Walked the last mile through streets that still remembered her feet, though they'd been someone else's feet, smaller, twenty years ago.

The house stood at the end of Vesper Lane the way a held breath stands in a body.

Boarded now — Meridian's plywood, stamped with a permit number she had memorized from the file. The demolition notice was zip-tied to the front door, its corners already curling. *NINETY DAYS* had been crossed out by some municipal hand and replaced with *60*. The oak in the side yard had dropped its first leaves; they lay on the dead grass like small brown hands, palms up.

Rafael stood on the sidewalk for a long time, doing the thing she did now whenever she came here, which was to let the vertigo come and not fight it. Grief, she had learned, arrives less like sorrow than like the floor tilting a few degrees — everything the same and nothing level.

The chalk ghost was still there.

Or some of it. A child's figure on the front step, drawn in the fat white chalk her father bought by the box, the kind that came in sticks like sugar cane. Rain had taken the head, the arms, most of one leg. What remained was a torso and a suggestion of motion, a body caught mid-turn, as if the little chalk person had heard something behind it and was looking back.

She had drawn it. She was almost sure she had drawn it, the last summer, the summer before. She could not remember what she had been drawing *from* — a game, a story her father told, something about a ghost that lived in houses and only wanted to be counted. *You have to count them or they get lonely,* he'd said, or she thought he'd said, or she had invented him saying it because she needed him to have said it. Twenty years of a voice in your head and you can no longer swear to a single sentence.

*Count them or they get lonely.*

She looked at the house. One ghost, accounted for.

"I'm counting you," she said out loud, and her breath made a small cloud, and the house did not answer, and she went up the steps anyway.

* * *

The back door was the one her father always forgot to lock. That door was gone now, or replaced, but the plywood over the kitchen window had been pried loose by some earlier pilgrim — a squatter, a teenager, a surveyor with a conscience — and the gap was exactly the width of a woman who had spent her life fitting herself into small rented spaces.

She went in through the kitchen the way she had gone out through it, once, at eleven years old, to sit under the oak and not hear her parents arguing.

Inside, the dark was total and then, as her eyes adjusted, it was not. The city's light came through the board seams in thin blades, striping the kitchen floor. She saw the counter where her mother had rolled dough for meat pies, the window over the sink where the dish rack used to stand, the doorframe where pencil marks had climbed — *Rafael, age 5, age 6, age 7* — until someone had sanded them away, or painted over them, or she had dreamed them.

The smell was the worst part. Not rot exactly. Rot she had expected. It was the smell of *stopped time* — dust and old plaster and the ghost of whatever they'd last cooked, some spice that had soaked into the walls so deeply the walls had become it. Her mother's kitchen. Her mother's turmeric and thyme, still here, still faithful, after the woman herself had fled the address and the name both.

"Okay," Rafael whispered, to herself, to the house. "Okay."

She had a plan. She'd had it for two weeks, turning it over on the night shift between shelving carts, and the plan was simple to the point of desperation: the wardrobe.

Because of a memory she could not verify and had stopped trying to. She was small — four, five — and her father was moving the wardrobe in the back bedroom, the tall dark one with the lion's-foot feet, and he was grunting with the effort and sweating, and she was sitting on the bed watching him, and he said — and here her memory did something it had never done before, it *held*, it gave her the sentence whole, in his voice, in his accent, the soft western vowels her mother had spent years sanding out of their household —

*"If anyone ever asks, Raffie, the house keeps its own accounts."*

He'd said it laughing. She was almost sure he'd said it laughing. And then he'd pushed the wardrobe back against the wall, and it had made a sound she could still hear, a wooden groan, and he'd wiped his forehead and lifted her down off the bed and that was all. A nothing moment. A moment with no weight at all, for twenty years, until a junior clerk named Theo Marsh had laid a misfiled report on a library table and said, *someone wanted that block empty,* and the moment had come back with its full tonnage.

The house keeps its own accounts.

She went down the hall with her phone's flashlight on low, cupped in her palm so the light fell in a small obedient pool. The hallway was narrower than memory. The wallpaper — her mother had hung it herself, Rafael had watched, had handed her the smoothing brush — still clung in long torn ribbons, cream roses gone gray. Her footsteps raised dust that hung in the light blades like something stirred but not yet wakened.

The back bedroom door was closed. It opened with a sound like a long exhale.

The wardrobe was still there.

That undid her, more than the kitchen, more than the chalk. Everything else could be explained — the house had been sealed, not emptied, Meridian wanting the contents to go with the structure, no reason to clear a room no one would see. But the wardrobe, standing in the corner exactly where a man had groaned it into place a quarter century ago, was so precisely, so *stupidly* itself — dark wood, lion's feet, one door hanging slightly crooked — that Rafael stood in the doorway and put her hand over her mouth and breathed through her fingers for a full minute, because for that minute it was not a wardrobe, it was her father's hands, it was the groan of the wood, it was *Raffie, come help me push,* and she was five years old and the world was one room and everyone in it was still alive and still lying to her.

Then the minute passed, because minutes do, and she crossed the room.

The wardrobe was empty of clothes, of course. Empty of everything, it seemed — she swept the light through the hanging space, over the shelves, into the corners. Dust. A coat hanger, wire, bent. A dead moth in the corner like a folded gray letter no one had opened.

She knelt. The lion's feet sat on a floorboard that looked like every other floorboard, except — she ran her fingers along the base of the wardrobe, along the wall side — except that the board nearest the wall was scuffed at its edge, gouged, as if it had been pried up before. As if it had been pried up by a man in a hurry, in bad light, with the wrong tool, and pushed back down with his palm and never touched again.

Her hands were shaking. She made them stop, or made them stop enough. She had no pry bar; she had a library pen, a flat steel letter opener from her desk drawer that she had told herself she was bringing for reasons she hadn't examined. She worked it into the gouge, and the board groaned — the same groan, she would swear later, the same wooden voice — and resisted, and she put her whole weight on it, kneeling on the dusty floor of the house that had erased her, and the board came up with a crack like a knuckle.

Underneath: a cavity, shallow, lined with what had once been a plastic bag, now crumbled to flakes.

And a ledger.

* * *

It was a school exercise book. That was the thing she could not get past, then or ever. Not a safe-deposit box, not a lawyer's envelope, not some cinematic black binder. A green-covered school copybook of the kind sold in every corner shop in the city for less than a dollar, the kind she herself had filled with bad handwriting at that same kitchen table, *Rafael Kavanagh, Grade 4* printed in the front in her mother's neat hand.

In the front of this one, in her father's hand — she knew it instantly, the forward-leaning letters, the way he crossed his t's like small checkmarks, *approved,* she thought, *he wrote like everything needed approving* — in the front was written:

*Duplicate. If found, I was right.*

She sat down on the floor of the bedroom, cross-legged, the way she had sat for homework, and she read.

It was a ledger. That was the only word for it, though it was also a diary and an accusation and a suicide note that had never needed to be a suicide note because he had, apparently, intended to survive it. Columns. Dates running from March 1997 to September 1998. Names — some she recognized from the harbor authority files she'd spent weeks in, some she didn't, some initials where names should have been, as if he'd lost his nerve partway through and then, page by page, found it again. Payments. Amounts, in his small careful figures, with the receiving party marked and the source of the money traced backward, entry by entry, like a man following a river upstream to its polluted spring.

And annotations. That was what made it more than paper. In the margins, in pencil, in handwriting that deteriorated as the pages turned — the early entries neat as a report, the late ones slanting, pressed hard, the pen tearing the paper in places — her father had argued with himself in writing.

*J. O'Rourke says take the money and forget the pilings. Says everyone does. Says the families get the settlement either way.*

*Settlement is not the same as safe. I keep writing that sentence and no one reads it.*

*They offered again. More this time. I said no and then I said let me think about it and I hate that I said let me think about it.*

*If I take it, it buys time. If I don't, I don't know what it buys.*

And then, on a page dated three weeks before the date on the back of the courthouse photograph — three weeks before the laughing man in the suit — a single line, underlined twice:

*B. wants it all to disappear. B. can make things disappear. I have seen him do it.*

She knew who B. was. She had known since the archive, since the margin initials on the suppressed report, since the carved letters above the reading room where she had spent a thousand nights under another man's name. But knowing it in a records office and knowing it on her knees in the dark in her father's house, in her father's handwriting, were different knowings, the way a map of the sea and the sea are different.

She turned to the last written page.

It was dated the day before he vanished. The handwriting had come back to itself, oddly — calmer, smaller, the letters of a man who had made a decision and found the decision heavy but bearable.

*Signed it. Took it. Told myself it was for them — for Adaeze and the girl — and it was, it is, I would do it again, that's the terrible part, I would do it again and I can't live in a man who would do it again.*

*So: copy kept. Original goes where they'll never think to look, which is nowhere, because they think I'm finished. I am not finished. A man who is finished doesn't keep accounts.*

*If I go quiet —*

And there the line stopped. Not torn out. Not crossed out. Just stopped, mid-sentence, the pen lifting, the rest of the page blank, the rest of the book blank, forty pages of empty green-ruled paper like held breath.

*If I go quiet.*

Nell's words, on the phone from the coast, in her voice like a rope thrown across water: *He called me the night before. He said, Nellie, if I go quiet, it wasn't my choice.*

He had written it down too. He had written it to no one, to everyone, to whoever pried up the floorboard — to a daughter who would not be born into understanding for another twenty years. He had written the sentence and not finished it, and Rafael sat in the dust of his bedroom with the unfinished sentence in her lap and understood, with a certainty that felt less like discovery than like recognition, that the sentence had been finished by other people. That the silence had not been his. That the man who kept accounts had been accounted for.

She was crying. She noticed it the way you notice rain — not at once, but as a fact of the room. She let it happen. The house had seen worse.

* * *

She did not know how long she sat there. Long enough for the light blades from the board seams to shift, for the city outside to change its sound from late to later. At some point she became aware of the cold coming up through the floor, and of the practical world reasserting itself: she could not be found here. Not with this. If the sedan came back, if anyone came back, a break-in plus a stolen ledger was a story Meridian would write for her, and she had read enough of their drafts.

She photographed every page. It took an hour, the phone's flash strobing the room like a small private lightning storm, her father's deteriorating handwriting flickering over the walls — and she thought, absurdly, *the house is haunted after all, and the ghost is a green copybook, and the haunting is arithmetic.* Names and payments and dates. The chalk ghost on the step, caught mid-turn. This was what it had been turning to look at. This was what it had wanted counted.

When the phone's battery dropped below twenty percent she stopped, and wrapped the ledger in the scarf she'd worn against the rain, and put it inside her coat, against her chest, where she could feel it with every breath like a second heartbeat.

She was about to push the floorboard back when she stopped.

There was something else in the cavity. Under the dust-flakes of the plastic bag, pressed into the corner where the joist met the sill: a photograph. Small, glossy, curling with damp.

She lifted it into the flashlight's pool.

Her mother. Young — younger than Rafael was now — standing in this very yard, in summer, one hand shading her eyes, laughing at something outside the frame. And at the edge of the frame, half-cut-off, a man's shoulder and one raised hand, mid-wave, mid-joke, mid-life. Her father's hand. She knew the watch on the wrist; she had seen it in a shoebox in an attic; she had spent a night holding it up to the light.

On the back, in her mother's hand, in the handwriting Rafael had not seen in twenty years because her mother now printed everything in careful neutral capitals, the way people write when they are hiding from their own letters:

*The last good day. — A.*

Rafael put the photograph in her coat pocket, over her heart, next to the ledger. Two documents, one heartbeat.

Then she pressed the floorboard down, pushed the wardrobe back into its groan, closed the bedroom door, and let herself out through the kitchen, past the ghost of turmeric, past the window her father never locked, into the cold honest dark of Vesper Lane.

The chalk figure was still on the step, torso and motion, looking back at something behind it.

Rafael stood over it for a moment. Then she crouched, and from her bag she took the piece of white chalk she had bought that afternoon from a corner shop — the fat sticks, the kind that came like sugar cane — and she finished the drawing. Head, arms, the other leg. A whole small person, standing on the step of the house, looking forward.

*You have to count them or they get lonely.*

"Counted," she said.

Her phone buzzed in her pocket. She almost didn't look. Then she did.

A message from Theo, sent forty minutes ago, no greeting, no punctuation, the way he wrote when his conscience was outrunning his caution:

*You need to see this tonight. The archive. They came for the misfiled box this afternoon — Meridian's people, with a court order. I stalled them. I don't know how long I can stall them. And Rafael — the signature on the withdrawal request is Brandt's. He knows the report exists. He's cleaning house.*

She stood on the step of the house that had finally given up its secret, holding its secret against her chest, and looked down the long dark of Vesper Lane toward the city, where a man with her library's name carved above his benefaction was, at this very hour, erasing the evidence.

Ninety days had become sixty. Sixty, it seemed, had just become tonight.

She started walking.
