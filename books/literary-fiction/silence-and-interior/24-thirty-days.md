## 24. Thirty Days

Imogen Reyes worked out of a converted dental office above a laundromat on Corliss Street, the kind of place where the receptionist was also the paralegal was also the person who watered the dying ficus in the corner. She had taken Rafael's case for free, which Rafael learned was not generosity but appetite. Imogen liked lost causes the way some lawyers liked yachts — as proof of what she could afford to lose.

"Let me make sure I understand the shape of this," Imogen said on their first morning, feet on her desk, a legal pad balanced on one knee. "Your father took money from Aldous Brandt to bury a safety report. Then he apparently regretted it, hid a duplicate ledger behind a wardrobe, and vanished. Twenty years later, the house he vanished from is being sold by the man who paid him off. And your theory is — what, exactly?"

"That the title is dirty," Rafael said. "The ledger shows payments routed through shell companies. If Meridian acquired the property through entities that were themselves instruments of fraud —"

"Stop." Imogen held up a hand. She had a way of stopping people that felt less like interruption and more like triage. "I've read the ledger. It's compelling. It's also a document with no provenance, found behind a wardrobe in a house your family abandoned, in your handwriting's neighborhood of the alphabet but not your handwriting, attesting to transactions two decades old involving companies that may or may not still exist. A judge is going to look at that and see a woman with a grudge and a Xerox machine."

"So you don't believe me."

"I believe you." Imogen set her feet down. "That's not the same as believing I can prove you. Belief is free. Proof costs. And right now, what we have costs more to introduce than it's worth." She flipped the legal pad to a fresh page. "But here's what I do have. The title chain on Lot 47 has a gap. Nineteen ninety-eight to two thousand and one. Three years where the deed transfers through an entity — Harborside Holdings — that the Secretary of State has no record of ever registering. Theo found it?"

"Theo found it."

"Your clerk friend is going to get himself in trouble." Imogen said it without judgment, the way you'd note the weather. "A defective link in the chain of title is a thin reed. But it's a reed. We argue the sale rests on fraudulently obtained title, we ask to intervene in the auction as an interested party — heir of the record owner at the time of the defect — and we ask for a stay pending investigation. The judge will almost certainly say no. But saying no on the record creates a paper trail. Paper trails are how long shots become shots."

Rafael looked at the ficus, at the water-stained ceiling, at this woman who had agreed to spend her scarce hours on a house she'd never seen. "Why are you doing this?"

Imogen smiled for the first time all morning. It didn't soften her; it sharpened her. "Because Aldous Brandt's foundation funds half the legal aid infrastructure in this city, and every firm that could take this case has already found a reason not to. I'm too small to be bought and too stubborn to be scared. It's a niche."

* * *

The filing took three days to draft and thirty seconds to destroy Rafael's sleep. *Motion to Intervene and Stay of Sale, *Kavanagh v. Meridian Consortium, LLC*, filed in the county chancery court on a Tuesday, served on Meridian's counsel by Wednesday morning. By Wednesday afternoon, Rafael's name appeared in a Meridian internal memo she would not see for another week, and by Thursday she had stopped taking the stairs in her apartment building, preferring the elevator's enclosed, windowless certainty.

Theo found her in the archive on Friday, carrying two coffees and a face like a weather report.

"You're famous," he said. "There's a memo going around the records office. Anyone handling Kavanagh-related requests routes them through the county attorney first."

"Is that bad?"

"That depends on whether you think the county attorney is neutral or whether you've read his campaign finance disclosures." He set a coffee beside her. "Also, Imogen Reyes called the office asking for certified copies of the Harborside Holdings transfer documents. The clerk who pulled them said the folder was — his word — 'lighter than he remembered.'"

Rafael's stomach did something slow and cold. "Lighter how?"

"Missing the transfer instrument. The actual deed from Harborside to the successor entity. Everything else is there — tax records, notices, the husk. Just not the organ." Theo sat down across from her, lowering his voice even though the archive was empty except for the hum of the dehumidifier. "Someone's been in that folder. Recently. The dust pattern on the box was wrong."

"You can read dust."

"I can read dust." He wrapped both hands around his cup. "Rafael. They know what we know. Which means the next thirty days aren't about finding the truth. The truth is already found. The next thirty days are about whether any of it survives long enough to be said out loud in a room with a judge in it."

* * *

The hearing was set for the last Friday of the month, in Department 4, before the Honorable Marian Osei, a judge Rafael researched until two in the morning and could not make up her mind about. Sixteen years on the bench. Former prosecutor, which was bad. Reversed twice by the appellate court for rulings that favored developers, which was worse. But also the judge who had once jailed a city councilman for contempt when he laughed during a witness's testimony, which Rafael chose, against her better judgment, to find encouraging.

The courtroom on Friday morning smelled of floor wax and old fear. Meridian's counsel arrived in force — four lawyers, three of whom appeared to have no function other than presence, arranged behind the lead attorney like a choir. His name was Callum Voss, and he had the particular polish of a man who billed by the syllable. Imogen, by contrast, had one folder and Rafael.

"Your honor," Voss began, before the case was even called properly, "we'll be moving to dismiss. What we have here is a late intervention, filed eleven days before auction, by a party with no colorable interest in the property, resting on a document of unknown origin that — and I want to be respectful here — appears to have been assembled in someone's kitchen."

Judge Osei looked up from the file. "Mr. Voss. In this department, we let the court characterize the evidence. Sit down."

The choir sat. Rafael felt something loosen in her chest that she would later recognize as the first moment in twenty years of feeling defended by an institution rather than merely processed by one.

Imogen argued for eleven minutes. She was not flamboyant. She walked the judge through the title chain like a woman walking someone through a dark house she knew well — here the gap, here the unregistered entity, here the pattern of acquisitions, forty parcels in fifteen years, all through shells, all cheap, all converging on one block. She did not mention the ledger. Rafael, watching, understood why: it was the most explosive thing they had, and Imogen was keeping it sheathed.

"The intervenor does not need to win today," Imogen concluded. "She needs only to establish a substantial question of title sufficient to justify a stay of thirty days. Thirty days. The auction has been pending for a year. Meridian will not be prejudiced by a month. My client, on the other hand, stands to lose the only physical evidence connecting this sale to the fraud that produced it — a house, your honor, that will not survive a demolition permit."

Voss's rebuttal was a symphony of condescension. He called the title gap "a clerical artifact of the nineties." He called the shell company pattern "ordinary estate planning at scale." He called Rafael — without naming her, which was somehow worse — "a grieving daughter whose grief has been weaponized by parties with interests of their own." Rafael felt the words land on her like weather. *Grieving daughter.* It wasn't untrue, and that was what made it a weapon. Grief made you irrational. Grief made you a woman who found documents behind wardrobes and believed in conspiracies because conspiracies were easier than abandonment.

Judge Osei asked exactly four questions. The first three were for Voss, and each one made the choir shuffle. The fourth was for Rafael, and it came without warning:

"Ms. Kavanagh. Your father. What was he?"

The courtroom went quiet in the particular way courtrooms do — a silence with everyone in it. Rafael stood. Her notes were in her hand and she did not look at them.

"He was a safety inspector for the harbor authority," she said. "In 1998 he wrote a report about structural fraud on the waterfront. The report disappeared. So did he. I was four years old. I'm not here because I'm grieving, your honor. I'm here because I've spent twenty years being told he was a failure, and the paper says otherwise, and the paper is the only parent I have left who tells the truth."

Someone in the gallery — a stranger, a woman with a gray braid — made a small sound, quickly suppressed.

Judge Osei looked at Rafael for a long moment. Whatever moved behind her face, she did not share it.

"Here's where we are," she said finally. "The motion to intervene is granted. Barely. The motion to stay is denied — the sale proceeds as scheduled." The courtroom exhaled, and Rafael felt the floor tilt. But Osei raised a hand. "However. Given the title defect identified by the intervenor, the court will require Meridian to produce the complete Harborside Holdings transfer file — the original instrument, not a copy — to the court and to counsel, within seven days. If the original deed cannot be produced, the court will entertain renewed motions. We're adjourned."

The gavel came down like a period at the end of a sentence Meridian had not finished writing.

* * *

In the hallway, Voss's choir dissolved into an elevator, and Imogen stood with her single folder under her arm, looking, Rafael thought, like a woman who had bet on a long shot and watched it come in third — not a win, but alive.

"She gave us a hook," Imogen said. "Seven days. They can't produce that deed, Rafael. It doesn't exist, or it exists somewhere they can't reach without admitting what it is. Either way, in seven days we're back in front of her with a hole in their file where the deed should be."

"And if they do produce it?"

Imogen was quiet for a moment. Outside the courthouse windows, the city went on not sleeping, cranes swinging over the waterfront like slow metronomes.

"Then we learn something too," she said. "About what they're willing to forge." She turned toward the stairs. "Go home. Sleep. You look like your father's reports — all substance, no rest."

Rafael didn't go home. She went to the library, to her shift, to the reading room with Brandt's name carved above the door, and she shelved returned books under the gaze of the man who had bought her father's silence and was now, she understood with a clarity that felt almost calm, buying time.

Seven days. She could count them the way her father must have counted his — the nights between the report and the money, between the money and the ledger, between the ledger and the vanishing. The house had held its breath for twenty years.

It could hold it seven days more.
