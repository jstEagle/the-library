## 9. First Page, First Refusal

The attic held its breath with me.

I had carried the first notebook down the locked stair as though it were a bird with a broken wing—cupped, careful, half-expecting it to struggle. The paper was soft at the edges, foxed brown like something left too long in a drawer of autumn leaves, and it smelled of dust and camphor and, beneath that, faintly, of the fen itself: cold water and peat and the sweet rot of reeds. My mother's smell. The house's smell. The smell I had spent thirty years scrubbing out of my clothes without ever once finding the source.

I set it on the kitchen table under the lamp, because the kitchen was neutral ground. The kitchen was where we had eaten, where we had not spoken, where the silence had been laid down in layers like linoleum until you could walk across it without hearing anything underneath. If the notebook was going to say what I feared it might say, it could say it here, where the walls already knew how to keep quiet.

Outside, dusk was coming down over Marrow Fen the way it always had—not gradually but all at once, a lid closing—and I caught myself reaching for the old word for it, the word Denny used to shout from the top of the dyke when the light went: *skerrow*, skerrow, the hour of the shuttered eye. It came to me whole, unbidden, the first word the language had returned to me since I'd arrived, and I stood at the window saying it silently, testing it like a tooth that had grown back.

Then I sat down and opened the book.

* * *

My mother's handwriting was worse than I remembered. That sounds like a small thing, but it isn't; handwriting is a face, and hers had changed. The letters I knew from shopping lists and school notes—the firm downstrokes, the impatient crosses on her t's—were still there, but they were crowded now by something looser, a slant that pulled the words downhill toward the bottom right corner of every line, as if the sentences were trying to slide off the page and away from her.

The first pages were arithmetic. That surprised me. Egg counts, coal deliveries, sums owed to Calloway's for flour and paraffin, the price of a lamb in a hard year. She had begun these notebooks as ledgers, and there was something heartbreaking in that—a woman who kept accounts because accounts were the one form of speech that couldn't be misheard. Numbers don't have dialects. A figure written down is the same in London as it is in Marrow Fen, and no one can tell from a column of shillings where you learned to pray.

Between the columns, though, she wrote. At first only single words, marginalia, the dialect surfacing like fish rising in still water. *Hafnel*, for the particular grey of a sky that means snow within the day. *Corrick*, the sound ice makes when it is thickening overnight—a creak, a groan, a long complaint of wood. Words I had not seen written before, because no one had ever written them; the fen tongue lived entirely in mouths, passed hand to hand like water, and here was my mother dipping her pen into it, committing the unspeakable language to paper in the margins of an egg ledger.

I translated as I read. That was the habit of thirty years and it asserted itself before I could stop it: the English arriving in my head a half-second behind the fen words, smooth, domesticated, safe. *Hafnel*: a sky the colour of wet slate, promising snow. See how tame it becomes. See how the wildness drains out of it the moment it is made useful. This is what I had done for a living, all this time—taken other people's sharp things and blunted them into commerce, and now I sat doing it to my own mother, converting her margins into glossary entries while my hands shook around the pencil.

It was on the fourth page that the ledger ended and the confession began—or rather, that the two stopped pretending to be different things.

There was no heading. No date, even, though earlier pages had dates. Just a break in the ruled lines, a gap of white space wide enough to fall into, and then, in handwriting suddenly smaller and more upright than any of the rest, as if she had forced herself to sit like a schoolgirl and write properly:

*I know which one of you it was.*

* * *

I want to describe accurately what happened in my body when I read that line, because accuracy is the only penance I know.

The room did not spin. There was no rushing of blood, no cinematic roaring in the ears. What happened was smaller and much worse: everything simply stopped. My breath. The tick of the carriage clock on the mantel, which I realized I had been hearing without hearing for two hours. Even the wind against the north window seemed to pause out of courtesy, the way the whole village had paused for forty years whenever I walked past, waiting for me to say the thing I would not say.

*I know which one of you it was.*

You. Which one of you. Two children. One sentence. And the grammar did what grammar does—it divided, it sorted, it demanded a selection—and I understood, sitting in that kitchen with the lamp burning and the dark pressing flat against the windows, that I had come three hundred miles and broken a lock and pried open my mother's last private room in order to find exactly this sentence, and that having found it, I could not bear it.

Because which one? Read it again. Read it the way I read it, the way I will always read it, the way a guilty man reads every ambiguous document in the world: *I know which one of you it was.* Not *what*. Not *what happened*. *Which one of you it was.* As if the event were a person. As if one of her children had been the agent of it—the going-through-the-ice, the running, the empty bed, the boots in the scullery crusted with fen mud that no one had ever cleaned because cleaning them would have been an act of conclusion.

She knew. All those years, all those silences at the table, all those times she looked at me across the stew and said nothing—I had told myself it was grief, that grief had taken her voice along with her son. But grief doesn't look at you like that. Grief doesn't watch you carry your plate to the sink and wash it and dry it and put it away with such terrible correctness. Knowledge looks at you like that. A mother who knows which one of you it was looks at you like that, and says nothing, and lets you live in the house with her for six more years, and never once tells you whether the silence is mercy or sentence.

I closed the notebook.

I want to be honest about what I wanted to do next, because the next part matters more than the reading. I looked around that kitchen for a fire. There wasn't one—the chimney had been declared unsafe decades ago, the grate filled with newspaper someone had stuffed there and never lit—and I stood up and crossed to the hearth anyway, absurdly, and knelt, and looked at the cold grate and the yellowed newspaper wadded in it, and understood that some part of me had already decided. Burn it. Burn the notebooks unread, burn the confession, burn the accusation, and go back to London and finish cataloguing the chairs and the tools and the good china, and let the auctioneer's gavel fall on a house full of priced objects and unpriced ghosts, and let the developer pour his concrete over the fen, and let the language die with its last witness unexamined.

That was the offer on the table. Silence had fed me for thirty years; it would have fed me thirty more. Silence is not the absence of choice. Silence is a choice renewed daily, hourly, at every moment you might speak and don't, and I had gotten so good at it that I could feel it operating even now, even here, my whole self leaning toward the cold grate like a plant toward a window.

There was no fire lit. That is the whole truth of why the notebook survived its first night in my hands: not courage, not resolve, not love of truth. Matches. Or the lack of them—I never even checked. I knelt at an empty grate in a cold kitchen and was saved by the absence of a flame, and if that sounds like cowardice dressed in coincidence, then you understand me better than most.

But I will say this for myself, because the record should be fair even to me: after a while, kneeling there, I became aware that the fire I was contemplating was not really for the notebook. The notebook was paper and ink and would have burned in four minutes. What I wanted to burn was the sentence. *I know which one of you it was.* I wanted it back inside my mother's head where it had lived alone for fifty years, wanted it unwritten, wanted the white space before it restored, wanted to be a man who had never read it, so that I could go on being the man who had never answered it.

And the language—which had come back to me at the window only an hour before, *skerrow*, the shuttered hour, given freely as if the fen had been holding it in trust—did not come to me now. I reached for the old word for fear, the one Denny used to whisper in the dark of our shared room when the corrick of the ice sounded across the fields, and it was gone again. Of course it was. The words return when they are needed honestly. I was not being honest yet. I was kneeling at a grate wanting to destroy my mother's voice, and the fen tongue, which is older than lying, declined to assist.

* * *

I took the notebook upstairs to my old room, which felt like surrendering high ground, and put it under my mattress like contraband, and lay on top of it fully clothed with the lamp off, listening to the house settle.

Sleep was not going to come; I knew that the way you know weather. But I lay there and let the memory come instead, because it was going to come anyway and I preferred to meet it standing, or lying, as the case was.

Denny teaching me the word. He was nine and I was twelve and he had learned it from old Tam Ossler down at the eel traps, who was the last person in the village besides our mother who still spoke the tongue without shame. *Brathwen*, Denny said, lying on his stomach on the bed with his chin in his hands, feet kicking the wall. Say it. Brath-wen. And I said it wrong, anglicized the th, and he laughed at me with that total, merciless, adoring laughter that only a younger brother has license to use, and made me say it again, and again, until I had it. It doesn't just mean brother, he recited, solemn as a priest, doing Tam Ossler's voice. It means the one who shares your drowning. Everyone drowns a bit, see. Everyone goes under sometimes. Your brathwen's the one who goes under with you so you're not alone down there.

And I had said, twelve years old and already practicing the craft of deflection that would become my life: That's stupid. You'd just both drown then.

And he had said, with the terrible simplicity of children: Yes. That's the point.

I lay on top of the hidden notebook in the dark and thought: he went under, and I ran. I ran home across the frozen fen without looking back, and I have been running for thirty years, and my mother watched me run and knew which one of us it was, and wrote it down, and said nothing, and the nothing she said was heavier than any words she could have chosen.

Or was it? That was the question turning over and over like a stone in a streambed. Because there was another reading—I could feel it waiting, patient, at the edge of the page like the white space itself. *I know which one of you it was.* Which one of you it was who followed him. Who tried to reach him. Whose hand lost him. Mothers know things. Mothers follow small boys onto frozen fens when the light is going. What if the *you* was not an accusation at all but a confession wearing grammar's plainest coat—what if she was writing about herself, sorting herself among her children, saying: I know which one of us let go?

I didn't believe it. That was the trouble. I wanted to believe it the way a drowning man wants driftwood, and wanting that badly is itself evidence of guilt. An innocent son turns the page. A guilty one lies awake calculating angles of exculpation from a single sentence.

Toward morning—I heard the birds start, the fen birds first, always first, before the sparrows—I got up and took the notebook out from under the mattress and brought it back down to the kitchen and set it on the table in the grey early light.

I turned past the ledger pages, past the marginal words, past the white space, to the sentence. It was still there. Confessions are patient; that is their advantage over the people they wait for.

Below it, the handwriting continued. I had not been able to see that last night—had closed the book within seconds, had fled to the cold grate, had fled to bed, had fled everywhere except forward. Now, in the flat honest light of dawn, with frost feathering the corners of the windows and the kettle beginning its low complaint on the hob, I saw that my mother had not stopped at one line. She had gone on. Page after page after page, the slanted downhill script filling the ruled lines, dense as reeds, all the way through the notebook and into the next one and the next, eleven volumes of it stacked in the attic above my head, a lifetime of the unsaid waiting in the fen tongue and the shaky English both.

I could close it again. I could drive to Norwich today and tell the solicitor I'd found nothing of value, household effects only, and sign whatever they put in front of me, and take the train south, and never learn what she wrote next. The offer would still stand tomorrow. Silence keeps office hours; it is always open.

I picked up my pencil. I wrote the date at the top of a fresh sheet of paper, the way translators do, the way accountants do, the way my mother did in her egg ledger, because the act of dating a thing is the act of agreeing to be held to it.

Then I turned the page, and began.
