## 12. The Word for 'Brother'

The kettle had gone cold on the hob again. Orsen noticed it the way one notices a stopped clock in a house where no one is waiting—not with irritation but with a small, private shame, as though the water too had given up on them.

They had been at the notebooks since dawn, working at the kitchen table under the one window that still sealed properly, their mother's handwriting spread out before them in its two registers: the fen dialect flowing quick and slanted down the left margins like water finding its course, and the English laboring after it in the wider column, blocky and uncertain, a woman wading through a foreign country with her skirts held up. Orsen had spent thirty years translating other people's certainty—contracts, manuals, the smooth corporate nothing of instructions for things that did not matter—and had forgotten what it was to translate someone who was herself reaching.

The morning's pages were from the second winter after Denny went into the fen. Orsen knew this because the mother dated her entries by frost: *the first hard frost*, *the week the ditches froze white*, *the thaw that didn't come*. The village had measured time by weather because weather was the only thing in Marrow Fen that kept its promises.

Orsen read the dialect first, always, letting the old words sit in the mouth without being asked to mean anything yet. And there, halfway down the page, was the word.

*Skeldan.*

They had known it would come. They had been circling it for days, the way you circle a hole in the ice, testing the edges. Every language has a word it keeps behind glass, and this was theirs—the word Orsen had taught Denny when he was five and they were nine, kneeling on the scullery floor with a wet cloth, scrubbing fen mud off his boots while he squirmed.

*Say it,* Orsen had told him. *Skel-dan.*

*Skel-dan.* He'd had a gap where his front tooth should have been, and the word whistled slightly through it, and Orsen had laughed, and he had laughed, and outside the window the reeds stood up stiff and silver in the November light.

In English, Orsen had told him, we only have *brother*. One word for all of it—for the boy who shares your bed when the boiler fails, who shares your blood, your surname, your sentence. But *skeldan* meant something the English tongue had never bothered to carve out a place for. It meant, literally, *the one who shares your drowning*. Not your death—your drowning. The going-under itself. The person beside you in the water, whether the water is a fen or a house or a silence, the one whose breath runs out when yours does, whose hands find yours in the dark not to pull you up but so that neither of you goes down alone.

*It doesn't mean I'll save you,* Orsen had explained, pressing the wet cloth into his small palm. *It means if we go under, we go under together.*

Denny had considered this with the terrible seriousness of five-year-olds. *What if only one of us drowns?*

*Then the other one drowns slower.*

He had liked that answer enormously. He had repeated it at school until Mrs. Tarrant sent a note home, and their mother had looked at Orsen over the note and said nothing, which was her way of saying everything, and Orsen had understood even then—at nine, at ten, at twelve—that some words were dangerous not because they were forbidden but because they were true.

Now the word sat on the yellowed page in their mother's hand, and Orsen read the sentence around it three times before allowing it to resolve.

*I dreamed of my skeldan last night. There is no English for what he was to me. My brother is a word for a boy in a photograph. Skeldan is a word for the water rising.*

Their mother had had a skeldan. A brother of her own, lost before Orsen was born—drowned young, actually drowned, actually in the fen, back when the village still called such things accidents without irony. Orsen had known this the way children know things: as furniture, as fact, as a face in a locket. They had never once thought about what it must have been to be her—to lose the one who shared her drowning, and then to bear two more skeldans into the same landscape, and watch the water come for one of them.

The pen in Orsen's hand had stopped moving. Ink pooled at the nib, a small black eye staring up from the translation draft.

* * *

The trouble with translating grief was that it arrived in the wrong order.

Orsen got up, walked to the scullery, and stood in the doorway looking at Denny's boots. They had left them there days ago, unable to move them, unable to catalogue them, and now they came back to them the way you come back to a wound to check whether it has closed. It had not. The mud on them had dried forty years ago into a kind of fossil record—cracked, grey-brown, holding the imprint of the fen bottom in its grain. If Orsen looked long enough, the mud became legible. Reeds. Peat. The black silt off the old causeway path, the one that ran out past the willows to where the ice thinned every year no matter how hard the frost bit, because the spring underneath never slept.

*Then the other one drowns slower.*

Had Orsen said that? Had those words actually passed between them, or had thirty years of guilt polished the memory into something sharper than it was? This was the thing no one warned you about translation—that the source text degrades while you work. That memory was not a document but a living manuscript, edited nightly by a hand you could not see.

Orsen went back to the table. Outside, the light had thickened toward afternoon, the fen visible through the window as a flat pewter sheet stitched with reed-beds, and somewhere out there was the causeway, and somewhere past the causeway was the place, though Orsen had not gone to look and did not intend to.

They turned to the next entry. Their mother's dialect grew more fluent as the pages went on—or perhaps Orsen was growing fluent again, the language coming home to the mouth like a bird to a roost it had been driven from. That was the strange mercy of it. In London, the words had been leaving Orsen one by one, the way tenants leave a building in a slow strike: *the word for dusk*, gone; *the word for the smell of peat smoke on wet wool*, gone; *the word for the particular silence of a house where something has happened*, gone. But here, bent over their mother's hand, the vocabulary returned—not all at once, but in seams, in trickles, as if the language had only ever been waiting to be needed honestly.

*Skeldan* appeared again. And again. Their mother used it the way other writers use a name in prayer.

Orsen began, without deciding to, a separate list—a translator's glossary, the professional habit asserting itself like a heartbeat. *Skeldan*: sibling; lit. sharer-of-drowning; implies mutual submersion, mutual witness. Compare *bróðor*, Eng., which implies only blood. Note: no verb form. You cannot skeldan someone. You can only be it, together, or fail to be it.

There was no verb form. Orsen wrote that down and then sat looking at it for a long time.

Because that was the shape of it, wasn't it. You couldn't do anything with the word. It described a condition, not an act. It said nothing about pulling, nothing about running, nothing about a boy going through the ice and another boy turning and running across the frozen fen with the sound still hanging in the air behind him—a sound Orsen had never described to anyone, not fully, not once, in thirty years, because describing it would require admitting how long it lasted. How it wasn't a crack and a splash, the way the village told it. How it was a long groaning, the ice speaking in a voice like a door in an empty house, and then a different sound entirely, shorter, human, cut off.

And then Orsen's own breathing, and the reeds whipping their face, and the half mile to the house covered at a dead run without one backward glance, because some animal part of them had understood—even at twelve, especially at twelve—that looking back was a thing you could not survive.

*You cannot skeldan someone. You can only be it, together, or fail to be it.*

Orsen put down the pen.

* * *

That night they dreamed in dialect.

This had not happened in decades. In the dream they were small, and the kitchen was whole—no sag in the ceiling, no damp bloom on the plaster—and Denny sat on the floor by the range with his boots off, his socks steaming gently, and he was asking Orsen to say the word again. *Say it. Say it again.* And Orsen said it, and said it, *skeldan, skeldan*, and each time the word came out it meant something slightly larger, the way a stone dropped in water means more rings each moment, until the word filled the kitchen and then the house and then the fen, and Orsen woke with it still in their mouth, intact, warm, alive.

They lay in the narrow bed in their old room listening to the house tick and settle, and understood that something had shifted and could not shift back.

For six weeks—for the length of the solicitor's letter, the train journey, the inventory, the locked stair, the single translated line that had nearly ended everything—Orsen had wanted one thing only: to get through. To catalogue the house, endure the auction, escape with whatever remained of themselves. Fear had been the engine. Fear of the notebooks, fear of the buyer's bulldozers, fear of Mrs. Calloway's knowing eyes, fear most of all of the thin place in the ice that waited out beyond the willows like a question mark written on the land.

But lying in the dark with the word still dissolving on their tongue, Orsen found that the fear had changed its chemistry. It was still there—it would always be there—but underneath it now, supporting it like bedrock under topsoil, was want. A plain, unglamorous, physical wanting. They wanted to know.

Not to be absolved. Orsen distrusted absolution the way a counterfeiter distrusts currency. But to know. To know whether their mother had seen them come running across the fen that night, soaked to the waist, and what her face had done. To know why she had never asked—not once, not in all the years afterward, not over Sunday dinners or Christmas puddings or the thousand small transactions of a life—*what happened out there, Orsen, tell me what happened.* To know whether her silence had been a verdict or a wound or something else entirely, something Orsen had no word for in either language.

And beneath even that, deeper, where the honest questions live: to know what Denny's last minute had been. Whether he had called out. Whether he had said the word.

The dark pressed close. Out in the fen, wind moved through the reeds with a sound like slow applause, or like whispering, or like nothing at all depending on what you brought to it.

Orsen got up, wrapped the blanket around their shoulders, and went down to the kitchen without lighting a lamp. They could work by moonlight; the snow-light off the fen was famous for it. They opened the notebook to the ribbon their mother had tied marking a page near the end of the second winter, and read, and translated, and the words came:

*I have not asked the child what happened. I know what that makes me. A skeldan does not ask, because to ask is to make the drowning speakable, and some drownings must stay under or they take the whole shore with them. But I watch him. God forgive me, I watch him carry it, and I let him carry it, because if he sets it down I am afraid of what I will pick up instead.*

Orsen read it four times. Then a fifth. The fire of comprehension moved through them slowly, the way frost moves through a windowpane—branching, intricate, irreversible.

She had watched. She had known there was something to watch for. She had chosen, deliberately, with open eyes, to let a twelve-year-old carry a weight she could have lifted with a single question—and she had written down that she knew exactly what that made her.

Which meant the notebooks were not a confession that might name Orsen. They were something worse and better: a record of a choice, made in full knowledge, that had shaped two lives. Her sin was not hidden in these pages. It was laid out, dated by frost, examined yearly like a wound she refused to let heal.

Orsen sat back. The blanket had slipped from one shoulder; the cold found the gap and stayed there, familiar, almost companionable. On the table the notebook lay open, and the moonlight lay across it, and for the first time since arriving in Marrow Fen, Orsen felt not dread but appetite. Tomorrow they would go forward through the pages, faster now, hungrier. Somewhere ahead the handwriting would change—they had already noticed it, in glimpses, the letters loosening, the dialect giving way sometimes to plain English as if she were too tired to keep up the wall—and somewhere ahead was the winter itself, the night, the fen, the truth of what their mother had done while Orsen ran.

And somewhere ahead, Orsen suspected, was a page that would send them out onto the ice themselves.

They did not know how they knew this. They only knew that the word was back in their mouth, and that it had no verb form, and that tomorrow they intended to find out everything.

Outside, far out on the black flat of the fen, the ice groaned once in its sleep—a long, low sound, like a door in an empty house—and Orsen, wide awake, listened to it all the way to the end.
