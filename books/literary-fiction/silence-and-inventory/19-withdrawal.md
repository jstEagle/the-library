## 19. Withdrawal

The solicitor's office in Marlow St. Edmund smelled of toner and old carpet, the particular staleness of rooms where decisions are made slowly and regretted quickly. Mr. Prentice—young for a solicitor, with the careful, over-pressed look of a man who ironed his own shirts—laid the auction documents on the desk between us like a hand of cards he already knew I'd lose.

"Sign here, and here, and the initial at the bottom of each schedule," he said. "The sale completes on the twenty-eighth. The developer's solicitors have been very efficient. I'll say that for them."

I looked at the papers. The house reduced to a lot number. The land reduced to a figure with a comma in it—£184,000, a sum I had never in my life possessed, a sum that would have bought me out of the rented flat in Camberwell, bought me years of silence in some better neighborhood, bought me the luxury of never thinking about Marrow Fen again except as a place I had been clever enough to leave.

My pen was already in my hand. That was the terrible thing. I had picked it up without deciding to, the way I had once picked up a kettle and poured water over my hands to feel something other than the feeling I was feeling, the way the body reaches for the next ordinary motion when the mind has gone somewhere it cannot follow. The nib hovered over the first signature line, and I watched my own shadow tremble on the paper, and I thought: this is the last moment in which I am still a person who might sign.

"Mr. Prentice," I said. "I want to withdraw the house from sale."

He looked up. He had a face built for mild disappointment, and it performed it now with real fluency. "Withdraw. Entirely?"

"Entirely. I'm not selling. Not to the developer, not at auction, not to anyone."

He removed his glasses, which seemed to be a ritual gesture, a way of buying time by rendering himself briefly blind. "Mr. Eastwick. Orsen, may I call you Orsen." He did not wait for permission. "You understand the position. Your cousin has legal title and has instructed the sale. Your right of occupation as next of kin cataloguing the estate was always contingent on the sale proceeding. If you withdraw now, you forfeit any share of the proceeds—which, given the state of the estate's debts, would likely have been modest, but not nothing. And you may find yourself personally liable for costs incurred. Surveys. The developer's due diligence. These things are not free."

"How much?"

"Potentially several thousand pounds."

I had eleven hundred pounds in a savings account that had not been touched since the year my mother died, because touching it would have meant admitting it existed, and it existed only as the last thing she ever gave me. I thought of the figure. I thought of the flat in Camberwell with its damp corner shaped like a kneeling figure. I thought of thirty years of commercial translation—manuals, contracts, pharmaceutical inserts, the endless conversion of one language's meaning into another language's blanks—and I thought: I have been paid my whole life for making things disappear into other things. This is the first bill that has ever come the other way.

"Several thousand," I said. "Against a house."

"Against a house and four acres of fen-edge land, yes. Which will be underwater within twenty years, if the Environment Agency's projections hold. I say that not to persuade you, only to be thorough. You'd be inheriting a liability, not an asset."

"Good," I said.

He blinked. "Good?"

"I don't want an asset. I want a house." I heard the sentence come out of me and was surprised by it, the way you're surprised by a word in a language you thought you'd forgotten. "There's a difference, Mr. Prentice, and I've only recently learned to translate it."

* * *

It took three days to make the withdrawal real, because bureaucracy is a fen of its own—everything looks solid until you put your weight on it, and then you're in to the waist. Prentice drafted the notice. The developer's solicitors responded within hours, which told me how badly they wanted it: a letter thick with words like *reliance* and *detriment* and *good-faith expenditure*, threatening costs, implying action. I read it standing in the kitchen at Marrow Fen with the notebooks open on the table beside me, my mother's handwriting climbing the margins like ivy, and I felt the threat land on me the way rain lands on a stone that has been rained on for forty years.

I wrote back myself. Not through Prentice—I typed the letter on my laptop at the kitchen table while the light went copper on the fen, and I kept it to one paragraph, because I have spent my life learning that the shortest sentences carry the most weight.

*The house is withdrawn from sale. I accept personal liability for costs lawfully incurred. The estate's contents, including all papers and manuscripts found on the premises, are not chattels for disposal but the literary remains of Margaret Eastwick, and will be dealt with as such. Any further communication regarding demolition or acquisition should be directed to me and to no one else.*

I read it over four times. Then I did something I had never done in any professional document: I signed my full name, and beneath it, for the first time since I was twelve years old, I wrote the word *Eastwick* without flinching.

* * *

Registering the notebooks was harder, and stranger, and it was Mrs. Calloway who showed me the way—not through any knowledge of law, but through a sentence she dropped like a stone into my thinking while I sat in her kitchen drinking tea I didn't want, on the second evening after the letter went.

"You'll want to talk to the girl at the records office in St. Edmund," she said. "Nell Farrow. She did the flood maps, but she's the one who knows about listings and protections and all that. She's the one who stopped them pulling down the old chapel, got it classed as something or other." She stirred her tea though there was nothing in it to stir. "Her gran was a Toller. Fen family, old stock. She'll know the words, if you need them."

"The words?"

"The old words." She looked at me over the rim of her cup with eyes gone pale as shallow water. "You know what I mean, Orsen. The ones your mother wrote in. The ones you stopped speaking the day you came home with your trousers soaked and your mouth shut."

I set the cup down. It rang against the saucer, a small bright note in the dim kitchen, and for a moment neither of us moved, as if the sound had been a shot.

"I never said I'd stopped," I said.

"You never said anything," said Mrs. Calloway. "That's rather the point of you."

* * *

Nell Farrow turned out to be perhaps thirty, with ink on her fingers and a cardigan the color of fen water, and she listened to me for eleven minutes without interrupting, which I suspect is a professional habit of people who work with records: they understand that what a person brings them is never quite the thing they describe.

"So," she said at last, turning her chair to face me across the desk in the records office, where the radiators ticked and the shelves breathed out the smell of paper and dust and glue. "You want to register a collection of notebooks as a literary estate. Not as personal property. As—what, exactly?"

"As a work," I said. "As a text. My mother wrote them over thirty years. Half of it is in the Marrow Fen dialect—the old fen speech, the one the county archive says has fewer than two hundred speakers left, most of them older than she was. Half is in English. Together they're a document. Of the dialect, of the place, of—" I stopped. I had been about to say *of what happened*, and I wasn't ready to say that to a stranger in a cardigan, not yet, not in this room with its fluorescent hum.

"Of the fen," Nell said, filling the silence in a way that made me understand she'd heard the shape of what I hadn't said. "There's a mechanism. It's not common, but it exists—deposit of manuscripts with cultural significance, especially where there's linguistic content. It doesn't stop them being yours. It does mean they can't simply be sold off as paper, and it means they get catalogued properly, preserved, eventually—maybe—published." She looked at me carefully. "You'd have to name an editor or executor for the literary estate. Someone with the right to translate, transcribe, prepare for publication."

"I'm a translator," I said.

"Then I imagine you're nominating yourself."

"I'm nominating myself."

She pulled a form toward her and began writing in the header blocks, her pen moving with the quick confidence of someone who knows exactly which boxes exist. "Name of author of record?"

"Margaret Eastwick."

"Name of estate?"

And here I paused, because the form wanted a title, and a title is a kind of door, and I understood that whatever I wrote in this box would be the name the notebooks carried into whatever future they had—would be how my mother was known, would be how Denny was known, would be how the winter of the ice was known, forever, or for as long as paper lasts. My mother had never titled them. She had only numbered them, one through fourteen, in that hand that changed over the years from justification to doubt to something like prayer.

I thought of the last entry. *Let him carry what I could not carry. Let the one who stayed forgive me, if words can reach that far.* I thought of the word for *brother*, the word that means *one who shares your drowning*. I thought of thirty years of inventory—every sentence swallowed, every name unspoken, the ledger I had kept in place of a life.

"Silence and Inventory," I said.

Nell wrote it down without comment. But when she finished the block she looked up, and there was something in her face that was not professional at all—something quick and warm, like a bird crossing a window.

"That's a good title," she said. "That's a title that knows what it's about."

* * *

That night I walked the house room by room, not to inventory it but to tell it. This is what I did, moving through the dark with a torch, speaking in a low voice to the rooms as though they were old dogs who had waited a long time by a door: *You're not being sold. You're staying. Whoever comes for you will have to come through me, and I am done being easy to come through.*

In the scullery I stopped at Denny's boots. They had stood there for forty years, mud-crusted, laces still knotted from the last morning he pulled them on, and I had catalogued them in week one with the clinical precision I use as armor—*pair of boys' leather boots, size 4, mud-encrusted, condition poor*—and the catalogue had been a lie of omission so complete it amounted to a second disappearance. I crouched down now and put my hand on the cold leather.

"They're staying too," I said. "You're staying. All of you. I've paid for it—Prentice can send the bill to Camberwell, and I'll eat rice until it's settled, and I don't care. That's the thing I came here to say. I don't care what it costs. I've been paying the other price for thirty years and it was dearer."

The house did not answer, of course. But houses hold sound the way the fen holds water, and my voice stayed in the scullery after I left it, and I chose to believe it stayed.

* * *

On the third morning, the developer's man came to the gate.

I saw him from the upstairs window—big coat, clipboard, the particular walk of a person arriving to take possession of something. He stood at the sagging gate for a full minute, reading the notice Prentice had nailed there, and then he came up the path and knocked, and I opened the door before the second knock, because I had been waiting for this the way you wait for weather.

"Mr. Eastwick." He had a smile like a folded map. "Gerald Voss. We've been corresponding." He held up the letter I'd written. "I'll be honest with you. My client is not pleased."

"My client," I said, "is a woman who has been dead eleven years, a boy who has been dead forty, and a language that is down to its last two hundred mouths. I'm afraid my clients' interests come first."

He glanced past me into the hall, at the boxes, at the dust sheets, at the stair to the attic standing open now, no longer locked, no longer avoided. "The land's going under within a couple of decades. You know that. You're sinking money into a drowning property to protect a stack of notebooks nobody can read."

"Nobody can read them *yet*," I said. "That's what a translator is for."

He studied me, and I watched him decide what I was—grieving fool, obstructionist, eccentric—and I let him decide, because it no longer mattered what strangers concluded. The only verdict that had ever mattered had been written in my mother's hand, and I had finally read it, and it had not named me.

"Costs will be pursued," he said.

"I know. Send the invoices."

"You understand you'll likely lose money on this. Real money. For nothing."

I stood in my mother's doorway, in the cold coming off the fen, and I thought about the word for *dusk* that had come back to me the night I spoke Denny's name on the ice—*the hour when the water takes the light home*—and I thought about what things cost and what they're worth, and how I had spent thirty years confusing the two, pricing everything and explaining nothing.

"Mr. Voss," I said, "you've been very patient, so I'll give you something free of charge. Everything in this house has already been priced once, by people who didn't know what it was. I'm not making that mistake twice. Good morning."

I closed the door—not a slam, just a close, the soft definite sound of a thing being finished—and stood with my back against it, listening to his footsteps go down the path, and my heart was going like a bird's, and I was smiling, actually smiling, alone in the hall of the house I had just made certain I could never afford to keep.

* * *

The notice of withdrawal appeared in the parish newsletter that Friday, because in a village the size of Marrow Fen the newsletter is the court record, the newspaper, and the confessional all in one. Mrs. Calloway brought it round herself, folded to the relevant line, her breath smoking in the cold doorway.

"*The Eastwick property, withdrawn from sale by direction of the next of kin,*" she read aloud, though I could see she'd already read it a dozen times. "Well. That'll be the talk of the Green by Sunday." She lowered the paper. Her hands were shaking slightly, and I saw that it was not the cold. "They'll want to know why, Orsen. Forty years they've been telling that story their way. You've stopped the sale, but you haven't stopped the story. A withdrawn auction is just a new silence, if you let it be."

I took the newsletter from her. The print was small and the paper was cheap and it was, I understood suddenly, the most important document I had held in forty years—more than the solicitor's letter, more than the notebooks, because the notebooks I could read alone, and this was read by everyone.

"I know," I said.

"Do you? Because I'll tell you what I know, and then I'll go home and leave you to it. I know that girl Nell registered those books under a name that means something. I know you sat in that pub in November and drank half a bitter and answered no questions. And I know that whatever your mother wrote in those books, the village is still carrying its version, and its version has been a weight on *me*, Orsen—on all of us who watched a soaked boy come home and say nothing. We carried it too. We carried it without knowing what we carried." She pressed the paper into my hands. "You've paid the money. Now pay the rest."

I stood in the doorway long after she'd gone, the newsletter in my hands, the fen flat and silver under a sky the color of old pewter, and I thought about the pub with its low beams and its forty years of rumor, and about the version of the story that lived there—*the mother drove him out, the fen took him, the one who came back saw something*—and I understood that the next thing I had to do was not translation at all.

It was testimony.

That evening I sat at the kitchen table with all fourteen notebooks in a row, my mother's numbered silence, and I opened the last one to the final page, and I began, line by line, to prepare the reading—not the private translation I had been making for myself, but a public one, plain English, plain voice, the confession shaped to be spoken aloud in a room full of people who had waited four decades to hear it. The fire was lit this time. I fed it nothing but kindling.

Outside, the fen darkened into the hour when the water takes the light home, and I wrote until the lamp was the only lit thing for miles, and in my chest Denny's name sat ready on my tongue like a word I would need soon.
