## 1. The Inventory

The word for *threshold* went first.

I can date it, more or less — a Tuesday in October, four years ago, a contract for a Swiss architectural firm, a paragraph about transitional spaces between public and private zones — and then the blank, clean and total, like a tooth gone from the back of the mouth. I knew *threshold* in English, obviously. I knew it in French, in German, in the flat bureaucratic Esperanto of European Union directives. But the word I'd grown up with, the word my mother used when she told me to wipe my feet, that word had simply left. Not faded. Left, the way a tenant leaves, taking the fixtures.

I keep a notebook for this. Blue cloth cover, bought in a stationery shop in Trieste because I liked the weight of it. On the left-hand pages I record the words my childhood language has lost to me — or, to be precise, that I have lost from it; the language itself, I suspect, is indifferent. On the right-hand pages I keep a different inventory. The sentences my family has never spoken.

The left-hand pages fill slowly now, a word or two a month, the losses slowing as the losses must. The right-hand pages have not gained a single entry in eleven years. That is not because the inventory is complete. It is because no one in my family has said anything new worth not saying.

* * *

The job was routine. A museum in Basel, exhibition texts for a retrospective of a photographer I will not name, forty panels of wall copy, each one a hundred and twenty words of measured, international English that wanted to become something else. The something else was the language of my childhood — the one with the threshold in it — and I have been translating into it professionally for twenty-three years, which is either a devotion or a symptom, depending on the day.

The panels were easy. Photographs of interiors, mostly: kitchens, waiting rooms, a hospital corridor at 4 a.m. The prose was the prose of everywhere — *the artist invites us to consider* — and I moved through it with the mild contempt of a professional for work that does not resist. By the third day I was ahead of schedule. I remember this. I remember thinking, with the small vanity that precedes every fall I have ever taken, *this one will be simple.*

Panel thirty-one was a photograph of a doorway. Not a door — a doorway, the absence where a door had been removed, leading from a kitchen into a room the camera couldn't see. The wall copy read:

*The empty frame suggests passage without arrival. The artist returns, again and again, to thresholds — the places where one life ends and another has not yet begun.*

I sat with my hands on the keys for a long time.

I knew the sentence in English. I knew it in four languages that had no business knowing it. I reached for the word in the language of my childhood the way you reach for a light switch in a house where you once lived, hand out, confident, and found nothing. Not a gap where the word used to be — gaps can be felt, can be mapped, can be worked around. Nothing. The space where the reaching happens had itself been demolished.

I did what professionals do. I paraphrased. I built a sentence of approach, a sentence of circumlocution, the grammatical equivalent of describing a missing person by their clothes. *The place where the wall opens. The line a house draws and does not close.* It was competent. It would pass review. Any reviewer in Basel would read it and feel nothing wrong, because the wrongness was not in the sentence. The wrongness was in me, in the specific quality of the silence where a word should have stood, a silence with a shape, the shape of a word I could no longer hear.

I opened the blue notebook. Left-hand page, new entry:

*prag — no. That's the wrong language. The word itself. The word for threshold. I have it in my mouth and it is not there. It is like holding my hand out to my mother and her not taking it, except the mother is a word and the hand is my mouth and —*

I stopped writing. Even my metaphors were going soft.

* * *

Here is what I know about how languages die in a person. They do not die of disuse, exactly, though disuse is the weather they die in. They die of replacement, and then of shame, and then of a final, quiet administrative act: the mind stops maintaining the file. Words need to be handled to survive, turned over, spoken to another living mouth, and mine had gone unspoken for so long that the language had begun to repossess itself, room by room, like a bank foreclosing on a house the family still technically lives in.

I was six when we moved. The new country, the new language, the school where my name was a small daily humiliation until I shortened it. My mother's rule was never announced as a rule. It was simply enacted: in this house, we speak the language that will not make your teachers frown. She enforced it with a gentleness that I have only recently, at fifty-one, come to understand as terror. She was not erasing our language. She was burying it under us, the way you bury something to protect it from fire, and then, over the years, she forgot where the hole was.

My father adapted the way water adapts. My brother Daniel adapted faster than any of us, shedding the old words like a coat in spring, and by ten he dreamed in the new language and by twelve he had stopped dreaming in any language I could follow. And my mother — my mother kept one language for the street and one for the kitchen and a third, wordless one, for her brother, whom she did not speak to, and had not spoken to, since the winter I was nine.

That is the first entry in the right-hand pages of the blue notebook, the one I wrote at thirty-nine, after a Christmas dinner at which my uncle's name was avoided with such athletic grace — three generations, eleven people, four hours — that I went home and sat at my desk and wrote, in the notebook, the sentence that had never been said:

*What happened the winter you stopped speaking to your brother.*

I have never asked it. I want that on the record, in whatever record this is. Forty-two years of family, and I have never once asked the question, and neither has Daniel, and neither has my father, who died seven years ago still not asking it, still passing the salt across from my uncle at weddings with the elaborate courtesy of men at a border checkpoint. The silence is not an absence in my family. It is a structure. It has load-bearing walls. We live inside it the way the photographer's interiors are lived in — around the removed door, past the empty frame, through the room no camera can see.

* * *

On the fourth day of the Basel job, I called my mother.

This was not unusual. We speak on Thursdays, twenty minutes, her voice pitched to the bright register she uses for the telephone, the register that means *nothing is wrong, nothing has ever been wrong.* I called on a Tuesday, which she noted — "Is everything all right, Oscar?" — and I said yes, everything was fine, I was working, and then, before I could stop myself, I said:

"What's the word for threshold? The old word. The one you used to say when I came in with wet shoes."

The pause was not long. Three seconds, maybe four. But I have spent my life listening to the pauses in my family the way musicians listen to rests, and this one had a whole movement in it.

"Why?" she said.

"A translation," I said. "A photograph of a doorway. It's nothing."

"It's nothing," she agreed, and I could hear her deciding something, the small click of a decision made, and then she said, in the language of my childhood, the word.

I will not write it here. I find I can't — not because it is forbidden, but because the moment she said it, something in me closed around it, the way a hand closes around a coin, and I understood that I had been given something and that the giving had cost her, and I did not yet know why, and I hated not knowing why, and I said thank you and told her about the weather in my city and the weather in hers, and we hung up at minute nineteen, a minute early, which in the economy of my mother is a great deal.

The word, once I had it, worked. I finished panel thirty-one in eleven minutes. The sentence sat in the language of my childhood like a stone that had always been in that wall. I sent the whole file to Basel and invoiced for the full amount and felt the specific satisfaction of a problem solved.

Then, that night, I opened the blue notebook and turned to the right-hand pages and looked at the single entry, the question I have never asked, and beneath it, for the first time in eleven years, I wrote a second entry. Not a sentence anyone in my family has never said. A fact:

*She knew the word instantly. She has been keeping it. She keeps the whole language somewhere — in the kitchen, in the third language, the wordless one — and she gave me one word out of it like giving one coin out of a hoard, and she decided to, in four seconds, and the decision cost her something, and I don't know what.*

I sat with that for a while. Then I closed the notebook, and that was when I saw the letter.

It had come that morning, mixed in with the invoices and the Basel contract and a catalogue from a German press. Heavy cream paper, handwritten address, a stamp from a country I had stopped associating with my family's language decades ago. The handwriting was not my mother's. It was not anyone's.

Inside was a single page, typed, the ink slightly uneven, the way typing goes when the machine is old and the hands are older:

*My name is Mirela. I am told I am the last one who speaks it properly. I am ninety-one years old and I am going to teach one person everything, and then I am going to stop. If you are reading this, someone who knows your work thinks you are the person. Come on Saturday. Come alone. Come before I change my mind, which I do daily.*

There was no signature beyond the name. No explanation of how the letter had found me, or who "someone who knows your work" might be. At the bottom, in different ink, in handwriting that shook, a single line had been added:

*Bring nothing. I mean it about the alone.*

I read it three times. Then I did what I always do with documents that frighten me: I got out the blue notebook, to transcribe the letter, to make it into inventory, to turn the living thing into a catalogued thing.

And I stopped, pen over paper, because I had just understood something, the way you understand a sound after it has stopped — that Mirela's letter was written in the language of my childhood, every word of it, and I had read it without hesitation, without reaching, without a single gap.

The language was not dead in me. It was buried, and something in the reading had opened the ground.

On Saturday, I would go alone. But first, on the left-hand page, under the word for *threshold*, I wrote the last word of Mirela's letter, the one I had almost missed, the one my mother had never once used in my hearing, in forty years of Thursdays:

*properly.*
