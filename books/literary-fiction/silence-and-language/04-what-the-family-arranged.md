## 4. What the Family Arranged

My mother sets the table for four every Sunday, and every Sunday the fourth place is a small violence no one names. She lays out the good plates — the ones with the blue rim that belonged to her mother — and she puts one at the head, where Uncle Emil used to sit, and then she moves it back to the cupboard before anyone arrives. I have watched her do this. I don't think she knows I've watched. It takes her maybe three seconds, lifting the plate off the table with both hands, the way you'd lift something sleeping.

Oscar arrives late, which is unlike her. She comes in smelling faintly of someone else's house — woodsmoke, dried herbs, a sweetness like old paper — and when Mother asks where she's been, Oscar says "errands," and the word sits on the table between them like an unclaimed coat.

"Errands," Mother repeats, and lets it go. That's the arrangement in miniature: a question asked so it can be dropped.

I carve the chicken because Father's hands shake now and he pretends they don't, and pretending is easier if nobody hands him a knife. The choreography begins before the food does. Nobody mentions Emil. Nobody mentions the winter of '79, or the house on Kessel Street, or the letters that stopped coming. Nobody mentions the word *river*, though I couldn't tell you why river would come up at all — except that lately everything in this family feels like it's flowing toward something we've dammed.

Mother talks about the neighbor's hedge. Father agrees with her twice, once too many times, the way he does when he's tracking the conversation from a step behind. Oscar eats carefully, watching our mother's mouth the way she used to watch the mouths of strangers on trains, back when she was a girl collecting accents like other kids collected stamps. I know that look. I grew up next to it.

"So," Mother says, bright as a struck match, "Daniel, tell them about the Henderson account."

I tell them about the Henderson account. It takes four minutes and costs me nothing, which is what stories are for at this table. Oscar nods along. Under the table, her knee is bouncing. My sister has never been able to lie still; it's why she's bad at lying and why she's the only one of us who ever tried to stop.

* * *

After lunch, while Mother washes dishes she won't let anyone else touch, I find Oscar in the hallway putting on her coat. Her scarf smells of smoke again. Not cigarette smoke. Woodsmoke, and under it something green, like a garden after rain.

"You've been going somewhere," I say.

"I told you. Errands."

"Oscar." I keep my voice down. Through the kitchen door I can hear water running, a plate being set down with exaggerated care. "You smell like a farmhouse. You have ink on your left hand and it isn't your usual ink — it's thicker, it's fountain pen, and you haven't used a fountain pen since university. And you came in twenty minutes late looking like a woman who's been *listening*."

She pulls the scarf loose. "Mirela Voss," she says. "The old woman up past the reservoir. She's teaching me."

The name lands in my chest like a stone dropped down a well. I wait for the splash. It doesn't come.

"Teaching you what?"

"You know what."

I do know what. That's the problem. Everyone in this family knows what, the way everyone knows which floorboard creaks and steps around it without discussing the stepping. Mirela Voss. Ninety-one years old and the last person alive who speaks the language our mother spoke as a child and buried like a body. There were articles about her two years ago — *Last Fluent Speaker of a Vanishing Tongue*, photographs of her hands, her fierce mouth. Mother read the article standing up, then folded it into quarters and put it in the drawer with the batteries, which is where things go in our house when they need to be neither kept nor thrown away.

"She put an announcement in the parish newsletter," Oscar says. "One student. Final apprenticeship. Three people applied before me and she sent all of them home." A small, helpless pride crosses her face. "She kept me waiting forty minutes on purpose. Then she made me sit in silence for half an hour and write down every sound I heard. That was the first lesson."

"And you didn't think to mention it?"

"To whom?" And there it is — the question that isn't really a question, the crack running through the middle of our family that we all walk over daily. To whom indeed. To Mother, who hasn't spoken that language in forty years and flinches when she hears it? To Father, who married into the silence and has spent his whole marriage politely not noticing the walls? To you, Daniel, keeper of the family's careful weather?

"Look," I say, and I take her arm, and I feel how thin her arm is through the coat sleeve, how much of my sister is still the girl who got sick every winter. "I'm not going to pretend I understand what you're doing. But I'm asking you to think about what it stirs up."

"It's just words, Daniel."

"No." I hear my own voice harden and can't soften it. "It isn't. Words are exactly what it is. Every word she gives you is a word Mother gave up. You think that language went extinct? It didn't go extinct. It was *put down*. Like an animal. By us. For reasons."

"What reasons?" Oscar asks, and her eyes are terrible, because she already knows I'll give her the family answer, the merciful one, the one we've all been handed and none of us has ever checked. "What happened that winter, Daniel? What did Emil—"

"Don't say his name here." The kitchen water has stopped running. I lower my voice until it's almost nothing. "Not in this house."

We stand in the hallway, two adults, children of the same quiet, and I watch my sister decide whether to fight me. She has their stubbornness — Mother's jaw set at Emil's angle — but she also has something the rest of us lost early: the belief that saying a thing aloud might make it lighter instead of heavier.

"Mother can't afford to lose what she's buried," I say finally. "You understand that? Some people survive by forgetting. It's not cowardice. It's how she gets up in the morning. It's how she's gotten up every morning since I've been alive. And you're over there with that woman, digging. Stirring things Mother can't afford to lose. Every word you learn is a word she has to pretend she doesn't hear."

Oscar is quiet a long moment. Then: "Is that what she'd say? Or is that what we decided she'd say, forty years ago, without asking her?"

"That's not fair."

"None of this is fair," she says, gently, which is worse than if she'd shouted. "That's rather the point."

From the kitchen, Mother calls out that there's leftover chicken if anyone wants it, her voice pitched high and easy, the voice of a woman performing domesticity like a witness performing an alibi. Father answers her. The ordinary machinery of the afternoon resumes around us.

* * *

I drive Oscar home because it's raining and because I want more time, and because part of me — the honest part, the part I keep in the glovebox with the registration papers — wants to see where she's been going. She directs me past the reservoir, up a road I didn't know was paved, to a low stone house with smoke rising crooked from its chimney. She doesn't ask me to stop. She just looks at it, the way you look at a place you've started to love against your better judgment.

"She knew her," Oscar says suddenly. "Mirela. She knew Mother. Before. I saw it today — I said the family name, just in passing, testing, and her whole face changed. She put down her teacup like it had gone hot."

"Then she knows things," I say, "and ninety-year-old women who know things die, Oscar, and take it all with them, and maybe that's a mercy—"

"Or maybe it's a theft."

"From whom?" I nearly shout it. "Who is the language *for*? There's no one left to speak it to. There's no village. There's nothing. It's a dead thing and you're performing CPR on a corpse because you can't stand that we buried it — well, some things get buried because burial is the only decent option left."

The rain ticks on the roof. Oscar looks at me with such sadness that I have to fix my eyes on the road.

"You said 'we' just now," she says quietly. "'We buried it.' 'Some things we bury.' Daniel. You were four years old that winter. Who taught you the burying?"

I open my mouth to answer and find, to my genuine astonishment, that I don't know. I have carried the family's silence my whole life like a stone in my shoe — I have walked on it, accommodated it, built my gait around it — and I have never once asked who put it there. Mother, obviously. Mother arranged everything. But even Mother was arranging something. Even Mother was following a plan older than her grief.

"Go home," I say, which means *stop*, which means *please*, which means every unaskable thing.

She goes. I watch her walk up the path in the rain, and before she reaches the door it opens — the old woman, tiny and straight-backed, silhouetted in lamplight — and Mirela Voss looks past my sister directly at the car, directly at me, across forty yards of falling water, and she raises one hand. Not a wave. Something older than a wave. A gesture I have seen somewhere before, in a photograph, in a drawer, in a life I wasn't allowed to remember.

I drive home with my heart going like a trapped bird, and when Mother asks where I've been, I say errands.

The word fits my mouth perfectly. That's the worst part. It fits like it was made for me.
