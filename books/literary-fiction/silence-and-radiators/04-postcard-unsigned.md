## 4. Postcard, Unsigned

The viewing was held on a Saturday in late September, in the shell of what had once been the Marrow Lane community hall, because Vera had apparently decided that selling a burned house required the same dignity as selling a dead man's watch collection: everything laid out under glass, everything priced, nothing explained.

Quentin arrived early on purpose. He had learned long ago that the first ten minutes of any gathering belonged to the organisers—the caterers, the auctioneers' assistants, the women who arranged chairs—and that if you moved among them with a clipboard of your own invention and an expression of mild professional concern, you could pass through almost any room unexamined. He wore his grey coat. He carried a notebook he did not need. He perfected, in the reflection of the hall's darkened window as he approached, the smile that ended conversations: not unfriendly, exactly, but sealed at the edges, a smile that said *I am here for reasons of my own and will thank you not to inquire after them.*

It worked on the two assistants from Halloway & Finch, who nodded at him as though he might be a surveyor. It worked on Mrs. Pell's grandson, who was stacking folding chairs against the wall with the sullen efficiency of the young. It even worked, briefly, on Daniel, who stood near the front of the hall talking to a man in a tweed cap about drainage easements, and who glanced up, frowned faintly—*do I know you?*—and then looked away again, satisfied that whatever the answer was, it did not require him to cross the room.

Twenty years, Quentin thought, and my own brother has to squint at me like a stranger at a bus stop.

He supposed that was the point of the smile. He had built it brick by brick over two decades, in post offices from Penzance to Perth, and it had never once failed him.

The lot itself was not open for viewing—that came later in the week, a supervised walk-through of the scorched ground, hard hats provided—but Halloway & Finch had assembled a table of *associated effects*, which was auction-speak for everything that had been dragged out of the house before it fell or raked out of its ashes afterwards. Quentin circled this table the way he had once circled the swimming baths as a boy, waiting for the lifeguard to look away before he climbed the high board. A fire-damaged mantel clock, its face clouded white like an eye with cataracts. A set of dining chairs, three surviving, their seats blistered. His mother's sewing basket, or something claiming to be it, the wicker charred to lace along one side. A tin of buttons fused into a single lump that the catalogue, with breathtaking optimism, described as *collectible.*

And then, at the far end of the table, half-shadowed by the clock, a cardboard estate box with the flaps folded shut and a pencil notation on one side in Vera's handwriting: *Papers — misc.*

He would have walked past it. That was the terrible thing, the thing he would turn over for nights afterwards—he would have walked straight past it, because he had spent twenty years training himself not to look at anything connected with 14 Marrow Lane, and the training held even now, even here. But a draught from the propped door lifted one flap of the box, and the corner of a postcard showed itself, white against the brown cardboard, and Quentin felt the floor tilt very slightly beneath him, the way it tilts on a ship when you have forgotten you are at sea.

He knew that white. It was the particular chalky stock of the postcards sold in the kiosk at St Ives station, six for a pound, which he had bought by the fistful in the early years until the kiosk closed and he'd had to source plainer ones, then plainer still, until finally he'd taken to buying blank card stock and cutting it down himself, because the message mattered more than the picture and there were only so many pictures of harbours a man could send into a void.

He did not touch the box. Not yet. He walked instead to the far side of the hall, where a trestle table held tea things nobody had poured, and he stood with his back to the room and counted sixty seconds, and then forty more, listening to the small sounds of the viewing behind him: the scrape of chairs, the auctioneer's assistant reading out lot numbers to herself, someone laughing too loudly about the buttons.

Then he went back, and he opened the box.

They were all there. Not all—all would have been four hundred and eleven, counting the ones lost to moves and changes of address, and the box was not large enough. But dozens. Dozens upon dozens, stacked loosely, face-down, the way you stack letters you intend to deal with later and never do. He lifted the top handful with hands that had gone strangely distant from him, as though they belonged to the man in the grey coat rather than to the person underneath it, and he turned them over one by one.

Postcard the ninety-seventh. Postcard the hundred-and-fortieth. Postcard the two-hundred-and-third, the one with the smudge where his pen had caught on the grain of the card because he'd written it standing up against a wall in Whitby in the rain. Each addressed, in his handwriting, which twenty years had worn from a boy's careful slant into something narrower and more guarded, to:

*14 Marrow Lane*

No town. No postcode. The Royal Mail had returned every single one, eventually, stamped *addressee unknown* or *no such address* or, in the early days, before the house had even finished being a ruin anyone thought about, simply *undeliverable*, in a rubber stamp so casual it might have been marking a parcel of socks. And each returned postcard had gone—where? He had assumed they were destroyed. He had assumed the sorting office binned them, that his words to a burned house died twice, once in the sending and once in the returning. He had never once imagined them coming home.

But here they were. Here they had been, apparently, for years. Some bore additional marks—a second stamp, a third, the palimpsest of journeys he couldn't reconstruct. One had a coffee ring across the message side, dried to a tidy brown circle, which meant someone had set a cup down on it, had read it with a drink in hand, had treated his postcard the way one treats any domestic paper. Someone had lived alongside these. Someone had kept them in a box marked *misc.*

He read the one on top, the 203rd, because he could not stop himself, because the words were his own and yet he read them like a stranger's:

*Mum grew tomatoes on the windowsill the summer we were nine. You remember. She talked to them. I have decided this is why mine die. Everything here is fine. Q.*

He had signed it Q. He always signed Q, or nothing at all, or—in the reckless middle years, when the ritual had begun to feel less like penance and more like conversation—one of the names that weren't his. *Your neighbour at number 12.* *A well-wisher.* Once, memorably, *the ghost*, which he had regretted within the hour and written three contrite follow-ups about.

Someone had read these. Someone had read *Mum grew tomatoes on the windowsill* and had not come looking for him—or had come looking and failed—or had known, had known all along, and had sat with the knowledge the way the family sat with everything, quietly, in assigned silences.

The hall seemed to thin around him. Sounds arrived late and slightly wrong, as if through water. He became aware that he was standing at a public viewing, in broad daylight, holding his own secret in both hands, and that the secret had been sitting in a cardboard box marked *papers — misc* for God knew how long, available to anyone with eight pounds and a curiosity about provincial ephemera.

Lot 47, the catalogue would say. *Collection of postcards, various, addressed to 14 Marrow Lane.*

His handwriting. His Q. Any one of them, examined for thirty seconds by anyone who had ever received a letter from him—and Imogen had, in the one year she'd been away at university before Australia; Daniel had, in the brief thaw of their twenties; Vera had, over solicitors' matters—any one of them could be matched to him the way a key matches a lock it was cut for.

He put the postcards down. He squared the stack, because his hands wanted something orderly to do, and then he realised that squaring the stack was itself a confession—that no stranger squares a stranger's papers—and he made himself step back and leave the pile slightly crooked.

*Think,* he told himself. *Who.*

Vera had written the notation on the box. But Vera was not the sort to hoard; Vera was the sort to file, and filed things got labelled properly, and *papers — misc* was not a label, it was a shrug. No—this box had the quality of something assembled in grief or guilt, quickly, from wherever the postcards had actually lived. A drawer. A shoebox under a bed. A biscuit tin on top of the wardrobe, which was where his mother had kept the Christmas cards, and the thought of his mother landed in him like a stone dropped down a well, taking a long time to reach bottom.

Daniel, then? Daniel, who had stayed, who had inherited the practical burdens, who might reasonably have been the one the dead letters were delivered to—except the house was gone, the door was gone, there was no letterbox to return them to. The Royal Mail would have sent them back to sender. To him. Unless—

Unless he had never given a true return address. He tried to remember. In the early years he had used poste restante, general delivery, picking the cards up himself from offices in towns he was passing through, so that the returns would have come back to those offices and been held, and destroyed, unclaimed. So how—

Or the family had redirected them. Had left instructions. Had said, to some postmaster, in some year he couldn't imagine: *anything for 14 Marrow Lane, send it to us.* Who does that? What kind of people adopt the mail of a burned house?

People who think the house is still receiving it, he thought. People who believe, somewhere below the level of sense, that the dead can be written to. People who—

Or people who know who is writing.

The idea arrived whole, cold, and unwelcome: that someone had recognised the handwriting years ago. That someone had watched the postcards arrive, month after month, year after year, addressed to a place that wasn't there, and had done the arithmetic—*who loved this house enough to keep writing to it? who grieves it without saying so?*—and had arrived at a name, and had said nothing, and had kept the cards, and had let him go on writing into what he believed was a void, when all along the void had eyes.

His skin prickled. He looked up sharply, scanning the hall, and for one vertiginous moment every face seemed to be watching him: the assistant with her lot numbers, Mrs. Pell's grandson with his chairs, the man in tweed, Daniel—Daniel, who was no longer talking about drainage, who was looking across the room at him now with a narrowed attention that had not been there before, an attention that said *I have placed you after all.*

Quentin deployed the smile. Full strength. The one that ended conversations.

Daniel did not end the conversation, because they were not having one. Daniel simply held his gaze a moment longer than politeness required, and then turned back to the tweed man, and Quentin understood that the look had not been *who are you* but *I know who you are, and we will speak.*

Not today, though. Today the hall was filling—neighbours, bidders, a local journalist with a camera bag—and the viewing had a rhythm to it, a tide, and Quentin rode the tide out. He examined the clock. He examined the chairs. He asked the assistant an intelligent question about provenance that he did not care about, and she answered it kindly, and none of it touched him, because all of him was in the box.

Near the door, on his way out, he passed the table one last time. He allowed himself a single glance downward, no more than courtesy requires of any browser, and that was when he saw it: tucked inside the front flap of the box, where a bookmark goes, one further postcard, separated from the rest, face-up.

It was not one of his.

It was older. The stock was thicker, glossier, a seaside card from a decade before his time—donkeys on a beach, colours gone soft as bruised fruit. And on the back, in handwriting he knew better than his own, because he had watched it write shopping lists and birthday cards and, once, a note left on the kitchen table that changed everything: his mother's hand, looping and quick.

There was no address. There was no salutation. There was only a single line, in ink faded to the colour of weak tea:

*If anything happens, look in the cellar.*

Quentin stood in the doorway of the community hall with September light falling on him and the sound of the auctioneer's assistant calling out chair counts, and he understood, with the calm that arrives at the centre of catastrophes, that the box had never been about his postcards at all. His postcards were the camouflage. Or the bait. Or the echo—his mother's line and his own two decades of silence answering each other across the years in the same cardboard grave.

Someone had put that card where it would be found.

Someone wanted the cellar looked in.

Behind him, footsteps crossed the hall at an unhurried pace, and a voice he had last heard on a platform, saying nothing at all, said now, pleasantly:

"You've been a long time over the buttons."

Vera stood beside him, looking not at him but at the box, her hands folded, her face arranged into an expression he recognised with a lurch because it was his own: the smile that ends conversations.

"Mind the lot numbers," she said. "Everything here is sold as seen."
