## 13. What Father Buried

The ledgers smelled of the cellar they had slept in for twenty years—damp stone and old smoke and something sweeter underneath, like apples rotting in a drawer. Imogen sat on an upturned crate in the corner of her mother's old kitchen, which was now Vera's kitchen, which was now nobody's kitchen, and turned the first page with hands that would not stay steady.

They had carried them out of Marrow Lane at two in the morning, Quentin and she, wrapped in a painter's drop cloth like newborns or corpses. Nine ledgers. Leather-bound once, their spines charred to black lace where the fire had licked down through the floorboards before giving up. Her father's handwriting marched across the pages in that upright, patient script she had spent her childhood imitating on school exercises—the same loops, the same decisive crossbars—as though the man had practised legibility his whole life so that his crimes would one day be easy to read.

*Marlowe, E. — deposit received — £400.*
*Marlowe, Edith — interest paid — £18 6s.*
*Pell, A. — deposit received — £1,200.*

She stopped at that one. Pell. Mrs. Pell, who still lived in the narrow house with the blue door three doors down from the ruin, who had brought a ham to the funeral and said nothing about anything ever again.

"Imogen." Quentin stood in the doorway with two mugs of tea he had made without asking how anyone took it, because twenty years away had not taught him that people change their habits; it had only taught him to assume they did. "You've been reading for an hour. You haven't said a word."

"I'm counting," she said.

"Counting what?"

"Names."

He came and sat beside her on the crate, close enough that their shoulders touched, the way they used to sit on the garden wall when they were children watching for the postman, and she felt him go rigid as he read over her arm. The kitchen was dark except for the lamp they'd brought, and outside the window the town lay under its ordinary Tuesday quiet, streetlamps burning above gardens where sprinklers ticked and dogs dreamed, and somewhere in it all were the living versions of these names.

*Harrow & Son, builders — disbursement — £310.*
*Bexley, R. — deposit received — £850.*
*Cole, Margaret (widow) — deposit received — £2,000.*

"Widow Cole," Imogen said softly. "She used to give us barley sugar. She put two thousand pounds in my father's cellar and he wrote it down like a grocery list."

Quentin set both mugs down on the floor without drinking. "Keep going," he said, and his voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a man walking on ice he already knows is thin.

She kept going. Page after page, year after year, the ledger's arithmetic unfolding like a confession no priest had ever heard. Their father had not been a savings man, exactly—that was what the town had believed, that Gerald Ash ran some informal kind of neighbourhood bank, holding money for people who distrusted the building society on the high street, paying them a little interest out of kindness or eccentricity. He had been, in the town's affectionate memory, a man who liked to be useful. What he had actually been was a sluice gate. Money came in from Marlowe and Pell and Bexley and Cole and thirty others whose names Imogen now read aloud into the dark kitchen, and money went out—to accounts she could not trace, to initials and codes, to *H.L.* and *V.R.M.* and a column headed simply *overseas*—and what came back was thinner every year, like a river being drunk from upstream.

"He wasn't keeping their savings," she said. "He was spending them. And writing down every penny so he'd know exactly how much he'd stolen from each of us."

"Not from us." Quentin took the ledger gently from her hands, as if it might bruise. "From them. From the whole street. From—" He turned back a page, then another, hunting. "Here. Look at the dates. The last entries are six weeks before the fire. He knew. By then he must have known there wasn't enough left to pay anyone back. That's why the cellar door stayed locked. That's why he started sleeping badly. That's why—" He stopped.

"That's why Mother was crying in the garden that summer," Imogen finished. She had never said it aloud before. It had lived in her for twenty years the way a splinter lives in a palm: too deep to grip, too true to ignore. "I saw her through the window. She had one of his letters in her hand and she was standing very still, the way you stand when crying would be a luxury. And I was fourteen and I decided I'd imagined it, because imagining it was easier than knowing it."

The lamp hummed. Somewhere upstairs Vera's floorboards creaked and went silent again, the house listening the way it always listened.

"Do you understand what this means?" Quentin said. "It means the fire didn't just take our house. Everyone on that street lost money that night. Mrs. Pell's twelve hundred pounds. Widow Cole's two thousand. The Harrows' building fund. All of it burned with the ledgers' proof—or they thought it did—and then everyone just... agreed to have never had it. Do you remember? After the fire, half the street stopped speaking to us. Not because we'd done anything. Because we were the evidence. We were the family whose misfortune reminded them what they'd lost, and they couldn't even mourn it properly, because mourning it meant admitting they'd handed their life savings to a neighbour in a biscuit tin economy because they trusted him more than the bank."

"And the ones who kept being kind," Imogen said slowly, "were the ones who'd lost the most."

She thought of Mrs. Pell's ham. Of Widow Cole coming round every week for a year with vegetables from her allotment, pressing them on Vera with a fierce tenderness that had embarrassed them all at the time. Of Mr. Harrow fixing the fence at the rental house they'd moved to, unasked, on a wet Saturday, and refusing payment, and saying only, *Your dad was good to us once,* which had sounded like gratitude and now sounded like grief.

"They weren't comforting us," she said. "They were comforting themselves. They were burying their own losses inside our tragedy. We gave the whole street a place to put what they couldn't say, and we never even knew we were doing it. We thought they pitied us. They were using us, Quentin. Kindly. But using us."

Quentin laughed—a single dry note with nothing in it. "And I burned the proof. I thought I was saving Mother. I thought I was destroying the thing that would shame us." He looked down at the ledger in his lap, at the neat columns, at the name *Pell, A.* with its tidy sum. "I was destroying the only record of what was taken from them. Twenty families. Maybe more. They've spent twenty years believing they imagined their own poverty."

* * *

Daniel arrived at seven the next morning, which meant he had driven through the night, which meant Imogen had called him, which meant the secret had lasted less than forty-eight hours—and she found she was not sorry. Secrets in this family were load-bearing walls. Take one out and you had to find out fast whether the whole structure came down.

He stood in the kitchen doorway in his coat, grey at the temples now, carrying himself with the careful authority of a man who chaired things, and looked at the nine ledgers laid out on the table like exhibits.

"So," he said. "It's true."

"It's true," Imogen said.

"The fire. You're telling me the fire—" He didn't finish it. He crossed to the table and picked up the top ledger and read it the way he read contracts, fast and total, his lips barely moving, and Imogen watched her older brother's face do something she had never seen it do in forty-odd years of knowing it: it came apart by degrees, quietly, like frost leaving a window.

"Sixty-one names," he said at last. "I counted sixty-one names across all nine books. Sixty-one households." He set the ledger down with exaggerated gentleness, the way you set down something when the alternative is throwing it through a window. "Do you know what Father's eulogy said? I wrote it. I was twenty-four and I wrote that he was *a pillar of this community, a man who held this street together.*" His voice cracked on *held*. "I stood in front of sixty-one people and praised the man who'd picked their pockets, and they let me, because the alternative was standing up in church and saying *he had my savings and my children's inheritance and it's ash now and I can't even prove it.*"

"Danny—" Quentin began.

"No." Daniel held up a hand, and for a moment he was eleven again, the eldest, allocating blame and mercy with equal severity. "Don't. Not yet. I need—" He pressed his knuckles to his mouth. Outside, a milk float whined past, absurdly, faithfully, twenty years on. "I need to think about what I'm going to do with this."

"What there is to do is tell them," Imogen said. "All of them. Before the auction. The lot sells, the last of it disappears, and those families go on forever thinking they were fools who lost everything to bad luck and a fire. They weren't fools. They were robbed twice—once by him, once by the silence."

"And telling them does what, exactly? Restores sixty-one sums of money that don't exist?" Daniel's voice rose and he mastered it, visibly, the effort like a man hauling a rope. "It gives them a villain. Wonderful. A dead villain they can't hate properly because hating him means admitting they loved him, and it burns whatever's left of this family's name on the way through. Mother's grave will be spat on by association. Vera's spent her whole life in this town—"

"Vera," Imogen said, "has spent her whole life in this town keeping this exact silence. Ask her sometime what that cost her."

"She'll say nothing. She always says nothing. That's the whole architecture of this family, isn't it? Someone decides what doesn't get said, and everyone else arranges their furniture around the gap." Daniel looked at the ledgers, and his anger drained suddenly into something worse—something tired and old. "You know what I keep thinking? I keep thinking about the winter after the fire. Harrow came round to board up what was left of the house, and he wouldn't take money for it, and I thought, how decent, how kind, this town is decent and kind. And now I find out he was boarding up his own grave. Three hundred and ten pounds. He shook my hand over that house."

The kitchen was very quiet. Imogen got up and put the kettle on, because someone had to, because in this family the kettle had always been the answer to questions too large for words, and while it heated she watched her brothers sit on either side of the table like two halves of a verdict that hadn't been reached yet. Quentin with his ruined calm and his postcard eyes. Daniel with his love shaped like a wall.

"There's something else," Quentin said finally. "In the last ledger. The final page."

Daniel pulled it toward him. Imogen had found it at four in the morning and had not shown either of them until now, and she watched Daniel's face as he read the entry dated nine days before the fire:

*M. has seen the books. Everything depends on discretion now. If it comes to light, the children must not carry it.*

"Oh God," Daniel whispered.

"She knew," Imogen said. "Mother found the ledgers nine days before the fire. And then the ledgers burned. And then she never spoke of it again for the rest of her life—not one word, not to any of us, not even at the end." She heard her own voice shake and let it. "We thought we were protecting her. Both of us, in our different ways—I by leaving the country, Quentin by—" She stopped, because Daniel was looking at Quentin now with an expression that contained a question he had been not-asking for twenty years, and the question had just changed shape entirely.

"The children must not carry it," Daniel repeated. "He wrote that. He knew what it would do to us. He knew, and he kept taking the money anyway, right up until the end, right up until—" He closed the ledger. "Right up until our brother burned it all rather than let her carry it alone."

The kettle boiled and nobody moved to pour it.

Imogen stood at the counter with her back to them, looking out at the grey morning, and understood that the ground had shifted beneath more than their family now. Somewhere down the street behind the blue door, Mrs. Pell was waking up to tea she would make in a kitchen her father's fraud had quietly emptied. Somewhere Widow Cole's grandchildren lived in a smaller house than they should have, on a story they'd inherited without knowing it was false. An entire street had arranged its memories around a fire, the way her family had, the way she had—and every one of those arrangements was built on the same missing fact.

"We can't decide this ourselves," she said, turning around. "It's not ours anymore. It never was just ours. Sixty-one households, Danny. This belongs to the whole street, and they've been carrying their half of it blind for twenty years." She looked from Daniel's closed face to Quentin's open one. "Auction's Saturday. Whatever we do, we do it before the gavel falls—because once that lot sells, the last piece of ground where any of this happened belongs to a stranger."

Daniel gathered his coat slowly. At the door he paused, and did not turn around, and said, in a voice stripped of everything but the truth:

"If you tell them, Immy, you'd best be ready for what they ask next. Because the next question after *what did Father bury* is always *who lit the match.*"

The door clicked shut behind him. Quentin and Imogen looked at each other across the nine burned ledgers, and neither of them said a word, and the silence between them was no longer the old silence, the family silence, the comfortable one.

It was the other kind. The kind that comes just before speaking.
