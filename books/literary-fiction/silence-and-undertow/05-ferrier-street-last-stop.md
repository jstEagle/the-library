## 5. Ferrier Street, Last Stop

The bus to Ferrier Street runs four times a day now, and by June it will run never, which is why I found myself at the depot on a Tuesday morning holding a day ticket like a relic from a civilization that no longer existed—mine, specifically.

The driver was a young man with the patient boredom of someone who had driven this route through its long dying. He looked at my ticket, then at me, then back at the ticket, as if trying to place which category of passenger still rode the 47: the elderly with nowhere else to go, the sentimental with nowhere better to be, or the mad.

"End of the line's not much to look at anymore," he offered, when I asked how far it went.

"I'm not looking," I said. "I'm remembering."

He nodded as though this were a normal thing people said, which on this route it probably was. The bus was empty except for an old man two rows up who slept with his mouth open and his hat over his eyes, and I had the whole ride to watch the city unspool backward through the window—the glass towers thinning into brick, the brick thinning into lots, the lots thinning into that particular geography of places cities forget they promised to remember.

I want to say I chose this trip for journalistic reasons. That would be the professional version: the critic returns to the scene, gathers texture, files color. But the truth is less flattering and more honest, which is the trade I've been making all year without admitting it. The truth is that a stranger at my mother's wake said *Ferrier Street* in the tone you'd use for a password, and the word has been sitting in me since like a stone swallowed wrong. You can feel it when you lie down. You can feel it when you stand up. There is no posture in which a swallowed stone is comfortable.

* * *

The neighborhood announced itself before I saw it, by smell—which surprised me, since smell is the one sense I have left in any real working order. Taste abandoned me twenty years ago; smell stayed behind like a loyal dog whose pack has moved on. And Ferrier Street smelled exactly as it had in 1999, or close enough that my body reacted before my mind could intervene: hot dust, cut grass gone to seed, creosote from the old fence posts, and underneath it all, faintly, water. Standing water. The quarry was still there. Of course it was. Quarries don't leave. They just fill up and wait.

Half the street was already gone. I mean that literally—one side of Ferrier Street existed and the other did not. Where the even-numbered houses had stood there was now a field of rubble and surveyor's stakes, orange string strung between them like bunting for a party nobody wanted. A mechanical excavator sat parked among the ruins with its arm folded, patient as a heron. The odd-numbered houses survived, mostly boarded, some still inhabited by the stubborn and the priced-out, their windows full of curtains that had clearly been hung by people who intended to stay until the last possible morning.

Number 19 was the third house from the corner. I knew this without deciding to know it, the way you know the way to your own kitchen in the dark.

It was still standing. That was the first cruelty—that it stood at all, brick and pebble-dash and a lean-to porch, while everything around it had been granted the mercy of demolition. It had been painted once, some institutional gray, and the paint was peeling in long curls that made the house look like something shedding. The gate was gone. The front garden had gone to waist-high grass and one defiant sunflower, self-seeded, blooming its head off at nobody.

Behind the house, past the collapsed section of back fence, I could see the trees. And past the trees, if I leaned, the flat gleam of the flooded quarry, blue-green and calm as a held breath.

Twenty years ago my sister drowned in that water. Twenty years ago I ate jollof rice in a kitchen that stood thirty feet from where I was standing, and tasted it—really tasted it—for the last time in my life. And twenty years ago my family packed up our grief and our guilt and moved across town, changed nothing officially, explained nothing ever, and let the address dissolve from use the way names do when everyone agrees not to say them.

We were the Nwosu-Okafor household from Ferrier Street. The old man at the wake had said it like he'd been keeping it in his mouth for decades, waiting for someone to come collect it.

* * *

Here is what memory does when you feed it geography: it stops being memory and becomes weather.

I stepped through the gap where the gate used to be and the past came down on me all at once, not as images but as sensations, whole and undiluted. The specific give of that ground underfoot—I had run across it a thousand times, barefoot, carrying Ada on my back because she liked being carried and I liked being strong. The height of the sunflower relative to the kitchen window. The exact angle of shade the big tree threw across the porch at midday, under which our father used to sit on Sundays with the newspaper he only pretended to read, listening to us fight.

My body remembered everything. That was the horror of it. My mind had spent two decades building a clean, well-lit room called Before, furnished with soft-focus versions of my sister, and my body walked onto Ferrier Street and tore the door off it. My knees went. Not dramatically—I didn't fall—but they went soft in a way knees shouldn't, and I put a hand out against the pebbled wall of number 19 and stood there breathing like a woman surfacing.

The wall was warm under my palm. Warm brick, rough render, a chip of paint coming away under my fingers. Ada had chipped that paint. She'd picked at it walking in and out for years, a small white wound in the gray, and I had told her to stop it, and she had laughed at me, eight years old and entirely unafraid of her seventeen-year-old sister, and she had kept picking it every single time we passed.

I found the chip with my fingertip. Still there. Twenty years, two owners, one demolition order, and Ada's small vandalism survived it all.

I am not a woman who cries easily. I cry strategically, at funerals, in appropriate quantities, with good lighting. What happened on Ferrier Street was not crying. It was my face simply failing to hold its arrangement, muscles surrendering their posts all at once, so that I stood against the wall of my dead childhood home with my mouth open and my eyes streaming and no expression at all, just weather moving through.

* * *

"You're the Okafor girl."

I hadn't heard the screen door. When I turned, wiping my face with the heel of my hand in the graceless way of the genuinely undone, there was a woman on the porch of number 21 next door—a wide-set woman in a housecoat, arms crossed, silver hair cropped short, watching me with an expression I couldn't immediately classify. Not pity. Not curiosity, exactly. Something more like recognition arriving ahead of explanation.

"I'm sorry?" I said, which was stupid, because I'd heard her perfectly.

"The Okafor girl." She came down the porch steps without hurrying, housecoat snapping a little in the breeze off the water. "The tall one. Mirabel." She said my name the way you'd set down something fragile. "You used to carry your sister on your back up this street. Right here." She pointed at the middle of the pavement, at nothing, at everything. "She'd have her arms around your neck like a little monkey, hollering faster, faster."

"We lived here a long time ago," I said. My voice came out sanded down. "I don't think we've met."

"No," she agreed. "You wouldn't think so."

There was something in the way she said it—not coy, not cryptic, just weighted, like a sentence carrying more freight than its size suggested. She stopped at the property line between the houses, an invisible border she seemed to respect out of long habit rather than law.

"Grace," she said, and held out a hand.

Her grip was dry and strong. Her eyes were brown and unhurried and they did not move off my face, and I understood, in the slow cold way one understands things one isn't ready for, that she had been watching for me. That maybe she had been watching for years. That a woman doesn't sit on a porch for twenty years facing a house where a child died without knowing precisely whom she was waiting for.

"You knew my sister," I said.

"I knew her a little." The phrase came out smooth, practiced, and something about its smoothness snagged on me—the way a too-fluent answer snags on an interviewer who has spent a career hearing rehearsed ones. "Everybody knew her a little. She was always out here. Always in the water, that one. Your mother used to holler herself hoarse."

"She did," I said. "Ada never listened."

"No," Grace said softly. "She didn't."

And then she said a thing that took the ground out from under me a second time, quietly, without apparent effort:

"That last summer she'd taken to going round the far side. Past the warning sign. Because the near bank got crowded on hot days, and she liked her own stretch of water. She told me so herself, not a week before." Grace's gaze went, briefly, to the treeline, to the flat gleam beyond it. "Far side. Past the sign."

I had never told anyone that. Not Chidi, not my parents, not the police, not the therapists, not the editors who'd coaxed my childhood out of me over long lunches hoping for memoir material. I did not know it myself—or rather, I had known it once, at eleven, and the knowledge had gone wherever the taste had gone, sealed into the same dark room.

"How do you know that?" I asked.

Grace looked at me for a long moment. On her porch behind her, a radio was playing low, some gospel station, and the wind carried it in fragments. Somewhere beyond the trees, water lay flat and patient over everything that had happened.

"Because I was here," she said. "That summer. Every day. We were all here." She reached into the pocket of her housecoat and brought out a card—small, cream-colored, the kind printed for funerals—and held it out between two fingers. "This is my number. It was my mother's card; I keep the number on it. You'll want to call me, Mirabel. Maybe not today. Maybe not soon. But you'll want to."

I took the card. My hand was not steady.

"Why would I call you?"

"Because you're asking yourself questions," Grace said. "I can see it. You've been asking them since the funeral, since somebody said the name of this street out loud, and now you've come all this way and stood in that garden and touched that wall—" she nodded at the paint chip, at my fingers still resting near it, and I snatched my hand back like a child caught stealing "—and the questions aren't going to let you alone now. Are they."

It wasn't a question. She delivered it gently, almost kindly, the way you tell someone the road ahead is washed out.

"My mother's funeral was three weeks ago," I said. "How did you—"

"Ferrier Street watches the papers," Grace said. "Your mother's face was in them. Yours too, underneath hers. Restaurant critic." Something flickered across her face—not quite disapproval, not quite sadness. "You write about food, they said. Beautifully."

"Yes," I said, and heard how hollow the word sounded out here, in the smell of dust and standing water, with my sister's paint chip under my thumbnail.

Grace climbed back up her porch steps, unhurried, and paused at the door with her hand on the frame. The gospel music seeped out around her, a choir singing about rivers.

"One more thing," she said, not turning all the way around. "That night it happened. The night your sister went in the water. There were other children at the quarry besides you Okafor children." Now she turned, fully, and her face was very still. "Somebody else saw. Somebody who's been carrying it a long, long time. Longer than you have, and lighter than you'd believe, and heavier than you could imagine—all three at once."

The wind came off the water and moved the sunflower in the ruined garden, and somewhere down the street the excavator started its engine, folding and unfolding its arm over the bones of the even-numbered houses.

"Call me," Grace said again. "When you're ready to hear it."

Then the screen door shut, and I stood alone in my sister's garden holding a dead woman's funeral card, reading the phone number written on the back in careful ballpoint, while behind me the water waited, flat and blue-green and keeping, as it had kept for twenty years, everything it was given.
