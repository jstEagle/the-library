## 7. Taste Memory

Here is what I remember, and I want to be precise about it, because precision is the only inheritance I have left: the jollof rice that night was smoky at the bottom of the pot.

Not burnt. Smoky. There is a difference, and the difference was my mother. Burnt rice is a failure; smoky rice is a decision. You let the bottom layer catch just enough to give the whole pot a dark, bass-note undertone, and you do it on purpose, and you guard the secret of the timing like a state secret, and when your daughter asks how you know when to take it off the fire you say, *a woman knows*, which is not an answer but is somehow a complete education.

I was fourteen. Ada was eleven. It was a Friday in June, though the month hardly matters — every month in Ferrier Street was June if you were a child with no bills. What matters is that it was the last meal I ever tasted, and I did not know that while I was eating it, which is the cruelest thing about last meals: nobody tells you. If they told you, you would chew slower. You would put down your fork between bites and look around the table and memorize everyone's face mid-chew, which is undignified and therefore perfect. You would taste it properly, the way you're supposed to taste things, instead of the way I tasted everything after — as information, as description, as prose.

But I'm getting ahead of myself, and anyway the girl I was had no idea she was living in the past tense. She thought she lived in the present tense forever. We all did.

* * *

The kitchen on Ferrier Street was too small for our family and exactly the right size for our arguments. That's the sentence I've been circling for twenty years, so let me commit to it: too small for six people, exactly right for the noise six people made. My mother cooked at the stove with her back to the room like a queen who refused to look at her subjects, which meant all conversation happened to her spine, and she answered without turning around, which made her seem omniscient. To this day I associate authority with the back of a woman's head.

My father sat at the table doing the crossword in pen. Not pencil — pen. He said a man should commit to his mistakes, which is either wisdom or arrogance depending on the decade of your life you're standing in. He wore his reading glasses low on his nose and he filled in squares with a flourish, and whenever he got a word wrong — which was rare, and therefore an event — Chidi would make a small sound like a kettle deciding whether to whistle, and my father would say, "Out loud, boy, or don't say it," and Chidi would say nothing, because Chidi's entire strategy in life even then was to be correct quietly and let other people discover it at their own expense.

Chidi was sixteen and already built like a man who carried furniture for money, though nobody in our house had ever asked him to carry anything. He ate the way he did everything else — methodically, seriously, with his elbows in — and he had taken personal ownership of the fried plantain, meaning he stood next to my mother at the stove and turned each slice with a fork at intervals only he understood, and if anyone reached past him he gave them a look that could curdle milk.

Ada was on the counter. Not at the table — on the counter, sitting cross-legged next to the fruit bowl, which was against approximately four rules and permitted by one larger unwritten rule, which was that Ada was allowed. That was the whole law. Ada was allowed. She was eleven and she had a gap in her teeth she was proud of in the way children are proud of their wounds before the world teaches them to hide them, and she was telling us about a boy at school who had eaten a worm on a dare, and she told it with such dramatic pacing — pausing, lowering her voice, making us lean in — that by the end we were all leaning over our plates like idiots waiting for a punchline from an eleven-year-old.

"And then," she said, "he swallowed."

Chidi made the kettle sound again, which from him was applause.

"Swallowed it whole?" my father asked, not looking up from the crossword.

"Whole! Like a—" Ada tipped her head back and demonstrated with an imaginary worm, and my mother said, "Off my counter with your worm mouth," without turning around, and Ada laughed and didn't move, because being scolded by our mother's spine was not real scolding; real scolding came with a face, and the face never came for Ada. The face came for me. I was the eldest daughter, which in our house meant I was the practice child, the one they got their technique on before the real performance arrived four years later.

I didn't resent it. I want to be honest about that, because everything else about that night has been bent out of shape by what came after, and I'd like one thing to stay straight. I didn't resent it. I liked being the practice child. Practice children get to watch. And I watched everything — that was my gift, even then, before anyone paid me for it. I watched my father fill in 14 Across with a flourish. I watched Chidi turn a slice of plantain that did not need turning. I watched my mother's shoulders move when she laughed, which was the only part of her laugh she ever showed the stove.

And I watched Daniel.

Daniel was my cousin, my father's sister's son, seventeen that summer, staying with us for two weeks while his mother sorted out whatever needed sorting — nobody explained adult logistics to children in those days; adults simply vanished and reappeared and you were expected to absorb the gaps without comment. Daniel was handsome in the way that gets written up in yearbooks, quick and charming, and he had a way of arriving in a room exactly when the laughter started, so that you remembered him as part of the laughter. He was sitting at the table across from my father, doing the crossword's margins — drawing in the boxes, some joke I can't reconstruct — and my father was letting him, which my father did not let anyone do, and I remember noticing that and filing it away the way I filed everything away, in the vast unlabeled archive of a quiet girl's attention.

Daniel was funny. I want to record that fairly too. He was genuinely funny, and kind to Ada in the way older boys are kind to small girls who worship them — patient, theatrical, generous with dares. Especially dares. That was his currency. *I dare you.* The worm story Ada was telling? A dare from Daniel, relayed secondhand through the playground like scripture. He collected other people's courage the way my father collected pens. I think about that now — how a boy can train a whole neighborhood of smaller children to perform for him, and how the training looks, from the kitchen, like charisma.

That night, at that table, it looked like charisma. It looked like family.

* * *

The rice came to the table in the big blue pot, because my mother believed serving dishes were for people with more time than sense, and we served ourselves straight from the source, spoons scraping the seasoned bottom where the smoke lived. There was chicken — stewed, falling off the bone, cooked in the same pot until the sauce and the meat became a single argument — and there was fried plantain under Chidi's protection, and there was a salad nobody touched except my mother, who served herself a portion of it every single meal as a statement of principle.

"You'll eat salad," she told Ada.

"I'll eat salad tomorrow," Ada said.

"That's what you said yesterday."

"And I meant it yesterday too."

My father, filling in a square: "In my day children ate what was—"

"In your day," my mother said, still facing the stove, "children ate what was put in front of them and also walked uphill both ways, yes, we've heard." And my father smiled into his crossword, because theirs was a marriage built on this exact exchange, forty repetitions a week, neither of them tired of it, both of them performing a script whose entire purpose was to prove the script still worked.

I took my first bite of the rice and the world organized itself around it.

I don't know how else to describe tasting to people who have never lost it. It wasn't just flavor. It was the tomato and the pepper and the onions my mother had pounded herself because the blender was broken and she refused, on principle, to buy another one until my father admitted the old one broke because he'd tried to blend soup bones. It was the smoke note rising underneath like a bass line. It was heat building slowly at the edges of my tongue, and the cool of the plantain arriving to negotiate with it, and the chicken giving up its salt into the sauce. Taste is not one sense; it's a committee meeting, and that night every member of the committee agreed.

"Mm," I said. Just — mm. Fourteen years old, mouth full, mm.

"Mm," Ada agreed from the counter, kicking her feet against the cabinet, and my mother said, "If you're going to sit on my counter at least hold your plate," and Ada held her plate, and the plate was the same blue as the pot, chipped at the rim, and I remember the chip. I remember the chip in the plate. Twenty years later I can draw you that chip. Nobody tells you which details will survive. Your memory is not a historian; it's a magpie, and it takes whatever shines.

Chidi went back for seconds, which was normal, and thirds, which was also normal, and my mother said, "Leave some for the street," which was a joke that meant *there is plenty*, because in our house abundance was expressed through complaint. My father finished the crossword and immediately began a second one he'd been saving, and Daniel leaned over and filled in a square upside down just to annoy him, and my father said, "Boy—" and caught himself, because Daniel wasn't *boy*, Daniel was a guest, and guests got a different register, warmer, more careful. I noticed that too. Filed it. The archive grows.

"What are you looking at?" Daniel asked me suddenly, grinning, catching me watching him the way I watched everything.

"Nothing," I said, which is what quiet girls say, and he laughed like I'd confirmed something.

"Mirabel watches everything," Ada announced, with the pride of a sister describing a superpower. "She remembers stuff too. Ask her anything. Ask her what I wore on Tuesday."

"A yellow dress with the broken strap you fixed with a safety pin," I said, not looking up from my plate, "and you said the pin was jewelry."

"She remembers *everything*," Ada repeated, delighted, as if I'd performed a trick, and Daniel looked at me a moment longer than the joke required — appraising, almost, the way you look at a lock you hadn't planned to pick — and then he went back to defacing the crossword, and the moment passed, and I let it pass, because I was fourteen and the rice was good and nothing that night asked to be examined.

* * *

After dinner the argument started, because after dinner always started the argument; it was practically a course. The subject that night was the quarry.

Someone — I believe it was my father, prompted by the local paper folded beside the crossword — had mentioned that the council was talking about fencing the flooded quarry behind the Hendersons' place. Fencing it properly, with signs. Too many kids swimming in it, the paper said. Two near-drownings last summer alone.

"About time," my mother said. "That water is black as ink. Nothing alive in it."

"It's been there a hundred years," my father said. "It'll be there a hundred more. Children swim in it the way children swim anywhere."

"Children drown in it the way children drown anywhere," my mother said, and the temperature of the room dropped one degree, the way it does when a parent says a true frightening thing in front of children on purpose, as inoculation.

"Not our children," my father said lightly. "Our children have better sense."

And Ada, from the counter, legs swinging: "Daniel says the quarry's bottomless."

"Daniel says a lot of things," my mother said.

"Is it bottomless?"

"It's deep enough," my mother said, "to stay out of."

"Have YOU seen the bottom?"

"I have not seen the bottom of the sea either, and I'm not swimming in it."

"That's different."

"It is not different."

"It IS different—" and Ada was laughing, needling, working my mother the way she worked everyone, because Ada's love language was provocation, and my mother's was resistance, and the two of them could sustain this duet for an hour the way other families sustained conversation.

"Bottomless means nothing," Chidi said, into his third plate. "Everything has a bottom. It's just deeper than your rope."

"How do you know?"

"Because everything has a bottom. Physics."

"Have you measured it?"

"No."

"Then it might be bottomless."

"Then it might," Chidi conceded, with the enormous patience of a brother who has learned that winning an argument with Ada costs more than losing it, and Ada declared victory to the room, and the room declined to contest it.

I said nothing during the quarry argument. I remember that. I sat with my hand around my glass of water — we drank water with dinner, homemade juice was for Sundays — and I listened to my family argue about the water behind the Hendersons' house, and I felt the particular safety of a child listening to adults worry about something far away. Drowning was a word that belonged to newspapers. Danger was a country we didn't hold passports for. My mother said *stay out of* the way she said *look both ways*, ritual language, language that existed precisely so it wouldn't have to mean anything.

Daniel, meanwhile, had stopped drawing in the crossword margins. I remember that too — isn't it strange, the things the magpie takes? He was looking at Ada with an expression I couldn't read then and have spent twenty years reading since, and I have never once settled on what it was. Interest. Calculation. Boredom. A boy looking at a little girl who adored him, weighing nothing at all, thinking nothing at all, being nothing more than seventeen and restless on a Friday night in June.

I choose, most days, to believe it was nothing. Some nights I can't.

* * *

Later — dishes done, the kitchen smelling of tomato and smoke and dish soap, the smell I would give a year of my life to stand inside again — Ada came to my room. She did this most nights. She'd sit on the end of my bed and deliver the day's full report, unedited, including parts I explicitly did not want, and I would pretend to read while she talked, and this was our love, and it was enough.

"Tomorrow," she said, "we're going to the quarry."

"We are absolutely not going to the quarry."

"Daniel's taking us. Him and Emeka and some others. Just to look. You can't swim, Mirabel, obviously, you're a grandmother—"

"Thank you."

"—just to look at it. Before they fence it. He says you should see things before they fence them. He says that's philosophy."

"He says that's how he gets an audience."

Ada flopped backward onto my bed, gap-toothed, triumphant, hair everywhere. "Are you coming?"

I looked at my book. I looked at her. I was fourteen and sensible and my mother had said *stay out of* three hours earlier, and I said the sentence I have rewritten ten thousand times since, in every tense there is:

"Somebody has to keep an eye on you."

She grinned like I'd given her a gift. Maybe I had. Maybe that was the last gift I ever gave her intact — a yes shaped like a duty. Or maybe it wouldn't have mattered what I said; maybe the day was already moving the way water moves, indifferent to the arrangements of children. I don't know. That's the honest ending to every version of this memory: I don't know, I don't know, I don't know.

What I know is the rice. The smoke note. The chip in the blue plate. My mother's shoulders shaking with laughter she sent at the wall. My father committing to his mistakes in pen. Chidi guarding plantain. Daniel filling in squares that weren't his. Ada on the counter, above the rules, allowed.

The last meal I ever tasted, and I ate it fast, the way children eat, the way you eat when you believe there are ten thousand meals ahead and no reason to slow down and memorize a single one.

That night I fell asleep to the sound of Ada humming through the wall — tuneless, insistent, the song she hummed when she was pleased with a plan — and the last thing I tasted, drifting off, was still there, faint and smoky at the back of my throat, the bottom of the pot, the best part, the part you have to scrape for.

I woke to Saturday. I woke to the last ordinary morning any of us would ever have.

I woke, and Ada was already dressed, and downstairs Daniel's voice was already in the kitchen, bright and early and welcome, and none of us knew — how could we know — that by Sunday evening the word *quarry* would stop being an argument and start being a wound, that my mother's spine would never shake with laughter again, that the blue pot would go into a box that stayed boxed, that silence was coming for our house like weather, and that it would arrive carrying paperwork.

But that was Saturday. On Saturday there was sun, and a dare, and a little sister pulling me by the wrist toward the door, saying *come on, come on, come on* — and I went.
