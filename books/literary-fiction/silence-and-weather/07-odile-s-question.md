## 7. Odile's Question

Odile Marchand had worked the day shift at the Fournier Street Library for eleven years, and in that time she had learned to read Astrid Yarrow the way he read spines: by their wear, by the softness of their corners, by what they refused to open onto.

He was unraveling. Not dramatically—Astrid did nothing dramatically, that was his whole architecture—but in the small ways that only someone who had watched him for a decade would notice. On Tuesday he shelved a returned copy of *The Shipping News* under geography, 910, and when she pulled it out and held it up without a word, he looked at it as though it were an object from another civilization, something excavated, its purpose forgotten. On Wednesday he called Mr. Okafor "Mr. Okada," and Mr. Okafor—who had been coming to the night hours for six years, who had once told Odile that Astrid was the only person in the city who never asked him why he couldn't sleep—had gone very still, and then very kind, and said, *That's all right, son,* in the voice people used for the grieving and the concussed.

And on Thursday, Odile found him standing in the biography stacks with one of her letters in his hand.

Not hers. His mother's. She knew that at once, the way you know weather through a wall—the particular hunch of him, the way he held the paper as if it might bruise. He'd taken to reading them down there, in the dead hours between two and four, when even the insomniacs thinned out and the building's old pipes ticked like a cooling engine. She wasn't supposed to see. He'd positioned himself with his back to the reading room, but the stacks on Fournier Street had been built in 1931 and the gaps between them were wide enough for regret.

She hadn't said anything. That had been three days ago, and since then the misfiling had gotten worse, and today he'd forgotten to log the newspaper deliveries, and when she'd come in at noon he'd still been at the desk, upright, blinking, wearing the same grey sweater as the day before, looking like a man who had been holding a door shut against something enormous and was losing the argument one finger at a time.

So: wine. Cheap wine, because the good stuff would make it an occasion, and occasions gave him somewhere to hide.

* * *

She waited until the last of the day patrons drifted out at six, then caught him in the staff room as he wound his scarf—that long ceremonial business of his, loop and tuck, armor assembled one layer at a time.

"Astrid."

"Mm." He didn't look up. The scarf required concentration, apparently. Eleven years of scarves.

"I'm staying late tonight. There's a crate of inventory in the basement that needs doing before the assessment, and I'd rather not do it alone." A lie so thin it was nearly transparent; the inventory had been done Monday. "I opened a bottle. It's terrible. Come have a glass before your shift."

His hands stopped on the scarf. She watched him run the calculation—she could almost see the abacus of him, the beads sliding: *refuse and explain refusing, or accept and control the terms.* Acceptance won, as it always did. Refusal required reasons, and reasons required content, and content was the thing he'd spent thirty years learning to live without.

"All right," he said. "One glass."

"One," she agreed, knowing already how the evening would go.

They sat in the reading room because the staff room felt too much like an interrogation cell, all fluorescent hum and noticeboards. The reading room at dusk was better: the tall windows going the color of weak tea, the lamps coming on in their slow amber sequence, the dust visible now in the light like the room was exhaling everything it had absorbed over ninety years. She poured the wine into two of the mugs from the staff kitchen because the good cups had been broken in some incident years before either of them arrived, and nobody remembered the incident, only the absence of cups.

"It's awful," he said, after the first sip. It was. Vinegar with ambitions.

"That's the point. If it were good we'd have to talk about the wine."

He almost smiled. She saw it arrive and get turned away at the door, the way his smiles always were—a smile designed to end conversations rather than begin them. She'd catalogued that smile years ago. There was the greeting smile, brief and closed like a shop shutter. There was the *I'm listening* smile, which meant he wanted you to stop talking. And there was the rarest one, the true one, which surfaced perhaps twice a year, usually over a book, usually unwatched, and which made him look suddenly, shockingly young—as if underneath all that practiced middle age there was a boy who had never been given permission to be delighted, and who delighted anyway, briefly, when no one was supposed to be looking.

She drank her terrible wine and waited. Waiting was her best skill. People thought patience was passive; Odile knew it was a kind of hunting.

* * *

Her own silence had lasted nineteen years, and it had started, as these things do, with a kindness.

Her sister Colette had written to her from Marseille in the spring of 2004—a letter Odile still had, in a drawer, in an envelope gone soft at the corners from handling—and told her that their father, dying, had asked for her. Not for Colette. For Odile, the younger one, the one who had left home at seventeen after the thing that happened in the boathouse, the thing neither of them had ever named aloud, the thing their mother had solved by simply never mentioning again, so thoroughly that Odile had sometimes wondered, in the decades after, whether it had happened at all or whether she'd invented it, the way you invent a dream you've told too many times.

Colette wrote: *He remembers. He told me. He wants to say sorry before it's too late.*

And Odile had read the letter at her kitchen table, and then she had put it in a drawer, and she had not gone.

She told herself it was mercy—his mercy, wanting to spare her the awkwardness of a deathbed apology, apologies being for the living really, and what good was sorry from a man who couldn't lift his own head? She told herself it was self-protection. She told herself, in the years after the funeral, when Colette stopped writing back, that the distance was mutual, natural, the ordinary drift of siblings. She became very skilled at these explanations. That was the thing about silence nobody warned you about: it didn't feel like a choice after a while. It felt like a fact. It fossilized. You built your whole life on top of it and called the shape of it your personality.

It was only last winter, sorting through Colette's things after the stroke that took half her words away—there was a great deal of taking-away in Odile's life lately, she noticed, libraries and sisters and words themselves—that she'd found Colette's diary, and read the entry from that spring, and learned that their father had asked every week. Every week, for five months. *Has she written? Has she come?* And died with the question still in his mouth.

Nineteen years of telling herself she'd been spared, and actually she had simply been late. Late past all forgiveness, past all use, past the point where arriving would have been anything but a monument to her own cowardice.

So she knew the look. She was looking at it right now, across two mugs of vinegar, at a man holding his silence around himself like the scarf he'd never finished winding.

* * *

"The assessment," he said, into the quiet. "When is it?"

"Three weeks. You know it's theater. They decided before they scheduled it."

"They'll close the night hours first."

"They'll close the night hours first," she agreed. "Then the local history collection. Then they'll sell the building to someone who'll put flats in it and keep the façade, because the façade is 'heritage.'" She turned her mug. "You're not sleeping."

"I sleep fine."

"Astrid." She let his name sit there between them, plain as a stone on a table.

He looked at the window instead of her. Outside, the city was doing its evening insomnia, lights coming on in office towers where people stayed not because there was work but because going home meant being alone with whatever followed them home. She'd read somewhere that the city's average sleep had dropped below five hours. Nobody slept anymore; they just took turns being awake near each other. Except him. He'd made an art of it.

"There's something," he said finally, and her heart did the foolish hopeful thing it had no business doing. "There are—" He stopped. Restarted, the way a stalled engine does, catching and failing. "My mother. After her stroke, I cleared the flat. There were things. In the wardrobe."

"Yes."

His eyes came up, startled. "You knew?"

"I saw you in the stacks. Tuesday of last week. Reading something you clearly didn't want anyone to see." She kept her voice level, unhurried. "I didn't say anything. It wasn't mine to ask about."

He exhaled slowly. She watched him decide—actually watched it happen, the way you watch weather change over water, a darkening, a shifting of pressure. He opened his mouth. Closed it. Opened it again.

"Odile, my mother—" And there it was, the whole ocean behind the word *mother*, pressing against a door he'd held shut for weeks, maybe months, maybe years. His hand moved toward the inner pocket of his jacket, where she'd seen him carry something folded, flat, rectangular. A letter. He carried one with him. She'd noticed that too; she noticed everything about him, which was its own confession, though not one she intended to make tonight, or possibly ever, or possibly in some other life where she was braver than she'd turned out to be.

His fingers touched the pocket. Stayed there.

"She hid something from me," he said. "Her whole life, she hid—"

The phone in the staff room rang. Of course it did. The universe had a sense of comic timing honed over millennia. He flinched like a man struck.

"I'll get it," she said, not moving. "Let it ring."

But the spell had broken; she could see that. Whatever had been rising in him had met the interruption and retreated, the way a tide goes out and takes something with it that you can't name until later. He picked up his mug and drained the wine like medicine.

"It's nothing," he said. "Family things. Old family things. I'm handling it."

"Handling it," she repeated. "Alone. At three in the morning, in the biography stacks."

"I work nights. Where else would I be at three in the morning?" A deflection so smooth it was almost elegant. Almost.

"What are you waiting for, Astrid?"

The question came out of her plainer than she'd planned. She'd meant to circle it, approach it sideways the way you approached most animals, but nineteen years of her own lateness stood up inside her and asked it directly, and there was no calling it back.

He went very still. Stillness was his native language; she'd seen him hold perfectly motionless while a patron cried at the desk, while Mr. Okafor rambled, while the world demanded responses he preferred not to give. But this stillness was different. This was the stillness of a man standing on ice and hearing it speak.

"Waiting for?" he said lightly. "Nothing. I'm not waiting for anything. I need to figure out the right way to—to approach it. Do it properly. There's a right order to these things. You can't just—"

"You can't just what? Tell the truth? Ask a question? Knock on a door?" She heard the edge in her own voice and didn't soften it. "You've been 'figuring out the right way' for three weeks. I've watched you misfile forty books and forget a man's name he's trusted you with for six years. Whatever's in those letters, it's eating you alive, and you're sitting here telling me there's a *right order*."

"You don't know what it is."

"No. I don't. Because you won't tell me." She set her mug down. "And I'm not asking you to tell me. I'm asking you what you're waiting for. Those aren't the same question."

For a moment—one long, suspended moment, the lamps humming, the tea-colored light dying out of the windows—he looked like he might answer. Like the door might actually open. She saw the loneliness in him, vast and unswept, a room no visitor had entered in thirty years, and she understood suddenly and completely that he had mistaken that emptiness for competence, for discipline, for strength, the way she had once mistaken her silence for mercy. Two people in a dying library, each fluent in the same dead language.

"My mother is dying," he said quietly. "That's what I'm waiting for. There's a letter I need to understand before—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening. "Before it's too late to ask her anything. So I'm reading. That's all. Reading."

It was almost the truth. She could hear the truth underneath it, bent slightly, the way light bends in water. He was waiting, yes. But not for understanding. Understanding was what he already had and couldn't bear. He was waiting for courage, or permission, or absolution in advance—some document that didn't exist, issued by no office, that would let him act without risk.

She knew, because she had waited for the same document for nineteen years, and it had never come, and the man in Marseille had died asking a weekly question into the air.

"Read faster," she said.

He laughed—a short, surprised sound, real, quickly suppressed. "That's your advice? Read faster?"

"It's the best advice I have." She stood, collected the mugs. "The rest of it—knocking on doors, saying the thing—you'll have to work out yourself. I'm not qualified. I kept a silence of my own for nineteen years and it cost me a sister and any chance of—" She stopped. He was watching her with those pale attentive eyes, and she found she could not finish the sentence, could not hand him her own box of letters to hold. Hypocrisy was a luxury for people with time. "Never mind. Old weather."

"Odile."

"Don't." She smiled at him, the shutter-smile, his own technique turned back on him, and saw him recognize it and almost flinch. "Go shelve your books. Mr. Okafor comes at ten. Try to remember his name."

* * *

She washed the mugs in the staff room sink and heard him move through the building ahead of her—the familiar sounds of his ritual, the lights checked, the returns cart wheeled to its station, the front doors unlocked at nine sharp for the people who had nowhere else. Through the doorway she watched him greet the first of them, the woman with the raincoat and the thermos, and give her the greeting smile, the closed one, and Odile thought: *he has no idea what he's carrying. Or worse—he knows exactly, and he thinks he can carry it alone forever, and he's wrong, and the finding-out will break him.*

She dried her hands. In her bag, in a soft envelope gone tender at the corners, was a letter of her own—Colette's last, unanswered, nineteen years old. She had brought it tonight without deciding to. Some part of her had known this evening would be about letters, about the weight of unsent things, and had armed her accordingly.

She didn't take it out. Not yet. But she moved it, that night, from the bottom of her bag to the outer pocket, where her hand could reach it.

And across the reading room, unaware, Astrid Yarrow settled into his chair beneath the lamp, drew a folded letter from his jacket, and began, once again, to read.
