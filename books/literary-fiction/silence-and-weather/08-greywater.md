## 8. Greywater

The bus let him off where the road gave up trying. Astrid stood with his bag at his feet and looked down a street that seemed to have been sketched by someone who lost interest halfway through: three finished houses, then scaffolding wrapped in green netting, then a foundation full of rainwater reflecting a sky the color of dishwater. A hand-painted sign at the corner read GREYWATER — POP. 1,204 (GIVE OR TAKE), and someone had crossed out the number and written *fewer* beneath it in red paint.

The fog came in off the water without hurry, the way a cat enters a room it already owns.

He had rehearsed this. On the train, in the ferry terminal, during the two-hour bus ride past fields that ran out of topsoil and into gorse — he had rehearsed everything. He knew Tomas Vey's address from a letter dated eleven years ago; he knew from the harbor master's office, phoned under a false pretext about boat surveys, that a man of that name still worked the north slipway. He had prepared sentences. He had prepared a cover story involving a job lead at a marine surveyor's office in the county seat, which was even true, in the way that a seed is true of a tree. What he had not prepared for was the smell: salt and diesel and something vegetal, kelp rotting sweetly at the tide line, a smell so specific it seemed to reach into his chest and rearrange things.

He picked up his bag and walked toward the water.

* * *

The boarding house was called the Meridian, which struck him as optimistic for a building whose porch listed visibly to starboard. The woman who kept it — wide-hipped, iron-haired, introduced only as Mrs. Callas — asked no questions beyond how long he'd be staying, and when he said *a week or so* she wrote it in pencil, which he took to be the local attitude toward all commitments.

"Here for work?" she asked, handing him a key on a wooden fob.

"Possibly." He smiled the smile that ended conversations. It didn't end this one.

"You'll want the chowder place, then, before it closes at six. And don't swim off the point. Currents." She turned back to her crossword as though he were already furniture, and Astrid felt the familiar relief of being successfully overlooked — except that here, in this town of four streets and one harbor, invisibility would require more craft than usual. Everyone was visible here. That appeared to be the entire social contract.

His room faced the water. He unpacked with the methodical care of a man arranging alibis: clothes in the narrow wardrobe, the shoebox of letters on the desk, squared to its edges. He did not open it. He had read every letter enough times to recite passages in the dark — his mother's slanted hand loosening over the years, the ink changing from blue-black to ballpoint gray, the addressee always, always *T.* — but he kept the box near him anyway, the way other men kept cigarettes.

Through the window, the sea lay flat and pewter-colored, undecided. Twice before dark it seemed to make up its mind toward roughness, and twice thought better of it.

* * *

He saw Tomas the next morning, and the seeing knocked something loose in him that he spent the rest of the day pretending was still attached.

Astrid had told himself he would take a walk, merely a walk, along the harbor road, coffee in hand, a man with nowhere particular to be. But his feet knew. They carried him past the fish sheds and the rusted crane and around the curve of the seawall to the north slipway, and there he was.

Tomas Vey was kneeling in the belly of an upturned dinghy, sanding its hull with long even strokes, and he was laughing.

That was the thing Astrid could not have prepared for. He had imagined the meeting a hundred ways — the stammered introduction, the studied casualness, the moment of recognition or its failure — but he had never once imagined laughter. Two fishermen stood nearby arguing about engine parts, and Tomas was laughing at something one of them had said, a big unguarded laugh that opened his whole face, and Astrid stood thirty meters away behind a stack of crab pots and felt like a man watching through glass.

They had the same hands. That was the first thing. Their mother's hands — broad palms, long fingers, the same slight crook in the right index finger that Astrid had always assumed was his alone, some private flaw of his own manufacture. Tomas pushed back his hood and Astrid saw his own hairline, his own jaw, aged differently, weathered differently, worn by a different life into a face that was nonetheless unmistakably, appallingly familiar. A stranger walking past would have taken them for brothers. A stranger would have been right.

Tomas said something to the fishermen, clapped one on the shoulder, and went back to his sanding, humming.

Astrid drank his coffee standing up until it went cold, then walked back to the Meridian by the longest route he could find, which was not long, because Greywater did not have long routes. Everything here was short and close together. There was nowhere to hide, and he had built his whole life out of hiding places.

* * *

The days arranged themselves into surveillance disguised as routine.

He learned the town the way he had learned the library stacks — systematically, obsessively, in the dead hours. He learned that the bakery opened at seven and sold out of the good bread by nine. He learned that the fog burned off most mornings around ten and crept back by four, that the town lived inside these margins like a man living between migraines. He learned that half-finished buildings were not a sign of decline here but a kind of architecture: the Masonic hall roofed but unglazed for eleven years, a house on Chapel Lane framed and waiting, the concrete bones of a promised marina development standing in the shallows like a whale skeleton. People started things in Greywater. Finishing them was another matter. The sea couldn't decide its mood and neither, apparently, could anyone else.

And he learned Tomas. Not by speaking to him — God, no, not yet — but by orbiting. Tomas worked the slipways mornings and took the afternoon ferry run twice a week to the island across the sound. He ate lunch at the harbor café at the corner table, always facing the door. He knew everyone. The greengrocer saved him bruised apples for whatever he fed the gulls; children waved at him from the sea wall and he waved back with his whole arm; an old woman stopped him outside the church and held both his hands while she talked, and he bent to hear her, patient, unhurried, easy.

Everything Astrid wasn't.

It wasn't self-pity, exactly. Astrid had made peace long ago with what he was: a man of small precise movements, a keeper of quiet, invisible by trade and temperament. Thirty years of night shifts had polished him like a stone in a stream. But watching Tomas laugh with strangers, watching him touch people — shoulders, hands, the tops of children's heads — Astrid felt the full cost accounting arrive at last, itemized. This was what their mother's arithmetic had bought. One son given ease with the world, given the unguarded face, given the belief that reaching out was safe. And one son given the library at 2 a.m., given the smile that ended conversations, given thirty years of practice at being overlooked because somewhere in his blood was the knowledge, older than memory, that love was a thing you could be subtracted from.

He sat on the sea wall that evening with the letters box on his knees and did not open it, and the fog came in and erased the harbor, and he let it erase him too.

* * *

On the fourth day it rained sideways and Tomas worked anyway, and Astrid watched from the café window with a book he wasn't reading.

The café was warm and smelled of fryer oil and cinnamon. He had begun taking the corner table himself, the one with sightlines to the slipway, and had constructed a plausible inner life for himself should anyone ask: marine surveyor, between contracts, evaluating the coast. Nobody asked. In Greywater you were allowed your silences; they simply assumed you'd get around to talking eventually.

Tomas came in at noon, dripping, and ordered without looking at the menu, and the woman behind the counter teased him about something and he teased her back, and then — Astrid's heart performed a maneuver it had not cleared with him first — Tomas scanned the room, found the only occupied table, and smiled at him. Not a polite nod. A smile, direct and warm, the smile of a man who had never once been taught to expect nothing.

Astrid raised his cup an inch. His hand was steady. He had trained it to be.

Tomas collected his takeaway bag and paused beside the table on his way out, rain running off his oilskins onto the floorboards. "You're the surveyor fellow," he said. "Mrs. Callas's lot. How's the coast treating you?"

"It's," Astrid said, and heard his own voice come out level, mild, the voice he used for patrons asking where the toilets were, "very undecided."

Tomas laughed — that laugh again, up close now, and Astrid could see the exact geography of it, the lines at the eyes, the way it crinkled rather than split him. "Give it a week. It'll commit to nothing, but it'll commit to nothing beautifully." He shifted the bag to his other hand. "You'll want to see it from the water anyway, if the job's real. Everything looks honest from the water."

"I'll keep that in mind."

"Tomas," he said, and stuck out his hand.

Every letter in the shoebox was addressed to that name. Every unsent word their mother had ever hoarded ended in those five letters, and here it was offered freely, casually, in a wet handshake, to the brother who had gotten everything including the name.

"Astrid," he said. "Yarrow."

"Good Norse name, that. Means divine strength, my grandmother used to say, though she said it about everything." Tomas grinned. "Well. If the fog lifts, I'm the fool working the north slip. Mind the currents."

And he was gone into the horizontal rain, whistling, and Astrid sat with his untouched book and his cooling tea and understood, with the clarity of a man stepping off a ledge, that he was in terrible trouble. Because Tomas was kind. Because Tomas was easy. Because the handshake had lasted two seconds and Astrid's hand still remembered it, and because tomorrow morning some traitorous part of him was going to walk back down to the north slipway not to watch this time but to speak, to stay, to belong — and every hour of belonging would be drawn against an account that was about to be called due.

Outside, the sea considered storming, reconsidered, and settled for sullenness.

Astrid walked back to the Meridian through the rain he barely felt, and in his room he finally opened the shoebox, and took out the last letter — the one dated three weeks ago, the one addressed not to *T.* but to *my sons*, plural, the one he had not yet been able to finish reading — and set it on the desk like a loaded thing.

Tomorrow, he told himself. Tomorrow he would decide what kind of man to be.

But he already knew he would go down to the slipway. He could feel it in the crook of his right index finger, that inherited flaw, flexing toward the door.
