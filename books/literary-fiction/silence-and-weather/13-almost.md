## 13. Almost

The storm had been building for two days before it arrived, the way weather comes to Greywater—not suddenly, but with a long deliberation, as though the sky were composing a letter it wasn't sure it wanted to send.

Astrid felt it in the harbor first. The water changed color, going from its usual indecisive grey to something darker, greener, with a metallic sheen like the back of a spoon. The gulls went quiet, then loud, then quiet again. And Tomas, mending a net on the dock outside the boatshed, kept glancing southwest without appearing to, the way a man checks a door he expects someone to come through.

"Barometer's falling like a stone," Tomas said that morning, tapping the brass instrument bolted beside the shed door. He said it with something close to happiness. Astrid had learned that about him in the week they'd known each other—the week that was now stretching toward two, held up by Astrid's invented job lead, his invented bad knee, his invented willingness to stay just one more day. Tomas loved weather. Not the fair kind; the kind that arrived. He talked about storms the way other men talked about old friends who might finally visit.

"You're looking forward to it," Astrid said.

"Been a dry autumn." Tomas pulled a knot tight with his teeth and spat out the twine end. "The town could use a proper blow. Clears the sinuses. Clears the air." He grinned, and Astrid looked away, because the grin was the same grin he'd seen in his own face, once, in a photograph of himself at twenty that he'd never understood until now. He'd always thought he looked like no one. It turned out he looked like Tomas Vey, and Tomas Vey looked like a man mending nets in a doorway, easy in his own hands.

Astrid's knee—real enough now, he'd actually twisted it hauling a dinghy up the shingle two days ago—had become the excuse that excused everything. He'd told himself he would stay three days. Then that the ferry schedule made four sensible. Then that Tomas had asked him to help tar the *Merrow's* hull before the weather turned, and you couldn't just leave a man alone with a job like that. Each extension had been a small forgery, and he was growing expert at them, and that expertise frightened him more than the lie underneath it did.

Because underneath it was the box. He'd brought it with him—of course he'd brought it, he could no more have left it in his flat than left his own lungs there. It sat in his rented room above the chandlery, under the bed, wrapped in the sweater he didn't wear. Some nights he took it out and held it without opening it, the way you'd hold a sleeping animal you were afraid of waking.

*Tell him,* said a voice that sounded like Odile, that had been sounding like Odile for days now, patient and merciless. *Tell him today. Tell him before the storm. Tell him—*

"Not yet," Astrid said aloud, to the empty boatshed, and heard how it sounded: like a man negotiating with a tide.

* * *

The storm came at dusk, and it came the way Tomas had promised: properly.

Astrid was at the harbor café when the lights went—first a brown flicker, then the whole street surrendering at once, the fishing boats' mast lights vanishing, the chandlery sign going dark mid-letter. The café did not panic. That was the thing about Greywater that Astrid was only beginning to understand: it had been half-abandoned, half-built, half-finished for so long that losing power was not an emergency but a genre of evening. The woman behind the counter lit three storm lanterns that had clearly been waiting all their lives for this. Someone produced a guitar. The rain hit the windows in fistfuls.

Tomas arrived twenty minutes in, soaked through, shaking water off like a dog, and dropped into the chair across from Astrid as though the chair had been assigned to him years ago.

"Power's out up the whole headland," he reported, delighted. "Old Mrs. Callum's tree came down across the road. Nobody hurt. The *Merrow's* holding—she'll hold. She's held worse than this." He wrung out his sleeve over the floor without apology. "You walked here in this?"

"I was already here."

"You were already here." Tomas said it the way he said most things, as if it were interesting, as if Astrid were interesting, and Astrid felt the familiar intoxication rise in him like heat—that vertiginous pleasure of being looked at by someone whose attention he had spent thirty years avoiding in everyone else. Being seen by Tomas was different. Being seen by Tomas was like being seen in a mirror that had opinions.

They stayed while the café emptied around them. The guitar player gave up. The counter woman, closing early, told them to keep the lantern and lock up when they went, and then it was just the two of them in a cone of yellow light with the storm pressing at the glass like something that wanted in, or wanted them out, or wanted, simply, to be witnessed.

"Come back to the shed," Tomas said. "There's a stove. Better than walking you up to the chandlery in this. You can wait out the worst."

Astrid should have said no. He catalogued, distantly, all the reasons he should have said no, and then heard himself say yes, and watched himself gather his coat, and understood that he had passed, some days ago, beyond the country where his choices were still his own.

* * *

The boatshed in lantern light was a cathedral of tar and rope and salt. Rain hammered the corrugated roof into a single vast drumbeat, and inside it, the world contracted to the stove's small orange heart, two upturned crates for seats, a bottle of rough cider Tomas kept for emergencies, and the smell of oakum and hot metal that Astrid was startled to realize he already loved, already thought of, absurdly, as *the smell of my brother's workplace*.

They talked. That was the other thing: with Tomas, talking was not the performance it was everywhere else in Astrid's life. He did not have to construct sentences in advance, test them for exits. Tomas asked questions and Astrid answered them, and the answers kept being true, and each true answer was a small miracle and a small theft, because every truth he gave was a truth that concealed the largest one.

"Your mother," Tomas said at some point, refilling the cider. "You've said she was a librarian too."

"Assistant. She never finished the training. She read everything, though. She read like other people breathe." Astrid heard his own voice go careful, the way it did around her. "She had a stroke in the spring. She's in a home now. She doesn't—she isn't—"

"You don't have to explain."

"She kept things," Astrid said, and stopped, and the storm filled the silence for him, and he let it, coward that he was, and Tomas nodded slowly, as if *kept things* were a phrase he understood from the inside.

"I had a brother once," Tomas said.

The lantern flame leaned in the draft. Astrid's heart performed a single enormous act of violence against his ribs and then, incredibly, resumed.

"Tell me," Astrid said. His voice came out level. Thirty years of practice will do that.

"Not much to tell. He was older. He died when I was small—I don't even remember it, I remember the funeral, the coat I wore, the taste of the sandwiches. My mother never spoke of him after. Not once. It was like—" Tomas turned the bottle in his hands, watching the lantern light swim in the glass. "It was like there'd been a room in the house that got bricked over. You could tell from the outside the house was bigger than the inside. But nobody would say what was behind the wall."

Astrid looked at his brother's face in the stove light and felt the truth rise in him like floodwater, like the sea coming over the mudflats, patient, total, unstoppable. This was the moment. This was the door, standing open, storm-lit, with Tomas standing in it, offering him the exact shape of his own wound to lay beside.

"Tomas," Astrid said.

"Yeah?"

The rain. The drumbeat roof. The cider sour and honest in his mouth. *I am your brother. She gave me her name and kept you a secret and kept herself a secret from us both. I found a box of letters behind a wardrobe and every one of them is addressed to you. I have known for months. I came here on purpose. I let you call me friend.*

"Tomas," Astrid said again, and then, "I should go."

Tomas blinked. "In this?"

"The chandlery's five minutes. I've walked in worse."

"You said your knee—"

"My knee's fine." It wasn't; it flared the moment he stood, a bright wire of pain, and he was almost grateful for it, almost grateful for any punishment that was simple and deserved. "Thank you for the stove. For the—" He gestured at everything: the shed, the storm, the week, the whole improbable architecture of belonging he had built out of fraud. "For all of it."

Tomas stood too. For a moment they were close, closer than they'd ever stood, close enough that Astrid could see the small scar through his brother's left eyebrow and know, with a certainty that arrived like the rain, that he had the same scar, in the same place, from falling off the same kind of wall at the same age, because they were the same blood, the same face, the same mother, and he was leaving, he was actually leaving, he was going to walk out into the storm with the truth still in his mouth like a stone he couldn't swallow and couldn't spit.

"Astrid." Tomas said his name strangely, testing it, the way you test a knot you suspect is tied wrong. "Whatever it is you're not saying."

Astrid looked at him. And then—because he could not say it, because the words had gone to pieces in his throat, because thirty years of silence is a muscle and his had become enormous—he did the only other thing his body knew how to do with a moment this large.

He put his hand briefly, hard, on the back of Tomas's neck, the way you'd hold a brother, the way you'd hold the only person left who shared your blood, and he said, "I'm sorry," which meant nothing and everything, and then he stepped out into the rain.

The storm took him like a verdict. It was warm rain, violent, vertical, the kind that comes down like a decision. He walked the five minutes to the chandlery and it took him forty, and by the time he climbed the stairs his knee had stopped reporting pain and started simply burning, and he stood dripping in the rented room with the box under the bed, and he did not take it out. He lay down on the narrow bed in his wet clothes and stared at the ceiling and listened to the storm work the town over, and somewhere in the small hours he understood, with the clarity that only comes at the worst hour, what he had done: he had kissed the moment goodbye. He had held his brother's neck and said *I'm sorry* and walked away, and now the moment was out there in the weather somewhere, drowned, unrecoverable, and there would not be another one like it. There would only be harder ones, later ones, ones that cost more.

He slept, eventually, the way the guilty sleep—suddenly, heavily, like a man falling.

* * *

Tomas stood in the doorway of the boatshed for a long time after the stranger disappeared into the rain, the lantern guttering behind him, watching the place where the road should have been and wasn't.

*Whatever it is you're not saying.*

He didn't know why he'd said it. It had come out of him the way the storm had come—days of pressure finding the weak place. But he'd seen it, in the stove light, in the second before Astrid stood up: something move behind the man's face like a fish under ice. He'd seen it before, too. Little things, over two weeks. The way Astrid watched him work, not curious but *famished*. The way he'd gone white, one afternoon at the harbor café, when old Mrs. Callum had asked if the two of them were brothers—"you have the same way of standing," she'd said, meaning nothing, and Astrid had laughed and dropped his cup, laughed too loud, the sound of a man stepping off a curb he didn't know was there. The way he never talked about his family beyond the mother, the stroke, the wall of *she kept things*. The way his eyes had gone, tonight, when Tomas mentioned the brother who died—wait. Hadn't that been it? The flinch. The stillness. The look of a man hearing a name he knew.

Tomas banked the stove and sat back down on the crate and did not sleep.

It was ridiculous. The man was a librarian from the city, a soft-handed, careful, wounded creature who flinched at raised voices and thanked you twice for everything. What could he possibly—except that Tomas had spent his whole life in a town that read weather, and he knew what pressure looked like before the front arrived, and Astrid Yarrow had been a falling barometer for two weeks, and tonight the needle had gone through the bottom of the dial.

*He was older. He died when I was small.*

Tomas turned that over. He had said it a hundred times in his life, to a hundred people, and it had always been smooth as sea glass, worn down by telling. But tonight, saying it to Astrid, he had felt the old grief shift in its bed like a stone in a storm, and he had heard his own story come out sounding rehearsed—and he had seen Astrid hear it that way too.

He got up, restless, and stood at the shed's open door with the rain coming down in the dark like the end of the world, and he thought about the way Astrid had gripped his neck. Not like a friend. He knew how friends touched you. That had been something older, something with a desperate, formal tenderness to it, like a man saying goodbye to a house he'd lived in before he could remember.

*Whatever it is you're not saying.*

In the morning the storm had blown itself out to a grey, rinsed, glittering calm, and the ferry was running, and Astrid Yarrow was gone—left before light, the chandlery woman said, paid his bill in cash, said something about his mother, about the city, about needing to go. He'd left the lantern on the café's table, wiped clean. He'd left no address. He'd left, Tomas thought, standing on the dock with the whole washed sky overhead, exactly the way a man leaves when he's stolen something and can't look at what he took.

Tomas looked out at the water for a long time. Then he went and got the *Merrow's* logbook, because that was the kind of man he was—everything written down, everything kept—and on the flyleaf, under the years of tide tables and fuel counts, he wrote: *Astrid Yarrow. Librarian. City. Knows something.*

And under that, after a while, because the pen wanted to and he let it: *Looks like me.*
