## 17. Confession at Low Tide

The bus into Greywater smelled of diesel and wet wool, and Astrid watched the fog take the town in pieces as they descended: first the cranes, then the chapel roof, then the long grey spine of the harbor wall, then everything else, dissolved. It was the last week of September and the sea had gone strange and glassy, holding its breath, as if it too were waiting for something to be said.

He had rehearsed the confession on the journey—on the platform at the interchange, on the second bus, walking the coast road from the stop with his bag cutting into his shoulder. He had rehearsed it in the shower of his rented room, in the pauses between waves. He had rehearsed it so many times that the words had worn smooth as sea glass, and he knew that this was the danger: that he could make even the truth sound like a performance, polished and painless, something a man could hear and nod at and walk away from unchanged.

So he had decided, finally, to stop rehearsing. He would say it badly. He would say it the way it had actually happened, in all its cowardice and its order, and let it be ugly. His mother had asked him to find Tomas. The last letter had asked it of both of them, in her careful, failing hand: *find each other, and forgive neither nor both.* He had found him. He had simply never told him what he'd found.

* * *

Tomas was on the mudflats.

Astrid saw him from the sea wall, a dark figure bent over one of the beached hulls, scraping barnacles from the strakes with a tool that caught the low light. The tide was far out—further than Astrid had ever seen it, the sea withdrawn like a held breath, exposing the whole grey anatomy of the bay: channels and pools, the ribs of an old wreck, mud that shone like hammered pewter. Gulls stood in the shallows with their shoulders up, offended by the weather or the light or the world in general.

He climbed down the iron ladder at the wall's end and walked out onto the flats.

The mud took his boots to the ankle with a sound like slow applause. Each step was a small negotiation, a sucking and releasing, and he understood, with a clarity that arrived too late to be useful, that the flats were the one place in Greywater from which you could not walk away quickly. Whatever he said here, he would have to stand in it. There was no elegant retreat. The town had arranged the geography of his confession without knowing it.

Tomas heard him before he saw him—of course he did; the man had eyes in the back of his weather—and straightened, shading his gaze with one hand. He was wearing oilskins gone soft with salt, and his hair was pushed back off his forehead, and he was smiling before he had fully registered who it was.

"Astrid." The name, easy in his mouth now, after three days in August and one aborted evening in the storm. "You came back. I thought—" He stopped, and Astrid watched him decide not to ask what he thought. "The knee better?"

"The knee was a lie," Astrid said.

Tomas's smile held, but something behind it shifted, the way light shifts on water when a cloud moves that you can't see. "The job lead?"

"Also a lie." His heart was going like a fist on a door. "The bad knee, the job lead, the first time we met at the café—I told you I was passing through, looking at the coast. That was a lie too. I came here for you. I knew your name before I ever saw your face."

The gulls made a sound like torn paper. Somewhere out in the channel, water moved with a low, liquid chuckle, the tide beginning its long return.

Tomas set the scraper down on the gunwale of the hull, carefully, the way a man sets down a tool when his hands have become untrustworthy. "All right," he said. "I'm listening."

And so Astrid told him. Badly, as he had promised himself. Standing in the mud with the tide miles out and the light going the color of old tin, he told him about the wardrobe panel, the shoebox, the letters addressed to *T.* He told him about the night library and the hours nobody wanted, and how he had read those letters at three in the morning among strangers who couldn't sleep, and how the ground of his whole life had shifted under him—the brother, born when their mother was nineteen, given away under a father's arithmetic and a town's silence. He told him about the closure notice, the four months, the way the library had been the only place the letters felt safe. He told him about Odile, and the wine, and the question she'd asked—*what are you waiting for?*—and the lie he'd answered with.

"And then," Astrid said, and here his voice did fail him, and he let it, "I found your name. Tomas Vey. Greywater. And I researched you for six weeks like a coward prepares a speech, and then I came here, and I sat in the harbor café, and I let you be kind to me. I let you show me the boats. I let you buy the coffee. I let you tell me about your foster mother, and the winter you broke your wrist, and the way the fog comes in off the water in October like something living. I let you give me three days of—" he stopped, swallowed. "I let you be my brother without telling you that's what you were. Because I wanted it. Because it was the first time in my life I'd ever belonged anywhere, and I knew that the moment I told you the truth, you'd have the right to take it away. So I took it from you instead. I decided for you. The way everyone decided things for you before you could decide them for yourself. I did the same thing. I'm the last in a long line."

The flats were silent except for the small sounds of the tide turning, far off. Tomas had not moved. He stood with his hands at his sides, oilskins dark with spray, and his face—Astrid's face, older and more lived-in, a face that had been out in weather Astrid had never stood in—was doing something complicated and quiet, like ice forming on a pond: not breaking, but changing state.

"You knew," Tomas said at last. His voice was level. That was the worst of it—the levelness. "In the café. You sat across from me and you knew, and I told you about my life, and you—" He stopped. He looked out at the water, at the wreck-ribs standing black in the mud, and Astrid watched him do the arithmetic, the same arithmetic their mother had done fifty years ago, the arithmetic that decided which child was survivable. "You let me hand you my whole self, piece by piece, like a man handing his coat to a stranger, and you knew."

"Yes."

"You could have told me the second day. The first day. You could have told me at the café and given me the choice."

"Yes."

Tomas nodded slowly. He picked up the scraper, looked at it, put it down again. When he spoke again, his voice was colder and more careful, each word set down like a stone in a wall. "Do you know what I did, after you left? After the storm, when you vanished? I thought I'd frightened you off. I went over every conversation we had, looking for what I'd said wrong. I actually apologized—to a man who wasn't there—for something I hadn't done. That's what your silence did. It made me stand in the rain apologizing to a lie."

"I know."

"No," Tomas said, "you don't. You know what you did. You don't know what it cost." He crouched suddenly and picked up a stone from the mud, flat and grey, and held it. "Our mother," he said. "You've read her letters. You know her reasons. Poverty, a father gone, the arithmetic. I've had fifty years to build my own version, you know. My version had a mother who couldn't keep me. Mine was kind. Mine had her young and alone and drowning. And now you come and tell me she wrote letters for fifty years and never sent one. Never *one*, Astrid. She had time. She had an address, eventually—someone found me, the records exist, you found me in six weeks of cowardly research. She had fifty years and a stamp's worth of courage, and she spent it on a wardrobe panel."

"She was afraid," Astrid said. "I think she was afraid her whole life that—"

"Don't," Tomas said, and the word cracked, just once, the ice finally showing its fault line. "Don't defend her to me yet. I'm allowed to be angry first. I've earned the anger. I've been earning it since 1974." He threw the stone, not at Astrid, but out toward the water, and it skipped once, twice, and sank. "And you. You I don't even know what to do with. You're the replacement child who got the mother and the letters and thirty years of being loved, and you came here and stole the one thing I had left, which was the not-knowing. Do you understand that? The not-knowing was the last clean thing I owned. You dirtied it. You sat in that café and you dirtied it, and you called it kindness."

"I called it nothing," Astrid said. "I didn't call it anything. That was the whole problem. I've spent my whole life not calling things anything. I shelved books at night for thirty years and perfected being overlooked, and I thought that was competence, and it was just fear with a routine." The mud held his boots; the tide was coming in behind him, filling the channels, and he could hear it, patient and total. "I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm not asking you to forgive her. I'm asking you to know. That's all I have. I spent three days being your brother in secret, and it was the happiest I've ever been, and it was theft, and I'm here to return it. All of it. The name, the letters, the mother, the face. It's yours. Do what you want with it. Hate it, if that's the honest thing. I'd rather be hated by you honestly than loved by you on false pretenses. I've had enough of false pretenses to last two lifetimes, and I only got one."

Tomas looked at him for a long time. The light was failing; the fog was coming in off the water in exactly the way he'd once described it to Astrid in the café, like something living, and it began to take the harbor wall, the boats, the far shore, in pieces.

"You look like her," Tomas said finally. It came out almost as an accusation. "Around the eyes. I kept thinking, in the café—there's something familiar about you, and I couldn't place it, and it was *her*. I was looking at our mother's eyes and I didn't know it. Fifty years, and the first family I get is a lie with her face on it."

"Tomas—"

"No." He raised a hand, not unkindly, but finally, like a man closing a ledger. "I need you to go. Not forever. I need you to go and let me be angry properly, without an audience, because if you stand there looking like her I'm going to say things I mean less. Go back to your library. Go read your letters. I'll—" He stopped. He looked out at the incoming tide, and his voice, when it came again, was quieter, and colder, and more careful than ever. "I'll find you when I know what I feel. Not before. Don't call. Don't write. Don't send Odile. Don't send anyone."

Astrid stood in the mud a moment longer, the tide lapping at his boots now, cold through the leather. He wanted—desperately, childishly—to say something that would fix it, and he knew there was no such sentence, and so he did the hardest thing his life had taught him, which was to leave a silence where he wanted to put words.

"All right," he said.

He turned and walked back across the flats, each step sucking and releasing, and behind him he heard nothing—no movement, no farewell, only the tide coming in over the place where his brother stood, patient and total, filling the channels one by one.

* * *

Tomas stayed on the flats until the water reached his knees.

He wasn't sure why. There was work he could have done—the hull wouldn't scrape itself—but his body had decided against work, against movement, against everything except standing in the cold shallows while the fog came in and the light died and the town's lamps came on one by one along the sea wall, small and yellow and impossibly far away.

*Brother.*

The word sat in him like a swallowed stone. He turned it over. He tried it against everything he knew, everything he had been told, everything he had constructed in fifty years of not-knowing: the foster mother with her kind, tired hands; the file with its redactions; the one photograph of a young woman he'd been allowed to see, holding a baby that might have been him or might have been no one, the caption lost. All of it rearranged itself around the stone. He was not, it turned out, an only child of no one. He was the older brother of a night librarian from the city, a pale, careful man who looked like their mother around the eyes and who had sat in a harbor café letting himself be loved on false pretenses.

He should have hated it more cleanly. That was what frightened him. The anger was there—he could feel it, cold and careful, an engine turning over—but underneath it, shamefully, stubbornly, was the other thing. The three days in August. The easy rhythm of work and talk, the man who listened like listening was a trade he'd spent a lifetime perfecting, the strange happiness Tomas had felt and hadn't been able to name. It had been real. That was the obscenity of it. The lie had been real. The brotherhood had been real before either of them knew its name, and now it had a name, and the name was a wound.

The water reached his thighs. He turned, finally, and waded back toward the shore, and the mud gave him up reluctantly, step by step, the way it gave everyone up, and he stood at the foot of the iron ladder dripping and shivering and looking out at the dark water that had taken the flats back entirely, as if the whole conversation had never happened, as if the mud had swallowed it the way it swallowed everything—except that it had happened. It was in him now. The stone was in him.

He thought about the letters, sitting in a shoebox in a wardrobe in a city flat. Fifty years of a woman's hand, addressed to *T.*, never sent. He hated them and he wanted them with the same breath. He wanted to burn them and he wanted to read every word twice. He wanted to ask her why and knew she was beyond asking, and hated that the only person left who could answer was the man walking away up the coast road with her eyes.

He climbed the ladder. His hands were shaking—not from the cold, or not only from the cold.

At the top of the sea wall he stopped. Below him the water was black and complete. Behind him the town smelled of woodsmoke and frying fish, ordinary, indifferent. And ahead of him, up the coast road, a figure was just visible in the last of the light, walking away with a bag over his shoulder, unhurried, not looking back—walking the way a man walks when he has finally said the thing he came to say and is prepared to live in the silence after it.

Tomas stood there a long time, dripping on the stone, the anger and the wanting turning in him like the tide.

Then he took out his phone, and looked at it, and put it away, and did not call.

But he did not delete the number, either. He stood in the dark a moment longer, memorizing it instead—seven digits, like a prayer he resented having to learn—and then he went home to a house that smelled of sawdust and salt, and sat down at his kitchen table with a pen and a sheet of paper, and discovered that his hands, which could mend any hull on that coast, did not know how to begin.

The tide, meanwhile, came all the way in, and covered the mudflats, and waited, as it always did, for the moon to tell it what to do next.
