## 18. Anger Is Also a Kind of Family

The mudflats at low tide smelled of iron and old salt, and Astrid learned to read them the way he had once learned the Dewey Decimal System: patiently, alone, in the hours nobody wanted.

He had meant to leave Greywater. He had bought the ticket, sat with it on the table of his rented room above the chandlery, and watched it sit there like a confession he couldn't finish writing. The bus came at six-forty in the morning, its headlights cutting through fog the color of dishwater, and he heard it pass below his window on the third morning and did not go down to meet it. On the fourth morning he didn't hear it at all, because he was already awake, standing at the glass, watching the harbor lights blur and burn through the mist, asking himself the question that would not resolve: *What exactly are you waiting for? What could possibly arrive?*

Nothing would arrive. He understood this. Tomas had said what he needed to say on the flats—*You don't get to be my brother by announcement*—and then walked away across the wet sand with his boots leaving prints that filled slowly with seawater, each one erasing itself behind him, as if the tide itself were helping Astrid forget. But forgetting was the one thing Astrid refused to do anymore. He had spent thirty years perfecting it. He was done being good at it.

So he stayed. And because a man cannot stand on a mudflat forever, he found work.

It was Petra Halvorsen who gave it to him, though she never quite admitted she had. Petra ran the fish shed at the east quay—sixty years old, forearms like mooring rope, a voice that carried over engines without effort. She had been there the day of the confession; everyone in Greywater had either been there or heard about it within the hour, because a town of fog is also a town of ears. She had seen the tall stranger from the city kneel in the muck and say something to Tomas Vey that made Tomas's face go white and then gray and then nothing at all. She had seen Tomas walk away. She had seen the stranger stay kneeling long after the tide began to turn, until the water reached his knees and he still didn't move, and finally she'd waded out herself—not gently—and hauled him up by the collar like a drowned kitten.

"You'll want work," she said, wringing out her sleeve. "Men who kneel in tides all day are men thinking about drowning, and I don't hire drowners."

"I'm not—" Astrid started.

"Six o'clock tomorrow. Bring gloves." She turned away, then stopped. "And I don't want speeches. You want to talk about whatever that was, you talk to him, not me. My job is fish."

He showed up at six. She put him on the sorting table, then on the winch, then on the boats themselves when her regular hand broke his wrist falling off a ladder. He gutted herring until his dreams smelled of brine. He learned the knots—the bowline first, then the cleat hitch, then the rolling hitch that holds under strain and can be loosened while still bearing weight, which Petra called "the only honest knot, because it admits it might need to let go." His hands, soft from thirty years of paper, cracked and blistered and then calloused into something new. At night he held them up under the bare bulb of his room and examined them like documents written in a language he was only beginning to translate.

He did not see Tomas. That was the arrangement, though no one had spoken it aloud. Tomas worked the north quay, mending boats, and Astrid worked the east, and the harbor was small enough that they orbited each other daily without collision—a shared universe held together by gravity neither would acknowledge. Astrid saw him always at a distance: the set of his shoulders over a hull, the way he laughed with the ferryman, the particular tilt of his head when he listened to someone, which was—Astrid discovered with a jolt that arrived fresh every time, like cold water—the same tilt their mother had. The same tilt Astrid caught sometimes in his own reflection and had never known where it came from.

They spoke through intermediaries. This was how it went:

Petra told Astrid that Tomas had fixed the *Maren* 's engine, and Astrid nodded, and later the ferryman mentioned to Tomas that the new fellow on the east quay had spotted rot in the *Maren* 's stern post before anyone else, and Tomas grunted and said the fellow knew boats for a city man, and the ferryman carried that back across the water like pollen. It was absurd. It was a conversation conducted entirely through other people's mouths, a letter chain with living links. Astrid recognized the shape of it immediately—he had grown up inside such a shape, a household where everything important traveled by indirection—and he hated it and clung to it in equal measure, because it was, at least, a connection. Anger, he was learning, was also a kind of family. It required two people. It required history. Tomas could have left Greywater; instead he stayed on the north quay, visible, furious, present. Astrid chose to understand this as a door left open exactly one inch, and he resolved never to push it, only to keep breathing against the gap so the air between them stayed warm.

* * *

Tomas Vey had lived his whole life in towns where weather was a subject of negotiation rather than fact, and he had thought himself immune to surprises. Then a stranger with his mother's hands had knelt in the mud and said the word *brother*, and the ground under Greywater had shifted the way it does in an earthquake felt mostly at sea—a swell, a list, everything familiar suddenly tilted three degrees.

Three months now. Three months of the tilted world.

He was sanding the hull of the *Kestrel* when Odile's name came up again, which it did with irritating frequency, because the whole town seemed determined to make him curious about the stranger whether he liked it or not.

"Your brother's friend is visiting," said Marta, who sold bait and gossip in roughly equal quantities, setting a thermos on his workbench uninvited. "The woman from the library. Pretty, in a severe way. She's staying at the Anchor."

"She's not my brother," Tomas said. "I don't have one."

"Mm." Marta poured coffee into the thermos cap and drank it looking at him over the rim. "Funny thing. Everyone says that word around here like it's settled law. *Brother.* Like it's on a plaque somewhere."

"It isn't settled anything. A man shows up, tells a story, wants—" He stopped. Wanted what? That was the question he kept failing to answer honestly. In the first weeks, the answer had been easy: *wants absolution, wants to hand me his guilt like a package and watch me sign for it.* But the stranger hadn't asked for anything. That was the unbearable part. He'd confessed and then simply—stayed. Worked. Learned the knots. Sent nothing, demanded nothing, waited like furniture waits.

"Wants what?" Marta prompted.

"I don't know. That's the problem." He took the coffee from her because refusing would have invited commentary. "My mother gave away a baby before I was born. Or after—I can't even get the dates straight, and I've gone over it a hundred times. She never said a word. Twenty-six years I ate at that woman's table. And now some man arrives with a shoebox full of letters and expects me to rearrange my whole life around her secrets."

"Is that what he expects?"

"He doesn't expect anything. That's worse." Tomas sanded harder than the wood deserved. "If he pushed, I could push back. If he performed grief, I could despise it. He just stands there being patient, and fixing boats, and knowing things about our mother I don't know, and I'm supposed to just—"

"Just what?"

"*Absorb* it." The word came out ragged. He set down the sandpaper. Out past the breakwater the sea was doing its indecisive thing, sun and cloud fighting to a draw, the water silvered and then dulled and then silvered again. "You know what the worst part is, Marta? Some days I catch myself almost liking him. The way he listens. The way he asks questions like the answers matter. And then I remember he got three days of that from me under false pretenses—three days I *gave* him, thinking he was just some lost librarian—and I feel like a fool twice over. Once for the lie, once for missing it."

Marta capped the thermos. "For what it's worth," she said, "he asked me last week whether you liked storms or hated them. Said he was trying to figure out what to do with his life and thought that was as good a place to start as any."

Tomas stared at her. "That's the stupidest thing I've ever heard."

"Yes," Marta agreed cheerfully, and left.

But that night, sanding done, sitting in his kitchen with the lamp low and the fog pressing at the windows like something wanting in, Tomas found himself turning the question over despite himself. *Storms or no storms.* Who asked a person that? Who built a picture of another human being out of details that small? Their mother—that was who. He remembered her doing it, actually remembered, standing at the window during the great gale of his fourteenth year, saying, *Your father would have loved this sky,* and him not understanding why she'd bring up the dead man then, and her going quiet in a way he'd filed away for decades as grief and now, God help him, had to re-file as something larger. A whole architecture of silence. How many rooms had that house contained that he'd walked past every day?

He wasn't ready. He knew that with certainty, and he also knew, with the same certainty, that readiness was a luxury the dead didn't extend to the living. Somewhere in a hospital bed or a grave—nobody would tell him, and he refused to ask—the woman who made that decision nineteen years before he existed was finishing her account. And he was here, sanding boats, keeping his anger polished like a tool because it was the only inheritance he'd agreed to accept.

The fog thickened. Somewhere out on the water a bell buoy rang, patient, idiotic, persistent.

"One inch," he said to the empty kitchen, not letting himself think about what the words referred to, and went to bed.

* * *

Astrid wrote letters he did not send. This was, he understood, the family trade.

He wrote them at night in the room above the chandlery, on the back of tide tables and the margins of charts, because he owned no proper paper and buying any felt like presumption. He wrote to Tomas: *Today I learned the difference between neap and spring tides. Petra says you once pulled a boy off the flats before the fog closed in, twenty years ago, and the boy is now the harbormaster. I checked. It's true. He pretends not to know me and then leaves parts on my step.* He wrote to his mother: *You would hate this work and love this light. The rain here can't commit. I think I know why you never sent the letters. I think sending them would have made it real, and unsent, they were still somehow yours—still a room you could close the door on. I am trying to learn the opposite lesson but I am a slow student, which is also your gift to me.* He wrote to Odile, one line only, over and over: *Thank you for reading it. Thank you for not comforting me.*

He kept them in the shoebox, which had come with him like a passenger, and which now held both generations of silence—hers unsent, his unsent, the box filling toward some critical mass he tried not to imagine.

On Sundays he walked the flats at low tide, because the flats were where the truth had been spoken and he believed places held what happened in them, the way books held their marginalia. He walked the line where he had knelt. The mud there was ordinary mud. Nothing glowed. The world declined to remember for him, which meant the remembering was his job alone, and he did it, every week, deliberately, the way other men attended church.

It was on one of these Sundays, in the last hour of daylight, that he saw the letter.

Not a real letter—an actual piece of paper, weighted under a stone at the high-tide line, folded once, his name on the outside in handwriting he knew better than his own by now, having studied it on envelopes and boat invoices and the chalkboard outside the bait shop for three months. Tomas's handwriting. Blocky, slanted hard to the right, letters like a man walking into wind.

Astrid stood over it for a long time. The tide was coming in behind him; he could hear it working at the flats' edge, unhurried, total. He crouched, lifted the stone, and read.

*I'm not writing this because I forgive you. I'm writing it because Marta told me about the storm question and it made me angry, and anger at you has started feeling too much like talking to you, and I'd rather do the real thing badly than the fake thing well.*

*I don't want to see you yet. Don't read that as never. Read it as not yet, and understand that I hate that I even wrote that much.*

*One question. Answer it however you want. Did she ever say my name? Not T. Not the letters. My name, out loud, to you, like a person.*

*—T.V.*

Astrid read it four times. The fourth time his eyes failed him entirely and he read it by memory, kneeling in the wet sand, the paper trembling, the tide arriving at his heels like a slow applause or a slow verdict.

Had she said it? He searched thirty years of kitchens and hospital corridors and Sunday phone calls. He searched the letters. And there—in the eleventh letter, the one written the winter Astrid was seven, the one about the school concert—he found it, buried in a sentence about nothing: *Astrid played the recorder badly and I clapped anyway. I wonder if Tomas would have had an ear for music. I used to hum to him. I hope someone hums to him.*

She had said it. Once, in ink, to no one. Which was not the same as out loud, and Astrid would not pretend otherwise, because pretending otherwise was the old craft, the practiced skill, and he was finished with it.

He walked back along the darkening shore to the chandlery, climbed the stairs, and sat at the table with the tide tables and the box. He took out a clean sheet—the last page of a chart of waters he'd never sail—and wrote, in careful, unhidden handwriting:

*She said it once. Written, not spoken. In a letter about my school concert, of all things. She wondered if you had an ear for music. She hoped someone hummed to you. I'm sorry it isn't more. It's everything there is.*

*There's something else. Her last letter—it's addressed to both of us. Both names, both sons. I haven't read it aloud to anyone. I've been saving it because saving things is my disease, but I don't want to die of it. When you're ready—not before, I mean that—I'd like to read it to you. Wherever you choose. Even if you stand ten meters away. Even if you say nothing after.*

*No hurry. I've gotten good at no hurry. It turns out it's the one thing I was ever trained for.*

*—Astrid*

He read it over twice. Then he folded it, weighted it under the same stone at the high-tide line, and walked away without watching, because watching would have been a kind of pressure and pressure was a debt he refused to add to the account.

Two days later, passing the bait shop, Marta handed him a folded page without a word and went back to her newspaper.

*I know the place,* it said. *The dune path above the north beach, where the gorse grows. Sunday, if the weather holds. If it doesn't hold, the Sunday after. I'll be there either way. Don't talk on the walk up. Just read.*

*Bring the letter. All of it.*

Astrid stood in the doorway of the bait shop holding the page, and outside the window the sky over Greywater had gone the deep bruised violet it went before real rain—the first threatened rain in months, the barometer falling, the whole coast holding its breath.

Sunday was two days away. The weather, for once, looked like it might finally make up its mind.
