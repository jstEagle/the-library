## 19. The Grave and the Rain

The invitation took Astrid three days to write and eleven seconds to send.

He had drafted it on the back of a harbor tide chart, then on a napkin, then on the lined paper the school gave him from the supply cupboard, each version failing in a new way. Too formal. Too pleading. Too much like a summons. In the end he wrote it the way he would say it, standing in Tomas's doorway with rain coming off the harbor in sheets:

*I'm going to my mother's grave on Thursday. I have her last letter. She wrote it to both of us. I'd like to read it out loud, where she can hear it. I'd like you to be there. You can say no, and I'll understand, and I'll still be your brother whether you speak to me again or not.*

He read it four times on the ferry platform, the wind trying to take it from his hands, and then he put it in the postbox on Marine Parade and walked away without looking back, which was, he thought, the first brave thing he had done in thirty years.

Tomas said yes. One line, typed with two thumbs: *Thursday. I'll drive.*

* * *

They went up on the morning train because Tomas said the coast road flooded and Astrid said he didn't drive, and Tomas looked at him for a moment too long—*thirty years in a city and never learned?*—and Astrid said, *There was never anywhere I needed to be,* and Tomas let it go, the way he had been letting things go for months now, each small mercy a plank laid across the water between them.

The train was half empty. Rain came at the windows in long diagonal strokes, and the fields beyond the glass were the green of things that had been waiting a long time to drink. Astrid sat with the letter in the inside pocket of his coat, over his heart, where he had carried it for six days, and Tomas sat across from him with his knees angled into the aisle, a man built for boats sitting in a seat built for no one in particular.

They did not talk much. That was the strange mercy of Tomas—his silences were not the silences Astrid knew how to make, the weaponized ones, the ones that ended conversations. Tomas's silences were rooms he let you stand in. He looked out at the rain and once he said, *She liked weather, did she? Your—* and stopped, and corrected nothing, and started again. *Our mother.*

*I think she counted it,* Astrid said. *In the letters. Rain days, fog days. She wrote the weather at the top of every letter like a date.*

Tomas nodded slowly, as if this confirmed something he had suspected about himself his whole life without knowing where he'd gotten it. *I keep a log,* he said. *Barometer readings. Forty years. My wife used to laugh at me.* His wife had left him eleven years ago; Astrid knew this from the harbor gossip, from everything he had learned in the long penance of staying. *She said I was keeping records for a country that didn't exist.*

The train went through a cutting and the world went dark and then green again, and neither of them said what they were both thinking: that the country existed, that it had existed all along, that they had both been keeping records of it in the dark.

* * *

The cemetery was on a hill above the town where their mother had spent her last forty years, a hill of wet grass and leaning stones, and the rain had gentled by the time they reached it into that fine persistent Greywater drizzle that was not quite weather and not quite air. Astrid had brought an umbrella and had not opened it. Tomas noticed and said nothing, and Astrid understood that this, too, was noticed and accepted: they would stand in it. Whatever this was going to be, they would stand in the weather to do it.

The stone was modest. *MARGARET VEY YARROW, 1948–2024. She kept her hours.* Odile had suggested the last line, over the phone, her voice rough. Astrid had not known until that moment that his mother had ever told Odile anything, and Odile had said, *She told me about the letters, Astrid. Near the end. She told me to make you go.* He had sat with that for a long time.

Tomas stood in front of the stone for a while without speaking. Rain collected in the engraved letters and made them shine. When he finally reached out and touched the granite, he touched it the way you touch a boat's hull—flat palm, checking for damage, for seaworthiness.

*She was nineteen,* he said. It was not a question. Astrid had told him everything on the mudflats, three weeks ago, the whole architecture of it, the arithmetic. But saying it here, at the stone, was different. *Nineteen, and they took you, and she went home and made another one and never told him.*

*Never told me.*

*And then she wrote to me for fifty years and never sent it.*

*Yes.*

Tomas took his hand off the stone. He looked older than he had at the harbor café, in this light, in this grief—older, and more like Astrid, which was the cruelest trick of the light Astrid had ever seen. The same heavy brow. The same way of holding the mouth shut on things.

*Read it,* Tomas said. *I didn't come to talk.*

* * *

Astrid took out the letter.

The paper had gone soft at the folds, the way paper does when it has been held a great deal. His mother's handwriting started strong at the top of the page—*Weather: rain, at last, real rain, the first in months, I sat by the window like a fool and watched it come*—and he began to read aloud.

His voice held, at first. He had spent thirty years reading aloud in a night library to people who could not sleep, and he knew how to make a voice carry without breaking, how to lay words down gently the way you lay down something sleeping.

*To my sons. Both of you, and I mean both, and I know that word has done damage in this family, and I am going to use it anyway, because you are both mine and I am done pretending otherwise.*

The drizzle came down. Somewhere below them the town made its small harbor sounds, a gull, a winch, a door.

*I gave one of you away because I was nineteen and poor and my father said a child needs a whole life and I only had half of one to give, and I believed him, and I have spent fifty years learning that what I actually did was give away the half of myself that knew how to be happy. Tomas, you were the easy one. You were born smiling. I have thought about that smile every day of my life and I want you to know it was not a sacrifice I made, it was a theft, and I was the thief, and no amount of arithmetic makes a theft into a gift.*

Astrid's voice caught on *theft*. He stopped. Breathed. The rain ran down the back of his collar and he did not move to stop it.

*Astrid. You were the one I kept, and I want to tell you what that cost, because you have spent your whole life being quiet and I know—I have always known—that you learned it from me. You learned that love is a thing you do in secret, that wanting things out loud is how you lose them. I made you a keeper of silences. I am so sorry. I am sorry in the way that is too late, which is the only sorry I ever managed to be on time for.*

His voice failed on the next line. Not cracked—failed, went out like a lamp, and what came out was air shaped like nothing. He stood there with the paper shaking in his hands and the rain coming down and his brother three feet away watching him with an expression Astrid could not read and did not dare to, and he thought: *I cannot finish this. I brought us up a hill to say goodbye properly and I cannot finish it.*

He tried again. Got four words. *I kept the letters*—and then his throat closed, and the word *letters* came out as a kind of animal sound, and he bent forward over the grave with the paper held out in front of him like something on fire.

He did not stop trying. That was the thing he would remember afterward, the thing that surprised him: he did not stop. He stood in the rain over his mother's grave and kept forcing his voice back into service, word by word, half a sentence, a phrase, his hand white-knuckled on the soft paper, refusing—refusing with everything the quiet thirty years had built in him—to let this be another silence. If his voice was going to fail, it was going to fail out loud, in the open, witnessed.

*And if you are hearing this read aloud, then one of you found the other, which means one of you was braver than me, and I hope you will tell him so, because I never managed—*

The page blurred. His voice went again. And then a hand came over his shoulder, steady, boat-calloused, and took the letter out of his fingers with the same flat-palm care Tomas had used on the stone.

Tomas held the paper in the rain and read the rest himself.

His voice was nothing like Astrid's—rougher, unpracticed, a voice that gave orders to ropes and engines and did not know how to be gentle with a sentence—but he read it anyway, slowly, stumbling once on their mother's handwriting where the ink had run, and Astrid stood beside him and listened to his own mother's last words arrive in his brother's mouth and understood that this was the ending she had written toward all along. Not one son. Not the other. Both of them, on a hill, in the rain, taking turns carrying her.

*—because I never managed to say it to your faces. I forgive you both for nothing, because there is nothing to forgive; the failures were mine. And I forgive you both for everything, because that is what mothers are for, and I was your mother for exactly as long as I was allowed and not one day less. Find each other. Keep each other's hours. And when the rain comes, real rain, the first in months—go outside. Don't sit at the window like a fool. Go out in it. It's the same rain on both of you.*

*Your mother, who counted the weather so she would know when you were both under the same sky.*

Tomas read the last line twice. Then he folded the letter along its soft old creases, careful as a man folding a sail in wind, and held it out, and Astrid took it back, and for a while neither of them moved, and the drizzle came down on the stone that said *She kept her hours*, and below them the town went on with its day.

*Same rain,* Tomas said finally. His voice was wrecked. He didn't try to fix it. *She wrote that to me forty years ago. Almost word for word. First letter I ever got from her—no, second. The first was just the weather. A whole page of weather, and at the bottom, *I hope it's raining where you are too.**

Astrid laughed, or made the sound that grief makes when it can't decide whether to be laughter, and Tomas made it too, and they stood there making that sound together over their mother's grave like two men who had just discovered they shared a native language.

Then Tomas put his hand on Astrid's shoulder—briefly, heavily, the weight of it deliberate—and took it away.

*Come on,* he said. *It's coming down harder. And I know a café where the owner owes me a favor, and you're going to tell me the rest of the letters. All of them. The ones I haven't heard. We've got time now.*

He started down the hill, not waiting, sure of his ground the way he was sure of everything, and Astrid stood one moment longer at the stone with the letter against his chest and the rain beginning, at last, to come down in earnest—real rain, the first in months, the kind you don't watch from a window.

Then he followed his brother down through the wet grass, out of the silence, into the weather.
