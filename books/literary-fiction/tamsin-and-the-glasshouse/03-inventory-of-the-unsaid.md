## 3. Inventory of the Unsaid

The letters did not open that night.

Tamsin knew this the way she knew which patrons would fall asleep over their books and which would read until dawn — by some arithmetic of posture and breath that had never once failed her. She sat at her desk in the night library with the biscuit tin square in front of her, its lid dented where a child — she? Ivy? — had once pried it open for shortbread, and she could not make her hands do the one thing hands were made for.

Instead she catalogued. It was what she had instead of prayer.

*One tin, rectangular, rust at the corners. Contents: correspondence, undelivered, est. thirty years. Provenance: behind wardrobe panel, second floor, 14 Marrow Lane.* She wrote it in pencil, because ink felt like a commitment she was not ready to make. The tin went into her bag, the bag under her desk, the desk into the long amber quiet of the library's small hours, and Tamsin Coral Galloway returned to the work of being unremarkable, which was the work she was best at.

At 2:14 a.m., the man in the grey coat came in again.

She had been noticing him for three weeks now. He arrived always after two, always with the same paperback swollen from rain damage, always to the same carrel by the window where the streetlight came through like something spilled. He did not read so much as hold the book open, the way you hold a door for someone who is not coming. Tonight he sat down, opened it to a page near the middle, and put his head in his hands.

Tamsin gave him eleven minutes. Then she crossed the floor with a glass of water she had no official reason to be carrying.

"The spine's broken," he said, before she spoke, as if they were mid-conversation. "I keep opening it to the same page. I don't even like this book."

"Then why bring it?"

He looked up. He had the face of a man who had been handsome recently enough that he still expected it to work. "It was hers," he said. "My wife's. She died in March." He turned the book over as though checking it for a pulse. "I keep thinking if I finish it, I'll have finished *her*, and I'm not — I don't want to be finished."

The library hummed around them: the vents, the elevator shaft, the sleeping man in the periodicals section who snored in iambic pentameter. Tamsin set down the water.

"You don't have to finish it," she said. "You could just — visit it. Read the parts you loved until they're worn smooth. Some books aren't meant to be completed. They're meant to be kept."

He stared at her. "That's either very wise or very sad."

"In my experience those arrive in the same envelope."

He laughed — a startled, rusty sound — and something in his shoulders came down an inch. He asked her name. She said, "The librarian," which was true, and he wrote it on the flyleaf anyway, *for the librarian*, and dated it, and Tamsin felt the strange vertigo of being seen in a building designed for exactly that and nowhere else.

She walked back to her desk. Her bag sat there with its rust-cornered weight, patient as a held breath.

She took out the first letter.

* * *

It was dated nine days after her birth, which was itself a fact she had to sit with — that her mother had known the date of her own daughter's birth well enough to date a letter against it, that the letter existed at all, that it had spent three decades six inches from her mother's sleeping head.

*Tamsin,* it began, and then nothing for four lines, four lines of blank paper where a mother's pen had hovered and failed, and then:

*They are calling you Baby Girl Galloway at St. Ambrose, which is a lie we agreed to, because your father said a name would make it real and then how would anyone survive the paperwork. You weighed six pounds two ounces. I know this because I asked the nurse twice, pretending the first time hadn't happened.*

*I am writing this in the car park. Your father is inside signing things. He wanted me to say that this is the right thing, and maybe it is, but I notice that the people who call a thing right are always the people who will feel it least.*

*You have a tuft of dark hair at the crown, like a question mark. The nurse let me hold you for seven minutes. I counted. I am going to count everything for the rest of my life, I think. That is what I understand now about giving a thing away: you do not stop owning it. You just lose the right to say so.*

Tamsin read it twice. Then a third time, standing, because sitting had become impossible, the words doing something to her inner ear, tilting the whole careful architecture of herself.

*Surrendered at birth.* The phrase assembled itself in her mind with the cold efficiency of a catalogue record. Not abandoned — surrendered, a word with paperwork in it, a word with signatures. And then reclaimed, because here she was, thirty years later, alive and employed and in possession of a surname, and somewhere between those two facts there had been a day — she flipped through the tin with suddenly shaking hands — yes. Here. A letter dated fourteen months later, her mother's hand looser now, drunk or sleepless or both:

*We got you back. Malcolm came home on a Tuesday and said, "Go and get her," and I didn't ask a single question because I was afraid the answer would be no. You cried the whole drive and I cried harder and when Ivy saw you she said, "Is that my sister?" as if you'd simply been late.*

*We agreed — all of us, even Aunt Ruthie, who can't keep a secret about anything else — that we would never speak of it. Malcolm's condition. He said a family could survive anything except its own history told out loud. So we are going to be a family with a locked room in it, and you will grow up in the hallway outside that room, and you will think the draft you feel is weather.*

Tamsin put the letter down on the desk.

The library clock said 3:40. Somewhere above her the city went on not sleeping, and Tamsin stood in the amber light and understood, with the terrible clarity that only comes at that hour, that the draft had been the design.

* * *

She began the inventory at 4:02 a.m., because her hands needed occupation or they would tear the remaining letters open all at once, and some part of her — the librarian part, the part that believed order was a form of mercy — insisted on sequence.

A fresh card. Her neatest hand. At the top: *GALLOWAY FAMILY: SILENCES, AN INVENTORY.*

*Item 1. My birth. Surrendered; reclaimed; never named. Duration: thirty years. Custodian: everyone.*

Her pen hovered. Once started, the entries came like water finding cracks.

*Item 2. The year Father left for "the Manchester office." There is no Manchester office. Mother kept setting his place at the table for eight months. Item 3. Ivy's wedding, at which Mother toasted "my two daughters, both exactly as I dreamed" while gripping the tablecloth. Item 4. The photograph album, 1998–2001, missing pages, spines intact — removed, not lost. Item 5. The way Mother says "our Tamsin was always such an easy baby." No one has ever been an easy baby. Easy is a story told afterward.*

Item six. Item nine. Item twelve. The silences had a shape, she realized — they radiated outward from a single point like cracks in glass, and every one of them, every odd Christmas and unexplained absence and too-bright cheerfulness, was load-bearing. The family stood because of them. Take one out and —

Take one out and what?

The grey-coat man rose from his carrel at five, tucking his dead wife's book under his arm, unfinished, kept. On his way past the desk he paused.

"For the librarian," he said, tapping the flyleaf. "In case you ever wonder whether anyone noticed you."

"I prefer not being noticed," Tamsin said. "It's a professional skill."

"It's a terrible skill," he said gently. "Nobody should be that good at it." And he went out into the blue pre-dawn, leaving her with the water glass and the cards spread across the desk like a hand of losing poker.

* * *

She read three more letters before her shift ended. Her mother's, mostly — her mother wrote the way she gardened, compulsively, in all weathers — but wedged between two of them, thin and stiff, a single page in a hand Tamsin recognized from the signature on her childhood permission slips, from the birthday cards that always arrived a day late: her father's.

It was undated. It said only:

*This is the last time I will write about it. We have agreed, and the agreement holds, and I want it noted — since apparently I need everything noted, Edith says — that I did what I did out of fear and not out of lack. Fear looks exactly like lack from the inside. If she ever reads these, tell her the room was locked to keep something safe, not to keep her out.*

*Though I suppose, from where she stands, there is no difference.*

Tamsin read it four times. *If she ever reads these.* He had imagined her. Thirty years ago, signing away his silence, he had imagined her standing exactly here, in a pool of lamplight, holding the evidence of the locked room.

And he had written anyway. Which meant some part of him had wanted the lock broken — had wanted it so badly he'd built the key into the wall behind the wardrobe and waited, all these years, for someone to pry the panel loose.

Or he had simply been careless. Families ran on carelessness as much as anything.

Tamsin slid the letters back into the tin. She did not seal the box — sealing felt like becoming her parents — but she closed the lid, and the dent in it caught the light, and she thought: *Ivy knows.* It arrived in her mind fully formed, the way certain catalogue records arrive, without visible sourcing. Ivy, who called at odd hours to report on nothing. Ivy, whose voice had gone carefully flat on the phone when Tamsin mentioned packing the bedroom. Ivy, who had stayed — who had *always* stayed, tethered to that house and that woman with a devotion that had curdled somewhere along the way into something Tamsin had never been able to name.

Ivy knew. Ivy had been made to promise.

Tamsin picked up the phone before she could talk herself out of it, and dialed the number she knew better than her own, and listened to it ring in a house two bus routes away where her sister was probably already awake, already making their mother's breakfast, already carrying whatever she carried alone.

Ivy answered on the fourth ring, breathless, guarded. "Tam? Is everything—"

"I found the letters," Tamsin said. "Behind the wardrobe. I need you to tell me what you know."

The silence on the line was long, and familiar, and itemized.
