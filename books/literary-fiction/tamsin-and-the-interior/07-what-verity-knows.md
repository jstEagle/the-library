## 7. What Verity Knows

Verity arrived the way weather arrives—announced by a pressure change, felt in the joints before she was seen at the door. Odile heard the car first, the particular expensive idle of it, and then the doorbell, rung twice in quick insistence, as though the house were a servant who had been dawdling.

She opened the door to her aunt standing on the step in a coat the color of wet slate, holding a bottle of sherry in one gloved hand and, in the other, nothing at all, which was somehow more pointed.

"Odile." Verity said her name the way she said everything, as if placing it carefully on a shelf. "You look terrible. That's grief, of course. Come here."

The embrace was brief and firm and smelled of bergamot and cold air. When Verity stepped back she was already looking past Odile's shoulder, into the hall, her gaze moving along the walls the way a surveyor's might, taking bearings.

"I've come to help," she said. "You can't do this alone, darling. Cataloguing an entire house. It's too much for anyone."

"I've been managing."

"Managing." Verity smiled and stepped inside, out of the cold, without being invited twice. "Yes. Your mother said the same thing about you, near the end. *Managing.*" She set the sherry down on the hall table with a small decisive click. "Where shall we start? Not the attic. Attics are for last. One loses heart in attics."

* * *

They began, as Odile had known they would, in the drawing room.

Verity moved through it like a woman revisiting a house she'd once owned and had been, she still felt, cheated of. She lifted the lids of things, turned the bases of things over, ran a gloved finger along the spine of a cabinet and examined it against the light. She was cataloguing too, Odile realized. But her inventory was a different kind. Where Odile had been writing *mahogany, water-stained, uncertain provenance*, Verity was writing, invisibly, *mine, possibly mine, not mine but valuable, who will notice if*—

"The Worcester," Verity said, lifting a teacup to the window. "Your mother got this from my mother. Technically it should have come to me. I won't make an issue of it now, of course." She set it down with the delicacy of someone making an issue of it in advance. "The will will sort all that out. These things always do."

"The will leaves everything to me."

"The will," Verity said, "is a document. Documents can be read generously or narrowly, and I have known this family's solicitor since before you were born." She said it lightly, a joke almost, and then looked at Odile with sudden directness. "You've been sleeping in the guest room."

It wasn't a question. Odile felt the floor tilt a degree, the way it had been tilting since the wardrobe, since the cedar-lined dark, since *To the one I could not keep*.

"The master bedroom needs airing," she said.

"Your mother kept that room like a chapel and you know it. You always were the only one she allowed in there to dust." Verity moved to the mantel and straightened a photograph that did not need straightening—Odile's mother at twenty, squinting into a summer that had happened before Odile existed. "You were always her little archivist. Even as a girl. Following her around with your lists."

"I make lists," Odile agreed. "It's how I think."

"And what does the list say so far?" Verity turned, sherry bottle already in hand, pouring two glasses without asking. "Room by room. What's the house worth, Odile? Not in money. I mean—" she handed over a glass, "—what's it *keeping*?"

The question came in on a slant, the way all of Verity's questions did, and Odile felt it pass close enough to part her hair.

"Furniture," she said. "Dust. Forty years of *Woman's Own* in the loo."

"Mm." Verity drank. Her eyes stayed on Odile over the rim of the glass, and Odile understood that she had just been weighed, and that the reading had been inconclusive, and that Verity did not tolerate inconclusion.

* * *

The sherry went down warm and treacly, and Odile let it, because she had decided something in the night and the sherry would help her hold to it.

She had spent the morning with the box in the study, the door locked, the letters fanned out on the desk like a hand of cards she was afraid to play. Forty-one. She had counted them again. She had read three more—the second, the third, the fifth—each beginning *To the one I could not keep*, each dated in that small upright hand, each sealed with a kiss's worth of licked gum that had never done its work. Her mother had sealed them all. Had gone through the whole motion of finishing—the envelope, the flap, the press of the thumb—and then had simply put them away. Thirty years of endings that never ended.

And in the margins of the oldest ones, in pencil, faded almost to nothing, a name appeared and was erased, appeared and was erased. Odile had held one up to the window and read the ghost of it: *D—*. Then, in a later letter, whole and unhidden, as if the mother had finally stopped being afraid of her own pencil: *Daniel*.

She had put them all back. All but one. She had learned that much already: never keep the whole archive in one room.

Now, over sherry, she watched her aunt perform grief and inventory in the drawing room, and she made the decision move from her chest into her mouth.

"Aunt Verity," she said. "Did my mother ever write letters? Real ones, I mean. To people. She wasn't a correspondent, was she. I've found almost nothing in the desks. Not a single letter to anyone."

It was a half-truth, angled like a mirror. She watched it travel.

Verity had been reaching for the decanter. Her hand arrived at it a half-second late. She poured, and the neck of the bottle touched the rim of the glass with a small sound, *tick*, like a clock deciding something.

"Your mother," Verity said, "believed that anything written down becomes evidence."

"Of what?"

"Of feeling." Verity smiled. She set the bottle down. "She was a great one for feeling, your mother. She just preferred it at a distance. Like weather. Like me, I suppose. We Coldharbour women—" she corrected herself, smoothly, "—we *Harrow* women, your mother married out of the name and never let me forget it—we keep our weather indoors."

"You've avoided the question."

"Have I?" Verity settled back into the good chair, the one that had been their mother's mother's, and crossed her ankles. "You've been in that wardrobe room. Your mother's room."

The floor went all the way over then. Odile kept her face still through what felt like a long falling. "I've catalogued it. Yes."

"And you found it—tidy."

"Immaculate."

"Immaculate." Verity tasted the word. "Yes. She was immaculate. Right up until the end, she was immaculate. You know what I did the day after she died, Odile? Before I even telephoned you? I sat in my kitchen and I thought: *what has she kept.* Because your mother always kept things. Not sentimentally—never that. Functionally. She kept things the way a government keeps things." Verity's glass turned slowly in her fingers. "So. If you were to find something, in that house, that you didn't understand—something old, something *private*—you'd come to me. Wouldn't you. Before you did anything. Before you *read* anything."

"Why would I find something I didn't understand? The house is furniture and dust."

"Mm," Verity said again, and the *mm* had teeth in it now.

There was a silence. In it, Odile heard the house itself: the tick of the hall clock, the settling of pipes, the ordinary respiration of a building that had been holding its breath for thirty years and was, she understood suddenly, beginning to exhale.

"Your mother loved you," Verity said at last, and it was the strangest thing she'd said all afternoon, because it was the first thing said without calculation, and it landed like a stone dropped down a well whose bottom Odile had never found. "Whatever else. Whatever you think you've worked out with your lists. She loved you the way people love when they've already lost something—desperately, and badly, and with both hands. Remember that, when you find whatever it is you're going to find."

"I haven't found anything."

"No," Verity agreed. "Of course not. More sherry?"

* * *

Verity stayed until the light went bronze and then blue. She examined the silver. She pronounced the dining room furniture "nineteenth century at best, and provincial." She stood a long time in front of the wardrobe room door—not her mother's room, Odile noticed; Verity did not go in, did not offer to, did not once look at it directly, which in Verity's language was a shout—and then she left, kissing the air near Odile's cheek and saying, at the door, with her hand on Odile's arm:

"Lock up well tonight, darling. The village isn't what it was."

Then the expensive engine, the expensive exhaust, the expensive taillights diminishing down the lane, and the house closing around Odile like a hand around a held breath.

* * *

She did not sleep in the guest room.

She took the box from the study—she had already moved it once, that morning, from behind the wardrobe to the locked drawer of the study desk, but the drawer suddenly seemed as legible as a label—and she carried it up two flights to the box room under the eaves, the one room in the house nobody had ever had a use for, and put it inside the old cabin trunk that had belonged to her grandfather, under a false bottom of moth-eaten blankets, and sat down on the floor beside it in the dark to think.

This was the arithmetic she could not make come out: Verity had not asked *where*. In all that angled conversation—*if you were to find something, you'd come to me*—her aunt had never once asked what the thing was, or where it was kept. Which meant either that she didn't believe Odile had found anything, or that she already knew what it was and where, and needed only to know whether Odile knew that she knew.

Odile sat in the cold under the eaves and understood that she had made an error. The half-truth had not been a test. It had been a flare. She had stood in the drawing room and lit herself up against the dark sky of the family, and now everything that hunted by sight knew exactly where she was.

She went down to the guest room at half past eleven and lay on top of the covers in her clothes, the hall clock ticking through its inventory of the hours, and she did not sleep, and she did not expect to.

* * *

She heard the front door at ten past three.

Not opened—unlocked. The lock was old and had a voice; it had woken her as a child when her father came in late, a dry double-click like a knuckle cracking. She had heard it perhaps four times in her life from this bed. Now she heard it and lay absolutely still, heart going like a bird in a box, and listened to the house admit someone.

Footsteps. Not Verity's—Verity's heels announced her the way her car did. These were soft, careful, a man's or a woman's, moving along the hall below with the unhurried confidence of someone who had walked this floor before. A torch beam slid along the underside of the guest room door, a thin bright line that paused, moved on.

Odile lay in the dark and did the only intelligent thing she had done all day, which was nothing.

The footsteps went along the landing. A door opened—the study, by its hinge-sound. Drawers. The desk drawers, one after another, opened with their small wooden voices and closed again. Long pauses between. Then the wardrobe room. The door there had a different sound, heavier, and when it closed the whole frame sighed.

She lay counting her own breaths. Through the floor she felt, rather than heard, the wardrobe being moved—she was almost sure of it, a long low drag of weight across boards, the same sound the boards had made under her own hands—and then a silence so complete she could hear the torch's switch click twice, softly, like a beetle.

Then the footsteps came back along the landing. Paused at the guest room door. The thin bright line under the door held there, motionless, for the length of ten breaths—Odile counted them, her own breaths, shallow and even, the breathing of the tenant, the breathing of the woman who had learned in this house that the safest thing to be was furniture—and then moved on.

The front door. The dry double-click of the lock, turned gently home from outside. A key.

*They have a key,* Odile thought, in the dark, with a clarity that felt almost like calm. *Of course they have a key. They've always had a key. This house was never mine and never hers. It was a strongbox, and everyone in the family has been a warder.*

She got up at first light. The house was grey and innocent, the hall clock ticking, the study drawers all closed, the wardrobe standing in its place exactly where she had left it—no, not exactly; two inches off, its shadow lying wrong against the wall, and behind it, when she moved it, the cedar cavity empty of everything except a smell, and a single pressed flower she had never noticed before, a stem of dried lavender laid in the empty dark like a signature.

They had found the cavity. They had found it empty. And somewhere under the eaves of this house, in a dead man's trunk, forty-one letters lay in the dark, and one of them—the fourth, the one she had kept in her cardigan pocket since morning, the one beginning *To the one I could not keep* and containing, in the third paragraph, a name—pressed against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

The kettle was still warm when she went down to make tea.

Someone had made tea, recently, in this house, and drunk it standing up, and left the cup in the drainer, washed, careful, immaculate—her mother's word, her mother's habit, her mother's whole life conducted at this sink—and Odile stood looking at it while the grey light came up over the garden wall, and understood that the hunt had begun, and that she was no longer the one invisible thing in it.
