## 18. Eve of Auction

The van smelled of rosin and cold coffee, and Theo sat in it for a long time before she went inside.

Across the street, the Sandoval house on Corliss Street stood lit from every window, the way it never had been when anyone actually lived there. Her family had always been frugal with light—Renata's voice in her head, *electricity isn't free, Theodore*—but tonight every lamp burned, because tomorrow strangers would walk through these rooms and judge what they saw. The house was dressed for its own funeral.

Theo checked her tuning kit on the passenger seat out of habit. Mutes, felt strips, the tuning hammer her father had given her, worn to the shape of her hand. She wouldn't need any of it tonight. Tonight there was no work to hide behind, and that was the whole problem. For thirty years she had made herself useful so she would never have to make herself known. A piano tuner can stand in any room in this city and be furniture. Furniture hears everything. Furniture is asked nothing.

Tomorrow at ten a.m., under a white tent on the lawn, an auctioneer named Gary Pell—she'd looked him up, he had a website, a gold blazer, testimonials—would raise his gavel over the house where her brother disappeared. And unless she did something, the only sound that room would ever hold again was money changing hands.

She got out of the van.

* * *

The front door was unlocked. Of course it was. Renata had stopped locking anything years ago, after the last burglary, on the theory that locks were a form of lying—you pretended the world couldn't get in when you knew perfectly well it could. Theo had thought it was just another Renata-ism, one more piece of inherited fatalism, but standing in the hall now she understood it differently. Renata had spent thirty years leaving the door open for a boy who wasn't coming back through it. You don't lock a door like that.

The parlor was full.

Not crowded—full, the way a jar is full, everyone arranged at maximum density with minimum contact. Ambrose sat in his father's old chair by the dead fireplace, hands folded on his knee, wearing the suit he wore to funerals and award ceremonies, which in this family had often been the same event. His wife, Carol, beside him, doing something luminous and useless with a casserole nobody would eat. Cousin Dee stood at the window with a glass of wine she'd been holding without drinking, her reflection ghosted against the lit street. Two second cousins Theo could never keep straight hovered by the bookshelf, speaking in the low murmurs of people at a wake who are mostly relieved the dying is over.

And Renata was in the kitchen. Of course. Renata was always in the kitchen, which was the family's control tower, its confessional, its war room. Through the doorway Theo could see her back, straight as a flagpole, hands moving over a counter she was wiping though it was already clean.

Nobody said hello. That was the arrangement, even now, even at the end: greetings acknowledged presence, and presence was dangerous. Presence invited questions. Instead Ambrose lifted two fingers off his knee—a salute, a benediction, a *we see you and we will not pursue it*—and Dee turned from the window long enough to meet Theo's eyes. Something passed between them there, quick and electric, and then Dee looked away, and Theo understood that whatever they'd almost built together in the past weeks—the ledger, the carbon paper, the name smudged beyond reading—Dee had decided tonight to be furniture too.

Theo hung her coat on the hook by the door. Her hook. Third from the left, still there, paint chipped around the screw. Thirty years and no one had moved it or claimed it, the way no one had ever formally assigned her the small back bedroom, but everyone knew it was hers, the way everyone knew not to mention Milo's room at the end of the hall.

She went into the kitchen.

* * *

"You came." Renata didn't turn around.

"It's my house too."

"It's nobody's house. That's the point of an auction." Renata set down the dishcloth with the precision of a woman racking a rifle. "There's coffee."

There was coffee, in the percolator that had outlived three presidents, and Theo poured herself a cup she didn't want because holding something gave her hands an excuse. Behind her, the kitchen radiator ticked as it cooled—no heat needed on a night this mild, but the pilot of memory kept it warm anyway—and Theo listened to it the way other people check a mirror. Still there. Still keeping its record.

"Everyone's here," Theo said.

"Everyone who counts."

"That's not what I mean."

"I know exactly what you mean." Now Renata turned, and Theo braced herself for the fury from the confrontation in this same room, weeks ago—*you want the truth or you want the family, you cannot price both*—but the fury wasn't there. What was there was worse. Tiredness. Renata looked like a woman who had been carrying a piano up a staircase alone for thirty years and had finally been told the staircase ended nowhere. "Whatever you're planning, Theodore—"

"I'm not planning anything."

"You have your mother's face when you lie. It's a terrible face. Everyone can tell."

Theo drank the coffee. It was terrible, burnt and bitter, perfect. "I'm going to say something tomorrow," she said. "At the auction. Before the bidding."

The kitchen went very quiet. Out in the parlor, someone laughed at something—not real laughter, the polite kind, the kind that exists to fill silence—and the sound seemed to come from very far away, from another house entirely.

Renata set both palms flat on the counter. Theo watched her sister-in-law—her aunt, technically, though *aunt* had always been too small a word for what Renata was; Renata had been the wall the whole family leaned against until leaning became the architecture itself—watched her take one breath, two, the way you do when you're deciding whether to fight or to finally put the weight down.

"The letter," Renata said.

"What about it?"

"You opened it?"

"No." Theo set down the cup. "You did. Thirty years ago. You read it, and you folded it back up, and you kept it in your desk, and you let all of us believe he was gone because of something we did or didn't do. You let *me* believe—" Her voice caught, and she hated it, and she pushed through. "I was nine years old, Renata. I spent thirty years thinking maybe if I hadn't been crying in the driveway, maybe if I hadn't run inside, he would have stayed. You knew. You had it in writing. He *chose* to go. Dad drove him."

"I know what I did," Renata said, and her voice didn't rise at all, which was how Theo knew it was costing her everything. "Do you think I've had one night's sleep since? Do you think I kept that letter because I wanted to? I kept it because returning it meant saying it out loud. Saying it out loud meant Dad died knowing we all knew. Meant Mom spent her last decade eating dinner across from the truth every night. Meant this family—" and here her hand made a small, exhausted gesture that took in the parlor, the house, the whole rigged machinery of them, "—had to be rebuilt from the studs, at an age when none of us had the strength to hold a hammer. So I chose the silence. Every day, for thirty years, I chose it again. Like taking a pill. You don't get credit for it. You don't stop needing it."

"And tomorrow it runs out."

"Tomorrow the house sells." Renata picked up the dishcloth again, wrung it, laid it down. "And whatever you say under that tent, say it knowing that after tomorrow, there is no Corliss Street to come home to. Whatever we are after tomorrow, we will be it somewhere else. Or we won't be anything at all." She met Theo's eyes, and for one unguarded second Theo saw the girl Renata must have been, the one who'd found a letter on the mat and made a decision that cost more than any of them would ever calculate. "That's not a threat. It's arithmetic."

* * *

Theo carried her coffee into the parlor and the conversation did the thing conversations in this family always did—dipped, smoothed, resumed at a lower temperature. She sat on the arm of the couch near Dee, close enough that their shoulders almost touched.

"Gary Pell," Dee said quietly, nodding toward the window, where a sign company van was pulling away from a freshly planted stake in the front yard. SOLD AT AUCTION, 10 AM SHARP. "Gold blazer. Testimonials. 'Every lot finds its home.'"

"He sounds nice."

"He sounds like a man who's never had to sell anything he loved." Dee finally drank some wine. Up close Theo could see she'd been crying earlier, expertly concealed, the mascara handled by a professional. "Ambrose brought the photo albums. Did you see? He's been showing people the good years. Lake photos. Milo on the dock."

"I saw."

"He rehearsed it in the car," Dee said. "I heard him. 'Milo was a restless boy, restless boys grow up and move along, nothing anyone could have done.' Word for word, like a speech. Like he's giving it tomorrow too." Dee's jaw tightened. "Thirty years of Sunday dinners and I have never once heard him say Milo's name without that sentence attached to it. Like a seatbelt. Click, buckle, safe."

"Maybe he needs it."

"Maybe we all need it. That's the problem, isn't it." Dee turned, and her eyes were wet and furious and brave, all three fighting for the wheel. "Theo. Tomorrow. Whatever you're going to do—I need you to know I'm not going to help you. I can't. Renata saved my business, she saved my *life*, and I know how that sounds, I know exactly what it costs me to say it, but I am not strong enough to be the one who—"

"I know."

"But I'll be there," Dee said. "Under the tent. At ten o'clock. And afterward—" she stopped, restarted. "Afterward, whichever way it goes, call me. Not Renata. Not Ambrose. Me. Promise me that."

"I promise."

Dee nodded once, briskly, businesslike, and then squeezed Theo's wrist hard enough to hurt, which was the most honest thing anyone in this family had done in weeks, and went back to her window.

* * *

Later—ten o'clock, eleven—the house began to empty. Carol ferried Ambrose out first, his hand raised in that two-fingered blessing, his funeral suit already becoming a costume for tomorrow. The second cousins left in a cloud of murmured condolences-for-nothing. Dee hugged Renata at the door, and Theo watched them hold on a beat longer than their truce required, two women bound by debt and love in proportions neither could separate anymore.

Then it was just Theo and Renata and the house.

"I'm turning off the lights," Renata said. "Except the porch."

"Except the porch," Theo agreed.

They walked through the rooms together, Renata ahead, switching off lamps, and Theo listening to the house settle around them—the tick of cooling radiators, the groan of pipes in the walls, the particular hum of old wiring that she had heard in a hundred houses and never in one that mattered like this. In the parlor, the upright sat with its lid closed, and Theo rested her fingers on it without opening it, feeling through the wood the faint vibration of the furnace two floors down, the house's pulse, steady, indifferent, keeping time.

In the hall, Renata paused at the stairs. "Your room's made up," she said. "If you're staying."

"I'll stay."

"There's coffee cake in the tin. Your grandmother's recipe. Don't tell Carol I made it, she'll be insufferable about the streusel."

It was almost funny. It was almost a normal night. Theo climbed the stairs and passed Milo's room at the end of the hall without looking at it, the way she had done ten thousand times, and then—stopped. Turned. Stood in the doorway.

The door was open. It was always open now; Renata had started leaving it open years ago, another unlocked door. The bed was made. The window was closed. On the desk, fossilized under dust, sat a half-finished model airplane and a stack of records and a pencil worn down to a stub, and Theo stood there and let the radiator in the corner speak—it had spoken to her weeks ago, the first night, the slam of a door and rain and a boy's laugh cut off mid-note—but tonight it said nothing new. Tonight it was just a radiator in an empty room, and the record it kept was already in her, transcribed, memorized, ready.

Two voices arguing downstairs, low and fast. A car door. Footsteps going down, never coming up. Rain. And underneath all of it, the thing she'd refused to hear for thirty years because hearing it meant the question existed: her father's engine starting, and not one set of footsteps returning, but two.

He left. He chose it. Dad drove him. And Renata read the proof and folded it shut like a letter, like a wound, like a door.

Theo went into her own small room and lay down on the made-up bed in her clothes and stared at the ceiling, and somewhere below her Renata moved through the house turning off lights, saving the porch for last, saving it always, and Theo understood that she was listening to the last night of a thirty-year arrangement, and that tomorrow it would end one way or another, and that she was not afraid.

That was the strange part. She kept waiting for the fear and it didn't come. What came instead was clarity, cold and clean as a struck tuning fork.

Because here was what she knew, lying in the dark in the house that raised her and erased her brother: the appraiser had valued the upright at four hundred dollars. Four hundred dollars for the piano her father bought used the year she was born, the piano Milo pounded out chopsticks on, the piano she had learned to hear buildings through. The appraiser wasn't wrong. That was the thing. He was accurate. Objects were worth what strangers would pay, and strangers would pay four hundred dollars, and the house was worth what strangers would pay, and the family was worth—

Nothing. To strangers. Everything to the people willing to pay the other price. The price Renata had paid daily for thirty years. The price Ambrose paid in rehearsed sentences. The price Dee paid in swallowed sentences. The price Milo had paid, seventeen years old and gone forever, and the price she herself had paid in invisibility, in the smile that ends conversations, in thirty years of being furniture in rooms where she should have been a voice.

The house was never the inheritance.

She had come back to Corliss Street six weeks ago to collect her things quietly, and instead she had collected the truth, piece by piece, ledger page by carbon copy by neighbor's testimony by uncle's confession, and the truth was the estate, and the truth was divisible among them all, and unlike the house it could not be sold out from under them, only spent or hoarded.

Tomorrow, under a white tent, with a gold-blazered man holding a gavel over her childhood, she would spend it. All of it. Out loud. In front of the neighbors, the bidders, the strangers with their numbered paddles. She would say: my brother was not taken, my brother left, my father drove him, my aunt read the letter, and we buried a living boy's question under a dead man's name, and we called it love, and it was not love, it was accounting.

Some of them would never forgive her. Renata might walk out. The name Sandoval might become, in this neighborhood, a synonym for a kind of mess. The house would go to a stranger who would never know what the radiators held, and that was right, that was correct pricing, because the house's true value had never been in the lot size or the millwork. It had been in the people, and the people were broken, and broken things cannot be auctioned, only mended.

Downstairs, the porch light clicked off. Then, after a moment, on again. Renata, at the window, keeping the vigil one final night.

Theo closed her eyes. In the walls, the pipes settled and sighed. Somewhere two states away, a man with her father's hands was probably asleep above a workshop, unaware that tomorrow his name would be said aloud in a tent full of strangers, that his existence would stop being a secret and start being a fact, and that facts, unlike secrets, can travel.

She slept better than she had in thirty years.

She woke at dawn to the smell of coffee cake and the sound, unmistakable, of the parlor upright being played—badly, hesitantly, note by note like a man relearning a language—and she lay still in her childhood bed and listened to Renata, of all people, picking out with one finger, slowly, the opening bars of the song Milo used to pound out on summer evenings while the sprinklers ticked across the lawn.

The tents were going up on the lawn.
