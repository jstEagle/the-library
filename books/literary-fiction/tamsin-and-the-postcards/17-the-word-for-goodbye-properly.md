## 17. The Word for Goodbye Properly

The first morning of the three days, the light came through my kitchen window the way it does in late autumn — thin, sideways, like a letter opened by someone else. I had been awake since before dawn, listening to the viaduct breathe above the cottage. It has a voice in cold weather. The iron contracts and speaks in small complaints, and I have heard it every morning for sixty years, and in sixty more mornings it will not be there to speak. This is what I was thinking when I put the kettle on: that even the things we hate to hear are company.

Casper arrived first, as he always did, walking up from the site office with his instrument case held against his chest like a hymnbook. Tamsin came ten minutes behind him, having driven over from the inn with her notebook already open on the passenger seat — I could see her mouthing yesterday's words at the windshield as she parked, practicing, unashamed. They had begun to arrive like this, staggered but converging, two streams finding the same channel. I had watched marriages for eighty years, most of them from the outside, and I will tell you what I saw forming in mine kitchen: not repair. Something better than repair. Two people learning, at the same slow speed, a language neither could use to lie to the other yet.

I made them tea they did not want and bread they did, and then I said, "Today we begin the hardest thing I own."

Tamsin set down her pen. Casper went very still, the way engineers go still — not frightened, exactly, but recalibrating, measuring whether the ground under him had shifted.

"It is not a word," I said. "It is a phrase, and it is long, and it is built of parts you already have. But before I teach it, you must understand what it is for, because if you learn it as sounds only, it will be dead in your mouth, and I refuse to hand you dead things."

* * *

Kessari has words for leaving the way the sea has kinds of water. There is the word for going out and meaning to come back. There is the word for being taken — used for the drowned, for the conscripted, for the children put on lorries in the Relocation Years, though the children were never given even that word; they were given nothing, which is its own entry in the grammar, a silence with rules. There is the word for sending someone off with anger still warm between you. And there is the phrase.

I wrote nothing on the board. Kessari has no board. I said it aloud, slowly, the whole of it, all eleven of its movements, and I watched their faces do what faces do — first the pleasure of hearing something beautiful, then the confusion of not understanding, then the beginning of hunger.

"What does it mean?" Tamsin asked, and her voice had gone quiet in the way people's voices go quiet in churches they don't belong to.

"Closest in your English," I said, "is this: *saying farewell so completely that the leaving cannot undo it.*" I let the English sit there, clumsy as it was. "But that is a translation, and translations are photographs of birds. The phrase means that you say goodbye with such completeness — every debt named, every forgiveness given, every love spoken plainly — that afterward, no matter how the parting happens, no matter how sudden or how cruel or how many years of silence follow, the leaving cannot reach back and take away what was said. The farewell stands. It holds its ground. Like a wall built before the flood."

I looked at Casper when I said *wall*. He looked at his tea. That man builds walls for a living and cannot yet build one inside himself. Patience, I told myself. I had waited forty years to say this phrase to anyone. I could wait four months for these two.

"You have never said it," Tamsin said. She is quick in the way that costs her. "Not once."

"No," I said.

"To anyone?"

"Not to anyone."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Outside, a gull argued with the wind about something neither would win.

"My husband," I said, because they had earned the story by now, or because I had finally earned the telling, I no longer distinguish, "went out on the water on a Tuesday. Ordinary Tuesday. We had quarreled the night before — about money, about nothing, about the usual small cruelties couples sharpen on each other when they are young and certain there will be time. In the morning he stood in that doorway" — I pointed, and my finger was steady, I made it be steady — "and he said, 'Tonight, then.' And I said, 'If you say so,' which is not a farewell at all, it is a postponement wearing a farewell's coat. And the sea took him at four o'clock, and the last thing I ever gave him was *if you say so*."

I poured myself more tea I did not want, so that my hands would have employment.

"The phrase existed. My grandmother used it when her sister left for the mainland, and I heard it, I knew it, I could have said it in that doorway in the time it takes to blink. But you must understand — saying it means finishing. It means you hold nothing back for later, you spend everything, and I was a young woman who believed in saving. Save the big words for the big occasion. Keep a little in reserve. And so I kept, and kept, and the sea does not respect reserves." I drank, though the tea had gone bitter. "Forty years I have owed a dead man one sentence."

"And the evacuated children," Casper said quietly. He had read the file, of course. Engineers read files. "In the Relocation Years. You didn't—"

"There were two hundred and six children put on those lorries," I said. "I was seventeen. I stood at the road with the mothers and I said to each child some small thing — *be good, write to us, mind the aunties* — small things, pocket things, because the soldiers were counting us and hurrying us and because — here is the shame, boy, look at me while I carry it — because I did not believe it was real. I thought they would be back by harvest. Everyone thought it. Saying the great phrase felt like admitting the great loss, and to admit it felt like helping it happen. So we all said small words, hundreds of small words, and the lorries drove away, and of the two hundred and six, eleven ever came back, and of the eleven, none remembered enough Kessari to say goodbye properly ever again." I set down the cup. "So no. Not them either. The phrase has sat in my mouth for sixty years like a stone I refused to swallow and refused to spit out."

Tamsin was crying without noise. I noticed that she does that — grieves silently, tidily, as if tears were laundry to be done discreetly. Her brother, I thought. There is a brother-shaped absence in that woman; I had seen her touch the postcard in her bag twice that week without taking it fully out. Later, I decided. Later I would make her spend that grief where it could be witnessed. A language cannot live in people who grieve privately forever.

"But you," I said, and I stood, and I went to the wall — the great papered wall, forty years of postcards, pictures of lighthouses and boats and hills sent to no postal service, addressed in ink to the drowned and the scattered and the dead — "you should know why I am teaching it to you before I teach it to you. Because it is not a lesson. It is an inheritance, and inheritances come with conditions."

They both looked up at me. Good students already, sitting straighter.

"I am seventy-nine years old," I said. "My heart is an old pump and my lungs have begun keeping secrets from me — you will both pretend you haven't noticed the cough, so I will pretend you haven't pretended. I will die soon. Not today, probably not this winter, but soon, and sooner than the viaduct, perhaps, or perhaps not, the viaduct and I are racing now, did you know that? Your bridge and my body, running toward the same finish line." Casper flinched. I let him. "When I go, this phrase goes with me unless it goes into you. And I find — I have found, these weeks, watching you two circle each other like careful weather — that I do not want it to die in me unused. I want to say it once. Correctly. To someone living, who can hear it, who can answer. Before I die. That is the condition. You will help me say it, and in learning to help me, you will learn it yourselves, and one day — not soon, I hope, but one day — one of you will stand in a doorway or at a bedside or at a grave, and you will say it, and somewhere in the ground or the sea or whatever country the dead keep, an old woman will feel a debt close at last."

* * *

That afternoon we began the parts.

The phrase is built like the viaduct, I told Casper, and he understood me instantly, which pleased me more than I showed. It has piers — three of them — and arches between, and the whole weight rests on the first word, which is the word for *I*, but not the ordinary *I*. A ceremonial *I*. The *I* that appears only in oaths and farewells, the *I* that cannot be used for lying, because the grammar itself refuses it. Say that word falsely and the sentence collapses; the elders said a liar's mouth could not shape it, and whether or not that is literally true, I have watched liars try, and their mouths do fumble it, like hands too large for small keys.

We worked on that single word for two hours. Casper's tongue kept retreating from the second consonant — he has a history in his mouth, I had noticed it the first week, a hesitation worn smooth by decades of swallowing — and Tamsin got it faster and then overcorrected into something that sounded like a sneeze, and for the first time in three days neither of them laughed, because they understood we were not playing anymore.

The second pier is the naming. You must name the person wholly — not the name they answer to but the name underneath, the name for who they were to you. Kessari has a whole declension for this: the-name-of-my-love, the-name-of-my-debt, the-name-of-my-wound, the-name-of-my-teacher. I taught them the forms and watched them each secretly try the form for spouse and then look away from each other, quickly, like people caught touching a hot rail. Sixty years of marriage in that glance, and twelve years of silence, and I said nothing, because a teacher knows when the grammar is doing the teaching for her.

The third pier is the longest and the worst. It is the accounting. You name what was good between you, and what was owed, and what was broken, and you forgive or ask forgiveness for each item aloud, in order, without abbreviation. The elders allowed three nights for this section when it was done fully. Three nights! Imagine — imagine standing in a lit kitchen with someone and going through it all, item by item, the ledger of a whole love, spoken until the speaking is finished. No wonder the phrase takes days just to approach. It is not difficult because the sounds are difficult. It is difficult because it is honest.

By evening they could manage the first pier and half the second, haltingly, and I sent them out into the blue dark with instructions to sleep on the sounds and nothing else. From my window I watched them walk down to the inn, and halfway there they stopped under the last working lamp in the village street, and they faced each other, and they talked — I could not hear the words, but I could see the architecture of the talk, the way Tamsin's shoulders dropped and Casper's hands moved, actually moved, describing something in the air. Then they walked on, closer together than they had been.

I stood at my window a long time, among my postcards, with my hand flat on the wall as if it were an animal I was calming.

* * *

The second day, the cough came at dawn and stayed till noon, and I taught sitting down, which I hated, and they pretended not to notice, which I hated more, and we got the rest of the second pier and began the third.

The third pier broke Casper.

Not visibly. He is a man whose breaking happens below the waterline, like ice on a pier — you see only the crack lines later, in the map of them. We were practicing the forgiveness formulas, the call-and-response of it, and I had him repeat after me: *what was broken between us, I name it; what was broken between us, I carry it no longer.* Simple words, learner's words, practice words with no one attached to them. And he said them fine, twice, three times, and on the fourth repetition his voice simply stopped, mid-word, like a clock running into a wall, and he stood there in my kitchen with his jaw working and nothing coming out, and Tamsin reached across the table and put her hand on his wrist and did not say anything either, and the two of them sat in that silence like people sitting in a house during a quake, waiting to learn what had fallen.

I gave them tea and let the moment be as large as it needed to be. I am old; I know that the moments you talk through are smaller than the moments you sit inside.

Later, when Tamsin had gone to fetch bread from the van, Casper said to me, not looking at me, looking at the wall of postcards: "There are things I've never said. To her. To anyone. Things I decided were — safer unsaid. And I'm starting to think the deciding is the damage."

"Yes," I said.

"That's all? Yes?"

"What would you like me to add? That it can be fixed by Thursday?" I let him sit in it. "Boy. Listen to me. I have been silent for sixty years, and I know every room in that house. Silence starts as shelter and ends as architecture. You build it room by room, each one reasonable, each one necessary at the time, and one day you look up and you are living in a fortress with no door you remember how to open. The phrase I am teaching you — the whole of it — is a door. It is the biggest door there is. And doors are terrible things to build and wonderful things to have."

He nodded. His eyes were wet and he let them be wet for exactly four seconds and then he was an inspector again, dry, calibrated. But the four seconds counted. With men like him, the four seconds are the whole harvest.

* * *

The third day, I almost said it.

This is the truth I will set down here, in the privacy of my own telling: on the third day, with the light coming gold through the window and both my students able to carry the first two piers without dropping them, I stood in my kitchen and I began the phrase — the whole phrase, the true one, addressed across forty years and one sea to a man named Elio Oduya who has been dead since he was thirty-one — and I got as far as the naming. *Elio, name-of-my-love, name-of-my-debt.* And my voice went out from under me like a floorboard. Not grief, exactly. Or yes, grief, but grief with a strange floor to it, because underneath the grief was this: if I say it and finish it, then it is done. Then there is no longer a debt outstanding between me and the dead. And I discovered, standing there with my hand over my mouth, that some part of me had been living on that debt. That the owing had been a kind of company. That a woman can hold a sentence unfinished for forty years the way you hold a door open for someone who is never coming, because while the door is open, the waiting has a shape.

Tamsin took my elbow. Casper turned his chair to face me. Neither spoke English at me, thank God. Tamsin said, badly, beautifully, the learner's version of the phrase's first pier — *I, the truthful I, am here* — and Casper said the second, the naming, using the form for teacher, *name-of-my-teacher*, and pronounced it nearly right.

And I laughed, or sobbed, it was the same sound, and I said, "You see? You see what it does? I taught you three pieces of wood and you have already built me a chair to fall into."

We sat in the gold light and I finished my tea and told them the rest of what they needed to know: that the phrase must be said aloud, never written — writing it makes it a monument, and monuments are where languages go to be visited instead of lived in; that it must be said to a face, living or remembered, but a face; and that when it is done correctly, the old ones said, you feel the leaving happen and pass through you and fail to take anything, like water failing to move a wall.

"When will you say it?" Tamsin asked. "Fully. To him."

I looked at the wall. Two hundred postcards, every one a small boat launched at the drowned. Somewhere in that paper sea, Elio's face on a card from 1961, a carnival, him laughing with his hat tipped back.

"Soon," I said. "Before the bridge comes down. I want the village to hear it. I want the stones to hear it — they carried the language longer than the people did, hiding it in their singing when the wind is right. And I want—" I stopped, because the next part was the true part and the true part was enormous. I said it anyway. Old women get to. "I want to find out if it works. If saying it completely really does make a leaving unable to undo itself. Because I have one more leaving coming, mine, and I intend to test the grammar against it."

Neither of them laughed. Good. It was not entirely a joke.

That night, after they had gone, I stood at the window and listened to the viaduct complaining in the cold, and I thought about debts, and doors, and the strange arithmetic by which a woman waits her whole life to say one sentence and then chooses two strangers to say it in front of. And I thought, too — I confess it — about the way Casper had gone silent on the fourth repetition, and the things stacked behind that silence, rooms and rooms of them, and how a man who inspects structures for cracks had never once inspected the great cracked structure of himself.

Something was going to give in that man soon. I had watched enough load-bearing silence in my life to know the sound it makes just before.

I hoped, when it gave, he would be standing near someone who knew how to listen.
