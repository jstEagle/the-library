## 20. The Archive People

They arrived on a Tuesday, in a grey estate car with university plates and the particular confidence of people who had never once been the last of anything. There were three of them: a Dr. Anselm Voss, who introduced himself as the principal investigator with a handshake calibrated to convey both warmth and hierarchy; a younger woman named Priya whose eyes went straight past Casper to the cottage as though it were already a filing cabinet; and a doctoral student who said almost nothing for two days and then, at the end, wept in the lane where no one could see him.

Casper met them at the site office because Maren refused to meet them anywhere else.

"Let them come to me," she'd said that morning, pouring tea she would not drink. "I have been visited by surveyors, by priests, by men with clipboards asking how many rooms. Let this be no different. They will sit where I put them."

She put them in her kitchen, which was to say she put them inside the archive itself — surrounded by forty years of postcards papering the walls like weather, like lichen, like the scales of some enormous patient fish. Voss set up his equipment on her table without asking: a recorder, a laptop, a shotgun microphone in a foam windscreen that looked, absurdly, like something meant to catch snowflakes. Priya arranged word lists in plastic sleeves. The doctoral student opened a notebook to a page already dense with IPA symbols, pre-filled, as if Kessari were a form to be completed rather than a thing about to happen.

Maren watched all of this from her chair by the window with an expression Casper was beginning to know well — the stillness of deep water, the surface unbroken while everything underneath moved.

"Begin," she said.

And they did.

* * *

What followed was three days of the most meticulous extraction Casper had ever witnessed, and he had spent his career watching men take structures apart bolt by bolt.

Voss worked from elicitation protocols Casper recognized in shape if not content — the same logic he applied to concrete cores and ultrasonic readings. Isolate the element. Test it under controlled conditions. Record the response. *Say the word for water. Now say it as if the water is far away. Now say it as if you are angry at the water.* Priya fed Maren color chips and asked her to name them; fed her photographs of animals, tools, weather; read her sentences in English and waited for translation with her pen hovering like a hummingbird over a flower it intended to kill.

Maren performed. That was the only word for it, and Casper understood performance — he had been performing competence for twenty years, giving the firm exactly the readings it required, answering questions with the portion of truth that kept everything load-bearing intact. She gave them clean vowels. She gave them paradigms, conjugations, the numbers one through twenty twice so they could verify consistency. She pronounced each item into the microphone with the clarity of a woman reading names off a memorial wall, and when they thanked her, she inclined her head the way she might toward rain.

But Casper sat in the corner those three days, ostensibly reviewing vibration data, actually watching, and he saw what the machines could not.

He saw that she never once used a full sentence unless they forced her to. He saw that when Priya held up the photograph of a fishing boat and asked for the word, Maren gave the word — *sennet*, or something near it — but not the story of the boat her husband had built with his brother before the brother was relocated inland and died of a sadness no one wrote down. He saw that when Voss asked her to produce the imperative mood, she produced it flawlessly and then looked out the window at the viaduct for a long moment, and Voss, following nothing, said, "Again, please, slightly slower," and she gave it to him again, slightly slower, a coin from an endless purse.

"They are very good at what they do," she told Casper that evening, after the estate car had gone back down the coast road to its hotel in the county town. Tamsin was washing dishes; the lesson hour had been cancelled, officially, because the archive people had requested evening sessions too, and Maren had declined them with a courtesy that closed like a door.

"You don't like them," Casper said.

"I like them fine." She turned her teacup a quarter-turn on its saucer, a habit he'd learned to read like a gauge needle. "The girl is careful. The boy loves the sounds — you can tell, he closes his eyes. Dr. Voss has published eleven papers on dying languages and will publish a twelfth." A pause. "They are doing necessary work. Someone must keep the bones."

"But?"

"But." She looked at him directly then, and Casper felt the look go through him the way his own inspection lamps went through concrete — locating the void behind the surface. "You have heard them work. Tell me what they never ask."

He thought about it honestly. It bothered him, how long it took.

"They never ask you to say anything to anyone," he said finally. "It's all — words at the microphone. Nothing addressed."

Maren smiled, and it was not entirely a kind smile. "Twelve years of marriage," she said, "and you learned to listen in four months. Do not tell your wife I said you learn slowly; she thinks you learn quickly, and a marriage needs its generous errors."

From the kitchen came the sound of Tamsin's hands stopping in the water.

* * *

On the third day, Voss made his error.

It was small. It was even reasonable. That was what Casper would think later — that the machinery of loss rarely requires villains, only competent people following protocols. Voss had been working through kinship terminology since ten that morning: mother's brother, father's sister, the words for cousin sorted by side and distance. At half past two he said, gently, professionally, "Now, if we could do the terms for deceased relatives — there's often interesting variation there. Your husband, for instance. How would you address him now?"

The kitchen went quiet in a way Casper felt in his teeth, the way he felt certain frequencies in the viaduct deck — not heard, registered.

Maren looked at the microphone. Then at Voss. Then, briefly, at Casper, and something passed between them that he could not yet translate but would spend years translating afterward.

"When my husband speaks to me," she said, "it is not for a file."

"I understand, of course, but if—"

"No," Maren said. Not unkindly. With the finality of a woman setting down a heavy object precisely where it belongs. "You have had three days. You have my numbers, my colors, my dead husband's boat reduced to a vocabulary item. Take these things to your university. Put them in your cold room with the other languages, the ones in drawers." She rose from her chair — slowly, her hand on the table, the cough stirring somewhere beneath her voice like sediment — and stood at her full height, which had never been great and was lessening, but which filled the room anyway. "I am going to teach now. You may stay and listen if you can bear to listen to something that is not for you."

Voss opened his mouth. Priya's pen hovered. And then — and Casper would remember this with a respect that surprised him — the doctoral student closed his notebook, and closed his eyes, and folded his hands, and became, visibly, simply a young man in a kitchen waiting to hear something true.

Voss packed up within the hour. He shook Casper's hand at the gate and said, "She's remarkable, of course. Difficult subjects make the best data — no offense intended." Casper said none was taken, and hated himself mildly for the smoothness of it, and watched the estate car diminish down the coast road with its cargo of vowels and color chips and kinship terms, the skeleton of Kessari bound for a climate-controlled drawer.

Priya lingered. She stood in the lane holding her plastic sleeves against her chest and said, quietly, to Casper: "For what it's worth — I've digitized nine last speakers. She's the first one who made me feel like a thief instead of a nurse." Then she got in the car, and the sea took the sound of the engine the way the sea took everything eventually.

* * *

That afternoon, unprompted, unpaid, Maren taught.

She taught the way water finds the low places. No protocol, no word lists. She began with the phrase for *the smell of bread in a house where someone is expected home* — a phrase, she explained, that took four words in Kessari and could not be shortened without lying — and she made Tamsin say it until the vowels stopped being English wearing a costume. She taught the difference between two words for *waiting*: the waiting that trusts, and the waiting that has given up pretending. She made Casper distinguish them aloud, and he failed six times, and on the seventh attempt she said, "There. That one cost you something. Keep that one."

Tamsin transcribed in her notebook, the pages filling now with a handwriting Casper hadn't seen since their early years — quick, slanted, alive, the writing of someone in a hurry to hold onto something. He watched her mouth shape syllables silently before committing them to paper, testing each one like a weight-bearing stone, and he thought: *she is better at this than I am,* and found, to his astonishment, that the thought produced not envy but a kind of gratitude so large it frightened him.

When the light began to fail, Maren dismissed them to make tea, and Casper stayed a moment in the doorway, watching her at the window with the postcards behind her — decades of messages to the dead, the whole smuggled country of a banned language pinned to plaster.

"Why us?" he asked. "Why teach us? We're nobody. We'll speak it badly our whole lives."

Maren didn't turn around. Outside, the viaduct stood against the darkening sky, its arches black, its fate signed and scheduled and sitting under a teacup somewhere in this house in another form.

"The doctor wants the language," she said. "Wants it the way a man wants a specimen — to keep it safe from living. Safe means dead, in his science. Dead things keep beautifully." She turned then, and the lamplight caught the lines of her face the way low sun catches the grain of old timber. "But you — you need it. Needing is different. Wanting fills a shelf. Needing fills a silence." Her eyes went past him, toward the kitchen, toward the sound of Tamsin filling the kettle. "I have watched you two stand in a room together like two halves of a bridge that forgot it was holding anything. Four months you have been learning, and already you say more to each other in my kitchen than I imagine you have said in years. Do not argue. I am old; I am permitted to be rude."

Casper said nothing. There was nothing to say that wasn't already said, and saying it aloud here, in this kitchen, would have been like pronouncing a diagnosis over a patient still breathing.

"The language does not live in archives," Maren went on, softer now. "Archives are where languages go to be embalmed. It lives in mouths. In mistakes. In a man greeting his wife badly every morning until the greeting becomes theirs, mispronounced, private, alive." She picked up her teacup and found it cold and set it down without complaint, the way she accepted most things. "So. Tomorrow we begin the grammar of grief. It is the largest part of the language. It will take longer than we have, which is the correct amount of time for anything worth learning."

Casper walked home along the base of the viaduct, the way he always did, listening to it groan its evening settlement above him. But tonight the sound seemed changed — or he had changed, which amounted to the same thing. He thought about Voss's cold room, the drawers, the beautiful dead. He thought about the doctoral student with his eyes closed, hands folded, receiving.

At the inn, Tamsin was already at the table with her notebook open, practicing under her breath, and she looked up when he came in, and — this was new, this was four-months new — she didn't stop. She just kept saying the phrase, the one about bread and expectation, getting it wrong, getting it wrong, getting it closer, while he hung up his coat and sat down across from her and let himself be expected.

In the morning, when they reached the cottage, Maren was standing in her garden looking at a letter she held at arm's length, and her face wore an expression Casper had never seen on her: not grief, not patience, but something older and more private, like a woman reading her own name on a door she'd believed bricked over.

"Well," she said, folding the letter with hands that trembled only slightly, and handing it to him as though passing across a border. "It seems someone has been reading my file. The council writes to say my relocation interview has been moved forward." A pause, and then, dry as driftwood: "Bring your notebook tomorrow. It appears we will be skipping ahead."
