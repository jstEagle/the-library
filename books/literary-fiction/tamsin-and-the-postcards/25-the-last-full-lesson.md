## 25. The Last Full Lesson

I woke before the gulls did, which is the sea's way of telling you the day will be short.

The light through the east window was the color of weak tea, and the viaduct stood over the village like a rib bone picked clean, holding up nothing but sky. Forty years I have lived under it. When I was a girl there was no bridge, only the path down to the water and the smell of the herring boats, and my mother's voice calling me home in Kessari, that language like water going over stones. Now the bridge is the loudest thing in my life and my mother's language lives in my mouth alone, and I have come to understand that these two facts are related, though I could not say how, except that everything loud eventually silences everything quiet.

My chest was wet again in the night. I know what wet means. I have buried enough people to know the order in which a body gives up its rooms.

So. No more curriculum.

* * *

They came at nine, as they always did, Casper first with his notebooks squared to the table's edge, Tamsin after him with her thermos and her fragments — the little book she carries now everywhere, filled with the half-words of the village, old Peder's lullaby line, the prayer the Henderson woman could only begin. They sat in their chairs, the two of them, and waited for me to begin, and I looked at them for a long time, the way you look at a fire you are about to leave unattended.

"No words today," I said. "No lists. No grammar." Casper's face did the thing his face does — the small professional alarm of a man whose structure is being altered without a permit. I raised my hand before he could speak. "Today I teach you what the language is *for*. Everything else was scaffolding."

I made them move the table against the wall. I made them bring the chairs into the middle of the room, facing the postcard wall — forty years of cards in a script no post office ever honored, my mother and my husband and the children of the Relocation Years all pinned there in their colors, faded now to the shades of old bruises. I sat facing them, because a teacher facing a wall is a coward, and I am done being a coward about the direction of my words.

"The first phrase," I said, "is the first phrase. Every Kessari child heard it before they heard their own name."

I gave it to them slowly, the way you give bread to someone who has been starving — not all at once, because a starved stomach will cramp.

"*Sulaneth kei.*" Birth-greeting. It does not mean *hello*. It means: *you have arrived, and the arriving is good, and we were made larger by it.* Literally — and I made them write the literal meaning, Casper with his phonetic spiderwork, Tamsin in her careful schoolgirl hand — it means *the door of you opened toward us.*

"Now say it to each other."

They looked at each other the way married people look at each other when asked to do something tender in public — a look I have seen on a thousand faces in this village, in the old days, in the church hall, at the herring dances. Twelve years, these two, and they had to reach across a distance the length of the room.

"*Sulaneth kei,*" Casper said to his wife, and his accent broke on the second syllable, and his ears went red, and Tamsin answered, "*Sulaneth kei,*" and her voice came out lower than she intended, and I pretended to be adjusting my shawl so they could have their moment unobserved.

A language is not vocabulary. I have told the university people this until they nodded at me with their recording machines nodding too. A language is a set of doors. English has doors, good heavy doors, but some rooms it has never built. Kessari built the rooms first and the words after, the way a house grows.

* * *

The second phrase took until the light had moved off the tea chest and onto the floor.

Apology. In Kessari there is no word that means only *I am sorry* — that thin coin English passes back and forth until it wears smooth and buys nothing. The Kessari apology is a sentence with a load-bearing middle. "*Nai voru keth-ala.*" — *I carried the harm, and here is my hands, open, so you may see I still carry it.*

"Say it," I told Casper, "and mean one thing you have not said."

I watched him. I have spent a winter watching this man, and I know his silences the way a fisherman knows weather — which silences are rest and which are storms held offshore. He opened his mouth. He closed it. Tamsin sat very still beside him, still the way water is still before it goes over a ledge.

"*Nai voru keth-ala,*" he said at last, and did not look at his wife, and looked at his wife, both at once, the way you can only do with a person you have practiced not-seeing for years.

Tamsin's hand moved on her knee. Half an inch. I have watched that hand for months; it is a hand that drafts letters it does not send. It moved toward him and stopped, and stopped was enough, because the phrase does not require forgiveness in the moment. It requires only that the carrying be witnessed.

"And you," I said to Tamsin.

She said it. Her Kessari is better than his — this unsettles them both, and I have decided not to soothe that, because the unsettledness is doing work. Her vowels are true. When she says *keth-ala* the word opens like a hand, and I thought: this one could have been raised on it. This one was robbed by an accident of geography, the way all the children after the Relocation Years were robbed, by distance and by decree.

"Again," I said. "Both. Until the middle of the sentence stops shaking."

Three times. Four. By the sixth, the words had stopped being foreign shapes and started being weights they were passing back and forth, and the room got very quiet in the way rooms get quiet when something true is happening in them, and I let the quiet stand as long as it wanted to, because quiet is not silence. I have spent thirty years learning the difference. Silence is a wall. Quiet is a held breath, and held breath is waiting.

* * *

At midday I fed them fish soup because a grieving lesson on an empty stomach curdles, and while they ate I took down the teacup and showed them what was under it — the demolition notice, soft as cloth now from handling, the date printed on it in a language that will bury this village. Casper looked at the date and then at me, and there was something in his face I had seen once before and not understood, and I understood it now. He knew something about that date. He had known it for some time. The knowledge was sitting behind his eyes like a stone behind a window.

I did not ask. Not because I didn't want to know — I wanted to know the way the drowning want air — but because he would have to carry it to me himself or it would not be a gift, and I am old enough to know that some confessions must be left to ripen or they come off the branch green and sour. Also, my chest hurt, and I am rationing my hours like a woman rationing candles.

"Forgiveness," I said instead, when the bowls were empty. "The third phrase."

This one I taught standing up, because in the old days it was said standing, at doorways, between people who had wronged each other past the reach of apology. "*Orennai sula.*" — *I release you from the debt of my hurt.* Not *I forgive you* — English forgiveness is a throne you sit on while the other person climbs the stairs. *Orennai sula* puts the two of you on the same ground. It says: the debt existed, I am not pretending it didn't, and I am setting it down, and we will both stand straighter without it.

I made them say it to the room. To the wall of cards. To the dead, who are owed it most and collect least.

Tamsin said it to the wall and her voice caught on her brother — I have seen the card she never answered; I have seen it in the way her eyes go to a certain height on a wall, the height where a postcard would hang. Casper said it to the window, to the north, to some father-shaped weather, and his jaw did the thing jaws do when a man is holding a door shut against what is behind it.

"Again," I said, though they hated me for it, and they said it again, and the third time it came out of them not as language but as fact, and I sat down because my legs were tired, and I thought: *there. That is the sound of a debt being set down. I had almost forgotten it. The room had almost forgotten it.*

* * *

The afternoon went long and gold. The viaduct ticked and settled overhead the way old houses do, and once the wind came through the arches and made a sound I have been hearing all my life without naming, and I thought of the story I would tell them soon, the story of the bridge that sang, but not today. Today there was one phrase left, and it is a long road, and I would not begin it with the light already leaning west.

But I could begin it.

"Sit," I said. "The last thing is not a phrase. It is a country. The phrase lives inside it the way a harbor lives inside a coast."

I told them about the long goodbye. *Sulaveth anna* — no. That is only its name, its street address. The thing itself takes a night and a day to say properly, and I told them what my grandmother told me: that you say farewell to a person so completely, so piece by piece — their hands, their anger, their laugh, the specific way they stirred the fire — that when the leaving comes, it finds nothing left to take. The leaving cannot undo what the saying has already finished. "We say it to the dying," I said. "We said it to the boats that didn't come back. I never said it. Not once. Not to Tomas before the water, not to the children on the lorries in the Relocation Years. I have been saving it for sixty years like a dress for an occasion, and the occasion is coming, and I will say it once, correctly, or I will die mid-sentence, and either way you two are going to learn every piece of it."

Casper wrote the first fragment. Tamsin read it back. The sound in the room when they spoke it — that low, tidal sound, vowels like water finding its level — was the sound of my mother's kitchen, of the herring dances, of the world before the decree, and I had to put my hand flat on the table to keep myself steady, because a language does not care how long it has been silent. It comes back through whatever mouths will hold it, the way water comes back through any crack.

* * *

The light was nearly gone when I did the last thing.

I had them stand. I stood too, though the standing cost me, and I put my hand on the postcard wall — on my mother's card, the blue one, the sea on it still blue after all these years — and I spoke the naming.

There is a form for it. There is a form for everything; that is what people misunderstand about old languages, they think we were vague, we who had seventeen words for the state of the tide and a tense for the indefinitely postponed. The naming is a form. It is done in front of witnesses, or it is not done.

"Casper Fairweather," I said, in Kessari, and then in English so there could be no mistake, because a naming that needs translating is a naming half-done. "Tamsin Fairweather. I have taught you the words for arriving, for carrying harm, for setting down debt, and I have begun to teach you the long farewell. I name you speakers. Not students — the students of Kessari are all dead, and I am done teaching students. Speakers. The language is not in my mouth anymore. It is in yours. From tonight, when it is spoken badly in your house, it is still spoken, and a language spoken badly is alive, and a language kept perfectly in an archive is a dead thing in a glass case, and I did not keep myself alive all these years to become a glass case."

My voice went out on the last words. I sat. Tamsin was crying without any noise at all, which is the English way, and Casper stood with his hands at his sides like a man at attention, and then he did something I did not expect. He said the birth-greeting. To me. "*Sulaneth kei.*" — *you have arrived, and the arriving is good.* To a dying woman, at the end of a day of endings. Badly pronounced. Perfectly meant.

I laughed until my chest complained, and then I let him hold my hand for a moment, this engineer with his measured life, and I thought: *well. The sea gives back more than it takes, sometimes, and always when you've stopped watching the water.*

* * *

They washed the bowls and left near dark, and I stood in the doorway — the threshold, *dorath-kei*, the first word I ever taught them — and watched them go down the lane together, two small figures under the great bone of the bridge, and I said the long goodbye's opening line to their backs, quietly, just to feel its weight in my mouth, just to practice.

Then I went in and took the demolition notice from under the teacup and read the date again, and this time I did not put it back. I set it on the table where they would see it, where he would see it, because I am a speaker's teacher now and speakers do not let the people they love carry stones behind windows.

Let him explain himself tomorrow. We have so little time, and I have decided I will spend none of it pretending.
