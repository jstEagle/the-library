## 31. Three Days

They came into the kitchen together, the way people do when they have agreed beforehand on the shape of the news, and I knew before Casper opened his mouth. I have watched faces carry bad tidings for ninety-one years. The face arranges itself first — the jaw set a little too carefully, the eyes doing their work of softening — and the words arrive late, like guests who have been rehearsing their apology on the doorstep.

Casper stood with his hands in his pockets, which he does when he is ashamed of something, and Tamsin stood slightly behind him with one hand on his shoulder blade, which she does when she is ashamed of him and fond of him in the same breath. The lamp was lit because the morning was grey, and the grey came through the window and lay on the table like a cloth someone had forgotten to remove.

"The numbers came back," he said. "From the night inspection. The wind found a frequency in the deck, and it matched — well. It matched what you told us. The singing."

I poured the tea. There is always time for tea. The dead are patient and the living are impatient and tea confuses both of them beautifully.

"The window's shorter," he went on. "Three days. Maybe less if the weather turns. They're moving the demolition to Friday. I had to tell you, Maren. I should have told you the moment —"

I laughed.

I could not help it. It came up out of me like water finding a crack in a wall, and it was not a polite laugh and not a frightened one, and I saw them both flinch as though I had begun speaking in tongues, which in a way I had. I laughed until my cough came, and Tamsin moved toward me with her hand out, and I waved her off with the teapot still in my other hand, which was reckless, but I have been reckless for three days' notice before and I survived it, though not in the way anyone planned.

"Oh, my dears," I said, when I could speak. "You looked so frightened. As if you were bringing me a sentence and not a schedule."

"Maren —" Casper began.

"I have been given deadlines my whole life." I set the pot down. "The government gave my mother a deadline when they took the school. The sea gave my husband one, though he never read the letter. The doctors gave me one last spring and I did not bother to attend the appointment. And now the bridge gives me one, and it is the first deadline anyone has ever given me that I respect, because at least the bridge is honest. It does not pretend it is doing this for my own good."

Tamsin sat down across from me. Her hands were folded and her knuckles were pale, and I saw that she had been carrying this news for hours already, that she and Casper had spent the walk from the site office deciding how to say it, which is its own kind of love, the deciding together, even when the deciding is wrong.

"Three days," she said. "Is it enough? For — everything?"

I looked at her for a long moment. This girl who came north in a car full of suspicion and stayed in a cottage full of dead people's postcards and learned a language that has no country. This girl who wept at a lullaby she did not grow up with, who left the room once because a tense was too honest for her, who came back the next morning and asked me to teach it to her again.

"Enough," I said. "You are asking the wrong question. It is the question of people who think a language is a house, that if you do not finish the roof the rain gets in. A language is not a house. It is a fire. You do not need to finish it. You need only to carry it while it burns."

I stood. My legs complained — they complain about everything now, they have become my most fluent critics — but they held, and I went to the wall.

You have not seen the wall, so let me tell you. Forty years of postcards, pinned and pasted and taped, addressed in a script no postal service ever used, to a mother, a husband, a classroom of children scattered across a country that did not want them. To the sea, once, in a fit of anger I have never regretted. The wall is my archive and my graveyard and my garden, all in one, and when I stand before it I stand before everyone I have ever loved, which is a thing most people cannot say of a kitchen wall.

"We skip to the end of the alphabet," I said.

Casper frowned. He is an engineer; he does not like sentences that arrive without their load-bearing walls. "Skip?"

"Kessari is not learned the way your languages are learned, beginning with the names of things and ending with the subjunctive. It was never written, so it was never arranged. It arranges itself by weight. The words a child learns first are the words for grief and greeting, because in the old days a child might need both before supper. So." I turned to face them. "Three days. Then we begin at the end."

* * *

That first day I taught them names.

Not the names of objects — they had those, they had been collecting them for months like children filling pockets with stones. I taught them the names of people. In Kessari, a name is not a label; it is a small history. My name, Maren, carries in it the word for *she who remained*, and my mother chose it in a year when everyone was leaving, and I have spent my whole life growing into it or being grown into it, I am no longer sure which.

"Say my name," I told Casper.

"Maren."

"No. You said it the way you say the numbers on your reports. Again. A name is spoken with the breath of the person who gave it. My mother is in the first syllable. Let her out."

He tried again. He is a careful man, and careful men make slow students, because they will not risk the wrong note and so never find the right one. But he tried again, and the second syllable softened, and something in my chest turned over, the way it does when I hear my own language from a mouth that did not inherit it. It is like watching a stranger carry your mother's chair across a room. The chair is the same. The hands are not. And yet the chair does not complain.

"Better," I said. "Tamsin. Your turn."

She said it perfectly on the first try, which did not surprise me. She has the ear of someone who has spent years listening to a quiet man and hearing everything he does not say. That is a training no conservatory can match.

We worked until the light went. I taught them how a name changes when the person is dead — the final vowel drops, the word shortens, so that the dead are carried in a smaller form, close to the chest, like stones in a pocket. I taught them my husband's name, Aldric, which becomes Aldri', and I said it aloud for the first time in perhaps a decade, and the cottage was very quiet after, and I let it be quiet, because a language needs its silences the way a song needs its rests, and I am the last person alive who knows where the rests go.

* * *

The second day was apologies.

I did not plan it that way. I had planned the sea. But I woke coughing, a wet sound now, a sound with weight to it, and Tamsin was in the kitchen before I had finished, with water and the little towel she keeps folded on the dresser for this purpose, and she looked at me with such fierce, practical tenderness that the lesson changed itself.

"Today," I said, when I could speak, "we learn to say sorry."

"There are words for that in English," Casper said gently.

"There are words for rain in English. That does not mean you know what it is to be drowned." I sat up straighter. I will not teach from a slumped position; dignity is a posture before it is a feeling. "In your language, sorry is a door that closes. You say it, and the matter is shut, and everyone agrees to walk away. In Kessari, sorry is a door that opens. You do not say *I am sorry*. You say —" and I gave them the phrase, *kessa'lin doru* — "*I have left a weight in you, and I ask to help carry it.*"

Tamsin wrote it down. She writes everything down; her notebook is becoming a second wall, and I approve of this more than I can say, because paper is a kind of memory that does not cough.

"Say it to each other," I said.

They looked at each other the way two people look when they have been married twelve years and have a cellar full of unsaid things. I have watched this look before, in other kitchens, in other decades. It is the look of two people deciding whether the flood will come now or never.

"I'll go first," Tamsin said, and her voice was not steady but it was not breaking either, and I understood that she had been practicing this in her head for days, perhaps for years, the way I practiced words against the backs of my teeth for thirty years when the language was banned and speaking it could cost a family its children.

She said it in Kessari, badly. She said it in English after, plainly, and I will not write here what she said, because some things belong to the people who said them and to the walls that heard them. But I will say that Casper did not interrupt her, and did not defend himself, and when she finished he said the Kessari phrase back to her, *kessa'lin doru*, his accent cracking in the middle like ice on a pond, and then he said things in English too, and I sat very still with my tea going cold and felt like a thief of the most precious kind — the kind who steals nothing, only stands in the room where treasure is being given away freely.

This is what I wanted, I thought. Not the archive people with their microphones and their word lists, their hunger for data, their careful, bloodless prompts. This. Two people saying sorry in a kitchen in a language that was never supposed to survive, and meaning it, and the meaning landing, and the room after it not collapsing but standing, standing, standing.

* * *

The third day — or what I believed would be the last full day, though the bridge, as it turned out, had opinions of its own — I taught the goodbye.

We began at dawn because I could not sleep and did not see why my students should. Tamsin made porridge and I ate half, which is generous of me lately. Casper came over from the inn with his recorder and his notebook and the careful face he wears when he is frightened, and I made him take the careful face off before we began, or tried to; it comes off slowly, that face, like a coat sewn on.

"The long goodbye," I said. "You have heard me speak of it."

They nodded. They had been circling it for weeks the way you circle a cold pool.

"In your languages, goodbye is small. A word at a door. A wave from a train. In Kessari, the farewell is a whole act, a whole architecture. It is not *goodbye*. It is *saying farewell so completely that the leaving cannot undo it*. Eleven phrases. Each one must be said to its full length, and each one does a different work — one for the body, one for the memory, one for the debt, one for the love, and so on. When it is done properly, the person who leaves is not gone. They are *answered*. There is a difference. You know there is a difference. Your whole language has only words for unheard and unanswered, and no word for alone, and you did not know why until you came to my kitchen."

I made them write out all eleven phrases. I made them say them back to me, one at a time, and I corrected them with the ruthlessness I am famous for in no circles whatsoever, since I am the last of my nation of one. Casper's tongue stumbled on the fourth phrase. Tamsin's breath ran out in the middle of the ninth, which is a long one, all about the future that will now happen without the person in it, and she had to take it in two breaths, and I told her that was permitted, that the elders permitted it, that a breath is a breath and the language is not a tyrant, only a very demanding grandmother.

Then I said it to them. All eleven phrases, to no one, to the air, to the practice of it — because I have never said it to anyone, not to the children on the lorries in the Relocation Years, not to Aldri' before the water took him, not to my mother through the closed door of the ward. I have carried this farewell in my mouth for seventy years like a stone I could not swallow and could not spit out, and I said it now in my kitchen to two strangers from the south while the bridge above us hummed in the wind like a throat clearing, and when I finished, the porridge was cold and the lamp had burned low and nobody had moved, and I thought: *yes. This. This is what I kept it for.*

* * *

It was that evening, with the light going copper on the water and the viaduct standing against it like a sentence waiting for its full stop, that I asked them for the promise.

I made them sit down. Both of them, across from me, the table between us, the teacups in their hands — I am a formal woman when it matters; my mother taught me that you do not bind a promise over an empty table.

"I have a request," I said. "It is not a small one. I will only ask once, and you may refuse, and the language will not love you less. But I will know."

They waited. Good students. They have learned to wait, which is half of everything.

"When I am gone — and I will be gone soon, whether the bridge hurries or not, my chest and the demolition are in a race and I have bet on the bridge — you will speak to each other. In Kessari. Badly."

Tamsin blinked. "Badly?"

"Badly." I let the word sit. "You will mispronounce. You will confuse the tenses. You will use the word for *boat* when you mean the word for *bread*, and you will say the greeting for *unheard* at breakfast when you mean *good morning*, and you will do it daily, every day, without ceremony and without apology. Do you understand why?"

Casper was looking at his tea. He understands more than he says; that is his whole tragedy, that he understands everything and says almost nothing, though he is mending, he is mending like a wall in spring.

"Because a language in an archive is a specimen," he said slowly. "A language in use is —"

"Is alive," I finished. "Is a marriage. Is a fire carried from house to house. The university men will have their recordings, and they are welcome to them; they have captured a thousand voices and resurrected none. But you two —" I pointed at them with the spoon, which my mother would have called barbaric and which I did anyway — "you two are not permitted to make a monument of me. I did not survive the Relocation Years, thirty years of mouthing words against the backs of my teeth, so that a hard drive in a southern university could have me. I survived so that someone, somewhere, would say *kessa'lin* over the porridge and get it wrong."

Tamsin was crying. Not the loud kind; the kind that runs quietly and is wiped away with the heel of the hand, the kind I have done a thousand times at this same table. "We promise," she said.

"Say it in Kessari."

She said it. Casper said it. The phrase for a binding promise is a long one — it contains the word for *rope* and the word for *root*, and the elders used to say that a promise in Kessari is a thing you tie to yourself and plant at the same time, and I watched them say it, badly, their accents fraying at the edges, and I felt the strangest sensation, which was lightness. As if a load I had been carrying had been transferred, not set down — transferred, the way you pass a sleeping child from your arms to another's, carefully, so it does not wake.

"There," I said, when they had finished. "Now it is tied and planted. Now I am not the last speaker. I am only the first teacher."

* * *

They stayed late that night. Casper recorded the eleven phrases one final time, and Tamsin transcribed them in her notebook in her small upright hand, and I sat in my chair by the wall of postcards and listened to my own language fill the room in other people's mouths, and I thought about my mother, and about the school that was closed, and about the children on the lorries who grew up in southern towns speaking southern words and dreaming, if they dreamed in anything at all, in silence.

And then, because the light was low and the cough had settled into my chest like a tenant who intends to stay, I asked Casper for a piece of paper.

He brought me one, and a pen, thinking I would dictate. I held the paper a long time. It was a postcard, actually — he had found the small stack of them in the drawer by the sink, the old ones, the ones I bought forty years ago from the shop that is now a ruin. Blank ones. I had always kept blanks. A smuggler keeps blank paper the way other people keep money.

There is one card on my wall that no one has ever asked me about. It is blank. It is stamped. It has been addressed to no one for forty years, because there are things a person owes that have no addressee left living, and I had always believed I would finish it myself, in my own time, in my own hand.

But my own time, it appears, is three days, and my own hand has begun to shake.

"Casper," I said. He looked up from the recorder. "Bring me the card from the wall. The blank one. Third row, by the window."

He found it. He held it out to me, and I looked at it, and I looked at him, and I understood that this was the last thing, or nearly the last, and that the last things are never the ones you plan.

"I cannot write it," I said. "My hand has opinions now. So you will write it for me. And not in Kessari — in whatever language you choose. And you will say in it whatever *you* owe. Not what I owe. What you owe. Do you understand?"

He went very still, the way he does, the engineer's stillness, the man measuring the load before he moves.

"Who is it to?" he asked.

"That," I said, "is between you and the card."
