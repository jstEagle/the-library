## 1. The Smile That Ends Conversations

The rain over Ixelles came down the way it always did in October—not falling so much as settling, a gray suspension that made the cemetery's plane trees look like photographs of themselves. Ansel Marchetti stood at the lip of her mother's grave with her hands folded at her waist and her face arranged into an expression she had rehearsed in the mirror that morning: sorrow, but dignified sorrow. Sorrow with posture.

She had learned long ago that grief, performed correctly, was a kind of architecture. You built walls out of stillness. You set windows of eye contact at precise intervals—long enough to seem sincere, short enough to invite nothing further. And at the door of every conversation you hung the smile: not warm, exactly, but gracious. The smile that ended conversations. People saw it and understood, without knowing they understood, that they had been received and could now be released.

She deployed it forty-one times before noon.

She knew because she counted. Counting was how she kept herself inside her own body when the ground wanted to open and take her too. Forty-one mourners, each one cataloged the way she cataloged everything—by category, by function, by what they would want from the estate. It was not cynicism. It was inventory. Her mother had taught her that a house survives on inventory; that a woman who knows what she owns cannot be robbed by surprise.

* * *

The morning had begun in the townhouse on the Rue de l'Abbaye, where the shutters were still closed against a light the house hadn't seen in years. Tomas moved through the rooms like a man defusing something, phone wedged between ear and shoulder, arranging cars, flowers, the order of procession. He wore his grief like a well-cut coat—visible, tailored, not to be commented upon.

"The limousine seats six," he said, not looking up. "We're nine."

"Then we're a family of compromises," Henri said from the kitchen doorway, glass already in hand. Not whiskey—the hour forbade whiskey, nominally—but white wine, pale as weak tea, which he drank steadily through the morning the way other men drink coffee. His charm was intact. His charm was always intact. It was the load-bearing wall of him.

Ansel stood in the hall beneath the coat hooks where Odile's black wool coat still hung, smelling faintly of lavender and mothballs, and thought: *I should take it down.* She did not take it down. Some things you leave until you understand what removing them will cost.

"You'll stand on my left," Tomas told her. "Eldest daughter, left side. It reads better in the photographs."

"There won't be photographs."

"There are always photographs." He tapped his temple. "Everyone's a photographer now."

* * *

At the church of Saint-Boniface the organ played Fauré, because Odile had once said at dinner, years ago, that Fauré's Requiem was the only honest music about death—it didn't argue with it—and the family had filed that remark away like all her remarks, to be produced at the correct ceremonial moment. Ansel sat in the front pew between Henri and Tomas and watched the coffin with its brass fittings and thought about how her mother had hated brass. Preferred pewter. Dull things. Things that didn't insist on their own shine.

Henri's hand found hers during the eulogy. It was dry and cold and slightly trembling, and she understood that the trembling was not grief but arithmetic—he was calculating, even now, what this funeral would cost and who owed whom. She squeezed back anyway. In public, the Marchettis were seamless.

The priest spoke of a life of quiet devotion. He spoke of Odile's charity, her discretion, her dignity. *Discretion*—the word landed strangely, like a key turning in a lock Ansel hadn't known was there. Her mother had been discreet the way fortresses are discreet. There had been whole decades in that house conducted behind doors Ansel had never been invited to open. Whole silences worn smooth as river stones from handling.

*Elle était une femme qui gardait les choses*, the priest said. A woman who kept things.

Yes, Ansel thought. What things?

* * *

The cemetery at Ixelles received them under its dripping trees. The mourners clustered at the grave in their dark wool, umbrellas blooming like black flowers, and Ansel took up her post beside the mound of earth and began the work.

She cataloged them as they came.

*The company people first*—three men from Marchetti Logistics, the firm her grandfather Auguste had built from a single warehouse in Anderlecht into a name that appeared on buildings and scholarship funds and the donor walls of hospitals. They shook Henri's hand with the particular reverence men reserve for money in mourning. One of them, Verhoeven, had wet eyes that seemed genuine, or a very good imitation of genuine, and Ansel gave him the smile and thirty seconds of warmth: *"Thank you for coming. She'd have been pleased. She always said you were the only one who read the reports properly."* He glowed and moved on, released.

*Then the cousins*—the estranged Belgian branch, arriving in ones and twos like guests at a border crossing, uncertain of their welcome. Ansel distributed warmth among them evenly, a rationing. Her aunt Marguerite gripped her arm too hard and whispered, "Your mother was the last one who *knew things*," and before Ansel could ask what things, Marguerite was pulled away by her husband, and the moment closed like water over a stone.

*Then the old neighbors from Uccle*, women in their eighties who remembered Odile young, remembered her dancing, remembered a Brussels that existed now only in their telling of it. These were the hardest. Their condolences were real, unarmored, and they disarmed Ansel in return. One of them, Mrs. Peeters, held both her hands and said, "She carried something, your mother. All those years. We never asked what." And smiled, and drifted off toward the gate, leaving the sentence hanging in the rain like laundry no one claimed.

Ansel wrote it down later, mentally, in the ledger she was keeping without deciding to: *Mrs. Peeters. Carried something. Never asked.*

That was the family trade, wasn't it. Not logistics. Carrying. Not asking.

Tomas worked the crowd beside her, efficient, gracious, shaking hands and murmuring thanks, occasionally steering difficult mourners toward easier ones the way a host steers a drunk away from the bar. Henri stood slightly apart with his wine—someone had brought him a glass from somewhere, wine materialized around Henri the way weather does—and received condolences with a nod that managed to be both humble and regal. Once, briefly, his eyes met Ansel's over the heads of the mourners, and there passed between them something she couldn't name. Not affection. Recognition, maybe. Two performers acknowledging each other across the footlights of the same stage.

By two o'clock the crowd had thinned to stragglers. The gravediggers waited with the patient insolence of men whose work begins when everyone else's ends. Ansel's feet ached in shoes chosen for dignity rather than mercy. Forty-one smiles. The armor holding. The rain settling.

And then the gray-haired woman.

* * *

She had been standing at the edge of the gathering since the beginning—far enough back to be almost scenery, close enough that Ansel's cataloging mind kept snagging on her and releasing, snagging and releasing, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Mid-seventies, perhaps. Gray hair cut short and practical, a dark green coat that was neither cheap nor new, hands folded in front of her like a woman waiting for a train that has been delayed for decades.

She was not in the register of expected mourners. Ansel had gone over the list with Tomas twice. This woman belonged to no category: not company, not family, not neighborhood, not charity. She was an uncataloged item, and uncataloged items in the Marchetti house were, by tradition, not permitted to exist.

When the last cousin had gone, when Tomas was walking Henri toward the cars, when the gravediggers shifted their weight and looked at the sky, the woman came forward.

Not quickly. She crossed the wet grass at the pace of someone who has decided there is no longer any reason to hurry, and stopped at the grave's edge, an arm's length from Ansel. Up close Ansel could see her face properly: lined, composed, with eyes the color of wet slate that did not perform anything at all. No condolence in them. No curiosity either. Something older than both.

"I don't believe we've met," Ansel said, deploying the smile, the full one, the one that ended conversations. "I'm Ansel Marchetti. Thank you for coming today—it meant a great deal to her, I'm sure, that so many—"

"Vandermolen," the woman said.

One word. Flat as a coin placed on a counter.

The smile stayed on Ansel's face—she could feel it staying, feel the muscles holding their position out of pure professional discipline—but something behind it went very quiet, the way a house goes quiet when a floorboard settles somewhere it shouldn't.

"I'm sorry?" she said.

But the woman was already turning. She looked once at the coffin, at the raw earth, at the flowers going dark with rain, and whatever passed across her face then was brief and private and not offered for inspection. Then she walked away across the cemetery, her green coat fading into the gray of the trees, unhurried, a woman whose train had finally arrived.

"Wait—" Ansel started, and stopped, because the gravediggers were watching, and because the word had done something to her voice.

Vandermolen.

She turned it over. It was not a name she knew. Not a friend, not a colleague, not a godparent, not a cousin from the estranged branches—she knew every branch, her mother had made sure of that, had recited the family tree to her like a catechism. Vandermolen was not on the tree. Vandermolen was not anywhere.

The mill. She caught herself translating automatically, the way her mother had taught her—*van der molen*, of the mill—and stopped, because translation was a form of pursuit and she was not ready to pursue anything. Not yet. Her feet were aching. Her brother was honking the car horn from the gate, one short impatient note that meant *we are done here.*

Ansel looked down at the coffin one last time. Rain beaded on the brass her mother had hated.

"Mum," she said quietly, testing the silence the way you test ice, "who was Vandermolen?"

The silence answered nothing. Silences in her family never did; they were load-bearing.

She walked up the hill toward the car, and she was smiling—she realized this with a strange detachment, felt the smile sitting on her face like something left there by another person—because the armor was still working, still doing its job, still ending conversations.

It just hadn't counted on one word being enough to end it instead.
