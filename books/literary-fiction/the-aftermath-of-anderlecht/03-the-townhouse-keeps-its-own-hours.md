## 3. The Townhouse Keeps Its Own Hours

The townhouse on the Rue de l'Abdication had always kept its own hours. As a child, Ansel understood this without being told: the house woke when the family slept, its radiators ticking like a slow clock, its floorboards contracting in the dark with small reports, like distant gunfire. Her mother had never called it a haunted house. Odile had simply said, *the house is old, it talks to itself*, and that had been enough to make the talking ordinary.

Now, nine days after the funeral, Ansel unlocked the front door at seven in the morning and felt the house take note of her. The hall smelled of cold wax and dried flowers and, beneath it, something older—the mineral smell of stone that has absorbed fifty years of cooking and grief and cigarette smoke. The chandelier hung dead above her, its crystals gray with dust, and she did not switch on the lights. There was no need. She had come to work, and work required only her eyes.

The estate inventory lay open on the dining table where she'd left it the night before: a legal pad of cream paper, columns ruled in pencil, her handwriting already slanting toward exhaustion. *Salon: two armchairs (burgundy velvet, worn), marble-top console, mirror (gilt, cracked lower left corner). Library: approx. 1,400 volumes.* The notary had asked for a full catalog before probate, and Ansel had volunteered—had, in fact, insisted—with a fluency that surprised her. It was the perfect cover. No one questioned an heir counting spoons.

Tomas had offered to hire someone. *There are firms that do this,* he'd said, not looking up from his phone. *Professionals. You don't have to touch every object yourself.*

*I want to,* she'd said, and watched him decide not to argue. That was Tomas's gift: he knew which battles were worth spending his charm on, and apparently his sister's grief-work was not one of them. Henri had raised no objection either. Her father had been at the house twice since the funeral, both times briefly, both times leaving with his car smelling faintly of the bar near Place du Chatelain where the bartenders knew his order before he sat down. He was handling things, he said. He was seeing to matters. He kissed Ansel's forehead with lips that tasted of coffee and something medicinal, and told her she was doing her mother proud.

So she cataloged.

She worked room by room, top to bottom, because the house rewarded method and punished improvisation. The third floor first—guest rooms that had hosted no guests in a decade, beds made with sheets gone the color of weak tea. Then the second floor: her parents' bedroom, which she entered the way one enters water known to be cold, quickly, touching little. Odile's side of the wardrobe still held her clothes, arranged by color as she'd always arranged them, rusts and olives and a single cardinal-red coat that Ansel remembered from her childhood the way other children remember a mother's face. She wrote *wardrobe contents: personal apparel* and closed the door gently, as if the room were sleeping.

It was on the first floor, in the sewing room, that the house changed its tone.

The sewing room had been Odile's sanctuary for as long as Ansel could remember—a narrow room facing the back garden, its window fogged by a film of condensation that no amount of caulking ever cured. A Singer treadle machine sat beneath the window like a patient animal. Bolts of fabric leaned against the wall in muted ranks. The room smelled of lavender sachets and machine oil, and it was the only room in the house where Odile had ever seemed entirely at ease, her hands busy, her mind somewhere Ansel had never been invited to follow.

Ansel stood in the doorway a long moment before entering. Nine days of cataloging had taught her the grammar of the house: which doors stuck, which drawers lied about their depth, which closets held nothing but winter coats and the smell of camphor. But the sewing room she had saved, without admitting to herself that she was saving it, the way you save the last letter in a stack because once it's read there is nothing after.

Inside, the light was greenish, filtered through the garden's bare plane tree. Dust hung in it, undisturbed. She began at the window and worked outward: the machine (Singer, treadle, serial number recorded), the notions basket (buttons sorted by size into glass jars), the fabric bolts (wool, linen, cotton; colors noted). Her pencil moved steadily. This was the mercy of inventory—it asked nothing of her but attention, and attention was easier than feeling.

The cedar chest sat under the eave, in the corner where the ceiling sloped down to meet the floor. Ansel had seen it a hundred times in her life. It had always been there, the way the radiator was always there, the way the smell of lavender was always there. It was a low chest, maybe a meter wide, its cedar slats darkened to the color of strong tea, brass fittings gone green at the seams. As a child she had been told it held winter blankets. She had never once seen it opened.

She knelt beside it now and ran her hand along the lid. The wood was cool and slightly rough, and it gave off, even through the closed lid, that dry sweet smell of cedar that seemed less like a fragrance than a memory of one. There was no lock plate visible on the front. She lifted the lid experimentally. It didn't move—not stiffly, not with age, but with the deliberate immobility of something locked from within. She pressed along the edges. Near the back hinge, her fingers found a small brass escutcheon set flush into the wood, almost invisible, and inside it a keyhole no larger than a lentil.

A locked chest in a sewing room full of buttons and thread. Her mother, who had labeled every jar, who had kept her receipts in dated folders, who had once returned a dress to a shop because a seam was crooked by three millimeters—that woman had kept one thing in this house under lock, and had let everyone believe it was blankets.

Ansel sat back on her heels. Outside, a tram passed on the avenue beyond the wall, its bell sounding thin and far away, and the sound seemed to belong to another city, one where chests held blankets and mothers were knowable.

She photographed the chest from four angles. She measured it. She wrote in the inventory, in her flattest hand: *cedar chest, locked, provenance unknown.* Then she sat with the page open on her knees and understood that she had just written a lie by omission, because the chest's provenance was not unknown. Its provenance was her mother's silence, and her grandmother's before her, and the whole upholstered weight of a family that had never once, in Ansel's forty-one years, left a door open behind them.

* * *

She found the ledger two days later, and it was not hidden at all.

That was the thing she would keep returning to, afterward—the ordinariness of its concealment. Not buried, not bricked into a wall. It sat in the library, on the second shelf of the case nearest the window, spine inward, wedged between a Dutch Bible and a bound volume of *La Meuse* from some forgotten year, dressed up as a book among books. Someone had wanted it findable. Or someone had wanted it findable only by someone who already knew to look, and had trusted the rest of the world to see only what it expected: old books, unread, inherited, dust.

It was a ledger, quarto-sized, bound in marbled board gone soft at the corners. No title. Inside, in a copperplate hand she recognized with a jolt as her grandfather's—the same hand that had signed her childhood birthday cards, *your Opa, Emile*—were columns of figures. Dates down the left margin. Amounts in francs, then, later, in euros, the currency changing mid-stream like a river altering course. And in the column where a ledger should have carried names, there were only initials.

*M.V.* Month after month. Year after year. *M.V., M.V., M.V.*, a rhythm so regular it read like breathing. Occasionally a second set: *H.D.* Once, startlingly, *R.L.B.* The amounts varied slightly—raised in some years, reduced during others she would later learn corresponded to recessions—but they never stopped. January 1975. February 1975. On and on, decade into decade, the handwriting aging, the ink changing from iron gall to ballpoint blue, until—

Until October 1974, where the entries began. And before that, nothing. The first page of entries followed eleven blank pages, as if the book itself had been waiting, empty, for something to happen.

Ansel stood in the library with the ledger open in both hands and heard the house around her with new ears. The radiators ticked. The floorboards spoke their small dark language. Somewhere upstairs a door she had closed that morning stood ajar—she was certain she had closed it—and moved on its hinges with a sound like a slow exhale.

Entries ending abruptly in 1974. Entries beginning immediately after. Whatever had happened had happened in the blank space between the eleventh page and the twelfth, and someone in this family had been paying for it, monthly, for fifty years, in initials instead of names because initials could be denied. Initials were deniable. Names were confessions.

She was still holding it when she heard the front door.

* * *

Henri came into the library the way he came into every room, which was to say as though the room had been waiting for him. He was carrying pastries in a white box tied with string—he still did that, even now, even nine days out, the charm deployed on schedule like a metronome—and he was saying something about the notary, about Thursday, about how Ansel shouldn't be alone in this big house, and then he saw what she was holding, and he stopped.

Not faltered. Stopped. It was like watching a clock halt mid-tick.

For a moment neither of them moved. The plane tree scratched at the window. Down in the kitchen, the refrigerator hummed its one note. Ansel had seen her father charming, drunk, furious, magnanimous, wounded, ridiculous. She had never seen him *still*, and stillness on Henri Marchetti was more frightening than any of it, because it meant the machinery underneath had finally shown itself.

"Where did you find that." Not a question. His voice had dropped into a register she'd never heard, flat and quiet, all the music drained out of it.

"Second shelf." She kept her own voice level. "Between the Bible and the newspapers."

"Put it back."

"Papa."

"Put it *back*, Ansel." He set the pastry box down on the console with exaggerated care, as if his hands needed a task or they would do something else. When he turned to face her fully, she searched his face for the father she knew—the wink, the shrug, the *chérie, don't complicate things*—and found instead a stranger wearing his features badly, like a mask fitted in a hurry.

"It's an estate document," she said. "I'm cataloging everything. That's the whole point."

"That is not an estate document." He crossed the room and held out his hand, palm up, and the gesture was so ordinary, so reasonable, that for half a second she almost obeyed it. Fifty years of conditioning lived in that palm-up hand. Every Marchetti instinct said: give Papa the sharp thing, give Papa the true thing, smile and hand it over and watch it disappear into the architecture of the family.

Vera Kesteloot's voice arrived in her memory, unbidden: *the name Vandermolen.* The gray woman at the graveside. The initials marching down the page. *M.V., M.V., M.V.*

"No," Ansel said.

The word landed in the library and stayed there. Henri's hand remained extended a moment longer, then slowly closed and lowered, and Ansel watched something happen in her father's face that she had no name for yet—the charm failing not gradually, like a lamp dimming, but all at once, like a wire cut. What remained was older than charm. Older, possibly, than Henri himself. It was the face of a man standing guard over a stain, and it made her understand, with a clarity that felt physical, that the townhouse had never been keeping secrets *for* the family.

The family had been keeping secrets for the house.

"You don't know what you're holding," Henri said quietly.

"Then tell me."

He looked at the ledger, at the initials, at the blank pages before 1974, and Ansel saw him weigh it—she actually saw the weighing, the scales moving behind his eyes—and then he smiled. It was the smile from the funeral, the one that ended conversations, the one she'd inherited without knowing its purpose until now.

"There's nothing to tell," he said. "Your grandfather was a careful man with his accounts. That's all."

And he walked out of the library, leaving the pastries untasted on the console, and Ansel stood alone with the ledger while upstairs, in the sewing room, the locked cedar chest waited under the eaves—patient, silent, keeping its own hours—holding whatever it was that the initials had been paid to bury, and the key that might open it nowhere in the house that she could find.
