## 4. What Grandmother Kept

The tin box weighed almost nothing.

That was the first thing Hazel noticed, standing in the dark of the old practice wing with a duffel bag over one shoulder and forty years of somebody's silence in his hands. A box that size should have held buttons, or fishing lures, or Christmas cards from a dead aunt. It should not have been able to hold whatever it was holding, because he could feel — before he'd opened it, before he'd even sat down — that it was heavier than its weight.

He carried it to Room 214 because there was nowhere else to go and every other room was worse.

The radiator in 214 had been drained years ago; the building was scheduled for demolition in five weeks and three days, and the conservatory had stopped pretending to maintain anything east of the atrium. The piano was still there, though. They always left the pianos until last, the way hospitals leave the dying until last, out of some institutional courtesy nobody names. A Kawai upright, institutional black, the lid scarred with initials some student had carved with a compass in 1987 or so. Hazel set the box on top of it without opening the fallboard. He wasn't ready for the keys yet. He might never be ready for the keys, but that was a problem for a different hour.

He clicked on his flashlight, cupped his hand around it like a boy reading under blankets, and pried the lid.

* * *

Inside: letters. Bundled in rubber bands gone brittle as cicada shells, each bundle tied by year — or what he assumed was year, because the earliest postmarks read 1963 and the latest read 2001, and between them lay strata the way an archaeologist finds strata, each layer pressing down on the one beneath. Letters addressed to Rosa Villalobos. To Emilio Villalobos. To Ruth. To his father, Daniel, in the brief window when anyone had still written to his father at all. Some were marked RETURN TO SENDER in faded red. Most bore no mark at all, which meant they had never been mailed — which meant someone had taken them out of hands that were reaching, and put them here, behind a wardrobe, in a panel no carpenter had ever been asked to build.

His grandmother had kept them. His grandmother — who pressed his trousers before recitals, who kept lemon drops in her coat pocket for after, who said *míralo, our Hazel* with such ferocious pride that strangers believed her — had built a wall inside a wall and filled it with the mail of the living.

Hazel sat down on the practice-room chair, the one with the wobble he remembered, the wobble that had still not been fixed in thirty years, and untied the first bundle with fingers that did not feel entirely like his own.

* * *

The early ones were ordinary. That was the strange mercy of it. Birthday cards. A letter from a music camp in Vermont thanking Rosa for her donation. A bill from a piano tuner in 1964, stamped PAID with a flourish. He read them the way you read a stranger's grocery list — dutifully, waiting for the thing itself to appear. The flashlight made a small warm room inside the cold one. Somewhere below him, in the part of the building that still had power, a compressor cycled on and off like slow breathing.

Then, in the fourth bundle, a different hand.

The handwriting was slanted, urgent, the loops of the g's unfinished, as if the writer kept lifting the pen mid-thought to chase the next thought down. The envelope was addressed to *Rosa Villalobos, Milton Flats,* and the return address was a street in Corvallis, Oregon, a town Hazel had never once heard mentioned at any family table.

The signature line read: *Your niece, Ines.*

He did not know anyone named Ines.

That was the sentence his mind produced, flat and stupid, and then immediately corrected itself, the way a body corrects a missed stair: *you do know that name, you've heard it, you've heard it somewhere.* But the somewhere would not surface. It moved under the ice of his memory like a fish, visible and unreachable.

He opened the letter. The paper had gone the color of weak tea.

---

*March 9, 1971*

*Aunt Rosa,*

*Mama says I should stop writing and I have decided to disobey her, which at sixteen is more or less my full-time occupation. I know you received the others because Tío Emilio told Papa at Easter that the family "has enough noise." I am choosing to understand this as encouragement.*

*I finished it. The sonata. All three movements, though the third is too long and I will cut it when I am older and wiser, which I am told happens eventually. I played it through for Señora Brandt on Thursday and she cried, Aunt Rosa, she actually cried, and then she said something I have written on the inside of my wrist where I can see it: "You are not writing what you have learned. You are writing what only you know."*

*I want you to hear it. I want you to be in the room when it is played properly, on a real piano, by someone who isn't afraid of the second movement. I know you think I am too young to have anything to say. Maybe I am. But the second movement doesn't care how old I am. It exists now. Somebody has to play it.*

*Come visit. Or write back. Either one. I'll take either one.*

*Ines*

---

Hazel read it twice. Then a third time, slower, the way you press a bruise to learn its shape.

There was no reply in the box. Of course there was no reply. There were no replies anywhere in the box; that was the entire architecture of the thing. He turned the envelope over. No red stamp. It had been opened — carefully, with a knife, the flap re-secured with a strip of tape so old it had yellowed into the paper — and then it had come here, into the dark, behind the wardrobe, into the country his grandmother kept for things she did not want to exist.

A cousin named Ines. Sixteen years old in 1971, which would make her — he did the arithmetic and didn't like the answer — nearly seventy now, if she was alive at all. Living in Oregon, or having lived. Writing letters that a woman he loved took out of the world.

He reached for the next one because his hands decided before he did.

* * *

---

*June 22, 1971*

*Aunt Rosa,*

*Papa says if I keep writing you will eventually have to answer or admit you are not answering, and either way I win. He is a lawyer and thinks everything works this way.*

*I have news and I don't know how to say it except straight out, so: Señora Brandt entered me in the Whitcomb Prize. Regional first. If I place at state they publish the winning work in the society journal, with the composer's name, and there would be photographs, and Mama would have to buy a dress, which she claims is the real prize.*

*Here is the part I need you to sit with, because I have been sitting with it for two weeks and I can't hold it alone anymore. Aunt Rosa says the sonata will be played under your name.*

*She came to Corvallis in April. You remember. She heard me play the second movement in the parlor and she went very quiet, the way she does, and then she said, "Ines, mija, this is not a piece for a girl from Corvallis. This is a piece for the family. Let it be played where it will matter." And I laughed because I thought she meant Portland. She did not mean Portland.*

*Yesterday Papa got a letter from her lawyer. There is a trust. There is paperwork. My name is on nothing. She says it is protection, that a minor cannot hold publishing rights, that it is all temporary and reversible and for my own good. Papa believes her because he wants the family to be whole again and because lawyers believe other lawyers. Mama believes her because Mama is tired.*

*I am sixteen and I am telling you, my aunt, the woman who taught me the Chopin B-flat minor, that I am being robbed in the gentlest voice you have ever heard, and everyone keeps handing me tea.*

*Tell me I'm wrong. Please. Be the one person who tells me I'm wrong.*

*Ines*

---

The compressor cycled off. In the silence Hazel became aware of his own breathing, shallow and quick, and made himself slow it down. He set the letter on the piano lid, aligned its edge with the first one, precisely, the way his grandmother had aligned everything — napkins, sheet music, the lives of children.

*Aunt Rosa says the sonata will be played under your name.*

Under whose name? He read the sentence again, hunting for a referent, and found none, and understood with a slow vertical drop in his stomach that the referent was not in the letter. The referent was in the room. The referent was sitting on a wobbly chair in Room 214 with a flashlight in his hand.

He was born in 1974.

* * *

He worked through the bundles in order, because disorder felt like something the box had already given him enough of. The letters from Ines came roughly monthly through 1971 and 1972, then thinned, then changed. He could chart the change the way you chart weather. The exclamation points disappeared first. Then the jokes about her father. Then the requests. What remained was shorter, colder, and worse.

---

*November 3, 1972*

*Aunt Rosa,*

*The Whitcomb results came. Second place. The winner's piece is fine. It is the kind of piece that is fine.*

*Señora Brandt asked why I withdrew the sonata from consideration. I told her the family had made other arrangements. She looked at me for a long time and then said, "Ines, in fifty years nobody will remember who won this year." I think it was meant kindly. I have decided to take it as a prophecy instead.*

*Rosa. I am going to ask you one question and I want you to answer it, not Emilio, not the lawyer, you. When you took the manuscript in April — you said for safekeeping. Where is it being kept safe? Name the drawer. Name the city. I will drive there myself. I will stand in the room. I just want to see it with my name on it, once, before I stop asking.*

*This is the last letter unless you answer. I don't say that as a threat. I say it the way you'd tell someone the tide is going out. Eventually there is just sand.*

*Ines*

---

There was one more from Corvallis, eight months later. It was four lines long.

---

*July 14, 1973*

*I got your letter. I read it eleven times. You wrote that the family has made its decision and that I should think of the larger good, and that someday, when I am older, I will understand what my music was able to do for people I love.*

*I am seventeen. I understand perfectly.*

*Don't write to me again.*

*Ines*

---

Hazel put that one down and stood up, because sitting had become unbearable, and walked to the window at the end of the hall where the glass was filmed with construction dust and the parking lot below was empty except for Marisol's car and the surveyor's stakes with their little orange flags, marking where the new building would rise out of the old one's bones. His reflection hung in the glass, gray and doubled, a man holding a flashlight in a condemned building at two in the morning, which was, he supposed, exactly what he was.

*Think of the larger good.*

He knew that phrase. Not from Ines's letter — from his own childhood. From the dinner table in the house on Delmore Street, where his grandmother had used it like a benediction whenever the subject of money came up, which was often, because the money was the reason for everything: the house, the lessons, the summer festivals, the trust that paid for Juilliard until he walked away from it at nineteen and let it sit there, offended, accumulating. *We are fortunate, Hazel, and fortune asks things of us. Think of the larger good.* He had thought it was piety. He had thought it was the particular piety of grandmothers who had survived things.

It was a payment schedule.

He went back to Room 214. The letters waited, patient as evidence, which is what they were. He untied the next bundle — 1974, the year of his birth — and found fewer envelopes now, and different senders. A lawyer's letter regarding *the Villalobos Family Trust, Instrument II.* A card from a concert society in Portland confirming a licensing agreement for performance and publication of certain works. And then, in March of 1974, a single sheet, not a letter but a program, folded once, from a recital hall he did not recognize:

**HAZEL VILLALOBOS, age 6, pupil of R. Villalobos**
*Program to include: Sonatina in D minor ("Family Exercise No. 1")*

Family Exercise No. 1.

He had played that piece. He could still play that piece. His hands knew it the way his hands knew the shape of a doorknob in the dark — the opening figure, those falling thirds, the strange aching turn in the ninth measure that no six-year-old writes and no six-year-old forgets. He had been told it was an exercise. A family exercise, composed — he'd assumed, the way children assume, without assuming anything at all — by someone in the family, long ago, for students. Every Villalobos child learned the exercises. That was the story. That was the water he swam in.

Falling thirds. The turn in the ninth measure.

The second movement of a sonata written by a girl in Corvallis who wanted her aunt to be in the room when it was played properly.

Hazel sat back down. The wobble in the chair rocked him gently, once, twice, like something trying to get his attention. Betrayal, he discovered, does not arrive all at once. It arrives the way cold water arrives in a bathtub — you notice the first inch, and you tell yourself it will warm up, and you keep sitting there while it rises, because rising is gradual and getting out requires a decision, and by the time the decision is obvious the water is at your chin and the door is further away than it used to be.

He had been six years old in that photograph of himself at the recital hall, in the clip-on tie, grinning, playing a stolen sonata's opening movement under a title designed to make theft sound like homework. And the woman who pressed his trousers and kept lemon drops in her pocket had sat in the audience with her hands folded, thinking of the larger good.

He picked up the flashlight. There were bundles still unopened — 1975 through 2001, twenty-six years of mail his grandmother had intercepted, from senders he hadn't seen yet, about subjects he could feel approaching like weather. His hands were steady. That surprised him. He would have expected shaking, and there was none; there was only a kind of terrible clarity, the clarity of a man who has finally been shown the blueprint of the house he grew up in and can see, now, which walls were load-bearing and which were built to hide something.

He untied the 1975 bundle. The topmost envelope was thicker than the rest, stuffed, urgent, addressed in that same slanted unfinished hand — but the return address was no longer Corvallis. It was a street in a town called Ashland, and the name beside it was not Villalobos.

The name was one he'd have to look up. But the handwriting he already knew.

And underneath it, visible through the thin paper, the first line had been written in ink pressed hard enough to emboss the page:

*You will want to know what happened to the manuscript.*
