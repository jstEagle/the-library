## 8. Letters to the Living

The letters spread across Marisol's kitchen table like a deck of cards someone had thrown in anger. Forty-one of them, sorted now into rough piles by decade — the fifties, the sixties, the seventies, a lonely straggler from 1989 that nobody had ever answered. The tin box sat empty at the center of it all, its lid propped against the salt shaker, rust blooming on the underside like a map of somewhere else.

Marisol stood at her stove with her back to him, making coffee she didn't drink at this hour and didn't need. She'd been quiet since he'd told her. Not cold — Marisol didn't do cold — but quiet in the way of someone recalibrating.

"You haven't slept," she said.

"I slept."

"You slept like a man guarding a door." She came over with two mugs anyway and sat down across from him. "Okay. Show me what you've got."

He'd been up most of the night doing exactly that. The letters weren't all to Villaloboses, which was the first surprise. Grandmother Rosa — *Abuela Rosa*, the sainted matriarch whose portrait still hung in Aunt Ruth's hallway, whose name was on the scholarship fund, whose hands Hazel remembered as soft and smelling of lavender and ironing starch — had intercepted mail going out as well as coming in. There were letters here addressed to editors, to a music publisher in Chicago, to a man named Aurelio Fuentes who wrote in careful, formal Spanish about royalties and rights and *the matter of the sonata*, a phrase that appeared three times before his correspondence simply stopped in 1967.

And there were letters addressed to family. Cousins Hazel had never heard of. An uncle who died before Hazel was born. A woman named Dolores who, judging by the return addresses, had moved four times in ten years, each address further from Milton Flats.

"She didn't just hide things," Hazel said. "She ran a sorting operation. Look — some of these have been opened and resealed. Badly. That one." He tapped an envelope with a flap glued down crooked, a child's repair done by old hands. "She read them first. Decided which ones the family got to see."

"And these?" Marisol picked up a thick bundle tied with kitchen twine. "These are sealed."

"Never delivered. Or returned." He'd checked the postmarks. Some had RETURN TO SENDER stamps, faded violet. Some had no stamps at all — hand-delivered to the house, maybe, and then pocketed. "Forty years of mail that went into a tin instead of into anyone's hands."

Marisol turned an envelope over slowly, the way you'd handle something that might be alive. "So who's still breathing?"

That was the question he'd been circling all night. He pulled a legal pad toward himself — Marisol's, borrowed, already crowded with his handwriting. He'd made a list. Names, dates, best guesses.

"Dolores Villalobos Reyes," he read. "Last known address, 1974, a town I can't find on a map anymore. Probably dead. Her daughter might not be." He flipped the page. "Father Mateo Villalobos — became a priest, letters stop in 1981, monasteries keep records, I could call. Aurelio Fuentes — if his estate has any paper trail, there's a lawyer somewhere who knows about the sonata already." He stopped. His pen hovered over the last name on the list, the one he'd saved for last because writing it down had felt like picking up something hot.

"Tomas," Marisol said, reading upside down.

"Tomas Villalobos." Hazel set the pen down. "My father's cousin. He's a lawyer. Was a lawyer — retired now, maybe. He left the family when I was nine. There was a fight at Christmas, at Abuela Rosa's house. I remember the sound of it more than the words. My father wouldn't say his name afterward. Nobody would. It was like he'd been subtracted."

"And you found a letter addressed to him?"

Hazel reached into the pile and drew out a single envelope, heavier than the others, thicker. It had been opened and resealed — Rosa's crooked glue again — but inside, the letter itself was intact, two pages in a slanted, urgent hand. He'd read it six times. He handed it to Marisol without a word.

She read it aloud, quietly, the way people do when they're not sure they're allowed to hear their own voice saying a thing:

*"Tomas — Your aunt has taken the manuscript again. I gave it to her for safekeeping and she will not return it and now she says it was never mine. I am sixteen years old and everyone tells me I should be grateful. If you are the person they say you are, you will understand why I am writing to you instead of screaming. Please don't tell her I wrote. — Ines."*

The kitchen was very quiet. Down the block, somebody's sprinkler ticked through its arc.

"Dated 1959," Hazel said. "Postmarked but returned. Addressee unknown. And then—" he slid a second envelope across, unopened, seal unbroken, wax still holding after sixty years, "—she tried again. This one never even bounced back. It just... stayed."

"Ines." Marisol said the name carefully, tasting it. "You said last night your grandmother kept letters from a cousin. This is her. The one who—"

"The one who doesn't exist. In our family, she doesn't exist. I went through every photo album at Ruth's house growing up. Every christening record Ruth keeps in that green ledger like scripture. No Ines. It's like the family amputated her and cauterized the wound."

Marisol set the letter down and looked at him for a long moment. Then she said, in her flat, no-nonsense way, the way she talked about mop heads and supply budgets and which supervisor was stealing hours:

"So call him."

"He'll be eighty-something years old. He hasn't spoken to this family in forty years. What do I even say? *Hi, I'm the grandson of the woman who stole your cousin's music and possibly your mail?*"

"You say: my name is Hazel Villalobos, and I'm holding a letter that's been waiting for you since 1959." She pushed the phone across the table. "Or you say nothing, and you put it back in the box, and you spend the rest of your life wondering. Your choice. But you didn't find those letters, Hazel. You went looking. So finish looking."

* * *

He waited until evening to make the call, which was cowardice dressed up as strategy. He told himself the time zones mattered — Tomas was supposedly in Sacramento, or Portland, or somewhere west where the light lasted longer — but really he just needed the day to talk himself into it, and the day refused. By six o'clock he was sitting in his truck outside the conservatory, engine off, the condemned wing dark behind its new chain-link fence, the number written on a scrap of paper soft with sweat from his palm.

Directory assistance had given it up easily. T. R. Villalobos, attorney at law, retired. One listing. As if the man had spent his whole exile waiting to be findable.

Hazel dialed before he could reconsider.

One ring.

He had braced for voicemail, for a screening machine, for an elderly voice saying *who is this* with suspicion honed over decades. He had braced for nothing at all — a dead line, a wrong number, another dead end like Dolores.

Two rings was what he expected. What he got was one ring and then a click, and a voice — dry, unhurried, faintly amused, the voice of a man who had been expecting this call the way you expect weather.

"Well," the voice said. "Took you long enough."

Hazel's mouth went dry. "I— Mr. Villalobos? Tomas?"

"That depends entirely on who's asking and what they want. Though I'll admit the possibilities are narrow. Nobody from that family has dialed this number in twenty-two years, and the ones who would are mostly dead." A pause. Papers shifting somewhere, a chair creaking. "So. You're either a bill collector, a journalist, or a Villalobos. Bill collectors call during business hours. Journalists lead with flattery. Which leaves—"

"Hazel." His own voice came out smaller than he intended. "Hazel Villalobos. Rafael's son."

The silence on the other end changed quality. It went from amused to very, very still.

"Rafael's boy," Tomas said finally. "The pianist."

"The janitor, actually. These days."

Another silence, longer. When Tomas spoke again, the amusement was gone, replaced by something careful and old. "I know what you are these days. I've kept track. That's what exiles do — we keep track. You walked out of the conservatory at nineteen in the middle of a master class and never played publicly again, and every single one of them called it a tragedy and not one of them asked you why." He exhaled, a long breath down the line. "I asked myself why for years. I had theories."

"What kind of theories?"

"The kind you don't share on a first phone call." A drawer opened; something was set down heavily, a glass on wood. "Let me ask you something instead, Hazel. And I want you to answer honestly, because I'll know if you don't — it's a professional habit. Why are you calling me? Now. At your age. Twenty-some years after everyone stopped saying my name."

Hazel looked at the fence around the wing, at the orange DEMOLITION NOTICE zip-tied to the mesh, fluttering in the evening wind. He thought about lying — about saying he was cleaning out the building, helping the school archive things, anything small and deniable. But Marisol's voice was in his head: *you went looking.*

"There was a box," he said. "Hidden behind a wardrobe in the old practice wing. Letters. Forty years of letters that my grandmother intercepted and never delivered. Some of them are yours. Two of them. One from 1959 that she opened and resealed, and one she never let leave the house at all."

The glass on the other end was set down very slowly, very precisely, the way a man puts down a glass when his hand is not entirely steady.

"They're from Ines," Hazel said.

"Yes," Tomas said quietly. "They would be."

"You know about her."

"I've known about her since I was eleven years old, when she climbed the tree outside my window and handed me a page of sheet music through the branches and said, *memorize this, and if anyone asks, you made it up.* She was sixteen and she already knew how the house worked." His voice had gone low and fast, sixty years of held breath releasing through a pinhole. "I know about the sonata. I know about the manuscript your grandmother took 'for safekeeping.' I know what happened at Christmas in 1962, and I know why I left, and I know that for sixty years I have waited for someone — anyone — from that beautiful, rotten, magnificent family to pick up a telephone and say the word *Ines* to me."

He stopped. Breathed. When he resumed, the lawyer had come back into his voice, measured and precise, but underneath it something was burning.

"So yes, Hazel. I've been waiting twenty years. Longer. And now that you're here, I need you to understand something before we go one sentence further, because it matters, and because I'm too old to pretend otherwise."

"Okay," Hazel said.

"This is not going to be simple. Whatever you think you found in that box — whatever story is forming in your head right now, the righteous one, the clean one — it is not clean. There is money involved. Trusts. Royalties that are still being paid, today, to accounts your family built on top of that girl's music. There are institutions — scholarships, named rooms, endowed chairs — that will come apart if this surfaces, and there are people, children, who study under those scholarships right now. And there is Ines herself, who may or may not be alive, and who may or may not want what you want." A pause. "Do you even know what you want yet?"

Hazel opened his mouth. Closed it. Outside, the work lamps along the fence buzzed and flickered on, one by one, lighting the wing for no one.

"No," he admitted.

"Good. Honest. We can work with honest." Papers rustled again, and Tomas's voice shifted into something brisker, almost eager, a man rolling up sleeves he hadn't rolled up in decades. "Now listen carefully, because I'm only going to explain the money once, and when I'm done, you're going to wish you'd never called me — and then, if you're who I think you are, you're going to call me back tomorrow and ask for more."

* * *

The call lasted two hours and eleven minutes. Hazel took notes until his hand cramped and then he just listened, the phone hot against his ear, while Tomas laid it out with the terrible clarity of a man who had spent a career making complicated things legible and had rehearsed this particular explanation alone, in an empty office, for half a lifetime.

By the end, Hazel sat in the dark truck with the dome light off, watching the wing loom black against the streetlamp glow, understanding for the first time the full architecture of what had been built on a sixteen-year-old's stolen pages.

He dialed one more number before he drove home. Directory assistance again. A care facility, two towns over, listed under a married name he'd found scrawled on the back of the 1989 letter — the last one in the box, the one nobody ever answered.

A receptionist answered. Cheerful, tired, ordinary.

"Yeah," Hazel said. "I'm trying to reach a resident. Ines —" he read the married name off the envelope, his voice steadier than he felt. "Is she... is she there?"

A pause. The sound of keys.

"We do have a resident by that name," the receptionist said. "Are you family?"

Hazel looked at the fence, at the dark windows of Room 214, at the tin box on his passenger seat with its forty years of undelivered words.

"Yes," he said. "I'm family."
