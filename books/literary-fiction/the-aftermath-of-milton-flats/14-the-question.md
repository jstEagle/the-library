## 14. The Question

The second visit was easier to arrange and harder to make. The first time I'd come to Cedar Hollow with a folder of photocopies and a plan, however vague. This time I came with nothing but the tin box, which Marisol had insisted I bring. "She wrote them," Marisol said. "They're hers. You're just the mailman who showed up forty years late."

So I sat in the visitors' lounge with a rusted biscuit tin on my knees while a nurse with a clipboard told me that Ines was having a good morning, which meant she'd eaten her eggs and argued about the radio, and that I should keep the visit under an hour if she seemed tired, and that she'd asked for me by name, which was unusual, and that I shouldn't make too much of it.

I made too much of it the entire drive over.

She was in the sunroom, in a chair by the window, wearing a cardigan the color of strong tea. There was a blanket over her legs she clearly didn't need and a crossword on the side table she clearly wasn't doing. When she saw the tin, she didn't flinch. She looked at it the way you look at weather coming in from a long way off.

"You brought them back," she said.

"Marisol's idea. She said they're yours."

"Marisol." She tried the name out. "The one who told you to stop being a coward."

"That's the one."

"Sit down, Hazel. You make the room nervous standing."

I sat. For a while neither of us did anything, and the room did what sunrooms do, which is hold light without warming it. A woman across the way was arguing gently with a television. Somewhere a cart rattled down a corridor, its wheels squeaking on the second rotation of every turn, a rhythm so regular it was almost musical, and I hated that I noticed.

"Open it," Ines said.

I opened the tin. The letters were in there, all of them now — hers and the others, the ones addressed to Tomas, to Ruth, to my father, to names I'd had to look up. Forty years of mail that had died behind a wardrobe in a building scheduled for demolition. I'd sorted them on my kitchen table into piles by decade, the way Marisol sorted cleaning supplies, and the exercise had told me something I didn't want to know: that the letters thinned out over time. People stop writing to the dead. Even the living ones.

Ines took the top letter, turned it over, read her own handwriting from 1987 like a woman checking a grocery list against her memory.

"I was angry when I wrote that one," she said. "You can tell. I press too hard when I'm angry. Look — the pen went through the paper."

"You were sixteen."

"I was right at sixteen, which is worse. Being right young is the cruelest thing that can happen to a person. Nobody believes you and you never get over it." She set the letter down with a care that contradicted everything she'd just said. "Rosa took the manuscript in March. I remember because the forsythia was out and I thought, well, that's an omen if I ever saw one, and I ignored it, because I wanted so badly to believe her. *For safekeeping.* Do you know what that phrase does in a family like ours? It doesn't mean she'll keep it safe. It means it's hers now. Safekeeping is how theft introduces itself."

"I played it," I said. "For eleven years. The sonata. The exercises. All of it."

"I know."

"Did you know? All along?"

She looked at me then, and there was nothing soft in it, and I was grateful, because everyone else in my family had always been soft with me, and softness is just another way of handling something you don't want to break.

"I knew the music," she said. "I'd hear it on the radio, on those recital broadcasts your grandmother arranged, and I'd think: that's my second movement, that's my bridge passage, she cut four bars there, she always cut four bars, she thought I was showing off." A dry sound, not quite a laugh. "And then I'd think: and there's some child playing it. Some child with our face. And I'd turn the radio off and go back to whatever life I'd built instead, which was a good life, Hazel. Don't you dare feel sorry for me. I married a man who thought I hung the moon, I raised two daughters who can't carry a tune and don't care, I taught school for thirty years and I was *good* at it. The music went somewhere else and I let it go. That was my answer to the question."

"What question?"

She didn't answer that. Not yet. Instead she said, "Your turn. Tell me what you've been doing with it. The truth. Not the version you'd tell a nurse."

So I told her. I told her about the mopping and the avoiding and Room 214, about the wardrobe and the panel and the dust, about reading her letters by flashlight in a building that smelled the same as it had when I was nine. I told her about Ruth and the taxes that were never really about taxes, about Tomas answering on the first ring, about the trust and the royalties and the scholarship with Rosa's name on it that paid for girls like the one who'd asked me to move a piano. I told her about playing in the dark, badly, then less badly, my hands remembering a music I'd been told was mine.

"And now you're here," she said, when I finished. "With a tin."

"Now I'm here with a tin."

"And what do you want, Hazel?"

The question landed so plainly that for a second I thought I'd misheard it. It was such a small sentence for the weight it carried. She didn't dress it up. She didn't say *what do you intend* or *what are your plans*, the way Ruth would have, wrapping the blade in linen. She just asked it, the way you'd ask a child what he wants for dinner.

"What do you want?" she said again. "The truth, or the family?"

* * *

Here is what I understood, sitting in that sunroom with the light going long and gold across the linoleum: the question wasn't new. The question was sixty years old. It was the oldest thing in our family, older than the money, older than the sonata, older than Rosa's ambition, which was itself only a symptom. The question had been sitting in the middle of every Villalobos living room like a piece of furniture nobody would acknowledge, and every one of us had arranged our whole life around it, the way you arrange a room around a piano you never play.

Rosa had answered it first, and she'd answered it with the family. She'd chosen the name, the dynasty, the myth — the prodigy factory, the scholarship fund, the recitals, the wing with our name over the door. And she'd built the whole cathedral on a stolen sixteen bars of somebody else's soul, because in her arithmetic, the family *was* the truth, and anything that threatened the family was a kind of lie. I used to think she was a monster. Sitting there, I understood her a little, and understanding her was worse. She'd looked at a gifted niece and a gifted grandson and seen not two people but one destiny, and she'd routed it through whichever vessel was more convenient. Ines was a girl from the wrong branch, unpolished, unconnected, inconvenient. I was a boy with the right name and a mother who'd do anything. Rosa didn't steal from Ines. Rosa *consolidated*. That was the horror of her: she never once thought of it as theft.

My father answered the question by not answering it. He'd inherited the myth the way you inherit a mortgage, and he'd spent his whole life making payments on something he never chose, keeping the books, keeping the smile, keeping the story straight at every holiday table. When I walked out at nineteen he didn't rage. He went quiet in a way I mistook for disappointment and now recognize as relief — the relief of a man watching his son refuse the inheritance he'd never had the nerve to refuse himself. He answered the question by letting me answer it wrong, and living with that.

Ruth answered the question with silence, which is its own kind of answer, and the most expensive one. She'd known. She'd known for decades — I was sure of it now, sure the way you're sure about a person's handwriting. She was the archivist, the keeper of accounts, and a keeper of accounts who doesn't publish the accounts is not a neutral party. She'd filed Ines away under *resolved* and dusted the shelf every Christmas. But she'd also kept the east door knowledge, the locks, the maps of the family's machinery in her head, waiting — I'd have sworn she was waiting — for someone else to open the box so she wouldn't have to. Ruth's answer was: let the truth survive, but let someone else carry it. Cowardice with excellent penmanship.

Tomas answered the question by leaving. He'd walked out over the feud, over the money, over the whole arrangement, and he'd built a life in another city with another name on the door, and he'd told me on the phone, in that dry lawyer's voice: "I didn't leave because of the music, Hazel. I left because I watched everyone choose, and I didn't like any of the answers." He'd taken himself out of the equation. Clean. And it had cost him every holiday, every funeral, every name-day call, and he'd paid it without complaint, but he'd paid it.

And Ines — Ines had answered it at sixteen, and then again at thirty, and then again at fifty, and every time she'd answered *the family*, in the sense of walking away from it, of letting the music go where it wanted, of building a life so full and decent that the theft became a scar instead of a wound. She'd told me: don't feel sorry for me. She'd meant it. But she'd also kept every returned letter, and she'd written *Aunt Rosa says it will be played under your name* with the pen going through the paper, and you don't press that hard unless some part of you is still waiting for someone to come and ask the question out loud.

And me. What had I answered?

At nineteen, I'd answered by walking out — but I hadn't known what I was walking out of. I'd thought I was refusing the pressure, the recitals, the machinery of being a prodigy. I hadn't known I was refusing a theft. My whole rebellion had been built on incomplete information. I'd spent thirty years thinking I'd chosen freedom, and now it turned out I'd chosen ignorance, and the two look identical from the inside until someone opens a wardrobe.

And since the box — what had I done? I'd read. I'd visited. I'd asked questions I already knew the answers to. I'd played in the dark where nobody could hear. I'd told exactly three people, one of whom was a lawyer who'd already left and one of whom was a woman who mopped halls beside me. I had a tin full of evidence and a demolition date and a truth that would die with the walls if I didn't do something, and what I'd done was nothing, beautifully, elaborately nothing, dressed up as diligence, dressed up as respect for Ines, dressed up as *waiting for the right moment* — which is the family phrase, the ancestral phrase, the phrase Rosa must have used when she took the manuscript. For safekeeping. Until the time is right.

I was not Rosa. But I was keeping something safe that wasn't mine to keep.

"You're doing it now," Ines said.

I looked up. She'd been watching me the whole time I wasn't answering, with the patience of a woman who'd waited sixty years and could wait out my silence like weather.

"Doing what?"

"Going somewhere. Your father does it. Ruth does it. You get a question you can't answer, so you go into the back room of yourself and start rearranging the furniture." She tapped the arm of her chair. "I asked you a simple thing. The truth, or the family. It's a binary, Hazel. I know young people don't like binaries, but this one's real. I've watched our family for sixty years and every single one of them answered it, whether they said so or not. Rosa said the family. Your father said the family, quietly, by never saying anything. Ruth said the family with footnotes. Tomas said the truth and moved to a different area code. I said the truth and then I got tired and said the family by saying nothing." She folded her hands on the blanket. "So. You. The truth, or the family."

"And if I say the truth?"

"Then you'll lose people. Real ones. Cousins who've done nothing wrong, aunts who'll cry at funerals and mean it, the scholarship girls, the name over the door. You'll find out which of your relatives were only ever related to you by myth." She said it without cruelty, the way a surgeon says *this will hurt*. "And if you say the family, you'll keep all of that, and you'll keep me too — I'm not going anywhere — but you'll keep me the way they kept me. Filed. Dusting the shelf. And you'll go back to mopping the floor of the building where they buried it, and you'll be very good at your job, and you'll die with clean hands and a full tin."

The television woman laughed at something. The cart went by again, squeak and all.

"And if I say the truth," I said, "what do you get?"

She was quiet for a moment. Outside the window, groundskeepers were mowing the lawn in long parallel passes, and the smell of cut grass came through the screen, absurdly ordinary, the smell of a world that had never heard of Villalobos.

"I get to hear it played correctly," she said. "Four bars restored. The ones she cut." Her voice didn't waver, but her hands did, just slightly, on the blanket. "I've been correcting it in my head for sixty years, Hazel. Every time. Every broadcast, every recording, every time one of my students butchered the simplified arrangement in a spring recital. I get to sit in a room and hear the thing I actually wrote. That's what I get. That's the whole of it. I'm eighty-one years old and my demands have gotten very small and very specific."

* * *

I drove back with the windows down and the tin on the passenger seat, belted in, because Marisol would have checked.

I didn't go home. I went to the conservatory, because it was Thursday and Marisol had the wing, and because I needed to stand in the place where the question had been buried and see it with new eyes. She was there when I came through the east door — Ruth's door, the one that locks from inside, the one Ruth had told me about in a single line of text, her one act of conversion, late and imperfect — running a buffer over the terrazzo in the empty rehearsal hall, the machine droning like a hive.

She shut it down when she saw my face.

"She asked me," I said.

Marisol waited. She was good at waiting. It was the first thing I'd liked about her, back when we were just two people on the night shift comparing mop techniques.

"The truth or the family. She asked me straight out, and I stood there in a sunroom like a man at a border crossing with no papers."

"And what did you say?"

"I didn't. I did the thing. The back-room thing. She called it — going somewhere, rearranging the furniture." I set the tin down on the piano lid, the only horizontal surface left in the gutted hall, and it made a sound like a period at the end of a sentence. "But here's what I figured out on the drive. Everybody answered. Rosa, my father, Ruth, Tomas, Ines — sixty years of answers, and the family is just the shape all those answers left behind. The myth isn't a lie anybody tells. It's what's left when everyone refuses to say the true thing out loud. And I've been doing it too. Since the box. I've been *for safekeeping-ing* the truth. Waiting for the right moment. And the right moment is a demolition date, Marisol. The walls come down in three weeks and the truth comes down with them unless it's already out."

Marisol leaned against the buffer, arms crossed, and looked at me for a long time. The work lamps threw our shadows up the wall, huge and wavering, two figures in a building that had already been condemned.

"You know what's funny?" she said. "I've been waiting for you to get here. Because I had a plan about two weeks ago and I've been sitting on it, because it needed you to want something first. A plan needs a man who's chosen." She pushed off the buffer and came toward me, and there was something in her face I'd never seen there — not the wry patience, not the kindness, but a kind of ignition. "You've chosen now. I watched you choose in the last thirty seconds. So. Do you want to hear it?"

The tin sat on the piano. The lamps buzzed. Somewhere in the walls, the old wing ticked and settled around us like a body cooling.

"Tell me," I said.
