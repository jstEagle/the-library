## 3. A Name Nobody Uses

The funeral home on Delancey Avenue had the particular smell of lilies and carpet cleaner, a combination that seemed designed to remind mourners that death was both natural and professionally managed. Rafael Underhill stood near the coffee urn with his hands folded in front of him like a man waiting for a train that had been delayed indefinitely.

He had practiced the smile in his truck mirror that morning. Not the real smile — he'd retired that one years ago, along with most of his other honest equipment — but the funeral smile, the one that said *I'm grieving appropriately and I would prefer not to be spoken to about it.* It involved a slight lift at the corners of the mouth, a softening around the eyes, and a subtle angling of the shoulders that suggested he was needed elsewhere. He'd tested it against the rearview at a red light on Route 9 and decided it was ready for public use.

It was getting a workout. The reception room held perhaps sixty people, and every one of them seemed to need something from the Underhill children — a memory confirmed, a casserole recipe requested, a hand held briefly by someone whose name Rafael could not retrieve no matter how hard he pressed on it.

"Your mother," said a woman with a brooch shaped like a cardinal, "was the finest neighbor I ever had."

"She was," Rafael agreed, deploying the smile.

"So private. Forty years on that street and I never once saw her backyard."

Rafael's smile held. Privately, he thought: *Nobody ever did.* The backyard of the house on Kessler Street was one of those places that existed more as rumor than fact, like the inside of a bank vault or the far side of the moon. His mother had kept a fence between herself and the world the way other people kept dogs.

Across the room, Marisol was working the crowd with the efficiency of a woman who had planned this event the way she planned everything — down to the guest list, the hymn selection, and the exact shade of beige the flowers should be so as not to draw attention to themselves. She wore black like a uniform she'd been issued decades ago and had kept pressed ever since. Every few minutes she caught Rafael's eye across the room and sent him a look that meant *the paperwork is in the car* or possibly *you're standing too close to the deviled eggs.* With Marisol it was hard to say. Her looks were a language with a very small dictionary and enormous range.

Dev stood by the window holding a cup of punch he hadn't drunk, talking to their mother's mail carrier, who had come out of what appeared to be genuine love. Dev had always been the one who collected affection without meaning to, the way other people collected parking tickets. He was crying a little, which he would deny later, and the mail carrier was patting his arm and saying something about how Mrs. Underhill always signed for her packages personally, never let anyone else touch the pen.

*Never let anyone else touch the pen,* Rafael thought, and felt something shift faintly in his chest, like a hairline crack opening in concrete. He looked away from them and studied the coffee urn instead, because grief was manageable if you aimed it at objects.

Dana found him there. She touched his elbow — not his hand, not his shoulder; they had negotiated their physical contact down to the elbow sometime around year nine of the marriage, in a treaty neither of them had ever put into words — and said, "You okay?"

"I'm fine."

"You've been guarding that coffee urn for twenty minutes."

"Somebody has to. It's a responsibility."

She almost smiled. For a moment they stood together in the small warm pocket of silence that still occasionally opened up between them, the way water finds a low place, and Rafael thought that maybe today, of all days, they might actually —

"The family," said a voice behind them, "of Grace Adeyemi. I'm sorry for your loss."

Rafael turned.

The woman standing at the edge of the reception was not someone either of them knew. She was tall, somewhere past fifty, wearing a green coat the color of deep water — an emerald so specific and unapologetic that it made every other coat in the room look like an apology. She had silver threading through her hair close to the scalp and hands that were clasped in front of her with a formality that belonged to another century. She stood slightly apart from the crowd, the way a person stands when they are attending an event they have been invited to by accident, or by fate, or by neither.

"You must be mistaken," Rafael said. His voice came out level, pleasant, professionally calibrated. "I'm afraid we don't know anyone by that name."

The woman looked at him. It was not a hostile look, exactly. It was the look of someone checking a measurement against a specification — confirming that the thing in front of her matched the thing described to her, tolerances and all.

"No," she said quietly. "I don't imagine you do."

And then, before Dana's hand could find his elbow again, before any question could finish forming itself in the air between them, the woman inclined her head — a small, old-fashioned bow, the kind you give to elders or to the dead — and moved along the edge of the room like a current along a seawall. Rafael watched her pass behind the cardinal-brooch woman, behind the mail carrier, behind Dev and his untouched punch. She paused once, near the photograph of their mother that Marisol had chosen — the good one, from the church anniversary dinner, where Mother was smiling with her whole face for perhaps the only time on record — and stood looking at it for a long moment with an expression Rafael couldn't read from this distance. Something between recognition and accounting.

Then she was gone. He heard the outer door open and close, a soft hydraulic sigh, and the reception resumed its murmur around the space she had occupied, closing over it like water.

"Adeyemi," Dana said. "That's Nigerian, isn't it? Yoruba?"

"I don't know." He did know. He'd known since college, since a roommate named Femi had laughed with delight at the sound of Rafael's surname and asked him where his people were from, and Rafael had said Ohio, and Femi had said *no, before that,* and Rafael had discovered that before Ohio there was nothing — a wall, smooth and load-bearing, built sometime before his earliest memories. He had never gone looking for what was behind it. In his line of work you learned early that some walls are decorative and some are structural, and you did not drill into the structural ones without a permit.

Marisol materialized beside them with the suddenness of weather. She had seen the whole exchange; Rafael could tell by the set of her jaw, which had shifted from *funeral composure* to *containment operation.*

"Who was that?" Dana asked.

"Nobody," Marisol said. "Someone confused. There's a memorial service for the Osei family at four o'clock in the other room. People wander."

"It didn't seem like confusion," Dana said. "She spoke to us directly."

"She was mistaken." Marisol picked an invisible piece of lint off her sleeve. "Rafael. The funeral director needs your signature on the cremation authorization. You're listed next after me."

"I'm always listed next after you."

"That's because I'm reliable." She said it without irony, which somehow made it worse, and steered him toward the director's office with a hand between his shoulder blades that was gentle and absolutely non-negotiable.

* * *

The signature took ninety seconds. The funeral director was a kind man with the practiced serenity of someone who had learned that every family is a disaster in formal wear, and he thanked Rafael for coming in and shook his hand and closed the door behind him with the tact of a man closing a door on forty years.

When Rafael came back out, Dev was waiting for him in the hallway, punch cup finally abandoned, eyes red-rimmed.

"Did you see her?" Dev asked. No preamble. Dev had never mastered the preamble.

"See who?"

"The woman in the green coat. She talked to me. Before — she came up while I was talking to Mr. Okafor—"

"Okafor?"

"The mail carrier. Anyway. She looked at me for a long time and she said—" Dev stopped. He rubbed his face with both hands, and for a second he looked twelve years old again, the baby of the family, the one the rest of them had silently agreed to protect from things, though none of them could ever have said from what things, specifically, since nobody had ever told them anything. "She said, 'You have her chin.' And I said, 'Whose?' And she said, 'Grace's,' like I would know. Like it was obvious. And then she left."

"Maybe she thought you were someone else."

"In a funeral home? At our mother's service?" Dev lowered his voice. "Rafael. She called us the Adeyemis. To my face. She said, 'I'm sorry for your loss' — she meant Mom — and then she said, 'The Adeyemi family has lost enough.' Just like that. And then she walked out."

Rafael leaned against the wall. The hallway was quiet; the reception's murmur came through the doors like surf. Somewhere a vacuum cleaner ran in another wing of the building, methodical and indifferent, erasing evidence.

"What did Marisol say?"

"That it was a mistake. That she was confused." Dev gave a wet, unhappy laugh. "You know what's funny? Marisol didn't even blink. Not really. She got that look — you know the one, the ledger look — and she started managing. When Marisol starts managing, it means there's something to manage."

Rafael said nothing. Twenty-two years of bridge inspection had taught him that the most important information in any structure is the information it's trying not to give you — the crack that's been painted over, the bolt that's been replaced with one slightly the wrong size, the span that everyone describes as *fine* with just a half-second too much certainty. His whole profession rested on a single principle: when a system responds too smoothly, too quickly, too rehearsed, you look closer. Systems don't practice being fine unless they have something to hide.

His sister had answered in under two seconds. That was a rehearsed answer. That was an answer with forty years of maintenance behind it.

"There was a card," Dev said suddenly. "In the basket by the guest book. I saw her put it there. I don't think she signed the book — she just slipped the card in under the programs."

They went back into the reception room together, two grown men moving with the conspiratorial gait of boys sneaking toward the cookie table, and found the basket on its stand beside the register of condolences. Programs in cream stock. A pen chained to the stand. And beneath the programs, tucked at an angle, a plain white card, heavy stock, no envelope.

Rafael picked it up.

On the front, printed in a clean serif font, was a name and nothing else:

**June Okafor**

No title. No firm. No phone number. On the back, handwritten in a small, upright script — the handwriting of someone educated by nuns, or by circumstance — was a single line:

*Ask your mother's house why it has a locked room.*

Rafael read it twice. The second time, the words rearranged themselves into something colder.

There was no locked room in the house on Kessler Street. He had grown up in that house. Three bedrooms, one bath, a basement that flooded every April, an attic accessed by a pull-cord ladder that Mother had forbidden them to use after 1987 for reasons never given. There was no locked room. There was the linen closet, which stuck. There was the crawl space, which smelled of mice. There was—

There was the room at the end of the upstairs hall. The small one. The one that had always been "storage," that had held boxes wrapped in plastic sheeting for as long as Rafael could remember, that Mother had kept shut with a door so heavy and well-fitted it closed with a sound like a vault. They had never questioned it. Children don't question architecture; they simply grow around it, the way trees grow around fences, incorporating the obstruction into their own shape until you couldn't tell anymore where the tree ended and the fence began.

"Read it," Dev said. "What does it say?"

Rafael handed him the card and watched his brother's face go through the same sequence his own had — puzzlement, denial, and then that slow settling, like silt in disturbed water, of something that could no longer be unsettled.

"Weird," Dev said, in the voice of a man describing a hurricane as *a bit windy.*

"Yeah."

"Storage room weird?"

"Storage room weird."

Across the room, Marisol was laughing at something the cardinal-brooch woman had said — a bright, controlled laugh, pitched to carry exactly as far as the nearest cluster of guests and no farther. She caught sight of the two of them by the basket, heads bent over the card, and her laugh stopped mid-note. From thirty feet away, through a room full of mourners and lilies, Rafael watched his eldest sister's face perform a small, terrible calculation, and arrive at a number she did not like.

She crossed the room. She did it unhurried, with a smile still fixed in place for the benefit of the audience, but Rafael had spent a lifetime reading spans under load, and he could see the stress moving through her, hairline fracture by hairline fracture.

"Give me that," she said quietly.

"You haven't even asked what it is," Rafael said.

"I don't need to." Her hand was out, palm up, steady. Steadier than his own, he noticed. "Whatever it says, whatever you two are inventing right now — today is not the day. We are going to get through the next three hours with dignity, we are going to thank these people, and then we are going to sit down as adults and discuss the estate. Which includes," she added, with the finality of a stamp coming down on a document, "everything in that house. Everything. Do you understand me?"

Rafael understood. That was the problem. He understood perfectly that Marisol's plan — sell fast, settle clean, distribute, disperse, done — depended entirely on the house containing nothing but furniture and forty years of ordinary dust. He understood that the card in his hand was, in engineering terms, an unexpected load applied to a member already rated questionable.

He also understood that he was not going to give it to her.

"Not yet," he said, and heard his own voice do something it almost never did: refuse. Gently, pleasantly, with the funeral smile firmly in place — but refuse. "After the service. We'll talk after."

Marisol looked at him for a long moment. Behind her, the reception flowed on, oblivious — casseroles being praised, phone numbers being exchanged, the dead being remembered in the past tense by people who had only ever known her present tense. Somewhere in that flow, a woman in a green coat was already miles down the road, carrying their mother's real name like a coal in her cupped hands.

"Fine," Marisol said at last. "After." But her eyes stayed on the card a beat too long, and Rafael saw — clearly, unmistakably, the way you see a crack widen under a load test — that she recognized the handwriting.

Not the name. Not the message.

The handwriting.

* * *

That night, long after the casseroles had been distributed and the guests dispersed, Rafael sat in his truck outside his own house — the condo, Dana's and his, the one with the parallel schedules — with the engine off and the card lying on the passenger seat under the dome light.

*Ask your mother's house why it has a locked room.*

He took out his phone and typed the name into the search bar. **June Okafor.** The results came back scattered and useless: a dentist in Atlanta, a deceased professor, a LinkedIn profile with no photo. He tried **Adeyemi** and stopped with his thumb hovering over the screen, feeling like a man standing at the railing of his own bridge, looking down at water he'd spent his whole life assuming was shallow.

He didn't search. Not tonight. Instead he sat in the dark and let the question do its slow work, the way water does, finding every seam.

Because here was the thing he hadn't told Dev, hadn't told Dana, hadn't told anyone: when the woman in the green coat had said *Adeyemi,* something in Rafael's chest had not gone cold. It had gone *warm.* One degree, maybe two — barely measurable, well within tolerance. But he was a man who measured things for a living, and he knew the difference between a structure responding to a stranger and a structure recognizing something it had been carrying all along.

Tomorrow he would drive to Kessler Street. Tomorrow he would stand in the upstairs hall, in front of the storage-room door with its vault-heavy swing, and put his hand on the knob.

Tonight he just sat there, holding a stranger's card, listening to his own heart conduct its quiet inspection — and finding, to his considerable unease, that the structure checked out fine everywhere except one small room at the end of the hall, which had been rated safe for forty years by the only inspector who'd ever lived there.

And she wasn't talking.
