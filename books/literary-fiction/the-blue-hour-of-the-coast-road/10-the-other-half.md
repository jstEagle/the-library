## 10. The Other Half

Sylvie's house sat on the far side of the estuary, a low white thing with blue shutters gone gray, facing the water the way her father's boats once had. She didn't invite Bram in so much as fail to stop him from following her up the path, and he understood that this was the invitation — grudging, provisional, revocable at any moment.

Inside it smelled of coffee and old paper. Not the sweet rot of his mother's house but something drier, more deliberate. Sylvie had kept an archive where Marguerite had kept a hiding place. That difference told him almost everything about the two of them before either spoke.

She set two cups on the kitchen table without asking how he took his. Black, apparently, for both of them.

"You said your father kept letters," Bram said.

"I said he kept *her* letters." Sylvie sat across from him, arms folded, a woman building a wall out of her own body. "Forty-one of them. Every one she ever sent. He answered most of them. Those are here too." She nodded toward a wooden chest in the corner, iron-banded, the kind of object that outlives its owners on purpose.

"May I?"

"You may read. You may not take. You may not photograph." She paused. "And you will read them in order. He was very particular about order. It was the only tidiness in him."

Bram almost smiled at that — the same compulsion lived in him, in his notebooks, in his catalogs of unsaid things — but the smile would have been a trespass, so he swallowed it.

The chest opened with a sound like a held breath released. Inside, bundled in linen ribbon gone soft as skin, lay envelopes. Her mother's hand on every one. And beneath them, in a slanted, impatient script he recognized from the return address he'd traced up the coast: Aurel's replies.

*The other half,* he thought. *There is always another half.*

He lifted the first reply. Dated eight months after the earliest letter in his shoebox. His hands were not steady. Sylvie watched them shake and said nothing, which was its own kind of mercy.

---

The letter began without greeting, as if the greeting were understood, as if names were for strangers.

*I walked past your window last night and did not stop. This is my new discipline. I am becoming a man who can walk past windows. Ask me anything else and I will fail at it, but this I can do now, I can walk past your window like a man with somewhere to be.*

*You wrote that you told him nothing and never will. I believe you. I have to believe you; belief is the only furniture left in this room. But I want you to know what I know, which is that a silence is not a wall, it is a debt. Someone always comes to collect.*

Bram looked up. The kitchen light had gone amber with the afternoon. Sylvie was watching the estuary, not him, giving him privacy the only way she could — by pretending not to grant it.

"He knew," Bram said. "From the beginning, he knew there'd be a price."

"Keep reading," Sylvie said. "You haven't gotten to the part yet."

---

He read for an hour. Maybe two. The letters moved through seasons the way tides move through a harbor — filling, draining, filling again. There were years when Aurel wrote weekly, buoyant, full of fish prices and boat repairs and jokes Bram couldn't hear the other side of. There were gaps. One gap of fourteen months that made Bram's chest hurt until the next letter explained it in a single line: *I heard about the boy starting school. I hope he has your stubbornness and none of mine.*

*The boy.* Him. A stranger's pride in him, folded into a sentence, sent to a woman who could never answer openly. Bram felt the jealousy from the wardrobe letters curdle into something worse — grief for a man he'd never met who had loved him at a distance, the way you love a fire you can see from the sea.

Then, near the bottom of the chest, the tone changed.

The date meant nothing to Bram at first. Then it did. He did the arithmetic twice, three times, because the first answer was impossible.

The year he turned nine.

---

*They came to the co-op on Tuesday,* the letter read, the handwriting suddenly cramped, pressed hard, a pen driven like a nail. *Not lawyers. Worse than lawyers. Men in good coats who used to nod to me on the quay. They sat in Delacroix's office and when they left, my license was under review. Fuel allocation suspended pending inquiry. My mooring rights questioned. An anonymous letter to the federation about irregular catches — irregular catches, Marguerite, I have fished these waters since I was six years old, I could find the herring blindfolded by their smell alone.*

*By Friday it was everywhere. No one would crew for me. Old Tomas, who taught me gutting, looked at his shoes when I passed. Your husband did not come himself. He never had to come himself. He signed nothing, said nothing, spent — what? A few thousand francs? Less than he spends on wine? And twenty years of my life became a rumor about me.*

*I want you to understand that I am not writing this to make you choose. I am writing it because if I don't put it down somewhere it will eat me alive, and you are the only place I have ever put things down.*

*But I will tell you what I told no one: he offered me money first. Did you know that? A man came to my door with an envelope. I threw it in the harbor. I should have kept it. I should have framed it. Because then everyone would have seen what my ruin actually cost him — pocket change. The price of a decent dinner.*

Bram set the letter down. His hands had gone cold despite the warm kitchen.

"Sylvie."

"I know which one you're holding." She still hadn't turned from the window. "My father called it the winter of the good coats. He talked about it the way other men talk about wars."

"There are more."

"There are eleven more like it. Then there are the ones after — the ones where he's working the mainland docks, living above a bar, writing to her like nothing happened because the letters were the only thing they hadn't managed to take." Now she turned, and her face was not hostile anymore. It was tired in a way Bram recognized from his own mirror. "Read the one from March. The last one before the gap."

---

March. The year Bram turned ten. He remembered that spring in fragments — new shoes, a tutor, the family moving to the city for reasons he was never given. Reasons he was never given. That was the whole architecture of his childhood: load-bearing silences dressed up as adult discretion.

Aurel's letter was short. Shortest in the chest.

*It's over. Not because I stopped — I will never stop, that's the disease of me — but because staying in your life is costing you more than I'm worth, and we both know whose arithmetic that is.*

*He came to see me himself. First time. He sat in the bar below my room and bought me a brandy like we were old friends, and he said: "She has a son. Whatever you think you feel, ask yourself what it becomes for the boy if it continues." And then he left the envelope again, and this time I took it.*

*Understand me. I took it because of you. Because a boy should not learn what his name costs other people. Because I have watched what happens to men this coast decides to forget, and I would rather sell myself than make you watch it too.*

*So this is the last letter. I am taking the mainland work. I am going to be a man who walks past windows professionally.*

*Tell him nothing. Tell him especially nothing when he's older and starts asking why his father flinches at certain harbors. Let the boy have the clean version. Clean versions are the only inheritance some of us can afford to leave.*

*One day, maybe, when he's grown —*

The letter stopped there. No ending. Just the dash, and then blankness, as if even the page had decided some doors stay shut.

Bram realized he was gripping the paper hard enough to crease it and made himself let go.

---

"The envelope," he said. His voice came out wrong, scraped. "He took the money. My father paid him off and then destroyed him anyway?"

"No." Sylvie stood, crossed to the chest, and pulled out a final bundle — thinner, businesslike, typed pages among the handwritten ones. "That's the part my father never wrote down because he never fully understood it himself. He thought taking the money ended it. It didn't. Look."

Typed pages. Copies — carbon copies, someone had kept receipts — of correspondence between a fishing federation and a licensing board. Dates spanning four years after Aurel left the coast. Every application Aurel made for a mainland license: denied. Every appeal: denied, citing irregularities. A man trying to rebuild a life with one hand tied behind him by paperwork, forever.

"My father applied for licenses in three departments," Sylvie said. "Denied in all three. He finally got work through a cousin, unlicensed, cash, no future in it. He died still technically unemployable in his own trade." She laid the pages flat. "Your father didn't pay him off. He paid him off and then made sure the payment was the last money he'd ever earn honestly. The bribe wasn't an exit. It was evidence. Take the money and you've confessed to something; refuse it and you're ruined anyway. Either way, Joubert wins."

The kitchen was very quiet. Somewhere outside a gull said something ugly and repeated it.

Bram thought about his childhood home. The good coats. The wine bill. The flinch his father had, which he'd noticed once at a harbor restaurant in the south and never asked about — the way the man's eyes went to the fishing boats and then away, quickly, like touching a bruise through clothing.

He thought about the fortune. The houses, the schools, the trust fund that had made his criticism possible — that had made *him* possible, the freedom to sit in three-star restaurants describing food he couldn't taste, cushioned at every point by money that had, at one end of its long pipeline, been squeezed out of a fisherman's mooring rights.

*Inheritance revealed as theft.* The phrase arrived in his head complete, and he hated it, and he knew it was true.

"He built everything on this," Bram said. "On making sure no one ever —"

"On making sure no one ever remembered," Sylvie corrected. "There's a difference. Ruin is loud. Forgetting is quiet. Your father was very good at quiet." She looked at him then, straight on, and for the first time since the doorstep there was something in her face that wasn't armor. Pity, maybe. Or recognition. "So was your mother, from the sound of those letters you found. Two people keeping the same secret from opposite ends of it. And you in the middle, raised on the clean version."

Bram stood. He needed to stand; sitting felt like drowning in slow motion. He walked to the window Sylvie had abandoned and looked out at the estuary, at the water his father had weaponized and Aurel had loved and Marguerite had written across for thirty years in a language no one else could read.

The rage came up through him like a tide coming into a harbor — not sudden, total. It filled every space. Rage at his father, dead twelve years and beyond the reach of anything. Rage at his mother, who had known, who had chosen the clean version for him, who had sealed the truth in a shoebox behind a wardrobe instead of putting it in his hands. Rage at himself, thirty years of the clean version, swallowing it whole, building a career on top of it like a house built over a sinkhole.

"You're angry," Sylvie said behind him.

"Yes."

"Good. My father said anger is just grief that's found an address." She gathered the letters back into their bundles with quick, practiced motions, restoring the order that was the only tidiness in him. "Don't waste it on the dead. They can't feel it and you'll exhaust yourself."

"Then what do I do with it?"

Sylvie tied the ribbon. She was quiet long enough that Bram turned around.

"There's a lawyer," she said. "Been calling the village about the house. Odile mentioned it — the developer's man, pushing papers. Everyone says the Joubert signature is a formality now." She handed him the last bundle, the typed carbines, the receipts of ruin. "Take these. You're the one who should have them. But Bram — before you sign anything, before you give that man one more piece of paper with your name on it — you might want to ask yourself what else your father bought on this coast, and who's still holding the receipts."

Outside, the light had begun to fail toward evening, and far up the coast road, in the direction of the house he was supposed to sign away in eighteen days, the first blue hour was gathering like ink in water.
