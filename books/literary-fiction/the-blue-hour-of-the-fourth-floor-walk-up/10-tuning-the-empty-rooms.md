## 10. Tuning the Empty Rooms

The building was emptier than it had ever been, and Zephyrine found he could hear everything.

Thirty years of tenancy had taught him that a full building is a muffled instrument—voices, televisions, radiators knocking their small complaints, all of it felted, damped. But now the walk-up stood hollow, its rooms stripped to plaster and floorboard, and every sound he made came back to him with nowhere to hide. His boots on the stairs. His breath in the stairwell. The jingle of his tuning kit like a bell on a cat that no longer needed to catch anything.

He started on the ground floor. Mrs. Okonkwo's upright, the one with the missing ivory on middle C that she'd refused for two decades to let him replace—"it's how I know which key is which," she'd said—and which now sat abandoned in an empty parlor, its bench gone to her daughter's house in Yonkers along with everything else worth carrying. He opened the lid. The piano had gone sharp over the winter, the way pianos do when nobody plays them, tightening toward a pitch no one asked for. He tuned it anyway. There was no one to hear it. That was the point.

*You tune for the room,* his father used to say, back when saying things out loud was still something this family did. *The room is your client too.*

His father had said a great many things before the winter of 1979, and then he had said nothing at all, forever, and the family had arranged itself around that nothing like furniture around a hole in the floor. Zephyrine had spent thirty years being the hole's most diligent custodian. He had catalogued the unsaid things the way he catalogued broken hammers: Aurelio's cough, never mentioned; Consuelo's unopened letters from her mother in Ponce; Marisol's cedar chest, locked since before Consuelo was born. And underneath all of it, at the bottom of the inventory where he kept the item he could not look at directly: *I tipped the creditors. I let him run. I chose Aurelio.*

He had confessed it in March, in Marisol's kitchen, with Aurelio three floors up dying by inches in Lenox Hill. The confession had not fixed anything. That was the thing nobody told you about truth—it wasn't a repair. It was more like removing a damper felts from strings that had been muted so long they didn't know how to sound. Everything rang now. Everything hurt. The family had spent April and May ringing at each other, Marisol furious in the way of a woman who has been robbed of her best-kept thing, Aurelio silent in his hospital bed in a way that was different from his old silence—he was deciding something, and everyone could see him deciding it.

What he decided, they learned in June, when he called Consuelo into his hospital room and told her everything. The debts, the bailiffs, the flight, the tip-off, the thirty years of blame laid on a man who had, whatever else he was, only done what Zephyrine asked him to do. Consuelo had come out of that room looking like someone who'd been handed a heavy box and told to carry it home. She carried it. She was twenty-six and she carried it better than any of them had.

The demolition notice had gone up in April, orange paper against brick, ninety days. The ninety days ended today.

Zephyrine climbed to the second floor. The Delgado family's spinet, water-damaged from the burst pipe two winters back, keys yellowed like old teeth. He tuned what could be tuned and left the rest, because there was such a thing as honesty even in piano work—you didn't make a dead string pretend. Third floor: the Alvarez console, the one the children had pounded on, its hammers grooved from a decade of enthusiasm. He voiced them gently with his needle file, softening the strike, giving the piano back some of the roundness the children had knocked out of it. Somewhere in Bay Ridge or Queens those children were growing up without knowing they'd once owned a piano's whole heart.

Fourth floor. His own rooms, empty now, his tools already moved to the studio apartment on 108th that smelled, inexplicably, of cedar. He had left the piano—the old upright his mother had bought secondhand in 1968, the one she'd written the lullaby on, though "written" was generous. She had played it into existence, note by note, in the blue hour of the evening, and never set it down on paper, and never recorded it, and had apparently decided, sometime last winter without telling anyone, that she was going to die soon and that the song should outlive the secrecy. So she dictated it to a retired social worker named Edith Kaminsky, who took it down in pencil on staff paper, and mailed the tape to a community radio station, and let the song go out into the night air of the neighborhood like a bird released from a house it had been kept in too long.

A song with no address, Zephyrine had called it, that first night in March. He'd been wrong. It had always had an address. It had been addressed to the brother they didn't speak of—the first brother, Tomás, who died at nine months in the winter of 1957, before Zephyrine was born, before any of them, the original silence around which all the other silences had been built. Marisol had finally said his name in April, standing in her kitchen with her hands flat on the counter as if bracing against weather. It had taken seventy-one years and a radio broadcast, but she'd said it.

Zephyrine sat down at the upright. He checked the pitch out of habit—A440, steady, the piano holding its breath the way it had held it through everything—and then he did not tune anything. He just played.

The lullaby came up out of his hands the way water finds a channel it cut long ago. Eight bars, then the turn, then the descending figure his mother had always hummed under, the part that sounded like someone walking down stairs very quietly so as not to wake a child. He had heard it maybe five hundred times in his childhood and never once played it, because playing it would have meant admitting he remembered it, and remembering it would have meant asking about it, and asking was the one crime this family had never codified because it was simply understood to be impossible.

Now he played it in an empty building on its last day, and the building heard it. That sounds like nothing, but Zephyrine had spent thirty years learning what buildings hear. The steam lines in the walls, dead now, cold, still carried the shape of the song the way a riverbed carries the shape of water after the water is gone. The plaster, stripped to lath in the corners, gave the sound nowhere to go except back. The fourth floor, which had watched him be invisible for thirty years, listened to him be audible for four minutes and eleven seconds, and did not collapse.

When he finished, he sat with his hands in his lap and let the sustain pedal up slowly, and the room went quiet in the ordinary way rooms go quiet, which is to say not quiet at all, just waiting.

* * *

They gathered on the sidewalk at six, the way you gather outside a place that is about to stop existing.

Not all of them. Not arranged, for once, in the old formation—Marisol at the center, Aurelio flanking, everyone else orbiting at distances calibrated to what they were allowed to know. That formation was gone. What replaced it was looser and less comfortable: people standing where they happened to stand.

Marisol came in a taxi, which she complained about, and leaned on Consuelo's arm, which she permitted. She was smaller than she'd been in March. Grief and confession had taken weight off her the way illness does, but her eyes were clear in a way Zephyrine had never seen—she had spent her whole life looking at her family through the gauze of what couldn't be said, and now the gauze was gone and everything was too bright and she refused to squint.

Aurelio came in a wheelchair, oxygen tank beside him, driven by a home aide whose name he pretended not to remember. His lungs had thirty years of boiler work in them and the doctors gave him language like *management* and *palliative*, words that meant the same thing *ninety days* had meant on the orange paper. He looked up at the walk-up with the expression of a man reviewing a ledger he has finally balanced. In May, from his hospital bed, he had told Zephyrine: *You paid my debt twice. Once in '79 and once every day since. I'm not going to thank you for the second one, because you shouldn't have paid it.* It was the closest thing to absolution the family produced, and Zephyrine had accepted it the way you accept a tuning that's close enough—knowing perfect is a fiction, knowing close enough is the actual art.

Consuelo stood apart, holding her phone, on which she had, two weeks ago, recorded her mother in Ponce singing half-remembered songs, the beginning of an archive no one had asked her to build. She had opened the letters. All of them. She told Zephyrine it was like reading mail from a stranger who turned out to be herself.

Mrs. Okonkwo came, uninvited and unsurprised, because she had lived across the street for forty years and considered the sidewalk public domain. She brought a folding chair and did not sit in it.

Nobody spoke much. That was the difference between this silence and the old one, and Zephyrine, connoisseur of silences, could have written the catalogue entry himself. The old silence had been load-bearing—a machine, maintained, each of them a part turning inside it. This new silence was just silence, the kind that exists between people who have said the hard things and are now discovering that the hard things were not actually the bottom, that beneath them there is more talking to do, years of it, the ordinary excavation of lives that were hidden even from themselves.

At seven, the city's crew arrived with floodlights and plastic sheeting, polite and procedural, and a foreman with a clipboard asked if everyone was clear of the structure.

"One minute," Zephyrine said.

He went back inside. He climbed the four flights in the dark, past the empty rooms, past the places where the radiators had stood like sentries for a century, and he sat down at the upright one more time, and he played the lullaby again—not for the building this time, but loud, open, the lid propped full stick, the way you play when you want the sound to leave the room and go find someone.

Through the open window, down four stories, onto the sidewalk where his family stood among the moving boxes of their own history, the song arrived. All of them heard it. Marisol closed her eyes. Aurelio took off his nasal cannula for the duration, which the aide protested and he ignored. Consuelo raised her phone and recorded it, because that was her ministry now: making sure things got kept.

And below, on the pavement, chalked in Consuelo's handwriting that afternoon, where the demolition notice had been until someone tore it down, was the address—not the building's, which was ending, but the song's, which wasn't:

*For Tomás, 1956–1957. And for whoever needs it. Ask at the Villalobos walk-up, fourth floor.*

The building came down over the following weeks, floor by floor, dust and rebar and the slow erasure of a century. But the song had already moved out. It lived now in a recording on a phone, in a tape at a community radio station, in the pencil notation Edith Kaminsky had made, in the hands of a woman in Ponce who didn't know yet that she'd sung it as a child. It had an address that was not a building. It was addressed, the way all real songs are, to whoever was listening.

On the last evening, walking away from the rubble with his tuning kit over his shoulder, Zephyrine passed the community station on Delancey, and through its window he saw the overnight host setting up, and taped to the mixing board, a request slip in unfamiliar handwriting:

*Play the lullaby again tonight. My daughter was born this morning. I don't know whose song it is, but I want it to be hers too.*

Zephyrine stood at the window a long moment, invisible as ever, and smiled, and kept walking.
