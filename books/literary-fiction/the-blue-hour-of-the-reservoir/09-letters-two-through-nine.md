## 9. Letters Two Through Nine

I did not sleep that night. I sat at my mother's kitchen table with the shoebox open in front of me like a wound I had decided to keep touching, and I read.

The first letter had been dated June 14th, 1968. The second was dated nine days later, and by then her handwriting had already changed. In the first letter she had written fast and slantwise, the pen digging through the paper. By the second letter the hand had steadied itself into something careful, almost formal — the handwriting of a girl who had decided that if she was going to do this thing, she was going to do it properly.

*Dear E,* it began. *I know you will not answer. I am writing anyway because Mrs. Okonkwo says a person should write to the dead the way they write to anyone else, only slower.*

*I went back to the water today. They have put up a new sign, red paint on board, TRESPASSERS WILL BE PROSECUTED, as if the water were a room we were not allowed into anymore. As if it had ever been ours. I stood there for one hour exactly. I counted. Counting helps. You taught me that, or maybe I taught you.*

*I want to tell you what happened but every time I get to the middle of it my mind goes white, like paper held up to the sun. So I will tell you around it. I will tell you the edges of the day and maybe the middle will come back on its own.*

*The edges: it was hot. Your shirt was the blue one with the torn collar. There was a dog barking somewhere far off, the same three barks over and over, like a clock that only knew one time.*

*That is all I have. Nine days and all I have are edges.*

*Your sister,*
*Ruth*

---

I set that one down and pressed my palms flat against the table until they stopped trembling. My mother's name was Ruth. Of course it was; I had known it my whole life, had said it at the funeral, had printed it on the order of service myself. But seeing it signed at the bottom of a letter to a dead boy made her a stranger again. *Ruth.* A girl's name. A girl who counted hours at the edge of forbidden water because counting helped.

The third letter was dated July 2nd.

*Dear E,*

*They closed the school early today because of the heat. Everyone poured out onto the pavement like spilled milk. I stood in the crowd and felt nothing, which I think is worse than feeling something. Grief is supposed to look like something. Mine looks like a girl standing in a crowd doing arithmetic in her head so she doesn't have to be where she is.*

*Mama asked me why I don't swim anymore. I said the pool was dirty. She believed me. People believe whatever you tell them if you say it flat enough. That is the trick, E. Say it flat. I am getting very good at saying things flat.*

*I dream about your hands. Not holding mine. Just your hands, open, the way hands look when they're not holding anything. I wake up and my own hands are open too, like they're waiting for yours to arrive, and they never do, and I lie there being furious at them for waiting.*

*Your sister,*
*Ruth*

---

By the fourth letter, August, the season had turned inside her. The heat that had opened the story was now the thing pressing down on it.

*Dear E,*

*I did a terrible thing today and I'm going to tell you because you're the only one I can't lie to anymore. Lying to the living is easy now. Lying to you makes my mouth taste like coins.*

*A woman at church asked Mama how we were managing. Managing. As if grief were a household budget. And Mama said, We manage, God gives strength, and I stood there nodding, nodding, and inside me there was a voice saying: tell her. Tell her what really happened at the water. Tell her that her God wasn't watching that day or He would have—*

*But I didn't. I smiled. I have learned to smile the way other people learn instruments, with practice, daily, until the fingers know their way without being told.*

*Here is the terrible thing: it worked. Smiling works. People stop looking at you. They stop asking. The world wants so badly to believe you are fine that it will take the smallest evidence and run with it.*

*I could go my whole life this way, E. That is what frightens me. Not that I can't speak. That I can, and I choose not to, and it gets easier every single time.*

*Your sister,*
*Ruth*

---

I read four more that night before dawn came gray through the kitchen window and made the page hard to see. I read them in order, because order mattered — I understood that even then, sitting in my mother's house with her ghost still warm in the rooms. These letters were not separate documents. They were one long sentence, and each envelope was just a breath taken in the middle of it.

The fifth letter, September:

*Dear E,*

*School started again. Your desk is gone. They gave it to a boy named Daniel who chews his pencils and laughs at everything. I watched him sit down in it and I felt nothing, which I told you last month is worse than feeling something, but I've changed my mind. Feeling nothing is not worse. Feeling nothing is a room you can live in. It has no windows but it has no weather either.*

*Mama has stopped setting your place at dinner. She does it without ceremony, just one less plate, and nobody says anything, and that silence at the table is so loud I have to leave sometimes. But here is what I noticed: after a while, I stopped leaving. After a while, the loudness became normal. That is how it happens, E. That is how a person disappears from a table. Not all at once. One plate at a time.*

*I wonder if that's what I'm doing to you. Taking you away one plate at a time. Making you manageable.*

*Forgive me. Or don't. Just keep existing somewhere so the forgiveness has somewhere to land.*

*Your sister,*
*Ruth*

---

The sixth letter was different. Shorter. The handwriting had begun its long slide toward the small, compressed script I knew from grocery lists and birthday cards — the handwriting of my mother's whole adult life, which I now understood was not her natural hand at all but the hand of someone permanently braced.

*Dear E,*

*Someone asked me today if I was the girl whose brother drowned. Asked me directly, in the street, outside the pharmacy. And I said yes, and she took my arm and looked at me with such pity that I nearly told her everything right there between the pharmacy and the fruit stand.*

*Nearly.*

*Then I heard myself say: Thank you. I'm managing.*

*Managing. That word again. It has become my whole vocabulary. How are you. Managing. Will you be all right. Managing. Say it flat, Ruthie. Flat as water with no wind on it.*

*Sometimes I practice telling the truth out loud when I'm alone. I say: Elias slipped. I say: I hesitated. I say: I swam and he didn't. Four words, five words, six words. Small words. A child could carry them. And then I say them louder, to the ceiling, to the walls, and the walls don't flinch, and I realize the truth is not heavy at all. It is light as a bird. It is only the keeping of it that weighs.*

*Why do I keep it, E? Answer me that. You owe me an answer. You owe me several.*

*Your sister,*
*Ruth*

---

I got up and walked around the kitchen. The refrigerator hummed. Outside, a delivery truck ground through its gears at some ungodly hour, and I thought: the city never stops making noise, and neither did she, inside, all those years, and none of it ever reached me. I had lived thirty-eight years in the house of a woman screaming silently and I had catalogued it as quiet.

I sat back down. Letter seven, October.

*Dear E,*

*Winter is coming and I find I'm glad. Heat is your season. Cold belongs to me now, or I belong to it. Cold keeps things. Cold is honest storage.*

*I have started keeping things. I don't know when it started. Your marble, the green one with the twist of glass inside — I took it from your tin months ago and I haven't put it back. I keep it in my pocket. At school I hold it under my desk during lessons and press it into my palm until it leaves a mark. The mark fades by evening. I make it again the next morning. This is my religion now, E. Small marks, renewed daily.*

*Papa drinks more. Mama prays more. I catalogue. That's the family division of labor. Papa drowns his, Mama buries hers, and I file mine. Alphabetically. Chronologically. Anything to keep it from being a thing that happened and becoming instead a thing that is kept.*

*Except it already happened. Filing doesn't change that. Nothing changes that. I understand now that I will be eleven forever. Some part of me fell into that water with you and stayed down there, and the part that climbed out is just the part that knows how to say "managing."*

*Your sister,*
*Ruth*

---

Letter eight arrived in November, and with it the first appearance of the strategy that would define the rest of her life. I recognized it the way you recognize your own face in a photograph taken before you were born.

*Dear E,*

*I have decided something and I need you to witness it, because you are the only witness I trust.*

*I have decided never to tell.*

*Not never-tell-like-a-secret. Secrets leak. Secrets sit in you and swell. I mean never-tell-like-a-rule. Like a librarian's rule. The truth goes on the shelf. It gets a number. It does not circulate.*

*Don't look at me like that. Yes, I hear how it sounds. But listen: everyone who knew is either silent or gone. Mama won't speak of it. Papa won't speak at all. The town has decided it was an accident, which it was, mostly, probably — and if I speak now, after all these months of smiling, I don't undo the drowning. I only undo myself. Everything I built to survive it comes down. Do you understand? The silence isn't hiding the truth anymore. The silence IS the structure. I live in it. Take it away and there's no floor.*

*Someday, maybe. Someday when I'm old and it doesn't matter. Someday I'll put it all in a box and—*

*No. Even writing that, I feel the pull of it, and I'm not going to promise. Promises to the dead are just another way of lying.*

*I'll say this much: if I ever do tell, it will be written. Never spoken. Spoken words fly off. Written words stay where you put them. If the truth must exist, let it exist quietly, in ink, where it can't interrupt anyone's dinner.*

*Your sister,*
*Ruth*

---

I read that paragraph three times. *If I ever do tell, it will be written.* She had invented me, in a way. Or invented the conditions that would make me. A woman who decided that truth belonged in ink, on shelves, uncirculated — and who raised a son in a library at night, teaching him wordless games, teaching him that not-speaking was a form of love. I had thought my silence was temperament. It was inheritance. It was policy, handed down like a recipe.

And the box in my lap was the loophole she'd left herself. The someday. The shelf where the truth was allowed to exist quietly — except she had never mailed it, never shelved it anywhere but behind a wardrobe, and so it hadn't circulated for fifty years, and now it was circulating through me instead.

Letter nine was dated December 21st, 1968 — six months and a week after the water. It was the shortest of all of them, and the most frightening, because in it the girl's voice and my mother's voice finally merged into one.

*Dear E,*

*Six months. Half a year since the water. I tried to remember your voice today and couldn't. I remembered that you had one. I remembered it was higher than mine. That's all. Six months and the archive is already failing.*

*So I understand now what I have to do, and I'm going to say it plainly because plainness is all I have left: I am going to forget you on purpose. Not all at once. One plate at a time, the way Mama did. I will fold you up smaller each year until you fit in a box, and I will put the box somewhere high, and I will live.*

*You should hate me for this. I'd hate me. But hating takes a voice, and I'm saving mine.*

*Merry Christmas, E. I hope wherever you are, the water is warm.*

*Your sister,*
*Ruth*

*P.S. — I am keeping the letters. All of them. Even the ones that make me look small. Especially those. If I'm going to survive myself, I want the evidence preserved. Someone should know what it cost.*

---

Dawn had fully arrived. The kitchen was the color of dishwater. Nine envelopes lay across the table in chronological order, and I looked at them the way I looked at any collection in the stacks — with a librarian's instinct for pattern — and the pattern was unmistakable. These were not letters to a brother. They were the minutes of a trial, held in secret, over six months, at the end of which a girl sentenced herself to silence and called it survival.

And it had worked. That was the unbearable part. It had worked for fifty years. She had married, had me, had lived a whole visible life above the waterline of that summer, and the only cost — the only cost — was everything underneath. Her voice. Her son's voice. An entire family's capacity for speech, amortized across generations like a debt no one knew they were paying.

I gathered the letters, squared their edges, returned them to the box. Then I stopped, because the box felt lighter than it should. I counted. Nine read. Ten envelopes remained.

Ten.

She had written a tenth letter. The last one. Dated — I tilted the envelope toward the window — forty-one years later. Two weeks before she died.

And the address on the front was not *E.*

It was a full name. A street in the old reservoir district. A woman's name I had never heard before, written in my mother's shrunken, braced, lifelong hand:

*Odile Fontaine.*
