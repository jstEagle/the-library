## 13. An Offer of Help

Marguerite noticed the cart before he noticed her. It was parked at the end of the 700s, the philosophy shelves, and it was wrong—books shelved spine-out instead of face-in, which was Priya's system, her small rebellion against the cataloguing software that wanted everything uniform. She'd been here four months. He had learned her rhythms the way he learned all rhythms: silently, from a distance, without ever once letting her know she was being observed.

She was crouched over the reshelving trolley with a clipboard, and when he came around the corner with his dolly of returns she looked up too fast.

"You're limping," she said.

"I'm not limping."

"You're favoring your left leg like it owes you money." She stood, tucked the pen behind her ear. "Also you've been here since yesterday. I left at six. You were at the desk. I came back at ten. You were in the stacks."

"The night shift is the night shift."

"The night shift is eight hours, Marguerite. Not thirty-six."

He set the dolly down and began unloading returns onto the cart, alphabetical by author, because that was how his hands worked even when his mind was elsewhere—which lately was always, which lately was a corridor of blue water and a shoebox that breathed on his kitchen table like something sleeping. He could feel her watching him shelve. He could feel the exact weight of her attention, the way he could feel a patron hovering two aisles away, waiting to ask where the bathroom was.

"Your shelf-reading is off," she said.

"It's not."

"Row 740. Dewey order breaks at the third decimal. You never miss decimals." She flipped her clipboard around as if presenting evidence to a jury. "You missed *four* last week. I fixed them, but I want you to know that I know."

Something hot moved up the back of his neck. He had been seen. Not glanced at, not greeted, not tolerated—*seen*, itemized, cross-referenced against his own standards. It was the most intimate thing anyone had done to him in years and he hated it the way you hate a light turned on in a room you thought was dark and empty and yours.

"I'll correct it," he said.

"Don't bother, I already did." She leaned against the cart, arms folded. "Marguerite. What's going on?"

"Nothing is going on."

"Okay." She nodded slowly, in the way people nod when they are not agreeing but filing. "Then nothing is going on, and you're coming to get coffee with me when shift ends, because nothing-going-on people can drink coffee."

"I don't—"

"It's not a question. It's a statement about the future. You're good with those. You file everything under them."

* * *

Priya Anand had worked days at the Fairview branch for eleven years before the budget cuts merged the day and night staffs, and she had transferred to nights voluntarily, which everyone found either admirable or insane. She said it was because she liked the quiet. But she talked more than any night person Marguerite had ever known, filling the dead hours between two and five with stories about her sister in Toronto, her bad knee, the documentary she was half-watching on her phone with one earbud in. She narrated her own life continuously, out loud, as though afraid that if she stopped speaking it might stop being true.

Marguerite understood this impulse completely. He simply performed its opposite.

At 6:14 a.m., when the sky outside the tall windows went from black to the color of an old bruise, she appeared at the reference desk with her coat already on and her keys jingling.

"Coffee," she said. "There's a place on Delancey that opens early for the hospital crowd. They don't ask questions. Well, they ask if you want room for cream, but that's the extent of their curiosity."

"Priya."

"That's my name, yes."

"I appreciate—" He stopped. The sentence had no honest ending. *I appreciate the offer* was true but incomplete; what he appreciated was that she had made it in a way designed to be refused, standing there already braced for refusal, so that neither of them would have to feel anything. And yet she hadn't left. That was the problem. A kind person would have left.

"You appreciate," she prompted.

"I have things to do today."

"Like sleep?"

"Like errands."

"At seven in the morning."

"Some errands keep morning hours."

She studied him for a long moment. Outside, the first bus of the day sighed past the windows, lit and empty. "You know what I think?" she said. "I think you've been carrying something heavy, and you've gotten so good at carrying it that you've forgotten it's heavy. You just think that's how walking works." She shouldered her bag. "The offer stands. Today, tomorrow, whenever the nothing going on becomes something. I'm here every night. I'm very easy to find. I'm the loud one."

She went out through the staff door and he sat alone at the reference desk with his hands flat on the cool wood, feeling exactly like a man who has been offered a rope while drowning and has declined it on the grounds that he prefers to drown in an orderly fashion.

* * *

Priya

Here is what Priya knew about Marguerite Sandoval after four months of nights: he arrived at 9:58 p.m. every evening, two minutes before his shift, as though arriving earlier would constitute a confession of eagerness. He drank nothing except water from a steel bottle he refilled at the staff sink. He ate lunch—a sandwich, unwrapped with surgical care—at 2 a.m. precisely, alone, in the break room, reading. He had never once asked her a personal question. He answered hers with the minimum number of words required by grammar. And he shelved books like a monk illuminating manuscripts, with a tenderness toward objects that made her chest ache sometimes, watching him from across the floor, straightening a spine, brushing dust from a top edge, handling other people's stories more gently than he seemed to handle his own.

Her mother would have said: *that man is lonely.* Her mother diagnosed loneliness the way some doctors diagnose diabetes—by looking at your ankles.

Priya herself wasn't sure. Some people were solitary the way cats were solitary; it wasn't a wound, it was a temperament. But Marguerite wasn't solitary like a cat. He was solitary like a house with all the lights off that you happen to drive past at night—you can't prove anyone lives there, but the mailbox is full and the grass is cut, and something about it makes you slow down.

And lately the lights-off quality had changed. Lately he moved through the building like a man listening for something. Twice she'd caught him standing motionless in the middle of an aisle, staring at nothing, a book open in his hand upside down. Once—and this was the detail that decided her—she'd watched him take out a small notebook, open it, hold his pen above the page for a long time, and then close it again without writing. He did this three times in one shift. Whatever lived in that notebook, it had stopped working. Or he had.

On Thursday she found him in the local history section, of all places, pulling volumes on the city's waterworks. Reservoirs. Aqueducts. She read the titles upside down as he stacked them: *A Century of Municipal Water. The High Service Works. Engineering the Elevation.*

"Doing a paper?" she asked.

He flinched. Actually flinched, shoulders jumping like she'd touched him. "No."

"Book club?"

"No."

"A very specific hobby?"

He looked at her then, and his eyes were the eyes of a man who has been awake too long inside his own head, and she felt the strange certainty—not a suspicion, a *certainty*—that whatever those books were for, it was eating him alive.

"Marguerite," she said, quieter. "I'm not trying to pry. I'm asking if you're okay."

"I'm fine."

"You're fine the way a ceiling crack is fine. Technically holding. Getting longer."

That startled a sound out of him—half laugh, half cough, quickly suppressed, as though laughing were also against regulations. And Priya thought: *there you are.* There was a person in there who could laugh. It was buried under so many layers of quiet that it came out sounding like a malfunction, but it existed.

"Look," she said. "I'm going to say something and you're allowed to hate it. My mom used to say that grief shared is grief halved, and grief hoarded is grief multiplied. She was talking about leftovers, mostly, but it generalizes."

"I'm not grieving."

"I didn't say you were. I said the offer generalizes." She pulled a volume off his stack—*The Drowned Valleys: Communities Lost to Reservoir Construction*—and held it up. "Whatever this is. Whoever this is about. You don't have to tell me. But you should tell *someone*, and I've noticed you don't really have a someone, and I am volunteering. Reluctantly. Against my better judgment. I have enough problems."

"Why volunteer, then?"

It was the longest question he'd ever asked her, and it deserved an honest answer, so she gave it one. "Because two years ago my marriage ended and I spent eight months telling everyone I was fine, and the only reason I'm not still doing it is that a woman in my spin class wouldn't stop asking. She was annoying about it. She was relentless. I resented her for months." She put the book back on his stack. "Then one day I told her the truth in a parking lot and cried into my gym bag, and it turned out that was the whole cure. Not the crying. The saying-it. So now I'm the annoying woman. It's a promotion."

* * *

Marguerite

He walked home at dawn the long way, past the shuttered bakery, past the laundromat where Mrs. Okafor had said *the accident at the water* as easily as she might have said *the summer of the fair*, and he carried Priya's offer the way you carry a live coal—carefully, at arm's length, aware that it could warm you or burn you and unsure which frightened him more.

The terror of it was precise and nameable, and he named it, in the privacy of his skull, in the language of his trade: she wanted to *catalogue* him. That was what friendship was, really—an act of classification. Once you let someone in, they began assigning you call numbers. They learned your habits and filed them. They learned your wounds and shelved them within reach. And then you were known, and being known meant being findable, and being findable meant being losable in a way that mattered.

His mother had taught him this without ever teaching it. All those games of silence—the hour-long car rides where they competed to see who could go without speaking, the dinners conducted entirely in raised eyebrows, the way she would press one finger to her lips when he was a boy and about to say something true, and he would swallow it, and she would smile, and the smile said *good, you kept it safe*. He had thought for forty years that she was teaching him self-sufficiency. Standing in his kitchen with the shoebox open in front of him, nine letters deep into a stranger's guilt, he finally understood she had been teaching him something else. She had been teaching him that words were dangerous. That the unsaid was a room you could live in. That silence was not the absence of speech but a fortress built one withheld sentence at a time, and she had built hers so well that it took death to breach it.

And now a woman with a clipboard and a bad knee and a relentless, annoying kindness wanted to walk through the gate.

He opened the ledger—it was still open on the table, mid-sentence, the entry from three days ago that he had never finished: *Things I did not say today: I told the patron the poetry section was aisle nine, but what I did not say was*—and the pen hovered, and the pen failed, and he set it down.

Instead he said, out loud, to the empty kitchen, in a voice rusty from disuse:

"She offered me coffee."

The words hung in the air like breath in cold weather. Saying them changed nothing and changed everything. There was a witness now, even if the witness was himself.

He thought about the letters. He thought about his mother at twenty-two, sealing an envelope, stamping it, addressing it to a brother who was already underwater, and then putting it in a box instead of a mailbox—because delivery was impossible, and yet the writing had been necessary, the words had demanded to exist somewhere outside her body. He understood that now with a scholar's intimacy. The letters weren't failures to send. They were rehearsals. Practice for a confession that kept getting postponed until postponement became a life.

Was that what he was doing? Practicing?

He picked up the pen. Under the unfinished entry he wrote, in his small careful hand:

*Day 4,112. A coworker asked what is going on. I said nothing. This was inaccurate.*

He looked at the line for a long time. Then, beneath it:

*A coworker asked what is going on. What is going on: my mother had a brother. My mother had a brother and he drowned and she wrote him letters for fifty years and I have read nine of them and I cannot finish a sentence in my own ledger and a woman named Priya wants to buy me coffee.*

He wrote it all in one breath, no punctuation where punctuation should be, the sentences tumbling like children down stairs. When he finished, his hand was shaking slightly, and the kitchen was lighter somehow, or he was.

He did not give her the page. He did not even imagine giving her the page. But he closed the ledger gently, the way he closed books he loved, and he thought: tomorrow. Maybe tomorrow he would let her buy the coffee. Maybe he would sit across from another human being at 6:30 in the morning and answer one question honestly. One. He could ration honesty like water.

He slept four hours and dreamed of the reservoir, its surface flat and blue and patient, and woke at dusk with the word *Delancey* in his mouth like a coin he'd swallowed.

When he arrived at the library that night, Priya was at the reshelving cart, and she looked up, and she didn't say *you came* or *I knew you would* or anything triumphant at all. She just said, "Shift ends at six. Place on Delancey. I'll meet you there," and went back to her cart, and the not-making-a-big-deal-of-it was so precisely calibrated to his needs that he wondered, briefly, wildly, whether she had read the ledger too.

But of course she hadn't. Nobody had. That was the whole architecture of his life.

Except that at 3 a.m., shelving in the 900s, he heard her phone chime softly and heard her murmur into her earbud, and caught only a fragment—"—no, I'm working, I'll come by after, there's someone I'm worried ab—"—and then she lowered her voice and the fragment was gone.

He stood in the aisle with a biography in his hands and understood that the walls of the fortress had a flaw he had never accounted for: other people talked about you whether or not you spoke. Being silent did not make you invisible. It just made you a subject of other people's sentences.

And for reasons he couldn't yet explain—reasons that smelled like pond water and old paper—he found that he didn't mind.

He minded much less than he expected.

What he minded, what kept surfacing all night like a body that wouldn't stay down, was the title of the book he'd hidden under the reference desk that afternoon, checked out to no one, kept for himself: *The Drowned Valleys.* Because in its index, on a page he'd photocopied and folded into his pocket, was a single line about the old High Service reservoir, and beneath the line, in parentheses, a date.

June 14th.

Three weeks from now. The anniversary of the closure of the north bank—the bank that had been closed to swimmers the summer two children went into the water.

He had a date now. Dates made things real. And real things, he was beginning to learn, demanded witnesses.
