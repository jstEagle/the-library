## 16. Odile's Door

The box weighed nothing and everything.

That was the thought I kept returning to as I stood on the cracked sidewalk outside 114 Delacroix Lane, holding eleven sealed letters in a shoebox gone soft at the corners from decades of dark. Eleven letters. Maybe a pound of paper, all told. And yet my arms ached as if I'd carried it the whole way across the city on foot, which I had not—I'd taken the 42 bus, then walked twenty minutes from the stop because the route didn't go into the old reservoir district, because no route did anymore, not since they'd started pulling the streets out like teeth.

Odile Fontaine lived in a narrow house the color of weak tea, set back behind a chain-link fence that someone had tried to make kind with morning glories. The flowers had won most of the fence. Only near the gate did the metal show through, rusted and honest.

I had been standing there for eleven minutes. I knew because I counted things when I was afraid. It was an old habit, one of the games my mother taught me without ever calling them games: count the porch boards (seven), count the windows facing the street (three, one curtained in yellow), count the sounds (a dog two yards over, a wind chime made of spoons, the tick of a cooling engine—mine, parked crooked at the curb).

I had learned silence so well that now, at the one moment in my life that demanded speech, I had nothing in my mouth but air.

* * *

I should explain how I got her address, because getting it had felt, at the time, like the hard part. It was not. The hard part was this porch.

Her name had appeared in the ninth letter—not Odile herself, who wasn't born yet in 1969, but the name Fontaine, attached to an address on Rue Dauphine where Elias had lived with "C." before the water took him. My mother wrote it the way you write a thing you can't look at directly, the ink pressed so hard it nearly tore the page. From there it was library work, which is to say it was my work, the only work I have ever been good at. City directories. A marriage record. A death record for a C. Fontaine, 1994. And then, in a voter roll from six years ago, printed in a font too cheerful for its contents: **ODILE FONTAINE, 114 DELACROIX LN.**

A hospice nurse, Priya had guessed, when I told her I'd found a phone number and couldn't dial it. She'd looked it up too, or pretended she hadn't already known. "They work nights," she said. "Like you. You could go at night."

"I always go at night," I said.

"That's what I'm saying, Marguerite."

She said my name like it was a door she kept trying to open. I changed the subject, which is another thing I do instead of speaking.

So: nights. I waited until a Tuesday, because Tuesdays felt less like an intrusion than weekends, and drove out with the box on the passenger seat, belted in, which even I recognized as a strange thing to do to a shoebox. I told myself I would knock. I rehearsed it at red lights. *Hello. My name is Marguerite Hartwell. My mother was Colette Hartwell. I believe she knew your father.*

Fourteen words. I had catalogued longer sentences than that by heart, whole paragraphs of other people's prose shelved in my skull. Fourteen words, and I could not make my hand meet the wood.

* * *

Here is what happens to me at doors.

When I was nine, my mother and I played a game we called Quiet Mouse. It began as a way to pass the long hours of her night shifts' aftermath—she worked evenings then, came home smelling of the hospital laundry, and slept badly, and I learned to move through our apartment like weather, present but pressing on nothing. Quiet Mouse had rules. If she held up one finger, I could not speak until she held up two. Sometimes she forgot to hold up two. Sometimes the forgetting lasted a whole evening, a whole weekend, and I sat in the blue light of the television with words backing up behind my teeth like water behind a dam, and I understood, in the wordless way of children, that the game was not a game, that my mother was somewhere far away inside herself, and that my silence was the rope I'd thrown her, the only rope I had.

I never stopped throwing it. That is the whole tragedy of me, if I am honest, which I am trying to be, standing here, counting porch boards.

At doors—the school office, the neighbors', the houses where I delivered flyers one humiliating winter—at doors I would stand with my finger on the bell and feel the silence rise in me like floodwater, and more often than not I would leave the package on the mat and go. There is a whole geography of my childhood made of thresholds I failed to cross. I know the exact height of every doorbell I never rang.

And now here was the tallest threshold of them all, and I was forty-six years old, and the game was still on. One finger raised. No second finger coming.

* * *

The yellow curtain moved.

It might have been the wind chime's doing, some draft through a gap—but I saw it settle wrong, settle like fabric does when a hand has just let go of it, and my whole body went cold and then hot. She was home. She was awake, or she had been, and there was a chance—a real, physical, terrible chance—that she was standing three feet away from me on the other side of a wall, looking at the shape of a stranger loitering at her gate in the dark.

I should have knocked right then. Fear makes strange fuel; sometimes it burns backward. Instead I stepped back off the porch, off the seven boards, onto the grass, and stood among the morning glories like a man hiding in a garden he'd been invited into.

Because here is the thing I could not stop calculating, the librarian's arithmetic running in me like a background process: what right did I have?

Not legal right. Legal right was thin anyway—letters addressed to Elias Fontaine belonged, by any sane reading, to his estate, his blood, his daughter. But moral right, which is heavier: I had read eight of the eleven letters. Steamed them open at my kitchen table over three nights with a kettle and a butter knife, my hands shaking so badly the first time that I scalded my own wrist and didn't notice for an hour. I had read my mother's guilt in her own young handwriting, watched it calcify month by month into the silence I grew up inside. And Odile Fontaine had read none of it. Her father had died—1998, heart, according to the record—with the letters still sealed somewhere behind a dead woman's wardrobe, and she had grown up, as I had, inside an inheritance of quiet, and she had never once been given the choice I was about to take away from her by knocking.

What do you say to a woman whose father drowned when yours survived? What do you say except: *My mother killed your father*, which is not true, and: *My mother saved herself*, which is also not true, and both of which are somehow the same sentence?

I had read the tenth letter only once. I would not read it again. In it my mother, nineteen years old, describes the afternoon at the reservoir in fragments, the way broken glass describes the window it used to be. Elias slipped. She hesitated. She swam alone. Five words that contain a whole life's sentence, served and paid. I had put that letter back in the box face-down, as if paper had a face, and I had not touched the box for two days, and then I had picked it up and driven here, because carrying it was easier than opening it again.

Courage, I have come to understand, is mostly a scheduling problem. You do not feel brave and then act. You act during the small window when the fear has briefly lost your address. And my window had closed somewhere around the seventh porch board.

* * *

A light came on inside the house.

Not the yellow-curtained room—a side window, warm and low, the color of a lamp left burning for someone expected. A shadow crossed it. Then another. Then the shadow stopped, and resolved, unmistakably, into the silhouette of a woman standing very still, looking out toward the street.

Toward me.

I did the coward's arithmetic one final time. *Come back tomorrow.* Tomorrow I would be rested. Tomorrow I would write the fourteen words on a card and hold the card. Tomorrow the fear would have found my address again, and the day after, and the day after that, and the letters would go back behind a wardrobe of my own devising, and I would shelve them next to every other unfinished conversation in the great dark catalogue of my life, and the ledger would grow by one entry, and the entry would read, in my small neat hand: *Delacroix Lane. Stood outside. Did not knock. Reason: —*

Reason blank. Every entry in my notebook ends in a blank reason. That is the shape of my whole interior life, a form with the field left empty.

The shadow at the window shifted. And then—God help me—the porch light snapped on.

It caught me full in the face, mid-retreat, one foot on the grass, the box clutched to my chest like something rescued from a fire. I froze. There is a particular quality to being caught in light after a lifetime of working in the dark; the night library taught me how to be invisible, how to move between stacks without disturbing the dust, and none of it helped. I stood blinking like something nocturnal dragged into day, forty-six years old, holding a shoebox, wearing the expression of a man who has been asked a question he cannot answer because he has forgotten how answers work.

The front door opened.

Not all the way. Six inches, maybe eight, held by a chain I could see glinting. And through the gap, backlit, a woman's voice—tired, level, the voice of someone who has worked a double and does not have time to be afraid:

"Whoever you are, you've been out there for half an hour. Either come say what you came to say or get off my grass."

Half an hour. Longer than I'd counted. Time moves strangely at thresholds.

I opened my mouth. The air went in and stayed there. Behind the chain, the woman waited—she didn't retreat, didn't close the door, just waited, with the patience of a person accustomed to waiting at the edges of other people's endings—and in the spill of the porch light I could see her face properly for the first time, and the sight of it knocked something loose in me that no letter had managed.

Because she had his jaw. Elias's jaw, from the photograph at the water's edge, the boy squinting into a sun that still had years left to shine on him. The same jaw, aged fifty years past the drowning, set in a face that had clearly spent its own lifetime deciding whether to open doors or close them.

She was still waiting. The chain glinted. Somewhere down the block the spoon-chime turned over in the wind.

And I heard myself speak—the first words to leave my mouth on this porch, cracked and too quiet and utterly unrehearsed, four words instead of fourteen, the wrong four, the only four I had:

"It's about the water."

The chain did not slide free. The door did not close. Behind it, Odile Fontaine went very still, in the way I recognized instantly, the way of a person hearing a sound from a room they had sealed decades ago—and the porch light hummed above us both, and the morning glories breathed against the fence, and neither of us moved, while behind me at the curb the engine of my car ticked and cooled, counting down the last of the night.
