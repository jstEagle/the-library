## 6. The Buyer

He arrived on a Tuesday, which felt right. Tuesdays were the days the house seemed most aware of its own neglect — the light came in at a slant through the front room and found every surface of dust, every water stain blooming on the ceiling like a slow yellow flower, and it did this without apology, as if the house had given up pretending to be anything other than what it was.

Freya heard the car before she saw it. A quiet engine, expensive in the way that didn't announce itself, pulling up the gravel drive with the caution of a vehicle that had been briefed on the terrain. She was standing in the upstairs hallway with a clipboard she'd bought specifically to make herself feel organized, listing rooms the way you'd list items in a lost-and-found: *front bedroom, one window, wallpaper peeling in strips; back bedroom, smaller, smell of camphor; landing, carpet worn to the weave.*

She watched from the landing window as Marcus Hale got out of the car. He was younger than she'd expected — mid-forties, maybe, with the kind of face that had been designed to be forgotten immediately. Grey jacket, no tie. He stood for a moment looking up at the house, and Freya tried to read his expression and failed. It wasn't greed. It wasn't even appraisal, exactly. It was the look of a man confirming that a thing existed, the way you'd check a box on a form. *Property present. Condition as described.*

The doorbell hadn't worked since before her grandmother died, so he knocked — three measured raps, patient, unhurried. Freya took the stairs slowly, giving herself time to arrange her face into something neutral. She'd decided, somewhere between waking and dressing, that she would not like this man. She hadn't yet worked out why. It was simply a position she'd taken up in advance, the way you reserve a seat.

"Ms. Thackeray." He offered his hand. His grip was dry and brief. "Marcus Hale. We spoke on the phone."

"We did."

"Thank you for making the time. I know this can't be easy."

It was the first of many sentences he would say that sounded like empathy and functioned as lubricant. She stepped aside and let him in.

* * *

He moved through the house the way water moves through a wreck — finding every channel, missing nothing, touching nothing. He carried a leather folder under his arm and a phone he consulted only twice, each time glancing at the screen with the faint frown of a man checking a tide table. In the kitchen he noted the original cabinetry without comment. In the parlour he paused at the window and looked out at the garden, which had gone to seed in the way gardens do when no one is left who remembers what they were supposed to be.

"Good bones," he said, to the window more than to her.

"That's what people say about houses they intend to take apart."

He turned and smiled — genuinely, she thought, or in some adjacent register. "We're not vandals, Ms. Thackeray. What we do is... continuity, of a kind. The house stays. The street stays. We just make sure the next sixty years are structurally sound." He said *structurally sound* the way a priest might say *absolution*.

"And the inside?"

"The inside is yours until closing. After that, it's ours to consider." He said it gently. That was the thing about him she couldn't get past, she realized later — the gentleness. Cruelty would have been easier to hate. This was something else: a courtesy so complete it had hollowed out everything behind it, leaving only the shape of consideration, like a glove with no hand in it.

They ended up in the front room because it was the largest and because he steered them there with the unconscious competence of a man who had done this several hundred times. He opened the folder on the card table her grandmother had used for jigsaw puzzles — the last puzzle still half-finished under its dust sheet, a thousand pieces of a canal scene, sky and water forever incomplete — and laid out documents with both hands, squaring the edges.

"I'll be direct," he said. "The figure is generous. More than generous. My firm has looked at comparable properties within five miles, and we're offering thirty percent above the top of that range."

"Why?"

The question came out blunter than she'd intended, but she let it stand. Hale didn't flinch. If anything, he seemed pleased by it, as though directness were a currency he respected.

"Because we want it, and we've learned that wanting something quietly costs less than wanting it loudly. Because the location is right for what we're planning. And because—" he paused, choosing, she could see him choosing— "because a house like this carries complications. Provenance issues, structural unknowns, the possibility of listed-status objections. We price those in. You benefit from our pessimism."

"You're paying extra for my problems."

"I'm paying extra so your problems become mine." He slid the first document toward her. "There's no trick in it. There's a hard date — six weeks from today, the same deadline your solicitor mentioned — and if we close by then, everyone walks away satisfied. If we don't, the offer lapses and we all go back to uncertainty, which I promise you is worse than it sounds. Uncertainty has a way of eating houses like this one. Roofs don't wait. Damp doesn't negotiate."

Freya looked at the papers. They were beautifully printed. Heavy stock, a watermark she didn't recognize, fonts chosen by someone paid to choose fonts. Somewhere in the middle was a number with enough zeroes after it to reorganize her entire provisional life — the month-to-month flat, the night shifts, the deliberate absence of photographs and keepsakes, the whole architecture of a self built to be dismantled at short notice. She could be free. That was the word the number kept whispering. *Free.* As if freedom were a place you could buy a ticket to, one-way.

She didn't touch the papers.

"Can I ask you something?" she said.

"Of course."

"Do you ever wonder what's in the houses? Before they're yours?"

Hale considered this with what appeared to be real attention. Outside, a magpie landed on the fence rail and regarded the garden with one bright, indifferent eye.

"I used to," he said. "When I started. First week on the job, I walked through a bungalow in Marlow and found a wall of photographs behind a wardrobe — hundreds of them, a whole family's century pinned up there. Nobody had known. The family selling it certainly hadn't. I stood there for twenty minutes." He folded his hands. "And then I called my office and told them the property was clean, and we closed in a month, and I have never once driven past it. So the honest answer is: I stopped wondering, because wondering is a luxury the work doesn't include. Someone has to be the person who doesn't wonder. Otherwise nothing ever moves forward."

*Someone has to be the person who doesn't wonder.* She thought of the locked drawer upstairs, the one no key fit. She thought of the tin box she hadn't yet found, though it was already waiting for her behind the wardrobe like a held breath. She thought: *this man is what my family would have looked like if it had incorporated.*

"I'll need time," she said.

"You have six weeks. But I'll be honest with you, Ms. Thackeray, because I think you prefer honesty to comfort." He gathered the documents back into their folder, unhurried, unoffended. "Time isn't neutral. Time in an empty house works against the seller. Every week, the roof costs more, the market cools, the offer looks less like generosity and more like luck. I'm not threatening you. I'm describing weather."

The magpie left the fence. The light in the front room shifted, and for a moment the half-finished puzzle under its dust sheet seemed almost visible through the fabric — the ghost of a picture, waiting for pieces that were never coming.

"I won't sign today," Freya said.

"No," Hale agreed pleasantly. "You won't. Almost nobody signs the first day. It's part of the process." He stood, buttoned his jacket, and shook her hand again — that same dry, brief grip, a transaction conducted through skin. "I'll leave my card. Call when you're ready, or call when you're not, and I'll talk you through whatever's worrying you. I'm very good at that part."

At the door he paused and looked back down the hall, taking in the staircase, the worn carpet, the doors standing open onto rooms full of priced and unpriced things.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the house will be fine. Better than fine. Whatever it's holding, we know how to carry it."

Then he was gone, and the gravel crunched under his tires with the regularity of a metronome, and Freya stood in the doorway listening to the sound diminish until it was indistinguishable from any other Tuesday afternoon — traffic, wind, a dog two streets over — except that now, underneath it all, she could hear it: a clock that had started ticking the moment he arrived, patient and impersonal, counting down from forty-two days.

* * *

That evening she made tea she didn't drink and sat in the kitchen with the estate inventory clipboard, writing nothing. The pen hovered. Every object resisted valuation — her grandmother's sewing tin, the chipped blue jug, the coats on the hall stand that still held, faintly, the smell of whoever had last worn them. How did you put a number on a coat? How did you put a number on a house that had swallowed three generations and kept its mouth shut?

Upstairs, the locked drawer waited in the dark of the back bedroom, keeping whatever it kept. She had tried every key on her grandmother's ring against it, and then every key in the kitchen drawer, and finally a hairpin, out of desperation, which bent. The drawer had the composure of something that knew it would be opened eventually, and was in no hurry.

Forty-one days, the clock said. Forty, then, by midnight.

She wrote at the top of the inventory page, in small letters, as if recording it for some future auditor of her conscience:

*Day 1. Nothing valued. Nothing discarded. Nothing signed.*

And below it, after a moment, because the house was listening and she had learned this much already:

*Nothing understood.*

She capped the pen. Somewhere above her, in the back bedroom, behind the wardrobe she hadn't yet moved, the past sat in its tin box with the patience of all buried things — and tomorrow, the assessor was coming, and the furniture had to shift, and the house would have no choice but to give up what it had been holding.
