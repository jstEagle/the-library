## 8. What Mother Knew

The bus to Wetherby took forty minutes and I spent all of them rehearsing.

I had a folder on my knee — photocopies, because I could not make myself carry the originals out of the house — and I had arranged the letters in the order I imagined the conversation would require: the first one, the polite one, from 1961; the money letters from the seventies; the one where the handwriting began to fray like a rope under load. I had a speech. I had questions, numbered in my head the way I numbered the shelves at the library, everything in its place, everything retrievable. *Who was A.V.? Why did you burn some of them? What did you know, and when did you decide I shouldn't?*

What I did not have was a way to ask any of it without watching my mother's face do whatever it was going to do. That was the part no amount of rehearsal covered. I knew her face the way you know a weather pattern — I could predict rain, but I couldn't prevent it.

The town announced itself the way all these towns do: a sign, a roundabout, a row of shops with the same three chains and the same two independents that had survived by being stubborn. My mother had moved here eleven years ago, after the divorce, into a ground-floor flat that smelled of other people's cooking and looked out on a car park. She had chosen it, she said, because it was easy. Everything about her life since the house had been chosen for ease, and I had never once thought to ask what the house had cost her to leave.

I pressed the bell and listened to it chime somewhere deep in the building, a sound like a small struck bell in an empty church.

She opened the door in her cardigan, the grey one with the loose button, and her face went through three expressions in the space of a second — surprise, calculation, and then the smooth neutral she wore like a coat. *Freya. You didn't say you were coming.*

"I didn't know I was coming until I was on the bus."

She stepped back to let me in. The flat was immaculate in the way of a place where nothing is ever truly used. The sofa cushions bore no impression. The magazines on the rack were fanned at angles suggesting arrangement rather than reading. On the windowsill, three small pots of herbs stretched toward the light with the desperate optimism of things kept alive out of habit.

"Tea?" she said, already moving toward the kettle, and I understood that this was the first move in a game we had been playing my whole life: she offered tea, I accepted, and by the time it was made and drunk the moment for saying anything difficult had been politely escorted off the premises.

"No tea," I said. "Mum. I found the letters."

The kettle did not go on. She stood with her back to me, one hand on the counter, and for a long moment the only sound was the fridge humming its one note.

"What letters," she said. Not a question. A door being held shut.

"The ones behind the wardrobe. Forty-one of them. From A.V. I've read most of them."

She turned around slowly. I had expected anger. I had expected her to tell me I had no business going through things that weren't mine, that I'd always been a snoop, that some doors are closed for reasons. I had a whole defense prepared. What I got instead was worse: she looked *tired*. She looked like a woman who had been carrying something heavy for so long that she'd forgotten the weight was not a natural condition of her body, and now someone had asked about it, and the asking hurt more than the carrying.

"Sit down, Freya."

"I'd rather—"

"Sit down."

I sat. She did not. She stayed standing at the counter with her arms folded, and when she finally spoke, her voice had the flatness of a statement read into a record.

"How many did you say?"

"Forty-one."

"Then she didn't burn them all." She said it to the window, to the herbs, to the car park. "Thirty years I thought — I counted what was left, after. I thought there were more I'd missed."

* * *

She made the tea after all. I think she needed her hands to be doing something while the rest of her decided what it could afford to lose. She set my cup down on a coaster, aligned it with the table's edge, and then sat opposite me with her own cup untouched, and she told it in the order she chose, which was not the order it happened.

"She wrote to me. Near the end. A letter, an actual letter, asking me to come and help her clear things out. She said she wanted to put the house in order." My mother's mouth did something that was almost a smile. "Your grandmother putting her house in order. As if it had ever been in order in its life. It was an instruction, Freya. Everything that woman said was an instruction dressed as a remark."

"What was in the letter?"

"Nothing. That was the point of her. She said, *come and help me with the wardrobe*, and I thought she meant her clothes, God help me, she had forty years of coats in there. I went. I spent a weekend sorting coats while she sat in the kitchen drinking sherry and watching me like a foreman." She paused. "And on the Sunday morning I moved the wardrobe myself, because she'd asked me to check the wall behind it for damp — damp, she said — and there was the cavity, and there was the tin."

The tin. I knew the tin. It was sitting on my kitchen table two towns away, dented at one corner, smelling of rust and pencil shavings.

"There were more than forty-one," my mother said. "There were — I don't know. Sixty? Seventy? I didn't count. I started reading the first one and by the third I knew what they were and by the fifth I knew what I was going to do, and I have thought about that decision every single day since, and I have never once been sure it was the right one."

"Burned some," I said. "You burned some."

"I burned the early ones." Her voice didn't waver, but her hands closed around the cup as if it were cold, which it wasn't. "The ones from before. The ones where he was — charming. Where he was funny. Where he wrote about the weather in whatever country he was in and asked after the garden and signed off with little jokes. Because those were the ones that would have made him *real*. Do you understand? A monster you can hate. A monster with a sense of humour, who remembered your grandmother's birthday and asked whether the plum tree had fruited — that's a person. That's a person you might forgive. And I could not — " She stopped. Started again. "I could not let that man be a person. Not in that house. Not to you."

"To me? Mum, I was a child. I didn't even know his name."

"Exactly." She set the cup down with a small precise click. "And whose doing do you think that was? You think a man just — vanishes from a family? You think your grandmother never once said his name by accident? She said it *once*. When you were six. She called the spare room 'Anselm's room' and then she went white as milk and never spoke of it again, and I spent the next thirty years making sure no one ever gave you the thread to pull. Because I'd read the letters, Freya. I knew what was at the end of the thread."

"And what's at the end of the thread?"

She looked at me then, really looked, and her eyes were my eyes, the same grey, the same sleeplessness in them, inherited like the colour.

"I don't know," she said. "That's the truth. I never got to the end. I burned the early ones and I put the rest back and I closed the wall up and I told myself I'd come back and finish reading them when I was brave enough, and then I told myself that for thirty years." A sound came out of her that was half laugh, half something else. "I told myself I was protecting you. And then I told myself I was protecting her. And the truth — the truth I only admit at three in the morning, in that bed, in that flat — is that I was protecting myself. Because as long as I didn't read to the end, I could keep believing there was a version of him worth the silence. A version of *us* worth inheriting."

* * *

The light moved across the car park while she talked. I learned things in no order at all: that my grandmother had known he was alive for years after the official story, had received money in plain brown envelopes, had banked it and never spent a pound of it and never told anyone where it came from; that the story of his death — the one my mother had been raised on, the one I had been raised on by absence — had been constructed like a wall, brick by careful brick, and that my mother had laid some of the bricks herself without ever questioning the architect.

"Why didn't you tell me? When she died. When the solicitor called. You knew I'd be in that house alone."

"Because I thought the wall would hold," she said simply. "It had held for thirty years. I thought you'd clear the coats, price the furniture, sign the papers, and drive away, and it would all go into the ground with her, and you'd be free of it. Free the way I never was." She looked at her hands. "I wanted you to have the clean version. I know now that there isn't one. I think maybe I knew it then."

I should have been angry. Some part of me was — a hot, small part that catalogued every birthday, every Christmas, every phone call where she'd asked how I was and meant *are you eating*, never once *are you haunted*. But the larger part of me sat in that immaculate flat and looked at this woman who had spent three decades standing guard over a secret she'd never fully read, armed with half a truth and a box of matches, and I felt something I hadn't expected, which was grief. Not for the letters she'd burned. For her. For the girl who'd moved a wardrobe on a Sunday morning and found her family's whole story was a room with a false wall in it, and who had chosen, at whatever age she was then, younger than I was now — she'd sealed it up and called it love.

"The last letter," I said. "It's in a language I can't read. Dev thinks it's a dialect — one of the dying ones. He's helping me."

She went very still.

"Read it to me," she said. "When you know. Whatever it says. I'd rather hear it from you than imagine it for another thirty years."

I promised. I don't know if I can keep it. But I promised, and she nodded, and for a moment we were just two women at a table in a flat that smelled of other people's cooking, holding the same silence from opposite sides.

* * *

At the door, she stopped me with a hand on my sleeve.

"The buyer," she said. "The one from the firm. Hale. He'll come back with more money. They always do. And there will be papers, Freya — there are always papers — and somewhere in the small print there will be a line about discretion. About keeping things quiet. Read every line." Her grip tightened. "Your grandmother signed things like that her whole life. It's how the wall got built. One signature at a time."

I walked to the bus stop through streets going blue with evening, the folder heavy against my ribs, and behind me the light in her window came on, small and yellow, in a flat she kept like a held breath.

Two towns over, the house was waiting. And somewhere inside it, behind a wall my mother had mortared shut with the best intentions, a sentence in a dead man's dying language was waiting to be understood — or, if I chose wrong, to be signed away.
