## 11. Anselm's Ledger

The office smelled of coal dust and carbon paper, and Anselm Voss had learned to love it the way a man learns to love the weather of the country where he expects to die.

It was February 1944, and the radiators in the Records Bureau of the Regional Administration clanked like patient animals. Outside, the snow came down in the grey, indifferent way snow comes down on cities that have stopped looking up. Inside, Anselm sat at his desk — third from the window, a position he had earned through two years of punctuality so flawless it had become a kind of invisibility — and copied names.

That was the work. That was all the work was. Names went into ledgers; ledgers went into cabinets; cabinets went into rooms that no one entered except men with keys and men with lists. He was good at it. He had discovered early that he possessed a handwriting that pleased his superiors: upright, unornamented, without flourish or tremor. A clerk's hand. A hand that made no claims about itself. When Herr Brandt passed behind him and paused — he could feel the pause the way one feels a shadow cross a window — Anselm did not lift his head. Lifting your head invited conversation. Conversation invited attention. Attention, in the winter of 1944, was a thing you survived by not receiving.

"Voss."

He looked up. Brandt stood at the end of the row of desks holding a folder the colour of weak tea, and there was something in his face that Anselm would spend the rest of his life learning to name. It was not menace. Menace would have been simpler. It was courtesy — the particular, terrible courtesy of an institution that has never once needed to raise its voice.

"A moment."

* * *

The room they used for such conversations had a map on one wall and a stove that gave off more smoke than heat, and between these two facts the room managed to feel like nowhere at all. Brandt set the folder down, squared its corners to the edge of the table — Anselm noticed this, noticed everything, noticing was the curse of clerks — and said:

"You're from the Kesslerstrasse. Number fourteen."

"I grew up there," Anselm said. "My mother still keeps the flat."

"And the family across — the Lindqvists. You knew them."

It was not a question, but Anselm understood that questions were being asked underneath it, and that the quality of his silence would be measured against some scale he could not see. He thought of Frau Lindqvist's geraniums, which she carried indoors each October and out again each April with the solemnity of a woman moving relics. He thought of Henrik, who was two years older and had taught him to skate on the canal, pushing him from behind, laughing, until Anselm fell and Henrik fell on top of him and the ice sang beneath them both like something enormous turning over in its sleep.

"We were neighbours," Anselm said. "For many years."

Brandt nodded slowly, as if this confirmed something already known, and opened the folder.

What followed took eleven minutes. Anselm counted them because counting was what he did instead of feeling, and because the clock on the wall had a second hand that moved in small stubborn jerks, and because if he did not count he would have to listen, and he was listening anyway, helplessly, the way the ear listens when the mind begs it not to. The Bureau had received information — incomplete, requiring corroboration — regarding the Lindqvist household. Certain declarations had not been filed. Certain persons were residing at the address whose presence constituted, under the regulations of the previous autumn, an offence carrying consequences that Brandt described in the passive voice, as though the consequences had assembled themselves out of the weather.

"The Bureau requires a statement," Brandt said. "From someone in a position to speak to the household's composition. Someone credible." He looked up then, and his eyes were pale and almost kind. "Someone local."

Anselm heard himself say: "I haven't lived there in four years."

"No," Brandt agreed pleasantly. "But your mother has. And mothers talk. And sons of mothers are presumed to listen." He turned the folder around and slid a single sheet across the table. It was a form. Of course it was a form. The century had learned that atrocity travels best on pre-printed paper, with boxes to tick and lines to sign, so that the hand involved need never feel itself deciding anything — only completing something. "You may take it home. You may take a week. I should say, however—" and here Brandt leaned back, and the stove popped, and somewhere above them a door closed with the softness of all the doors closing everywhere in that country at that hour— "that the Bureau is aware of certain irregularities in your own file. Your father's classification. Your brother's deferment, which was granted on grounds that might bear re-examination. Nothing is alleged, Voss. But files are living things. They grow. Or they close."

He said *close* the way a doctor says *heal*.

Anselm took the form. His hand did not shake. That was the first horror, though he did not know it yet — that the hand held steady, that twenty-three years of a body trained in obedience performed exactly as designed, and signed nothing, and refused nothing, and carried the paper home folded twice in his inside pocket, over the heart, where it lay against his chest all evening like a second, colder heartbeat.

* * *

The Kesslerstrasse in February was a street of drawn curtains and rationed light. Anselm walked it from the tram stop as he had walked it ten thousand times, and the walking was unbearable because it was unchanged. The lamp at the corner still buzzed. The Lindqvists' windows still glowed that particular amber of a household that boiled its water twice and wasted nothing. Smoke rose from their chimney in a thin, honest thread. Somewhere inside, Frau Lindqvist was doing whatever she did at seven o'clock — he could see her shadow move across the curtain, round-shouldered, unhurried, a woman stirring a pot in the last kitchen in Europe that believed in tomorrow.

He stood in the snow for a long time. Long enough that his shoulders whitened. Long enough that a neighbour — old Pfeiffer, from number nine — nodded to him in passing and said, "Cold evening, young Voss," and walked on, and Anselm understood that Pfeiffer had seen him standing there and had filed it, the way everyone filed everything now, in the great cabinet of the city where every drawer was a mouth.

The form lay on his mother's table that night while she slept, and he read it by the blue flame of the spirit lamp so as not to wake her. It required, in Section Four, a list of persons resident at the address. Their full names. Their dates of birth. Their relationship, if any, to the declarant. At the bottom, a line for the signature, and beneath the line, in letters so small they seemed ashamed: *I certify that the foregoing is true and complete.*

True and complete. He thought: I could write *no one*. I could write *the flat is empty*. I could write anything, and the pen would obey, because pens always obey; that is the whole tragedy of pens. And then he thought of his father's file, and his brother's deferment, and his mother's hands, which had begun to shake two winters ago and never stopped, and he thought of Henrik on the ice, laughing, falling, the singing of the frozen canal — and he hated himself most of all for the fact that the memory arrived not as a warning but as a price, entered neatly in a column, weighed against another column, the arithmetic performing itself in him with the smooth efficiency of the very machinery he served.

Everyone would do it. That was the sentence he built that night, brick by brick, to shelter in. Everyone would do it. The Pfeiffers, the Brandts, the men at the fourth desk who hummed while they stamped. Everyone. And if everyone would, then the doing of it was not a soul's act but a weather's act, and no one could be rained on for rain.

He was twenty-three years old, and he believed this completely, and the believing cost him nothing that night, and would cost him everything for sixty years, and even then would not be paid in full.

* * *

He delivered the form on a Tuesday.

He remembered it was a Tuesday because Tuesdays were stamp days, and the Bureau's rhythm changed on stamp days — a deeper industry, a hum in the floor. He handed the completed sheet across Brandt's desk and watched it disappear into the tea-coloured folder, and Brandt glanced at it once, briefly, the way a man glances at a receipt, and said, "Thank you, Voss. This will be handled discreetly," and then, with a small human flicker that Anselm would examine for the rest of his life like a specimen under glass: "Your brother's deferment is quite secure now. These things settle themselves, given cooperation."

Given cooperation. As though Anselm had merely cooperated with gravity.

The Lindqvists were taken on the Thursday. Anselm knew because he was at his desk when the report came through — not the report itself, which went upward, but the routing slip, which passed across his blotter for countersignature, because he was junior and juniors countersigned. Four names. Henrik's among them. In the column marked *disposition*, a code he knew: the letter and numeral that meant east, that meant transport, that meant the word no one wrote because the word had been replaced by the code, and the code by the stamp, and the stamp by the signature, and the signature by his own.

His own. He looked at his initials in the margin — *A.V.*, small, upright, unornamented — and felt nothing, and the nothing frightened him less than it should have, because the nothing was efficient, and efficiency was the highest virtue his century had taught him.

That evening he walked past number twelve. The windows were dark. The geraniums stood in the front room, abandoned mid-winter, dying in their pots with a patience that shamed him more than any accusation could have. He stood in the snow again, and this time no one passed, and the street let him stand there alone with the fact of himself, and the fact did not argue, and did not console, and did not go away.

* * *

He began the ledger in March.

Not the Bureau's ledger — his own. A school exercise book, cheap, grey-covered, bought from the stationer on the Ringstrasse by a man buying paper, only paper, nothing suspicious in paper. He told himself it was penance. He told himself it was evidence. He told himself, in the years afterward, that it was testimony. All three were true and none was sufficient, which is the nature of ledgers: they record; they do not redeem.

He wrote the four names on the first page. Then he wrote the date of the Thursday. Then, because his hand was a clerk's hand and could not help itself, he wrote beneath them, in the same upright script: *These names left this house because my hand put them on a form.* And then, because honesty arrived in him only in instalments, he added the other entries as the years demanded them — the money, later, the payments that came through channels he pretended not to understand; the second name, in '46, a man in the port office whose silence Anselm had purchased and whose file Anselm had fed; the third, the fourth, the slow sediment of a life conducted in the currency of other people's exposure. Each entry dated. Each entry initialed. *A.V.* Forty-one times over three decades, the initials accumulating like rings in a tree, marking not growth but seasons of the same unforgiving weather.

He hid the ledger first in Riga, then in Trieste, then — when exile had become the permanent address of his life — he sent pages of it, disguised as letters, to the one person on earth he had both wronged and trusted beyond reason: the woman on the Kesslerstrasse who had married him before the war and buried him officially after it, and who kept his words behind a wardrobe for thirty years because she could neither forgive nor destroy them, and because a house that holds a secret holds its shape around the secret the way a fist holds its shape around nothing at all.

But that is later. On the night he began the ledger, there was only the spirit lamp, and the grey exercise book, and the four names, and the snow coming down outside the lodging-house window in the grey, indifferent way snow comes down on cities that have stopped looking up.

At the bottom of the first page, after the initials, he wrote one more line — not in German, not in any language the century could requisition or read, but in the dialect of his grandmother's village, the dying tongue of the valley, the language of hearths and not of offices, which had no future tense as the schools understood futures and no passive voice at all, because in that valley nothing was ever done to anyone by no one; things were done, and the doing stayed in the doing forever.

He wrote the sentence slowly, sounding it out, half-remembered, the grammar of guilt arriving in him whole from a childhood he had thought erased. And when he finished it he looked at it for a long time, and understood that he had written the only true thing he would ever write, and that it could not be translated — not because the words were rare, but because the tense did not exist anywhere else, and a man cannot be judged in a court that has no grammar for his crime.

He closed the book. He blew out the lamp. And forty years later, in a house on the Kesslerstrasse, behind a wardrobe, a granddaughter would open a tin box and find the last page waiting for her — a sentence in a dead dialect, patient as a geranium in a dark window, asking, in a tense that English refuses to build: *what will you do with me?*
