## 3. What the Archive Keeps

Vera Lindqvist had read the file four times on the tram, and by the fourth reading she had stopped seeing the words and started seeing the woman behind them.

*Signe Vahl. Born 1931, Hällsmark district. Last verified fluent speaker of Vessic. Status: living. Custody recommendation: pending.*

*Custody.* The ministry loved that word. It appeared in their documents the way ivy appeared on old buildings — everywhere, decorative, and quietly strangling whatever it grew over. Vera had argued for *stewardship* in her preliminary report. Her supervisor had crossed it out with a red pen and written in the margin: *We are not gardeners, Lindqvist. We are keepers.*

She had wanted to write back: *Keepers of what, exactly? A language is not a spoon. You cannot put it in a drawer and have it remain a language. A language kept is a language killed and stuffed.*

But she had said nothing. She was very good at saying nothing. It was, she suspected, the primary qualification for employment at the Ministry of Cultural Continuity — an institution whose name contained more hope than its hallways had ever held.

The night library stood at the corner of Vaskergade and the canal like a man who had fallen asleep standing up and refused to admit it. Its windows glowed amber against the blue-black hour, and through the glass Vera could see shapes moving inside — not the brisk shapes of daytime institutions but slow ones, drifting ones, people who had nowhere better to be at two in the morning and who had, perhaps wisely, decided that a library was the most dignified place to be nowhere.

A brass plaque beside the door read: **OPEN WHEN YOU CANNOT SLEEP**. Below it, smaller, someone had taped a handwritten card: *Yes, really. Come in.*

Vera came in.

The warmth hit her first — old radiators working far harder than they should, the smell of paper and floor wax and, faintly, someone's forgotten soup. Then the quiet, which was not silence but something alive: the rustle of pages, the tap of a keyboard somewhere, a man in the corner whispering to himself as he read, which Vera decided after a moment was not strange at all but actually rather lovely.

Behind the front desk sat a woman with grey-streaked hair pulled back too tightly, as though she'd made a deal with disorder and intended to honor it only in spirit. She did not look up.

"We close at dawn," she said. "Not before. People get confused about that."

"I'm not confused."

"Everyone's confused at this hour. It's why they're here." The woman looked up then, and her eyes were the pale, assessing grey of winter water. "You're not one of ours. You sleep. I can tell. You have the posture of someone whose insomnia is professional rather than sincere."

Vera blinked. "Is there a difference?"

"Enormous. Sincere insomniacs slouch. Professional ones sit like they're being graded." She extended no hand. "Casper Iversen. Night librarian. You'll want Signe Vahl, I assume. Everyone from your building does."

"My building?"

"The Ministry." Casper said the word the way other people said *mold*. "You have the coat. Ministry coats all have that particular crease where you keep your credentials in the inside pocket, like a badge you're ashamed of."

Vera looked down at her coat. There was, indeed, such a crease. She found herself absurdly pleased to be seen so precisely and equally annoyed about it. "I'm Vera Lindqvist. And I'm not here to take anything."

"No," Casper agreed mildly. "You're here to make an inventory of what could be taken. That's always step one. Step two takes longer and involves forms."

* * *

Signe Vahl received her on Thursdays — or rather, Signe received her whenever she happened to be awake and inclined, which over three weeks resolved itself into Thursdays, because even chaos keeps a calendar if you watch long enough.

The old woman lived in a room above a shuttered bakery, and the room was a country unto itself: plants in tins, a chair worn into the shape of one particular body, stacks of cassette tapes labeled in a handwriting that started confident and ended, near the bottom of each stack, uncertain — the letters drifting, as if the hand writing them had begun to forget where it was going.

"Sit," Signe said, in a voice like dry leaves deciding to be kind. "You're the one from the government. Don't look surprised. Casper told me. She tells me everything except the things that matter, which is her way of loving people."

"I'm not— I mean, yes, I work for the ministry, but I'm not here officially. Not today."

"Everyone from the government says that. It's practically the national anthem." But Signe smiled when she said it, and poured tea from a pot that had seen more decades than some nations, and gestured Vera into the second chair, the guest chair, which creaked apologetically under anyone who wasn't Signe.

"I want to learn," Vera said, before she could lose her nerve. "Before we — before anyone records anything. I want to understand it first. What it is. What it isn't."

Signe studied her over the rim of her cup. "And what does your ministry think it is?"

"A holding," Vera admitted. "An endangered oral holding. They want to record it. Transcribe it. Translate it. Build a complete archive — every word, every idiom, preserved for posterity."

"Preserved." Signe tasted the word like tea gone cold. "You know what we called preservation, where I come from? We had a word for it. *Savenna.*" She pronounced it slowly, letting Vera hear the shape of it — the soft opening, the way the second syllable seemed to apologize for the first. "It means what happens to fruit when you boil it down to keep it. Sweet. Shelf-stable. Dead. You can spread it on bread, but it will never again grow on a tree."

Vera felt something turn over in her chest. "That's beautiful."

"It's a warning." Signe set down her cup. "Your ministry wants jam, girl. Jam is honest, at least. It doesn't pretend to be an orchard."

* * *

Over the following weeks, Vera came back — off the clock, off the record, telling herself each time that this was research and knowing each time that it wasn't.

Signe taught her fragments. Greetings. The word for bread (*lommi*, said with the whole chest, as though the bread were being blessed). The word for grief, which was also, infuriatingly and perfectly, the word for weather — *vessa* — because in Hällsmark, Signe explained, grief was simply something that moved through you and passed, and pretending otherwise was considered a form of arrogance.

"There's no word for 'own' in Vessic," Signe said one Thursday, apropos of nothing, watching rain crawl down her window. "Did your archive discover that yet? You can borrow. You can hold. You can carry-for-someone. But own?" She snapped her fingers, a sound like a small door closing. "The concept didn't survive translation. We decided, as a people, that it was more trouble than it was worth."

Vera wrote it down anyway — she couldn't help it; her hand moved to the notebook the way other hands moved to prayer — and then, catching Signe's expression, closed it again.

"Sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be accurate." Signe leaned back. "Write it down. Write everything down. That's what I'm for now, apparently. A bridge that complains while you cross it." She laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and the cough went on a moment too long, and Vera watched the old woman's knuckles whiten on the arm of her chair and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that time was not an abstraction here. Time was a person, and she was running out.

"Signe," Vera said carefully, "the ministry's order will come soon. The recording project. Full documentation. They'll assign a linguist, a technician—"

"I know. Casper told me."

"And you'll let them?"

Signe was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the rain kept up its patient work on the glass. When she spoke, her voice had changed — flattened, careful, the voice of someone stepping around a hole in the floor they know is there.

"I'll decide what I let them have," she said. "There are rooms in a language, girl. Rooms behind rooms. Your ministry wants the parlor. Fine. Let them have the parlor. Some doors—" she looked at Vera then, and her eyes were suddenly very old and very tired and very sharp, all at once, "—some doors stay shut. Not out of spite. Out of mercy."

"What's behind them?"

"That," said Signe, "is exactly the right question. Which is why I'm not answering it."

* * *

Vera walked home along the canal at four in the morning, past the library with its stubborn amber windows, and did the thing she was worst at: she was honest with herself.

She loved the language. That was the truth of it, the inconvenient, career-endangering truth. Three weeks of fragments and she was already ruined — already saying *lommi* to the baker, already thinking of bad days as weather, already unable to hear the phrase *endangered oral holdings* without flinching, because holdings were property and Vessic was not property, Vessic was a way of refusing to believe that anything could be owned, and the ministry meant to own precisely that refusal, to pickle it in formaldehyde and label it and shelve it.

She was going to help them do it. That was the other truth. She would write the reports. She would calibrate the microphones. She would smile at Signe across the tea table and transcribe every word, and the archive would grow fat and complete, and somewhere in a sub-basement a server would hum with the ghost of a language that no longer had anyone left to mispronounce it.

Unless.

Vera stopped at the canal rail. The water carried the city's lights downstream in pieces.

Unless the rooms behind the rooms mattered. Unless Signe's sealed silences were not eccentricity but architecture — load-bearing walls. Unless there was something in Vessic's past that the old woman was protecting them all from, or protecting the language from, or — the thought arrived unbidden and unwelcome — protecting herself from.

There was a gap in the historical file. Vera had noticed it weeks ago and filed it away as clerical neglect: the Hällsmark speaker community, forty-one documented members in 1962, gone by 1970. No dispersal order. No relocation record. Just absence, tidy as a pulled tooth.

Signe never spoke of those years. When Vera had asked, gently, about the others — the aunt in the tapes, the neighbors whose laughter surfaced in old recordings like fish — Signe had changed the subject to bread, to weather, to the quality of the light.

Tonight, walking home, Vera finally asked herself the question she'd been carrying like a stone in her shoe:

What if the last fluent speaker of Vessic was also the reason there was no one left to speak it with?

The library window glowed behind her, warm and blind. Somewhere inside it, Casper Iversen was shelving books and avoiding her mentor, and neither of them knew yet how much the silence between them and the silence inside Signe were about to become the same silence.

Vera turned up her collar and walked on, toward the ministry, toward the forms, toward the order waiting on her desk with her name at the bottom and a blank line where her signature would go.

She had three weeks, maybe four, before the recording project began.

She intended to find out what was behind the doors first.
