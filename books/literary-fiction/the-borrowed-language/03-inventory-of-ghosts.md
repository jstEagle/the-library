## 3. Inventory of Ghosts

Sleep did not come. It rarely did after a night like that one, but this was different from the usual insomnia—the ordinary insomnia of men who work while cities dream and must reverse themselves each morning like owls. This was a fever with a melody attached.

Freya lay on his back in the narrow room above the laundromat on Delancey Street, where he had lived for eleven years because the rent was paid in cash and no one asked questions, and he listened to the radiator tick through its small metallic liturgy and tried to make the music stop. It would not stop. *Lullaby for the Unsent Letter*—his title, his private, embarrassing, adolescent title—had come back over the airwaves at 2:47 in the morning, played by hands that were not his hands, and now it lived inside his skull like something swallowed.

He got up. He made tea he would not drink. He sat at the kitchen table—a table that had once, briefly, held manuscript paper—and did what he had always done when feeling became too large to hold: he imposed form upon it.

He found a pencil stub in the drawer where the takeout menus lived. He found the back of an electric bill. And he began, in his small, upright, conservatory-trained hand, to write a list.

Not a list of suspects. He was not ready to call them that. A list of *witnesses*. A list of ghosts. The people who had ever heard the lullaby, from its first stumbling incarnation to the version that had gone out over the frequency of WQXR-adjacent nothing at 2:47 a.m., unnamed, unattributed, described by the host in her smoke-and-honey voice as "a recent discovery, sent to us anonymously, which we believe may be the work of a significant new voice."

A significant new voice. Freya read the phrase again in memory and felt something between laughter and nausea. The voice was forty-six years old and smelled of floor wax.

Three names. Only three. He wrote them down because writing things down was how he survived himself; because a suspicion left floating in the mind grew mold, but a suspicion pinned to paper could be examined under light, turned over, tested for false notes the way one tests a chord.

**1. Anton Voss.**

The pencil hovered over the second name for a long time before it touched down. Anton was dead four years now—dead in the way that mattered legally and biologically, though Freya had never entirely believed in the death of Anton Voss, who had seemed less a man than a weather system, capable of exerting pressure from any distance. Dead men could not mail manuscripts to radio stations. But dead men had living students, living executors, living filing cabinets. And Anton had been the first person ever to hear the lullaby.

Freya closed his eyes and let the memory come, because he had learned long ago that memories were like cats: they came when you stopped chasing them.

* * *

He was nineteen, and it was February, and the practice rooms on the fourth floor of the conservatory were so cold that the radiators knocked like prisoners demanding release. He had written the thing in a single sitting—not composed, *written*, the way you write a letter you know you will never send—which was fitting, since it was a letter. It was for Ilse Marchetti, who was twenty and played the cello the way other people prayed: with her whole body, with her eyes shut, with an intensity that embarrassed anyone who happened to witness it. He had been in love with her for eight months and had said nothing, because saying things was not a skill Freya Winterbourne possessed; he had been raised in a house where feelings were handled the way hazardous materials are handled, sealed in containers, labeled, stored away from children. So he had put the sentence into nineteen bars instead. Cello and piano. A lullaby shaped like an apology for a letter never written.

He had played it for her once—or almost. That was the terrible part, the part he still could not reconstruct without flinching. He had brought her to Room Nine at midnight, when the building emptied, and set the manuscript on the stand, and she had looked at the first page with those dark, unguarded eyes and said, "For whom?" And he had said, "No one," which was the most articulate lie of his life, and she had known it was a lie—he saw her know it—and she had picked up her cello anyway and begun to play.

She got nine bars in before the door opened.

Anton Voss stood in the doorway with his coat still on, snow melting on his shoulders, and Ilse stopped as if shot. And Anton—who never apologized, who entered rooms the way weather enters rooms—looked at the manuscript, then at Freya's face, and said quietly, "Continue."

Ilse did not continue. She lowered her bow and looked at Freya, waiting for him to say something—to claim the music, to introduce it, to perform the simple social act that would have made it *his*. And Freya, nineteen years old and fluent in exactly one language, said nothing. The silence lasted perhaps four seconds. In those four seconds, Anton crossed the room, lifted the manuscript from the stand, glanced at it with the terrifying speed of a man reading his own handwriting, and said, "Interesting. Come to my studio tomorrow, both of you. I want to hear it properly."

Then he had folded the pages—*folded* them, a violence Freya felt in his sternum even now—and put them in his coat pocket, and walked out, and Ilse had turned to Freya and said, "Well? Aren't you going to tell him it's yours?"

And Freya had said, "It doesn't matter whose it is," which was the second-most-articulate lie of his life, and the one that had cost him everything.

Because it did matter. It mattered so much that two weeks later, when Anton played the finished lullaby in his studio—revised, polished, the rough edges planed off by a master craftsman's hand, and better, Freya admitted then and admitted now, *better* than what Freya had written—it mattered so much that when Anton said, "This will go in my portfolio for the Meridian Prize submission, as a secondary work; your name is on too many watchlists for us to risk it, boy, you understand, the immigration people, they smell talent like blood in water"—when Anton said all this, wrapping theft in the language of protection, and Freya sat there believing him, half-believing him, wanting to believe him because the alternative was to admit that his mentor, his god, the man whose approval was the only sunlight he got, had just picked his pocket in front of him—

Ilse had heard it twice. That was the arithmetic. Once in Room Nine, nine bars, interrupted. Once in Anton's studio, complete, revised, with Anton at the piano and Ilse at the cello, playing a piece she believed—she must have believed—was either Anton's or nobody's, while its actual author sat three feet away pretending to study the score.

She had played it beautifully. She had played it the way she played everything, and Freya had watched her play his love letter back to him without knowing it was addressed to her, and he had thought: *this is what I deserve.*

* * *

Freya opened his eyes. The tea had gone cold. On the electric bill, the pencil marks waited.

**1. Anton Voss** — dead. Four years. Executor unknown. Studio archived by the conservatory, sealed, catalogued, gathering dust in the sub-basement. *Could a dead man broadcast? No. Could a dead man's papers? Yes.*

He wrote that down. His hand was steady. He noticed his hand was steady and was grateful for it, the way a drowning man is grateful for driftwood without asking where it came from.

**2. Ilse Marchetti.**

Writing her name did something to the room. The radiator kept ticking, the refrigerator kept humming, but the apartment acquired a second temperature, the temperature of that corridor last week—no, ten days ago now—when the new artistic director had come down the east stairwell with a folder under her arm and Freya had pressed himself against the wall with his mop bucket, invisible by design, and she had passed him, and slowed, and looked at him.

Three seconds. He had counted. Counting was another of his survivals. Three seconds was one second longer than a stranger looks at a janitor, two seconds longer than courtesy requires, and in that extra second something moved across her face that he had refused, with tremendous discipline, to name. Recognition? Pity? The vague discomfort of a beautiful woman confronted with a ruin of someone she might once have known?

She had said, "Good evening." Not good morning, though it was nearly seven a.m. and the light was coming up gray behind the frosted windows. *Good evening.* As if she knew what shift he worked. As if she had checked.

Or—as if she remembered.

Freya set the pencil down and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes until he saw constellations. Twenty years abroad, the gossip said. Berlin, then Vienna, then some chair in Lucerne. She had left the year after the scandal—everyone left the year after the scandal; the scandal had been a wind that blew through the conservatory and took certain lighter objects with it, and Ilse Marchetti had been one of them, though Freya had never learned whether she left because of him or merely in the same season as him. They had not spoken since. Not once. Not a letter, not a message relayed through friends, because by the end they had no friends in common who would carry anything between them; the scandal had burned those bridges with everything else.

But she was back now. She was director now. And the lullaby—the lullaby that only three living ears had ever heard, one pair of which belonged to a corpse—had surfaced on a late-night radio program, performed beautifully, *understood*, played with a rubato in the seventh bar that Freya had never written down but had always imagined, a slight leaning-back, a hesitation like a breath drawn before a confession—

Whoever had made that recording knew the piece. Not knew *of* it. Knew it. Knew the hesitation. And there were two candidates, and one of them was dead.

Unless the dead man had left instructions. Unless the recording predated his death. Unless—

Freya got up and paced. Four steps to the window, four steps back. The laundromat below had gone quiet hours ago; the city outside was doing its 4 a.m. impression of a city that did not exist, all siren-less and blue.

Think of it as counterpoint, he told himself. Think of it as a problem in voice-leading. Every note must resolve or be accounted for. So account for them.

**3. Freya Winterbourne.**

He wrote his own name third, and stared at it, and understood that the list was dishonest, and that its dishonesty was itself information. Because there was a fourth possibility, wasn't there? The ugliest one, the one that had been scratching at the underside of his thoughts since the mop hit the floor: that he had played it for someone and forgotten. That in twenty years of nights—in thousands of hours alone in empty buildings with pianos standing open like unlocked cars—some fragment had escaped him. That he had sat down at some 3 a.m. piano in some rehearsal room and played it through, once, thinking himself alone, and that walls in old buildings have ears the way rivers have currents, and that the piece had leaked out of him the way grief leaks out of people who believe they have contained it.

He tested this hypothesis the way he tested all hypotheses: ruthlessly. Had he ever played it? He searched. He went through the archive of his own nights the way he had once gone through scores, bar by bar, and found—nothing conclusive. There were gaps. There were whole years in the middle decade that had the texture of anesthesia. He had played *something* in those years; he remembered the physical fact of his hands on keys the way an amputee remembers itching. But whether the lullaby had been among the things his hands had done in the dark, he could not swear.

That was the crack. He felt it open in him like ice on a pond in early spring—that sound, that specific hairline *tick* of structural failure. Because if he could not trust his own memory, then the list was worthless, and if the list was worthless, then anyone could have the piece, and if anyone could have the piece, then it was not a letter at all anymore. It was public property. It was a stray. It was out in the world wearing no collar, answering to no name, and some stranger on the radio was calling it *a significant new voice* while its actual voice scrubbed toilets two floors below the plaque with his name on it.

No. Stop. Discipline. This was exactly how denial worked—he knew its methods intimately, having practiced them for two decades. Denial did not argue; it expanded. It took a single unresolved question and used it to dissolve every certainty around it until you were floating in a warm gray soup of *maybe I imagined it all*. He had watched Anton use that trick on students. He had used it on himself so often he could hear its fingering.

Facts. Return to facts.

Fact: the piece existed. He had written it. Nineteen bars plus coda, D minor moving to the relative major only at the very end and then refusing to land—the unresolved final cadence that was the whole point, the musical equivalent of a sentence that stops before its verb.

Fact: it had been broadcast at 2:47 a.m. on a program called *Night Music*, hosted by a woman named Corinne Vale, whose voice he now knew better than he wished to.

Fact: the performance contained the hesitation in bar seven.

Fact: Anton Voss was dead, and his studio was sealed, and sealed rooms do not mail things—but executors do, archivists do, and the conservatory's own library had taken possession of the Voss papers fourteen months ago. Freya knew this because he had dusted the shelves of the sub-basement archive himself, had run his cloth along the acid-free boxes marked VOSS, A. — ESTATE, and had felt, that day, the particular vertigo of a man cleaning the tomb of the person who ruined him.

Fact: Ilse Marchetti was in the building. Ilse Marchetti had looked at him for three seconds. Ilse Marchetti had said *good evening* at dawn.

He looked down at the electric bill. Three names, and beneath the third, in smaller letters, almost unconsciously, he had written a fourth line:

*Who benefits from my silence?*

He had not meant to write it. But there it was, in his careful hand, and it was the right question, and he hated it, because the answer was: everyone. Everyone benefited from Freya Winterbourne's silence. The conservatory benefited; its history stayed tidy. Anton's legacy benefited; untarnished, monumental. Halloran—no, he didn't know about Halloran yet, couldn't know—but the field benefited, the whole elegant machinery of credit and reputation benefited, because the machine ran smoothly precisely as long as men like Freya stayed in the boiler rooms and the women like Ilse stayed abroad and the dead stayed conveniently dead.

Only one person lost, under the current arrangement. And that person had gotten used to losing. That was the discipline of denial: it was not cowardice, exactly. It was a craft. You got good at it. You developed technique. You learned to hold the dissonance indefinitely, to live inside the unresolved chord, to call the suspension home.

But the radio had changed the key.

Because now the dissonance was *audible to others*. Someone out there was playing his sentence—his unsent letter, his nineteen bars of cowardice and love—and calling it anonymous, and anonymous meant available, and available meant that any day now someone else would step forward and sign it. That was how these things went. He had seen it happen with the quartet, twenty years ago; he had watched Anton's name get engraved where his should have been, and he had told himself the same soothing lie he was reaching for now: *it doesn't matter whose it is.*

It matters, said the part of him that had been silent for twenty years, the part that lived below the discipline, below the craft, down in the boiler room of the self where Dov-like furnaces roared. *It matters. You know it matters. You have always known it matters.*

Freya looked at the window. The blue was turning to gray at the edges; the city was rehearsing its return. In ninety minutes he would need to sleep or not sleep, and in eleven hours he would clock in at the service entrance on Delacroix Street, and somewhere in that building—upstairs, in the director's office with its view of the atrium, or downstairs, in a sealed archive box marked VOSS, A. — ESTATE—sat the answer to a question he was no longer sure he wanted answered.

Because here was the thing he had not yet let himself think all the way through, the thought that waited at the bottom of the list like a note held past its value:

If Ilse had the piece—if she had carried it for twenty years, across borders and decades, the way you carry a letter you cannot deliver—then she had known all along what it was. Who it was for. Whose handwriting was on the manuscript, because composers' manuscripts are fingerprints, and she had sight-read his handwriting at nineteen in Room Nine before Anton ever touched it.

Which meant she had listened to him say *it doesn't matter whose it is*, and she had left anyway, and she had stayed gone for twenty years, and now she was back, and she had looked at him for three seconds too long, and she had said *good evening* at dawn.

Either she did not know he was the one pushing the mop.

Or she had always known, and the knowing had changed nothing, and that possibility was worse than any theft.

Freya folded the electric bill along its existing creases and put it in the drawer with the takeout menus, beneath the loose batteries and the dead flashlight, where he kept the few documents of his inner life. Then he lay down on the bed with his clothes on and did not sleep, and at 6:15 the laundromat's dryers started their rotation below him like slow hearts, and at 7:00 he rose, and made coffee he drank this time, and took the bus to the conservatory, and clocked in, and pushed his cart to the east wing.

His plan—for it was a plan, the first plan he had made in twenty years that pointed anywhere but down—was simple and terrible: he was going to find out what was in the Voss boxes. Tonight, when the building emptied. Tomorrow at the latest. He would dust his way down to the sub-basement, and he would open the estate of the man who had borrowed his courage and spent it, and he would look at the inventory of ghosts with his own eyes.

He was halfway through mopping the corridor outside the practice rooms when the elevator opened at the far end of the hall, and a voice he had spent two decades not hearing said, lightly, to someone accompanying her:

"And this wing we'll renovate last—the acoustics in the old rooms are too good to lose. Especially Nine."

Room Nine.

Freya kept mopping. His hands did not shake; he noted this with distant pride. And then the footsteps came closer, and paused—just for a moment, just long enough—beside the wet gleam of his clean floor, and Ilse Marchetti said, to him or to the air or to the ghost of a nineteen-year-old boy who had once stood in this exact spot and lied:

"They tell me the night staff keeps this place better than the day crew ever did."

And she walked on, leaving him alone with the mop, the echo, and the sudden, vertiginous certainty that the inventory needed revising—that the ghosts were not all in the archive, that at least one of them was walking the halls upstairs in expensive shoes, and that she had just, in the language of their shared and borrowed tongue, opened the door of Room Nine and asked him, in effect, to come in.
