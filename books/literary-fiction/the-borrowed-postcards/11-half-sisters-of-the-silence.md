## 11. Half-Sisters of the Silence

My uncle Emeka lived forty minutes away in a bungalow so tidy it looked like it had been ironed. I knew this because I'd been there twice as a child—once for a Christmas that smelled of nutmeg and disapproval, and once for a christening where he'd held the baby at arm's length like a document he was being asked to sign.

He answered the door on the second knock, wearing a cardigan the color of weak tea, and his face did something complicated when he saw me. Grief first. Then arithmetic—he was already calculating what I wanted and how much it would cost him.

"Xanthe." He said my name carefully, as if returning a borrowed tool. "You look like your mother."

"People keep saying that."

"It's not a compliment." He stepped back to let me in. "It's a warning."

* * *

The bungalow was exactly as I remembered: doilies under everything, a wall of framed certificates, and a clock that ticked louder than clocks should be legally allowed to. On the mantel stood photographs of the family arranged by importance, and my mother occupied a small frame near the edge, slightly angled away from the center, as though even in picture form she'd been seated at the far end of the table.

He made tea without asking whether I wanted any, which in his language was hospitality and interrogation combined. We sat across from each other in armchairs positioned like opposing counsel.

"I'm cataloging the house," I said. "For the sale. There are boxes of things I can't identify. Letters. Postcards."

His teacup paused halfway to his mouth—a pause of perhaps half a second, but I had spent twelve years learning to read silences, and this one was fluent.

"Postcards," he repeated.

"Unsent ones. Addressed to her. In handwriting I don't recognize."

He set the cup down with great precision, the way you'd set down something that might go off. "Your mother was a private woman."

"She was a locked woman," I said. "There's a difference, and I've recently been given the tour."

"Xanthe—"

"A woman named Odile came to the house. She wrote a song for my mother. Forty years ago. My mother hummed it while she cooked dinner for us, Uncle Emeka. For years. And nobody—" My voice did something embarrassing, so I stopped and restarted. "Nobody thought I should know?"

The clock ticked through what felt like an entire fiscal quarter.

"You've been talking to that woman," he said finally.

"She came to *my* door. Holding a tape she'd kept since before I was born."

"And you let her in." It wasn't a question; it was a verdict.

I looked at him—at the cardigan, the doilies, the certificates attesting to a life of correct answers—and felt something hot rise in me that had been waiting weeks for permission. "Yes. I let her in. I let her sit at my mother's piano and play a song my mother never recorded, never mentioned, never once let slip except through her hands while stirring soup. So yes, Uncle. I let her in. Someone had to open a door in this family, and apparently the gene for it skipped every generation until mine."

* * *

Here is what I expected: denial. Deflection. A lecture about privacy and propriety and letting the dead rest, delivered in the tone he used for discussing taxes.

What I got instead was worse. He was quiet for a long time, and then he said, "Do you want the honest answer or the kind one?" and my stomach dropped, because in my experience no one offers you that choice unless both options hurt.

"Honest," I said, because I was tired of inheriting the other thing.

He folded his hands. "We knew."

The clock ticked. The tea went cold between us at remarkable speed.

"We knew," he said again, as if once hadn't been punishment enough. "Not—not everything, not in the beginning. But your grandmother found letters when Adaeze was twenty-three. Burned them, most of them, but not before everyone read enough. And then there was the summer she came back from Lisbon with that look on her face, and Papa sat her down in the kitchen and had what our family calls a conversation, which means one person talks and the other person learns their sentence."

"Lisbon," I said. "1987."

"You've seen the postcard." He didn't seem surprised. Nothing about him seemed surprised anymore; surprise, I realized, was a luxury his generation had declined at some point decades ago, along with soft cheese and public feeling.

"There's a postcard," I said. "Unsent. From her, to herself, or to Odile, or to no one—I don't know yet."

"To no one," he said. "That was the arrangement. Everything could be felt as long as it was addressed to no one."

I gripped the arms of the chair. "So the whole family knew. Grandmother. You. Who else? Did my father know?"

He winced, which was answer enough, but he gave it anyway, because he had decided somewhere in the last five minutes to pay the full toll. "Your father suspected. He chose not to confirm it. That was his contribution to the architecture." He rubbed his eyes. "Everyone contributed, Xanthe. That's what I'm trying to tell you. It wasn't one person's cowardice. It was a load-bearing silence. We all held up our corner."

"A load-bearing—" I laughed, and it came out ugly. "You make it sound structural. Like a bridge."

"It *was* a bridge," he said sharply. "It carried all of us over a very bad river. Your grandfather would have put her out. The church would have finished the job. Her own sister—your Aunt Ngozi, whom you remember as generous—wrote her a letter in 1991 telling her that if she ever contacted that woman again, the family would close ranks against her completely. Against *her*, mind you. Not the woman. Her." He shook his head slowly. "And your mother read that letter, and she folded it, and she put it in a drawer, and she stayed. She married. She had you. She hummed her song into soup pots where no one could hear it properly. And we called that survival, and we were right, and it worked, and I have hated every day of it for forty years."

The room tilted slightly. I held onto the chair arms.

"You hated it," I said. "And you did nothing."

"I did nothing *loudly*," he said, "for four decades, which is its own vocation." And then, horribly, his eyes filled. My uncle, who had once told a crying nephew that tears were a leak in the roof of a man's dignity, sat in his ironed bungalow with wet eyes and did not wipe them fast enough.

"I loved her," he said. "She was the only person who ever made me laugh until I couldn't breathe. Do you know what it's like to watch someone you love get smaller every year—smaller at Christmas, smaller at funerals, smaller in photographs, until they're standing at the edge of the frame holding a plate of food nobody asked them to bring? And to know why? And to say nothing?" His voice cracked on the last word. "I said nothing for so long that saying nothing became the only thing I knew how to say to her. At the end, at the hospital, I held her hand and I wanted to tell her—I don't even know what. That I saw her. That I always saw her. And what came out was 'the nurses here are very competent.' Competent. That was my eulogy to my sister. A review of the staffing."

I should have gone to him then. Some part of me registered that—the old man folding in on himself like a letter being returned to sender—but the larger part of me was still standing in the rubble of a demolished assumption: that the silence had been my mother's alone, her private vault, her failure. It hadn't been a vault. It had been a house, and the whole family had lived in it, and they'd all paid rent in the currency of things unsaid, and my mother had been the landlady of a property she never chose.

"Why are you telling me this now?" I asked. "At the funeral you barely spoke to me. When I called about the estate, you said, and I quote, 'sell it quickly and cleanly.'"

"Because selling it quickly and cleanly was the plan," he said simply. "That was always the plan. Box it, price it, disperse it, forget it. That's how you survive a thing—you make it efficient." He looked at me with something that might have been awe or might have been alarm. "But you're not doing that, are you. You're opening drawers. You're inviting strangers in. You're digging."

"Yes."

"Stop digging, Xanthe."

"No."

"*No?*"

"No," I said, and heard my mother's stubbornness come out of my mouth like a ghost clearing its throat. "You buried her alive in that house, all of you, with love, I believe you, with actual love, and you called it protection. Well, I'm the one who inherited the property, and I'm doing an inspection. Every room. Every box. Every postcard addressed to no one."

He stared at me for a long moment. Then he did something I had never seen him do: he laughed. It was short and startled and rueful, a laugh escaping custody.

"God," he said. "She used to say no exactly like that. Same pitch. Same jaw." He stood, went to a cabinet, and poured himself a whisky at eleven in the morning, pouring a second one and setting it in front of me without ceremony. "Your grandmother used to say Adaeze was the quiet one. Quiet! As if quiet were a personality and not a sentence being served."

I took the glass. We drank in silence, and for once the silence between us felt less like a wall and more like a shared bench—two people sitting in the same weather.

"The woman," he said eventually. "Odile. What does she want?"

"I don't think she wants anything," I said. "I think she's just been carrying it. Forty years of carrying it. She keeps a photograph of them on a pier. She kept the master tape. She taught music to children for a living and wrote one song in her life, as far as I can tell, and it was for a woman who hummed it into soup."

Emeka turned his glass slowly on the coaster. "And the postcards. The ones in the shoebox. Have you read them?"

"All of them. Except—" I hesitated. "Except the last one. It has one unfinished sentence. I've been trying to work out how it ends."

"Don't," he said quickly.

"Why not?"

"Because whatever you decide it says, you'll be wrong, and then you'll have to live inside your wrongness, and it will fit you badly, the way all our sentences fit us badly." He drained his drink. "Believe me. I'm an expert in ill-fitting sentences."

"Then help me get it right. Tell me what she would have said."

"I don't know." He said it flatly, and then, quieter: "That's the whole tragedy, Xanthe. None of us knew. We built a fortress around a woman and none of us ever learned what was inside her. We protected a stranger for forty years and called her our daughter, our sister. She was in the house the entire time and we never once let her speak." He set down the glass with a click. "Don't repeat our mistake by finishing her sentence for her. Find out what she actually left behind. All of it."

* * *

At the door, shrugging into my coat, I remembered something. "The sealed bedroom. The back one. Was it always locked?"

He had been mid-handshake; the handshake stalled. Something moved across his face—guilt, or its fossil record.

"When I was fourteen," he said, "she caught me trying the handle. She didn't shout. She just stood at the end of the hall and said, 'That room is where I keep the true thing.' And I never tried again." He opened the door. Morning light fell into the hallway, ordinary and merciless. "Whatever's in there, Xanthe—it's the true thing. It's the only room in that house she ever defended."

I drove home with the windows down despite the cold, needing air that hadn't been breathed by anyone's secrets. The hedgerows went by, brown and patient. Somewhere behind me my uncle was washing two glasses and putting them away, resuming his life of correct answers, minus one.

Somewhere ahead of me was a house with thirty days left on its listing, a shoebox of postcards addressed to no one, and a door at the end of a hallway that my mother had guarded for thirty years.

I had cataloged every room in that house.

Except one.
