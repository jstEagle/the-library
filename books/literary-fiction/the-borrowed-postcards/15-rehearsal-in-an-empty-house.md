## 15. Rehearsal in an Empty House

I had rehearsed the proposal for three days, which was absurd. I was seventy-one years old. I had stood in front of examination panels, hostile parents, one bishop, and a school inspector who smelled of pipe tobacco and disapproval. I had told a gifted eleven-year-old that she was lazy and meant it kindly, and told a hopeless one that she was gifted and meant it as a mercy. I had, once, in 1974, played Chopin's Funeral March at a wedding because the organist was drunk and someone had to.

And yet I stood on Xanthe Okafor's doorstep holding a folder of manuscript paper like a boy delivering a valentine, and I could not make my hand press the bell.

The house had grown quieter since the offer was accepted. That was the thing nobody tells you about sold houses: they begin rehearsing their emptiness early, the way an actor learns his exit lines before the last act. The garden had gone to seed in the honest way of October. A "SOLD" sign leaned against the hedge with the posture of something ashamed of itself. Through the front window I could see stacked boxes, labeled in Xanthe's careful hand — a hand that was not her mother's, and yet was, the way an echo is a voice.

I pressed the bell. I heard it ring somewhere deep in the house, in a room that had probably not heard a bell in weeks, and I thought: *this is what forty years of not ringing a bell looks like.* I had become an expert in the acoustics of the unpressed doorbell. It was, I reflected, the only instrument I had never mastered.

Footsteps. The door opened, and Xanthe stood there in a man's flannel shirt and reading glasses pushed up into her hair, holding a mug of something she had clearly forgotten she was drinking.

"Odile." Not a question. She had stopped being surprised by me somewhere around the fourth visit. I counted that as progress, though progress toward what, neither of us had said.

"I've come," I announced, "to propose something impossible."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she stepped aside. "You'd better come in. The kettle's on. Well — the kettle was on an hour ago. It may have achieved enlightenment by now."

* * *

The kitchen was the only room in the house that still looked lived in, because it was the only room Xanthe still permitted herself to live in. The parlor had been stripped for photographs; the estate agent's pictures had made it look, in Xanthe's words, like "a very tasteful crime scene." But the kitchen still had the kettle, two chairs, and a window over the sink where a row of herbs — thyme, sage, something I didn't recognize — carried on in pots like employees who hadn't been told the business was closing.

I set the folder on the table between us.

Xanthe looked at it. "Is that—"

"Sheet music. The song. Written out properly. All of it — the verses she never hummed, the middle section, the ending I wrote in 1991 and hated and rewrote in 2003 and hated slightly less." I kept my hands flat on the folder, as if it might otherwise fly away. "Forty years, Xanthe. It has existed in three places: my head, her humming, and a shoebox. I am proposing a fourth."

The kettle, achieving enlightenment at last, clicked off.

"A fourth place," Xanthe repeated.

"The open house." I said it quickly, the way you pull a bandage. "The final viewing. The estate agent will fling open the doors, strangers will walk through measuring things with their eyes, and I will sit at the piano and play the song through. Once. In full. And you—" and here my voice did something I did not authorize it to do, something thin and young — "you will read the sentence. Her sentence. The one you finished. Aloud. To the room."

The silence that followed had texture. I had spent a career teaching children to count rests, and I can tell you that this one ran a full eight bars.

"You're joking," Xanthe said finally.

"I have never joked in my life. Ask anyone. They'll tell you it's my greatest failing as a teacher. Children would say, *Miss Fenn, is this a joke?* and I would say, *Music is not a joke,* and they would sigh, and I would think, well, that's why you play it like that."

"Odile." She took off her glasses, which I had learned was what she did instead of raising her voice. "I can't sing."

"Good. I'm not asking you to sing."

"I can't *speak* it. That sentence — I wrote it at three in the morning on the back of a postcard, and it made sense at three in the morning, and I have read it four times since and every time it's like — like reading a letter you wrote while feverish. You admire the handwriting and you can't believe the content."

"Then you'll read it feverishly," I said. "That's the entire tradition of art, Xanthe. Everyone admires the handwriting and no one believes the content, and we do it anyway, in public, terrified, because the alternative is a shoebox."

She stood up. She went to the window. She looked at the herbs. I recognized the maneuver; her mother had invented it. Agnes Okafor, confronted with any feeling larger than a teacup, would develop a sudden and passionate interest in the nearest window, as though the street outside had never once in forty years contained anything worth seeing. I had watched her do it across a hotel bar in Lisbon in 1987 while I told her I loved her, and she had looked at a tram with the concentration of a woman decoding enemy transmissions.

"You want me to stand in my mother's house," Xanthe said, to the window, "in front of strangers, and say out loud the thing she couldn't say to me, to Odile, to God, to the *postcards* — the postcards, Odile, which she addressed and stamped and never mailed, which is a level of almost that I can't even—"

"Yes," I said.

"And you'll play."

"I'll play."

"In front of people. The song you wrote for her. The song that has been — I don't know, a *secret*, the whole architecture of it, the whole—" She turned around. Her eyes were wet and furious, which is the combination I have always found most honest in a human face. "Do you understand what you're asking? If we do this, it's not a shoebox anymore. It's not yours. It's not mine. It's *out*. You can't take it back. Forty years of keeping it, and you want to spend it in one afternoon on strangers who came for the square footage?"

"Yes," I said again, because it was true, and because I had learned from her mother that the second *yes* is the one that costs.

Xanthe sat down across from me. She put both hands around the cold mug as if it were a small animal she was trying to warm.

"Why?" she asked, and it was not a challenge. It was the real question, the one underneath, and I owed it the real answer, so I gave it the only way I know how, which is badly and at length.

"Because I am seventy-one years old," I said. "Because I have taught four hundred children to play other people's music and I have never once played my own in front of anyone, and I have told myself this was humility, and it was not humility, it was the same disease she had. Because when she died I discovered that a secret is not a possession. It's a debt. And I have been paying interest on it for forty years in a currency I don't even have a name for, and I would like, before the keys change hands, to *settle the account.*" I looked at the folder between us. "Also because the song is better than either of us and it deserves an audience larger than a kitchen."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere in the house a box settled, or a floorboard, or a memory.

"She'd hate it," Xanthe said.

"Agnes would be *furious,*" I agreed. "She would look at that window as though it had personally betrayed her. She would say, *Odile, you cannot be serious,* in the voice she used for the word *Odile*, which was a whole sentence by itself." I smiled, and found to my surprise that the smile did not hurt. "And then she would hum the melody, because she could no more stop loving it than she could stop loving me, and she knew it, and that knowledge was the tragedy and the joke of her whole life."

Xanthe laughed — a short, wet, surprised laugh, the kind that ambushes people in kitchens.

"The joke," she said. "You think it was a joke."

"I think every silence that large has to be at least half comedy or it kills you. She spent forty years not saying one sentence, Xanthe. One sentence. She wrote it on postcards. She wrote it in songs. She built an entire *architecture of almost* — and it was agony, yes, and it was cowardice, yes, and it was also — forgive me — *funny*, in the way that only the great refusals are funny. She addressed the envelope. She licked the stamp. She put it in her handbag. She took it out. For twelve years. If that isn't a farce, I don't know what the word means."

Xanthe was quiet for a while. Then she said, "You loved her very much," and it wasn't a question either, and I didn't answer it, because some things are said by not saying them, and I have had a lifetime's practice.

* * *

We did not decide anything that afternoon. We drank the enlightened tea. We argued. She said the estate agent would object; I said the estate agent could stand in the hall and object rhythmically, it might improve the acoustics. She said she couldn't sing; I said for the fourth time that nobody was asking her to sing. She said, "What if nobody stays to listen," and I said, "Then we will have played it to an empty house, which is where it has lived for forty years, so the venue will at least be familiar."

But I could see it working in her, the way you can see weather working in a sky — the idea moving through her, terrifying her, and her not shutting the window on it.

It was three days later that she called me. Evening. I knew before I answered; the telephone has its own weather.

"I've been thinking," she said, "about what you said. About the debt."

"Yes."

"I can't sing it. I can't even read it aloud yet — I tried, in the kitchen, alone, and I got as far as her name and had to sit down on the floor, which is undignified in a house with hardwood." A breath. "But I can hold the music. I can stand there and hold the sheet music while you play. Like — I don't know. Like a page-turner. Like the person whose whole job is to be there at the right moment and turn to the next part."

"That," I said, and my voice did the unauthorized thing again, "is the most important job in the performance. Ask any accompanist. The page-turner holds the entire future of the piece in her two hands, and everyone forgets her, and she does it anyway. It is the purest form of love in music. I have waited my whole life for a good page-turner."

There was a pause, and then Xanthe laughed, that wet surprised laugh again, and said, "You're impossible."

"I'm seventy-one. I'm allowed."

"Saturday," she said. "The open house is Saturday. Two o'clock. The agent does his speech at half two, and everyone drifts into the parlor around quarter to three because it's the room with the light." Another breath, longer. "Odile — if I stand there and I can't do it. If I open my mouth and nothing comes. If I just — stand there holding the pages like an idiot—"

"Then you will have stood in your mother's house holding her song in your hands in front of witnesses," I said. "That is not nothing, Xanthe. That is the furthest anyone in your family has gotten in forty years."

Another silence, but a different kind — the kind that comes after a decision, when the rest has gone quiet because its work is done.

"Saturday," she said. "God help us."

"He won't," I said. "But the piano might. It's a Broadwood. They were built for exactly this kind of recklessness."

* * *

I went home on the bus, because I have never learned to drive and at seventy-one I regard this as one of my finer moral achievements, and I sat upstairs at the window with the folder open on my knees, and I played it through in my head, as I have done every night for forty years, and for the first time it did not sound like a secret.

It sounded like a dare.

That is what I want to say about it, since no one has asked: a secret is a wall you build around yourself, and you tell yourself it is protection, and some of it is, but a dare — a dare is aimed. You aim it at the thing that has been defeating you. Agnes aimed her silence at the world and it defeated her gently, over decades, the way weather defeats a house. I am aiming this song at the silence itself, at the family curse of it, at the great inherited *hush* that took a woman who wrote songs and turned her into a woman who hummed, that took a girl who had questions and turned her into a woman who catalogs boxes.

And if it fails — if the strangers mill about, if the estate agent clears his throat, if Xanthe stands there with the pages trembling and no sound comes — then at least we will have aimed. There is a dignity in aiming that no secret has ever had.

I was still sitting there when the phone rang again. Xanthe. Her voice was strange — tight, and moving fast, the voice of someone reporting an accident.

"Odile — you're still up? Good. Listen. There's a car outside the house. Someone's at the door. It's — it's my uncle. He's got a box. He's taking things out of the house, Odile, at ten o'clock at night, and he's got a box, and I think I know which box it is."

I closed the folder.

"Xanthe," I said. "Do not let him leave."
