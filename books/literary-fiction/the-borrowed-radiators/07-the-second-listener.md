## 7. The Second Listener

Marisol Vega had learned to read envelopes the way other people read faces.

Eleven years behind the microphone at KFRQ—ninety-one point three on the dial, forty watts of stubbornness broadcasting out of a converted dry cleaner on Delmore Avenue—had taught her that the mail was the truest part of any radio station. Requests came wrapped in flattery. Complaints came wrapped in certainty. But the packages that arrived without return addresses, padded in brown paper, taped with more care than postage required—those were confessions. Those were people handing you something they couldn't hold anymore and trusting you not to drop it.

She had dropped things before. She was not proud of it. But she had learned.

So when the first tape had come, nineteen years ago—a cassette in a case gone soft at the hinges, no note, postmarked from a town she'd had to look up—she hadn't played it right away. It sat on the shelf above the mixing board for two weeks, between a promotional copy of an album nobody requested and a jar of pens that didn't write, until one night at 2 a.m., dead air breathing its particular loneliness into the studio, she fed it into the deck just to hear what a stranger had trusted her with.

What came out was a song with no name. A man's voice, older than the melody it carried, singing lyrics that sounded like they'd been written by someone apologizing to a house.

She played it again. Then again. Then, because it was 2 a.m. and no one was listening except the insomniacs and the truckers and the grieving, she put it on the air without a title, without a credit, and said only: *From somebody's drawer. If this is yours, call me.*

Nobody called. The song stayed.

That was how the orphan shelf began—not as a format, not as a mission statement, but as an accident of tenderness. Over the years it grew: tapes mailed in, tapes left on the doorstep in shoeboxes, once a reel-to-reel in a grocery bag with a handle tied shut. Songs whose owners had died, or vanished, or simply never claimed them. Marisol played them in the small hours and spoke their absence aloud, because a song with no name still deserved to be called something.

And every year, like a tide that kept its own calendar, another package came from the same direction.

* * *

Wren Eastwick arrived at the station on a Thursday, in the hour before sunset, when the light through the front window turned the dust into weather. Marisol saw her through the glass before she reached the door—young woman, mid-thirties maybe, carrying herself the way people carry themselves after a funeral, which is to say carefully, as if their own skeleton were borrowed and might be repossessed.

"You're the DJ," the woman said, when Marisol opened up. Not a question. "The one who plays songs nobody owns."

"I play songs whose owners haven't come forward yet." Marisol stepped aside to let her in. "There's a difference. Most of them, I'm still waiting."

"My name is Wren Eastwick." She said it like she was setting something down. "You played a song two nights ago. No title. You said—" and here her voice did something complicated, tightened and loosened at once, "—you said it came from somebody's drawer."

Marisol felt the floor of the studio tilt slightly, the way it always did when the past walked in wearing present clothes. "Come sit down," she said. "I'm going to make coffee, and you're going to tell me why your face looks like that."

The studio was small enough that intimacy wasn't a choice; it was architecture. Two chairs, a mixing board with faders worn silver at the tips, the orphan shelf running along the wall in milk crates, each tape labeled in Marisol's handwriting with a date and a number, because names were a luxury the mail rarely provided. Wren sat with her spine straight and her hands folded, a woman conducting an inventory of the room, and Marisol recognized that too—the cataloguing gaze of someone who had recently learned that everything can be taken away and so everything must be counted.

"Two nights ago," Wren said. "The song. The lyrics were about radiators. About borrowing warmth." She stopped. Started again. "My father wrote those words. He never recorded them. He never played them for anyone that I know of. He died three weeks ago, and I found them in his house, on a sheet of paper, with no music attached. And then I heard them on your station, sung by a stranger, credited to a name I've never heard. T. Okafor."

Marisol set down the coffee pot slowly. Nineteen years of small hours had taught her to let silence do the heavy lifting, but this silence had weight in it, and she chose her words the way you choose footing on ice.

"That song," she said, "has been coming to me for a long time."

"Coming to you?"

"In the mail." She crossed to the shelf, ran her finger along the spines of cases until she found the ones she wanted, and brought back four cassettes, laying them on the board like a hand of cards. "Same handwriting on the labels—or no labels, same handwriting on the *packages*. Same postmark region. Same brown paper, same brand of tape, same careful taping along every edge, like whoever wraps them is sealing something, not shipping it." She tapped the oldest one. "This was the first. Nineteen years ago."

Wren looked at the cassettes the way people look at x-rays. "Nineteen years."

"Every year or two, another one arrives. Sometimes the same song, different recordings—different rooms, you can hear the rooms. Sometimes fragments. Once, just thirty seconds of a piano losing a fight with itself." Marisol almost smiled. "I built half my overnight format around them. I call them the drawer songs. My listeners think it's a bit. An aesthetic. Orphaned music, anonymous donors, the romance of the unclaimed." She looked at Wren. "It's not a bit. Somebody out there has been mailing me a dead man's song for two decades, one piece at a time, and I have never known why. Never known who. Never even known if the person mailing them was the person who made them."

"And the dates," Wren said quietly. "Do the dates mean anything?"

Marisol went still.

Because yes. Yes, that was the thing she had never told anyone, the thing she kept in the same internal drawer where she kept her mother's last voicemail and the sound of her own name said by someone who no longer loved her. The packages didn't arrive randomly. She had checked, years ago, late one night with a calendar and a red pen, telling herself it was professional curiosity and knowing it was something hungrier than that.

"They arrive on anniversaries," she said. "Not holidays. Not birthdays I can find. Dates that don't announce themselves." She pulled a battered planner from under the board, pages soft as cloth from handling, and opened it to a page dense with her own cramped annotations. "March ninth. August twenty-second. January fourth. November thirtieth. Always within a day or two of the same dates, year after year, like clockwork that runs on grief instead of batteries." She looked up. "I used to think whoever was sending them was marking deaths. Now I think—I don't know what I think. I've been keeping somebody's vigil for nineteen years and I don't even know whose."

The confession came out of her before she could dress it up, and once it was out she heard what it really was: not information but guilt, nineteen years of it, pressed flat. She had built a small fame on these songs. Listeners wrote in about them. A college radio podcast had done an episode. And through all of it she had been, she understood now, standing in a dark room holding a candle for someone whose name she'd never asked—keeping a promise she'd never been told existed, for people she'd never met, while the actual heirs of whatever these tapes were lived out there beyond the signal, not knowing their own inheritance was spinning at forty watts into the night.

"I'm sorry," Marisol said, and meant it in her bones. "Whatever this is—if it's yours, if he's yours—it passed through my hands for years and I never sent it home. I didn't know there was a home to send it to."

Wren was looking at the four cassettes. Her hand hovered over the oldest one without touching it, the way you hover over a door you're afraid will open.

"Play it," she said. "The first one. Please."

* * *

Marisol threaded the tape with hands that were steadier than she expected them to be. Nineteen years since she'd played it straight through—she'd aired pieces of it, sampled it, built a whole mythology around it—but playing it alone in the studio and playing it in front of the daughter of whoever was on it were different acts entirely, and she knew it the way you know a room changes when a certain person enters it.

"I want you to know," she said, finger on the play key, "that when I first heard this, I thought it was a love song. Then I thought it was a eulogy. I've gone back and forth for nineteen years. You might settle it in about four minutes."

She pressed play.

Hiss first—the long oceanic hiss of old tape, the sound of time itself given a frequency—and then a room. You could hear the room before anything else: hard walls, something metallic resonating somewhere, a basement or a garage or a church hall with the radiators clanking. And then voices, two of them, young, so young that Marisol felt her chest tighten the way she always did at the sound of anyone's youth preserved in magnetic particles.

"—no, no, you're rushing it, you always rush it—"

"I'm not rushing it, the *song* is rushing, I'm just keeping up—"

"It's a ballad, Tomás. Ballads don't rush. Ballads *linger*. That's the whole job of a ballad."

"Linger. Listen to him. He learned the word *linger* last week and now it's the whole job—"

Laughter. Both of them laughing, the laughter of people who have laughed this way a thousand times and expect to laugh this way ten thousand more, laughter with no shadow in it, no awareness of itself, and Marisol watched Wren across the board and saw the exact moment the younger woman recognized the second voice—saw her whole body change, the inventory-taking stillness breaking open like ice in spring.

"That's him," Wren whispered. "That's my father. He sounds—he sounds *young*. I never—" Her hand rose to her mouth. "He never laughed like that around me. I mean he laughed, but not—this isn't a man laughing. This is a boy."

The guitar came in then, hesitant, finding its feet, and the other voice—Tomás, the one who rushed—began to sing, and it was the song, the radiator song, but rawer, unfinished, the lyrics stumbling and being repaired in real time while the father's voice corrected and coaxed and harmonized, and the two of them wove together the way only voices do when they've been weaving for years, finishing each other's phrases, arguing through the music and inside it—

*"You borrow heat, you borrow bread,*
*you borrow winter's only bed—*
*and what you take you must return,*
*isn't that the way we learn—"*

"—it's *clunky*, that line's clunky—"

"*Your* line's clunky—"

"It's OUR line, that's the problem—"

More laughter, dissolving into the verse again, and Marisol realized she had her eyes closed, that she was gripping the edge of the board, that nineteen years of mystery had just acquired a face and a family and a funeral, and that the guilt she'd named earlier was nothing compared to what she felt now, hearing the living room of two dead-or-buried boys' friendship playing out in her headphones like a message that had been waiting decades for its addressee to walk in off the street.

The song wound down. The tape kept rolling—people always forgot the tape kept rolling—and there was shuffling, a chair scraping, the clank of that metal thing again, closer now, and then quiet, and then the other voice, Tomás, speaking low, almost too soft for the microphone, almost as if he'd forgotten it was on:

"Hey. Hey, listen. Before you go. I want to say the thing. The sentence."

A pause. The father's voice, wary and warm at once: "You don't have to say it."

"I want it on tape. So it exists somewhere besides our heads. In case—" A breath. "In case heads aren't reliable. Say it with me."

And then, in a language Marisol had never heard, liquid and structured and utterly foreign, the two young voices spoke together—one sentence, unhurried, deliberate, the kind of sentence you speak when you are building something meant to last—and though she understood none of it, Marisol heard the shape of it clearly: an apology braided into an invitation, a debt acknowledged and handed over, a door held open.

The tape hissed. Clicked. Ended.

The studio was silent except for the deck's motor winding down and, beneath it, Wren Eastwick crying—not loudly, not performatively, but with the terrible efficiency of someone who has been holding an inventory together for weeks and has just heard the one item on the list she didn't know was missing.

Marisol reached across the board and put her hand over Wren's, because some transmissions don't need a transmitter.

"The sentence," Wren managed finally. "Say it again. Play it again. I've seen that sentence. Written down. In my father's handwriting, in a letter I can't read, circled twice like it was the only thing in the world worth circling." She lifted her head, and her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely ferocious. "Whoever Tomás is—wherever he is—he's the only other person alive who knows what it means. And Marisol—" her voice dropped, steadied, became something almost like awe, "—he's been sending you these tapes for nineteen years. On anniversaries. Which means he knows the dates. Which means somewhere out there, he's still counting."

Marisol looked at the four cassettes on the board, at the shelf of orphans behind them, at the planner full of red-inked dates that had never meant anything to anyone but her.

"Yes," she said slowly, and felt the whole shape of the mystery turn over like soil. "And here's the thing I didn't tell you yet. The last package came six weeks ago." She pulled open the drawer beneath the board, drew out a fifth envelope, unopened, brown paper, edges taped with that same obsessive care. "But it's lighter than all the others. Too light for a tape."

She held it out to Wren between two fingers, like something that might still be warm.

"There's paper inside. Just paper. And Wren—it isn't addressed to me."
