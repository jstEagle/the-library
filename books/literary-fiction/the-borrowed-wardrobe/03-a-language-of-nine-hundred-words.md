## 3. A Language of Nine Hundred Words

The woman was there again on Tuesday, and again on Wednesday, and by Thursday Coral had stopped pretending he wasn't watching for her.

He told himself it was professional habit. A person who stood in the same place at the same hour every day was a pattern, and patterns were his business. He measured patterns. He flagged them, photographed them, filed them in reports that nobody read until something cracked. The woman on the walkway was a pattern. That was all.

She stood at the midpoint of the pedestrian lane on the Kestrel Street Bridge, both hands resting on the railing, facing the river. Her coat was a heavy wool thing the color of wet slate, buttoned to the throat regardless of the weather, and her hair was white and close-cropped, a halo in the early light when the sun came up over the feed mill. She did not look at the water so much as address it. Her lips moved. Sometimes her chin lifted, the way it does when a person is reaching for a note at the top of their range.

Coral had a clipboard, a laser level, a crack gauge calibrated to a tenth of a millimeter, and a job to do, and still he found himself pausing on his way past her, once, twice, a third time, the way you check a reading that keeps coming back wrong.

On Friday he stopped for good.

"Ma'am," he said. "Are you—everything all right?"

She turned with the unhurried gravity of someone who had been expecting the question for days. Up close she was smaller than she'd seemed from a distance, and older, her skin a deep brown mapped with fine lines like stress fractures in old concrete—lines he knew were not damage but record. Her eyes were quick, though. Quick and amused.

"Everything is fine," she said. Her voice was low and deliberate, each word set down like a stone in a wall.

"You're out here every morning. I wasn't sure—" He gestured vaguely at the river, at her mouth. "I thought maybe you were confused."

"Confused," she repeated, tasting the word. "You thought I was senile. Standing on a bridge talking to water."

"I didn't say—"

"You didn't have to. Your face said it for you." She didn't sound angry. If anything, she sounded like a teacher who had just received an excellent wrong answer. "I am practicing."

"Practicing."

"Practicing." She turned back to the river. Below them the water moved in its long brown muscle, carrying last night's rain toward the sea. "My mother's language has fewer than nine hundred words left in it. I am the only one who speaks them. When I forget one, it is gone. So I practice. Every morning I say them to the river, because the river is the only listener who will not die before I do."

The wind came up the gorge and flattened her words slightly, and Coral found he had leaned in to catch them without deciding to.

"That's—" He stopped. He was a man who measured things, and he could not measure what she'd just said, and the failure made him feel the way a bad reading does: unsteady, untrustworthy of his own instruments. "That's not possible. A whole language. One person."

"One person," she agreed. "One old woman with bad knees and a good memory, for now." She looked at him then, fully, and he had the strange sensation of being inspected—of being the bridge, and her the man with the clipboard. "You watch me. Every day. You think I don't notice. You have a way of looking at things like you're taking their temperature."

"It's my job. I inspect the bridge."

"I know. I've seen your truck. *Department of Infrastructure, Structural Integrity Division.*" She pronounced it with a dry precision that made it sound like a joke someone had once made at his expense. "So you are a man whose whole work is noticing whether things are about to fall down."

"Something like that."

"And who notices you?"

The question landed in a place he hadn't guarded. He opened his mouth and found nothing in it, which was, he would think later, the first honest thing he'd offered her.

"That's what I thought," Maren Okonkwo said.

* * *

She was there Monday. And Tuesday. He did not stop again—told himself he wouldn't—but he slowed as he passed, and on Wednesday she spoke without turning around.

"Her name was Ngozi. My mother. The language is Iyasele. It was hers before it was mine. It was her mother's before that, and her mother's mother's, all the way back to a village on a river you have never heard of, in a country that changed its name twice while my family was learning to be from somewhere else."

Coral stood. He had a gauge to read on pier three. He stood anyway.

"Nine hundred words," he said.

"Fewer. I counted last winter. Eight hundred and sixty-one that I still trust myself to say correctly. Then there are maybe forty more that live at the edge, words I know the shape of but not the weight. A word you can't say correctly is a word already dead. It just hasn't been buried yet."

"Eight hundred and sixty-one." He heard himself repeating her numbers and hated it, the way he hated catching himself checking a measurement twice. But numbers were how he held on to things. "And when you—"

"When I die, the count goes to zero. Not gradually. All at once. Eight hundred and sixty-one words, and then a silence the size of a language." She said it the way she said everything, without drama, and that was what made it unbearable. A lesser woman would have wept. Maren Okonkwo reported.

"Couldn't you—" He gestured at the world. "Record them. Write them down. There are people who do that. Universities. Linguists."

"Shelves." She said the word like a curse in any language. "You want to know what happens to a language on a shelf? It becomes a specimen. A butterfly with a pin through it, labeled in a dead man's handwriting. *Iyasele, Niger-Congo branch, attested speaker: one.* My mother's voice in a file, played by graduate students who will never once use it to call their mother in from the rain." She shook her head slowly. "A language is not words. A language is what two people do with words. One is a museum. Zero is a grave. I am not interested in the museum."

"What are you interested in?"

She was quiet for a long moment. The river said what it always said.

"A student," she said.

* * *

He told Dana about her that night, or he told her the shape of it, the way he'd learned to give his wife shapes instead of things.

"There's a woman on the bridge," he said, over the sound of the television. "Old lady. Says she's the last speaker of some language."

"Mm," said Dana, from inside her phone, inside the blue light of it, inside whatever her sister had posted or whatever the market had done that day or whatever small grievance was currently being nursed in the group chat that had replaced, sometime in the last decade, the conversation they used to have across this same table. "That's sad."

"She says there are eight hundred and sixty-one words left."

"Eight hundred and sixty-one." Dana looked up then, and for one second their eyes met, and Coral felt the old reflex fire—the one that wanted to say *isn't that something, isn't that the strangest thing you ever heard, imagine knowing every word you own and counting them down like coins*—and then he watched the reflex die, watched the moment close like a crack gauge settling, and said instead, "Pass the salt."

She passed the salt. The television said what it said. The marriage did what it did: moved, measurably, predictably, a few millimeters a season, and Coral sat inside it the way he sat inside every structure he inspected, noting the movement, filing it, doing nothing.

Later, in bed, staring at the ceiling, he thought about what she'd asked him. *And who notices you?*

He thought about the fact that the answer had been no one, for so long, that he'd stopped counting it as an answer. Dana noticed the gutters. Dana noticed when the oil needed changing, when his mother's grave needed flowers, when he'd worn the same shirt twice. She noticed his surfaces with the diligence of a woman maintaining a property. But *him*—the thing under the paint, the load-bearing wall, the room nobody entered—

He turned over. Sleep came the way it always came, like a bridge holding.

* * *

Thursday morning it rained, a fine cold drizzle that made the deck plates shine and sent the joggers home. He assumed she wouldn't come. She came. She stood at the railing in her slate coat with the rain beading on her close-cropped hair, saying her words to the river, and Coral stood ten feet away with his clipboard against his chest like a shield and watched her, and hated himself for watching, and watched.

"You'll catch your death," he said finally.

"I'm eighty-one. Death and I have an arrangement. I don't hurry and it doesn't hurry." She turned. Rain ran off the end of her nose. "You came back to ask me something."

"I didn't."

"You stood there for four minutes. Men don't stand in the rain for four minutes unless they're asking something." She waited. She was very good at waiting; he could see that. It was the waiting of someone who had outlasted governments.

"Why me?" The question came out before he'd agreed to it. "The other day. You said you wanted a student. There are—there must be a hundred people who'd—students, professors, people who know about languages. I read gauges. I climb around under bridges and write reports nobody reads. Why would you ask *me*?"

Maren looked at him for a long time. The rain ticked on the railing. Somewhere below, a barge pushed its slow silver wake against the current.

"Because you listen like a man carrying a secret," she said.

The words went through him like a probe through soft concrete—finding the void, the delamination, the thing the surface was hiding. He felt his face do something he couldn't control, some small collapse, and he looked away at the river because the river couldn't see him.

"I don't know what you mean," he said.

"Of course not," she said, kindly, which was worse.

* * *

He refused her properly on Monday.

He'd prepared for it. He was, in his way, a careful man, and he had composed the refusal over the weekend the way he composed reports: complete, courteous, structurally sound. He waited until she'd finished her morning recitation—she'd gone longer today, her voice rising and falling in a music he couldn't parse, and he'd caught himself, twice, trying to find the pattern in it—and then he stepped over and delivered it.

"Mrs. Okonkwo. Maren." Her name felt strange in his mouth, two syllables of someone else's life. "I'm honored. I mean that. But I can't be what you're asking for. I'm not— I don't have the ear for it. I'd be a waste of your time, and your time is—" He stopped himself from saying *limited*, which was true and cruel. "You should have someone who can do it justice."

She listened to the whole thing without blinking. Then she nodded slowly, as if he'd presented her with a load calculation she'd been expecting.

"You refuse very politely," she said. "You've had practice."

"I—"

"Tell me about your mother."

The river went on. The wind went through the cables of the bridge and made its low note, the one he'd learned to hear through, the one he no longer heard at all.

"I don't see what that has to do with—"

"It has everything to do with it. A man who won't take a gift is a man who was taught, early, that he didn't deserve one. So. Your mother."

"She died," Coral said. The words came out flat, pre-measured, the way he'd said them ten thousand times in ten thousand forms. "When I was twelve. An accident. Drowning." He kept his eyes on the water while he said it, which was where his eyes always went when he said it, and he understood, in a distant clinical way, that this was itself a measurement, that some instrument in him had been taking this reading for thirty years.

Maren was quiet. When she spoke, her voice had changed—lower, closer, the voice from inside the wall of words rather than the voice presenting them.

"In my language," she said, "there is no word for *accident*."

He looked at her.

"There are words for what happens—a thing slips, a thing falls, water takes someone—but no word that means *no one is responsible*. That word is an invention. A comfort. We invented it so we could stop looking." She studied his face with those quick, unhurried eyes. "Iyasele does not let you stop looking. That is one of the eight hundred and sixty-one things I could teach you, and I have not even begun, and already you are standing here like a man whose footing has shifted."

"Stop," Coral said. It came out harder than he meant. "Please. I said no. I'm sorry, but the answer's no."

"Very well." She gathered herself, buttoned the top button of her coat against a wind that hadn't changed, and looked out at the river one last time, the way a person looks at a room they're leaving. "You know what the strange thing is, Coral Thackeray? I have asked four people before you. A librarian. A music teacher. Two of my grand-nephews, on the phone, from far away. Every one of them refused, and every one of them refused the way you did—politely, carefully, with excellent reasons. And I understood them. It is an enormous thing, what I am asking. It is asking a man to carry a whole world in his arms on the day the world is ending." She turned to him. "But you are the first one I have asked who looked at me afterward like he was the one who owed an apology."

She walked toward the far end of the bridge, her steps small and even, the slate coat moving against her legs. Coral stood with his clipboard and did not follow. The river carried its rain to the sea. On pier three, he knew, the crack gauge would read exactly what it had read yesterday, and the day before: movement, but stable. Within tolerance. Nothing to report.

He stayed on the walkway a long time after she was gone.

* * *

That night Dana said, "You're late," and he said, "Traffic," and the lie was small and smooth and structural, and she accepted it the way load accepts load, without comment, and they ate.

He washed the dishes. He dried them. He stood at the counter afterward with his hands on the edge of it, and in the window over the sink his reflection hung in the dark glass, faint, a man-shaped absence in his own kitchen.

*Eight hundred and sixty-one words,* he thought. *And then a silence the size of a language.*

He thought about his mother's kitchen, which he had not let himself think about in years—the yellow curtains, the radio, the way she'd called him and Ellis in from the yard with two notes of a whistle, and how there had been a word, some word, she used when they'd tracked mud in, a word that wasn't angry exactly, that meant something more like *oh, you beloved disasters*—and he realized with a slow, cold shock that he could not remember the word, that it had gone where all of her had gone, that a whole vocabulary had died with her in the water and he had never counted it, never once, never stood at a railing and said it out loud to keep it alive.

Dana came to the doorway behind him. He felt her there, a warmth at his back, and waited for her to say something—*come to bed, you're standing in the dark, what's wrong with you*—anything, one word, and he would have turned around, he understood suddenly that he would have turned around, that some door in him had come off its latch.

She said nothing. Her footsteps went up the stairs.

Coral stood in the dark kitchen, holding the edge of the counter, and heard the silence in the house, and thought: *that is what it sounds like. That is what zero sounds like.*

In the morning, he packed his gauge and his clipboard into the truck, and then, before he could think better of it—before the careful man in him could file the impulse under *not to code*—he took a blank notebook from the glovebox, the one he used for sketching pier details, and put it in his coat pocket.

He told himself he was only going to apologize.
