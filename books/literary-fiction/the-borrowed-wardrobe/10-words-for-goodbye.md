## 10. Words for Goodbye

Maren had rearranged her kitchen.

Coral noticed it the moment he stepped through the door on the first Tuesday of October—the small table turned so that both chairs faced the window instead of each other, the two of them looking out together at the strip of yard and the fence and, beyond the fence, the gray suggestion of the river. A kettle sat on the stove though she hadn't offered him tea in three weeks. On the table lay her notebook, open to a page he hadn't seen before, dense with her slanted handwriting, certain words underlined twice.

"Sit," she said. "Today we learn the hard ones."

He pulled his chair up beside hers. Outside, a crow worked at something in the grass, patient as a coroner.

"The hard ones," he repeated.

"Every language has words that are easy because nobody needs them." She tapped the page with one finger, the nail yellowed and thick as a coin. *Olu*—spoon. *Kembe*—door. You can go your whole life without saying them aloud and nothing is lost. But there are other words." She looked at him then, and her eyes in the morning light were pale amber, almost gold, like resin held up to a window. "Words you will need exactly once. And when you need them, if you don't have them, you will stand there with your mouth open like a fish, and the moment will pass through you, and you will never get it back."

* * *

She began with leaving.

"*Fenide*," she said. "To go away from a place. Simple. The feet do it." She said it again, slower, breaking it into its bones: *fe-ni-de*. "But listen—" and here her voice changed, dropped into a lower register, the way a room changes when someone dies in it, "*fenidu.* To leave a person. Not a place. A person."

"The same word?"

"One letter different. One letter, and everything different." She made him say both. His tongue stumbled on the *du*, that soft falling sound, like something set down carefully. "Again."

"Fenidu."

"Again."

"Fenidu."

"You are leaving me right now, in this sentence. Say it like you mean it or don't say it at all."

He laughed, uncomfortable, and she didn't. She waited, hands folded on the table, and the crow outside kept working at whatever it had found, and finally Coral said it again—*fenidu*—and heard, to his own surprise, weight enter the word. Not performance. Weight. As if the syllable had been waiting in him all along, unloaded, and now carried something.

"Better," Maren said quietly. "Now the worse one. *Fenidor.* To be left."

The kitchen was very still. The refrigerator hummed its one note. He said it, and the *or* at the end opened like a mouth.

"My mother used to say there are only two kinds of people in the world at any given moment," Maren said, gazing out at the yard. "Those who are fenide and those who are fenidor. Sometimes the same person within one hour. My mother left our village in 1953. Her mother stood at the road and did not wave. Do you know why?"

Coral shook his head.

"Because waving would have made her fenidor out loud. Some people would rather swallow it whole." Maren's mouth curved, not quite a smile. "I am not my grandmother. I wave. I have spent eighty-one years waving."

* * *

They worked through the morning. Words for departure: the word for the last look back (*sivane*), the word for a road taken without intention of return (*olome*), the word—which had no English equivalent, Maren insisted, and Coral believed her—for the specific sorrow of watching someone walk away who does not turn around (*iruma*). That one took him four tries. The *ru* rolled in a place at the back of his throat he'd never used, some unused room in his body, and when he finally got it, Maren closed her eyes briefly as if hearing an old song from another room.

Then she turned the page, and her finger came down on a new section, and her voice changed again.

"Now we mourn."

"Maren—"

"These are the words I have kept longest. Forty years in this country and no one to give them to. They are getting impatient." She smoothed the page with her palm, an oddly tender gesture, like calming an animal. "*Dumai.* To weep openly. Everyone has dumai. Even the English." A dry glance at him. "*Dumaise.* To weep alone, where no one sees. Most men are fluent in dumaise and illiterate in everything else."

Coral's chest did something complicated and silent.

"*Sombwe.* The mourning period. Literally: the time of sitting low. When someone dies, the family sits low—on the floor, on low stools—for seven days. You do not stand above your grief. You sit inside it until it knows your name and you know its name, and then, on the eighth day, everyone stands up together. Nobody stands alone." She looked at him. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? In my mother's language, grief is not private property. It is a room the whole family enters and sits down in."

"We don't have rooms like that," Coral said. It came out before he could stop it, flat and true.

"No," Maren agreed. "You have hallways. Long ones. People walking past each other for years." She let that sit between them, then touched the next line. "*Kavari.* To bury. And *kavarise*—" her voice caught, just slightly, the first crack he'd ever heard in it, "—to carry the dead yourself. On your own shoulders. There is honor in kavari. The person who carries the body carries also the debt of the living to the dead. It is not a burden. It is a position. Like an office you hold."

* * *

It was near noon when she taught him the last one, and she taught it slowly, the way you hand someone something breakable across a wide gap.

"*Ndeva.*" She said it twice. "It means: I will carry this. Present tense. Not *I carried*, not *I will have carried*. Present. Because the carrying does not finish. You do not put grief down like luggage at a station. You carry it, and it becomes part of how you walk." She pressed her thin hand flat against her own sternum. "My daughter died in 1994. Aneurysm, forty-one years old, healthy as a horse in the morning. I have been saying ndeva for thirty years. Every day it is still true. The word does not heal anything, Coral. Do not confuse it with medicine. What it does is—it makes the carrying *sayable*. And a thing that can be said can be shared. And a thing that can be shared is half as heavy, most days. Some days it is heavier, because now someone else knows. But most days. Half."

Coral sat with his hands around his teacup, which had gone cold an hour ago, and felt the words moving through him like water through a channel cut long ago and rarely used.

No one had ever taught him these in English either.

That was the thought that arrived, unbidden, unwelcome, precise as a gauge reading: forty-nine years old, and no one had ever given him a single word for this. His mother drowned when he was twelve, and the adults in his life had handed him casseroles and phrases about being strong and God's plan, and then, when those ran out, they had handed him silence, and he had been using it ever since. He was fluent in silence. Twenty years of it where Ellis was concerned. Thirty-seven where the dock was concerned. He knew the grammar of not-saying better than any living man knew Iyasele—and unlike Maren's nine hundred words, his language had no dictionary, no archive, no student lined up to receive it. If he died tomorrow it would die with him, and no one would even know a language had ended.

"Coral." Maren's voice, gentle. "Where did you go?"

"Nowhere," he said. Then, because the morning had done something to him, loosened something: "Away. Fenide."

Her eyebrows rose, pleased and sharp. "And which are you, truly? The leaver or the left?"

He looked out the window. The crow had finished and gone. The river was invisible from here but he could feel it anyway, the way you feel a tooth—not the thing itself, only its persistent presence.

"Both," he said. "Within one hour. Like your mother said."

Maren studied him for a long moment. He had the strange sensation of being inspected—thoroughly, professionally, the way he himself ran a sounding pole along a pier and read the depth by feel. Then she nodded once, as if confirming a measurement, and turned back to the notebook.

"There is one more," she said. "I save it always for last, because after it, the lesson is over and I want the lessons to be long." Her finger found the final line on the page. "*Ndovase.* It means: I am carrying this, and I am not carrying it alone. It is ndeva plus a person. You cannot say it truthfully by yourself. It requires—" she searched, "—a witness. Someone who hears you say it and agrees, silently, to help hold the weight. In the old days the whole village said it back to the mourner. *Ndovase. Ndovase.* Until the words wore a groove in the air."

"Say it to me," Coral said.

Maren blinked. For the first time since he'd known her, she looked genuinely surprised—a girl's surprise surfacing through the old woman's face like a fish rising.

"I beg your pardon?"

"You've been teaching me to say these things. Say one to me. Ndovase. Whatever you're carrying." He heard himself, and didn't recognize the voice, and let it keep going. "You said a thing that can be said can be shared. You've been listening to me for weeks. Let me—" He stopped. Started again, clumsier. "Let me listen back."

The kitchen hummed. The light had shifted; noon was passing through the window and taking its warmth with it. Maren Okonkwo looked at him for a long time, and then she put her hand flat on the table between them, palm up—an offering, or a request—and said, in a voice smaller than any he'd heard her use:

"Ndeva. My daughter. Ndeva, my language. Ndeva, every person I have ever had to fenidu." Her breath shuddered once. "Ndovase."

"Ndovase," Coral said back.

It was not much. Two old words in a bright cold kitchen, spoken by a dying woman and a hollowed-out man. But he felt the groove wear itself into the air, exactly as she'd promised, and understood that something had been built between them this morning—small, load-bearing, invisible, like the rebar inside concrete. You never see it. Everything rests on it anyway.

* * *

He drove home along the river road with the windows cracked, saying the words under his breath like a man counting money. *Fenidu. Fenidor. Sombwe. Kavari. Ndeva.* At a red light he caught himself mouthing *ndovase* into the rearview mirror and had to laugh at himself, alone in the truck, a fifty-year-old inspector practicing grief in a dead woman's language.

But the laughter thinned out fast, because underneath the vocabulary, all morning, something else had been rising in him the way groundwater rises in a test pit—slow, unstoppable, indifferent to what's built above it. Ellis. The phone call eleven days ago, the voice lower and rougher than he remembered, asking about the anniversary, asking to see the grave. Coral had said yes before he could think, and he had spent every day since not thinking about it, which was its own kind of fluency.

The anniversary was Saturday. Four days.

His mother had been dead thirty-seven years and he had never once stood at her grave and said a true sentence over it. He had said the false ones—*she's in a better place, it was quick, she wouldn't have wanted us to grieve*—the way you recite a form you didn't write. And now Maren had gone and given him the real words, *kavari, kavarise, ndeva*, and they sat in his chest like stones in a wall, holding something up, and he understood with terrible clarity that in four days he would stand on that cemetery grass with Ellis beside him, and one of them would have to speak, and neither of them had ever learned how.

Unless—

He pulled into his driveway and sat in the idling truck. Through the kitchen window he could see Dana moving behind the glass, and beyond her, on the counter, his notebook where he'd left it that morning, lying open, face-up, in the light.

He hadn't closed it. He couldn't remember whether the dock pages were showing.
