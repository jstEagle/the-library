## 14. Maren Forgets a Word

The kitchen smelled of ginger and rain. October had come down hard overnight, and when I arrived at Maren's house at nine, the gutters were running loud enough to sound like applause, like something being celebrated somewhere out of sight.

She had the kettle on before I'd hung up my coat. This was the ritual now: tea first, then the notebook, then the words. Three months of Tuesdays and Thursdays had worn a groove into the morning so deep that my body knew the way without consulting me—where the loose floorboard was, which chair wobbled, how she liked me to sit with my back to the window so the light fell on her face instead of mine. She said it was because she needed to see my mouth. I suspected it was because she liked to watch the river through the glass behind me while she thought, but I never said so. Some things you keep even from your teacher.

"Today," she said, setting down two cups, "we do the building words."

I opened the notebook to a fresh page. My handwriting in Iyasele had improved until it almost looked intentional, the diacritics sitting where they belonged like birds on a wire. *Ìlè. Ògàn. The words for wall, for post.* I had learned that her language built its vocabulary the way rivers build banks—not by decree but by constant, patient contact.

"You're smiling," she said.

"I'm not."

"You are. It's very small. Like a crack in a teacup." She lowered herself into her chair, both hands on the armrests now; I noticed she used them more than she used to. "But it's there."

* * *

We worked through the morning the way we always did, her giving the word, me repeating it, her correcting the music of it before the letters. In Iyasele, tone was not decoration. Say *bà* one way and you meant to perch; say it another and you meant to spoil. English, I was learning, let you be sloppy. It forgave you everything, which was another way of saying nothing you said mattered much.

Maren stood, once, to demonstrate a word for the beam that holds a roof's weight—she pressed her palms flat against the air above her head as if holding up the ceiling of her own kitchen—and I wrote fast, trying to catch the shape of her hands along with the shape of the sounds. That was the part no recording would keep, I understood already. The archive wanted audio files and metadata. They would get the voice. They would not get this woman pressing her palms against the sky.

It was nearly eleven when she asked me for the word for bridge.

She didn't ask, exactly. She reached for it the way you reach for a cup you've set down a thousand times in the same place, without looking, certain of its weight and position. Her mouth opened. Her hand made a small arc in front of her—the gesture that went with it, the span of it—and then nothing came out.

Her lips moved around an absence.

"Maren?" I said.

"One moment." She closed her eyes. Her hand completed the arc again, slower, as though the word might be hiding at the end of it. "It begins—" She stopped. Started again. "It is a long word. A beautiful one. It has the river in it twice."

I waited. The gutters roared outside. The kettle ticked as it cooled.

"It has the river in it twice," she said again, and I realized she wasn't repeating herself for emphasis. She was telling me everything she still had. The word's shadow. Its silhouette. The space it occupied in her mouth for eighty-one years.

"Take your time," I said, and hated how gentle it came out, how it sounded like what you say to someone in a hospital.

She opened her eyes and looked at me, and I saw something I had never seen in her face before. Not confusion. Confusion would have been easier. This was recognition—the precise, appalled recognition of a woman watching her own hand fail to close.

"Coral," she said. "Say the word for bridge."

"I don't know it. You haven't taught me yet."

"No." A small, terrible laugh. "No, I haven't. I was saving it."

* * *

She had been saving it. That was the thing I couldn't get past, that afternoon or any afternoon after. She had kept the word for bridge back the way you keep the last piece of cake, the best story, the final card in your hand—because it was hers, because it was the word that described the meeting of her mother's country and her father's, the crossing between the woman who sang to her in Iyasele and the world that demanded English. *The river in it twice.* She had carried it across an ocean and six decades and a widowhood and now, sitting three feet from me with her tea going cold, she had set it down somewhere and could not find it.

We searched together. That's what I want to record, if these pages ever mean anything to anyone: we searched together, and it was not sad the whole time. She had me go back through the notebook, reading every word aloud while she listened with her eyes shut, hunting for it by ear, the way you find a lost key by walking the same route home. I read for forty minutes. Walls and posts and beams and doors. Rain words, water words, the farewell vocabulary we'd done the week before. Once, halfway through, she raised a finger—*there*—and then shook her head before I'd finished the syllable. *No. That's ferry. That's the word for ferry.*

"How do you know it's not the bridge word?" I asked.

"Because the ferry word is short and impatient. The bridge word takes its time." She smiled, and the smile frightened me more than the forgetting had. "You'll know it when you hear it. It sounds like something that refuses to fall."

At noon she waved off my offer to make lunch and sat instead with her hands folded, doing something I recognized from my own work. On the Kestrel Street Bridge there is a section of railing, north side, third panel, where the welds have begun to whisper. Nothing you'd see. Nothing most inspectors would flag. But put your palm against it on a windy day and you can feel it deciding. I have spent thirty years listening to structures decide whether to hold.

That was what Maren was doing. Sitting very still, listening to herself decide.

"I have it," she said suddenly, and my heart lifted before she finished the sentence. "I have where I keep it. In my mother's kitchen. There was a window over the sink, and past the window, the footbridge to the market road. Every morning she washed rice and said the word. Every morning, sixty years ago." She looked at me with fierce, wet eyes. "Coral, I can see the rice. I can see her hands. I cannot hear her voice."

* * *

I did what I could, which was nothing, which was everything. I made her eat. I refilled her tea. I told her about the welds on the Kestrel Street railing, how a structure can lose a bolt here, a plate there, and carry its traffic all the same, and how the inspector's job is not to stop the losing but to notice it honestly, to write down the truth of the wearing while the wearing is still only wearing.

"You're saying I should write it down," she said.

"I'm saying the word might come back. And I'm saying if it doesn't, we still have eight hundred others."

"Eight hundred and sixty-two," she said automatically, and then her face changed, because we both heard it. Eight hundred and sixty-two minus one, minus whatever else had gone missing quietly in the night, unmissed because no one had asked. A language doesn't die all at once. It dies the way a bridge fails—slowly, invisibly, hairline by hairline, until one day the load arrives and finds nothing left to stand on.

She pushed her cup away. "Get the recorder."

"We don't have to work today."

"Get the recorder, Coral." Her voice had iron back in it, and I loved her for it and obeyed. "If my mouth is going to start closing doors, then we go through every open one before it does. Today we take the house words. All of them. Kitchen, threshold, roof, hearth. The words for the inside of a life." She was already pulling the notebook toward her, her pen moving, listing. "And tomorrow the body words. And Thursday—" She stopped writing. Looked up. "Thursday we take the bridge word apart. If I can't say it whole, I will give you its pieces. The river part. The standing part. The part that means *across*. A child could rebuild it from those. Perhaps a child in Lagos will have to."

I hadn't told her yet about the letters. About Adaeze. That discovery was still folded in my jacket pocket like contraband, and I had been waiting for the right morning to unfold it, some morning when she seemed strong, when the lessons were going well, when I could present the niece in Lagos not as evidence of ending but as evidence of continuation.

There was never going to be such a morning. I saw that now. There was only this one, with the rain applauding on the gutters and an old woman listing the rooms of her memory before the lights went out in them.

"Maren," I said. "There's something I found in your papers. Last week, when you sent me for the photograph album. Letters from a woman named Chinyere. To your mother. And one newer letter, addressed to you, unopened."

Her pen stopped.

"She mentions a daughter. Adaeze. Born in Lagos. Who spent her childhood summers in your mother's village." I took the breath I'd been taught by three months of lessons, the deep one, the one that comes before the hard words. "Adaeze may remember songs. Fragments. Children keep what adults lose—it's the one advantage of having a smaller house."

Maren looked at me for a long time. Outside, the rain eased, and in the new quiet I could hear the clock in her hallway, which I had never heard before, ticking with the patience of something that knows exactly what it's doing.

"My mother's sister's granddaughter," she said slowly, working the arithmetic of blood. "She would be—" Another pause, longer. I watched her reach for the number and find it, relief crossing her face like sun through cloud. "Fifty-one. She would be fifty-one years old." Then, quieter, almost to herself: "I held her at a naming ceremony. 1974. She cried the entire time, and my mother said, *listen, even now she argues in the old tongue.*"

She laughed—a real laugh, cracked down the middle—and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand.

"Bring me the letter," she said. "Tonight. Bring me the letter and we will write to Lagos tomorrow. And Coral—" She caught my sleeve as I stood, and her grip was stronger than I expected, urgent as a current. "Whatever happens with the recordings, whatever the university wants, whatever I forget—you find that girl. You tell her the bridge word has the river in it twice. You tell her it sounds like something that refuses to fall. Even if I never say it again, she must know there was a word. She must know it existed."

I promised. I stood in her kitchen with rain drying on the windows and promised an old woman I would deliver a single sentence across an ocean, and understood, driving home through the wet streets with the recorder warm on the seat beside me, that I had just been given the only kind of assignment that matters—the kind you accept knowing you may never see it finished.

That night I wrote to a stranger in Lagos. And the next morning, when I pulled into the inspection lot on Kestrel Street and put my palm against the north railing, third panel, I felt the welds whispering under the wind, and for the first time in thirty years I did not just listen.

I took out my pen and wrote down the truth of the wearing—all of it, every millimeter—and signed my full name at the bottom.
