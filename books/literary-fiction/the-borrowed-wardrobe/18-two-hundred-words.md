## 18. Two Hundred Words

The kitchen smelled of her for nine days after the funeral home came and took the chair away.

That was the wrong way to think of it, Coral knew. They hadn't taken the chair. They had taken Maren, and the chair remained, pushed back from the table at the angle she'd left it, as if she'd only stepped out to see about the weather. Her nephew in Toronto—there had been a nephew at the service, a tall, kind-faced man who shook Coral's hand twice and said, *So you were her student. She told us about you*—had given him a key and a week and a half to go through the papers before the house went to the estate lawyers. *Take what's yours,* the nephew had said. *She'd have wanted the language to go with you.*

Coral had been sitting in that kitchen every evening after work, and most of the morning too, going through her papers the way he went through bridge decks: systematically, respectfully, alert to hairline fractures.

There were more papers than he'd expected. Maren Okonkwo had been a woman who kept things. Bank statements going back thirty years, bundled with rubber bands gone brittle. Recipes in three languages, two of them dead. Photographs in shoeboxes—a girl in a Lagos schoolyard, a young woman in a nurse's uniform standing beside a hospital sign in a country Coral couldn't name, a wedding he knew nothing about, a face he'd never heard her mention. He photographed everything with his phone, page by page, the way he'd photographed the crack in the Kestrel Street pier cap, because documentation was the only form of love he'd ever been trained in.

And there were the letters.

They lived in a biscuit tin at the back of the pantry, behind a tin that still held biscuits gone to dust. Coral found them on the ninth evening, when the light through the window had gone the color of weak tea and he was ready to quit. He'd been looking for vocabulary—praying, honestly, for a second notebook, some lost list that might hold the two hundred words that had died unrecorded. Instead he found correspondence.

Most of it was old. Airmail envelopes with stamps from a Nigeria that had a different name when the stamps were printed. Letters in English, in a careful, school-taught hand, addressed to *Auntie Maren* from someone signing herself *Ngozi*. The dates ran backward through the decades like water through a cracked culvert. Coral read them in order, oldest first, sitting very still in Maren's chair.

Ngozi had been her sister's daughter. The sister was long gone—there was a letter from 1987 that Coral had to read three times before he understood it was a condolence letter, and that the sister's name had been Adaeze the elder, and that grief had a way of repeating names in families the way rivers repeated their own bends. Ngozi had grown up, married, moved, written less. The letters thinned through the nineties, stopped in 2003, resumed in 2011 with an apology and a photograph of a daughter.

*This is Adaeze,* the letter said, *named for my mother, as you asked. She is seven. She sings the song you taught me, the one about the river asking the rain where it has been. She does not know what the words mean. I barely do anymore. But she sings it.*

Coral set the letter down on the table and pressed both palms flat against the wood, because his hands were shaking and he needed them still.

A child, singing words she did not understand.

He thought: *fragments*. He thought: *childhood language is a sediment; it settles in you below the waterline and stays long after the current of fluency has moved on*. He had no training for this thought. He was a bridge inspector. But he had spent thirty years listening to structures tell him what they used to be, reading the ghost loads in a span, and he knew—professionally, bone-deep—that nothing built into a thing ever fully leaves it. You could find the original engineer's intentions in the rust patterns. You could find a childhood in a song.

The last letter in the tin was the newest. Postmarked four years ago. An address in Surulere, Lagos. A phone number written in the margin in Maren's own hand, in ballpoint, in the careful block letters of someone who knew she might need it fast.

*Adaeze has a son now,* Ngozi had written. *When he was born I sang the river song to him and I cried and I did not know why. Do you remember, Auntie? Do you remember the word for the sound water makes under a bridge? I have lost it. I have lost almost all of it. Tell me you have kept it.*

Coral sat in the kitchen until the streetlight came on outside and the room went blue.

* * *

He did not sleep that night. He lay beside Dana, who was asleep or pretending, and stared at the ceiling and did the arithmetic he'd been refusing to do for nine days.

Two hundred words. That was what remained unrecorded when Maren's heart had stopped between the word for *harvest* and the word for *forgive*—he had checked the notebook a dozen times, as if the gap might close on its own, as if language were a joint that might settle back into tolerance. Two hundred words out of nine hundred. A language that had taken however many thousands of years to build, that had survived slavery and migration and a woman's whole long solitary life in a country where no one could answer her, and it had come down to two hundred words and a dead woman's biscuit tin.

And now, maybe, a girl in Lagos who sang a song she didn't understand.

He got up at three in the morning and sat at the kitchen table with the notebook and Maren's grammar pages and the letters spread around him like a spread of drawings for a repair job. This was the part he understood. This was the part that felt like his own trade: you assessed what remained, you determined the load it could carry, you designed the intervention. The archive at the university—Professor Anselm, who had returned his emails with increasing warmth—would take the recordings they'd already made, forty-one hours of a voice that no longer existed anywhere else in the world. Anselm had written, after the news of Maren's death, a message Coral had read five times: *What you two made together matters more than what remains undone. But if there is any possibility of recovery—any at all—pursue it. Languages are rarely resurrected. They are sometimes reassembled.*

*Reassembled.* Coral knew reassembly. He'd watched crews splice a fractured girder back into service, watched them grind the welds and test them and test them again. The beam was never what it had been. It carried load anyway. It carried load *because* someone refused to write it off.

He could go to Lagos. The thought arrived whole, already load-tested. He had vacation time banked like a miser's coins—eleven days, twelve, he'd never once taken a full week in nine years. He had savings. He had an address, a phone number, a song.

He picked up the phone to call the airline and then set it down again, because his hands were shaking again, and this time he understood why, and it had nothing to do with Maren.

* * *

It came to him the way failures come to him on a bridge: not all at once, but as a sound, a small wrong note under his feet that he'd been walking past for days.

He could not do this.

Not the Lagos part—the Lagos part he could do; the Lagos part was inspection and documentation and travel logistics, and God knew he was competent at logistics. What he could not do was carry two hundred words across an ocean and hand them to a stranger while his own language lay in ruins around him. He could not stand in a living room in Surulere and ask a woman to give him the words her grandmother had lost while he himself had never once, in forty years, said the sentence *Ellis was there the night our mother died* out loud to anyone. He could not ask Adaeze to exhume her childhood while he sat on his own.

The thought arrived with the force of the obvious, which is how the worst truths always arrive.

He was a man who had spent his whole life carrying other people's loads. He'd carried Ellis's silence for thirty years, carried it so faithfully that the family had mistaken it for loyalty. He'd carried his mother's death like a deck load with no tie-downs, everything shifting every time the wind came up. He carried Dana's loneliness without naming it, carried his own without feeling it, carried Maren's language now like a man hauling stones across a river because someone had to and because carrying, at least, meant you didn't have to stand still and speak.

And a load carried is not a load owned. He had learned that word from her—*owner*, *tenant*—not in Iyasele but in her English, in one of her sidelong sermons. *You live in your life like a tenant, Coral. You pay your rent on time. You keep the place clean. But you do not change anything, because it is not yours.* She'd said it in October, over the tea she made too strong, and he'd laughed it off, and she'd looked at him with those patient, terrible eyes and said nothing, because she was a woman who knew that some words need time to settle like sediment.

He was a tenant in his own grief. He had never once opened the door of it.

The notebook lay open in front of him. He looked at the last page Maren had written on—the word for *harvest*, in her looping hand, and beneath it the English, and beneath that, in smaller letters, a note she'd left for him, one of her marginal sermons: *A word is a bridge. It only works if someone is standing on both ends.*

Both ends. He had been standing on his end of every bridge in his life, waiting for someone else to cross. Ellis at one end, silent for thirty years. Dana across the dinner table, talking past him into the middle distance. His mother at the far end of the longest span, unreachable, unmourned properly, because to mourn her properly he would have had to say what he saw, and saying it might have broken the family, and so he had chosen the family over the truth and called it love, and the family had broken anyway, slowly, the way things break when you don't maintain them.

He could not rebuild Iyasele's missing two hundred words while his own confession sat unspoken. He knew it the way he knew a span was overstressed—not from a calculation but from the sound, the small wrong note, the vibration in his chest every time he picked up the phone to book the flight. The words would not carry. He would get to Lagos and stand in front of Adaeze and ask her to trust him with her inheritance, and somewhere in the transaction she would feel it, the way Maren had felt it in the first week—*you listen like someone carrying a secret*—and the whole structure would come down under a load it was never rated for.

First the confession. Then the crossing.

* * *

He wrote the list at five in the morning, on the back of an inspection form, because that was the paper in front of him and because there was something right about it—his two lives meeting on one sheet.

1. Call Anselm. Tell him about the niece, the letters, the song. Get his advice on field recording. He will say yes. He will probably want to come; do not let him.
2. Book the flight. Not yet. Not first.
3. Call Ellis.

He sat and looked at that third line for a long time. The streetlight outside had gone off; the window was turning gray at the edges, the particular gray of a morning that intended to be honest.

He had not called Ellis since the cemetery. Eleven days. They had stood at the grave and circled the wound like two men pacing the same crack from opposite faces of the wall, and Ellis had left first, and Coral had let him, because letting people leave first was the one skill he'd ever mastered. But Ellis had answered when Coral called about the anniversary—had answered on the second ring, as if he'd been sitting by the phone for thirty years, which maybe he had. And at the grave, in the last minute before he walked away, Ellis had said, *I'm not going anywhere this time. I'm done going.* And Coral had said, *Okay,* and both of them had known *okay* was a lie of omission so large it had its own weather.

He thought about Maren's last lesson. He thought about the word she'd lost—*bridge*—and how she'd sat there with her hands open in her lap and said, *It is gone. It will come back or it will not, and either way the river does not care. The river only cares that you keep talking to it.* And then, because she was Maren, she had added, softly, *Coral. Whatever you are carrying. It is heavier than any word I have ever lost. I can hear it in how you say the water words. You say them like a man who has been under a long time.*

He picked up his phone. He did not call Ellis. Not yet—his hands were steady but his voice wasn't, and this thing deserved a steady voice. Instead he opened the notebook to the farewell vocabulary, the lesson they'd finished two weeks before she died, and he read the words aloud in the empty kitchen, quietly, testing his own voice the way you test a weld: *the word for leaving*, *the word for I will carry this*, *the word for what is owed and the word for what is forgiven*, and the long one, the beautiful one, the one that took him a week to pronounce—*the word that means: I am telling you the truth so that we can both put it down*.

His voice cracked on the long one. He said it again. Cracked again. Said it a third time, and on the third time it held, the way a weld holds on the third pass, and he sat there in the blue-gray kitchen of a dead woman who had taught him everything except how to be done being afraid, and he understood that this was the work now. Not the archive. Not the flight. Not the two hundred words.

The call.

He turned the phone over in his hands. Ellis's number was still in it from October, saved under *Ellis*, no surname, as if the family name were a weight the entry couldn't bear. He looked at it the way he'd looked at the fracture in the Kestrel Street pier cap: measuring, dreading, knowing that the measurement was not the repair.

Outside, the first real light came up over the rooftops, and the river he couldn't see from here but knew was running anyway went on asking its old question in a language older than any of them.

Coral Thackeray pressed the number, and while it rang—once, twice, three times, Ellis never had been quick to answer anything—he said the water words to himself under his breath, like a man going under, like a man coming up.

On the fourth ring, his brother's voice said, *Coral?*

And Coral opened his mouth, and began.
