## 21. The Niece in Lagos

The plane touched down at Murtala Muhammed International at dusk, and the first thing Coral noticed was the sound—Lagos arrived as noise before it arrived as anything else. Horns layered over horns, a generator's drone somewhere behind the terminal wall, voices doing what voices did here, which was everything at once. He had spent thirty years listening for hairline cracks in steel and concrete, and this city sounded like a bridge under full load: stressed, alive, holding.

He had Maren's letters in his carry-on, wrapped in a plastic bag against the humidity, along with the notebook—the one Dana had found in his truck two years ago, the one that had started the loudest fight of their marriage and, somehow, ended its longest silence. The notebook had traveled with him through the archive sessions, through the funeral, through the winter of recording Maren's voice onto a university server until her words ran out at roughly seven hundred and eleven. Two hundred missing. Two hundred words that had lived only in one woman's mouth and were now living only in his memory of her mouth.

And possibly, if the letters told the truth, in Lagos.

* * *

Adaeze Okonkwo met him at a café on Awolowo Road three days after he landed, because the first two days belonged to jet lag and to the particular paralysis of a man who had crossed an ocean on the strength of forty-year-old correspondence between two women who were both now dead. She arrived ten minutes late, apologizing in English, then Yoruba, then something else—a third language that moved differently in her mouth, rounder, more musical—and when she saw him stand up too quickly and knock his chair backward, she laughed, and the laugh was so exactly Maren's laugh that Coral had to grip the table.

"You must be the inspector," she said. "Aunty Maren wrote about you. In the last letter." Her English carried the soft architecture of British boarding school over something older underneath. "She said you listened like the words might be worth money."

"She said I listened like someone carrying a secret," Coral said. "That was her reason."

Adaeze sat down across from him. She was perhaps fifty, younger than he'd imagined from the letters, with Maren's high cheekbones and none of Maren's caution. "And did you? Carry one?"

"Yes."

"Is it heavy still?"

"It's lighter than it was." He thought of Ellis's voice on the phone, breaking open like a joint under load; he thought of Dana kneeling across from him at the kitchen table, both of them down there together, which hadn't been the plan but had been the truth. "I put most of it down."

"Good." She said it simply, without curiosity, as if other people's secrets were cargo she declined to help carry. Then she reached into her bag and brought out a battered exercise book, its cover soft with handling, its spine held together with masking tape gone amber. "I brought what I have. You should understand—it isn't much. Songs my mother sang. Things Aunty Maren said when she visited, before—" she made a small gesture, taking the whole Atlantic into account, "—before. I was six the last time. Seven. I remember sounds, not meanings. Half of what I know, I probably say wrong."

Coral looked at the exercise book and felt his chest do something complicated. "Maren spent her last year terrified of saying things wrong. She'd stop mid-word sometimes, hold up a hand, go back and check herself against notes she'd written fifty years ago. She called it guarding the door."

"And you think I'm not guarding it."

"I think," Coral said slowly, "that she guarded the door so long that when she died, the language died behind it. Two hundred words went with her. Maybe they're in your book. Maybe they're wrong. But wrong and alive is better than right and buried."

Adaeze studied him for a long moment, and then she opened the exercise book to the first page, and began to sing.

* * *

They worked in her sitting room in Surulere for nine days, because the café had been impossible—too much noise, too many eyes—and because Adaeze's house had a ceiling fan that clicked on every rotation like a patient metronome, and Coral came to need that click the way he needed the tick of his own kitchen clock.

Her method was nothing like Maren's. Maren had taught the way she inspected everything, carefully, word by word, demanding repetition until the syllables stopped feeling ridiculous in his mouth. Adaeze taught by flood. She sang a song about the river spirit who carries children who wander too close to the water, and when Coral asked what a particular phrase meant, she shrugged and sang it again, louder, until he sang it back, and whatever it meant, it meant itself first and translated later.

"That one I'm sure of," she said, tapping the page. "*Oshumiri*—the river that remembers. Aunty Maren used it when she talked about her mother. Everything else—" she flipped pages, "—everything else we find out together."

Together turned out to mean: Coral with his notebook of seven hundred and eleven recorded words, cross-referencing; Adaeze with her childhood fragments; both of them with a laptop playing back Maren's voice from the archive recordings, the old woman saying *welcome, student* in a language that now existed in fewer human throats than could fit in this room.

On the fourth day they found the first resurrection.

It happened over a song Adaeze half-remembered, a counting song, numbers folded into a melody about a market woman. Coral recognized the cadence before he recognized the content—Maren had taught him numbers early, calling them "the bones of a language, everything hangs off them"—and when Adaeze sang the fifth verse, a number surfaced that wasn't in the notebook, wasn't in the archive, wasn't anywhere except inside a dead woman's head for the past however many years.

"*Saanu*," Adaeze sang, and Coral's pen stopped moving.

"Say it again."

"*Saanu.* It's eight. Or it's what I always took for eight. My mother counted us into the car with it—*saanu*, *saanu*, stop pushing your brother."

Coral played back the archive recordings, scanning. Numbers one through seven, nine, ten—all there, all recorded in Maren's careful voice in the third session, when she'd been strong enough to sit upright for two hours. Eight was a gap. He remembered the gap. He remembered Maren pausing at it, frowning, saying *it will come back to me, don't write around it, never write around a hole.*

Eight had come back. It had come back four thousand miles away, in the mouth of a woman who'd learned it from a song her mother learned from Maren's sister, imperfectly, maybe wrongly, sung to squabbling children in a car.

"*Saanu*," Coral said aloud, testing it, and the word sat in his mouth like a stone warmed by sun. "Eight."

"You're crying," Adaeze observed, not unkindly.

"I'm inspecting," Coral said, and wiped his face with the heel of his hand. "It's what I do. I just usually wait until nobody's watching."

* * *

Not everything came back whole. That was the lesson of the second week, and it was a harder lesson than the first.

There was a word—for it, or near it—that appeared in three of Adaeze's songs and in none of Maren's recordings: a word that seemed to describe the state of being far from home and unable to say so. Adaeze rendered it one way; the melody suggested another stress pattern entirely; and when Coral reconstructed it from context and sang his version back to her, she cocked her head and said, "Maybe. That might be it. Or that might be a word my mother misheard forty years ago and passed down like a bad recipe."

"So we don't know."

"We don't know." She poured palm wine from a bottle into two glasses, an evening ritual by then. "In my church we have a saying—the drum doesn't apologize for its own rhythm. Aunty Maren wanted the language pure. Untouched. Perfectly preserved, like a body in a glass case." She handed him his glass. "But languages aren't bodies. They're rivers. You cannot step in the same one twice, and you cannot dam one without killing it. Whatever we rebuild here will be different from what she spoke. Some of it will be wrong. And in twenty years, if a child sings our wrong version, that wrong version becomes the true version. That's how every language ever spoken got here. Through error, through forgetting, through mouths that couldn't quite remember."

Coral turned his glass in his hand. "She would have hated that."

"She would have hated it and then, eventually, she would have understood it. She left Nigeria at nineteen. She knew better than anyone that a language survives by leaving home." Adaeze drank. "You loved her."

It wasn't a question, but Coral answered it anyway. "I didn't expect to. I went back to that bridge because she was there every morning mouthing words at the river, and I thought she was confused, and correcting people is the one thing I know how to do." He smiled. "She corrected me instead. Told me she was practicing. Practicing. Like the river was going to test her."

"The river does test you," Adaeze said seriously. "Everyone here knows that."

* * *

On the eleventh day, they found the word for bridge.

Or—they found *a* word for bridge. This was the word Maren had lost in front of him, in her kitchen, in the final autumn: the word she'd known all her life and reached for and found only air, the loss that had shown Coral what death actually looked like when it worked word by word instead of all at once. He had written in the notebook that night, his handwriting shaking: *she lost the word for bridge. the thing that connects. she lost the thing that connects.*

Adaeze found it in the middle of a lullaby, of all places, a song about carrying a child across water on your back.

"*Okpuru*," she sang, and pointed at the line, and explained: the crossing-place, the carried-over, the thing that bears weight between two shores.

Coral wrote it down and stared at it. "That's not the word she forgot."

"No?"

"No. She forgot a different one—I can hear her almost saying it, the shape of it in her mouth. Two syllables, starting further back in the throat." He demonstrated the ghost of it, the empty shape. "This is another word. Another word for the same thing."

Adaeze leaned forward, suddenly fierce. "Then that's better. Don't you see? If there are two words for bridge, then the language is richer than we thought, not poorer. She didn't lose the concept. She lost one door to it. Here's another door." She tapped the notebook. "Write them both. Write hers as a blank with a question mark, and write mine beside it. Let whoever comes after decide."

"Whoever comes after," Coral repeated. "You make it sound certain there'll be someone."

"Aren't you?" Adaeze looked at him with Maren's directness. "Why else did you fly across an ocean with a dead woman's notebook?"

* * *

They finished on a Sunday, or finished as much as finishing was ever going to happen. One hundred and sixty-three words recovered, some certainly wrong, some certainly right, all of them now written in Coral's careful inspector's hand and recorded on his phone in two voices—one Nigerian, one American, neither of them Maren's, both of them carrying her forward anyway. Forty-odd words remained unrecovered, ghosts with shapes but no substance, and Coral had learned enough to leave them as honest blanks rather than fill them with invention. Maren had taught him that much: *never write around a hole.*

On the last evening, Adaeze cooked—jollof rice and fish and a bitterleaf soup whose name, she informed him, was also an Iyasele word, one of the ones that had survived—and afterward they sat on her small balcony while Lagos performed its nightly transformation from roar to murmur, generators ticking off one by one, the city exhaling.

"My mother used to say Aunty Maren abandoned us," Adaeze said. "Left for America, married an American, let the language get thin in her mouth. There was anger, for years. Letters that stopped coming." She paused. "Then, near the end, the letters changed. Did she tell you why she picked you? On the bridge?"

"She said I listened like a man carrying a secret."

"Mm. She wrote something else too. In the last letter—the one that came after she died, forwarded twice." Adaeze got up, went inside, and returned with a single aerogram, its edges soft as cloth. She didn't hand it over; she read from it, her voice careful. "'I have found a student. An inspector of bridges. He thinks he is learning my language, but I am teaching him his own. When he has it, I will die, and it will be all right, because he will know what to do with what I gave him. He will not keep it in a case. He will spend it.'"

Coral sat very still. Below them, a radio somewhere played highlife, and a woman laughed, and a motorcycle buzzed past like a large insect.

"I didn't teach him fast enough," Adaeze continued, softer. "Two hundred words short. She wrote that too: 'If I run out of time, he will find Adaeze. She sings. Between the two of them, the language will breathe.' She planned this. She planned you."

"I know," Coral said. "I've known since the notebook. What I didn't know—" he stopped, started again. "What I didn't know was whether spending it would feel like enough. Whether one man in Ohio teaching a community class to twelve people, whether that counts as keeping a language alive or just slowing down a funeral."

Adaeze considered this with the seriousness she gave everything. "My grandmother had a proverb. Iyasele, probably—half of what she said was. 'A fire does not ask how many hands warm themselves.'" She refilled his glass. "Twelve people. Then twenty-four. Then their children hear the songs and don't even know they're learning. That's how it works. That's how it always worked, before archives, before purity, before anyone thought a language needed saving instead of just speaking."

* * *

He flew home on a Thursday. At the airport, Adaeze pressed the exercise book into his hands—the original, tape-spined, soft-cornered, saturated with forty years of her family's handling—and refused to take no.

"It belongs with the rest," she said. "With her voice. With your notebook. A language shouldn't live in one place. That's how they die—of loneliness." Then she hugged him, hard, the way family hugs, and said into his shoulder, in Iyasele, a sentence he understood completely and could not have translated for money: *carry this across the water.*

On the plane, somewhere over the Sahara, he took out his phone and scrolled through the recordings: Maren's voice, Adaeze's voice, his own clumsy accent threading between them. Seven hundred and eleven plus one hundred and sixty-three. Eight hundred and seventy-four. Not nine hundred. Never quite nine hundred. But alive, and growing, and no longer alone.

He opened a new note and typed the date, and beneath it: *Community class starts Tuesday. Dana's coming. She says she wants to learn the word for bridge.*

He smiled at that—at Dana, who had read his secrets in a truck and chosen understanding over ammunition; at himself, who had once measured millimeters of movement on the Kestrel Street Bridge and called that a life. Then he added one more line, the way Maren had taught him, never writing around a hole:

*Tell Ellis. Ask him to come to class. There are words for brothers. We never learned them yet—but there's time.*

The plane dropped through cloud, and Lagos fell away beneath him, and Coral Thackeray rode the crossing-home with a dead woman's book on his knees and her language breathing in his pocket, already rehearsing, already practicing, the way she had practiced every morning at the rail—to the river, to the air, to anyone who would listen.
