## 5. Night Shift Confessions

Beatrix had learned the building's rhythms the way she'd once learned a score: by repetition, by failure, by the slow accumulation of muscle memory. The radiators in the east wing knocked twice before settling. The elevator on the practice-hall side groaned at the third floor and only the third floor. And at 6:40 every morning, forty minutes before her shift ended, Marisol Vega arrived with a thermos of coffee so strong it could have stripped varnish.

They met at the loading dock, where the night's trash waited in its bins like evidence. It was a ritual neither of them had named. Beatrix would be wringing out a mop or logging a broken light fixture, and Marisol would appear in the doorway with steam curling off her cup, and one of them — usually Marisol — would say something about the weather, or the pigeons, or the state of the men's room on the second floor, and the day would hand itself over to the night, or the night to the day, without either woman having to say anything true.

Beatrix preferred it that way. Truth was a door she kept locked, and she had twenty years of practice walking past it without looking.

But this morning, Marisol didn't reach for the small talk. She leaned against the doorframe, thermos cradled in both hands, and studied Beatrix with an expression Beatrix couldn't file anywhere.

"You played here," Marisol said. "Before."

The mop handle was suddenly very interesting. "I mopped here," Beatrix said. "For three weeks now."

"Come on." Marisol said it gently, the way you'd correct a child who'd lied about something harmless. "I've been here eleven years. I know what the old-timers look like when they walk through the recital hall. You don't look at the floor like the rest of us. You look at the stage."

Beatrix said nothing. Outside, the first delivery truck of the morning hissed against the curb. Somewhere above them, a radiator knocked twice and settled.

"1998," Marisol went on, quieter now. "The Whitmore Competition. I wasn't working here yet — I was still cleaning offices downtown — but my cousin was in the second violin section that year. She talked about it for years. The girl who was going to win everything." A pause. "She said your name last Christmas, actually. We were watching some competition broadcast, and she said, *you know who I always wondered about?*"

"Marisol—"

"I'm not asking for the story." Marisol raised a hand, palm out, a gesture of truce. "I'm telling you I remember it different. That's all. That's the whole thing I came to say."

"Different from what?"

"From the papers." Marisol unscrewed the thermos cap, poured a measure into the cup, and held it out. Beatrix took it without deciding to. The coffee was terrible and hot and it steadied her hands, which had begun, faintly, to tremble. "The papers said you collapsed. Nerves. Pressure. A breakdown, they called it, like a machine part failing. But my cousin said the whole orchestra knew something else was going on that season. Something wrong in the building. People whispering. Doors locked that used to be open." She shrugged, a small economical motion. "And then it all stopped, and everyone agreed to stop talking, which is not the same thing as nothing happening."

Beatrix drank the coffee. It scalded going down and she was grateful for the pain; it gave her somewhere to put her face.

"The family arranged things," she said finally. It wasn't the story. It was barely even a sentence. But it was more than she'd said aloud to anyone in twenty years, and it surprised her coming out, the way a note surprises you when your fingers find it before your mind has decided on it.

Marisol nodded slowly, unsurprised, and Beatrix understood that she had just confirmed something the other woman had suspected for weeks.

"My father," Beatrix said, because apparently the sentence wanted to continue, "is Edmund Pemberton."

Marisol's face did something complicated — recognition, then recalculation, then a careful blankness that Beatrix recognized as its own kind of professional skill. Janitors learned to be blank. It was how you survived hearing things in empty buildings.

"The foundation," Marisol said. "That's putting up the money for—" She stopped herself. Looked down at her cup. "For the demolition."

"For the tower," Beatrix said. "Yes."

The silence between them changed texture. It was no longer the polite silence of strangers sharing a doorway. It was the silence of two people standing on the same fault line, each having pretended not to feel the ground move.

"There's a room," Marisol said at last, very quietly, though there was no one within fifty feet of them but pigeons. "Third sub-basement. Under the old instrument storage. It's been locked since before my time — since the nineties, the old-timers say. Facilities doesn't have a key. Security doesn't have a key. When they redid the HVAC plans two years ago, the architect asked what was down there, and the answer from the administration was *storage, nothing important.*" She looked up. "Nobody keeps a room locked for thirty years if there's nothing important in it."

"What do the staff say is in it?"

Marisol smiled, and it was a tired smile, the smile of a woman who had spent eleven years being invisible to people who thought invisibility meant deafness. "The staff say it's where the conservatory keeps the things it's ashamed of. Old instruments. Old records. Old names." She rinsed her cup under the utility sink, unhurried. "My cousin said something else too, that Christmas. She said the year you left, half the music library got re-catalogued overnight. Whole drawers moved. She thought it was strange — libraries don't work that way, things don't just get renumbered — but she was nineteen and it wasn't her business." Marisol capped the thermos. "It's not mine either. But I figured you should know the room exists. In case it's ever your business."

She left the way she always did, at 7:00 sharp, her cart squeaking down the corridor toward the daylight wing. Beatrix stood alone at the loading dock with the empty cup warming in her hands and the word *re-catalogued* turning over and over in her chest like a stone in a tumbler.

* * *

Marisol Vega had a system, and the system was simple: see everything, say almost nothing, and never let them see you counting.

Eleven years in this building had taught her that janitors were the conservatory's true historians. The deans came and went. The star students flared and vanished. But the floors remembered. The floors knew which practice rooms the prodigies favored, which professors wept in their offices after tenure meetings, which corners of the recital hall collected the most tears on audition days. Marisol had cleaned up after a generation of ambition, and she had learned to read the residue.

So she had known about the Pemberton girl within a week of the new hire's arrival.

It wasn't the name — the name on the employment form was B. Pemberton, and plenty of people were named Pemberton in a city where the family had endowed three buildings. It was the hands. The new night janitor handled the mop the way musicians handled things: with economy, with wrists, with a kind of listening attention, as if the mop might tell her something if she held it right. And it was the way she stood in the recital hall at 3 a.m., sometimes, when she thought no one saw — Marisol saw, Marisol always saw — facing the stage with her head tilted, the posture of someone following an inner sound.

And it was the smile. Marisol knew that smile. Her mother had worn it for years after her father left — a smile built to end conversations, a door painted on a wall. Nobody smiled like that unless they were protecting something with their whole body.

Tonight, Thursday, 11:20 p.m., Marisol stayed late. Officially she was deep-cleaning the second-floor restrooms ahead of Saturday's admissions auditions. Unofficially she was waiting for the building to empty and for the night shift to make its rounds, because she had spent three days thinking about the loading dock, and about the sentence the woman had said — *the family arranged things* — and about what a person does with information that isn't hers but feels like it should be.

Her mother had a saying: *lo que se calla, se pudre.* What stays silent, rots. Marisol had spent eleven years letting things stay silent. She was good at it. She was tired of it.

She found Beatrix in the west corridor, buffing the terrazzo outside the chamber hall, the machine humming under her hands like a held chord.

"Walk with me," Marisol said. "Ten minutes. There's something you should see, and I'd rather show you than describe it."

Beatrix shut off the buffer. For a moment her face did the smile thing, automatic, defensive — and then, visibly, she caught it, and let it go, and underneath it she just looked tired and young and frightened. "Show me what?"

"Come on."

Marisol took her down the service stairs to the first sub-basement, past the boiler room with its cathedral of pipes, past the dead vending machine that had sold candy to students in the eighties and now served as a shelf for lost gloves. At the end of the corridor, behind a fire door marked STORAGE — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL ONLY, a narrow stair descended another two flights, to a level that appeared on no public floor plan. Marisol knew it appeared on no plan because she had once, out of boredom, read the entire building code file in the facilities office, cover to cover, during a January with no snow days.

The second sub-basement was instrument storage: rows of covered pianos, timpani shells, a forest of music stands wrapped in plastic like saplings waiting for spring. Marisol walked to the far wall, where a single door sat flush with the concrete — steel, gray, unmarked except for a keypad that had long since gone dark, and below it, an old-fashioned keyhole with a plate that read ARCHIVE.

"This is as far as anyone goes," Marisol said. "This is as far as I've ever gone. But I want you to look at the door."

Beatrix stepped closer. She didn't touch it. She looked the way she looked at the stage in the recital hall — reading something invisible.

"It's newer than the wall," Beatrix said.

Marisol felt something loosen in her chest — relief, maybe, or respect. "Say that again."

"The hinges are from the nineties. The wall is thirties, forties. Someone cut this door in." Beatrix crouched, examining the threshold. "And the keypad's dead, which means whatever security it once had was disconnected, but the lock itself was kept. You disconnect electronics when you want a room to stop being official. You keep the physical lock when you still want it closed." She straightened slowly. "Who has the key?"

"That's the question, isn't it." Marisol leaned against a covered grand piano, and beneath the tarp the strings answered her weight with a faint sympathetic hum, one low note, like the building clearing its throat. "Facilities says no. Security says no. But here's what I know, and I've never told anyone this, so understand what I'm doing right now." She waited until Beatrix's eyes met hers. "Six years ago, I came down here on a Sunday. Off schedule. My own key opens the instrument storage, and there was water pooling near this wall — a pipe leak, I reported it, it got fixed. But when I came down that Sunday, the door was open. Just a crack. Just enough to see light inside."

"And?"

"And there was a man inside. Older, well-dressed, not facilities, not security — I've seen every uniform this building owns, and he wasn't wearing any of them. He was sitting at a table under a lamp, going through boxes. Papers. He heard me and closed the door before I got ten steps closer. But I saw the table. And I saw—" She hesitated. This was the part she had never said, the part that had lived in her like a splinter. "I saw framed certificates on the wall. Awards. The kind with gold seals. And I saw a photograph, big, on an easel like it was being hung or being taken down. Black and white. Two girls at a piano."

The boiler ticked somewhere above them. Beatrix had gone very still, the stillness of a person listening to a frequency no one else can hear.

"Two girls," she repeated.

"One of them was you." Marisol said it plainly, because there was no kind way to say it and cruelty would have been worse. "I didn't know it then. I know it now. I've watched you for three weeks. I know it the way I know the floors."

Beatrix turned back to the door. She raised her hand and laid her palm flat against the steel, and Marisol watched her do it and understood that she was watching someone greet a grave.

"When the demolition crews come," Beatrix said quietly, "they'll come through here. Sub-basements first. Structural survey, then the excavators. Whatever's in that room gets inventoried by the foundation's own contractors and hauled away in dumpsters." A pause. "Or it gets destroyed on site. Either way, after ninety days, nobody can ever prove what was in here."

"So we're thinking the same thing."

"We might be." Beatrix turned. In the dim emergency lighting her face was half shadow, and for the first time Marisol saw what lived under the smile — not numbness exactly, but something patient and enormous, like water backing up behind a dam. "Why are you showing me this? You don't know me. You could lose this job."

Marisol considered the question honestly, because the woman deserved honesty if she was going to give her anything at all.

"My mother cleaned houses for thirty years," she said. "Thirty years, and she knew everything about every family she worked for, and she said nothing, ever, because saying something meant losing the job, and the job was food. And the families knew she knew. That was part of what they paid for — not just the cleaning. The silence." She pushed off from the piano; again the low sympathetic note rose and faded. "I swore I'd never be that quiet. And then I got this job, and I've been that quiet for eleven years, because the job is food, and I have a daughter, and the math doesn't change just because you hate it." She looked at the steel door. "But the building's coming down anyway. They're taking the job from me regardless. So the math finally changed." She met Beatrix's eyes. "You're the only person who's walked through this building in eleven years who looks at it like it matters. So. That's why."

Beatrix was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, "There's a manuscript. A piece I wrote when I was nineteen. It was never published, never recorded, never performed beyond one run-through in a rehearsal room upstairs. If it exists anywhere in this world, it exists in that room. And if it exists in that room, then someone put it there, and catalogued it, and kept it locked — which means someone decided it belonged to them."

"Your father's foundation."

"My father's foundation."

Marisol nodded slowly. Then she reached into her breast pocket and took out her own key ring — a dozen keys, each tagged in her neat block capitals — and held it up between them.

"I can't get you in," she said. "Not tonight, not with these. But the man six years ago came in on a Sunday, off schedule, with a lamp. Which means somebody lets certain people in, on certain days, and those people aren't staff, and staff aren't supposed to notice." She let the keys drop back into her pocket. "So here's what I propose. You watch the schedules. I watch the doors. Between the two of us, we'll learn who visits that room, and when, and how they get in — because whoever holds that key has been keeping your sister's piano in the dark for thirty years, and I think it's time somebody turned on the lights."

Beatrix looked at her for a long moment. Then she extended her hand — a musician's handshake, brief and firm, the kind exchanged before a performance that both players know will demand everything.

"Sunday," Beatrix said. "Somebody comes on Sundays."

"How do you know?"

"Because Sundays," Beatrix said, turning back toward the stairwell, toward the long climb up into the ordinary world, "were always when my father did his real work."
