## 13. Ninety Days, Then Sixty

The notice on the conservatory's front doors had been updated.

Someone from the city had come by with a laminated sheet and a staple gun, and now the original ninety-day order wore a smaller addendum beneath it like a footnote to a verdict: *Revised schedule per permit amendment — demolition commencing in sixty (60) days.* Sixty days. The number sat there in official typeface, patient as a headstone, and Beatrix stood in the entryway at six in the morning with her mop bucket dripping onto the marble, reading it the way you'd read your own diagnosis.

Forty-five days ago she had walked through these doors as a stranger wearing a janitor's uniform. Thirty days ago she had found her own name catalogued under her father's foundation. Twenty days ago Daniel Okafor had sat across from her in a diner booth in Ashford and told her he'd thought she was dead.

And now the building itself had been given a death sentence with a firm date.

She touched the laminated edge. Cold, smooth, indifferent. Somewhere above her, in the practice halls, the morning light was doing what it always did — pouring through the tall east windows and laying long gold bars across the floorboards where she used to pace before recitals, fourteen years old, palms sweating, Iris in the third row mouthing *you've got this* with the ferocious confidence of someone who believed it on her sister's behalf even when her sister couldn't.

Sixty days. Then the bars of light would fall on rubble.

Beatrix picked up her bucket and went to work.

* * *

She drafted the complaint on Thursday night, in the archive room, on a legal pad she'd bought at the pharmacy along with cough drops and a newspaper she didn't read past the first page.

It was astonishing how much of it she already knew. That was the thing no one told you about growing up inside a machine: when the machine finally turned on you, you discovered you'd absorbed its blueprints without ever meaning to. She knew the names of the licensing bodies. She knew the registration numbers from the copyright filings — she'd copied them out in her small careful hand weeks ago, kneeling under the archive's dead fluorescent tube while Marisol kept watch in the corridor with a radio playing low. She knew the dates: the manuscript dated eleven months after her breakdown, the first royalty disbursement dated four months after that, the steady quarterly payments flowing into the Pemberton Foundation for Arts and Heritage like a tide that never missed a schedule.

She knew, most of all, the signature. Her father's signature on the recording contract, reproduced now in a photograph on her phone that Daniel had sent her, his hand unsteady across the screen as he typed: *I kept my copy. I don't know why. Maybe because some part of me always knew I'd need to prove I wasn't crazy either.*

So she wrote. She wrote *IN THE MATTER OF* and then her own name, and it was the strangest sentence she had ever composed — stranger than any music, stranger than the false obituary of a career she'd been living inside for twenty years. *Plaintiff, v. The Pemberton Foundation for Arts and Heritage, et al.* She laid out the counts in the language she'd learned from three afternoons in the law library, sitting in the back row with her cap pulled down: copyright infringement, conversion, unjust enrichment. Fraud, maybe. She wasn't sure. She listed the exhibits. Exhibit A, the manuscript. Exhibit B, the licensing records. Exhibit C, the contract. Exhibit D —

Exhibit D was where her pen stopped.

Exhibit D was Iris.

Because that was the truth underneath all of it, the load-bearing wall the whole structure stood on, and a complaint was not built to hold that kind of weight. A court could return her royalties to her. A court could put her name back on the song. A court could, if she was very lucky and very relentless and had two years and money she didn't have, make the foundation pay damages to *Beatrix Pemberton*, plaintiff, a woman who existed on paper.

But what court would reopen the night Iris died? What filing could reach into a stairwell twenty years gone and ask the one question the family had arranged its entire architecture around never asking? Her father's lawyers would do what her father's lawyers had always done. They would file motions. They would request conferences. And then, when the discovery started to get close to anything real, they would offer a settlement — generous, quiet, wrapped in a confidentiality agreement thick as a hymnal — and everyone would sign, because that was how the Pembertons ended things. Not with verdicts. With versions.

Her mother had signed one, once. Beatrix had seen the shape of it without ever seeing the document — the way her mother stopped asking questions in the spring after the funeral-that-wasn't-a-funeral, the way her eyes went smooth and distant whenever Iris's name surfaced, which was never, because the name had been retired like a jersey number. Whatever they'd paid her, it had come with silence attached. Silence was the family currency. Her father minted it; everyone else spent it.

And Beatrix understood, sitting on the archive room's cold floor with the legal pad balanced on her knees, that if she filed this complaint, she would spend too. She would trade the truth for a check. The lawyers would make sure of it — not out of malice but out of competence, which was worse. They would find the version of the story that was provable and settle for it, and the unprovable part — the stairwell, the argument, the sister who had confronted their father about stolen music and fallen — would stay exactly where it had always been, sealed in the dark, earning interest.

Twenty years of royalties said the seal held pressure just fine.

She looked down at what she'd written. Four pages of clean, furious, useless prose.

* * *

Marisol found her there near midnight, the way she always did — a soft knock, two raps, their old signal — and came in carrying two paper cups of vending-machine coffee and an expression that meant she'd already read the weather on Beatrix's face.

"You look like you lost a fight with a dictionary," Marisol said, handing over a cup.

"I'm drafting a lawsuit."

Marisol lowered herself onto the upturned crate she'd claimed as furniture months ago, next to the shelving unit where Beatrix's juvenilia sat catalogued under her father's name. "Yeah? How's it going?"

"It's going perfectly. That's the problem." Beatrix turned the pad around so Marisol could see. "Every count is solid. Every exhibit checks out. Any lawyer worth her fees takes this case and starts counting her percentage."

"So what's wrong with it?"

"Nothing." Beatrix set the coffee down. "That's what's wrong with it."

She explained it the way she'd been turning it over in her head all week, in the gaps between mopping and the long stretches of standing still in empty corridors listening to the building breathe. A suit gets filed. The foundation's counsel — and they would be excellent, they had always been excellent — answers with a motion to dismiss, which fails, which is fine, motions are cheap. Discovery begins. And somewhere in discovery, before it reaches the father, before it reaches the tape or the stairwell or the word *Iris*, a partner at some white-shoe firm calls and offers seven figures and a mutual nondisclosure agreement, and the case settles, and the settlement includes a clause restoring her rights to the composition, and everyone calls it a victory.

"And then?" Marisol asked.

"And then I can't talk about it. Ever. Neither can Daniel. Neither can you, probably, once they figure out what you know. The song comes home to me in a box with a bow on it, and the box is locked, and the key is a signature." Beatrix heard her own voice go flat and precise, the voice she used for inventory lists. "My father buys back the story for less than it cost him to steal it. He's done it before. He's very good at it."

Marisol was quiet for a while. She drank her coffee. Down the hall, the old boiler ticked through its nightly convulsions, a sound Beatrix had come to think of as the building's heartbeat, arrhythmic and stubborn.

"My sister," Marisol said finally, "got hurt at a plant in Newark, years ago. Chemical burn, both hands. The company settled fast — fast like they'd had the paperwork printed before the ambulance arrived. Good money. NDA. My parents signed because the lawyer said it was the best deal we'd ever get." She turned the cup in her hands. "You know what I remember? Not the money. I remember the company's lawyer shaking my father's hand at the end, warm as anything, and saying, *We're glad we could put this behind us.* Put it behind us. Like it was a car we were pushing out of the driveway." She looked up. "Twenty years later nobody at that company knows my sister's name. The machine that burned her doesn't even have a scar."

"That's what a win looks like," Beatrix said. "That's what they'd give me. A scarless win."

"So don't take it."

"It's not that simple. It's the only remedy I have. Courts are the— " She gestured vaguely, at the legal pad, at the world. "They're the place this is supposed to go. You're injured, you petition, you're made whole. That's the entire civic religion. I was raised on it. My father endowed a *chair* at the law school."

Marisol snorted. "Of course he did."

"But here's what I keep coming back to." Beatrix pulled the pad back onto her knees and looked at the four pages, at the tidy columns of counts and exhibits, the whole reasonable architecture of grievance. "A lawsuit asks a judge to decide what happened. And a judge can only decide what's admissible. Everything true about Iris is inadmissible. Dead women don't testify. Broken families don't either — we signed our own NDAs years ago, all of us, just by agreeing at every holiday table to pass the potatoes and never say her name." She pressed her thumb flat against the page until the paper dimpled. "If I sue, I get my song back and lose my sister twice. Once to the stairwell and once to a settlement conference in a glass tower. I won't do it."

"What will you do?"

And there it was — the question, the honest one, the one Beatrix had been circling for weeks the way water circles a drain. She thought about the answer she didn't have yet. She thought about the second movement, forty-one bars of it, abandoned at nineteen, still waiting in the archive drawer like a patient that couldn't die because no one had pronounced it. She thought about the demolition date, sixty days, and how strange it was that the thing she feared most had become the only clock she trusted.

"I don't know," she said. "But I know it isn't this."

She tore the pages off the pad. All four. She folded them once, lengthwise, the way her father folded documents he wanted to seem casual about, and Marisol watched her with an expression that was half alarm and half something else — recognition, maybe, or relief.

"You're going to throw those away?"

"Not throw away." Beatrix stood. "Burn."

* * *

There was a metal drum in the loading dock behind the kitchens, left over from last winter when the facilities crew had burned off paint scrapings and broken pallets. It still held a bed of ash and a smell of old smoke. Beatrix crouched beside it with the folded pages and a book of matches she'd taken from the front desk, and for a long moment she simply knelt there, in the cold, under a sky rinsed thin and starless above the conservatory roofline.

This was the moment, she understood, where a sensible person would stop. Sensible people did not renounce remedies. Sensible people hired the good lawyer and took the settlement and rebuilt a modest life on the proceeds and told themselves, at dinner parties, a softened version of the story. Her father had built an empire on sensible people folding at exactly this point — the point where the fight became expensive, where the system offered its warm handshake and its check, where putting it behind us sounded almost like mercy.

She struck the match.

The corner of the top page caught, curled, went orange. Fire moved through her own handwriting faster than she expected — *plaintiff*, *defendant*, *unjust enrichment* blooming into light and then into nothing, the words she'd labored over for three nights lifting off the paper as heat and going up into the dark where no one could subpoena them. She fed the pages in one at a time. Exhibit A. Exhibit B. The count for fraud, which she'd never been sure of anyway. The whole clean, correct, doomed machinery of lawful redress, burning down to a skin of ash that shivered when she breathed near it.

It didn't feel like defeat. That surprised her. She had expected grief, or terror — the vertigo of stepping off the path everyone agreed was the path. Instead she felt the specific, unfamiliar lightness of a decision that had actually been hers. For twenty years every important thing about her life had been arranged by other people: the breakdown, the retirement, the silence, even the song, which lived on without her in other people's hands. This — kneeling in a loading dock, watching her own lawsuit turn to smoke — was the first act in two decades that no one had scripted.

"You know," Marisol said from the doorway, arms crossed against the cold, "most people just shred."

"Shredding is administrative." Beatrix watched the last ember pulse and die. "This is testimony."

"To who?"

"To whoever's listening." She stood, brushed ash from her knees, and felt the night air move over her face, smelling of salt off the coast road and cold stone. Sixty days. The number had stopped feeling like a threat sometime in the past hour and started feeling like something else — a measure, a meter, a tempo marking. *Allegro ma non troppo.* Enough time, if she worked fast and slept little, to finish what she'd abandoned at nineteen.

Because that was the realization the fire had handed her, plain as a receipt: she didn't need a court to give her back the music. The music had never actually left. It was upstairs, forty-one bars deep, waiting for the rest of itself. And there was one more thing she needed besides the movement — one question, twenty years old, that no judge would ever ask and only one man alive could answer.

She was done petitioning. It was time, instead, to go home — to the estate, to the dinner she'd been avoiding since the broadcast, to sit across the table from her father and ask him, in front of whatever family remained willing to attend, the question they had all arranged their lives around:

*What happened the night Iris died?*

Beatrix dropped the matchbook into the drum and walked back inside, past the demolition notice with its patient sixty days, and began, quietly, to plan a family dinner.
