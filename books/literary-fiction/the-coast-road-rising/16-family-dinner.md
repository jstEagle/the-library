## 16. Family Dinner

The invitation came on cream cardstock, the kind of paper that costs more per sheet than I earn in a shift.

*Beatrix — Father would like you to dine with us Sunday. Seven o'clock. — A.*

No question mark anywhere in it. Alistair had learned that from our father: never ask when you can summon. The card sat on my kitchen table for two days, propped against the salt cellar like a small pale tombstone, and I let it sit there while I mopped floors and catalogued stolen manuscripts and did not sleep. On the third day I wrote *RSVP* in the margin and mailed it back with no other word, because there was a version of me that wanted to refuse out of principle and a version of me that understood something truer: he had finally run out of ways to avoid me, and I was not going to waste that.

Twenty years of arranged silence, and now he wanted dinner.

* * *

I dressed carefully, which surprised me. Not for him — never again for him — but because I wanted to arrive as myself, not as the girl they'd buried or the janitor they'd ignore. I chose the grey dress Iris used to call my "funeral for someone else's sins," and I wore my mother's small pearl earrings, the ones Alistair hadn't managed to claim in the estate division after she died. Armor, but quiet armor. The kind you wear into rooms where the weapons are all verbal.

The drive up the coast took forty minutes. The house appeared the way it always appeared — too late to turn around, white walls rising out of the cypress like something the cliff had grown rather than built. Pemberton House. My father bought it the year after Iris died, with money I now knew the provenance of. Every stone in it was paid for with my music. I parked at the end of the gravel drive and sat with my hands on the wheel, engine ticking, watching the light go amber in the west-facing windows.

There was a time I'd loved this house. That was before I understood what houses like this are for. They're not homes. They're arguments made permanent — proof, set in mortar, that the man who built them won whatever war he thought he was fighting.

I went in through the front door because I refused to use the service entrance, even though the service entrance was closer to where I'd be eating, if I'd been staff. Which, in every way that mattered financially, I was.

* * *

Alistair met me in the hall. He looked good, damn him — silver at the temples now, tailored charcoal suit, the family charm polished to a high gloss. But I'd spent weeks learning to read the foundation's paperwork, and paperwork teaches you to read men. Under the gloss his eyes were tired in a specific way, the tiredness of a man who has been plugging leaks with his fingers and is running out of fingers.

"Beatrix." He kissed the air near my cheek. "You look well."

"I look employed."

"That too." His smile held. "Father's in the study. He's had a difficult week."

"Has he." I let my bag hang from my shoulder and didn't move toward the study. "The journalist?"

Something flickered across his face — quick, quickly mastered. "You've heard about that."

"I work nights, Alistair. I have a lot of time to read."

He studied me then, really studied me, and I watched him do the arithmetic he'd been doing since the radio broadcast: how much does she know, who told her, what will it cost. Twenty years ago that same calculating look had been turned on me from hospital corridors while doctors used words like *breakdown* and *instability* and *stress-related*, and my brother stood beside our father nodding, nineteen years old and already fluent in the family language.

"Dinner first," he said finally. "Then whatever this is."

"This," I said, "has been waiting twenty years. It can wait through the soup."

* * *

My father sat at the head of the table in a wheelchair, and the sight of him landed in my chest like a dropped weight.

Edmund Pemberton had been a large man — large voice, large presence, large hand on your shoulder steering you toward what he wanted. Stroke had taken the left side of him and most of the volume, but not, I saw immediately, the command. He sat propped with cushions, right hand resting on the table's edge like a garrisoned flag, and when I entered the dining room his eyes found me and held, and the whole room rearranged itself around his attention the way it always had. The candles seemed to burn lower. The staff — two of them, silent, professional — moved differently.

"Beatrix." His voice was a ruin of itself, slurred on the sibilants, but the name came out clear. He'd practiced it, maybe. Or maybe some things survive anything.

"Father."

"You look like your mother."

It was the first thing he'd said to me in eleven years that wasn't mediated by lawyers, and it was a deflection, and we both knew it, and still something in me lurched. I sat where the second-place setting indicated — to his right, Alistair across from me, the geometry of succession — and unfolded my napkin and said nothing, because silence was the one instrument I'd learned to play better than anyone in this family.

Soup was served. We ate. The candles did their work. Outside, the sea made its long argument with the cliffs below the terrace, the sound I'd fallen asleep to for eighteen years of my life, the sound I now realized I'd been missing without knowing it, the way you miss a heartbeat you were once inside of.

Halfway through the main course — lamb, of course, the celebration meat, though what we were celebrating I couldn't imagine — my father set down his fork. It took him a visible effort, the left hand rising to assist the right, and the small struggle of it was worse than any display of power would have been.

"The conservatory," he said. "They're demolishing it."

"In ninety days. Sixty-one now." I kept my voice level. "Your project."

"My project." He looked at me, and there was something in his face I had never seen there before and took a moment to identify because it was so foreign to it: hesitation. "You're working there."

"As the night janitor. Yes."

"Alistair told me." A pause. The staff refilled water glasses and withdrew, doors closing behind them with soft finality. "I asked him not to tell me. He told me anyway. He never could follow an instruction that inconvenienced him."

"He's improved," Alistair said lightly, "at following the ones that matter."

"Has he." My father's gaze didn't leave mine. And then, in the ruined voice, quieter: "They played your piece on the radio. The one from—" He stopped. Even now. Even diminished, even at the end of everything, the machinery of avoidance engaged automatically. "Someone performed it."

"Someone recorded it twenty years ago," I said. "Under contract. With your foundation collecting the royalties ever since. The manuscript in the archive is dated eight months after my breakdown, Father. Eight months after I supposedly destroyed my own career. Someone dated that manuscript, catalogued it, licensed it, and banked the proceeds. I've read the licensing records. They're very thorough."

Alistair set down his wine glass with exaggerated care. "Beatrix—"

"No." My father raised his good hand, and Alistair closed his mouth. "Let her say it. I've been waiting for her to say it for weeks. Since the broadcast. Since the journalist started calling." He drew a breath that whistled slightly. "Say the rest of it, Beatrix. You've come all this way. Say what you came to say."

And there it was — the door, standing open, the door the whole family had spent twenty years bricking over, and my father sitting in front of it with his ruined body and his undiminished eyes, telling me to walk through it.

I put down my napkin. My hands were steady. I noticed that the way you notice weather — with distant surprise.

"What happened the night Iris died?"

The question fell into the room and lay there.

Alistair went white. "Father, you don't have to—"

"*What happened,*" my father said, and it wasn't to me, it was to Alistair, a flaying single syllable, and then he turned back to me and his voice changed entirely — dropped its command, dropped its architecture, became just an old man's broken instrument trying to play one last honest note. "That's the question. Isn't it. That's the question this whole house was built on top of. Every dinner, every Christmas, every board meeting, every scholarship in Iris's name that we gave with such public generosity — all of it, furniture arranged around that one empty chair." He looked at the window, at the dark coming down over the water. "You want to know what happened."

"I've wanted to know for twenty years."

"Yes." Almost a whisper. "So have I. Every day. That's the punishment no court could have designed better."

* * *

He talked for a long time. It was hard going — the stroke had made his speech a labor, and he had to stop sometimes, and drink water Alistair brought with hands that shook slightly, and start again. But he didn't stop talking. I think he'd rehearsed this too, in the nights when the house was quiet, composing the confession the way he'd once composed funding proposals, and there was a terrible craft in it, the old precision showing through the damage.

Iris had known. That was the beginning of it. Iris, brilliant and unstable and more honest than either of us, had found the correspondence — early drafts of the foundation's catalogue, letters between my father and the conservatory's dean arranging the transfer of "Pemberton juvenilia" to foundation ownership. She'd recognized the handwriting in the margins. Mine. She'd recognized the music itself, because she'd heard me write it, note by note, at the piano down the hall from her room, for years.

"She came here," my father said. "That October. You were already — after the breakdown, you were at the clinic, you weren't speaking to any of us. She came here alone on the train. She had the letters in her bag. She put them on this table, right there, where your plate is." His good hand trembled against the wood. "She said she was going to the newspapers. She said — " and here his voice failed him for a moment, and he pressed his knuckles to his mouth, and when he continued the words came slower, dragged out of him like nails. "She said, *You did this to her, and you'll do it to me, and I'm the only one left who can stop you.*"

The candle flames leaned in the draft from somewhere, and straightened.

"It was raining. She wouldn't stay for dinner — she never stayed, she was angry, she was magnificent, she was so much like—" He stopped himself from saying *your mother*, or maybe from saying *me*. "She went up to get her coat. She'd left it in the guest room. I followed her. I was going to offer her money — God forgive me, I was going to offer my daughter money, because it was the only language I had left. She was ahead of me on the stairs. She turned around to say something to me. To tell me what I could do with my check, I imagine." The ghost of something crossed his face — not quite a smile, not quite grief, some hybrid the human face isn't built for. "Her heel caught. On the third stair from the top. The one with the loose brass strip I'd been meaning to have repaired for a month."

He closed his eyes.

"She fell past me. I tried — my hand touched her sleeve. I couldn't hold her. I heard it, Beatrix. I heard everything. And then I knelt there on those stairs holding her hand, and I heard Alistair calling from the study, and do you know what I thought? Before I called for help, before anything — do you know the first thing I thought?"

I didn't answer. I couldn't. The lamb had gone cold on my plate and the sea was roaring louder than it had any right to and my sister was falling down those stairs forever, turning to speak, heel catching, twenty years of falling compressed into the space between one candle flame and the next.

"I thought: *they'll say it was Beatrix.*" His eyes opened, wet, unflinching. "Because you were unstable. Because you'd had the breakdown. Because the story was already written — I'd written it, I'd spent a year writing it, the fragile genius, the jealous sister, the poor girl who cracked under pressure. It was all prepared. All I had to do was let it stand. So I stood up off that floor with my daughter's blood on my hands, literally, and I called the doctor, and I said the words that bought this family twenty years of silence: *Beatrix was here earlier. She was distraught.*"

"You weren't even—" Alistair's voice cracked. "You told me she'd been here that afternoon. You let me believe—"

"I let everyone believe what was useful." My father said it flatly, without excuse. "That was my gift. That was my genius, if I had one. I made the true thing unsayable and the false thing convenient, and I did it so smoothly that none of you ever had to lie. You only had to be silent. Silence asks nothing of the conscience. That's why it works." He turned to me fully, the wheelchair creaking, and the effort of the movement cost him visibly, and he made it anyway, because Edmund Pemberton faced what he faced. "And then you stopped speaking to us, and I told myself it was guilt, that you remembered something, that the truth was leaking out of you in the only way left — and I let that story stand too, because it kept you away, and away was safe. And the royalties kept coming. And the foundation grew. And I told myself I'd make it right someday, with the memorial, with the scholarships, with—" He gestured at the room, the house, the whole edifice, with a hand that shook. "With all of this. Which is ash. Which was always ash."

The clock in the hall struck nine. Neither of them moved. I realized my cheeks were wet and had no memory of starting.

"Why now?" My voice came out rough. "Why tell me now?"

"Because you finished it." He said it simply. "The broadcast. I heard the second theme — the one she used to hum, the one you wrote for her birthday, the movement you never completed. And I thought: she's finishing it. After everything, she's finishing it. And I understood that whatever I had built to contain the truth was smaller than the truth, and had been all along, and that the only thing left in my power—" his breath caught, held, released, "—the only thing left in my power was to stop lying to the last person alive who deserves it."

He reached beneath the cushion of his wheelchair, slowly, with both hands, and drew out a small cassette tape, unlabeled, its plastic gone yellow with age.

"I started dictating this three years ago," he said. "For my memoir. I never wrote the memoir. But I kept talking, some nights, into that machine, when I couldn't sleep. Everything. All of it. What happened on the stairs. What happened after. What I did to you." He laid the tape on the table between us, next to my untouched plate, and pushed it with one finger until it touched my hand. "It's yours. Do with it what Iris would have done."

I picked it up. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

Behind me, Alistair rose from his chair, and I heard him cross to the window, and stand looking out at the dark water, and say nothing at all.

* * *

I drove home with the tape on the passenger seat, and the coast road unspooled ahead of me in the headlights, black cliffs on the right, the sea invisible but enormous, and I did not cry, and I did not speak, and somewhere past the point where the road bends above the old quarry I understood that the tape was not the ending of anything.

It was a loaded weapon, and tomorrow morning, my brother was going to have to decide whether to fire it — or bury it, one last time, deeper than all the others.
