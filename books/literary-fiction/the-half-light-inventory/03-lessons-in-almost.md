## 3. Lessons in Almost

Aurelia Sanch's apartment smelled of camphor and dried thyme and the particular dust of rooms where nothing has been moved in decades except by necessity. She had arranged a chair for me at her kitchen table — a kitchen chair, wooden, its seat worn to two pale crescents by someone else's sitting, someone long gone — and she sat opposite with her hands folded like a woman about to conduct an audit.

"Objects first," she said. "Words that live in things last longer than words that live in air."

She reached beneath the table and set out her inventory one piece at a time, as if dealing cards: a brass key gone green at the teeth. A spool of black thread. A river stone, smooth as a closed eye. A tin cup dented on one side. A photograph face-down, which she did not turn over.

Each object received its word.

*Vess.* The key. She said it twice, making me repeat it until my tongue stopped fighting the soft *v*, the way it slid into the *s* like water finding a channel. "Key," she said. "But also *what opens*. In Vessic you do not say key without meaning door, without meaning the hand that turns it. No word stands alone. That is the first cruelty and the first mercy of the language."

*Tessik.* The thread. She pulled a length of it across the table between us, taut, and held my eyes over it. "Thread is what binds. But *tessik* also means promise. Also means debt. You will learn that Vessic does not believe in separate things. It believes everything is holding onto everything else."

I repeated the words. My voice sounded wrong in her kitchen, too careful, a man handling borrowed china. Aurelia watched me the way I imagined she had once watched weather — with patience and a certain professional disappointment.

"You hum," she said, not asking. "When you work. I heard you through the wall for years before I ever heard your voice."

"I didn't know it was Vessic," I said. "The song. My mother used to—"

I stopped. It was the stopping itself that was fluent; twenty years of practice had made my silences better than my speech. Aurelia let the silence sit there between us like a third student, then picked up the river stone.

*Sennu,* she said. Stone. Patience. The thing that waits.

* * *

My mother spoke Vessic the way other people breathe — constantly, unconsciously, mostly to herself. She counted stairs in it. She scolded the radiator in it. On Sunday mornings she sang the counting song while she braided nothing, her own hair, her fingers moving through it in patterns I can still feel in my own hands when I tune a piano and the strings resist.

She tried to teach me. That is the part I have spent twenty years not saying. People assume the language simply faded from her, or that she kept it private, a jewel too fine for a child's pocket. The truth is duller and worse: she taught me every week, patiently, and I refused. Not dramatically. I was seven, then nine, then twelve, and the neighborhood already told me daily that Vessic was a smell from another country, a stammer, something to be grown out of like shoes. When she said *tessik* I said thread. When she said *sennu* I said rock. I gave her back English, flat and correct, the way you return a gift you're embarrassed by.

The last time, I remember because the memory has a sound attached — the radiator ticking, her voice rising through it from the kitchen below while I lay on my bed pretending to be asleep so she would stop calling me down to practice. She called my name in Vessic. *Ines. Ines.* And then something else, a sentence, her voice doing that thing it did when it was half laughing, and I pressed my face into the pillow and let the pipes carry it past me.

Three weeks later she was dead, and the sentence went wherever unfinished sentences go, and I have been listening through walls ever since, collecting everyone else's words because I would not keep hers.

* * *

"You never learned from her." Aurelia said it on the fourth visit, setting down the tin cup — *kessa*, cup, but also *what receives* — and looking at me with the terrible directness of the very old, who have run out of time for tact. "Why."

The question landed in my chest like a dropped tuning fork, still humming.

"She worked a lot," I said. "Evenings. There wasn't—"

"Ines." She said my name the way my mother had, and I understood suddenly that this was why she had chosen me, or why the language had, or whatever mechanism Aurelia believed in; she could say my name in Vessic and make the air around it change pressure. "You deflect like a man stepping around broken glass. I am ninety-one. I have buried a husband, two children, and an entire language. Do not bring me your small evasions and expect them to pass as answers."

I looked at the cup. At the thread. At the stone that meant patience, which I was failing.

"She asked me to learn," I said finally. "And I wouldn't. I thought—" My throat did the thing it does. "Everyone here acted like it was nothing. Like it was less than nothing. I wanted to be from somewhere. So I made her language the price of it."

Aurelia nodded slowly, without softening. "And now you want it back."

"Now there's almost none left to get back."

"Yes." She turned the cup in her fingers. "That is what dying languages are. They are the sentence you didn't finish. Everyone who loses one is carrying the same grief, they just don't know each other yet." She pushed the cup toward me. "*Kessa.* Say it."

"*Kessa.*"

"Again. It means receive. Your mother was trying to give you something every day of your life and you were refusing delivery. Now sit there and receive."

So I received. An hour of it: *kessa*, *vess*, *tessik*, *sennu*, and then new objects produced from drawers and shelves — a button (*pilla*, also *a small moon*), a burnt match (*fenna*, also *the end of anger*), a hairpin (*luma*, also *what holds what falls apart*). Each word arrived attached to its thing, and each thing seemed to look at me with the smugness of objects that know they will outlast the people naming them. The brass key will be green metal in a drawer when we are both done. The stone will wait. That was the discipline of it, I began to see: Aurelia was not teaching me vocabulary. She was teaching me to stand in a room with impermanence and speak anyway.

At the end she grew tired — I saw it come over her like a shade drawn — and she rested her hand flat on the table beside the face-down photograph.

"One more thing today," she said. "Not a word. A question you will answer next time, and you will answer it truly, because I will know." Her eyes were bright and merciless. "Your mother died mid-sentence. Everyone says so. What was the sentence?"

"I don't know," I said. "I wasn't there. I was upstairs and I—"

"You were upstairs pretending to sleep," Aurelia said. "Through the pipes. Yes. I know how boys listen in this building." She gathered her objects back into their cloth, one by one, unhurried. "Find out what she was saying, Ines. A language cannot live in a person who is hiding from his own dead. Next week. Bring me the sentence, or bring me the truth that you can't, and we will work with that instead. Truth works even when it's broken. It's the only material that does."

* * *

I walked home the long way, past the shuttered bakery and the laundromat that had become a phone-repair shop and back again, because the long way passed the bus shelter on Delmore where the 47 idled between runs, diesel-breathing, patient as the stone. I stood there a while watching people get on with their groceries and their strollers and their tiredness, all of them carried, none of them noticing the carrying.

Then I climbed my stairs, four flights, counting them in Vessic under my breath the way my mother used to — *nes, du, tren, kessa* — and stopped at the fourth-floor landing with my hand on the rail.

Because I had counted wrong. Or rather, I had counted right, and the numbers were right, and one of them — *kessa*, four, *receive* — was the same word as the cup, and I stood there in the stairwell heat with the radiators ticking their old tick and understood that my mother had been counting me all along, numbering the steps I took away from her, in the only language that ever meant more than one thing at a time.

From Marta's apartment below, faintly, came the sound of a radio, and under it, or maybe only in my head, the beginning of the counting song.

I had six days to find a sentence.
