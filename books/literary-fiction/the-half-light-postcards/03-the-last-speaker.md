## 3. The Last Speaker

Vera Okonkwo had learned to measure the neighborhood by its sounds, and in the last decade the sounds had been leaving one by one.

First the bakery below her flat had gone quiet — Mr. Halloran's ovens cooling for good on a Tuesday in March, the smell of yeast replaced by dust and the sweet rot of damp plaster. Then the church bells, silenced when the diocese deconsecrated St. Brigid's and sold the roof lead. Then the children, fewer each year, their voices thinning out of Ferry Lane like water down a cracked drain. What remained was traffic on the ring road, a low animal hum that never slept, and the morning rattle of the number 12 bus — the last thread still stitching the old quarter to the city that had decided, long ago, to forget it.

She was at her kitchen table with the radio murmuring obituaries of people she didn't know when she heard footsteps on the external stair. Heavy, hesitant. A man who climbed slowly because he wasn't sure he wanted to arrive.

Vera did not get visitors. The young people from the heritage society came twice a year with recording equipment and left with her voice in their machines and nothing in their hands. Everyone else had stopped asking anything of her years ago.

She opened the door before he knocked. It was a habit from another country, another century — you opened the door first so that fear never got to decide.

The man on the landing was perhaps forty, tall, wearing grief the way some men wear borrowed coats: correctly, but not comfortably. Dark hair going grey at the temples too early. He held a shoebox against his chest like something rescued from a fire.

"You're Vera Okonkwo," he said. Not quite a question.

"I am. And you're standing on my stairs."

"I'm sorry. I — Dominic Pemberton. My mother was Edith Pemberton. She died three weeks ago." He said it evenly, rehearsed, and then his eyes flicked down to the box and the rehearsal failed him. "I found something in her house. In her desk. And the neighbor — Mrs. Adeyemi, on Corben Street — she looked at one page and went white and told me to come to you. She said if anyone alive can read this, it's you."

Vera held out her hand for the box without answering, and after a moment he gave it up, and she carried it inside, and he followed because there was nowhere else in the world for him to go just then.

* * *

Her flat was a country that had been accumulating for eighty-three years. Books in four scripts leaned against walls in leaning towers; dictionaries splayed open face-down like sleeping birds; a wall of photographs, most of them of people long dead, arranged without order because Vera remembered them all and needed no system. The window over the sink looked down onto the shuttered bakery and, beyond it, the crooked spine of Ferry Lane, where the demolition notices had gone up in the spring like a rash.

She set the box on the kitchen table, under the light, and lifted the lid.

Forty-one postcards. She counted them the way you count wounds — quickly, because counting slowly would mean feeling them. They were old stock, the kind the corner shop used to sell: harbor scenes, the cathedral, a carnival float, all of Ashmoor's postcard past, none of them ever posted. No stamps. No postmarks. Only addresses, written in a hand that shook more as the stack descended through the years, all of them to the same name, the same street.

*Serafina. Ferry Lane.*

And across the back of each one, dense and slanting and unmistakable, Vessic.

Vera had spent fifty years translating other people's languages — court documents, medical forms, the small desperate paperwork of immigrants — and she had made her peace with being a bridge between shores. But Vessic was not a bridge. Vessic was the shore itself, the drowned village under the water, the tongue her own grandmother had scolded her in, the language of maybe two hundred people once, and now — she did the arithmetic she did every night before sleep — now possibly eleven. Nine. She'd lost track of Old Tomas in the winter.

She knew the handwriting before she knew she knew it. You know your own mother's hand; you know the hand of the woman who taught you to write your name in a script no school would accept.

Edith Pemberton's hand.

Vera put the lid back on the box. Her fingers were steady, which surprised her. Inside her chest, something very old turned over like a sleeper disturbed.

"Where," she said, "did you get these."

"Her writing desk. Locked. I only opened it after the funeral." He sat where she gestured, perched on the edge of the chair, a man in a stranger's kitchen trying to take up less space than a man needs. "I can't read any of it. Mrs. Adeyemi called it Vessic? She said it's from the old quarter."

"It is from the old quarter. It *is* the old quarter, what's left of it." Vera kept her voice level. "It came here with the fishing families, ninety years ago. Nobody wrote it down much. Nobody taught it in schools. The schools beat it out of us, if you want the truth, and then the city finished the job by scattering everyone." She looked at him. "Who was your mother to you?"

He blinked. "My mother."

"Before that. What was she to the quarter?"

"I don't understand."

"No," Vera said. "I don't suppose you do."

She made tea because her hands wanted occupation. The kettle's growl filled the silence while Dominic Pemberton sat very straight, watching her with an expression she recognized from decades of watching faces across negotiating tables and hospital bedsides: the expression of a man who has just understood that the person opposite him knows something, and that the knowing is not going to be given away cheaply.

She set the tea down in front of him. Chipped blue cup. He wrapped both hands around it like a child.

"The woman these are addressed to," he said. "Serafina. Do you know her?"

The name landed in Vera's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She let it fall all the way before she answered.

"Serafina Marchetti. Ferry Lane, number six. She sold fish on Thursdays and sang better than anyone I have ever heard, in any language, and she has been dead eleven years." She watched his face do several things at once. "And yes, Mr. Pemberton. I knew your mother. That is not a surprise to either of us, is it? You didn't come to me because of Mrs. Adeyemi alone. You came because somewhere in that house of yours, among the things she left behind, there was a letter or a document or a memory with my name in it. Am I wrong?"

He looked into his tea. "There's a photograph," he said finally. "Three women on a street corner. My mother, young. Laughing. And two others. On the back it says *Ferry Lane, 1974*. One of the women looks like you."

"Then it is me." Vera sat down across from him. Through the window behind him, the afternoon light was going amber over the rooftops of the condemned street, and she thought: they will knock all of this down, and then there will be nothing left to point at, and then even the liars will be free. "I will tell you what I can tell you, and then we will discuss terms, because I am eighty-three years old and I do not have time for the slow way anymore."

"Terms?"

"Your mother wrote forty-one letters in a dying language to a dead woman and never sent one of them. Think about what kind of person does that. Someone who wants to say something and cannot bear to be heard. Someone apologizing, I would guess — I haven't read them yet, but I know the shape of thirty years of guilt when I hold it in my hands." She folded her hands on the table. "You want them translated. Every word. And I could do it in an afternoon, Mr. Pemberton. I could sit here tonight and give you all forty-one by morning, and you could walk out of here with your inheritance complete."

"But you won't."

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because words taken whole are worth nothing. Because your mother — whoever she was to Serafina, whatever she did or didn't do — chose to learn this language slowly, badly, over years, letter by letter, and there is a reason for that, and the reason matters more than the content." Vera heard her own voice harden and did not soften it. "And because I am the last teacher of this language in this city, perhaps in this country, and I have been waiting twenty years for a student who came for the right reasons, and I am too old and too tired to keep waiting. So."

"So?" 

"You become my student. Properly. Once a week, in this kitchen, starting Thursday. You learn Vessic the way it must be learned — from the ground, from greetings, from the names of fish and weather and grief. And when you can read them yourself — truly read them, not have them handed to you — you translate your mother's postcards with your own mouth and your own understanding. Not before."

Dominic set down the cup. For a long moment he said nothing, and Vera let him have the silence, because she had learned that men tell the truth in silence or they reveal its absence.

"That could take a year," he said. "Or longer."

"It took your mother thirty years to say whatever she had to say. You can give me a year."

"And in exchange you'll teach me. All of it. Everything you know."

"Everything I know," Vera said, and felt the weight of that promise settle onto her shoulders like a coat made of lead. Everything she knew was not only grammar. Everything she knew included a hearing room in 1981, and a woman in a good coat reading testimony from prepared pages, and Serafina Marchetti standing up in the public gallery with her fists closed, and the sound the room made when the decision came down against them. Everything she knew included why Edith Pemberton's handwriting shook worse with every passing year.

She was not ready to give him that. Perhaps she never would be. But the boy had his mother's stubborn jaw, and the box sat between them on the table like a debt, and the number 12 bus rattled past below, carrying the quarter's remaining living back and forth across a city that had already drawn its maps.

"One condition of mine as well," Dominic said quietly. "You answer my questions. Not all at once. But eventually. If I'm going to be your student, I deserve to know what I'm learning and why."

Vera almost smiled. Almost.

"Eventually," she agreed. "That is a word I can live inside for a while yet."

She stood, went to the shelf by the window, and took down a battered exercise book, its cover soft with handling — a book she had prepared years ago and never used, blank pages ruled in pencil, waiting. She wrote his name on the first line in careful Latin letters, and beneath it, in Vessic, the first word she would teach him on Thursday.

*Salvë.* Hello. Be well. Come in from the cold.

"There is one thing you should know before you commit," she said, handing him the book. "This language is dying, Mr. Pemberton. When I die, unless you learn it properly, it dies with me — everything it remembers, everything it keeps. Your mother's postcards are part of what it keeps. So understand what you're agreeing to. This isn't translation. This is succession."

Dominic looked down at the strange, beautiful letters under his name — letters that had been his mother's secret, and Serafina's address, and this old woman's whole life — and Vera watched him decide.

"Thursday," he said.

"Thursday," she agreed. "Bring the box. And bring your questions. You may ask them. I may not answer."

At the door he paused, one hand on the frame, and looked back at her with an expression she had seen before on other faces at other thresholds — the look of a man stepping into a story he suspects will cost him.

"Mrs. Okonkwo. The woman in the photograph. Was my mother happy here? On Ferry Lane?"

Vera held his gaze, and behind her own eyes the year 1974 turned over like a bright page, three women laughing on a street corner with the whole world still unruined.

"Come Thursday," she said, "and we will begin with hello."

She closed the door and stood a moment in the quiet, listening to his footsteps descend the stair, slower now, heavier with purpose. Then she uncovered the shoebox again, and touched the lid once, lightly, the way you touch a grave.

Thirty years of unsent words, and God had sent her the son.

Thursday, then. There was time — though less of it, every day, than there had been.
