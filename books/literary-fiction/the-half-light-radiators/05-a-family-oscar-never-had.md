## 5. A Family Oscar Never Had

The man arrived on a Tuesday, which was already suspicious. Tuesdays were the library's emptiest nights — the insomniacs had not yet organized themselves into their midweek despair, and the students who came to sleep openly at the carrels had not yet finished their first round of energy drinks. On a Tuesday, the only sounds were the tick of the radiators and the soft, apologetic thump of books being returned by people who felt guilty about it.

Oscar noticed him before he noticed her noticing him, which she considered a professional skill. He stood in the local history section with his coat still on, holding a volume about municipal water rights as though it were a menu he'd been handed in a language he didn't speak. Tall, somewhere north of thirty, with the kind of easy handsomeness that suggested he'd never once had to think about his posture. His hair did something artful near the temple that hair does not do on its own.

He looked up, caught her looking, and smiled.

Here is what you need to understand about Oscar Ravensworth: she had a smile for every occasion, and none of them were real. There was the smile that said *the bathroom is down the hall*, the smile that said *no, we don't have that book, and no, I won't check again*, and the famous one — the smile that ended conversations, deployed like a fire extinguisher behind glass. She reached for it now, out of habit, the way you reach for a light switch in a familiar room.

But he was already walking toward her, and there was something in his approach — an unhurried warmth, a man who had never been told *no* by a librarian and didn't expect to start tonight — that made her hand stall halfway to the switch.

"You're the night person," he said. It wasn't a question. "I've seen you through the window. I thought, nobody actually works nights at a library unless they love it."

"I work nights because days are crowded," Oscar said. Her voice came out drier than intended, which was also habit.

"Even better." He set the water rights book on the desk like a man laying down cards. "I'm going to be honest with you, because librarians can smell dishonesty — it's a documented fact. I'm researching my family, and I've hit a wall, and the wall has your name on it. Metaphorically." He extended a hand. "Theo Varga."

Oscar took the hand. It was warm and dry and held hers exactly one beat longer than necessary, not enough to be improper, just enough to be noted. Somewhere in her chest, an old machine she kept locked in the basement of herself turned over once, coughed, and went quiet.

"Ravensworth," she said. "Oscar Ravensworth. And I don't know anything about your family."

"Not yet," Theo agreed cheerfully. "But you know Ilse Varga."

* * *

He came back the next night. And the one after that. This was, Oscar would later reflect, the whole architecture of the thing: not charm as a single strike but charm as weather, persistent, atmospheric, until you forgot the sky had ever been any other color.

He always arrived around ten, always with his coat on as if he might leave at any moment and simply never did. He sat at the reading table nearest the desk and spread out photocopies — deeds, survey maps, a photograph of a house so stately it looked embarrassed to be in a photograph. The Varga house. Oscar recognized it without wanting to; she had been inside it twice now, descending into its basement at three in the morning to learn a language that existed nowhere else on earth, and she had said nothing about those visits to anyone, least of all to the grandnephew currently asking her questions in a voice like warm honey poured over gravel.

"So she comes here," Theo said on the third night, tapping a pencil against the table. "My great-aunt. Three times a week, sometimes four. The neighbors say she leaves after midnight. An eighty-four-year-old woman, out past midnight, walking to a library." He shook his head with fond exasperation. "What does she even do here?"

"Reads," Oscar said.

"Reads." He smiled. "In what?"

"Whatever's on the shelf."

"Oscar." He said her name like they'd been friends since childhood, like he'd earned the familiarity through some long shared history she couldn't remember. "Come on. My mother says Ilse speaks some made-up language to herself. Margit thinks she's losing it. I think—" he leaned back, laced his fingers behind his head, studied the ceiling, "—I think my great-aunt is the sharpest person in our family and everyone finds that inconvenient. So. What does she do here?"

*Made-up language.* Something in Oscar went very still and very cold, the way water goes still and cold at the bottom of a lake. She thought of Ilse's hands shaping sounds that English had no room for, the exacting fury when Oscar got a vowel wrong, the strange tenderness afterward. *Hobby,* he'd called it in his own head, maybe. *Losing it,* his sister thought.

"She reads," Oscar repeated. "And occasionally she talks to me. About books. You know how old people are."

She heard herself say it and was surprised by how easily the lie came, how smooth it was, like a coin worn faceless from handling. Theo laughed — a real laugh, or a very good copy of one — and said, "You're protecting her. That's decent. Most people would've sold her out by now for a coffee."

"And yet here you are, buying me nothing."

"I'm buying you dinner," Theo said. "Sunday. Our place. Ana — my mother — makes this lamb thing that has ended friendships and started marriages. Bring your appetite and your discretion, in whatever ratio you prefer."

"I haven't agreed to discretion."

"You haven't agreed to anything. That's why I like you." He began gathering his papers with the unhurried efficiency of a man who had gotten exactly what he came for. At the edge of the desk he paused. "Ilse mentioned you, you know. Last month, at Margit's birthday. She said — let me get this right, it stuck with me — she said, 'There's a girl at the library who listens properly.' Nobody in my family listens properly, Oscar. We perform at each other. It's our national sport." He tapped the desk twice, a little drumroll of intimacy. "Sunday. Seven o'clock. I'll write down the address, though honestly you could follow the smell of the lamb."

* * *

After he left, Oscar stood at the desk for a long time, doing inventory.

This was a thing she did, alone, on slow nights: she counted what she had. One apartment, small, second floor, radiator with opinions. One job, nocturnal, beloved in a way she found embarrassing to admit even to herself. One cat who tolerated her. And — she ran the count again to be sure — zero family. Not estranged family, not complicated family, just none. Parents gone in the ordinary way, no siblings, aunts scattered like dropped change. She had spent thirty-one years perfecting the smile that ends conversations because conversations, continued, led to questions, and questions led to the awkward silence where her family should have been, and she had learned early that it was easier to be pleasantly impenetrable than to watch someone's face do the small arithmetic of pity.

And now a man with artful hair had said *our place* and *my mother makes* and *nobody in my family listens properly* as though these were ordinary things, things everyone had, and had then — this was the part she kept circling back to, the part that itched — he had said *she mentioned you.*

Ilse had mentioned her. Out loud. In a room full of Vargas. *There's a girl at the library who listens properly.*

Oscar shelved a cart of returns with unnecessary force. She was aware, in a distant clinical way, that she was being seduced — not romantically, or not only, but in the older sense: led gently by the hand toward wanting something, so that the wanting would do the work that persuasion couldn't. She had read enough novels to see the machinery. She could see every gear of it turning, gleaming, well-oiled.

She was going to Sunday dinner anyway.

* * *

The address Theo wrote down was in the old quarter, where the houses predated the streetlights and wore their ivy like inherited jewelry. Oscar arrived at seven precisely, having spent forty minutes deciding what to wear and hating herself for every one of them, and stood on the sidewalk holding a bottle of wine she'd chosen with the agonized care of someone defusing a bomb.

The door opened before she knocked. Theo, sleeves rolled, flour on one forearm like a credential.

"You came," he said, delighted, as though she were a stray he'd been feeding. "I told them you'd come. Come in, come in — mind the cat, she's a tyrant, you'll love her."

The hallway smelled of lamb and beeswax and something older underneath, stone and paper, the smell of a house that had been lived in by the same family for longer than the city had kept reliable records. Photographs climbed the staircase wall in ascending generations. Oscar caught a glimpse of a young woman with severe eyes and unsmiling mouth — Ilse, fifty years ago, beautiful in the way that fortresses are beautiful — and then Theo was drawing her forward, announcing her:

"Everyone, this is Oscar. From the library. The one I told you about."

The living room contained three Vargas and a fire that had been lit for effect. A woman rose from the sofa — mid-sixties, silver-blond, wearing elegance the way other people wear uniforms — and this, Oscar knew instantly, was Ana, the mother, the architect of the family's weather. Beside her sat a younger woman with Theo's good bones and none of his warmth, scrolling something on her phone with the focused hostility of the professionally bored: Margit, presumably. And in the armchair by the fire, a man Oscar hadn't expected — elderly, sharp-faced, asleep with his mouth open, a newspaper tented on his chest.

"My uncle Aurel," Theo said, following her glance. "Don't wake him, he's better this way."

"Charming," said Margit, without looking up.

"Theo's told us about you," Ana said. Her voice was low and pleasant and entirely unreadable, a lake with no visible bottom. "How lovely that Ilse has a friend."

*A friend.* The word landed strangely, and Oscar felt, faintly, the shape of the thing beneath it — the way you feel a current under calm water. *Friend* was not what they were wondering about. *Friend* was the word they were using while they wondered.

"We're colleagues," Oscar said carefully. "Of a sort. She's teaching me things."

"What sort of things?" Ana asked, smiling.

And here it was, the question, arriving on the first pass, wrapped in lamplight and hospitality. Oscar thought of Ilse's hands. Of the card she had not yet shown her, the one she kept in a tin and touched but did not read aloud. Of the sentence that had waited seventy years and could wait a few more hours.

"Old books," Oscar said. "Bibliography. Cataloguing. She has a scholar's memory. It's remarkable."

Something flickered across Ana's face — disappointment? relief? — too fast to name. "Well," she said warmly. "That explains the late nights. Sit, sit. Theo, take her coat. Margit, put the phone away, we have a guest."

Margit put the phone away with the air of someone granting a pardon.

Dinner was, objectively, wonderful. The lamb deserved its reputation. Theo kept the table moving like a good host steering a boat through weather — stories about the house, about the neighborhood, about Uncle Aurel's legendary stubbornness (Aurel woke for the lamb, ate in formidable silence, and returned to sleep, having contributed nothing but presence). Ana asked Oscar questions in the manner of a customs officer: Where did she study? Did she have people nearby? Was her flat far from the library? Each question reasonable, each answer received with a nod that filed it away.

And underneath the food and the firelight, Oscar listened — properly, the way Ilse had apparently told the family she did — and heard the other conversation, the one happening in pauses and refills. Margit saying *the hearing* and stopping when Oscar turned her head. Theo saying *after the new year, surely.* Ana saying, once, softly, to no one: *"She will not be managed by strangers."*

They were worried about Ilse. Or they were worried about the estate, which wore worry about Ilse as its coat. Oscar couldn't tell where one ended and the other began, and suspected the Vargas couldn't either.

"This is nice," Theo said at some point, gesturing at the table with his glass, at the candles, at the sleeping uncle, at all of it. "You should come more often, Oscar. Seriously. Sundays are open. God knows we could use someone at this table who isn't suing anyone."

Margit snorted into her wine. Even Ana's mouth twitched.

And Oscar — who had spent her whole adult life declining invitations with the smile that ends conversations, who had built a fortress of competent loneliness brick by brick and called it independence — heard herself say:

"I'd like that."

She meant it. That was the terrible part. Sitting in the half-light of the Varga dining room, among people who performed at each other instead of listening, she wanted it anyway — the standing invitation, the seat that was hers, the lamb and the tyrant cat and the photographs on the stairs. She knew it was a trap. She could see the trap. Traps, she understood now, worked best on people who were hungry, and she had been hungry so long she'd stopped registering it as hunger.

* * *

Ana walked her to the door at the end of the evening, over Theo's protest that he'd do it, over Margit's announcement that she was going to bed. In the hallway, beneath the ascending photographs, Ana stopped and turned, and for a moment the pleasant lake-bottom voice was gone and something older looked out of her face.

"You're fond of her," Ana said. "Ilse."

"Yes," Oscar said. Truthfully.

"She'll tell you things, eventually. She tells everyone things, at her age. Things that aren't true." Ana smoothed the front of her dress, a small precise gesture, a woman straightening a picture frame. "When she does, I hope you'll remember that families are complicated, and that the people who loved her longest know her best. Better than a girl she met in a library."

It was not a threat. That was what made it effective. It was delivered with such gentleness, such genuine-seeming sorrow, that it took Oscar several seconds on the sidewalk afterward, cold air in her lungs, wine humming in her blood, to understand that she had just been warned.

Behind her, the great house glowed in its windows. Above her, the sky was doing the thing it did in this city most nights — neither dark nor light, the half-light that made everything look like a memory of itself.

In her coat pocket, her phone buzzed. A message from a number she didn't have saved, though she knew it by heart now, the way you know a frequency.

*Tuesday. 3 a.m. Bring the notebook. We start on the verbs of owing. — I.V.*

Oscar stood between the two of them — the house full of Vargas behind her, the message from the last fluent speaker in front of her — and realized, with a clarity that felt almost like falling, that sooner or later, both sides were going to ask her what she knew.

And only one of them had ever taught her the words to answer.
