## 13. Theo Reads the Notebook

The trouble with secrets, Oscar thought, is that they insist on being kept in places you can find them. You cannot hide a thing from yourself. You can hide it from your landlord, your coworkers, the insomniacs who wandered the library at two in the morning looking for novels about lighthouses — but the secret itself sat in your own bag, in your own handwriting, in a spiral notebook with a coffee ring on the cover like a wax seal on a letter no one was supposed to open.

She had been carrying the notebook for eleven weeks. It had grown fat. Kessari, it turned out, did not fit neatly into English margins. Ilse's grammar required diagrams — arrows, boxes, little constellations of obligation — and Oscar's pages had filled with them, the way a wall fills with a child's drawings, one on top of another until the original plaster disappears.

She kept it in the green canvas bag she took everywhere. She kept it under the desk during shifts. She kept it, on lesson nights, on the table in the basement between them, because Ilse demanded to see her work and corrected it with a pencil sharpened to a cruelty.

"You have written *vess-ke* here," Ilse said on a Tuesday in March, tapping the page. "That means *I owe the debt*. You mean *the debt is owed*. The debt does not care who owes it. The debt is the subject. You are the accident."

"The debt is the subject," Oscar repeated.

"In Kessari, everything is the subject except people. People are weather. People happen to things." Ilse said this without bitterness, the way she said most true things, and then she looked at Oscar for a long moment and added, "You are learning to happen to things. It will feel like illness at first."

It did feel like illness. It felt like fever, like the weeks after a surgery when the body is busy rearranging itself around the wound. Oscar went home at dawn, slept four hours, woke with Kessari syllables dissolving on her tongue, and came back at night. Her English, she noticed, was beginning to sound translated.

* * *

Theo Varga arrived on a Thursday, which was not one of their lesson nights, and that was the first mistake in the chain of mistakes. Oscar had grown careless about the calendar. She had begun to think of the library as a place where Ilse simply was, the way a church is a place where a bell simply is, and she had stopped checking the door with the old vigilance.

"Oscar Ravensworth," Theo said, from the front desk, at 11:40 p.m., holding a paper cup of something steaming. "I brought you coffee. It's terrible. It's from the place on Delancey that's open too late, which I understand is your natural habitat."

She had seen him three times since the Sunday dinner. Each time he had been exactly this: warm, quick, carrying something. A book he thought she'd like. A joke about the competency hearing, delivered lightly, like a coin flipped to a street performer. Once, a photograph of Ilse at twenty, which he had let Oscar hold for a long time before asking for it back.

"How is she?" he asked now, setting the cup down.

"She's well. She's — teaching me."

"Right, the language." He smiled. He had a way of smiling that made you feel the smile was a favor being done for both of you at once. "My great-aunt's hobby. How goes the hobby?"

"It's not really a hobby," Oscar said. "It's a language."

"Of course." He said it the way you say *of course* to a child explaining that the moon follows the car. "Listen, I won't keep you. I'm doing some digging on the estate — you know how it is, lawyers want paper for everything, provenance for the silverware, a birth certificate for the house itself, practically. And I remembered you're down here all hours. You see everything. Has she mentioned the house? The past, I mean. Her mother, her father? Old stories?"

"She tells stories," Oscar said carefully. "Mostly about words."

"Words." Theo turned the word over like a stone he suspected had a bug under it. "Well. If she ever tells a story about the house, I'd love to hear it. Family history is — " he spread his hands — "family history."

He left. The coffee was terrible, and Oscar drank all of it, because it was warm and someone had thought of her, and she hated herself a little for how much those two facts could purchase.

* * *

The second mistake was the basement door.

It had never locked properly. This had never mattered, because the basement had never contained anything worth stealing — a boiler with opinions, boxes of discarded card catalogs, the long table where Oscar now sat with an old woman twice a week learning a language that existed in exactly two heads on earth. The lock had been broken since before Oscar's employment, and she had mentioned it to building services twice, and building services had the institutional memory of a goldfish.

She mentioned it a third time on the Friday after Theo's visit. She mentioned it because Ilse, arriving at three a.m., had paused at the top of the stairs and said, "This door opens for anyone. You are keeping a language in a room that opens for anyone."

"It's just a basement," Oscar said.

"It was just a sentence, once," Ilse said, "before my mother decided it should mean something." And she went down the stairs, and Oscar stood at the top feeling the sentence settle over her like a coat she hadn't asked to wear.

She put the notebook in the green bag after that. She put the bag under the desk. She put the desk between herself and the world, which is what desks are for, and for nine days it worked.

* * *

The tenth day was a Sunday, and the library closed at six, and Oscar had the early shift, and she left at 6:20 with the bag — she was almost sure she left with the bag — and got home and unpacked groceries and did not check.

The call came at nine. It was Marcus, the evening librarian, a gentle, hapless man who alphabetized the returns by feel and once shelved *Beloved* under B for "Birds, Symbolic."

"Oscar — hey — weird question. Is your notebook, like, valuable? Because there's a guy here, says he's your friend, Theo? He says you left it behind and he's just going to wait for you, but he's been reading it, and honestly he seems kind of — "

"I'm coming," Oscar said, and she was already putting her shoes on the wrong feet.

The library was twenty minutes away by train and she made it in twelve. She came through the front doors at a speed unbecoming of a night librarian and found them in the reading room by the tall windows, Theo in one of the green chairs with the notebook open on his knee, Marcus standing nearby with the expression of a man watching weather turn.

"— and this bit," Theo was saying, not looking up, "this bit here, this is the interesting bit."

Oscar crossed the room and held out her hand. "That's mine."

"I know," Theo said. He did not give it back. He turned another page, and the paper sounded loud in the quiet room, and Oscar understood that she was watching a man decide something, and that the decision was nearly made.

"Marcus," she said, without taking her eyes off Theo, "could you give us a minute?"

Marcus gave them a minute the way a rabbit gives a hawk a minute, gratefully, at speed.

Then it was just the two of them and the tall windows and the notebook, and Theo looked up at last, and the smile was gone.

It was not that his face had become cruel. That would have been simpler. It was that his face had become *accurate* — the charm set aside like a tool he'd finished with, and underneath it something patient and appraising, a face that had been waiting behind the other face for a long time, the way the basement lock had been waiting behind its broken door.

"You've been busy," he said.

"It's a language," Oscar said. "It takes — "

"Sit down, Oscar."

She sat. She was surprised at herself. She noted the surprise, filed it away, and sat.

Theo turned the notebook around so it faced her, open to a page from three weeks ago. Her own handwriting looked foreign to her, urgent, the letters of a person running. At the top of the page, in English, because Ilse had made her write it in English once, once only, so that she would never be able to pretend later that she hadn't known — at the top of the page she had written: *The sentence. First three words. Do not lose this.*

Below it, the Kessari, in her careful blocky script, with the arrows and the boxes, the grammar of debt laid out like a map of a house.

"*Kessar-vel imma,*" Theo read aloud.

His pronunciation was bad. It was so bad it was almost a different language. But the shape of it was right — the bones of it were right — and Oscar felt the floor of the evening tilt.

"Where did you hear that," she said. It was not a question.

"From my grandmother," Theo said. "Once. When I was nine. She was dying, and she was past caring who heard, and she said it to the nurse, or to the window, or to God, I don't know. *Kessar-vel imma.* I never forgot the sound of it. You don't forget a sound like that. It sounds like — " he searched — "it sounds like something being nailed shut."

He closed the notebook, gently, and put both his hands flat on the cover, and looked at her.

"I asked around, after the hearing. There's a linguist at the university, retired, I bought him dinner. He told me Kessari is effectively extinct. He told me the last fluent speaker was Ilse Varga, and that the Varga family — our family — came from the Kessar valley, which is why we have the name, which I always thought was Hungarian. He told me the language has no future tense." He smiled a small smile, without warmth, a smile used for emphasis. "Can you imagine? A language with no future. Everything that happens, happens because of something owed. That's what he said. Everything is debt."

Oscar said nothing. In the silence she could hear the building ticking, the old radiators cooling, the library settling into itself the way it did after closing, all its small sounds going to bed.

"So here's what I think," Theo said, and his voice stayed pleasant, which was the worst part, the pleasantness stretched over the thing like a sheet over furniture in an empty house. "I think my great-aunt has spent her lessons telling you stories. Old stories. And I think one of those stories has a sentence in it, and I think the sentence is about the family, because the only Kessari my grandmother ever spoke in front of me was about the family. And I think that sentence — " he tapped the cover, twice, softly, like knocking — "is the reason my mother flinches whenever anyone mentions the east wing. And I think you know what it says."

"I don't," Oscar said. "I only have the first three words."

"Then you'll have the rest soon." He stood, and picked up the coffee cup from the desk, and looked into it as if the evening's residue were readable there. "You know what's funny, Oscar? I liked you. I actually liked you. At dinner, I thought — here's someone with no angle. Do you know how rare that is in my family? Someone with no angle." He set the cup down. "But everyone has an angle. Yours is just smaller. Lonelier. You wanted a grandmother and you found one with a secret, and now you're keeping her secret like it's yours."

"It's not a secret," Oscar said. "It's a sentence."

"In my family," Theo said, "those are the same thing."

He put the notebook in her hands — gave it back, finally, and the giving was worse than the keeping, because it meant he no longer needed it. He had what he'd come for. He had the sound of the sentence, and the shape of the debt, and the knowledge that the last fluent speaker of a dead language had spent her last lucid months pouring that language into a night librarian with a green bag and a broken lock on her door.

"Margit will call you," he said, at the door. "She'll be nicer about it than I am. She's better at this. But it'll be the same offer, and the offer will go up, and I want you to understand something before it does." He paused, and for one moment — a half-second, a flicker, a frame of film spliced in where it didn't belong — his face did something unguarded, something that looked almost like fear. "Whatever she says that sentence means, whoever she says it's about — my mother is not what you think she is. None of us are what you think we are. That's not a threat. That's just — the family motto, if we had one."

And then the charm came back on, one last time, like a porch light snapping on as he left — "Get home safe, Oscar. It's late. It's always late with you" — and the door swung shut behind him, and the library was quiet, and Oscar sat in the green chair with the notebook in her lap for a long time.

* * *

At three a.m., Ilse was waiting in the basement, as she waited, as she always waited, small and upright at the long table with the pencil sharpened to a cruelty.

"You are late," she said.

"Someone found the notebook," Oscar said. "Theo. He read it. He recognized the first words — he'd heard them once, from his grandmother. He knows the sentence is about the family. He knows it's about the estate."

Ilse was quiet for a moment. Outside, somewhere above them, the city went on not sleeping.

"And what did you tell him?" she asked.

"Nothing. I told him I only had the first three words."

Ilse nodded slowly, and Oscar watched her weigh this, watched her decide — he believed it, or he didn't, and either way it changed nothing, because a man like that does not need the sentence to move; the possibility of the sentence is load-bearing enough.

"Then we work faster," Ilse said, and pulled the card from her handbag — the old card, soft-cornered, her mother's handwriting on it like a scar — and laid it on the table between them. "The middle clause, Oscar. Tonight. We do the middle clause tonight, and we do not stop until it lives in you the way it lives in me."

She looked up, and her eyes, in the basement's yellow light, were clear and terrible and certain.

"Because I think," Ilse said, "that we have less time than I thought."

Oscar sat down. She opened the notebook to a fresh page. Somewhere above them, a door she could not lock stood open onto the night, and below them, under the floor, under the city, under everything, the sentence waited to be finished.
