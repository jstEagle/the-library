## 6. Wren's Handwriting

The envelope was the color of weak tea, and it weighed almost nothing.

I sat in Callum Reyes's truck outside the harbor bar for a long time after he'd gone back inside—long enough that a gull took an interest in me, landed on the hood, and regarded me with the flat professional patience of a creature that has waited out slower prey. I held the envelope on my knees the way you hold something that might be sleeping. The paper was soft at the corners, handled often. Someone had carried this for years, taking it out, putting it away, carrying it again.

My name was on the front. *Juniper.* In handwriting I knew.

That was the thing I couldn't get past, the thing that made my hands unsteady in a way that had nothing to do with cold. I knew that handwriting the way you know a voice through a wall. Wren had written our names on everything when we were kids—not labels exactly, more like claims. *Juniper's room* on my door in pencil. *Wren's half* down the center of the desk we shared, and then, after some negotiation I don't remember, *Wren's half (but Juniper can use it)*. He'd dotted his i's with little circles until he was eleven and decided circles were childish, and even then he sometimes forgot, and there they were now, decades later, two small circles floating over my name like bubbles rising through dark water.

I opened the truck door and was sick onto the gravel, which was undignified but efficient, and then I sat back up and wiped my mouth with the back of my hand and thought: he wrote to me. He wrote to me and he is dead and the letter has been sitting in a stranger's glovebox for however long while I mailed postcards into a hole for eleven years like a woman feeding coins to a well.

The gull left. Even it had better things to do.

I drove back to Ivy's house because it was the only place I had, and because I could not read this letter in a parking lot. Some letters require a roof.

* * *

I made tea I didn't drink. I sat at Ivy's kitchen table under the humming fluorescent tube she'd never replaced because "it still works, doesn't it," which was her philosophy about most things including her hip. I set the envelope down. I picked it up. I put it down again. If grief were an Olympic event, the opening of envelopes would be my discipline; I have trained for years in the art of holding sealed things without breaking them.

Finally I did what I always do with unbearable documents: I started in the middle, where it's less true.

*—and the man who pulled you out was named Ostrander, though I don't think that was really his name either. Nothing about that night used its real name.*

I stopped. Put the page face-down on the table like it might behave better that way. Picked it up again.

Here is what nobody tells you about a dead person's handwriting: it moves. Not literally—I'm not writing a ghost story—but the eye keeps sliding off it, refusing to land, because the hand that made those letters belongs to a boy who died at fourteen, and every instinct I have says that boy cannot have formed a sentence, let alone pages of them, let alone sentences with dry little jokes in them, which these had, God help me, jokes. My brother's ghost had a sense of humor and it was exactly as terrible as I remembered.

I went back to the beginning, properly this time, and read it the way you eat something when you're not sure it will stay down: slowly, in pieces, with pauses.

*June. If you're reading this, then somebody finally asked, which means you know enough to ask, which means I'm sorry. That's the first thing. I'm sorry. I've been sorry so long it's basically my job.*

*I tried to write this eleven times. Callum can confirm. He watched me burn drafts in a coffee can like a Victorian heroine. He said, why don't you just tell her the whole thing straight through, and I said, Callum, have you met our family.*

Our family. Present tense. He'd been dead three years by then, if Callum's arithmetic was right, and still he wrote *our family* like it was a going concern, like the four of us were scattered around some invisible table waiting for him to finish talking.

*So here it is, straight through, or as straight as I can make it. I got out of the house that night. You need to hear that first, because everything else hangs on it. I got out. I was not in my bed. I was downstairs, and I saw things, and that's the part I've spent my whole life trying to unread.*

My tea had gone cold in its mug. I noticed this with the strange gratitude of a person looking for any excuse to stand up and not be reading. I poured it out anyway, watching the leaves spiral down the drain, and stood at the sink for a full minute doing nothing, letting the faucet drip, before I came back.

*You remember the fire a certain way. I know you do, because everyone does—the way it was told to you afterward, over and over, until the telling wore grooves in it and your memory rolled along those grooves like a marble. Mom woke us. She got you out the front. She couldn't find me. That's the story. It's even mostly true. But June, I wasn't lost. I was outside already. I was standing behind the woodpile when the back door opened, and I saw who came out, and I saw what they were carrying, and I heard what Mom said to them, and then I ran, because I was fourteen and I understood, in the wordless animal way fourteen-year-olds understand things, that seeing meant dying.*

Who came out. What they were carrying. I read those lines four times and each time my mind supplied a different picture and rejected it, like a lock testing keys. Behind the woodpile. Our woodpile, the one we stacked every October, Wren on the ground handing up splits and me on top building the wall, arguing about whether the bark side went up or down. He'd stood behind it and watched our mother walk out of a burning house alive, and I had spent eleven years believing she died in it, and he had known—

No. Not yet. I was getting ahead of the letter, ahead of him, and it felt suddenly important to let him speak in order, to give him the sequence he'd chosen, because a boy who burned eleven drafts deserved to have his ordering respected.

*They sent me away the next week. Aunt Ivy's idea, and Mom's, and mine too if I'm honest—I wanted to go, once I understood what staying would mean. There was a family upstate, friends of Ivy's from before, good people, the kind who don't ask questions because they've got questions of their own. New name. New school. I was "Ned" for a year and hated it, then "Theo," which suited me better. I answered to so many names that summer that when someone finally called me Wren I didn't turn around.*

*But here's the part I need you to sit with, June, and I mean sit, actually sit down, because I know you—you're pacing right now, aren't you?*

I looked down. I was standing at the window.

*The night of the fire, before I ran, Mom made me promise something. She grabbed my wrist through the smoke—her grip was unbelievable, I had bruises—and she said: you take care of your sister by staying gone. Not by writing. Not by calling. By being nowhere she can find. And I said, what about you, and she smiled at me, and June, I have thought about that smile every day for twenty years and I still don't have it fully decoded. It wasn't brave. It wasn't sad. It was the smile of somebody who has just finished a very long math problem and gotten the answer she expected.*

I sat down.

*She made me swear I wouldn't tell you I was alive. Not ever, unless you found out some other way and asked. Those were her terms. She said you'd try to come find me, and finding me would lead them to her, and she couldn't—she said she had one more thing she had to do and it required being dead. I didn't understand it then. I want to be clear about that. I was fourteen and concussed and coughing up soot and I agreed to anything with her hand on my wrist. I've had twenty years to understand it and I understand maybe sixty percent. Ask Callum about the rest. Or don't. Some of it you should hear from no one, just arrive at it yourself, the way you arrive at the shape of a room by walking it in the dark.*

I read that paragraph aloud, quietly, just to hear how it sounded in a voice, and hearing it in my own voice was a mistake so total that I laughed—a single cracked note, nothing like laughter—and pressed my fist against my mouth. My mother had been alive. Somewhere in my body, some ledger I hadn't known I kept quietly adjusted itself: eleven years of mourning reclassified, audited, stamped *pending*. And underneath the adjustment, arriving late like a guest to its own party, the other thing: *he knew. He knew she was alive and he let me bury her.*

And beneath *that*, quieter still, in the margin of my own thinking, in handwriting suspiciously like his: *and you let him stay buried, June. Eleven years of postcards. You never once asked anyone to dig.*

* * *

It was fully dark by then. I turned on the kitchen light and the fluorescent tube buzzed and flickered the way it always had, and I thought, absurdly, *Ivy would say it still works,* and I had to put the letter down again and look out the window at the dark yard for a while.

There was more. Of course there was more; the envelope had weight to it, actual pages, seven of them, both sides, his cramped slanted script getting looser toward the end like a man walking downhill. I made myself a deal: two more paragraphs, then food, then the rest. This is a system I use. It works about as well as you'd expect.

*I sent money home every month. You'll have figured that out by now, or Callum will have told you, since apparently he tells people things now, traitor. Don't be angry at him. I made him promise the same thing I promised her: silence until asked. He's a better man than the job required.*

*The money went somewhere specific. I'll let you find it—it's better if you find it, trust me, I've watched people get told things and I've watched people find them and the finding sticks. But I'll say this much: she was not where you thought she was, and she was not what the town said, and the last years of her life were quieter than the ones before, and I paid for the quiet. That was my whole job, June. That was what I did instead of growing up. Other boys learned cars and girls and how to lie to their fathers; I learned wire transfers and PO boxes and how to be nobody in nine towns.*

Nine towns. I thought of the shoebox upstairs in my own bedroom, forty-odd returned postcards filed by year, my tidy archive of grief, and wondered—with a lurch that was half horror and half something dangerously close to comfort—whether any of them had ever been anywhere other than the dead-letter office. Whether he had held them. Whether he'd read *Dear house* and known exactly which house.

*One more thing and then I'll let you breathe. You're going to want to rebuild that night. You're methodical, you always were—you alphabetized the spice rack when you were nine and cried when Dad reshelved it wrong. So you'll take the official story apart and lay the pieces out and try to fit them together, and they won't fit, and I want you to know in advance that this isn't your memory failing. Your memory is fine. It's the story that's broken. They gave you a story the way you give a child a blanket—because you needed covering, not because it was shaped like the truth. Check the details, June. Check the door. Check the neighbor. Check the hour. When the pieces don't fit, believe the pieces.*

Check the door. Check the neighbor. Check the hour.

I stood in Ivy's kitchen reciting it like a catechism, and something old and cold moved through me, because there *was* a detail, wasn't there, one I'd carried for years without examining, the way you carry a stone in a coat pocket you never clean out. The back door. In my memory of that night—which was less a memory than a museum diorama, assembled from other people's descriptions—the back door was locked. Firefighters said so. Locked from the inside. Everyone said it explained everything and nothing, and fourteen-year-old me, wrapped in a neighbor's coat smelling of cigarettes and fabric softener, had accepted it the way children accept weather.

A locked door in a burning house. A brother who was never in his bed. A mother who walked out of a fire and into a new life, smiling like a solved equation.

*Last page, June. I saved the hard part for last because I'm a coward and also because I wanted you to still be reading when you got here.*

I turned the page. My hands were steady, which surprised me; I'd have bet on shaking. Maybe the body knows when it's past the point where shaking helps.

*Don't hate her. Whatever you find—and you'll find it, you're you—don't hate her, and don't hate yourself, and if you must hate someone, hate the man who made all this necessary. She bought your life with hers. I helped her spend it. It was the only family project we ever finished, the three of us, and I need you to know it was done out of love, the fierce kind, the kind that doesn't ask permission.*

*Come see the water sometime. I know you never liked the ocean—it's a soup that bites—but the light out here does something to the morning that I think would fix you a little. It fixed me a little. That's the best I can promise anybody.*

*Your brother,*
*Wren*
*(also Ned, also Theo, also Ashcombe, which was ours first, before it was a secret)*

*P.S. The postcards. Every one. I kept every one. Ask Callum.*

*P.P.S. The circles over the i's came back when I was twenty-two. I decided I'd rather be childish than erased.*

I put the letter down on Ivy's table, aligned its edges with the table edge, squared it like a document in evidence, because my hands needed a job. Then I sat in the buzzing light and let the silence do what it does, which is fill up with everything you've been keeping out of it.

He'd been alive. He'd been alive and reading my postcards—every one, he said, every one—and he'd signed himself *Ashcombe*, the name the stranger had spoken at Ivy's funeral like a key turning in a lock I'd painted shut. The name died with the house, I'd thought. The name had gone underground and kept living, like a bulb.

And the last line of the main text kept surfacing, patient as a buoy: *hate the man who made all this necessary.* Not *the fire*. Not *bad luck, old wiring, cruel chance*. A man. Singular. Definite article, like a landmark on a map everyone else could already read.

I knew who he meant. I had known since I was fourteen, in the wordless animal way, and I had folded that knowing up small and put it in the pocket I never cleaned out, next to the locked back door and the neighbor who vanished by spring and the hour of the fire that never matched the hour of the alarm.

Tomorrow, I thought. Tomorrow I would take the official story apart and lay the pieces out.

Tonight I went upstairs, took the shoebox from the closet, and set it on the bed beside Wren's letter—two archives, his and mine, finally in the same room—and slept, improbably, deeply, like a child who has been read to.
