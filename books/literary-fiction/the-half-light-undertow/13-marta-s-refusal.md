## 13. Marta's Refusal

I had made a life out of not listening. Fifty years of practice will make anyone good at it. I learned to not-listen the way other people learn piano—daily, patiently, with small refinements. I did not listen to the radio when the announcers talked about the reservoir's "recreational potential." I did not listen to the church bell that wasn't ours, imported from a town forty miles inland because ours was under forty feet of water. I did not listen, most of all, to the memory of a woman singing on a flatbed truck with her hands open like she was letting birds go, because listening to that memory meant asking what had happened afterward, and I had spent half a century declining to ask.

So when Felix Eriksen came up my gravel path on a Tuesday in October carrying a canvas bag that clinked and rustled like it was full of small, nervous animals, my first instinct was the old one. Don't listen. Offer him coffee. Talk about the weather, which on the reservoir shore is always either rain or the threat of it, and thereby say nothing at all for an hour, and send him home.

But he was carrying his mother's face in front of him like a lantern, and you cannot not-listen a lantern.

"You knew," he said, standing at the bottom of my porch steps, not coming up, which I noted. He had manners. His mother had manners. His father had the kind of manners that are just a wall with a coat of paint on it. "You knew the song was recorded. You knew Vane's name was on the tape."

I knew a great many things. I said, "There's coffee on," which in my grammar means: yes, and come up, and we will do the thing we have both been dreading.

* * *

My kitchen has one window and it faces the water, which is not a view so much as a sentence I am forced to read every morning. The reservoir. They gave it a Norwegian name, Kaldsvann, cold water, as if the cold were the notable thing about it, as if the notable thing weren't that beneath it lay a churchyard, a schoolhouse, an orchard, my grandmother's loom, and the foundation stone of a house my father built with his own hands and his own bad back. Cold water. As if the crime were a temperature.

Felix sat at my table with his hands around a mug he wasn't drinking from. He had the tapes in the canvas bag, two of them, reel to reel, and a digital recorder besides, because the young archivist—Noor, he said her name was, and he said it the way you say the name of someone who has done you an enormous kindness—had made copies. He explained it all again, though he'd already told me on the phone, and I let him explain, because I understood he wasn't explaining to me. He was explaining to himself. The story still didn't fit in his mouth. Vane, his benefactor, his mentor, the man who had paid for his education and his apartment and his whole gilded second life—that man had been the company's linguist, the one who came to the valley with a microphone and a smile and left with our refusal in his pocket, sold and delivered.

"He recorded her," Felix said. "He recorded her refusing. And then he—" He stopped. He looked at the window, at the water, at the sentence he couldn't finish.

"He testified," I said. "That's the word they used. He testified that the villagers were offered fair compensation and refused it out of stubbornness. That we were unreasonable people. That our attachment to the land was sentimental and incoherent." I turned my mug a quarter turn on the table, a habit, a little ritual of keeping my hands busy so they wouldn't do something else. "A song is not a legal document, Felix. But a song that says *this valley belongs to us and we will not go*—that's a claim. A claim you can't file in a court, so you erase it in a courtroom instead. He gave them the eraser."

"And then he found me," Felix said. "After my parents died. He found me and paid for everything."

"Yes."

"Why?"

I looked at him for a long time. He was forty-one years old and asking a question a child could have answered, and the reason no one had answered it for him in thirty years was that everyone who could have was either dead or me.

"Because a man who buries a thing likes to keep a key to the grave," I said. "And because guilt, in men like that, doesn't look like guilt. It looks like generosity. He couldn't give the valley back, so he gave you a life. He's been paying installments on a debt for thirty years and calling it patronage."

Felix put his head down on the table, briefly, like a man resting between blows. Then he straightened and took the tapes out of the bag and set them on the wood between us, and the kitchen got very quiet, the way it does when the weather changes.

"I need you to listen with me," he said. "I've listened alone. I can't do it twice alone."

* * *

I will tell you what I expected and then what happened, because the difference is the whole story.

I expected to weep. I am seventy-eight years old and I have wept at insurance commercials; I fully expected the voice of Ingrid Eriksen coming out of a machine in my kitchen to undo me entirely. I had braced for it the way you brace for cold water. I had even, I'm ashamed to say, rehearsed a small dignity about it, some arrangement of my face that would permit grief without surrendering to it.

What happened instead was this: I listened to the voice of a woman I had loved like a sister, a woman fifty years dead, and I felt—nothing. A flatness. A white calm, like the sky over the water in February. She was arguing with a young man whose voice I recognized with a shock that went through me like a fishhook—Aldous Vane, thirty years old, courteous, reasonable, his vowels polished by a university—and she was refusing him, and she was magnificent, and I sat there listening to my own history played back to me and felt as though I were reading someone else's mail.

"Listen to how he talks," Felix whispered. "He's *kind* to her. That's the worst part. He's kind."

"He was kind," I said. "That was the whole technology of it. Nobody in that valley was ever shouted at. We were *understood*. We were *appreciated*. The company had a whole vocabulary for us. Our attachment. Our traditions. Our understandable reluctance. They talked to us the way you talk to children at a funeral—gently, and with no intention of changing the plan."

On the tape, Ingrid said—her voice thinner than I remembered, or my memory had thickened her—the words I had carried in my head for fifty years without once playing them at full volume. She told Vane the valley was not compensation's to buy. She told him the song was not folklore, it was *title*. She sang the first verse, right there, into his microphone, daring him to record it, and he did, the fool, the villain, the instrument—he recorded the very thing he'd been sent to bury, because he was young and arrogant and it did not occur to him that a refusal could be evidence for us as well as against us.

Then she sang the last line. The one Felix couldn't parse. The one with the verb.

And Felix's hand came across the table and gripped my wrist, hard, an old man's strength in a young hand, and he said, "Stop it. Stop the tape," and Noor's copy machine whirred somewhere in my memory and I reached out and stopped the tape because he asked, and the kitchen filled back up with its own silence.

"She's saying it," he said. His eyes were wet and furious. "She's saying the sentence and I can hear every word and I understand *none of it*. It's like—Marta, it's like watching someone write in a language I used to dream in. I know the sounds. I can sing it back. I cannot tell you what it *says*. The grammar is gone. The verb—" He made a sound that was half laugh and entirely grief. "The verb can mean *to forgive* or it can mean *to hand over*. Those are the only two readings left. And they're opposites. Do you understand? My whole life turns on a word that can only mean two opposite things, and I've lost the third thing, the thing it actually meant, and everyone who knew is—" He looked at me.

Everyone who knew is me.

* * *

Here is what I did instead of answering him. I got up and made more coffee nobody wanted. I washed a pan that was already clean. I did all the small domestic evasions that fifty years had made fluent in me, and Felix sat at my table and let me, because he is a translator and he knows what it looks like when someone is struggling to render a sentence from a language they'd rather not speak.

Because here is the truth, and I have carried it the way you carry a stone in your boot on a long walk—so long you've forgotten it's there, except that you walk crooked.

I knew what the verb meant. I have always known. Ingrid and I were girls together in the valley; we learned our grammar from the same grandmother, in the same kitchen, with the same stick across our knuckles when we got it wrong. The old language had a structure for obligation that the new tongues burned out of us—it had a form of the verb that only existed between people bound to each other, a form that said *this action is carried between us and does not end with me*. And the verb in Ingrid's last line was in that form. Not *I forgive you*. Not *I hand it over*. The form meant: *I carry it forward, and now it is yours to carry, and I am watching to see what you do with it.*

It was not forgiveness. It was not surrender. It was a hand on the back of the neck and a voice saying: *this is yours now, and I will know.*

And I knew it, and I did not tell him, and I want you to understand why, because it is not a flattering reason and I am too old to flatter myself.

I didn't tell him because telling him would make it real. If the sentence meant *carry it forward*, then it meant the debt was alive, and the debt had a heir, and the heir was sitting in my kitchen with his mother's face and Vane's money in his pocket. If I told him, then I would have to stop not-listening. Fifty years of chosen silence, and I had gotten so good at it that I'd begun to mistake the silence for peace, the way you mistake a numb limb for a comfortable one.

Also—this is the smaller, meaner truth—I was afraid. Vane's foundation had paid for my roof when the storm took the old one. Not much. A grant. "Preservation of regional heritage," the letter said, and I took it, and I knew what it was, and I took it anyway. We all took things. That was how the drowning worked, in the end—not the water, the taking. They didn't have to hold us under. They just had to make sure that by the time we understood what had happened, our hands were full.

* * *

"Tell me," Felix said.

I stood at the window with my back to him. The reservoir did its flat silver nothing. Somewhere under it, the orchard. Somewhere under it, the schoolhouse bell, which they never salvaged because it was easier to buy a new bell than to raise an old one. That is the whole engineering philosophy of the twentieth century in one sentence, if you want it.

"The verb," I said. "The one you can't parse. You're looking for it in the wrong tense of your life. You're looking in the language they let us keep—the market language, the weather language, the language of buying bread and naming fish. The form she used only existed in the binding grammar. The grammar we used for vows. For debts between kin. For—" I stopped.

"For what," Felix said, very quietly.

"For passing a thing from the dead to the living," I said. "It doesn't mean forgive. It doesn't mean hand over. Those are what it looks like when you drag it into a language that only has two directions for giving—toward and away. The form she used means *to carry forward*. It means: I do not release this and I do not keep it. I place it in you. It becomes your weight and your inheritance and your answerability. And the grammar marks who is watching. She wasn't forgiving the company, Felix. She was *deposing* them. She was putting the valley in your hands in front of witnesses and saying, in the old form, *I will know what you do.*"

The silence after that was the longest of my life. I could hear the reservoir not saying anything. I could hear my own heart doing its stubborn old work.

When I finally turned around, Felix was crying—not loudly, not the way the young cry, with witnesses and drama, but the way the old cry, alone, in kitchens, with the water running so no one hears. Except he wasn't hiding it. He let it run down his face and drip off his chin onto my table, and he didn't wipe it away, and I thought: Ingrid, look at him. Look what they gave you back.

"She was leaving it to me," he said. "The whole time. The song was a will."

"The song was a will," I said. "And Vane spent fifty years convincing you it was a lullaby."

* * *

He stayed until dark. We drank the coffee I kept making, and somewhere in the third hour the flatness in me broke, as I knew it would, and I wept after all—late, undignified, fifty years overdue—and Felix held my hand across the table with his mother's face shining out of him, and neither of us said anything for a while, because there is a grammar for that too, and we were both fluent.

Before he left, at the bottom of the porch steps, he turned around.

"The grammar," he said. "The binding form. Is it—could it be reconstructed? From the songs? From what's left? Because if the sentence indicts them—if the old grammar marks who must answer—then the sentence itself is evidence. Not sentiment. *Evidence.*"

I looked at this man, this translator who had spent his life rendering other people's meanings into other people's words, and I saw him understand, all at once, that his mother had left him not a memory but a task, and that the task was written in a language only the dead and I could read.

"It can be rebuilt," I said. "Slowly. From the songs. From what my grandmother beat into us before they beat it out. It will take everything you have."

"Good," Felix said, and he said it the way Ingrid used to say it, flat and bright at once, and then he went down my gravel path into the dark with the tapes in his bag, carrying his inheritance like a lantern after all.

I went inside and did not wash the cups. I left them on the table, two cups, facing each other, and I sat looking at them for a long time, and for the first time in fifty years I was not not-listening. I was waiting. Somewhere out there in the half-light, a man was about to start rebuilding the grammar of the drowned, and I knew—old woman's knowing, grandmother's knowing, the kind they can't buy with a foundation grant—exactly whose name was going to surface first when the water finally gave up its dead.
