## 17. Publication Eve

The kitchen in Marta's house smelled of cardamom and old wood, and Noor had spread the documents across the table like a fortune-teller who'd given up on cards and gone straight to evidence.

"Okay," she said, for the fourth time that evening. "One more time. The translation goes up at six a.m. on the archive's independent server — my server now, technically, since I own the domain and the archive owns nothing anymore but its reputation, which is about to become a historical curiosity. The audio files go up with it. Both tapes. Unedited. Then the press release to eleven outlets, timed for seven."

"Eight," Marta said.

Noor looked up from her checklist. "Sorry?"

"You said eleven outlets. Send it to eight." Marta was peeling an apple in one unbroken ribbon, which I had come to understand was how she did everything — completely, and without any visible interest in whether anyone was watching. "The other three are owned by people who will sit on it until they've decided how to sell it."

I had known Marta for perhaps three weeks and she had already reorganized my understanding of journalism, grief, and apples.

"She's right," I said.

"I know she's right," Noor said, crossing out three names with a pen stroke that had some violence in it. "That's not why I'm annoyed. I'm annoyed because I spent two days cultivating those contacts."

"You can cultivate them again after," Marta said. "Cultivation is what young people do instead of patience."

Noor muttered something in a language I didn't recognize, which, given the last several months of my life, felt less like an insult and more like a theme.

Here is what we were doing, laid out on a kitchen table under a single bulb: forty pages of translation. Two digitized tapes. A grammatical appendix I had written at four in the morning over eleven consecutive nights, reconstructing a structure my mother's language used for accountability — who speaks, who receives, who must answer — a structure so precise it could name a corporation inside a sentence the way a hand closes around a wrist. And one verb, the verb, the one that had cost me half a year and most of my certainties: *to carry forward*. Not forgive. Not surrender. My mother had stood in front of the company men and told them, in front of a microphone Vane himself had set up, that her people would carry their claim forward into whatever came next, and Vane had sold that recording as proof of unreasonable stubbornness, and fifty years later I had finally heard what she actually said.

Tomorrow it would be public. Tonight it was still ours.

There is a particular quality to the night before you destroy your own life on purpose. It isn't dread, exactly. Dread assumes you don't want what's coming. This was more like the stillness of a house the day before you move out — every object suddenly legible, every room announcing what it had been for. I kept thinking about my apartment in the city. The good desk Vane's fellowship money bought. The window that faced nothing in particular but faced it expensively. The notebook of dying words, which I had brought with me and which sat on Marta's shelf now like a rescued animal, waiting to find out if it was a pet or a specimen.

"Felix." Noor snapped her fingers. "You went somewhere."

"The apartment," I said. "I was inventorying it."

"Keep the desk," Marta said. "Desks are innocent."

* * *

We worked until ten. Noor built the upload twice, tested it against a dummy server, encrypted the originals and stashed copies in places she refused to specify even to us — "the fewer people who know where the bodies are buried, the fewer people who can be persuaded to dig them up" — and Marta made tea that tasted like something grown deliberately, stubbornly, in poor soil.

It was during the second pot that my phone rang.

I should explain what that phone had become. Since the archives, since the tapes, since Vane's first summons, it had taken on the quality of a weather instrument — I checked it the way farmers check barometers, reading pressure rather than messages. Most of the pressure lately came from colleagues. Three emails from the institute's director asking me to "clarify my research direction." One from a journal withdrawing an invitation to review. One, oddly, from a man I'd translated manuals beside for nine years, saying only: *heard rumors. hope they're wrong.* People smell smoke long before the fire, and they write to you about the smoke because writing about the fire would require taking a side.

But this call was from a number I knew by heart, because I had dialed it gratefully for twenty years.

Marta and Noor both looked at me. I picked up.

"Felix." His voice was warm the way radiators are warm — mechanically, at a cost. "I hoped I'd catch you before tomorrow."

"Before tomorrow," I said. As if tomorrow were a train.

"I'll be brief. I know you're busy. I know what you're planning." A pause, letting me hear that he knew precisely, down to the server timestamps, which meant someone at the archive had talked, which meant Noor's paranoia had been not paranoia but bookkeeping. "And I'm calling because I would rather this end tonight, between us, than tomorrow, between lawyers."

"Between lawyers," I repeated. "You've been rehearsing that sentence. It has a nice balance."

"Felix." He sighed, and the sigh was masterful — it contained disappointment, affection, weariness, and just enough self-accusation to seem human. Twenty years ago that sigh had made me feel like a disappointing son. Tonight it sounded like a recording of a sigh, played by a man who had once recorded other people's refusals and sold them. "I'm going to offer you everything one more time, and then I'm going to tell you what happens if you refuse, and I want you to hear both halves all the way through, because you owe me that much attention if nothing else."

I sat down. Marta kept peeling her apple. Noor turned her phone face-down on the table, which I understood to mean: *keep him talking.*

"Say it," I said.

"The institute passes to whoever succeeds me. You know this. My health is what it is — the doctors use words like 'manage' and I use words like 'how long.' If you walk away from this madness, the directorship is yours within the year. The archive becomes yours. The funding, the presses, the whole apparatus I spent fifty years building — yours, Felix, to do with what you like. You could fund field recordings in every dying language on the coast. You could do more good in ten years than—" he caught himself, elegantly, "—than either of us has done alone. All of it, if the song stays untranslated. Or translates itself, as songs do, into folklore, into mist. Songs survive better without footnotes."

"And if I publish?"

Another pause. When he spoke again the warmth had been switched off at the meter.

"Then I sue. For breach of confidentiality — you read those tapes under access granted through my foundation, and my lawyers assure me the terms are elastic enough to hang a man with. For defamation, if your little appendix insists on naming names, and it does, because you were always a literal boy. The litigation lasts years. You have no institutional protection; the moment you publish, every colleague who ever owed me a favor discovers their principles. Journals stop returning your calls. Conferences develop scheduling conflicts. You will finish your career translating appliance warranties in rented rooms, and you will do it alone, because the profession is a village too, Felix, and villages exile people who dig up the church floor." He let that sit. Then, softer, almost tender: "I gave you a language when yours died. I gave you a life. Is this what you do with gifts?"

And here is the thing I want to get right, because it matters: for about four seconds, I wavered.

Not because the institute tempted me — though it did, God help me, it gleamed like a lit window in winter. But because he was right about the gift. He had paid for my education after my parents died. He had taught me phonetics, ethics of translation, how to hold a dead language gently without embalming it. Every good thing in my professional life ran through him like current through wire. To refuse him felt, at the cellular level, like ingratitude — like biting the hand that had fed me back into existence.

But that was exactly the architecture, wasn't it. That was the whole design. A debt so large and so early that questioning it felt like self-harm. He hadn't given me a life. He had given me a lease, and called it a deed, and I had spent twenty years paying rent on my own gratitude.

"Marta's here," I said.

A silence on the line. "Is she."

"She says hello. She also says — no, actually, she hasn't said anything yet. But she's going to say something devastating in the old language at some point, and I thought you'd want fair warning, since comprehension is apparently optional in your line of work."

"Felix—"

"You recorded my mother refusing you," I said. "You recorded her carrying forward, and you sold it as stubbornness, and you built an empire on the difference between those two words. I spent half a year learning to hear that difference. Do you know what the strange part is? I'm not even angry anymore. Anger needs someone who could have done otherwise, and listening to those tapes, I don't think you ever could. I think you were born a linguist and became a landlord."

"That's very fine," he said coldly. "Very literary. It will sound less fine in a deposition."

"Probably. I've never been good in rooms where other people control the grammar."

"Then hear the last of it. Publish, and you lose everything I built for you. Everything. There is no version of tomorrow in which you remain in this profession. None. Think about—"

I hung up mid-sentence.

Not dramatically — there was no slam, no flourish. I pressed the red circle the way you close a door on a room you've already emptied. The screen said *Call Ended*, which struck me as the funniest thing the phone had ever displayed.

Marta looked at me over her apple. "Well?"

"He offered me the world and then threatened to take away the part of it he owns."

"Which is most of it," she said.

"Most of it," I agreed. "But not the part with the apple."

For the first time in the weeks I'd known her, Marta smiled fully — a quick, crooked, entirely unsaintly expression that made her look seventeen for a moment. "Good," she said. "Now help me finish these. Tomorrow we'll need our hands steady."

* * *

Noor left at midnight, walking to the bus stop with a hard drive in her coat pocket and her chin up like a woman carrying coals to Newcastle out of principle. Marta went to bed, telling me the light would still work in the morning, which was either comfort or physics.

I stayed at the table.

The final proof of the translation lay in front of me, and I read it through one last time, not checking the grammar — the grammar was done, reconstructed, defensible, mine — but checking myself. Somewhere in the middle pages my mother's voice moved through the sentences, the protest voice, the voice from the tape, and near the end came the sentence itself, the one with the verb, rendered now as she had meant it:

*We do not forgive, and we do not surrender. We carry you forward, and you will answer for what you carry.*

Below it, the appendix explained the structure: how the language assigned every utterance a speaker and a debtor, how accountability lived in the grammar the way load-bearing walls live in a house, invisible until you try to remove them. How a company could be named inside a sentence of grief.

I thought about the notebook on the shelf. Years of dying words, collected like a landlord keeping receipts for a house he no longer owned — that was how I'd thought of it, anyway, in the gray city, in the good desk's shadow. But sitting in Marta's kitchen with the manuscript finished and the tapes loaded and Vane's threat still warm in the phone's memory, I understood I'd had the metaphor backwards. I had never been the landlord. I had been the tenant — of Vane's generosity, of the profession's approval, of a borrowed life furnished with other people's furniture. And tomorrow, at six a.m., the lease ended.

What frightened me wasn't losing the apartment, the fellowship, the name I'd made. What frightened me was how small the fear was next to the other thing — the thing underneath, which was not fear at all but something with the same posture and opposite sign. Relief, maybe. Or readiness. Or the feeling, familiar from childhood, of standing at the edge of water you've finally decided to enter.

I wrote one line in the notebook before bed. Not a dying word. I'll tell you what it was another time, or maybe I won't, because some entries aren't for the record.

At 5:40 in the morning, Noor's message arrived: *Uploading. Server's clean. Press release queued.*

At 5:58, Marta put tea in front of me without being asked.

At 6:00 exactly, the translation went live, and somewhere in the dark above a drowned valley, the sentence my mother sang began traveling outward at the speed of light toward eleven mailboxes minus three, and there was no version of the world in which I could call it back.

I didn't want one.

By 7:15, the first responses were landing — a journalist's terse acknowledgment, a stranger's confused question, and one email with a subject line that made both women look up from their cups at the same time.

It was from the institute's legal office. Subject: *Immediate Cease and Desist — and One Enclosure.*

There was an attachment. Neither of us opened it right away. Outside, the reservoir lay flat and silver in the early light, holding its drowned village the way it always had — patiently, and without forgiving anyone.

Marta reached across the table and turned the laptop toward herself.

"Well," she said. "Let's see what the old man sent."
