## 2. The Inventory of Unsaid Things

The notebook was a school exercise book, the kind sold in packs of ten at the stationery shop on Delmas Street, with a marbled cover that flaked blue wax onto his fingers whenever he opened it. Ulyana had bought it in the autumn after the fire, when he was twenty-two and still sleeping in apartments he could not describe to anyone without lying about how he'd come to afford them. He had filled eleven pages in the first week and then slowed, the way a penitent slows, because confession is easy in bulk and unbearable in detail.

Fifteen years later the notebook lived in the bottom drawer of his desk, beneath a false bottom made from a cut folder, beneath the spare keys to an apartment he no longer sublet. He took it out every Sunday evening, after the postcard was written and stamped, and read it the way other men read scripture: not for information but for structure. It was the only text that explained him to himself.

*Entry one,* it began, in ink gone brown at the edges. *Father broke the dining chair over the table because Vivienne set her fork down wrong. Mother swept up the wood before dinner was finished. We ate standing.*

He had written it flatly, without adjectives, as though the facts were specimens pinned under glass. That had been the discipline of it — no weeping, no accusation, no plea. Just the inventory. The unsaid things, catalogued so they would stop circulating in his blood like unspent currency.

There were two hundred and six entries. He knew the number the way he knew his own shoe size. Some were single lines; some ran pages. Entry forty-one covered three days in June of the year Vivienne turned sixteen, and even now he could not read all of it in one sitting. Entry ninety-eight was a single sentence he had written and then crossed out and then rewritten so many times the paper had thinned to translucence: *Mother saw the bruise on my wrist and asked if I'd been careless with the door.*

He did not open the drawer on the evening the lawyer's second letter arrived. He opened it because it was Sunday, and ritual is the spine of a man who has otherwise no skeleton. But the letter lay on the desk where he'd left it that morning, its cream stock catching the lamplight, and the notebook seemed heavier than usual, as if the entries had gained weight in the week since he'd last consulted them.

He sat. He turned past the early entries, past the years of chairs and doors and the particular silence their mother kept — not absence of sound but presence of refusal, a silence with texture, like felt — until he reached the entries concerning Vivienne.

*Entry 112. V. left for the coast on the 6:40 bus. She did not say goodbye. Mother packed her a sandwich anyway and stood at the window until the street went dark. Nobody spoke her name again for four months.*

*Entry 113. I have decided to write down everything she would have said if she had stayed. This is presumptuous. I am doing it regardless.*

That had been the beginning of the postcards, though he hadn't understood it then. The notebook was the ledger; the postcards were the tithes. Every Sunday he paid something out to the burned house — a small truth, a small cowardice, addressed to a lot where no mailbox stood — and the notebook recorded what remained unpaid.

Tonight he read entry 114, and 115, and the cluster around 130 where he had tried, once, to write down what their father had done in the last winter, the winter before the fire, and found his hand refusing. There was a page there where the ink simply stopped mid-word, a pen lifted and never returned to the sentence. He touched the gap now with one finger, the way you touch a missing tooth.

Outside, the city performed its Sunday hush. A siren rose somewhere near the river and gave up. The radiator ticked through its cooling like a slow clock counting nothing.

Ulyana closed the notebook and, instead of returning it to the drawer, laid it flat on the desk beside the lawyer's letter. The letter concerned the auction — terms, deposits, the date circled in someone else's confident pen — but what had stopped him that morning was a paragraph near the end, written in the dry courtesy lawyers use when delivering bad news about other people:

*Please be advised that our office has also been retained by a second interested party with respect to the Delacroix lot. We are obligated to inform you of this potential conflict should you wish to seek independent counsel.*

A second party. He had read the sentence four times and each time it had rearranged itself into a different shape of dread. There was no one else. The family was a subtraction sum: father dead, mother dead, house ash, and Vivienne —

Vivienne, who had left on the 6:40 bus and never sent a forwarding address. Vivienne, whose name he had not spoken aloud in nine years except once, drunk, to a bartender who hadn't been listening. Vivienne, who had inherited nothing but the same blood and therefore the same claim.

He picked up the phone before he could talk himself out of it, which was unlike him, and dialed the law office, which was more unlike him still. A paralegal answered, young, bored, mercifully incurious.

"The second party," Ulyana said. "For the Delacroix lot. Can you tell me who retained counsel?"

A pause, the sound of keys. "I'm sorry, that information isn't — " Another pause. "Actually, it's public record once a bid registration's filed. Hold on." Keys again. Then, flatly, as if reading a weather report: "Delacroix, V. Registered Tuesday."

He thanked her in a voice he didn't recognize and put the phone down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

So. Fifteen years of believing himself the sole archivist of the family's ruin, the lone monk copying out the sins in a cell of one — and all that time Vivienne had been keeping books of her own. Perhaps not marbled exercise books. Perhaps not entries at all. Perhaps hers was kept in the body, in the way she must have flinched at certain words, certain kitchens, certain fathers' voices on strangers. But she had registered a bid. She had hired a lawyer. She had come back into the record after nine years of silence, and she had done it quietly, on a Tuesday, while he was writing his weekly postcard to a house that no longer existed.

He thought: *she has an inventory too.* And beneath the thought, colder: *she has had one all along, and mine may be incomplete.*

This was the vertigo of it. For fifteen years he had believed the notebook exhaustive. Two hundred and six entries, every silence mapped. But a catalogue written by one witness is not a history; it is a testimony, and testimonies have edges, and beyond the edges lie the things the other person saw that you did not. What had Vivienne seen from her side of the table? What had she heard through her bedroom wall that he, sleeping nearer the stairs, had missed? What had she known about their mother — about the felt-textured silences, the sandwiches packed for daughters who wouldn't say goodbye — that he had filed under *complicity* when it might have belonged under some word he didn't own?

He got up and walked to the shelf above his desk, where the unsent drafts lived in a cigar box: forty-odd postcards never mailed because they were too true or too false, he could never tell which. He fanned them like a losing hand. On the top one, written three years ago in a moment of weakness he had immediately regretted: *I think you remember the fire differently than I do, and I think one of us is lying, and I am afraid it is me.*

He had sealed it, stamped it, and put it in the box instead of the slot outside. Cowardice, yes. But also this: he had not wanted to know which of them was lying. The not-knowing had been load-bearing. Remove it and the whole architecture of his quiet life came down.

Now the lawyer's letter sat on the desk like a demolition notice.

* * *

He dreamed that night of the kitchen, which he rarely did anymore, and in the dream the dreamer was not himself but Vivienne — he knew this the way you know things in dreams, without evidence — and the kitchen was whole, unburned, the yellow wallpaper with its pattern of failing pears, and their mother stood at the sink with her back to them both, washing a single glass over and over, and neither child could make her turn around. In the dream Ulyana-Vivienne tried to speak and produced only the sound of the notebook's pages turning, hundreds of pages, a wind of entries, and their mother said, without turning, without stopping the water: *You can write it all down. It won't make it true.*

He woke at four with the lamp still on and the notebook still open on the desk to entry 114, the one about the sandwich, and he understood suddenly that he had gotten it wrong. Not the facts — the facts were right, the facts were always right, that was the trap of him. He had gotten the *weight* wrong. He had written *Nobody spoke her name again for four months* as though the silence were the wound. But the silence had been the bandage. Underneath, all those months, the household had been speaking her name constantly, in every creak of the stairs, in every place setting set for three by habit and then corrected to two, in their mother's hands pausing over the pear wallpaper. The inventory recorded what wasn't said. It had never once recorded what the not-saying meant.

And if he had misread that — the easiest entry, the safest entry — what else had he misfiled? Entry forty-one? Entry ninety-eight? The page where the ink stopped mid-word?

He sat in the gray beginning of morning and did the arithmetic he had avoided for fifteen years: a sister with a lawyer, a lot going under the hammer, six weeks, and between them a fire that each of them had carried alone on the theory that the other had started it.

He took out a fresh postcard — the plain kind, unillustrated, bought in bulk — and wrote the address from memory, the address that was now a fenced lot with weeds coming up through the foundation seams. He wrote: *I found out you're bidding. I don't know yet whether that makes us opponents or the only two people at the same funeral. Come to the open house. Bring your version. I'll bring mine.*

Then he did what he had never done in fifteen years of Sundays. He did not seal it, and he did not file it in the cigar box with the other confessions too dangerous or too dishonest to send. He stamped it, carried it down three flights in his slippers, and posted it in the blue box on the corner, hearing it land among the anonymous mail of the living.

Walking back, breath steaming, he passed the reroute notice stapled to the bus shelter — BUS 44, SERVICE ADJUSTMENT EFFECTIVE NEXT MONTH — and stopped, because the notice meant the last direct line to the old neighborhood was dying, and whatever reckoning was coming would have to arrive on foot.

Six weeks, he thought. Two hundred and six entries. One of us is lying.

He went home to find out which.
