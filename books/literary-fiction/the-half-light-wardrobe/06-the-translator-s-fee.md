## 6. The Translator's Fee

Marisol Vidal worked out of the back room of a shuttered bookbindery on Rue Charlemagne, a space that smelled of glue and old leather and the particular dust of languages that had stopped being spoken. Ulyana found her through a listing pinned to the corkboard of a community center he had never otherwise entered—a card, handwritten, *Translation: literary, legal, personal. No machine work.* The word *personal* had snagged him like a fishhook.

She was smaller than he expected, seventy if she was a day, with white hair cropped close and reading glasses pushed up into it like a second crown. Her hands were stained faintly blue at the fingertips, ink worked so deep into the skin it seemed less like residue than like circulation.

"You have something for me," she said, not as a question, when he stood in her doorway holding the rolled paper inside a plastic sleeve like a relic.

"A sentence." He set it on her worktable, beneath the lamp. "It's in my mother's handwriting. I don't know what language."

Marisol put on her glasses and bent over the paper without touching it, the way one approaches a sleeping animal. The sentence ran in a tight, slanted script, the letters leaning eastward as if walking into wind. She read it twice. Then she took off her glasses and looked at him over the rims.

"Romanian," she said. "With some Hungarian spelling habits. People who grew up near borders write like this—two countries arguing inside every word." She paused. "Do you want me to tell you what it says?"

"Yes."

"No, you don't," she said mildly. "But you will. Not today." She slid the paper back across the table. "Come back Thursday. Bring me the story of why this matters, and then I'll bring you the words."

"I'm paying you for a translation, not a conversation."

"Then find another translator." She said it without heat, the way one states the weather. "I've been doing this forty years. A sentence taken out of its story is a corpse without a name. I can give you the words, but they'll mean nothing, and you'll pay me, and we'll both pretend that was the transaction. I don't do that anymore. I'm too old to pretend efficiently."

Ulyana stood there, holding his dead mother's handwriting, feeling the peculiar vertigo of a man whose carefully constructed invisibility has just been seen through by a stranger in ninety seconds.

"Thursday," he said.

* * *

He came back Thursday because he could not think of a reason not to, and that absence of reason frightened him more than any argument she might have made. He brought nothing. He sat in the chair across from her worktable, among the bound spines and the brass tools, and waited for her interrogation to begin.

It did not begin. Marisol worked at another table for twenty minutes, letting him sit in the silence until it softened, until it became almost companionable. Then she set down her brush and said, "Tell me about the house."

"It burned down fifteen years ago."

"Before or after your mother wrote this?"

"After. It survived the fire. Inside the wardrobe frame."

"So someone hid it before the fire, or during it." She turned her chair toward him. "Which?"

"I don't know."

"You're lying," she said, without accusation, almost with tenderness. "You suspect. There's a difference between not knowing and refusing to know, and your face is doing the second one." She folded her hands. "Start anywhere. Start with the fire. What did you see?"

And here was the thing Ulyana had built his whole adult life to avoid: a question asked by someone who would actually wait for the answer. His postcards had never required answers. That was their entire genius—he could confess to an address that could not reply, could pour himself out weekly into a mailbox and receive nothing back but the mute courtesy of the postal system. Fifteen years of confession with zero risk of being known.

"The smoke," he said finally. "I remember the smoke more than the flames. It came out of the windows like the house exhaling."

"Were you inside?"

"No."

"Were you supposed to be?"

The question went through him like a needle finding the exact center of a bruise. He looked at his hands. "I was fourteen. I should have been at my aunt's. I came back early."

"Why?"

"I don't—"

"You keep saying that." Marisol's voice stayed level, unhurried. "You know, in my work, when a text repeats a phrase, that repetition is never accidental. It's structure. It's the author telling you where the weight sits. You've said *I don't know* four times since you sat down. Where does the weight sit, Ulyana?"

No one had used his name yet. Hearing it in her mouth, accented slightly, made him feel suddenly, terribly located—as if he had spent years as a postcard with no return address and she had just written one on his back.

"My father," he said. The word came out of him like a splinter leaving skin. "Something about my father. The sentence—I think it's about my father. What he did. And my mother knew, and she—" He stopped.

"Stayed?" Marisol offered quietly.

His head came up. "You translated it already."

"I told you. I read it the first day." She removed her glasses entirely now, and her eyes were brown and very old and very kind. "I wanted to see how long you'd sit across from me knowing I knew. Most people last ten minutes. You lasted two visits. That tells me the sentence isn't information to you—it's a verdict you've been serving since you were a child."

The room tilted slightly. Ulyana gripped the arm of the chair. Being seen, he discovered, felt exactly like drowning and breathing at once—the same violent intake, the same burning, the same refusal to come up for anything but more.

"There was a night," he heard himself say, from somewhere far away, "the winter before the fire. My father came home and Vivienne and I—we heard things. We always heard things. And afterward my mother sat at the kitchen table and she didn't cry, she just... straightened the salt shakers. Over and over. Like she was aligning the house with some axis only she could see. And I understood, even at thirteen, that she was deciding something. And whatever she decided, she decided to stay, and we all lived inside that decision like weather."

"And the fire?"

"The fire came later. After she was gone." He swallowed. "Or—no. Before. I'm confusing the order. Isn't that strange? Fifteen years of writing to that house and I can't keep the order straight anymore."

"It's not strange," Marisol said. "It's the most honest thing you've said. Memory doesn't fail people who are telling the truth. It fails people carrying two versions." She rose, went to a drawer, and returned with a blank sheet of good paper and a pen, which she laid before him. "Write the sentence out yourself. In your own hand. Copying a text teaches you things reading doesn't."

He wrote it slowly, letter by letter, imitating his mother's eastward lean, and watching his own hand produce her script produced in him a grief so specific it felt architectural—grief with load-bearing walls.

"When will you give me the full translation?" he asked.

Marisol took the paper, studied his copy beside the original, and was quiet for a long moment.

"Thursday after next," she said. "There's a word in it I want to check against older usage. It can mean *stayed* or it can mean *remained behind*—and in your mother's hand, the difference might be everything." She looked up at him. "But I'll tell you this much now, free of charge: whoever wrote this wasn't confessing. Confessions are written to be found by the people you wronged. This was written to survive you both. Do you understand the difference?"

He didn't. He nodded anyway.

At the door, Marisol touched his sleeve—briefly, lightly, the way one steadies a lamp.

"Your sister," she said. "Does she know about the paper?"

The question landed like ice water. "How did you—"

"You flinched when I said *verdict*. People flinch like that when the verdict has a co-defendant." She smiled, small and tired. "Thursday after next. And Ulyana—bring the true order of events. Not the one you've been mailing around all these years."

He walked out into the gray afternoon with his mother's sentence burning in his pocket like a coal, and his phone buzzed against his hip: a message from the auction house, confirming the open house viewing on Saturday—and a second notification beneath it, from a number he hadn't saved in fifteen years.

Vivienne.

*I know you have it,* the message read. *We need to talk before Saturday.*

* * *

He did not answer. He walked instead to the old neighborhood, uncharacteristically, without plan, and stood outside the chain-link fence as the light went amber and died. The wardrobe frame lay where he'd left it, half-swallowed by weeds, its hollow rail now empty—the sentence living temporarily in Marisol's drawer, in a room that smelled of glue and patience.

Fifteen years of postcards, and in two visits a stranger had mapped the whole submerged continent of him. He thought about drowning again, how the worst part was never the water but the moment you stopped fighting it—how the body, given no choice, finally opens its mouth.

His phone buzzed again. He didn't look.

Above the fence line, the house lot sat dark under the first stars, and somewhere behind him, far off, he heard the diesel sigh of Bus 44 making its run—perhaps for one of the last times—carrying someone toward the neighborhood, or away from it, and he understood with sudden certainty that whichever direction it was going, he and his sister were on the same route now, whether either of them bought a ticket or not.
