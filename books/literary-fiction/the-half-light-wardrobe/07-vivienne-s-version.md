## 7. Vivienne's Version

Vivienne had rehearsed the confrontation on the bus, in the shower, against the ceiling of her insomnia—three venues, one script. She had imagined Ulyana flinching. She had imagined him denying. She had not imagined him opening the door already holding the paper.

It was folded into quarters now, she could see the creases through his fingers, and he stood in the doorway of his apartment the way a man stands at the edge of a pier he has decided not to jump from: committed to staying, but only just.

"You went back to the lot," she said.

"You followed me."

"I hired someone to follow you." She let that sit between them, ugly and honest. "You've had fifteen years of rituals, Ulyana. I'm allowed one."

He stepped aside—not an invitation so much as a surrender of the doorway—and she entered the apartment she had never seen and somehow already knew. The postcards were everywhere. Pinned above the desk, stacked in shoeboxes labeled by year in their mother's looping hand, fanned across the kitchen table like a tarot spread no one had the courage to read. Hundreds of them. Maybe thousands. All addressed to a house that had stopped existing before most of them were written.

"You wrote to her," Vivienne said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't the accusation she'd come to make, but it came out first anyway, the way blood comes out before the deeper thing.

"I wrote to the house." He set the folded paper on the table, deliberately, the way a chess player sets down a piece to signal the end of the game. "The house listened better."

* * *

She had been nineteen the night of the fire. She needed him to understand that first—that she had been old enough to be believed, and had not been believed, and had learned from that disbelief everything she knew about leaving.

"It was October," she began, because he would want precision, because precision was his armor and she intended to strip it off him joint by joint. "The seventeenth. You were fourteen. You remember it as an accident—I know you do, you've built your whole cathedral of postcards on the foundation of *accident*. But I was there, Ulyana. I came home early from Marta's because I'd forgotten my keys, and I saw her go into the garage with the can of paint thinner she'd bought on Tuesday, and I saw her look at the window first. At the window where Dad's chair was."

Ulyana's face did something complicated and then did nothing at all, which was worse.

"She waited for him to fall asleep in front of the television," Vivienne continued. Her voice was steady and she hated how steady it was; she had wanted to be shaking, shaking would have been more honest. "She waited the way she always waited—for the rage to pass, for the snoring to start, for the house to go quiet enough that whatever she did next wouldn't interrupt anything. That was her whole life, Ulyana. Not interrupting anything. And then she struck the match, and she walked out the front door, and she sat down on the curb next to *you*, and the two of you watched our father's chair burn through the living room window like it was a parade."

"Stop."

"No." She crossed to the table, put her palm flat over the folded paper, felt its small resistance under her hand. "You're going to hear all of it. You watched. You were fourteen, and you watched, and when the neighbors came running and the sirens came and everyone asked *where's your mother, where's your brother*, you said—" she made her voice small, made it his, fourteen years old and terrified and loyal to something rotten—"'We were outside. We were just outside.' And you have been *just outside* ever since. Fifteen years of postcards to a crime scene."

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight and grain, like the charred rail of the wardrobe frame she'd seen in the photographs the surveyor took. She watched him stand there among his unsent confessions and understood, with a lurch, that she had misread the room entirely. The postcards weren't hiding. They were a man trying to say it for fifteen years in a language with no word for what their mother had done.

"Say it," she whispered. "Say I'm wrong. Say it was the wiring, say it was Dad's cigarettes, say anything except what we both know."

* * *

What he said instead was: "She asked me if I wanted to stay inside or come out."

Vivienne's hand lifted off the paper as though it had grown hot.

"That's what she said to me." His voice was flat, careful, a man walking on ice he could hear cracking. "That night. She stood in the hallway with her coat already on, and she said, *Do you want to stay inside or come out?* And I thought—I was fourteen, Vivi, I thought she meant the cold. I thought she was being strange the way she was always strange, asking questions that meant other things. So I said outside. And she nodded, and she held the door for me, and she held it a beat too long, and I looked back and Dad was asleep in his chair and the television light was going blue on his face, and I still didn't understand until the garage door opened and the smell came out."

"And you didn't stop her."

"I didn't *know*." But even as he said it, his own voice betrayed him, thinned by the truth underneath. He pressed his knuckles to the table. "No. That's the lie. That's the version I mailed to a burned house every week for fifteen years. I half-knew. I knew the way children know things they can't afford to know. I chose outside, Vivienne. She gave me a door and I walked through it and I let it close behind me and I never once turned around, and when the fire started I didn't run toward it. I sat down on the curb next to her because sitting next to her was the only safe place left in the world, and it was the least safe place in the world, and I knew that too."

The apartment seemed to lean in around them. Outside, a siren rose and fell somewhere in the middle distance, and both of them flinched at it, identically, and neither remarked on it.

"So you knew," Vivienne said slowly. "And she knew that you knew. And she stayed anyway. Stayed married to him, stayed in the town, stayed—" she gestured at the shoeboxes, the pinned cards, the whole mausoleum of his devotion—"*stayed*, while you wrote to her ghost every week apologizing for something you didn't do."

"For something I *watched*."

"There's a difference."

"Is there?" He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, airless. "Because I've spent fifteen years not knowing. And then I found this." He unfolded the paper with hands that were not quite steady and smoothed it flat on the table between them, and there it was in their mother's handwriting—the language neither of them spoke fluently anymore, the mother tongue gone foreign in their mouths, seven words in ink that had survived fire by hiding inside iron.

"When did you find it?"

"Before the open house. Before I knew you knew about it." He looked up at her. "How long have you known?"

"Since the surveyor's report. There was a photograph of the frame. Something rolled inside the rail—I could see the edge of it." She exhaled. "I told myself it was probably nothing. A shopping list. A recipe. I told myself that for three weeks, Ulyana, and then I hired a lawyer instead of hiring a translator, because buying the land felt safer than reading the sentence."

"Why?"

"Because if it says what I think it says, then she wasn't the saint I made her after I left. Do you understand? I built my whole escape on her. *Mom will handle him. Mom knows what he is.* I flew to another coast and told myself she was holding the line without me. If she was holding the line, then I was allowed to go. But this—" her finger hovered over the paper without touching it— "this might mean she wasn't holding any line. This might mean she decided, one Tuesday in October, that the line wasn't worth holding anymore. And then everything I did afterward—leaving, staying gone, building a life on top of the ash—it wasn't survival. It was desertion."

They stood on either side of the table like two people at a gravesite who had arrived separately and discovered they were mourning the same stranger.

"We were both guarding it," Ulyana said finally. "The same crime. You guarded it by leaving. I guarded it by writing to nobody. Fifteen years, Vivi, and we were doing the same thing from opposite ends of the country."

"The sentence," she said. "Has anyone read it yet?"

"A translator. Marisol. She has it." He hesitated, and she watched him hesitate, watched the last reflex of concealment move through him like weather. "She asked me why it mattered. I couldn't answer her. I sat in her kitchen for two hours and couldn't answer her, because the real answer is that I need to know whether my mother forgave herself or condemned herself, and either way it ruins me."

Vivienne picked up the paper. He let her. That was new—that was the first new thing in fifteen years, his fingers opening instead of closing.

"Then we go together," she said. "Tomorrow. Both of us. Because I am done letting that woman speak to us one at a time."

He looked at her for a long moment, and something moved behind his eyes that she recognized from the curb, from the firelight, from the night two children sat shoulder to shoulder watching their childhood burn and called it warmth.

"She also left something else," he said quietly. "In the frame. Under the paper. I didn't tell Marisol. I haven't told anyone."

"What?"

"A key."
