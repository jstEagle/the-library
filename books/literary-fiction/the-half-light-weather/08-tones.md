## 8. Tones

The kitchen smelled of smoked fish and woodsmoke and something older underneath it all, something Yara could not name yet but was beginning to suspect had a name in Nkọrọ, because everything did.

"Say it again," Mama Efua said. She sat on her low stool by the fire with her hands folded in her lap like a woman at church, patient as a stone that had decided to be patient on purpose.

Yara said it again. "*Ósù.*"

Mama Efua closed her eyes as though Yara had struck a wrong note on an instrument she loved. "No."

"It's what you said."

"I said *ósù*. You said *òsù*." The old woman opened one eye. "One of these words means 'the moon when it is thin.' The other means 'a debt that has gone sour.' You have just told me, very politely, that my moon is a bad debt."

Yara laughed despite herself. "They sound identical."

"They are not identical. Nothing in Nkọrọ is identical to anything else. That is the whole trouble with your people—" she caught herself, and amended with dignity, "—with *you*, since you are mine now too. Your ears were trained on flat ground. Nkọrọ lives on hills. Up and down. High and low. If you walk on flat ground here, you fall into someone's compound and they will feed you out of pity, which is worse than being fined."

It was the fourth morning of lessons, and Yara had begun to understand that she had not, in any meaningful sense, been learning the language at all. She had been learning its doorstep. Mama Efua taught the way water teaches a rock: without hurry, without mercy, with absolute confidence in the outcome.

This morning there was a bowl of coarse salt on the table between them, and Yara didn't know why yet. She'd learned not to ask. Questions were answered eventually, but only after their time had come, like fruit.

"Again," Mama Efua said.

"*Ósù.*"

"*Òsù.*"

"*Ósù.*"

"You are shouting the high tone instead of singing it. Shouting is English. Singing is ours." Mama Efua reached over and tapped Yara's sternum with one dry finger. "The voice does not live in the throat. It lives here. When you say *ósù*, this must lift. Like bread rising. Try."

Yara tried. Her chest lifted. The syllable came out somewhere between a question and a hiccup.

Mama Efua's face did something complicated — disappointment folding itself around amusement and losing. "Better," she said, in the tone of a woman who had once told a goat its dancing was better. "Now the other one. Let it fall. Let it drop like a stone into a well. *Òsù.*"

"*Òsù.*"

"There! Did you hear yourself?"

"I heard myself make two noises."

"You made two *words*. One is the moon. One is a wound that money made." Mama Efua leaned forward, and the firelight moved in the deep creases of her face so that for a moment she looked carved rather than old. "That is today's lesson, and it is the only lesson, and you will spend the rest of your life on it if I die tomorrow. In Nkọrọ, the truth and the wound are the same syllable. Only the pitch tells you which one you are carrying. Say a thing flatly, carelessly, and no one knows whether you have spoken truth or opened a wound. So we do not speak flatly. We never speak flatly. A language where meaning lives in the music cannot afford lazy music."

Yara wrote it down in her notebook, though she suspected the notebook was beside the point. Some things went through the pen and some things went through the skin, and this was clearly the second kind.

"The salt," Mama Efua said, nodding at the bowl.

"Yes?"

"Taste it."

Yara took a pinch and put it on her tongue. It tasted of nothing. It always tasted of nothing. Five years of nothing — wet paper, warm water, the ghost of a memory of flavor. She had built a career on describing food she could not taste, the way a blind painter might build a career on light, and she had told herself for five years that this was fine, that description was its own kind of knowing, that the hole in her was a room she simply kept locked.

She put the salt down.

"You tasted nothing," Mama Efua said. It was not a question.

"How did you—"

"Because you chewed it like a woman doing arithmetic. Nobody chews salt like arithmetic unless the salt has stopped talking to them." The old woman studied her for a long moment, and Yara felt seen in a way that was uncomfortable and clean at once, like a wound being washed. "How long?"

"Five years," Yara said. Saying it aloud in this kitchen, to this woman, made it suddenly enormous. In London it had been a private disability, a professional secret, a small dishonesty folded inside a larger performance. Here it sounded like what it was. "Since — a long time ago. Since something happened."

"Mm." Mama Efua did not ask what. That was its own kind of courtesy, or its own kind of strategy; with her, Yara was beginning to learn, the two were often indistinguishable. "And you became a person who writes about food."

"People liked what I wrote."

"Of course they did. You write beautifully about the door of a house you cannot enter. Beautiful writing about a locked door is still beautiful. But it is hungry work." She tapped the salt bowl. "In Nkọrọ, we say the tongue keeps accounts. Everything you refuse to taste, the tongue writes down anyway. And one day it presents the bill."

"That sounds like a proverb."

"It is a proverb. I invented it just now, but if you repeat it enough it will become one. That is how proverbs are made. Old women inventing things and repeating them until everyone forgets who to blame."

*

The days found a shape. Mornings in the kitchen, afternoons walking — Mama Efua walked the town every day, slow as a tide, and people came out of doorways to greet her, and Yara began to hear the greetings as music rather than noise. High-low-high. Low-high. A falling third that meant respect, a rising one that meant affection between equals, a flat call across a compound that meant *I see you and I am not surprised, because I am from here and I see everything.*

Her own mouth remained stubborn. She could hear everything — that was the strange gift of it, the cruel joke of it. Her ear had become perfect precisely because her tongue refused. She heard the tones the way she imagined a deaf composer hears orchestras: totally, and from outside.

"Why won't it come?" she asked on the sixth day, after failing, for the hundredth time, to distinguish *kà* (to count) from *ká* (to open) from *kâ* (to refuse, with contempt).

"Because your mouth is guarding something," Mama Efua said, shelling beans without looking up. "A mouth is a door too. Yours has been locked longer than your tongue."

"What would my mouth be guarding?"

"Ask it. Not me. I am eighty-one years old; I do not have time to be asked questions your own body already knows the answers to." She blew the chaff off the beans. "But I will tell you this. Every student I have ever had who could not speak — and there have been three, counting you — was keeping a silence they mistook for politeness. Politeness is short. Silence that lasts five years is a debt."

There it was again. Debt. The word kept surfacing in this kitchen like a fish rising to check the weather.

*

On the eighth day, the harmattan haze thinned and the light came in sideways and gold, and Mama Efua announced that they would cook.

"Cooking is the advanced class," she said. "Anyone can recite proverbs. But to cook in Nkọrọ you must name every stage of the fire, and the fire does not wait for you to find your tones."

So Yara stood at the hearth with a wooden spoon while Mama Efua named things: the word for oil going quiet in the pot, the word for the first hiss of onion, the word for the moment pepper hits hot oil and the whole kitchen flinches — a wonderful word, *kpírí*, that seemed to contain the flinch itself. Yara repeated each one. Most came out wrong. The fire did not care. The onions burned slightly, which Mama Efua declared was also a word, and a useful one.

Then, near the end, Mama Efua said: "Taste the soup. Tell me what it needs."

And Yara froze, spoon in hand, because tasting was the locked room, the door she did not open, the whole private architecture of her ruin.

"Taste it," Mama Efua said again, gently, and there was something under the gentleness that Yara would only understand much later — the patience of a woman who had chosen her student deliberately and knew exactly which locks she was picking.

Yara dipped the spoon. Lifted it. Put it in her mouth.

Nothing. Warm, salty-textured nothing, the familiar wet paper. She swallowed and shook her head. "I can't. I'm sorry. I told you—"

"I know what you told me. Taste it again. Slower. And this time, say the name of the soup while it is in your mouth. *Ìlà ósù.* Say it properly. High-low-high. Let your chest lift."

"This is superstition."

"Everything worth doing is superstition until it works. Say it."

Yara put the spoon back in her mouth. Broth, smoke, pepper she could feel but not taste. And she said it, around the spoonful, awkward, half-swallowed: "*Ìlà ósù.*"

High. Low. High. Her chest lifted on the first note, dropped through the middle, rose again — the little hill, the melody, the music. The syllables came out rounder than anything she had managed in eight days, because her mouth was full, because the soup was holding the shape of the words the way a riverbed holds the shape of water.

And then—

It was faint. It was almost nothing. It was a thread of salt at the back of her tongue, one bright filament of it, the way you sometimes catch one true note in a choir of strangers. Salt. Actual salt. The taste of the estuary at dawn, of her grandmother's kitchen, of tears she had never cried about any of this.

Yara gripped the edge of the table.

"Do you hear me," she whispered, and then, absurdly, "do you *taste* me," and Mama Efua was laughing, low and satisfied, the laugh of a fisherman watching a line go taut.

"There she is," the old woman said. "There is the student. One taste. Don't grab at it. Grabbing frightens it, like a bird on a branch."

"It's real? It's — I can taste the salt—"

"You sang the word correctly with your mouth full, and your tongue remembered that it belongs to a place. That is all. That is everything." Mama Efua took the spoon from her hand and set it down with ceremony. "The tongue keeps accounts, I told you. Today it accepted a small payment. There is a great balance outstanding. But a beginning is a beginning, even when it is the size of a grain of salt."

Yara laughed, and the laugh cracked halfway through and became something else, and Mama Efua, with the tact of eighty-one years, turned to stir the pot and let her have the moment without an audience.

When Yara had recovered, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand, feeling lighter and more terrified than she had in years — because if the lock could open, then the thing behind the lock was real, and the thing behind the lock had a name and a date five years ago — she said, "Why salt? Why did you start with salt?"

"Because salt is what the sea leaves when it takes everything else," Mama Efua said. "And because your family—" she paused, and Yara felt the pause like a change in wind direction, "—has spent sixty years taking the salt out of other people's kitchens and selling it in yours. But that is a lesson for another day."

The sentence hung in the steam between them.

"Another day," Yara repeated carefully. "Which day?"

"The day you ask me about the recordings." Mama Efua said it plainly, the way she said everything, and ladled soup into two bowls. "Eat. It needs more pepper. Everything in this town needs more pepper; that is why nobody here ever says anything bland."

*

That night Yara lay in her room at the resort — Dele had insisted, and the air conditioning hummed its expensive hum against the night sounds of Ilase — and she held the taste of salt in her memory like a coal carried in cupped hands.

One correct phrase. One grain of flavor. Her whole body humming with it, fragile as a soap bubble, and beneath the joy a cold current moving the other way, because Mama Efua had said *your family* and *sixty years* and *the recordings*, and had said them the way she said everything: early, plainly, before their appointed time, like a woman setting a table for a meal she expected you to try to leave.

Yara got up. She opened her notebook to a fresh page and wrote, in English, because the Nkọrọ wasn't ready:

*Tone is the difference between truth and wound. Same syllable. Same mouth.*

Then, underneath, smaller:

*What did Grandfather record? Who was Informant B?*

She looked at the second line for a long time. She hadn't known those words until tonight — *Informant B* was a phrase from Dele's proud tour of the Heritage Wing, weeks ago, a plaque he'd gestured at, archival labels, grandfather's field recordings playing on loop for tourists in the lobby, "our founder's pioneering documentation of local culture." She had thought nothing of it then. She thought of it now, at two in the morning, with salt still ghosting her tongue.

Tomorrow, she decided. Tomorrow she would ask Mama Efua about the recordings, as instructed.

But tonight, she picked up her phone and searched the resort's own website, scrolling past the infinity pool and the wedding packages to the Heritage Wing page, and there it was: *Authentic Nkọrọ songs, recorded 1961–1964 by founder Chief Obafemi Galloway. Available in our boutique on vinyl and CD.*

Sixty years of looped voices. And somewhere among them, she understood with a certainty that arrived whole, like a tone arriving at pitch — somewhere among them was a young woman's voice, singing, unnamed.

Yara put the phone face-down on the nightstand and lay in the dark, tasting salt that was no longer there, listening to the estuary breathe.

The lesson for another day was coming faster than Mama Efua had promised.
