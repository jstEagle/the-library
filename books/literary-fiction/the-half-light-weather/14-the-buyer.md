## 14. The Buyer

The message arrived on a Tuesday, which Yara would later think of as fitting, because Tuesdays were the days Mama Efua taught her the words for money and its many disguises.

*Mr. Henrik Voss, Director of Acquisitions, Meridian Heritage Group, requests the pleasure of a meeting with Mr. Dele Galloway regarding the Galloway Archive. Discretion assured.*

Yara saw it because she was still staying in the family wing of the resort — a guest in her own childhood home, sleeping under a ceiling fan that clicked like a patient metronome — and because Dele had taken to leaving his tablet on the breakfast table while he took calls by the pool, as though proximity to his sister had made him careless, or perhaps as though some part of him wanted her to see.

She read the name twice. Meridian Heritage Group. She knew it the way anyone in her world knew it: the conglomerate that bought lighthouses and turned them into cocktail bars, that owned the ruins of three Roman villas and a decommissioned coal mine it marketed as "Industrial Sublime." They bought the past the way other companies bought futures — in bulk, at a discount, with the rough edges sanded off.

And now they wanted the Galloway Archive. Sixty years of stolen songs, sealed in climate-controlled cabinets in the Heritage Wing, playing on loop for tourists who ate grilled prawns twenty meters from where Informant B's voice sang for rice and salt.

Dele came back from the pool smelling of sunscreen and ambition, and found her still holding his tablet.

"You're reading my mail," he said. It wasn't quite an accusation. With Dele, nothing ever quite was; everything arrived softened, wrapped in charm, like fruit packed in sawdust so it wouldn't bruise.

"Your mail was lying next to my toast."

"That's how mail gets read." He sat down, poured himself coffee he wouldn't drink, and studied her over the rim. "Voss flies in Friday. He wants the whole archive — recordings, notebooks, rights, the lot. Nine figures."

Yara set the tablet down carefully, the way you set down something that might be alive. "Nine figures. For Grandfather's theft."

"For the most complete collection of Nkọrọ material in existence," Dele said, and there it was — the family gift, the ability to describe a thing until the describing replaced the thing itself. "Which is what it is, Yara. Whatever else it is."

"And they want—"

"Clean provenance." He said it lightly, but she watched his hand flatten on the table, a small unconscious gesture, like a man steadying paper against wind. "Standard due diligence. They've been burned before. Bought a collection in Cambodia that turned out to have a contested chain of custody, and there were lawsuits, headlines, the whole circus. So Voss's people will want documentation. Who recorded what, when, under what agreement, with whose consent."

"And do we have that?"

The pause lasted two seconds. In their family, two seconds was a confession delivered at full volume.

"We have documentation," Dele said.

Yara looked at her brother — her brother who had taught her to swim by throwing her off the jetty and laughing until she surfaced furious; her brother who sent her London reviews to his entire executive team with exclamation points; her brother who had greeted her at the airport five weeks ago and held her face in both hands and said, *you came home* — and she understood, with the cold clarity of someone watching water rise, exactly which direction this conversation was going.

"Dele. What documentation?"

"The kind that exists," he said pleasantly, "by Friday."

* * *

He did not show her the papers. That was the first thing. In all the years of their shared childhood, Dele had never once hidden anything from her except vegetables, and even those he'd only redistributed on her plate with theatrical generosity. Now there was a folder in his office that closed when she entered, a laptop angled away, a new lock on the archive room that hadn't been there in June.

So she did what she had always done, what she'd built a career on: she paid attention.

She noticed the archivist, a soft-spoken young man named Emeka who wore his grandfather's reading glasses like a costume of authority, working late three nights running. She noticed the date format on a document Dele left briefly visible on the kitchen counter — British style, day-month-year, when every genuine Galloway record from the sixties used the American format their grandfather had adopted from his Houston suppliers, a quirk Yara had catalogued herself in chapter four of her abandoned memoir. She noticed that the signature on the 1963 consent form — *I, the undersigned, freely grant...* — was written in a confident modern ballpoint blue, not the violet fading ink of the era, and that the signatories' names were spelled correctly, beautifully, in a country where sixty years ago half the speakers signed with a thumbprint because Grandfather hadn't bothered to teach them letters he then sold back to them as heritage.

She stood in the corridor outside the archive room on Thursday night, holding the forged consent form she had photographed through the gap of a door Emeka had propped open with a fire extinguisher, and she felt the floor tilt beneath her in a way no amount of delta flatness could explain.

Because here was the thing she had been circling for weeks without landing: shame was survivable. Shame was a family business. The recordings themselves, the erasures, Informant B reduced to a letter — those were wounds her grandfather had inflicted, debts she had inherited, horrors she could write about and survive writing about. Ruin, yes. But ruin of a certain kind. The kind that happened in newspapers, in opinion columns, in the polite guillotine of public opinion. Her own review, pulled five years ago, had nearly killed her and hadn't; she knew the weight of reputational death and she had learned, here in Ilase, word by word, tone by tone, that she could carry it.

But forgery was not shame. Forgery was a courtroom. Forgery was men in suits who did not read beautifully around holes, who asked questions in the flat grammar of criminal law: *Who prepared this document? When? With what intent?* Forgery had no literary grace notes, no elegant sentences to hide inside. If Voss's lawyers ever looked closely — and buyers paying nine figures hired people whose entire profession was looking closely — the chain would lead not to a dead grandfather safely beyond subpoena, but to a living brother with a signature, a printer, and intent.

Her brother. In handcuffs, or in the gray anteroom of an investigation that would drain him for years, with his daughter's school fees and forty employees' salaries hanging off the same hook.

Yara sat down on the corridor floor, in the dark, in the building her family had built on a buried wrong, and laughed once — a short, terrible sound — because she finally understood the architecture of the trap. Dele hadn't just forged a document. He had forged her silence into something load-bearing. Every hour she waited made her complicit; every word she spoke might bring the roof down on him. He didn't even need to ask her to keep quiet. The structure did it for him.

It was, she had to admit, masterful. Their grandfather would have wept with pride.

* * *

She went to Mama Efua's kitchen the next morning carrying bread and guilt in equal measure, and found the old woman shelling beans into a basin with the unhurried violence of someone who had outlived everyone who ever rushed her.

"You look," Mama Efua said, "like a person who has seen a document."

Yara nearly dropped the bread. "How—"

"You walk differently when your mind is heavy. Like a heron with wet wings." She nodded at the stool across from her. "Sit. Tell me."

And Yara told her — the buyer, the nine figures, the clean provenance, the ballpoint-blue signatures. She told it plainly, in English, because her Nkọrọ still failed her at the edges, and halfway through she realized Mama Efua had stopped shelling. The beans lay still in her brown palm like small planets waiting to be named.

"He has written lies," Mama Efua said at last.

"Yes."

"With his hand. His own hand."

"Yes."

Mama Efua was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the estuary breathed its slow grey breath, and somewhere a generator coughed awake, and a child called a greeting to another child in the flattened English that was eating the town syllable by syllable.

"In Nkọrọ," Mama Efua said, "we do not say a man *tells* a lie. We say he *carries* it. Ó bù úkwụrụ. The lie is a burden he has chosen to lift, and it bends him slowly, and everyone can see the bending except him." She resumed shelling, one bean, another. "Your grandfather carried his. Now your brother lifts the same burden, and adds to it. This is how burdens become family heirlooms."

"What do I do?" The question came out smaller than Yara intended, smaller than she had allowed her voice to be in fifteen years of professional composure. "If I expose it — the real story, the recordings, all of it — the forgery comes out too. Not as history. As crime. His crime. They'll prosecute him, Mama. My brother."

Mama Efua looked at her then, and her eyes — clouded at the rims, sharp at the center, like the lagoon in dry season — held something Yara couldn't immediately name. Pity, maybe. Or recognition.

"When your mother's mother sang," she said, "and your father's father pressed the button on his machine — did he think about what it would cost her children?"

"No. I don't think he thought about anyone's children."

"No." A bean dropped into the basin. "He thought about his. And now your brother thinks about his daughter. And you think about your brother. Everyone protecting someone, and the language unprotected underneath all of you, like ground." She brushed meal from her palms. "You are asking me for permission to stay silent a little longer. I cannot give it. But I can tell you this: a debt does not get smaller because you wait. It grows interest. Your brother's debt is growing interest right now, every day the false paper sits in the true archive."

"And if telling the truth destroys him?"

Mama Efua picked up the basin, settled it on her hip with the ease of seventy years' practice, and stood.

"Then he will learn," she said, "what your family should have learned in 1963. That some things cost more to keep than to lose." At the doorway she paused, and her voice gentled into something almost tender. "Bring me my tea. Heavy on the milk. We will practice tones this evening. You will need a steady mouth for what is coming."

* * *

Friday morning, Henrik Voss arrived on the resort's private airstrip in a plane white as a pill, and Yara watched from the terrace as Dele walked out to meet him — two men in linen, shaking hands in the heat, smiling the smiles of men conducting commerce over an open grave.

She could see them from Mama Efua's kitchen window, if she leaned. She had come here ostensibly to study, and for an hour she did study — the debt-settling register, the forbidden words Mama Efua had begun feeding her like medicine, each one bitter and precise — but her eyes kept sliding to the glass, to the small distant theater of the airstrip, where Voss gestured expansively and Dele laughed at the correct moments and a briefcase changed custody like a child in a divorce.

"They will sign today," Mama Efua said, not looking up from her tea.

"Probably."

"And the false papers will go into the true ones, and the whole bundle will fly away in the white bird, and the songs will belong to people who think a song is a thing you can own." She sipped. "Unless."

"Unless what?"

"Unless someone stands in front of the bird." Mama Efua set down her cup. "You know, when I was a girl, there was a fisherman in Ilase who swore he could stop the tide by standing in it. Everyone laughed. The tide moved him, of course. But the tide *noticed* him. And the story of the man who stood in the tide outlived the tide's schedule." She looked at Yara directly. "You have already written the story. I have watched you carrying it in your bag for two weeks like a stone. The only question left is whether you send it before the bird leaves, or spend the rest of your life explaining to yourself why you didn't."

Yara's throat tightened. "If I send it now — today — the forgery is in it. Dele goes down for fraud, not just for what Grandfather did. He'll never forgive me."

"No," Mama Efua agreed placidly. "Probably not at first."

"That doesn't frighten you?"

The old woman smiled then, and it transformed her face the way rain transforms dust — suddenly everything underneath was visible, green and quick.

"My mother sang for rice and salt and lost her name to a ledger," she said. "Forgiveness frightens me much less than forgetting. Go home, Yara. Look at what your brother built with the lie. Then decide what you will build with the truth."

* * *

That night Yara stood in Dele's office while he poured two glasses of the good whisky, and she watched him watch her, and between them lay the unspoken document like a third person in the room.

"To family," Dele said, raising his glass.

"To family," she echoed, and drank, and hated how easily the old liturgy still rose in her throat.

He talked for a while — about the sale, about what it meant, about how the archive would finally be "properly stewarded," about a trust he promised to endow, about Adaeze's future — and Yara listened with the terrible double hearing she had perfected as a critic, catching both the music and the machinery beneath it. And when he finished, she said quietly:

"The signatures are wrong, Dele."

The glass stopped halfway to his mouth. For one moment — one long, naked moment — her brother's face held nothing at all, no charm, no warmth, no ownership, just the bare frightened architecture of a man caught in the act of being caught. Then the mask came back down, smooth as a well-run hotel.

"I don't know what you mean."

"Ballpoint blue. British dates. Correct spellings." She set her glass down. "Grandfather's informants signed with thumbprints, Dele. You gave them beautiful handwriting they never had. It's almost kind, what you did. It's also prison."

The silence stretched. Somewhere below, the resort's evening music started up, tinny and festive, singing to investors about authenticity.

"If this goes wrong," Dele said finally, and his voice cracked open just enough to show the boy inside the empire, the boy who had thrown her off jetties and laughed, "it isn't just me. It's Adaeze's school. It's two hundred jobs. It's Mum's name. It's everything." He stepped closer. "Wait. Just wait. After the sale closes, after the trust is funded — then tell whatever story you want. Write me as the villain. I'll even read it. But give me ninety days, Yara. Ninety days, and I fix everything properly."

And there it was: the offer she had known was coming since Tuesday, dressed in love, priced in days. The same offer, she realized, that an editor with Galloway ties had once made her about a review — *wait, soften, later* — the same gentle arithmetic of almost, the family art, practiced now upon her by the last person she had hoped would never practice it.

Yara looked at her brother for a long time.

"Ninety days," she said at last, noncommittally, and kissed his cheek, and walked out past the Heritage Wing where a dead woman's voice was singing a welcome song on loop to an empty lobby.

In her room, she opened her laptop. The draft was still there, fourteen pages of it, waiting like a loaded thing. She read the paragraph about the forged consent forms — his handwriting, his dates, his intent — and her finger hovered over the keyboard, trembling slightly, steady enough.

Outside, far off toward the airstrip, a plane engine began to turn over in the dark.

She began to type.
