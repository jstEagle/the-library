## 18. Festival of Ghosts

The stage had been built overnight on the resort's croquet lawn, which Dele's people called the Event Meadow and everyone in Ilase still called the place where Chief Bassey's goats used to graze. It was a handsome stage, all things considered — draped in white and green, flanked by two enormous banners bearing the Galloway crest: a heron standing in water, which had always struck Yara as an odd choice of emblem for a family whose talent was for standing very still while other people drowned.

She arrived early, because she could not bear to arrive late. The technicians were testing microphones, sending feedback shrieks across the lawn like wounded birds. Workers were arranging chairs in neat rows — three hundred of them, then more, because word had traveled down the coast road and the town was coming. Not because they loved festivals. Because they loved a gathering, and because a gathering at Galloway Grand was, historically, where things got given away or taken back.

Yara stood at the edge of the stage with her hands folded, wearing the indigo dress Mama Efua had insisted she buy from the market woman who dyed cloth behind the fish smokers. *You will not stand beside me looking like a Londoner,* the old woman had said that morning, prodding her shoulder with surprising strength. *Today you will look like what you are.* And when Yara asked what she was, Mama Efua had only smiled and said, *Wet.*

The rehearsal blessing had been agreed upon weeks ago. Mama Efua would open the festival with a short welcome in Nkọrọ — dignified, ceremonial, the kind of thing that made investors feel they were touching something ancient without being asked to carry any of its weight. Dele had approved the text himself. Yara had seen it. Four sentences. A greeting to the elders, a praise-line for the river, a wish for prosperity. Harmless as boiled yam.

*Whatever I say up there,* Mama Efua had told her last night, in the dark of her bedroom, her voice thin but level, *you will say it again, louder, in English. Word for word. Do you hear me?*

*I hear you.*

*Even if it costs you your brother?*

Yara had been quiet too long, and the old woman had laughed — a dry rustling laugh like wind through palm fronds.

*That is how I know you are ready. The ones who answer quickly are lying.*

* * *

By noon the chairs were full and people were standing along the perimeter, under the awnings of the refreshment tents, on the low wall by the estuary path. Yara spotted faces she knew from the kitchen lessons' aftermath — the fisherman Emeka who sold Mama Efua her periwinkles; old Nneka from the dye stall; a cluster of children from the primary school, fidgeting in their uniforms, among them the three Yara had begun teaching on Sundays, who waved at her with the shameless enthusiasm of the young.

Dele worked the crowd like a man watering a garden he expected to be photographed. His wife Adaeze stood near the front in yellow lace, holding their daughter Amara's hand. The investors sat in the first two rows — silver-haired men and women in linen, lanyards swinging, the acquisition delegation from Meridian Heritage Group flown in that morning on a chartered plane. Their lead negotiator, a Dutch woman named Vos with the patient eyes of someone who had purchased many old things, kept glancing at her watch.

At half past twelve, Dele took the podium.

"Friends, guests, elders of Ilase," he said, his voice carrying beautifully; he had inherited their father's baritone and their mother's instinct for when to deploy it. "Today we celebrate something our family has cherished for three generations: the living heritage of this town, this river, this language."

Yara, standing in the wings, watched him and felt the strange tenderness-and-grief of watching someone perform sincerity so well that even they believed it. He meant every word. That was the horror of Dele. He had never once lied to himself. He had simply decided, decades ago, which truths were load-bearing and which could be quietly removed, and he had built a beautiful house on the remaining beams.

"And now," Dele said, "it is my profound honor to introduce the last fluent speaker of Nkọrọ, a treasure of this community, our honored guest — Mama Efua Etim."

Applause. Warm, genuine, ignorant. Mama Efua rose from her chair at the front row — she had refused the wheelchair until the last possible moment — and walked to the steps with her cane tapping out a slow, deliberate rhythm, like a drummer tuning herself.

Yara met her at the bottom step. Up close, the old woman looked smaller than she had that morning, her wrapper loose around her, her skin papery in the flat grey light. But her eyes were fully lit, and there was something in them Yara had never seen before: a kind of joyful terror, the look of a woman standing at the edge of cold water she has decided, finally, to enter.

"Remember," Yara whispered. "Word for word. I'm right here."

"I know where you are." Mama Efua patted her cheek once, almost fondly. "I put you there."

Then she climbed the steps, one at a time, and crossed the stage to the microphone, and the lawn went quiet in the way only a delta town can go quiet — completely, expectantly, the silence of people who have learned that important things are said rarely and must be caught whole.

Mama Efua adjusted the microphone stand downward. She looked out at the crowd for a long moment. She saw the investors. She saw Dele. She saw, Yara was certain, the ghosts — the row of women sixty years dead who had sung into her grandfather's brass-trimmed recorder for sacks of rice and salt, their names filed away as letters of the alphabet.

Then she began to speak.

"Ọsụkpọ ilase, mma mma o."

Her voice came through the speakers clear and cracked and enormous. Yara stepped to the second microphone, as arranged, her heart slamming against her ribs.

"Greetings to the town of Ilase, my beautiful people," she translated.

A ripple of pleasure through the crowd. This was the rehearsed part. This was fine. Beside the stage, Dele exhaled and allowed himself a small smile.

Mama Efua continued. "Ọsụkpọ ndị mmiri, ndị nke anyị nna ha zoro olu ha n'akwụkwọ ego."

And Yara's mouth went dry, because this was not the rehearsed text. She knew the words — God help her, after five months she knew the words — and she knew exactly what they meant.

*Greetings also to the water people, those whose voices our grandfather hid inside money.*

She said it. Her voice shook on *money*, and she heard the crowd's pleasure curdle into confusion. In the front row, Vos stopped checking her watch.

Dele turned toward the stage slowly, the smile still fixed on his face but draining from his eyes, a man watching weather change over open water.

Mama Efua gripped the microphone with both hands now, and her voice gathered itself, rose, found the register Yara had learned at her bedside — the forbidden one, the debt-settling words, the language families used when accounting could no longer be avoided.

"Nke a bụ okwu ụgwọ. Anyị na-ekwu ya n'ihu mmadụ niile, ma ọ bụ anyị ekwughị ya ma ọlị."

"This is a debt-word," Yara translated. "It is spoken before everyone, or it is never spoken at all."

Somewhere in the crowd a baby began to cry and was hushed. The estuary wind had died entirely; the flags hung limp; even the water seemed to stop moving and listen.

"Sistịm m, nwanyị a —" Mama Efua's free hand pressed flat against her chest — "a kpọrọ ya Informant B. Ọ dịghị aha ya n'ihe odide Galloway. Ọ bụ abụ ya na-aga n'ọnụ ọnụ ndị a kwa ụbọchị, afọ iri isii. Abụ ya. Olu ya. Ndụ ya. A na-ere ya dị ka ihe ịchọ mma ụlọ oriri."

"My mother," Yara said, and her voice broke clean in half on the word *mother*, and she let it break, because Mama Efua had told her that a broken voice carrying truth was worth more than a whole one carrying nothing, "was called Informant B. She has no name in the Galloway records. Her songs have played from these speakers every day for sixty years. Her songs. Her voice. Her life. Sold as decoration for a hotel."

The silence on the lawn was total now. Three hundred people and more, motionless. In the second row, an elderly man Yara recognized as a retired schoolteacher had begun, very quietly, to weep.

Dele started forward — one step, two — and Adaeze's hand closed on his arm, and whatever passed between them stopped him. He stood at the edge of the stage like a man at a shoreline watching a boat leave without him.

Mama Efua turned her head and looked directly at him. Her voice did not rise. It dropped instead, into something low and terrible and calm.

"Dele Galloway. Nwa nwa onye ohi. Ma ọ bụghị gị m na-akpọ ohi. Ọ bụ ụlọ gị. Ụlọ ahụ guzo n'ala ezuru."

"Dele Galloway. Grandson of a thief. But it is not you I call a thief. It is the house. The house stands on stolen ground."

A sound moved through the crowd — not a gasp, something older than a gasp, a collective intake like the tide drawing back before it comes in. The investors had gone very still. Vos had taken out her phone and was, with the instinct of her profession, recording.

Yara's hands were trembling so badly she clasped them together at her waist. Every part of her wanted to run — off the stage, out of the light, back to the person she had been five months ago, the woman who wrote beautifully around holes. But Mama Efua had taught her the tones, and the tones had taught her this: that the same syllable means *truth* or *wound* depending on where you place your voice, and that you choose, every time you speak, which one you are saying.

She stayed.

Mama Efua raised one hand toward the banners, toward the heron standing in its painted water.

"Egret a. Ọ guzo n'ime mmiri afọ isii. Ọ jidere ihe ndị ọzọ nwere. Taa, anyị na-achọ ya azụ."

"This egret has stood in the water sixty years, holding what belongs to others. Today, we ask for it back."

And then — Yara would remember this for the rest of her life — Mama Efua began to sing.

It was the song from the archive. Track fourteen, the one that played on loop in the Heritage Wing between the hours of ten and six, the one Yara had heard a hundred times in the marble corridor without knowing she was hearing a young woman's voice pressed flat into plastic sixty years ago. But sung live, in the open air, unowned, it was a different creature entirely. It climbed. It doubled back on itself. It carried the particular grief of a language that had learned to keep its own company, and it poured out over the lawn and across the water, and the townspeople began — hesitantly, then together — to hum. Old Nneka was singing the harmony. The schoolchildren had gone silent and wide-eyed. Even the investors' stillness changed quality, from discomfort to something like awe.

When the song ended, Mama Efua lowered herself slightly, leaning on the microphone stand, breathing hard. Yara moved to her side and took her elbow, and the old woman leaned into her, heavy, real, alive.

"One more," Mama Efua murmured, so softly the microphone barely caught it. "The last one. Say it well."

Yara nodded. Her face was wet. She didn't wipe it.

Mama Efua straightened and spoke the final lines, each word placed like a stone in a wall:

"Anyị anaghị achọ ego. Anyị anaghị achọ nganga. Anyị chọrọ aha ndị anyị. Anyị chọrọ asụsụ anyị n'ọnụ ndị ga-eji ya eme ihe, ma ọ bụghị naanị ire ya. Nke a bụ okwu anyị. Taa, anyị kwuru ya."

Yara lifted her chin and gave it to the crowd, to the investors, to her brother, to the dead women of the water:

"We do not want money. We do not want apology performed like a dance. We want our names. We want our language in the mouths of people who will use it, not merely sell it. These are our words. Today, we have spoken them."

She lowered the microphone. For three full seconds nobody moved.

Then the retired schoolteacher stood up, and then old Nneka, and then the fisherman Emeka, and then dozens more, rising in a slow wave, and someone began to clap — not the polite festival applause Dele had rehearsed his staff in, but the deep, arrhythmic clapping of people marking a thing that had happened to them. The schoolchildren were cheering. Somebody down by the water fired a starter pistol, whether in celebration or confusion Yara never learned.

On the stage, Mama Efua turned to her, and her face was shining with sweat and effort and something that looked unmistakably, impossibly, like happiness.

"You said it well," she said. "Your tones were almost correct."

"Almost?"

"The debt-words take a lifetime. You have had five months." She patted Yara's hand. "You are forgiven for being young."

* * *

Afterward, the lawn emptied in slow eddies. The investors left early, Vos pausing only to shake Mama Efua's hand with genuine gravity and say, in the tone of a woman revising a spreadsheet in real time, "We will need to discuss provenance further." Dele stood alone by the emptying chairs for a long time, and when Yara approached him he didn't turn around.

"She rehearsed the blessing," he said quietly. "I read it. I approved it."

"Yes."

"You knew."

Yara said nothing, which was its own answer.

He nodded slowly, staring out at the estuary, where the grey water lay flat as hammered tin. "The sale is dead," he said. "You understand that. Everything is—" He stopped. Started again. "Go home, Yara. Whatever you're going to do next, just—"

"Dele."

"Don't." His voice cracked on it, just slightly, the baritone failing him at last. "Not here. Not today."

She touched his sleeve. He let her, for exactly three seconds, and then walked away across the Event Meadow, past the grazing ground of Chief Bassey's goats, a tall man getting smaller, and Yara stood on the stage among the ghost-banners and understood that she had traded her brother for a sentence, and that she would spend the rest of her life deciding whether the exchange rate was fair.

Her phone buzzed in her bag. Then buzzed again. Then rang — her editor, calling eleven hours ahead of schedule, from a London morning.

Mama Efua, gathering her cane, glanced at the screen, then at Yara's face.

"Answer it," she said. "And then come sit with me awhile. I find I am tired in a new way."

Yara answered. The voice on the other end was urgent, excited, asking questions about publication timing, about legal review, about whether she could confirm the forgery by morning — and as she listened, walking Mama Efua slowly across the lawn, Yara noticed that the old woman's grip on her arm had tightened, and that her steps had begun to list, gently, like a boat taking on water.

"Mama Efua?"

The old woman smiled up at her and said something in Nkọrọ — soft, unhurried, perfectly toned.

But Yara couldn't translate it. Because the words had gone strange in her ears, swimming, and the lawn tilted, and somewhere very far away her editor was still talking, and Mama Efua's knees were folding, and Yara was catching her, all of her, shouting for help in two languages at once.
