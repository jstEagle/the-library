## 4. The Student Question

I have outlived every person who ever called me by my true name, which is a strange arithmetic. My mother gave me a name in her tongue that meant something like *the river remembers what the stone forgets*—a mouthful, yes, but names are not meant to be convenient, they are meant to be true. Then the missionaries shortened it, then the army shortened it further, then the passport office made it into something that fit in a little box on a form. By the time I reached this country, I was a man with a name like a receipt.

The language itself has fared no better. It lives now in my mouth and nowhere else on this continent, as far as I know, and I have looked. I have written to linguistic departments at three universities. Two never answered. The third sent me a form asking whether I would be willing to be "archived." Archived. As though I were a filing cabinet with a heartbeat. I wrote back to say that a language is not a specimen; it is a fire, and it must be passed hand to hand or it goes out, and no vault ever warmed anyone.

So when I say that I had been thinking about the tuner for some weeks before I asked him, you will understand that this was not a whim. It was triage.

* * *

The piano in 4B had not been tuned since the Carter administration, and it did not need to be. That was the beauty of the arrangement. I had told the building manager—first the old one, then the new one, then the one who answered to the name of a corporation rather than a man—that the piano was essential to my health. My hands, I said. Arthritis. The doctor recommends I keep the instrument in good order even if I cannot play it daily. This was a lie of the most respectable kind, the kind with a medical alibi, and it worked. Once a season, Yara Reyes climbed my stairs with his black case and his silence, and sat at my piano for an hour, and touched nothing that needed touching.

Or so I believed at first.

I am an old man and I am permitted my vanities, but I am not permitted self-deception, not anymore; the language took that from me when it made me its last keeper. A keeper learns to listen the way other men learn to sleep—with the whole of the body. And what I heard, season after season, was this: the boy did not tune the piano. He *listened* to it. He played a note and then he waited, head tilted, as though the note were going to tell him something. And the strange thing, the thing that made me put down my tea and sit up straighter in my chair, was that the note *did*. He heard things in that piano that I had stopped hearing forty years ago, back when my ears were still young enough to be arrogant.

He would strike the A above middle C and then press his palm flat against the soundboard, the way my mother used to press her palm against the ground before a storm, reading what the earth intended. He would open the lid and look at the hammers not as a repairman looks at parts but as a priest looks at a congregation—some worn, some strong, each with its own history of being struck.

Once, I heard him say something under his breath. Not English. Not anything I could place, which delighted me, because there are very few sounds left on this earth that I cannot place. It was a small sound, a private sound, the sound of a man talking to himself in a room he believes is empty.

I know that sound. I made it for forty years.

* * *

The day I asked him, it was raining, and the radiator in my unit was doing its winter oration—that long, knocking argument with itself that the pipes of Milton Flats conduct from November to March, as though the building were a parliament of old men who could not agree on anything except the cold. I had learned the radiator's dialect long ago. Three knocks meant the boiler was thinking. A long hiss meant Mrs. Adeyemi downstairs had turned hers off to save money, which she did every winter and denied every spring.

Yara arrived at ten, as he always did, with the punctuality of a man who has decided that if his life must be small, it will at least be exact.

"Mr. O," he said. He never called me anything else. I had given him the name myself, in the beginning, because my full name in his mouth would have been a burden and I liked him too much to burden him. "Mr. O" was a compromise, like most names in this country.

"Sit," I said, before he could open his case. "Not at the piano. Here." I indicated the chair across from mine, the one my visitors' legs knew and my loneliness knew better.

He hesitated. He was good at hesitating; he had made an art of it, the way some men make an art of leaving parties early. But he sat. He sat the way he did everything—carefully, as though the chair might be listening and he did not wish to be overheard.

I made tea. This took longer than it should have, because my hands have begun to negotiate with me about small tasks, and also because I wanted him to have time to consider leaving and decide against it. He watched me pour with that attention of his, the attention that made the hair on my arms stand up, because it was not the attention of a tuner or a tenant or a young man being polite to an old one. It was the attention of a *listener*. In my language we have a word for such a person—*onye nti*, the one-of-ear—and I had not used that word aloud in so long that when I thought it, it came out in my head sounding like a coin dropped in an empty room.

"Your piano is in tune," he said. "It was in tune last time. It'll be in tune next time."

"I know."

He looked at me. For a man who had spent five years in this building perfecting the art of not being seen, he had remarkable eyes. Dark, level, and patient—the eyes of someone who has learned that looking is safer than speaking, and has never quite forgiven himself for the learning.

"Then why do I come?" he asked. It was not quite a challenge. It was the question of a man who has carried a question so long it has worn a groove in him.

"Because you like to hear it," I said. "And because you have nowhere else you are permitted to listen."

He did not deny it. That, more than anything, gave me the courage to go on.

* * *

I will tell you what I told him, or nearly, because my English still arranges itself around my meaning like a coat that almost fits.

I told him about my daughter. Adaeze. In Lagos. I told him that for thirty years I had written to her, and for thirty years she had written back—short letters, dutiful, the letters of a woman repaying a debt of fatherhood she had not incurred. And then, four years ago, the letters had stopped. Not angrily. That was the worst of it. No accusation, no door slammed. Just a slow fading, the way a radio station goes when you drive out of its range—her voice still there, still saying something, and then only static, and then not even the static.

I had called. Her number still worked; I could hear the ring tone, that bright little melody, ringing and ringing in a house where my daughter had decided, with great politeness, not to be home.

I did not tell him why. Not that day. A man cannot hand someone a whole inheritance at once; it must come in parcels, and the heaviest parcel must not come first. But I told him this much: that there was a reason, and that the reason and the language were the same thing, and that I had made a choice long ago that I had spent every year since regretting in a language I refused to stop speaking, as though the speaking itself could undo the choice.

"Every language is a way of being human," I said to him. "Did you know that? Not a way of *describing* being human. A way of *being* it. When my mother scolded me, she scolded me in a grammar that made the scolding a kind of love. When my father prayed, the prayers had sounds in them that English cannot even carry—it would be like trying to pour a river through a keyhole." I tapped my chest. "In here, I am still that boy. In here, everyone I have ever lost is still speaking. But the door is closing, Yara. It is closing at the speed of one heartbeat, and when it shuts, it shuts on a whole world, and no one outside will ever know what was in it."

The radiator knocked three times, as if seconding the motion.

He looked at his hands. Tuners' hands, I had noticed—scarred here and there, careful, always slightly curled as though around an invisible keyboard. Hands that belonged to a man who touched things for a living but had never once, in five years, let himself be touched.

"Mr. O," he said slowly, "I'm not—I don't have an ear for languages. I barely got through two years of Spanish in—"

"Lies," I said, pleasantly, and he stopped. "You have the finest ear in this building. I have watched you listen to a piano the way doctors used to listen to chests, before the machines. And do not tell me about the Spanish. Spanish was a school subject. What I am offering is not a subject."

I leaned forward. My knees complained. I let them; a little theater helps the medicine go down.

"I am asking you to be my student. My last student. The word in my tongue for a thing like this is heavier than *student*—it is closer to *heir*, but an heir of the mouth, not the property. I am asking you to carry something that will make you strange in certain rooms and rich in rooms you cannot yet imagine. I am asking you to let an old man's world die *through* you instead of *with* you. There is a difference. The difference is everything."

The rain filled the pause. Downstairs, faintly, Mrs. Adeyemi's radio was playing highlife music, and I thought: *there, that rhythm, my mother danced to that rhythm, and the woman dancing to it now does not know the words to even one song in the tongue it was born in.*

Yara stood up. Not abruptly—he was never abrupt—but with the particular gentleness of a man backing away from something alive.

"I should look at the piano," he said. "The bass strings have been buzzing. I noticed it on the way in."

"The bass strings are fine."

"I'll check anyway."

And he opened his case, and took out his tools, and knelt at the piano, and did nothing that needed doing, and I sat in my chair and drank my cooling tea and understood that I had watched a man flinch.

* * *

I was not angry. I want that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger the universe keeps of old men's grievances: I was not angry. I had flinched myself, once, from a thing that was offered to me—though that is a story for a different day, and it is a heavy parcel, and today I am speaking only of the asking, not the reason the asking was necessary.

But I will confess to this: that night, after he left, I sat by the window and I was angry at myself, briefly, for hoping. Hope is a debt you take out against a stranger's courage, and I had taken out a very large one against a very young man, and he had declined to cosign. Fair enough. He owed me nothing. He had tuned my piano for five years and listened to my radiator's opinions and never once asked me why a man with no working piano insisted on keeping one, and that restraint, I understood now, was not shyness. It was a wall. And I had walked up to a man's wall and knocked on it and been surprised when he did not open it.

But then—and this is the part I want you to hold on to, because it is the part that changed everything—I heard him, through the floor, through the old bones of Milton Flats, as he descended the stairs past 3B and 2B and down into the lobby where his van was parked.

He was humming.

Not a tune. A *tone*. One tone, low, round, repeated. And I sat up so fast my tea went over, because that tone—that exact pitch, that exact placement in the throat—was a word. One word of my mother's language. The word for *listen*.

He had been carrying it in his mouth, I realized, for who knew how long. Saying it to himself in empty rooms, in stairwells, in the bellies of pianos. A man who claimed to flinch from my offer, walking out of my door with a syllable of a dying tongue tucked behind his teeth like a stolen coin.

I did not sleep that night. I lay in the dark and listened to the radiator make its parliament noises, and I thought: *the fire is not out. The fire has found a hand.*

And I thought: *I will not ask him again. I will teach him instead, the way the river teaches the stone—without asking, without announcing, just by passing over him long enough.*

The next morning, I telephoned the building manager and requested my seasonal tuning.

"Mr. O, they tuned your piano in October," the new one said, in his corporate voice.

"The bass strings buzz," I said. "It is a medical matter."

And I put down the phone, and I got out my shoebox—the one I had not opened in four years—and I set it on the table between my chair and the student's chair, and I waited for Thursday.
