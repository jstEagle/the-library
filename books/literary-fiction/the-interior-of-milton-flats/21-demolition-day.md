## 21. Demolition Day

### Yara

They say a building dies twice. Once when the last person who loved it stops loving it, and once when the machines arrive. Milton Flats got its machines on a Tuesday in October, and the people who loved it stood across the street with folding chairs and a thermos situation that could have provisioned a small war.

I arrived at six-forty in the morning because I couldn't stand to be late for it. You can be late to a funeral and be forgiven; you cannot be late to this. The demolition crew had already strung their orange fencing along the sidewalk like the building was being prepared for surgery, which in a sense it was—the removal of everything inside it that had ever mattered.

Mrs. Adeyemi was there before me, of course. She had set up her chair at the exact spot where the lobby door used to face, as if she expected the door to notice her. She had coffee in a steel urn and paper cups stacked in a pyramid, and she was directing traffic to a crowd of zero.

"Yara," she said, and handed me a cup without asking whether I wanted one. Five years in that building taught me that Mrs. Adeyemi's coffee was less a beverage than a form of governance. You drank it because refusing would require a conversation, and conversations with Mrs. Adeyemi always ended with you doing something you hadn't planned to do.

"Morning," I said.

"You slept?"

"Some."

"Liar." She looked at me over the rim of her own cup. "Me neither. Forty-one years in that building and they give us six months' notice and a voucher for moving expenses that wouldn't cover my piano."

"Did the piano make it out okay?"

"It is in my daughter's garage under a tarp, sulking. Pianos sulk, Yara. You know this better than anyone."

I did know it. I had spent five years apologizing to pianos on behalf of their owners—pianos left against cold walls, pianos with cups standing on their lids, pianos nobody played until I came and coaxed them back into tune and then left them to go quiet again. There was a particular loneliness to an instrument in an emptying building. In the last month, tuning the lobby piano for what I knew was the last time, I had felt like a hospice worker with a tuning lever.

The crowd gathered the way crowds gather at demolitions: slowly, then all at once. Dez showed up with his mother and two younger cousins and a camera he'd bought with money from the story—the story, the one built from photographs of my notebook, the leak that had cracked Corliss Development open like a bad radiator valve. He was wearing a jacket I didn't recognize and trying to look like someone who hadn't been crying, which meant he'd definitely been crying.

The Okafor twins came. The woman from 6C whose name I still only knew as 6C even after everything, because some habits survive revolutions. A handful of former tenants who'd taken buyouts and come back anyway, standing slightly apart, wearing the guilty faces of people who signed things.

And Mr. Okonkwo-Bass.

He arrived at seven-fifteen in a wheelchair pushed by a home-health aide named Patricia, whom he had already, by all accounts, reduced to laughter and insubordination. He was dressed as if for church—a charcoal suit, a tie the color of deep water—and when I saw him my chest did something complicated and structural, like a joist settling.

"Yara," he said. "You came early."

"You came at all."

"The doctors said 'discouraged.' They did not say forbidden. There is a difference, and old men live in that difference." He patted the armrest of his chair. "Also, Patricia owes me a favor. Do not ask what it is."

* * *

### Mrs. Adeyemi

Forty-one years. I want that number to sit here properly, because numbers are the only witnesses we get to keep. Forty-one years ago I carried my first child up those stairs while the radiators banged like a drummer who had never been told about rhythm, and I thought, this building is alive, and I was right, though I did not yet know what kind of life it had. Buildings are like husbands. You learn their moods through the pipes.

I made the coffee myself, four thermoses, because if we were going to watch a thing die we would do it caffeinated and upright. That is the whole of my philosophy and I have never once deviated from it.

The young ones cried. Let them. Crying is a luxury of people who still believe things can be fixed. I did my crying in March, privately, into a dish towel, when the notice went up and I read the word "demolition" and understood that no hearing, no journalist, no ledger full of Yara's beautiful handwriting was going to save the walls themselves. We saved the people. We did not save the place. Both facts are true and they do not cancel each other out, though God knows I spent the spring trying to make them.

Mr. Okonkwo-Bass asked me for a second cup at seven-thirty, which surprised me, because he had always drunk his coffee the way he spoke his language—carefully, in small amounts, as if rationing himself for a longer journey than anyone else could see.

"Mrs. Adeyemi," he said, "you will tell me when it starts. My eyes are not what they were."

"I'll tell you," I said. "Though I think your ears will know first."

He smiled at that. "Yes. Ears first. Always ears first." And he looked at Yara across the crowd the way you look at a son you raised without ever formally adopting him, and I pretended not to see, because some things you honor by not looking directly at them.

* * *

### Dez

Okay so here's the thing nobody tells you about blowing something up: they don't blow it up. Not like in the movies. No dynamite, no big red button, no countdown. It's a machine—an excavator, huge, yellow, with a claw on the front like a mechanical dinosaur—and it just *eats* the building. Bite by bite. Starting from the top corner, third floor, unit 3A, which used to belong to a guy named Roland who ironed his shirts at eleven at night so the whole floor could hear the board creak and know somebody was still up.

I filmed it. Obviously. Everybody films everything now, but this felt different, like the camera was doing the crying for us. My mom held my little cousins' hands and my cousin Kofi asked why they couldn't just fix it instead, and my mom said, "Because fixing costs money and breaking makes money," which is the most adult sentence I've ever heard and also the most depressing.

Yara was standing near Mr. O's wheelchair. I went over because—look, I don't do feelings out loud, everybody knows this, it's basically my brand—but I figured today was the day you make exceptions.

"Hey," I said.

"Hey," Yara said.

"So. Your notebook. It worked. Like, actually worked."

He nodded. He's not a talker. Five years I lived four floors above him and I thought he was weird, and then it turned out he was just carrying everything anybody in that building ever told him through a wall, and you can't exactly chat casually when you're doing that.

"I never said sorry," I said. "For assuming you were a spy. For the stairwell thing."

"You were right to check," he said. "That's how you find out who people are. You accuse them and see what they do next."

"What did I do next?"

"You photographed my notebook and sent it to a journalist without asking." He almost smiled. It was the most expression I'd ever gotten off him in daylight. "So. You know. Mixed results."

* * *

### Yara

At eight-oh-two, the excavator reached for the roofline, and the building made a sound.

I want to describe it accurately, because accuracy is the only eulogy I trust. It wasn't a crash. It was a groan—not metal, not wood, something older, the sound of forty years of radiators clearing their throats at once, the sound I had listened to through five winters of tuning forks and felt pads, each unit with its own voice, 2B's pipes high and complaining, 5C's low and patient, the lobby boiler with its asthmatic rattle that I could diagnose from the sidewalk. The building spoke as it came down, one long syllable, and every person on that sidewalk flinched like we'd been called by name.

Mr. O's hand found mine. His grip was lighter than it used to be—the illness had taken most of it—but it was there, and it was deliberate.

"Now," he said. "Before the dust. Say it with me."

The phrase. The one from the hospital cot, lesson nineteen, the goodbye reserved for permanent partings—not *see you later*, not *safe travels*, but the words you spend on a leaving that has no return ticket. He had made me repeat it forty times until it stopped sounding foreign in my mouth, until it stopped sounding like anything but mine.

"*Nọ nudo,*" he said, quiet, and I said it with him, "*nọ nudo*," and then the rest of it, the long clause underneath, the one that means roughly *go well into the place I cannot follow, and carry my name with yours.*

Around us, the tenants went still. They didn't understand the words. They understood the occasion. Mrs. Adeyemi took off her hat. Dez lowered his camera. Even the excavator seemed to pause between bites, though machines don't pause, and I know that, and I choose to remember it differently.

Mr. O's eyes were closed. Tears ran into the creases of his face and he let them, unashamed, a man who had renounced his language publicly once to survive and had spent his last year paying the debt back with interest, teaching it to a piano tuner who barely talked, because the language didn't care who carried it. It only cared that someone did.

"Again," he whispered, as the third floor folded into the second.

"*Nọ nudo.*"

"Again. Louder. A building this size deserves volume."

So I said it louder, and Mrs. Adeyemi repeated it after me, wrong in every vowel and completely right in spirit, and then Dez tried it, laughing and crying at once, and then it was going around the crowd like a collection plate, everyone passing along sounds they didn't understand, holding them carefully anyway, and I thought: *this is how a language survives. Not dictionaries. This. A sidewalk full of people saying goodbye badly together.*

The wall came down at eight-nineteen. The dust rose up brown and enormous, swallowing the fence, swallowing the folding chairs, and Mrs. Adeyemi pulled us all back behind her like a one-woman barricade, and through it—through all of it—I kept my hand in Mr. O's and kept speaking, because he squeezed my fingers at every phrase, correcting nothing, only confirming.

When the dust settled enough to see, there was the lobby, open to the sky for the first time since 1961. The piano had been removed weeks ago—it sat in storage, insured, waiting for whoever would claim it—but the spot where it stood was visible, a clean rectangle in the filthy parquet, and I stared at it the way you stare at a grave marker.

"There," Mr. O said. "Where the piano lived. That is where I first sang to myself and caught the tuner listening." He opened his eyes. "Best mistake of my life, hiding in that room."

"You weren't hiding."

"I was always hiding. Then I stopped." He looked up at me. "You understand the difference now?"

I did. It had taken a notebook, a leak, a hearing where I stood up in front of lawyers and city officials and said the goodbye phrase out loud before I said anything else, opening testimony with a farewell to a building, and watched a room full of strangers go silent and then rise. But yes. I understood the difference.

* * *

### Dez

By nine it was half gone. Half. A building that took forty years to fill up took ninety minutes to empty out, and honestly I couldn't watch anymore, so I watched Mr. O instead, because he was watching like it was a play he'd paid good money for.

Patricia kept checking her watch. Hospital rules—he had till noon, she said, and Mr. O said, "Then at noon you may attempt to remove me and we shall see which of us is more stubborn," and Patricia just laughed, because by then everybody knew you didn't win arguments with him, you just lost them slowly.

My mom brought Mr. O a cup of Mrs. Adeyemi's coffee around nine-thirty, and he drank it and made a face and said, "Strong," and Mrs. Adeyemi, three chairs down, said, "Thank you," because she knew that was a compliment, and that whole exchange was more marriage than either of them has ever had and I'm just saying.

Then he waved Yara over and said something to him in the language, low, private, and Yara answered, and I swear on everything, Yara *answered*, fluently, a whole sentence, and Mr. O put his head back and laughed—that big rolling laugh that used to come through my bedroom floor at night when I was a kid and I never knew what it was, just that it meant somebody upstairs was happy.

I got it on video. The laugh, I mean. And the two of them talking in a dead language in front of a dying building. Forty seconds of footage. I'm not posting it. Some stuff isn't content. But I'm keeping it, because someday—when Mr. O is gone, and he knows it and we all know it and nobody says it—somebody's going to need proof that this happened. That the language had a second speaker, right there on the sidewalk, saying goodbye to the walls that taught him to listen.

* * *

### Yara

At noon Patricia won, as she was always going to. Mr. O let himself be wheeled toward the accessible van, pausing once to look back at the remains—the first floor still standing, stubborn, the doorway where I'd once stood frozen while an inspector walked past, the ghost of the mail table where notices appeared like weather.

"Thursday," he said to me. "You will come Thursday. Tape eleven. I saved tape eleven for last on purpose, and I will not explain why, because a student must earn his explanations."

"Thursday," I said.

"And Yara—" He gripped the armrests, gathering himself. "When they finish, and the lot is empty, and they put up the sign with the renderings of the glass tower—do not come here anymore. Grief needs a home, but it should not be a construction site. Come to the hospital instead. Bring the tapes. We have work, you and I, and my daughter is calling now, did I tell you? She calls on Sundays. Her vowels are terrible. We will fix them, the three of us, across an ocean, one Sunday at a time."

I hadn't known that. The daughter, calling. Something opened in my chest that I decided not to examine on a sidewalk.

"Thursdays and Sundays," I said.

"Thursdays and Sundays," he agreed, and let Patricia load him into the van, arguing cheerfully about the seatbelt the entire time.

I stayed until three. Most people drifted away by one—back to new apartments, new neighborhoods, lives in progress. Mrs. Adeyemi stayed with me. We didn't talk much. At one point she said, "The piano. When you tune it again, wherever it ends up—you'll tune it for all of us, won't you? Every note."

"Every note," I said.

At three o'clock the last wall came down, and the sound it made was small, almost apologetic, a building exhaling after holding its breath for sixty years. Dust rose, hung, settled. And underneath it, faint, or maybe imagined, or maybe real in the way that matters—I heard the radiators one last time. All of them. Every unit, every voice, the whole building's chorus letting out its final note, flat and imperfect and entirely itself.

I stood there until Mrs. Adeyemi touched my elbow and said the crew needed the sidewalk.

That night, in my new apartment with its strange young pipes and their unfamiliar accent, I unpacked the shoebox of cassettes and set tape eleven on top, unplayed, waiting for Thursday. Under my breath, to the radiator, in a language only two living people spoke—one of them asleep in a hospital across town—I said goodnight.

The pipes knocked back. New building, same grammar. I answered it.
