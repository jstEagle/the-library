## 7. Intermission

The conservatory had a rhythm the way a heart has one, and Rosalind learned it from the floor up, which was the only vantage the building offered her now. Mornings began with the radiators knocking awake in the basement, a percussion section tuning itself in the dark. By eight, the practice rooms on the second floor filled like lungs drawing breath—scales first, always scales, the same five-finger patterns she had once played with her ankles crossed on a piano stool too high for her, and then the repertoire, the Chopin and the Brahms and, increasingly, the contemporary stuff that sounded to her like weather systems arguing with themselves. By noon the halls smelled of coffee and rosin and the particular sourness of adolescent ambition. By three, someone was always crying in a stairwell. By six, the crying had usually resolved into something quieter, and the cleaning began.

She had a route now. She had been given a lanyard and a ring of keys and a cart with a squeaking left wheel that announced her like a town crier, and the students had learned to ignore the squeak the way they ignored the radiators, the way they ignored everything in the building that was not themselves and their hands and the impossible music trapped inside them like birds in a house.

She did not mind being ignored. Being ignored was a kind of weather she had learned to dress for. There was a comfort in it that she would not have admitted to anyone, that she barely admitted to herself: the invisibility of the janitor was the invisibility of the ghost, and a ghost, at least, is permitted to stay.

It was on a Thursday in the third week of October that the boy found her.

She was in the corridor outside the practice rooms on the second floor, the same corridor she had walked a thousand times in saddle shoes and then in heels and then in nothing at all but the memory of both, wringing out a mop in the janitor's sink with the window cracked to let the ammonia out. The window looked down on the courtyard, where a magnolia was losing its leaves in the slow, reluctant way of trees that had once been admired and now were merely old. She was watching a leaf let go when she heard the squeak of her own cart and turned to find a boy standing beside it, holding a folder of sheet music against his chest the way you hold a wound.

He was maybe seventeen. Long fingers, bitten nails, the particular pallor of someone who had spent October indoors. His hair did what hair does when it has been pushed out of the eyes too many times. She recognized the pallor. She had worn it for years.

"You're the janitor," he said, and then, wincing at himself, "sorry. That's—obviously."

"It's all right," she said. "It's on the lanyard."

"I'm Theo. Theo Marsh. I'm second-year, piano track." He said it the way students said it, as though *piano track* were a diagnosis. "I don't know if you— I mean, I don't suppose you'd—"

He stopped. She wrung the mop and waited. Waiting was a skill the mop had taught her; you could not rush water out of it, you could only keep applying pressure and let the thing give up what it was holding.

"I have a competition in three weeks," he said. "The Kessler. It's a big one, it's—everyone says it's the one that matters, at this stage. And I can't get the second movement of my piece to work. It works in my hands at midnight when no one's listening and then it dies in the lesson and it died in the mock run-through yesterday and Professor Anand says I'm *manufacturing difficulty*—" he said the phrase with the bitterness of a direct quote, "—and I don't know what that means, and I've been walking around for an hour trying to figure out who to ask, and you were here."

"I was here," she agreed.

"I know it's not your job. I know you're—" He gestured at the mop, the cart, the ammonia, the whole architecture of her afternoon, and she watched him realize, with the slow horror of the young, that he had no idea how to address what she was. "I just thought. You've been in every practice room in this building. You hear everything. You hear *everyone*. You must know what people sound like when it's working."

She set the mop in the sink and let it lean there, dripping.

"And what does it sound like," she said, "when it's working?"

He blinked. She had the sense that no one had asked him a question in weeks, that he had been living inside a machine of instruction and correction and evaluation, and that a question, an actual question, was a door he had forgotten the building contained.

"Like—" He thought. She could see him thinking; it moved across his face like weather. "Like the room gets bigger. Like the room isn't a room anymore."

"Yes," she said, before she could stop herself, and the word came out of her with a certainty that startled them both.

* * *

They ended up in the small practice room at the end of the corridor—Room 209, not hers, never hers, she had been careful about that, careful the way a person is careful walking past their own grave—and he played the movement for her while she sat on the radiator cover with her hands folded in her lap like an audience of one who had paid nothing for her ticket.

The piece was a Schubert sonata. The late B-flat, which was a choice that told her things about him: that he was either very confident or very frightened, and that these were probably the same thing in him. He played the first movement competently, beautifully in places, and then came the second movement, the slow one, the one that was killing him, and she heard immediately what was wrong, and hearing it was like putting her hand into cold water up to the wrist.

He was playing it safely.

He was playing it the way you play something when you have decided, somewhere below language, that the thing you feel about it is too much, that the room will not be able to hold it, that if you let the phrase breathe the way it wants to breathe you will be standing in front of everyone with your chest open and no one will know what to do with that, least of all you. He was playing the dynamics written on the page and none of the ones written underneath them, the ones in no ink at all. He was manufacturing difficulty, in a way—manufacturing the difficulty of restraint, which is the hardest difficulty there is and the one teachers are worst at naming, because it looks like polish from the outside and feels like drowning from within.

"Stop," she said, at the bar where it always died.

He stopped. His hands hovered over the keys.

"Do you know what the pedal is for?"

He looked at her as if she'd asked him what the piano was for. "Sustaining. Blending. Legato—"

"It's for lying," she said. "It's for making the audience believe the sound is still alive when the string has already been struck and is already dying. Every note you play is dying. The pedal is how you let it die beautifully instead of just letting it stop. And you're not using it to let anything die. You're using it to keep everything alive at once, all the sound piled up, because you're afraid of the silence between the phrases. Listen." She hummed the phrase, badly, her voice a rusted hinge after twenty years of disuse, and marked the silence. "There. That gap. That's not nothing. That's the whole movement. That's where Schubert is saying the thing he can't say in notes. And you're papering over it because the gap scares you."

"It does," Theo said, very quietly.

"Good," she said. "It should. Play it again and let it scare you. Don't fix it. Let it be the scariest four seconds in the piece. If you try to make it comfortable, the jury will hear you being comfortable, and comfortable is the one thing that movement is not allowed to be."

He played it again. It was not fixed. It was, if anything, more broken—but it was broken in the right place now, broken along the seam the composer had built the piece around, and she heard the room get bigger for exactly one phrase before he flinched and closed it again.

"That," she said. "That. Do you hear what happened there?"

"Something happened," he said, staring at his hands. "I don't know what."

"You will. That's the terrible part. You'll spend the rest of your life chasing that and mostly missing it, and every so often it'll happen and you'll think, *there it is, that's the thing,* and you won't be able to say what the thing is, and you'll want to tell everyone and there will be no one to tell, because—" She stopped.

The radiator ticked. Somewhere down the hall, someone was playing the same eight bars of a Liszt étude over and over, grinding them down like someone sharpening a knife.

"Because it's a lonely thing," Theo finished, not looking at her.

"It's a lonely thing," she said.

* * *

He came back the next day. She had known he would; she had watched him carry the certainty out of the practice room the way you carry a lit match in wind, cupped and careful, and she had thought, *put it out, put it out, don't bring that here,* and had said nothing, because she had never in her life successfully told anyone anything, including herself.

He came back with questions about fingering in the third movement, and then about how to warm up on competition mornings, and then—on the fourth day, sitting on the floor of the corridor with his back against the lockers while she polished the brass plate beside the recital hall door—about whether she had ever played.

"Everyone plays," she said, working the cloth in small circles. "It's a conservatory."

"No, I mean—" He caught himself. "I mean, you hear things. You said the thing about the pedal, the thing about the gap, and Professor Anand has been teaching me for a year and never said it like that. You talk like someone who—" He stopped again. He was always stopping, this boy; he lived at the edge of sentences. "Were you a student here?"

The cloth went still on the brass. In the polished plate she could see the corridor upside down, and herself in it, small and inverted, a woman standing in a world where up and down had quietly traded places.

"Once," she said. "A long time ago."

"Did you compete? The Kessler?"

"Everyone competes."

"Did you win?"

She looked at the brass plate a moment longer. ENSEMBLE HALL, it said, in letters that some previous janitor, some previous ghost, had kept bright for decades without anyone once looking down and reading them.

"I played," she said. "That's a different question, and the answer to it is more complicated than you'd think, and I have to do the doors now, Theo."

He took the hint the way the young take all hints about the past—gracelessly, and only halfway, and with the firm intention of returning. She watched him go with his folder hugged to his chest and thought: *he'll ask someone. He'll ask Anand, or he'll ask in the office, or he'll look in a program archive, because they're all the same, they're all hungry, they're all exactly what I was,* and she felt the old fear move through her like water through a wall, finding every crack.

But underneath the fear, quieter, in a room of herself she had not entered in years, something else was sitting at a piano with its hands in its lap, waiting to be asked to play.

* * *

That night the letters were waiting for her, as they were always waiting, in the bottom drawer of her dresser under a sweater she never wore. She had not opened the tin in four days. She had a system now, a discipline: one letter, no more, and only on nights when she was steady, because the letters were not letters, they were doors, and behind every door was Marta's voice, dry and exact and Estonian-accented even in English, saying things Rosalind was not sure she could survive hearing in any order at any speed.

She had read four so far. One to a cellist named Ilmar Tamm, full of a bitterness about the conservatory's board that Rosalind had found almost funny until the third paragraph, where it stopped being funny. One to a woman named Agnes Vihalem, apparently an old friend from Tartu, mostly about the weather and Marta's hands, which were failing, which had been failing for years, which Marta described with a precision that was worse than complaint: *the left ring finger goes where it likes now, like a guest who has stopped pretending to be interested in the conversation.* One that was only an address and a date and the words *I am sending this because I said I would,* which Rosalind had read five times trying to decide what it was refusing to say. And one to a name she did not recognize—E. Kask—which was the shortest of all, and which had made her put the tin back in the drawer for two full days, because the handwriting on that one was different, slower, pressed so hard into the paper that the pen had nearly gone through.

She had not yet found the one addressed to herself. She had looked. She had told herself she hadn't looked, and then she had looked again, and it was not there, and its absence had a shape, the way an empty chair has a shape.

Tonight she took the tin out and held it on her knees under the lamp and did not open it. Down the hall in her building, a couple was arguing in a language she didn't speak, their voices rising and falling with the rhythm of a piece neither of them was going to finish. She thought about the gap in the Schubert. *Let it be the scariest four seconds in the piece.* She had said that to a boy as if she had learned it from life, and she had, she had, but she had learned it from a woman with failing hands who used to stand at the back of practice rooms and say, in her flat Baltic voice, *the silence, Rosalind, the silence is also music, why do you run from it.*

There were more letters. She could feel them in the tin under her palms, the weight of them, the paper-and-ink weight of everything Marta had never sent. And somewhere in that weight was the one with her own name on it, or was not, and either answer was a door, and she was so tired of standing in corridors.

She opened the tin. She took the next letter from the top without reading the addressee first, a discipline she immediately broke, because the discipline had never once held.

The envelope said: *To whoever finds these, if anyone ever does.*

Rosalind sat very still. The argument down the hall rose to a crescendo and cut off, and in the silence that followed—the gap, the gap is also music—she slid her finger under the flap, and the paper gave, and Marta's voice, for the first time, was addressed to her.
