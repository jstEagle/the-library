## 4. Frequency 87.3

I want to be clear about what I did not do in the first week: I did not become a madman. I became something worse — a man with a system.

The distinction matters to me. A madman hears a song from his dead mother's kitchen on an unlicensed frequency at four in the morning and starts telling people about it. A man with a system buys a notebook, a signal meter he has no idea how to read, and a thermos, and begins the quiet bureaucratic work of proving to himself that he is not insane. Anderlecht is full of men with systems. It's how we grieve here. We don't light candles; we open spreadsheets.

The notebook was one of the library's discarded ones — a marbled composition book, water-swollen at one corner, stamped DISCARD in red across the inside cover like a small execution order. I rescued it from the weeding cart because it seemed fitting. Everything important in my life was going to live in a book nobody officially wanted.

On the first page I wrote, in my best cataloguing hand:

**Frequency 87.3 FM. Observations begin 11 March.**

And beneath it, because I am who I am:

*Signal strength: unknown. Content: one (1) song, duration approx. 3 min 40 sec. Provenance: disputed.*

Disputed. As if there were another party claiming authorship. As if somewhere in the city a rival archivist had filed a counter-record asserting that the melody my mother hummed over boiling potatoes for twenty years belonged instead to the air itself, or to some dead Belgian composer, or to coincidence. I suppose, in fairness, coincidence had a strong case. Coincidence always does. That's what makes it so unbearable.

* * *

Here is what I knew by the end of the first week, which is to say here is what I had proven with the rigor of a man who once catalogued 4,000 paperbacks by smell alone during a humidity crisis:

The station broadcast only between two and five in the morning. Not approximately. Exactly. At 2:00:00 the static on 87.3 changed texture — I can't describe it better than that; static has textures the way silence does, and this static went from the flat grey of an empty channel to something grainier, warmer, like a room where someone had recently been breathing. And at 5:00:00 it stopped mid-breath, no fade, no sign-off, as if someone had simply stood up and left.

No call sign. No station identification. No advertisements — and this was the detail that convinced me the thing was real rather than a hallucination with good production values, because even my subconscious, which had spent eleven years generating increasingly elaborate dreams about overdue books, would not have thought to omit advertising. Every pirate station I'd ever heard of begged for money within the first hour. This one wanted nothing. It just played.

And what it played. That was the other thing I had to map, because the song did not come every night.

Night one: nothing. Three hours of that warm grainy static, and me in the parked car outside my building with the engine off and my breath fogging the windshield, feeling like the world's least glamorous surveillance operative. Night two: nothing again, plus a parking ticket, which felt personal. Night three, at 3:17 a.m., the song.

It came in exactly as it had the first time — that opening phrase, six notes descending like stairs in a house you grew up in, and then her rhythm underneath it, the syncopation she never knew she was doing, the little hitch before the fourth bar where she'd lift whatever spoon was in her hand and conduct herself for half a measure. The recording was rough. There was a hum under everything, fifty-cycle, mains electricity, and once, faintly, a door closing somewhere in the room where it had been captured. Whoever had recorded it had done so standing close to her. Close enough that when she reached the hitch in the fourth bar, you could hear — and I am not inventing this, I have played it back forty times — you could hear someone else inhale, softly, the way people do when they're listening hard.

She had an audience of one, once. Now she had an audience of one again, sitting in a cold car outside his own apartment building, gripping a steering wheel like it might steer him somewhere.

The song ended at 3:21. Then nothing else all night. No DJ. No voice. Just static until five.

So it wasn't a music station. It was a message with a delivery schedule.

* * *

By the second week I had restructured my entire life around a three-hour window, which sounds dramatic until you remember my entire life previously had been structured around a different window — midnight to dawn, the library — and the new window slotted into the old one like a shelf bracket into a pre-drilled hole. I worked nights. I drove home. Instead of sleeping, I sat in the car and listened. I slept from six to noon, badly, dreaming of frequencies, and woke to catalogue the results.

The notebook filled. I gave each broadcast a call number, because of course I did. **87.3/M-001** through **87.3/M-014**. I logged date, time-on, time-off, signal quality (a five-point scale of my own devising: *dead, ghostly, marginal, present, close*), weather, and any incidental audio. The incidentals were the part that kept me honest, because they were evidence of a physical world. On M-006 I logged: *"00:40 elapsed — cough, male, dry."* On M-009: *"2:12–2:19 — sound of water running, then stopping. Pipes?"* On M-011, the night it rained: *"Signal degrades with precipitation. Antenna likely outdoor, exposed, possibly rooftop."*

Possibly rooftop. I sat with that note for a long time. Because a rooftop antenna meant a building, and a building meant an address, and an address meant that the past — which I had spent eleven years treating as a sealed wing of the library, accessible by appointment to no one — had a *location*. You could stand under it. You could knock on its door.

I want to explain what that did to me, and I'm going to try, though I suspect I'll fail, because the feeling didn't have a name and I've since looked.

For eleven years I had organized myself around absence. My mother died in October — I'll say it plainly now, here, in the privacy of my own notebook narration, because I haven't said it plainly anywhere yet — my mother died eleven years ago, and I responded the way I respond to everything, which is that I shelved it. High shelf. Restricted access. I visited her apartment monthly and stood in the hallway like a meter reader. I catalogued strangers' lives at the library with a tenderness I never once turned toward my own. I told myself this was order. It was actually just grief wearing a lanyard.

And then a song with no address came out of the dark and asked me to find it, and I discovered that wanting something is a completely different mechanical action from enduring something. Endurance is passive. Wanting has a direction. For the first time in years I caught myself walking faster than necessary. I caught myself checking the clock at work — not the way I usually check clocks, which is to confirm that time is still passing, but the way you check a clock when you are *waiting*, which is a verb I had apparently removed from my vocabulary sometime around the funeral.

Odile — no, I hadn't met Odile yet. Let me not get ahead of my own file. At this point in the record there was no Odile, no Jonas, no lieutenant, none of it. There was just me, a swollen notebook, and a frequency.

* * *

The mapping took nineteen days.

I triangulated crudely, the way an amateur must: driving the ring road with the radio up, noting where the signal strengthened and where it dissolved into the general electromagnetic slurry of the city. Signal strength rose along the western edge of the Marolles and fell away sharply past the canal. On the twelfth day I found the sweet spot — a stretch of dead-end street behind a row of shuttered fabric warehouses where the needle on my cheap meter, which I had finally learned to read by pretending it was a very opinionated patron, jumped into the red and stayed there.

The tower was at the end of that street.

I say tower generously. It was a lattice mast, maybe fifteen meters, bolted to the roof of a warehouse that hadn't sold fabric since before I was born, with a guy-wire running down to a rusted anchor in the pavement and a cable snaking over the parapet into the building. Painted on the mast's base plate, almost gone: a phone number from an era when phone numbers had seven digits. Someone had climbed this thing recently — the cable was zip-tied in places where the original clips had failed, and the ties were still pale, unweathered. Recent maintenance. Someone was keeping this signal alive the way you keep anything alive: imperfectly, repeatedly, at height, in the dark.

I stood there at 4 a.m. in the rain-shadow of the warehouse and felt something I can only describe as professional respect. Eleven years I'd kept a library's night shift going alone, and I knew exactly how much invisible labor goes into making a thing available to strangers who will never know your name. Whoever ran 87.3 was a colleague. A colleague broadcasting my mother's unfinished song into the sleeping city, three hours a night, asking nothing.

Why?

That question had been circling me for nineteen days and it finally landed. Nobody broadcasts without wanting something. Even the silent ones — especially the silent ones. A station with no call sign, no ads, no voice, playing one unfinished song on a loop schedule measured in days — that wasn't programming. That was a *lure*. That was a fishing line dropped into the dark water of the city, and I had been swimming along minding my own avoidance for eleven years, and I had bitten.

Which raised the uncomfortable follow-up: bait implies a specific fish. Had they been fishing for anyone? Or for me?

I laughed out loud at that, alone on a dead-end street at 4 a.m., and the laugh echoed off the warehouse shutters and came back sounding like someone else's. Grief makes you grandiose. Of course it wasn't for me. The odds were astronomical. Songs leak. Tapes migrate. Some musician had known her, or known someone who knew her, and the melody had drifted into their repertoire the way melodies do, the way my mother's humming had drifted into me without either of us signing anything.

Except.

Except the hitch in the fourth bar. Except the fact that the radio version continued precisely past the point where — well. I'm getting ahead of the file again. At this point I hadn't yet opened the cassette; I hadn't even admitted there might be a cassette. At this point all I had was the sense, growing nightly, that the song on 87.3 was not a cover. It was a continuation. And continuations require a source.

* * *

I went back the next night with tape and paper.

The note took me forty minutes to write, which is absurd for nine sentences, but I rewrote it six times, trying to calibrate the exact register between *deranged stranger* and *government official*, and landed, inevitably, somewhere in between: the deranged government official. It read:

*To whoever maintains this transmitter —*

*I've been listening to 87.3 for three weeks. The song you play. I know it. I know where it comes from, or I know part of where it comes from, and I think you may know the rest. I'm not police, press, or collecting society. I shelve books at the municipal library between midnight and dawn. If you'd rather talk than be found, leave a sign on this pole and I'll watch for it. If you'd rather I stop listening, tell me that too and I will, though I can't promise honestly.*

*Sable Halloran.*

I signed my real name. I stood there holding the note against the mast base and understood, with the clarity that only comes at four in the morning in the rain, that signing my real name was the whole point. For eleven years I had filed myself under *miscellaneous*. I had been a tenant of my own biography, paying rent in routine, touching nothing, altering nothing, leaving the premises exactly as found. This note — wet, misspelled twice and corrected, taped to a rusted pole at the edge of the Marolles — was the first document I had authored in over a decade that committed me to anything.

I used three strips of gaffer tape, because if you're going to leave a message for a ghost station, you do it properly. Then I stepped back and looked at the mast rising into the rain against the orange sodium wash of the streetlight, cables humming faintly with current they were carrying up to the dark, and I felt the strangest sensation of having posted a letter to the past and included a return address.

Then I did the thing I would have mocked, in my old life, in anyone else. I walked to the corner, where I could see the mast without being seen from it, and I waited for dawn, watching to see whether anyone would come take the note down.

Nobody came. Of course nobody came. It was 4:30 in the morning and raining and the fish does not inspect the hook while the fisherman sleeps.

But as I finally drove home through the emptying dark, the heater finally winning against the damp, I passed the canal and saw the sky going that bruised grey that means the city is about to resume pretending, and I realized I was smiling. Actually smiling, alone, at a windshield. And beneath the smile, quieter, the other thing — the true thing, the one I'd write in the notebook later, in cataloguing hand, as if writing it small would keep it contained:

*M-020. Note deployed, 04:31. Subject experienced, for the first time in eleven years, the sensation of wanting. Prognosis: poor. Subject intends to continue.*

At 2 a.m. the next night, the static on 87.3 changed texture before the hour turned. Early. Off-schedule. And for the first time, a voice came through — low, unhurried, a man's voice with age in it, saying just four words before the song began:

*"All right, Halloran. Listen."*
