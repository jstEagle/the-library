## 22. The Owner's Copy

A year is a unit of measurement the way a shelf is a unit of grief: technically accurate, emotionally useless. You can stand back and say *one year* and it means nothing until you notice that you've stopped flinching at the fourth stair of your mother's building, or that you now buy two coffees instead of one and drink both, which is either abundance or a problem I've decided not to inventory.

It was October again. Anderlecht had put on its usual autumn costume — wet stone, sodium light, the canal smelling like a rumor of itself — and I was doing what I always did between midnight and dawn, except that everything about it was different.

I'll explain that paradox, because it took me most of the year to understand it myself.

* * *

The workshop meets Thursdays at seven, in the small seminar room off the children's wing, the one with the radiator that knocks like a polite ghost asking to be let in. We call it *The Owner's Copy*, though officially the library calls it "Community Oral History Practicum, Level One," because libraries are required by some ancient covenant to make beautiful things sound like tax forms.

There are eleven of us. There were fourteen in September, but three drifted off, which Odile says is the correct attrition rate for memory work. "You cannot archive the unwilling," she told them at the door, not unkindly, handing each one a pamphlet as they left. It sounded like a blessing and a sentence at the same time. That is Odile's entire range, and I have come to love it the way you love weather.

Odile is eighty-one now. She moves through the room like a woman who has read every book in every house she's ever entered, including the ones people keep under floorboards. She co-leads with me, though "co-leads" undersells it. I teach the mechanics — how to hold a recorder, how to ask a question that doesn't lead the witness, how to sit in silence long enough that the silence starts talking. Odile teaches the rest, which is to say she teaches the part that can't be taught. She teaches people how to receive a story without dropping it.

Last week a man named Ferre, sixty-ish, retired tram driver, told us about his father's habit of singing Flemish sea shanties while shaving, badly, with the door open, so the whole apartment participated whether it wanted to or not. Ferre got to the part where his father died — quick, in the bath, mid-shanty — and stopped. The room did what we'd trained it to do, which was nothing. Just sat there holding the shape of the thing.

And then Ferre said, "Nobody has ever asked me about the singing before. Everyone asks about the dying."

Odile wrote something in her notebook. Later she showed me. It said: *The archive begins where the condolence card ends.*

I have started keeping a notebook too. Mine is less aphoristic. Mostly it says things like *ask about kitchens* and *the radio question worked on Marta V.* and, once, in handwriting shakier than usual, *she would have liked this room.*

She being my mother. I write it now without the flinch. Progress, for me, looks like a noun surviving contact with a pronoun.

* * *

Here is what changed, structurally, in the year since the broadcast. I'll give it to you the way I'd give it to an auditor, because old habits are load-bearing.

Item: the case reopened in November. The lieutenant — I still can't put his name in my mouth; on paper it looked like any other name, which was somehow worse — was charged in February. He never stood trial. A stroke in April, in his garden, face-down among roses someone else planted. The papers called it "before justice could be served," which was generous. Odile called it "the last edit," and refused to say more, and I understood that refusing to say more was itself a kind of testimony, the kind she'd been practicing since 1989.

Item: Jonas donated the reels to the national archive in June, under the heading *Marta Halloran Collection, 1989–1990*. He insisted on the name. The archivist on duty tried to file it under "Verhoeven/Tomas (related materials)" and Jonas stood at the counter, all six-foot-something of him, hands flat on the wood, and said, "She carried it. She gets the heading." They caved. Jonas reports that he then went home and slept for fourteen hours, "like a man who had finally put down a piano."

Item: the frequency still exists. 87.3, still unnamed, still adless, still broadcasting only between two and five in the morning. Jonas runs it from the pool, which is marginally less derelict than before — he fixed the roof over the shallow end, installed a space heater he calls "the sun," and painted one wall the exact green of the tiles in his memory of the place when it was full of water and children. On quiet nights he plays whatever he wants. On loud nights he plays what the city needs.

Item: I still work nights. This surprised everyone, including me. After the broadcast, after the case, after all of it, people kept asking when I'd move to days, as if daylight were a promotion. But the night shift is where I learned to see people — the insomniacs, the students, the man who comes in every Tuesday to read obituaries from cities he's never visited ("checking I'm not in them yet," he says). The night shift taught me that a city keeps its truest records after midnight. I didn't want a promotion out of that. I wanted tenure.

Item: I entered my mother's apartment fully, emptied it fully, catalogued it honestly, and opened every window. Then I gave up the lease. The last thing I took out was the piano bench cushion, for no reason I can defend except that it smelled faintly of her — lavender and pencil shavings — and I am a librarian, and librarians know that smell is the only format that never degrades.

That's the audit. Balanced, more or less. What the ledger doesn't show is the texture, and the texture is tonight, so let me stop itemizing and tell you about tonight.

* * *

Tonight is the anniversary. Not of the broadcast — well, yes, of the broadcast, that too — but of the song. One year ago tonight, give or take the fog of those weeks, I sat in a parked car at four in the morning and heard my dead mother humming through a pirate transmitter, and the past reached out and touched me without permission, and I sat there until sunrise because standing up felt like agreeing to live in a world where that could happen.

Jonas announced the anniversary broadcast two weeks ago. No fanfare. Just his voice, gravel and warmth, at 3 a.m.: *"One year ago tonight, this station played a song with no address. Tonight it gets its address."*

He wouldn't tell me what he planned. "You'll hear it with everyone else," he said, "or it doesn't count." Jonas has strong feelings about shared experience. He spent forty years believing recorded sound could testify, and then watched it actually testify, and came out the other side believing that listening is a communal act or it's nothing. I think he's right. I also think he just likes knowing I'm annoyed.

So tonight I closed the library early — well, "early" meaning I let the last stragglers stay until one, because I am constitutionally incapable of expelling a person from a warm room — and set up the reading room the way Odile and I do it for the workshop's final session: chairs in a circle, no podium, a single lamp, and the good biscuits, the ones I pay for myself because the library's biscuit budget is a fiction invented to hurt us.

Eleven regulars. Plus Odile, arriving at half past one in a taxi she insisted on paying for herself, wearing a scarf I recognized — wool, rust-colored, the kind of thing knitted by someone who loved you in a practical register. She sat down, surveyed the circle, and said, "Well. We're all here to listen to a dead woman sing. Pass the biscuits."

That's the eulogy she'd want, if she wanted one. She doesn't. She's told me twice, in writing, with witnesses.

* * *

At 2:58 the room went quiet in that specific way rooms go quiet when eleven strangers have agreed, wordlessly, to be nervous together. Ferre checked his portable radio, adjusted the antenna, checked it again. A young woman named Ines — nineteen, architecture student, joined the workshop after the broadcast made her curious about "what else the city is hiding" — held her phone up like a candle. The radiator knocked twice, politely, and settled.

At 3:00 exactly, static. Then Jonas's voice:

*"Good morning, Anderlecht. You know the rules here — no names unless they're earned. Tonight, one name, earned across thirty-five years and one act of unbearable courage. What you're about to hear was written in a kitchen on Rue de la Cible, performed once in a squatted theater, silenced for decades, finished by the only person who could finish it, and carried — hidden inside a piano, if you can believe it, and you should, because pianos are where this city hides its heart — by a woman named Marta Halloran.*

*"This is 'The Inventory.' It's hers. It was always hers. Sing along if you know it. If you don't know it, learn it."*

And then my mother's voice filled the reading room.

Not the cassette version — the young voice, mid-song, cut off by a door and shouting. The complete version. The one we spliced, the one we broadcast, the one Jonas has apparently remastered over the winter with the tenderness of a man restoring a painting he was apprenticed to protect. Her voice moved through the verses I knew and then into the ending I hadn't known existed until last year, the ending she must have written in the years of silence, the ending that goes:

*Count what remains, not what was taken.*
*The shelf remembers every hand.*
*I was the tenant of my own sorrow*
*until I signed for it with my own hand.*

I had heard it maybe fifty times since the retrieval. I had helped splice it. I had listened to it alone in the booth at four in the morning more times than I will admit even to this page. And it still did what it does, which is reach into the chest and rearrange the furniture.

But here is the difference between last year and this year, and it's the whole point of everything, so lean in:

Last year I heard it alone, in a parked car, and it was a haunting.

Tonight I heard it in a circle of eleven people, and Ines — nineteen, born fifteen years after the theater, twenty years after the raid — began, hesitantly, to hum the chorus. And then Ferre, who cannot carry a tune in a bucket, joined her. And then Odile, who has a surprisingly clear soprano buried under eighty-one years of archivist gravity, sang the verse about the shelf remembering every hand, straight through, eyes closed, hand resting on my forearm like a paperweight keeping a document from blowing away.

And I sang too. Badly. Completely. Alone in the way that matters — alone in my own voice, my own grief, my own name for it — and together in every way that counts.

My mother's song, sung by strangers, in a library she furnished, on a frequency that refuses to die, credited at last. If grief is an inventory, then this was the moment the ledger balanced. Not resolved. Balanced. There's a difference, and I have a year of receipts proving it.

* * *

After the broadcast ended — Jonas played it twice, because he is generous and also a little cruel — nobody wanted to go home. So we did what our people do. We told stories.

Ferre told the shanty story again, better this time, with the melody attempted. Ines talked about her grandmother's habit of writing addresses of demolished buildings in the margins of her cookbooks, "so the food would know where it came from." A quiet man named Bram, who has attended four sessions and spoken perhaps ten sentences total, described, in a voice like a door opening, how his mother used to count the stairs aloud every night — "and I thought it was a tic, and after she died I found a notebook where she'd written the number differently every single time, and I realized she wasn't counting the stairs, she was counting the days, and I will never know of what" — and then he sat down and cried, and we let him, and the radiator knocked its soft applause.

Odile held court near the lamp. At one point she caught my eye across the circle and tapped the arm of her chair, twice, which is our signal — developed over a year of workshops — meaning *this one's ready, be gentle, and also don't be gentle, gentleness is sometimes a form of condescension.* I went over and sat beside Bram and asked him about the notebook. Not about the mother. About the notebook. Because the archive begins where the condolence card ends, and Odile's aphorisms are load-bearing too.

By four-thirty the circle had dissolved into the wet street, carrying their stories home like borrowed books, due back whenever they're ready. Odile stayed. She always stays last. We stood in the empty reading room, two keepers of records, surrounded by shelves that hold everything except the things that matter, which we hold instead.

"You know," she said, looking around, "your mother shelved books here. Did I ever tell you? Before you were born. Part-time, the winter of '74. She complained that the Dewey system was 'a tyranny of decimals.'" She smiled at something thirty-five years away. "I filed her complaint. Officially. It's in the archive somewhere. *Suggestion re: classification, received and respectfully declined.*"

"You archived my mother's complaint about decimal points."

"I archive everything. It's my whole personality. Ask anyone." She pulled her rust scarf tighter. "Come. Walk me to the taxi. My hips have opinions and they're all negative reviews."

At the door she stopped, turned, and looked at me with the expression she reserves for documents of unusual importance.

"A year ago you were a tenant," she said. "Of your own life. Of her death. Of that song." She touched my cheek, briefly, the way you handle a first edition. "Look at the ledger now."

Then she was gone into the dark, and I stood in the doorway of the library like an idiot, crying a little, in the good way, the way that means the roof is holding.

* * *

Which brings me to the ledger. The actual one. The guest ledger at the front desk, the big cloth-bound book where visitors sign in during special events — exhibitions, readings, the memorial concerts the city holds in the atrium every spring. Tonight, before the broadcast, before the circle, I had done something I'd been circling for weeks the way I once circled a transmitter tower with a roll of tape and a note.

The library's children's room houses a piano. Upright, walnut, donated anonymously in 2011 by "a friend of the library." Children bang on it daily. Inside it, for eight years, a reel of tape waited like a heart inside a chest, holding the voice of a police lieutenant giving an order, and the last sounds of a man named Tomas Verhoeven, and the breathing of a woman deciding what silence was worth.

The donation record lists the donor as anonymous. But donations have paperwork, and paperwork has a line for next-of-kin, and this summer — after the archive transfer, after Jonas's counter standoff, after Odile's *last edit* — I asked the head librarian, formally, in writing, to amend the record. To attach a name. To note that the anonymous friend of the library had been identified, posthumously, with her family's consent, as Marta Halloran, 1946–2014, night-shift survivor, kitchen composer, keeper of terrible and necessary things.

She approved it. She cried at her desk, which she'd deny. Librarians cry the way archives leak: slowly, structurally, somewhere you'll never find.

So tonight, before the others arrived, I opened the guest ledger to the current page, and beneath the signatures of the evening — Ines, Ferre, Bram, the obituary man who'd come for the memorial concert in spring and stayed for everything since — I wrote my name. Not in the staff column. I've signed the staff column eleven hundred times; it's a reflex, like breathing or alphabetizing.

In the relationship field, where visitors write *friend of the performer* or *colleague of the speaker*, I wrote:

**Family of the donor.**

Four words. Eleven years, one song, one reel of tape, one letter that waited behind a radiator, one lieutenant, one pool with a painted wall, one eighty-one-year-old archivist with a scarf the color of endurance. Four words.

I stood there after writing it, pen still in hand, and felt the strangest sensation — not relief exactly, and not joy, but something adjacent to both, the feeling of a key turning in a lock you'd forgotten was a lock. For eleven years I had been a tenant of my mother's death, paying rent in avoidance, keeping her grief on a high shelf where I wouldn't brush against it. Tenants don't change anything. Tenants wait for the lease to end.

Owners sign. Owners amend the record. Owners open windows.

Outside, the city was doing its 4 a.m. work — a tram sighing somewhere, gulls arguing over the canal, the wet stone shining like a ledger waiting to be written in. Somewhere across town, in a swimming pool with one green wall, a man in his sixties was logging the night's broadcast in a notebook older than I am, and somewhere in the national archive, under a heading that reads *Marta Halloran Collection*, a reel of tape sits in climate-controlled silence, testifying forever, available to anyone who asks.

And somewhere in the children's room, thirty feet from where I stood, a seven-year-old tomorrow will bang out noise on a walnut upright, having no idea what sleeps inside it, and that is exactly right. Some things should be played. Some things should be guarded. The wisdom is in knowing which is which, and I learned it from a woman who hummed while she cooked and never explained herself, because she was busy protecting a boy who would take thirty-five years and one pirate frequency to understand.

I closed the ledger. I turned off the reading room lamp. And on my way out, I did what I now do every night, the small ritual that replaced the old one — I paused at the piano, rested one hand on its lid, and said, quietly, to the room, to the city, to whoever keeps the master copy:

"Goodnight, Mum. Inventory's balanced. I'm keeping the copy."

The radiator knocked once, politely, and let me out.
