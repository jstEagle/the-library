## 5. Breaking and Entering, Gently

The kitchen window of 14 Alder Street had always stuck. That was the thing I remembered first—not the yellow notice curling on the door like a dead leaf, not the sag of the porch roof or the way the hedge had grown into a green fist around the front steps—but the window over the sink, the one that needed a hip and a curse to open even when my mother was alive to curse at it.

I stood in the backyard for twenty minutes on a Tuesday night after my shift, telling myself I was only checking the property line. Telling myself that librarians were professionally curious about abandoned things. The moon was doing its best through cloud cover, and the yard looked exactly as it had in my worst dreams: the clothesline pole still standing with no line, the birdbath full of black rainwater, the fence boards leaning against each other like drunks holding each other up.

I hadn't come inside. I want to be clear about that, because what happened next has an order to it and I've spent enough nights shelving other people's lives to know that order matters.

I came to look. Then I came to touch. Then, somewhere between the third visit and the fourth, I came with a flashlight in my coat pocket, and a flashlight is never just a flashlight.

* * *

The window gave on the second shove, the way it always had—a shriek of swollen wood, then surrender. Cold air rolled out to meet me, air that had been sitting in that house for three years learning how to be still. It smelled like dust and mouse and, underneath it, faintly, impossibly, like the lemon oil my mother used on the table that wasn't there anymore.

I climbed through the kitchen window of my childhood home like a burglar, which is what I was, and landed on linoleum that cracked under my sneaker like old ice.

"Hello?" I said, to no one, because you say hello. Fifteen years of night shifts teach you that empty rooms deserve acknowledgment too.

The kitchen was bare except for the stove, still there, white enamel gone the color of weak tea. A drawer hung open. On the counter, someone had left a plastic water bottle half-full and a granola bar wrapper folded into a neat triangle.

I stopped breathing.

People don't fold their trash into triangles. Squatters don't fold their trash into triangles. Teenagers don't fold anything. But there it sat, precise as origami, and beside it a second wrapper, also folded, also neat, arranged parallel to the first like books awaiting a patron.

I should have turned around. I had a flashlight, a working knowledge of every exit, and fifteen years of practice at leaving places before anyone noticed me. Instead I said, louder this time, "I'm not here to hurt anybody," which is what people say right before somebody gets hurt.

From the dark of the hallway came a voice. Young. Female. Steadier than mine.

"You're not the landlord."

It wasn't a question, but I answered it anyway. "No."

"Then who are you?"

And here's the strange part, the part I keep turning over like a stone with something alive under it: I told her the truth. "I grew up here."

Silence. Long enough for me to count the sounds of the house settling—the tick of cooling pipes that hadn't carried water in years, the scritch of something small in the walls, my own pulse doing its work in my ears.

Then a flashlight clicked on, held high, blinding me, and behind it stood a girl. Maybe sixteen. Thin as a bookmark, all elbows and wariness, wearing a man's flannel shirt over a hoodie over whatever else she owned. Her hair was cut short and unevenly, the kind of cut you give yourself with kitchen scissors and a grudge.

"Prove it," she said.

* * *

We stood in the kitchen like two animals meeting at a water hole, each deciding whether the other was predator or weather. She kept the flashlight on me. I kept my hands where she could see them, which felt theatrical until I realized it wasn't—she'd have seen hands before, plenty of them, doing things hands do.

"My mother's name was Odile Vasquez," I said. "There was a burn mark on the counter by the stove, from a pan, from the night she made flan for the first time and fell asleep waiting for the caramel. There was a pencil mark on the doorframe—" I pointed down the hall without looking away from her "—third doorway, measuring kids' heights. Mine stops at age eleven. After that we moved out here to the kitchen because the furnace broke and she couldn't afford the oil heat upstairs, so we slept on the couches and she called it camping."

The girl's light wavered. Not lowered—wavered, like a hand remembering it was tired.

"The doorframe's still there," she said finally. "I sleep in the back room. The one with the couch."

"I know. I slept there too."

She considered this. I watched her consider it, watched the arithmetic move behind her eyes—stranger, risk, alternative—and I recognized the calculation because I'd been running it my whole adult life. Every apartment I'd ever taken was month-to-month. Every friendship I'd ever kept had an exit built into it, unspoken but load-bearing. You learn to hold your life the way you hold a full cup crossing a crowded room.

"There's a blanket in the hall closet," she said at last. "It smells weird. Don't use it." A pause. "You can look around. Just—put things back."

"Put things back," I repeated.

"I know where everything is," she said, with a ferocity that surprised us both. "If you move something and I can't find it, that's—I need to know where everything is."

I understood. God help me, I understood completely. When your whole world fits in one room, order isn't a preference; it's a spine.

"I'm a librarian," I said. "Putting things back is most of the job."

Something flickered across her face—suspicion giving way, reluctantly, to a kind of professional respect. "Which library?"

"Milton Flats branch. Night desk."

"That's open all night?"

"Since the budget cuts made day staff impossible and nobody thought to close it. Insomniacs, cab drivers, students, one retired archivist who treats the card catalogue like a shrine." I hesitated. "Me."

"You live there?"

"No," I said, and heard how it sounded, and didn't correct it.

* * *

Her name was Junie. She offered it forty minutes later, the way you hand over a hostage—slowly, watching for tricks. We'd migrated by unspoken agreement to the back room, where a sleeping bag lay on the old couch and her possessions occupied a space roughly the size of a suitcase opened flat: three books, a phone with a cracked screen, a jar of pens, the folded wrappers, a photograph she turned face-down when I glanced at it.

She wouldn't tell me where she'd come from. She told me everything else instead, in the sideways manner of people who've learned that direct questions get direct lies.

"How long have you been here?" I asked.

"Define here."

"In this house."

"Six weeks." She picked at the flannel's fraying cuff. "Before that, a place on Corliss. Before that—" she waved a hand, a gesture that contained multitudes and dismissed them all. "The house on Corliss got sold. This one was empty. Empty houses don't check references."

"Does anyone know you're here?"

"The guy who owns it now, maybe. Some company bought the whole block. There was a letter taped to the door about an auction." She said *auction* carefully, like a word in a language she was still learning. "I took the letter down. It seemed rude, leaving it up."

I laughed before I could stop myself—a short, startled sound, the first laugh I'd produced in that house since I was eleven years old. She almost smiled. Almost. It crossed her face like a fish under ice.

"You're the one whose name is on the auction list," she said suddenly. "Vasquez. I saw it on the paperwork that got mailed here by mistake. Vasquez, B." Her eyes narrowed. "That's you."

"That's me."

"So it's your house."

"It was my mother's house. Now it's the bank's house, soon to be some developer's house." I looked around the back room—at the couch where I'd slept through the winter my mother called camping, at the water stain on the ceiling shaped like a country that doesn't exist. "Houses change hands. People just happen to be standing in them when it happens."

Junie stared at me with an expression I couldn't read, and then I could, and wished I couldn't. It was pity. A sixteen-year-old squatter was pitying me, and the worst part was that she was right to.

"That's the saddest thing anybody's ever said to me," she announced, "and a foster caseworker once told me my file was 'challenging.'"

* * *

We negotiated a truce the way nations do—all the real terms unspoken, the stated ones almost meaningless. I would come during evening hours, not overnight; she needed her nights undisturbed, she said, though she didn't say why. I would bring food, because the granola wrappers suggested her provisions ran toward cardboard. She would not report me to anyone, and I would not report her, and neither of us would ask the other the question hanging in the cold air between us: *where do you go when this ends?*

Because it would end. The yellow notice on the door said so. Three weeks, maybe four, until the auction, until the block changed owners, until whatever came next came for both of us.

I found myself at the doorframe on my way out. The pencil marks were still there, ghosted under years of dust: *Bram, 7. Bram, 9. Bram, 11.* My mother's handwriting, looping and sure. Above my last mark, nothing. She'd stopped measuring because I'd stopped growing, and then I'd stopped coming home at all, and the wall had kept my height like a receipt for a purchase never completed.

"She waited," Junie said from behind me. I hadn't heard her follow. "Whoever she was. There's mail on the front hall floor, piles of it, and some of it's addressed to a V. Vasquez, and none of it's been returned. She paid for a mail forward or something. Or meant to." A pause. "People who give up don't pay for mail forwarding."

I put my hand flat against the doorframe. The wood was cold and solid and indifferent, and I loved it so much my chest hurt.

"Goodnight, Junie."

"Night, librarian." And then, as I swung my leg back over the kitchen sink and the window frame: "Hey. Bring coffee next time. Real coffee. There's a percolator in the cabinet above the stove and I've been staring at it for six weeks like it's a museum piece."

* * *

I walked home through streets gone soft with 3 a.m., past the laundromat's blue glow, past the diner where the same two truckers ate the same two breakfasts at the same two hours, and I thought about what Junie had said, and what she hadn't.

A teenager was living in my mother's house. A teenager with a jar of pens and a face-down photograph and a system of folded wrappers so precise it amounted to a prayer. The city had lost her somewhere between placements, and she had found the one building on earth where I had any claim at all, and she was keeping it alive the only way she knew how: by needing it, fiercely, out loud, every single day.

I had spent fifteen years being invisible on purpose. She'd spent sixteen years being invisible by other people's choices. And tomorrow night I would climb back through that window with coffee and a flashlight and, if I was honest, a plan taking shape in me like a photograph developing—because a house full of objects is just a house, but a house with an inventory, a catalogue, a record—

Well. I was a librarian. I knew what happened to things that got catalogued.

They got found.

At the corner of Alder and Ninth I stopped and looked back. One window on the second floor glowed faintly—candlelight, or a phone screen, or a girl reading by whatever light she had, keeping watch over a house that belonged to a bank, a developer, a dead woman, and, briefly, improbably, to her.

I raised my hand. I don't know if she saw. But the light stayed on until I turned the corner, and I carried it with me the rest of the way home, wherever home was, and started making a list.
