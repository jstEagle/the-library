## 12. What the Father Kept

Theo Marsh woke at 3:14 a.m. for the fourth night running, and this time he didn't pretend it was the coffee.

He lay in the dark of the master bedroom on Kestrel Ridge—the house with the eleven-foot ceilings and the view of a city he owned pieces of—and listened to his own pulse. Beside him, Dana slept the sleep of a woman who had made her peace with what her husband did, or at least stopped asking about it. Down the hall, Caleb's door stood open to an empty room. That was new. That was three weeks new.

3:14. In his head, unbidden, the number arranged itself into something useful. Fourteen Alder Street. The auction file. The Hendersons' lot at the corner that would close in March if his attorney could keep the easement dispute crawling. The parcel map he kept meaning to stop looking at before bed and never did.

He got up. He didn't turn on lights; he knew the house by its silences. In the kitchen he poured two fingers of the good bourbon he told guests he kept for them, and he stood at the counter with the city glowing beyond the glass, and he opened the folder he'd started keeping in the drawer under the microwave like it was a pack of cigarettes he'd quit twice.

Alder Street. Six parcels. Five signed or nearly signed. One holdout problem shaped like a dead woman's house.

The numbers were fine. That was the thing nobody understood about Theo Marsh—not Dana, not his brother-in-law who called him a shark at Thanksgiving with affectionate contempt, not the investors whose quarterly calls had begun to acquire a certain temperature. The numbers were always fine. Marsh Development Group did not do fine; Marsh did excellent, Marsh did best-in-class returns, Marsh did the mixed-use infill project that the *Business Journal* had put on its "Ones to Watch" list with an artist's rendering of a building that did not exist yet rendered in a golden light that does not occur in nature.

The numbers were fine. It was everything else that had begun to leak.

* * *

The call came at 7:40 the next morning, while Theo was still in the kitchen and Dana was still upstairs, and he knew before he answered because Voss never called before eight unless something was burning.

"Two things," said Wendell Voss, who handled capital relationships the way other men handled snakes—gloved, respectful, from slightly above. "One, Corliss wants to move up the timeline."

"Corliss can want things."

"He can also pull things. You know how his money works—it's patient right up until it isn't. He's reading the same demographic reports we are. Every month this sits, somebody else buys a block somewhere else in this city and the comps move against us." A pause with a spreadsheet inside it. "He wants the auction inside thirty days. Twenty-eight, ideally. He used the word 'momentum.'"

Theo looked out at the ridge. Somewhere below, past the tree line, Milton Flats was waking up—doughnut shops, bus lines, the library where the lights burned all night like a pilot flame nobody tended.

"Thirty days is tight for title work on the Henderson easement."

"The Hendersons are done. I'm talking about the auction itself. Everything else can close behind it." Voss let the silence do its work. "Second thing. There are occupants in the Alder Street property."

Theo turned off the burner he hadn't realized he'd turned on. "Squatters?"

"Unknown. Neighbors report lights. Movement. Someone's been seen going in through the kitchen. Could be kids, could be worse. Could be your son's little archivist friend and her collection project—I don't know, I don't care. What I care about is liability. If some teenager breaks her ankle in a condemned structure on a property we're marketing, we're in discovery until 2031. And if there's an adverse possession angle—" Voss said the phrase the way you'd say *cancer cells* "—we need them out, documented, and gone before the sale. Clean title means clean everything."

"They're trespassers, Wendell. We post notice, we file, they're out."

"We file nothing yet. Filing creates a record, records create questions, questions create—" Voss exhaled. "Theo. Your father built strip malls. You're building a legacy. Don't let a girl with a clipboard and a squatter turn 'legacy' into a headline. Handle it quietly. Quietly first. Loudly only if quiet fails."

Theo hung up and stood holding his phone, and the word *quietly* sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

Because here was the thing about Theo Marsh that no one at the quarterly calls knew: he remembered every house he'd ever helped erase. Not the addresses—he had those in files—but the small details, the ones that slipped through the due-diligence photos. The crayon height-marks on a doorframe in the Delmore duplex, photographed because the appraiser thought it showed deferred maintenance. The wedding photo left behind in a closet on Fenwick, face down, which the crew chief had boxed as "misc. personal effects" and which Theo had personally driven to the county storage facility and then, sitting in his truck in the parking lot, been unable to make himself carry inside. He still had it in the truck's glovebox. Three years now. His daughter—if he'd had one, if things had gone differently—would have been about the age of the bride in the picture.

He did not think of himself as a man who kept trophies. But he kept that.

* * *

At the office, the acceleration memo went out by ten.

His project manager, Renata, read it twice and looked up over her glasses. "Twenty-eight days. For the whole block?"

"For the auction. The rest closes behind it, like Voss said."

"You'll lose the preservation angle. If there's any chance someone files for historic review—"

"There's no chance. It's a boarded-up house on a street of boarded-up houses. Nobody's filing anything."

Renata held his gaze one second longer than the org chart strictly permitted. "And the occupants?"

"Notice posted today. Then a formal trespass letter, certified mail, if they don't clear out within the week." He heard his own voice doing the thing it did in investor meetings—level, reasonable, the voice of a man merely describing weather. "I want it documented that we gave them every courtesy."

"Every courtesy," Renata repeated, without inflection, and went back to her desk, and Theo understood that she would do exactly what he asked and that she had added him, silently, to some internal ledger of hers that he would never get to read.

By noon the notice was taped to the door of 14 Alder Street. Yellow paper. He'd approved the color himself years ago—high-visibility, municipal, official. He had driven past the street at lunch to see it go up, telling himself it was oversight, and had watched from the car as a tall old man in a cardigan stood on the porch reading it, taking his time, the way you'd read a letter from someone who died. The old man peeled it off carefully, folded it, and put it in his pocket like evidence.

Theo drove back to the office with the windows up.

* * *

That evening he tried Caleb's door again, out of habit more than hope.

The room had gone museum. Bed made, desk cleared, the guitar Caleb had begged for at fifteen standing in its stand like furniture in a model home. On the desk, squared to the edge, a stack of books—city history, mostly, checked out of the Milton Flats branch, the checkout slips still in them. Theo picked up the top one. *A Walking Guide to the Old Fourth Ward.* The slip showed a due date three weeks out and a borrower name that wasn't Caleb's.

*C. Marsh,* though. His son had used his own name. At the public library. Where anyone could see it.

Theo put the book down exactly where it had been, aligned the way he'd found it, and stood in his son's clean room feeling the specific vertigo of a man who realizes the surveillance has been running the other direction for some time.

Dana found him there. She leaned on the doorframe in her robe, arms crossed, watching him watch the room.

"He's staying with friends," she said. "Or a friend. He won't say which."

"I know."

"Do you?" She came in, picked up the walking guide, turned it over in her hands. "Because I talked to his mother—that's me, Theo, I'm still allowed to be his mother even when he's decided we're the enemy—and he asked me one question. He asked if you ever read the letters Grandpa kept. Your father's letters. From the houses he tore down."

Theo said nothing.

"He said, 'Does Dad know what Grandpa kept?' Like it was a riddle." Dana set the book back down. "What did your father keep, Theo?"

"My father kept receipts," Theo said. "In shoeboxes. Tax records going back to 1978. He was not a sentimental man."

"Mm," said Dana, and left the door open behind her, which was its own kind of comment.

But after she'd gone upstairs, Theo stood a while longer in the empty room, and the lie he'd told sat in him wrong, the way a misfiled document sits wrong in a drawer—you can close it, but you know.

Because his father had kept more than receipts. Theo had found the boxes himself, two years ago, clearing the house after the funeral: shoeboxes, yes, dozens, but not all tax records. In the bottom of the last box, wrapped in a 1987 grocery circular, a photograph of a woman standing on the porch of a house on Delmore Street, the day before the bulldozers. His father, twenty-six years old, hard hat under his arm, standing next to her. Both of them smiling. On the back, in his father's blunt carpenter pencil: *Ruthie D., wouldn't take the check. Stayed till the end. Best man I ever met.*

Best man. About a woman his father had paid to leave and who hadn't gone.

Theo had put the photograph back in the circular, put the circular back in the box, put the box in his own garage, and told himself that grief makes archivists of everyone and it meant nothing. He had told himself that for two years, at roughly the same frequency as he told himself the numbers were fine.

* * *

Friday, Voss called again. Corliss wanted twenty-one days now, not twenty-eight. Momentum.

"Also," Voss said, "the trespass letter bounced. No one's accepting service at the property, and the neighbors—" a thin sound that might have been a laugh "—the neighbors have apparently organized some kind of signature campaign. The clerk says forty people came in Tuesday. Forty. For one house."

"It's not about the house," Theo said. "It's about precedent. They know that."

"Then you understand better than most why Corliss doesn't want to find out what happens if they win one." Voss paused. "There's one more thing, and I'm telling you as a friend, which should tell you how bad it is. My guy at the county says someone pulled the historic registry index for the Fourth Ward. Full run, 1902 to present. Signed out under a retired city archivist named Okonkwo."

The name landed in Theo's chest like a coin dropped down a well. He waited for the splash. It took a long time coming.

"An old man with a library card," Theo said finally. "Wendell. I have attorneys."

"You have a clock. Twenty-one days, and if a review petition lands before the auction, the sale voids and Corliss pulls the capital and we spend eighteen months in administrative purgatory while this city decides whether a dead woman's kitchen is a landmark." Voss's voice dropped into the register he saved for boardrooms. "So here's what happens Monday. Legal serves the occupants directly—names, if we can get them. Injunction papers drafted and ready. And you, Theo—you get in front of this. Publicly. A statement about renewal, about housing, about what the block becomes. Get ahead of the story before the story gets ahead of you. Make it boring. Boring is safe."

Boring is safe. Theo wrote it down, actually wrote it down on the legal pad beside the phone, because that was what he did instead of praying.

After he hung up he sat in his office a long time, the city laid out below him in its evening grid, thousands of lit windows, each one a due-diligence photo waiting to happen. Somewhere down there a boy was sleeping in a house Theo owned on paper, patching windows Theo had scheduled for demolition, checking out books under his own name like a declaration of war conducted through the polite medium of the public library.

Somewhere down there an old man was reading a registry index line by line, looking, Theo understood with terrible clarity, for the one thing that would make 14 Alder Street too heavy to lift.

And somewhere in Theo's garage, under a tarp, in a box marked *T.M. personal*, a photograph of Ruthie D. waited in its grocery-circular shroud for him to look at it again.

He opened the top drawer of his desk instead. Inside: the parcel map, the yellow notice proof, the certified-mail receipt that had come back undeliverable, and a fresh copy of the injunction draft his attorney had couriered over that afternoon. *In the matter of Marsh Development Group v. Occupants of 14 Alder Street.* Occupants. As if they were weather. As if they were mold.

He read the names his investigator had finally assembled. Vasquez, B.—adult, unknown occupation. An unidentified minor. And a third name, typed with a question mark beside it, that made him sit very still:

*Marsh, C.*

His own name. His son's name. On the wrong side of the caption.

Theo closed the folder. Outside, the city hummed on, indifferent, buyable. Monday the injunction would file, the clock would hit nineteen days, and whatever his son thought he was doing in that house, Theo would end it with paperwork—cleanly, quietly, the way his father would have, the way his father hadn't, the way he still couldn't decide which one he was.

He reached for the bourbon and found the bottle already empty, and could not remember drinking it.
