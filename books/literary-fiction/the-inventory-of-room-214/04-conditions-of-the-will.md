## 4. Conditions of the Will

Prue Okafor's office smelled of toner and old carpet, the way all lawyers' offices smell, as though truth itself had been run through a copying machine one too many times and come out slightly warm and faintly singed.

I sat in a chair that was trying very hard to be leather and losing, across a desk from a woman in her sixties with reading glasses on a chain and the particular stillness of someone who has watched families detonate over real estate for forty years and has learned to enjoy it in a clinical sort of way. Behind her, framed certificates. Beside her, a box of tissues placed with the strategic precision of artillery.

My mother's lawyer. Which meant, I supposed, that she had known my mother better than I had, which was not a high bar to clear but was somehow still humiliating.

"You came," Prue said, looking at me over her glasses.

"You sound surprised."

"I've been surprised before. It's how I know what it feels like." She opened a folder. "Your mother died on a Tuesday. She planned her funeral on a Wednesday, eleven years ago. She revised this document four times since then. The last revision was nine days before she died." She let that sit there between us like an unpaid bill. "She knew exactly what she was doing, Ms. Yarrow. I want you to understand that before we begin, because afterward you may be tempted to believe otherwise."

"I don't really have expectations," I said. "That's kind of my whole thing."

"Mm." Prue looked at me for a long moment, the way librarians look at patrons who insist the book they want doesn't exist when it's sitting right behind them. Then she put on her glasses and began to read.

I will spare you the boilerplate, or rather, I will compress it, because grief is already tedious enough without legal prose slowing it down further. There were bequests: a modest sum to a hospice that had cared for her at the end; her jewelry—such as it was—to me; instructions about cremation that she had apparently specified down to the urn ("inexpensive," Prue read, with the ghost of a wince, "she wrote 'inexpensive' twice, underlined"). My mother had never been a woman who trusted sentiment to survive translation. Everything had to be written down, doubled, underlined, witnessed.

And then there was the hotel.

"I devise the building known as the Vance Hotel, together with its contents, fixtures, and appurtenances—" Prue paused, and something flickered across her face, some professional reflex being suppressed, "—to my daughter, Marguerite Yarrow, absolutely and free of encumbrance, *provided*—"

There it is, I thought. There's always a provided. Inheritance is just a trap with your name engraved on the lid.

"—*provided* that within thirty days of the date of probate, said Marguerite Yarrow shall personally conduct a complete inventory of every room in the Vance Hotel, numbering one hundred and twelve guest rooms, plus common areas, service areas, and basement, cataloguing all items of personal property found therein. Said inventory to be conducted by the beneficiary herself, alone, without delegation. Upon completion and delivery of said inventory to this office, title shall vest absolutely. Should the condition fail—for any reason—the property shall pass instead to Graywater Demolition Partners as consideration for contract execution, and the estate shall be considered settled."

The office went quiet. Somewhere a clock ticked with the smugness of clocks everywhere.

"Alone," I repeated.

"Alone," Prue confirmed.

"One hundred and twelve rooms. Plus common areas. Plus basement."

"Plus basement."

"In thirty days."

"Twenty-nine now. Probate was filed yesterday."

"And if I don't—if I just walk away—the hotel goes to the demolition company. As payment. For knocking it down."

"That is a fair summary, yes."

I laughed. I couldn't help it; it came out of me like a hiccup, sharp and inappropriate. "That's not a will. That's a hostage situation."

Prue removed her glasses and polished them on her sleeve, which gave her time to arrange her face into something neutral. "I've drafted a great many wills," she said. "Most people use them to give things away. Your mother used hers to make sure you couldn't leave without going through every single room of the place she spent her whole life keeping you out of." She set the glasses down. "I'm not permitted to interpret. But I am permitted to observe, and I observed a dying woman who was very frightened that you would arrive after her death, sign nothing, feel nothing, and be gone by morning."

The words landed with uncomfortable accuracy, mostly because I had rehearsed that exact itinerary on the train. Sign nothing. Feel nothing. Leave by morning. Apparently my mother had known me well enough to draft around me, the way you draft around a structural flaw in a building. Load-bearing wall, my avoidance. She'd built her will straight through it.

"She could have just asked me," I said.

"No," Prue said. "She couldn't. You'd have said no."

I wanted to argue. I opened my mouth and found the argument missing, misplaced, overdue by fifteen years. The library of my objections had been catalogued once, long ago, and then closed.

"There's more," Prue said. "Page nine."

Of course there was page nine. Page nine is where wills keep their teeth.

"The demolition contracts," she read, "are scheduled to execute thirty-one days from today. The city has approved razing. The developer has financing. Your mother was aware of all of it. Her condition is calibrated precisely: complete the inventory inside the window, or the building—and everything in it—goes to the machines." She looked up. "Everything in it, Ms. Yarrow. Whatever 'everything' turns out to include."

"What does everything include?"

"I genuinely don't know," Prue said, and for the first time she sounded less like a lawyer and more like a tired woman who had liked her client and been kept at arm's length for decades. "Edith Yarrow told me many things over the years. What to file, what to shred, what to say if certain people called. She did not tell me why. I stopped asking around 1998."

"Certain people," I repeated. "Which certain people?"

"Ms. Yarrow." She folded her hands. "You are a librarian. You of all people should appreciate that some collections are closed until the proper credentials are presented. Go count your rooms. Come back when you find whatever it is she was so afraid of—or so hopeful of. I honestly never determined which."

* * *

Here is the thing nobody tells you about other people's points of view: you spend your whole life assuming you're the only one narrating, and then occasionally you get evidence otherwise.

What follows I learned much later, from Prue herself, over bad coffee, in a confession I'll get to eventually. But because I have decided to tell this story properly—which is to say, dishonestly, in the order that hurts most—I'll give you her side of that morning now, while I was still sitting in the chair that wasn't leather, saying nothing.

Prue Okafor had met Edith Yarrow thirty-two years ago, when Edith had walked into the office carrying a shoebox of documents and a face like a locked door, and asked, in a voice that allowed no follow-up questions, to change her name and her daughter's name and to make both changes permanent. No explanation. Prue had asked anyway—one question, just one—and Edith had looked at her with such flat, exhausted finality that Prue had never asked again. That was the arrangement: Prue executed, Edith concealed, and between them a friendship grew anyway, the way moss grows on the north side of a wall, in the dark, without anyone deciding it should.

Over three decades Prue had watched the woman pay cash for everything, flinch at the telephone, subscribe to the local paper only through the mail, and take a different route home each week as though followed. She had watched her raise a daughter who was polite and watchful and gone by nineteen. And she had sat across from her, nine days before the end, while Edith—oxygen tube, hands like paper—had made her read the inventory clause aloud twice and then said:

"She'll try to do it fast. She'll try to do it like a chore, like shelving books, like anything she can do without feeling it. Make her slow down, Prue."

"How?"

"You can't. That's the point. She has to find the thing that makes her slow down on her own. Just—don't help her hurry."

And then Edith had looked toward the window, toward the part of the skyline where a certain derelict hotel stood with its second floor dark for fifteen years, and she had said, so quietly Prue almost missed it: "Room 214 stays locked until Marguerite opens it. Not before. Not ever before."

Prue had thought, as she had thought a hundred times over the years: *this woman is not hiding from something. She is guarding something.* Hiding and guarding look identical from the outside. They are opposites from the inside.

So that morning, watching the daughter sit in the not-leather chair with her spine straight and her face arranged into competent blankness—a face that was Edith's face, younger, relocked—Prue Okafor suspected what she could not prove: that the old woman had reasons. Reasons stacked like sandbags, reasons paid for in decades, reasons that would explain the name change and the fear and the padlock and even, possibly, the stranger in the ill-fitting coat who had appeared at the graveside two days earlier saying *Yarrow-Vance* like a man testing whether a floor would hold his weight.

Yes, Prue had heard about that. Graywater was a small city; it heard everything within seventy-two hours or it wasn't trying.

"He claims he was invited," the daughter had said at the funeral, cornering Prue by the wreaths. "Did you invite him?"

"No," Prue had said, and it was true, and it was also incomplete, because Edith had left an envelope in the safe marked *For the young man, if he comes,* and Prue had not yet decided whether delivering it counted as helping Edith or betraying her, and she was a lawyer, she was very good at not deciding things.

All of this was happening behind her professional face while I sat across the desk thinking I was alone in the room with my inheritance-shaped trap. This is what I mean about narration. Everyone's writing their own book. Some of them are just better at hiding the manuscript.

* * *

"So," I said aloud, back in my own head, my own voice, my own stubborn first person. "Let me summarize. I inherit a building I hate, on the condition that I spend thirty days touching every object in it, alone, or else it gets bulldozed along with whatever she didn't want me to see, and the whole thing expires in twenty-nine days."

"That's accurate."

"It's cruel."

"It's a mother," Prue said simply. "In my experience the two categories overlap more than greeting cards suggest."

I stood. My legs felt strange, like furniture rearranged in the night. Outside the window, Graywater lay gray and damp under a sky the color of wet newspaper, and somewhere in it, visible from certain angles, the Vance Hotel stood with its dead windows and its hundred and twelve rooms full of other people's abandoned lives, waiting for me to come catalogue them like the world's saddest night shift.

"I'll need keys," I said. "A flashlight. Probably a tetanus shot."

Prue opened a drawer and produced a ring of keys so heavy it clanked against the desk like a bell. "Top one is the front door. Second is the service entrance. Third through ninth are various floors." She hesitated, thumb resting on the last key, small and brass and unremarkable. "This one doesn't open anything I know of. Your mother kept it separate. She said—" Prue's mouth tightened. "She said, 'When she asks for 214, give her this.' Not before."

"I'm not asking for 214."

"No," Prue agreed, studying me. "Not yet."

She slid the keyring across the desk, and I took it, and its weight ran up my arm like a current, like a sentence beginning that I already dreaded finishing.

"One more thing," Prue said as I reached the door. "The demolition company sent a site assessor yesterday. He was seen photographing the ballroom chandelier." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "Tall fellow. Ill-fitting coat."

I stood in the doorway with thirty days and a hundred and twelve rooms and a key I hadn't asked for, and understood that whatever my mother had locked in that hotel, I would not be counting it alone.
