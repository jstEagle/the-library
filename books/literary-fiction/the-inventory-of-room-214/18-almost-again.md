## 18. Almost, Again

The cousin's car had barely cleared the parking lot before I understood that my hands were shaking.

Not the dramatic shaking of heroines in novels—no trembling teacup, no dropped letter fluttering to the floorboards like a wounded bird. This was the small, industrial tremor of a body that has been holding itself together on willpower and vending machine coffee for nineteen days and has just been informed by a man with a gold pen that it can be made to disappear. My fingers buzzed against the front desk of the Vance Hotel like they were picking up a radio station from another decade.

Gerald Vance-Ashcombe. That was his name. He'd said it twice, the way men say their names when they believe the name should do work for them. He'd sat in the lobby's one surviving armchair with his ankles crossed and offered me a buyout figure that was, I'll grant him this, generous enough to be insulting. And then he'd smiled with all the warmth of a fire exit and said something about how sad it would be if the demolition schedule were moved up, and how these things were so often out of anyone's hands, and how my mother had been a difficult woman, hadn't she, and difficult women left difficult paper trails.

*Paper trails.* He'd actually used the phrase. As though the letters behind the front desk—the forty years of unsent letters addressed to V, tender at the start and furious at the end—were litter. As though my mother's whole sealed heart was a compliance problem.

I watched his taillights go and stood in the dark lobby listening to the building settle around me. Old hotels don't creak the way houses do. They *shift*, the way large animals shift in sleep, and when you've grown up inside one you learn its vocabulary without knowing you're learning it. The Vance was saying something. It had been saying something for nineteen days, and I had been cataloguing its sentences room by room, and now a man wanted to bulldoze the conversation mid-word.

I checked my phone. 11:47 p.m. The screen's blue light made the lobby look underwater.

There are exactly three people I could have called at that hour. One was dead. One was Elias, who was asleep in Room 306 with his father's deathbed letter under his pillow like a talisman, and who I had accused of fraud twelve hours earlier and then apologized to so badly that the apology had basically been a second accusation. And one was Theo.

Theo, who was four hundred miles away at the Halloway Branch, probably shelving returns or eating cold noodles over the circulation desk, because Theo worked nights the way other people breathe—without thinking about it and without making a performance of it. Theo, to whom I had been almost-saying a sentence for fifteen years. Not the same sentence I'd practiced for my mother. A different one. Or maybe—I was beginning to suspect—not different at all. Maybe there was only ever one sentence, and we spend our lives choosing which person gets to hear it first.

I dialed before I could talk myself out of it. This is an important detail. Every significant thing I have ever done, I have done in the window between deciding and deliberating, and the deliberation always arrives afterward, punctual as a bill, to tell me what a mistake it was.

He picked up on the second ring. "Halloway Branch, land of the living dead."

"It's midnight."

"I'm aware. There's a patron asleep in biography. He came in at nine, asked where we kept the books about shipwrecks, and then chose Napoleon." A pause, the sound of him shifting, the particular quiet of a big room at night coming through the phone like weather. "You sound wrong."

"I sound fine."

"You sound like the time you told me your mother died and then immediately reorganized the returns cart."

"That was grief."

"That was alphabetization as a coping mechanism. Which, for the record, is very effective. I've adopted it myself." He let the silence sit for a moment, which is Theo's superpower—he doesn't fill silences, he *furnishes* them, so you can sit down in them. "What happened tonight?"

And here is where I should tell you what I did instead of answering, because it was magnificent. I gave him a status report. I told him about the inventory being nearly complete—floors one through five done, sixth floor tomorrow, basement finished. I told him about the reading room idea, the deed, Prue Okafor's bad coffee and worse confessions. I narrated my own life in the tone of a quarterly earnings call, and I heard myself doing it, and some tired, furious librarian deep in my chest looked up from her desk and said: *you know he can hear you doing this, right?*

"Maggie," Theo said.

My whole body stopped. Not metaphorically. I mean my lungs filed for a brief recess.

He'd never called me Maggie. Nobody had called me Maggie since I was nine years old, since before the name went into the ground with the scandal and came back up as Marguerite Yarrow, night librarian, woman of no fixed alarm. I'd introduced myself to him six years ago as Marguerite. He'd never once shortened it, never once asked. And now he said *Maggie* into the dark of the Halloway Branch like he'd known it was there the whole time, waiting, like a book someone had reshelved in the wrong section and he'd finally found it.

"Where did you get that name?" My voice came out strange. Small. Nine years old.

"You talk in your sleep."

"I don't sleep at work."

"You fell asleep in the break room in March. During inventory week. You said—" and here he hesitated, and I realized Theo was nervous, actual nervous, the man who reads aloud to insomniac teenagers without blinking, "—you said, 'Don't tell Daddy about the chandelier.' And then you said 'Maggie Vance' twice. And I looked it up later. Library habit. There wasn't much. There was a newspaper archive thing about the Vance Hotel, and a scandal, and a family that changed its name, and a little girl mentioned once, in passing, unnamed. And I decided it wasn't mine to know until you gave it to me." A breath. "It's been six years, Marguerite. I figured either you'd give it to me or you wouldn't, and both were allowed."

The lobby was very quiet. Somewhere upstairs a pipe knocked twice, the old building clearing its throat.

"You knew," I said. "For months. Years. And you just—waited?"

"What was I going to do? Interrogate you? Demand your secrets like they were overdue?" I could hear him smiling, but it was a careful smile, the kind you can hear someone wearing. "Marguerite, I've watched you practice saying things for six years. You do it when you think no one's listening. Little sentences. Half sentences. You get to the hard part and you shelve it. I used to wonder what the hard part was. Then I found out your mother died and you flew home to a hotel with your name on it, and I thought—oh. It's not a confession. It's an address."

I sat down on the floor. Not gracefully. I sat down the way a marionette sits down when someone cuts one string too many, right there in the dust behind the front desk, next to the hollow where forty years of unsent letters had lived. Above me hung the chandelier my nine-year-old self had sworn not to tell Daddy about. Somewhere on the sixth floor was a sealed door with my initials on a brass plate. And four hundred miles away, a man who shelved books beside me every night was telling me he'd known my name the whole time and had simply held it, gently, like a coat check ticket, waiting for me to claim it.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about the sentence you practice for fifteen years. You rehearse it so many times that it wears smooth, like a stone in a pocket, and eventually you forget it was ever sharp. You forget it was ever *dangerous*. You carry it around as a comfort object, an almost, a thing you reach for and put back. And then someone says your real name at midnight, kindly, without demand, and the stone turns over in your hand and shows you the edge.

"Theo," I said.

"Yeah."

"I need to say something and I need you to not help me."

"Noted."

"No—seriously. Don't be kind. Don't be patient. If you're patient I'll stop, because patience gives me an exit, and I am a world-class expert at exits. Just—be a wall. Let me hit it."

Silence. Then, evenly: "I'm a wall."

I closed my eyes. The dark behind my eyes was full of the hotel: the lily-and-dust smell of the lobby, the abandoned objects of floors one through five, the photograph in Marta's box with the face torn out, my mother's handwriting going from honey to acid across four decades, the crib behind the padlocked door, the wall of photographs of a life my mother had watched from a distance like a woman looking through glass at everything she'd paid to lose. Gerald's gold pen. The red-stamped notice. Thirty days, twenty-two spent. The wrecking schedule that could be quietly moved up by a man whose father had forged the scandal in the first place.

I opened my mouth to give Theo the status report version. The earnings-call version. *Things are complicated here, there's a legal situation, I may need to stay longer.* I felt the smooth stone turn in my hand, familiar as a key.

And I said:

"I'm Maggie Vance, and I've been hiding my whole life."

It came out louder than I intended. It bounced off the marble. The hotel took it and shifted, that great sleeping animal, and I swear on the Halloway Branch's complete works of Willa Cather that the building held its breath.

Theo didn't say anything. Good wall.

"My name isn't Yarrow. Well—it is, legally, it's the name my mother bought us when she bought our silence, but it's a coat, it's a—" I was talking fast now, the words coming up like water finding a crack. "My father didn't leave us. I've told everyone he left us, I've told *myself* he left us, for thirty years, because that's the story my mother gave me and a story you get at nine becomes your skeleton. He was paid to leave. Paid by my mother's family, paid over a scandal that wasn't even his fault—that was *forged*, Theo, I have a ledger, I have proof—and he took our name with him when he went because they made the name part of the price. Yarrow-Vance. We were the Yarrow-Vances. And a stranger said it at my mother's funeral and I wanted to strangle him and it turned out he's my half-brother and my father is dead and I will never—" My voice cracked and I let it, I let it crack right down the middle in an empty hotel lobby at midnight, which is possibly the most honest acoustics available in the modern world. "I will never get to ask him why he stayed away. Why he took the money and stayed gone for thirty years and only wrote a letter on his deathbed. And I keep almost saying all of this, I've been almost saying it since I was nine, I almost say it to you every single night at the desk, and I know you know, you just told me you know, and I still couldn't—"

"Maggie."

"You're supposed to be a wall."

"I am a wall. Walls can say your name." His voice had gone rough at the edges. "Keep going."

"There's a man trying to demolish the hotel before anyone reads what's inside it. There's a room up there with my whole childhood in it that I unlocked two days ago and I still haven't been able to go back in. There's a reading room I want to build and a name I signed on a deed today for the first time since I was a child—Marguerite Yarrow-Vance, all of it, the whole ridiculous hyphenated truth—and my hand shook so badly the signature looks like a seismograph. And I have spent fifteen years perfecting invisibility. Fifteen years. I got *good* at it. I made a career of it. Night shifts, back offices, a name like a gray sweater. And it turns out invisible was just another word for buried, and I am so tired, Theo. I am so tired of being almost."

The pipe knocked again. Or maybe it was the building applauding. I was past caring which.

"So that's it," I said. "That's the sentence. I'm Maggie Vance. Vance, like the hotel. Vance, like the scandal. Vance, like the girl in the newspaper article who got one unnamed mention and then vanished into a pseudonym and a night shift. I've been hiding my whole life, and I'm done, and I have eight days to prove a forgery and save a building and finish saying everything I never said to my mother, and I called you at midnight because you're the first person I ever wanted to say it to."

Silence. Long enough that I checked whether the call had dropped.

Then Theo said, quietly, "For the record, I would have waited fifteen more years."

"That's terrible."

"It's true. I'm a very good waiter. It's the job description—we wait for people to return things." A breath, and I heard him decide to stop being careful. "But I'm glad you didn't make me. And Marguerite—Maggie—you should know that saying it sounds different on you. You sound bigger. Like a room with the furniture uncovered."

I laughed. It came out wet and cracked and completely undignified, and I laughed again anyway, sitting on the floor of a condemned hotel in the dark, and somewhere above me the chandelier caught a sliver of streetlight through the boarded windows and threw it across the marble like the building was showing off.

"Eight days," I said.

"Eight days. What do you need?"

"A wall. Possibly several. And—" I looked up at the dark staircase, at the sixth floor, at the door with my initials on it that I had locked again after unlocking it, because some doors you have to open more than once. "And I think I need witnesses. When I finish this. Whatever finishing looks like. I think the whole point of a secret is that somebody finally hears it."

"Then hear this," Theo said. "I'm requesting vacation days in the morning. I'll be there Thursday. And Maggie—when I get there, you're going to show me the chandelier."

"Don't tell Daddy about the chandelier," I said automatically, and then we both laughed, and it was the strangest, lightest sound the Vance Hotel had made in thirty years.

We hung up just after one. I sat on the floor a while longer, in the dust behind the front desk, next to the hollow where the letters had been. My hands had stopped shaking. In their place was something steadier and far more alarming: momentum.

Because here's what Gerald Vance-Ashcombe didn't understand, sitting in my lobby with his gold pen and his threats. He thought the danger was the paper. The ledger, the letters, the proof—documents, which can be disputed, dismissed, shredded by better lawyers. But documents weren't the danger. Documents were just the *inventory*.

The danger was what I'd just done on the telephone. The danger was a story that stops being private. You can burn a letter. You cannot un-hear a voice.

I got up, found the fuse box, and turned on the lobby chandelier—all of it, every bulb, blazing at one in the morning like a signal fire. Then I climbed the stairs to the sixth floor, stood in front of the brass plate with my own initials, and took out the locket.

Eight days. Time to stop practicing.

Tomorrow, I was going to need everyone in Graywater City in that lobby—and I knew exactly how a librarian fills a room.
