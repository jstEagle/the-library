## 1. Postcards to Ash

The postcards went out every Thursday, which was the day the shop received its deliveries and the day Yara had decided, ten years ago, that she could bear it. Not Sunday, when grief wore its good coat and everyone expected her to be sad. Not the anniversary, which was a trap with a date on it. Thursday. Ordinary. A day for boxes of fountain pens and reams of cream paper and the small administrative machinery of other people's correspondence.

She wrote them at the counter after close, under the lamp Mr. Fenwick left burning for the moths, and she never sent them.

That was the part no one knew — not that she wrote them, but that they accumulated in the bottom drawer of her desk at home, four hundred and some postcards in bundles tied with butcher's twine, each one addressed in her careful hand to *The Amberdale House, Coast Road, Pencarrow Cove*, an address that had not existed since the fire made it a matter for insurance assessors instead of postmen. The Royal Mail would have returned them, *addressee unknown*, *no such premises*, if she'd ever paid for stamps. She bought the stamps anyway. She kept them in a tin. It seemed important that the intention be complete even if the act never was.

Tonight's card was a view of a lighthouse somewhere in Cornwall that wasn't hers. She'd chosen it from the rack because the sea in the photograph was the wrong color — a hard tourist blue instead of the grey-green slate of the cove — and this felt honest. She did not want to pretend she was writing from anywhere near the truth.

*Dear M,* she wrote. Then she stopped, as she stopped every week, at the place where the sentence forked.

There were so many ways to go from *Dear M*. Dear Meriel, who had been Merry before the fire, before the nickname became unbearable, before it became the only thing anyone could call her without someone at the table leaving. Dear Merry, who was eleven when the house burned and who had been, according to every official document Yara had ever read or refused to read, asleep in the back room where the stairs came up.

*The shop got a delivery of Japanese notebooks today,* she wrote instead. *The paper is so thin you can see your hand through it. I thought of you because you used to say you wanted to write something worth hiding.*

Yara looked at that last line for a long time. It was almost something. Almost was the whole craft of it, ten years of almost, sentences built like the house had been built — sound from the road, hollow toward the cliff. She crossed nothing out. Crossing out was a form of saying.

Around her the shop settled into its evening noises: the radiator ticking, the till humming its standby note, the door of the flat upstairs through which Mr. Fenwick's television murmured like weather. Yara had worked at Penhale & Sons Stationers for six years now, long enough that tourists asked her for directions as though she were civic furniture. *Where's the coastal path? Is there a café open? How do we get to Pencarrow Cove?*

Never once had she said: you don't want to go there. Never once had she said: I grew up at the end of that road, in the house that isn't there anymore, and I have not driven past it since the funeral, and I take the inland route to Truro like a woman detouring around a country she's been exiled from.

"There's a bus," she told them. "Or it's about forty minutes on foot."

Forty minutes along the coast road, past the turning where the hedge opened and you could see the headland, and then the cove itself, and then — she didn't finish the inventory even in her own head. That was the trick of living small. You kept the map folded.

* * *

Her flat was above the bakery on Fore Street, two rooms and a bathroom, and she had lived there long enough that the bread smell had become invisible to her, though visitors always remarked on it, hungrily, as if she were lucky. She supposed she was lucky. This was what she had assembled from the wreckage, and it was a life: a job she was good at, a landlady who left her alone, a drawer full of unsent mail, a sister-shaped silence she carried the way you carry a bag you've stopped noticing until you set it down and your arm floats up, weightless and wrong.

On Thursdays she came home, ate standing at the counter, and read the new card aloud once, quietly, to hear how nearly it approached the truth. Then it went into the drawer with the others.

Tonight she didn't read it aloud. Tonight she sat on the bed with the card face-down on her knee and looked at the tin of stamps on the windowsill, and something in her chest did the thing it had been doing more often lately, a slow structural settling, like a house deciding which way it was going to lean.

Ten years. Ten years of cards that said *the shop got a delivery* and *it rained all week* and *I saw a dog on the beach that looked like yours, the one you called Captain, do you remember, of course you don't, you never met him, I invented him after, I invent things after.* Ten years of writing to a dead girl as if she were simply away, at school, grown and estranged, anything but ash. Because ash was a fact and facts were the one currency the family had agreed to stop trading in.

Her mother had said it at the funeral, gripping Yara's wrist hard enough to bruise: *We don't look back. We don't ask. We go forward, all of us, together.* And Teo, fifteen, standing beside her with his collar crooked and his eyes fixed on the middle distance, had nodded like a soldier accepting orders. Forward. Together. Which had lasted eleven months, until Teo left for university and then for London and then for a life so far forward it had no return address either, and her mother moved inland to be near her sister, and her father began drinking in the manner of men who are not drinking but dissolving, and *together* revealed itself to have been a word meaning *apart, simultaneously.*

Only Yara had stayed within reach of the coast, close enough to smell salt on the wind some days, far enough that she could pretend the ache in her chest was just the weather coming in.

She turned the card over. *You used to say you wanted to write something worth hiding.*

Merry had said that at nine, sitting on the sea wall with her knees drawn up, watching the fishing boats. Yara remembered it with terrible clarity — one of the memories she'd have traded for any amount of blankness — because Merry had said it in Kerowen first, that old cove language their grandmother spoke and their parents pretended not to understand, and then translated it herself, proudly, the way children show off a secret. *Worth hiding,* she'd said. *Not worth showing. Worth hiding. There's a difference.*

Yara hadn't understood the difference at nine. At thirty-one she understood it completely. Her whole life was a letter worth hiding.

She picked up the pen. Below the line about the notebooks, she wrote:

*I need to tell you what happened that night.*

And her hand stopped. Actually stopped, mid-word, the pen pressing a small dark crater into the card, and Yara sat very still in her narrow room above the bakery and listened to her own breathing, which had gone shallow and quick, and understood that she had arrived at the edge of something she could not write across. Not tonight. Perhaps not ever. The sentence sat there like a door she'd found in a wall she'd lived beside for years without knowing it was there.

She could not write *what happened*, because she did not know what happened. That was the arrangement. That was the family architecture: a fire with no cause, a night with no witnesses willing to speak, a girl asleep in the back room, a brother who never talked about the door he couldn't open, a mother who said *forward, together* with bruises forming on her daughter's wrist. Everyone had carried their piece of the night into separate rooms and shut the doors, and the house of their family stood on the silence the way the old house had stood on its foundations, and everyone knew, the way you know a floor is rotten, that it could not hold much longer.

Yara looked at the unfinished card for a long time. Then she did something she had never done in ten years of Thursdays.

She put it in the tin with the stamps, unaddressed, unsigned, unfinished. Not in the drawer with the completed almosts. In the tin, with the intentions. As if it were waiting for something — a stamp, an address, a word — that had not yet come into the world.

* * *

The bell over the shop door rang on Saturday morning while Yara was restocking the ink, and she said "Welcome to Penhale's" without looking up, and the woman who answered said, in a voice like gravel rolled in honey, "They said in the newspaper you were the one who writes letters."

Yara looked up. The woman was old, straight-backed, dressed in a man's oilskin jacket gone soft with decades, and her eyes were the exact grey-green of water over slate, of the cove, of everything Yara had spent ten years taking the inland route to avoid.

"I don't send them," Yara heard herself say. "I just write them."

"I know," the woman said. "I'm Meriel Kestrel. My grandmother taught your grandmother Kerowen, and my grandmother is dead, and I am dying too, and I have been reading about you in the paper." She set a folded newspaper clipping on the counter, smoothed it flat with a hand brown and knotted as driftwood. "A feature on letter-writers. They photographed you at this counter. You looked," she said, considering, "like a woman posting letters into the sea."

"They misspelled my name," Yara said, because it was the only true thing she could find.

"Yara Amberdale." The old woman said it slowly, tasting it, and then said something else — three short words in a language Yara hadn't heard spoken aloud since she was a child, since a small girl on a sea wall had translated secrets for her — and the sound went through Yara like cold water, like a hand closing around her wrist, like a nickname only the dead were allowed to use.

It sounded, impossibly, like *Merry*.

"What did you say?" Yara asked. Her voice came out strange.

Meriel Kestrel looked at her with those slate-water eyes, and smiled the way the tide smiles at a sandcastle, patient and certain.

"That," she said, "is lesson one. If you decide to come."
