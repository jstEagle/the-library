## 5. What the Tide Keeps

The word for *fog* in Kerowen is *hess*, and it is also the word for *mercy*, and Meriel Kestrel had been saving that fact for a morning when the fog actually came, because she believed — she told me this later, and I believe her — that a language should be learned inside its own weather.

So on the fourth Thursday of my lessons, when the cove wore its grey hood and the window of her cottage went soft as breath on glass, she set down her tea and said, *Hess*, pointing at the window. Then she touched her own chest, lightly, two fingers over the sternum. *Hess.*

"That's morbid," I said, in English, because English was still my refuge, my coat I could put on and take off.

"It's honest," she said. "Fog is what the sea does when it wants to be kind to itself. You can't see how far you've come or how far you have left. Some days that's mercy." She wrote the word on the slate she kept for me, though she hated writing Kerowen — said the alphabet was a cage with the door painted on. "Now. *Aneth.* Say it."

*Aneth.* Tide. The pulling-back. We had done tide three weeks running because I kept getting the vowel wrong, flattening it into something English, and she kept correcting me with a patience that felt less like teaching and more like waiting.

"Again."

"*Aneth.*"

"Better. Do you know what the full word is? What the children's rhyme calls it?"

I shook my head.

"*Aneth-vel.* The tide that keeps. Not the one that takes — there's another word for that, *aneth-dur*, the hungry tide, the one that pulls things out and doesn't give them back. But *aneth-vel* keeps. Things go out with it and it holds them, somewhere past the sandbar, and brings them home when it decides they're ready." She looked at me over her spectacles. "Your sister used to sing about it. Did you know that?"

The room tilted slightly, the way rooms do when a sentence has too much weight in one corner. "Nell didn't speak Kerowen."

"Nell sang everything," Meriel said. "Half of it was nonsense and half of it was older than nonsense." She turned back to the slate as if we were discussing weather after all. "Today we'll do verbs of holding. There are nine. English gets by with one, which is why English speakers drop so much."

But I wasn't listening anymore. I was doing the arithmetic I'd been refusing to do for ten years: Nell, age seven, singing in a dying tongue; Meriel Kestrel, last speaker, living four miles up the coast road from our house; the fire, the night, the volunteer firefighter whose name appeared once in the local paper and then never again — *a passerby assisted* — and my mother reading that paragraph at the kitchen table with an expression I had filed, all these years, under *grief*, because filing things was the family business.

"You were there," I said.

Meriel's chalk stopped mid-stroke.

"The night of the fire. You were the passerby."

She set the chalk down with great care, the way you set down something you might need to throw. "Yara—"

"You pulled us out." My voice sounded far away to me, like it was coming off the water. "Teo and me. That's why you came to the shop. That's why you wanted—" I stood up so fast my chair went over backward and neither of us moved to catch it. "Ten years. Ten years you knew where we were and you never—"

"Sit down, child."

"Don't call me child. Don't—" My hands were shaking and I made fists of them, which is a thing I learned young, in that house, in that family, where feeling anything visibly was a kind of trespass. "Why now? Why did you wait until everyone who could tell me themselves was dead or gone?"

Meriel looked at me for a long moment. Then she did something I will remember on my deathbed, whatever else I forget: she reached across the table and took both my fists in her hands, and opened them, finger by finger, like a woman unfolding a letter.

"Because I'm dying," she said simply. "And secrets are heavy luggage, Yara. I've been carrying yours since you were eleven years old, and I am so tired."

"I don't have secrets."

"No," she agreed. "You have one secret wearing ten years of postcards."

I should have left then. Any sensible person would have left. Instead I sat down on the floor, next to the fallen chair, because my legs had made the decision without consulting me, and Meriel came around the table and sat opposite me on the cold flagstones, her back against the dresser, her breathing audible now in a way it hadn't been in September.

"Tell me," I said. And then, because even now I couldn't ask plainly: "What did you see?"

* * *

She told it plainly, which is the only way it can be told. She was walking home along the coast road from a meeting of the heritage society — the irony of that, she noted, was not lost on her — when she saw the glow above the trees and ran. The house was already eating itself. Your father was on the lawn, she said, screaming your mother's name, and your mother was on the lawn too, and neither of them was going toward the door.

"I went," Meriel said. "Someone had to."

The smoke was a wall. She found Teo first, in the hallway, curled at the foot of the stairs like something washed up. She carried him out and gave him to his father and went back. She found me on the landing outside Nell's room, she said, standing very still, not coughing, not moving, just standing in the smoke like a girl waiting for a bus.

"Were you going in?" she asked me now, gently. "That night. Were you trying to get to her?"

"I don't remember," I said, which was true, and also the sentence I had built my whole life on top of.

"Mm," said Meriel, who had spent eighty-one years learning the difference between *I don't remember* and *I won't*. "I carried you out. You weighed nothing. You kept saying a word over and over, in your sleep for weeks afterward, your mother told me — no. No, your mother never told me anything. That's not right either." She pressed her palms flat to the floor, steadying herself against something. "Let me say it in order."

"In order," I whispered.

"Afterward, your mother came to this cottage. Three days after the funeral. She sat where you're sitting — no, where the chair is, people sit in chairs in my house, mostly — and she asked me what I saw inside. And I told her the truth, because I hadn't yet learned what your family does with truth. And she listened, and thanked me, and then she took my hands the way I'm taking yours now—" and indeed she was, both my hands in both of hers, her grip surprisingly strong, "—and she said: *you must never tell them what you saw in that room. Promise me. It will kill them, and it will kill them worse than the fire.*"

The fog pressed against the windows. Somewhere outside, a gull said something unspeakable.

"What did you see," I said. Not a question anymore. A demand with the grammar stripped out of it. "In Nell's room. What did you see in my sister's room?"

Meriel closed her eyes. When she opened them they were wet, and furious, and old.

"I saw that the lamp was already broken," she said. "Before the fire reached it. Broken on the floor by the door, knocked from the shelf, oil spread across the boards. And I saw that the window was open, and the curtain pinned back, and the little chair set under it — the way we used to teach the children to escape, at school, in case of fire. Chair under the window. Window open. Someone had taught her, Yara. Someone had shown her the way out, and she almost made it, and the lamp—" Her voice cracked like ice on a pond. "The lamp was not what killed her. The lamp started it, but the smoke came faster than the chair was tall. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Your mother didn't want you to know that Nell had been *ready*. That she'd climbed onto the chair and opened the window and the smoke beat her by a minute. A minute, Yara. Your mother thought that would kill you worse than blame would."

"And instead she let me grow up thinking—" I couldn't finish it. The sentence wouldn't come in any language.

"She let you grow up thinking whatever you already thought," Meriel said softly. "Which is the cruelest thing a parent can do. Silence doesn't protect a child from a story. It just means the child writes the whole book alone."

I got up. I don't remember deciding to. One moment I was on the floor holding an old woman's hands and the next I was standing in the doorway with fog coming in past me like it had been invited, and Meriel was saying my name — *Yara* — and then, when I didn't stop, saying it in Kerowen, *Yarath*, which I hadn't learned yet, which she had never taught me, a form of my name I didn't know existed, some older tenderer grammar of it, and I walked out into the fog anyway, down her path, onto the coast road, the road I had avoided for ten years, walking north without deciding to, because north was the cove, and the cove was the house, and the house was ash and a locked gate and a FOR SALE BY AUCTION sign I had refused to look at directly for six weeks.

Behind me the cottage light shrank to a smudge. Ahead of me the fog did its mercy-work, hiding how far I'd come and how far I had left, and I understood, finally, why a language would make those the same word.

I walked until the ruin rose up out of the grey like something surfacing from *aneth-vel*, the tide that keeps — and there was a figure standing at the gate.

My brother. Looking at the house. Holding a folded auction notice, and turning, as he heard my footsteps, to see who had come out of the fog.
