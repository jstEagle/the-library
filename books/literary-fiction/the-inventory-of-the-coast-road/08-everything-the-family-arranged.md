## 8. Everything the Family Arranged

The care home smelled of lavender and disinfectant, a combination that had always struck Teo as an argument between two kinds of forgetting. He signed the visitor book in his old handwriting, the one he'd trained out of himself at university and which returned, unbidden, whenever he came back to this coast — the letters leaning east like something blown.

His father was by the window in the day room, doing the crossword in pencil. Not solving it. Just moving the pencil along the empty squares as if counting them.

"Dad."

Gerald Amberdale looked up. It took him three full seconds to find his son's face, and Teo watched him do it — watched the search happen, the small panic behind it, then the settling. "Teodor. You're thin."

"I'm the same as I was at Christmas."

"You're thin." His father set down the pencil with great care, aligning it parallel to the newspaper's edge. A man who had spent forty years surveying land could not leave anything unaligned. "Have you come about the house?"

Straight to it. That was Gerald's gift: he could make any conversation a transaction within two sentences. "I've come to see you."

"You've come about the house." His father's eyes were clear today. That was the cruelty of it — some days the fog took him entirely, and on the clear days he remembered everything, including how much he'd rather not. "Everyone comes about the house now. The solicitor writes. The auctioneer telephones. Your sister—"

He stopped.

"Yara," Teo said. "You can say her name, Dad. She's not dead."

"No," his father agreed, and looked back out the window at the car park. "No, she isn't."

* * *

Teo had told himself the visit was practical. There were documents only Gerald could sign — the family's interest in the estate had never been fully unwound after the mother died, some half-finished business from a decade ago that the solicitors kept flagging like a hazard on a chart. If Teo was going to bid, he needed the paperwork clean. That was the story he'd rehearsed on the drive over: land value, development potential, a sensible acquisition. The coast road was becoming fashionable again; someone would buy that plot regardless, so it might as well be someone who understood what the ground was worth.

It had nothing to do with the fact that he'd seen Yara leaving Meriel Kestrel's cottage on Tuesday, carrying a notebook against her chest like a schoolgirl, or that he'd stood in the lane for ten minutes unable to move his feet in either direction.

"So," his father said, when they'd exhausted the weather and the state of the coffee. "You're bidding."

"I'm considering it."

"Don't consider. Decide. That was always your trouble, Teodor. You stand in doorways."

The word went through him before he could stop it. *Doorways.* His father said it without weight, without intention, the way you say any word you've said ten thousand times. But Teo felt it land where it always landed — in the memory of a hallway, eleven years old, smoke coming under a door in a grey rope, and his hand on a handle that was already too hot, and his voice cracking open on a name while somewhere below him glass went and the whole night tilted toward orange.

He'd been standing in a doorway the night the house burned. He'd been standing in doorways ever since.

"The paperwork," he said. "For the sale. I need your signature on the release."

Gerald nodded slowly. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed — a dry sound, like paper folding. "Your mother would have hated this. An auction. Strangers walking through the rooms with little numbered paddles."

"There are no rooms, Dad. It burned."

"All the more reason she'd hate it." His father turned the pencil over once, end to end. "She wanted it gone, but gone properly. Not sold. Erased. There's a difference, you know. Selling is just changing hands. Erasure is a kind of mercy — her kind, anyway."

"What are you talking about?"

Gerald looked at him for a long moment, and Teo saw him decide something. It happened in his face the way weather happens over water: nothing you could point to, but afterward everything changed direction.

"Do you know why we never talked about it?" his father said. "The fire. Any of it. Ten years, and not one conversation — not between me and you, not me and Yara, not even me and your mother, though God knows she did most of the arranging."

"We didn't talk because there was nothing to say."

"There was plenty to say." Gerald's voice stayed level, surveyor's-flat. "That was the problem. We made sure of it. Do you think silence happens by accident in a family? Silence is built. It's load-bearing. Your mother built ours brick by brick, and she made us all promise to keep it standing."

* * *

They walked, because his father walked better when he was talking, and because the day room had begun to fill with the pre-lunch shuffle of residents and a television nobody was watching. They went out through the conservatory into the garden, gravel paths between raised beds, the sea visible past a line of wind-bent pines. His father moved slowly but with purpose, hands clasped behind his back like a man inspecting a site.

"She made us promise the week after the funeral," Gerald said. "In the kitchen of the rental house. You remember the rental house?"

"I remember boxes."

"She sat us down — you, me, Yara — and she said, *this family survives this by never speaking of it again.* Her exact words. I wrote them down afterward in my head, over and over, until they wore smooth. She said grief was a fire too, and the only way to put it out was to starve it. No oxygen. No mention. She said if we loved each other we would protect each other from remembering."

"And you agreed."

"I agreed because I thought she was right." His father stopped at the end of the path, facing the sea. "And because agreeing was easier than asking her the question I should have asked, which was: *what did you see, Marion? What do you know that requires this much protection?*"

The wind came off the water and Teo pulled his jacket closed. "Did she know something?"

"She knew everything. That was her burden and her method both." Gerald turned to look at him, and up close his eyes were wet, though his face hadn't changed at all. "She was downstairs when the lamp went. Did you know that? She heard the crash, heard the shouting stop, heard the flame catch. She was in the hall before either of you children even woke. And she got out through the kitchen and stood on the grass and let it burn, because by then the volunteer woman was already inside, already bringing you two out, and your mother — your mother stood on the lawn and watched her own house go and decided, right there, that no one would ever learn what had actually happened. Not what caused it. Not who was in the room. Nothing."

Teo's mouth had gone dry. "Caused it. Caused it how?"

"You'll have to ask your sister that." His father resumed walking, slower now. "Or don't ask her. That was always the other option. That was the option we chose for ten years, and look how well it served us. One child posting letters to a pile of ash, and the other buying the pile of ash at auction. Two very different ways of never leaving."

"You knew about the postcards?"

"Everyone knew about the postcards. The postmistress knew. Half the town knows. Your mother knew before she died and forbade us to acknowledge it — acknowledging it would have meant the silence had a crack in it." He laughed again, that folded-paper sound, but it broke halfway through. "Do you understand what she did, Teodor? She didn't protect us. She arranged us. Like furniture. Like inventory. Every one of us placed exactly where the truth couldn't reach us, and told it was love."

* * *

They sat on a bench by the pines, and his father dozed a little, and Teo watched the tide turn far below in the cove — the same cove, the same water, pulling the same way it had pulled the night everything ended. He tried to assemble the morning into sense. His mother, downstairs. His mother hearing the lamp. His mother choosing erasure over truth and calling it protection, and building a family around the absence the way you build a house around a load-bearing wall you never mention.

And him — Teo — about to complete it. That was the part that sat in his chest like swallowed stone. Because what was an auction, really? What was a bid, a deed, a transfer of title? It was the final arrangement. Strangers' paddles rising, a stranger's name on the register, the plot rezoned and fenced and eventually built over — and the last physical address where his family had ever been true would be paved into something with no history at all. His mother's mercy, finished by her son. Erasure, executed properly at last, with paperwork.

He thought of Yara in the lane outside Meriel Kestrel's cottage, holding her notebook. Learning, apparently. While he'd spent the week studying comparable sales.

His father stirred. "Decided yet?" he asked, without opening his eyes.

"Not yet."

"Good. Standing in doorways." A pause. "Though I'll tell you what I've learned here, in this place, watching people arrive with their boxes and their photographs. Doorways are where the decisions live. Everyone pretends otherwise — pretends the decision is out there, in the room, waiting. It isn't. It's in the frame. In the moment your hand is on the handle and you can still choose which side of the door you end up on." He opened his eyes. "Your mother chose her side. I chose mine. Don't inherit our choice, son. Make your own. Even if it's wrong. Especially if it's wrong — at least it will be yours."

* * *

Teo drove back along the coast road because the inland route felt like cowardice now, and the road gave him the ruin at the bend where it always gave it to him: black gable against the sky, gorse reclaiming the garden, the chimney standing like a man who'd refused to sit down. Auction in four days. Closing soon after, if he won.

At the top of the hill he passed a figure walking up from the cove path — dark coat, canvas bag, silver hair moving in the wind. Meriel Kestrel. She stopped as his car drew level, and for a moment, through the glass, they regarded each other: the last speaker of a dead language and the last son of a burned house.

Then she raised one hand — not quite a wave, more a summons, or a warning — and pointed, deliberately, at the ruin behind him. Said something through the window he couldn't hear. Made the shape of it with her mouth anyway, twice, slow, like a lesson.

He drove on. But at the junction he pulled over, and sat with the engine ticking, and took out his phone, and found Yara's number unchanged these ten years though he'd never once dialed it.

Four days. One question. He typed: *Are you free tomorrow?*

And stared at the send button, hand on the handle of the door.
