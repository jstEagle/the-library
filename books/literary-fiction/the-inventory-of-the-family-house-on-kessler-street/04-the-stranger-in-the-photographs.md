## 4. The Stranger in the Photographs

The club program was folded into the back of a photograph, the way a receipt is folded into a wallet by someone who means to deal with it later and never does. Astrid found it at two in the morning on the fourth night after the tapes, when sleep had become a country she could no longer enter without a passport she didn't own.

The paper was soft as cloth from handling. *The Blue Lantern Room — Presents — Friday & Saturday Evenings — October 1963.* And there, beneath a woodcut of a lantern that looked more like a gallows lamp than anything festive:

**THE MELODY SISTERS — VERA & JUNE — VOCALS, GUITAR**

Vera. Her mother's name was Eleanor. Eleanor Whitlock, née Osei, who signed checks in a hand so small it looked apologetic, who had told Astrid once, when Astrid was nine and asked if she'd ever wanted to be anything, that she'd wanted to be *finished*—and Astrid had thought it a strange, sad joke, and now it was neither.

Vera. A name chosen the way you choose a coat you intend to abandon at the end of the evening.

And June. June, who had a face in the photographs—high cheekbones, a gap between her front teeth that showed when she laughed, and she was laughing in four of the eleven photographs, always leaning toward Vera, always toward the camera's edge as if she might step out of the frame and into the room where Astrid was sitting with a dead woman's fountain pen in her hand.

Astrid laid the program flat under the desk lamp. The Blue Lantern Room. She typed it into her phone with her thumbs, that clumsy modern prayer, and got a defunct jazz bar in Portland, a lantern festival in Kyoto, a poem. She added *Dayton* and got a realtor's listing: a vacant storefront on Fifth Street, 2,400 square feet, "character property, sold as-is," photographed in the flat gray light of an Ohio February. The last review she could find was from 1966, in a defunct paper archived by the library—*the Lantern remains the best room in the city to hear a woman sing a song she wrote herself.*

A woman sing a song she wrote herself.

Astrid sat with that sentence for a long time, the way you sit with a diagnosis.

* * *

The bartender's name was Hollis Grange, and he was ninety-one years old, and he answered his phone on the second ring as though he'd been waiting all day for it to be this particular call.

"Blue Lantern," he said, when she explained. "Sure. I poured there twenty-two years. Started when I was nineteen, which tells you I'm either old or lying, and it's both."

Astrid had rehearsed a preamble on the drive back from the library—something about research, a family history project, a gentle framing that would let her retreat if she needed to. It dissolved the moment he spoke. "My mother," she said instead. "Eleanor Osei. She might have performed there. Under the name Vera."

A silence on the line, long enough that she checked whether the call had dropped.

"Vera," Hollis said, and the way he said it was different from the way he'd said Blue Lantern. Softer. Like a word you keep in a drawer. "Vera and June. The Melody Sisters. Honey, nobody's said those names out loud to me in fifty years."

"You remember them."

"I remember *everything* about them, which is a problem at my age, because the important things are supposed to go first and those two never left." She heard him shift, a creak of a chair, the small percussion of a man settling in for a story he'd had no one to tell. "Friday and Saturday nights, October of '63 through—when did they stop? Spring of '64. Vera played a parlor guitar with a crack in the body she'd mended herself with glue and a clothespin. June sang harmony a third above, and when they hit the blend—" he stopped. "You know how you can hear a room go quiet? Not polite quiet. The kind where people forget their drinks exist."

Astrid's throat was tight. "What did they play?"

"Vera's songs. All of them hers—June told me once, laughing, said Vera wouldn't sing a note she didn't write, was stubborn about it, said a borrowed song was a borrowed coat. They did covers to open the sets, standards, things the crowd knew. But the middle of the night, eleven, eleven-thirty, the room would empty of strangers and fill with regulars, and Vera would take off the armor and sing her own." A pause. "There was one she never finished. June used to beg her. *Finish the damn song, Vera.* And Vera would say, *It's not done because it's not true yet.*"

Astrid wrote that down on the legal pad without deciding to. Her handwriting shook. *It's not done because it's not true yet.*

"Hollis," she said. "What happened in the spring of '64?"

The creak of the chair again. When he answered, his voice had changed register—the way men's voices do when they move from anecdote to caution, a gear she recognized from funerals, from hospital corridors, from every conversation conducted at the edge of something.

"They disappeared. Not together—June first, then Vera, two weeks later. No goodbye show, nothing. June's cousin came and got her guitar. And I asked around for years, off and on, and got nothing but a shape of a story. Somebody said a man had been asking questions about them. Somebody said June's family found out something. Somebody said—" He stopped himself, and Astrid heard the whole sentence anyway, the one the era had taught everyone to leave unfinished. "It was 1964, honey. Two women, one Black and one white, singing love songs to each other in a public room. You didn't need a detective. You needed to be lucky, and they ran out."

Astrid looked at the photographs spread across the desk. June's laugh, frozen mid-note for sixty years.

"June's last name," she said. "Do you know it?"

"Calloway. June Calloway. And before you ask—no, I don't know where she went. But her people were from around Chillicothe way, I think, or Columbus. There was a sister." He exhaled, a long staticky breath. "You said this was your mother. Vera was your mother."

"Yes."

Another silence, and then, gently, in the voice of a man who had tended bar for twenty-two years and therefore knew exactly how much truth a person could hold at one time: "She had a good face, your mother. When she sang, she had the best face I ever saw in that room, and I saw a lot of faces. Whatever she did after—whatever she had to become—that face was real. Don't let the rest of it take that one from you."

Astrid thanked him. She did not trust herself to say more.

* * *

Daniel called at nine the next morning, which meant he'd been awake since six, which meant he'd been composing.

"Hey," he said. "Good time?"

"I'm up."

"How's it going over there? The, uh. The sorting."

The sorting. As if the house were a load of laundry. Astrid stood in the kitchen with the phone wedged between ear and shoulder, her free hand flat on the counter where her mother had kneaded bread every Sunday of Astrid's childhood, a ritual so consistent it had functioned as a clock.

"Going," she said.

"Listen, I've been thinking. I talked to Marcus at work, his brother-in-law does relos—relocation appraisals—and he says the market on these older houses is moving fast right now. Like, *fast* fast. If we listed in the next few weeks we could be looking at a clean close before the holidays." He was talking faster now, the way he did when he'd decided something and was presenting it as a discussion. "I know it feels big, but honestly, Astrid, every week we hold it is a week of taxes and upkeep and—you know. And you being alone down there."

"I'm not alone."

"No, I know, I mean—"

"There are people in this house, Daniel. There have been people in this house the whole time. We just didn't know their names."

A pause. She could hear him deciding whether to ask, and she could hear, beneath that, the other thing—the ten years of things neither of them asked, stacked in the silence like the tapes in the basement, unlabeled, unplayed, waiting for someone with the nerve to press a needle down.

"What does that mean?" he said finally.

"It means I found something. About Mom."

"Found what?"

And here was the moment—the hinge, the door, the whole architecture of her marriage balanced on it. She could tell him. *My mother was a musician, Daniel. My mother was in love with a woman named June and wrote her songs and then buried it all under a house and a husband and a daughter and forty years of Sunday bread.* She could say it in one breath, and he would be kind, because Daniel was kind the way weather is kind, generally, in the aggregate, useless in any particular storm.

"I'm still figuring it out," she said.

"Okay." A relief in his voice so quick he probably thought she hadn't caught it. He had. She always caught it now; that was the disease. "So—two weeks? Can I call the appraiser? I just think Mom would want it handled, not dragged out. She was a private person. You know how she was about the house."

*She was a private person.* The family epitaph, the sentence they'd all agreed to carve. Astrid looked at the program on the table, at VERA & JUNE in woodcut letters, at the whole loud public life her mother had conducted under a false name in a room full of strangers because it was the only place she could be true.

"Yeah," Astrid said. "She was private."

"I'll have Marcus's brother-in-law reach out Monday. And hey—come home soon, okay? The apartment's weird without you. Quiet."

"I know," Astrid said. "It's quiet here too."

It was not quiet. It was the opposite of quiet. It was full to the rafters with voices, and the only person who'd ever bothered to listen had done it alone, in a locked room, at night, and had never once told anyone what she heard.

* * *

After the call she sat at her mother's desk and made a new column on the legal pad. Not objects. Names.

*Hollis Grange — bartender — has the shape of the story.*
*June Calloway — Chillicothe or Columbus — a sister.*
*Vera — my mother — unfinished song, unfinished sentence, unfinished life.*

She looked up Chillicothe. Forty-seven miles. She looked up Columbus. Seventy-two. She looked up *Calloway* in both counties and got eleven results, most of them dead ends, one of them a woman named Denise Calloway-Reed listed in a church newsletter from 2019 as directing a community choir—*in memory of her mother, June, who taught every child in this county to sing.*

Astrid set down the pen.

Her mother had taught her to be quiet. Had taught it so well that Astrid had married a man she spoke to in headlines, had spent ten years filing her own unsayable things in the basement of herself, locked, unlabeled, waiting. And forty miles away there might be a woman who had grown up on June Calloway's knee, who might have the missing verse, the missing years, the missing mother.

Astrid picked up the phone.

It rang four times. Then a voice—older, unhurried, a voice with a choir's breath control—said, "This is Denise."

"Ms. Calloway-Reed," Astrid said, and her own voice did something she didn't recognize, something her mother's tapes might have known. "My name is Astrid Whitlock. I think your mother knew mine. I think—I think she was the other Melody Sister."

The line went very quiet.

Then, softly: "Vera," Denise said. "Oh, honey. You'd better sit down."
