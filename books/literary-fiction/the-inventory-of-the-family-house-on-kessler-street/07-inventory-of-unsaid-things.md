## 7. Inventory of Unsaid Things

The legal pad lasted nine days. On the tenth morning I turned it over.

The object list ran to four pages: two hundred and eleven items, each with a room, a description, a condition. *Piano bench, walnut, hinges rusted. Sewing machine, Singer, belt perished. Photographs, assorted, 1 box.* It was a good list. It was a complete list. It was also a lie, the way a map is a lie, all those roads and no weather on it anywhere.

I sat at the kitchen table with the pad in front of me and the tapes in their shoebox beside me, and I turned the pad over and drew a line down the fresh page, and at the top I wrote: **Inventory of Unsaid Things.**

Then I sat there for a long time, pen hovering, because I didn't know how to count a silence. You can't weigh it. You can't describe its condition. But my mother had spent sixty years not saying things, and I had spent forty learning the technique from her, and a thing practiced that long leaves evidence. It leaves a shape. The silence had a shape and the shape was this house.

I started writing.

*1. She never said who taught her to play guitar. I asked once, when I was twelve. She said, "Nobody, I just picked it up." Nobody picks up a guitar and writes forty songs. Nobody picks up a guitar and learns to fingerpick like that.*

*2. She never said why we never had music in the house. Radio only. Hymns at church. The piano was tuned every year and never played.*

*3. She never said who J. was.*

The pen was shaking. I kept going.

*4. She never asked about my marriage. Not once in fourteen years did she ask if I was happy. She asked if Daniel was well. She asked if we were comfortable. Comfortable. As if happiness were a temperature you could regulate.*

*5. She never said why she stopped singing.*

*6. I never asked why she stopped singing.*

I stopped there, at the symmetry of it. Six and seven were the same silence, held from both ends, like a rope in a tug-of-war where both teams had walked away and the rope just lay there on the ground for forty years.

The house was very quiet. It had been quiet for ten days, the particular quiet of a house with one person in it and a dead woman's habits still governing it—the thermostat at sixty-eight, the porch light on a timer, the good towels nobody used. I had been living inside her silences like a tenant.

*7. When I was nineteen and came home crying after—after the thing with Marcus, she made tea and said, "You'll be fine. You're strong." She never asked what happened. I never told her. We sat in this kitchen and drank tea over the body of the thing I couldn't say, and she let me not say it, and I understood even then that this was her love language: permission to conceal.*

*8. She never told me she was afraid. I found out she was afraid from a bartender in a strip mall in Dayton.*

The phone rang. I let it go to the machine, and Daniel's voice filled the kitchen, polite and slightly too loud the way his voice always got when he was performing reasonableness. "Astrid. It's Wednesday, so I assume you're alive. The realtor sent comps for the Kessler Street house. We're looking at a range that would clear the loan and leave a decent margin. Call me when you get a chance. I know you're—doing what you're doing. I just think we should move on this before winter."

*Doing what you're doing.* Fourteen years of marriage and he still described my inner life the way you'd describe a hobby. *9. He never asked what I found in the basement. I told him I found tapes and he said, "Old radio shows? That's fun," and I let him believe it, and I have been letting him believe things for fourteen years.*

I wrote that one down with a hand that had gone cold.

Because here was the thing I had been circling for ten days the way you circle a drain: the tapes weren't just my mother's. The habit was mine too. I had a basement. I had a locked door. I had things in boxes that Daniel had never seen, and I don't mean objects.

I mean that in the fall of 2011 I stood in a hospital corridor in Chicago holding a phone call I should have forwarded and didn't. I mean that there is a version of our marriage that ended in a fertility clinic waiting room in 2016, where I told him I'd changed my mind about trying, when the truth was I hadn't changed my mind, I had changed my mind about *telling him what I wanted*, because wanting things out loud felt like standing at a microphone in 1963 Dayton with your name on the marquee and the whole town watching. I mean that I have said "fine" so many times that the word has worn smooth as a river stone, and he has built his whole understanding of me out of river stones.

My mother chose silence to survive. What was my excuse?

I kept writing until the light changed. The list grew. Small things, old things. The time she changed the subject when I asked about her college years. The way she said *we don't discuss that* about my cousin's divorce, and about money, and about Aunt Ruthie's first husband, and about the year 1964, which I now understood had been a year the way a wound is a place.

*23. She never once said "I wanted." Not I wanted to sing. Not I wanted her. Not I wanted a different life. She said "it was a different time" as if time were weather that happened to her, as if she hadn't made a choice inside it.*

I was on item thirty-one when the gravel crunched in the driveway.

I knew his footsteps. Fourteen years. He came up the porch steps and I heard him stop at the door and I heard him not knock for a moment—Daniel, who always knocked on his own mother's door, Daniel, who had been raised to announce himself—and then the key I'd given him turned in the lock.

He stood in the kitchen doorway with his coat still on, holding a folder of realtor paperwork like a shield, and he looked at me, at the table, at the shoebox, at the reel-to-reel machine sitting on the chair beside me with a tape still threaded on it, reels slack, mid-song.

I hadn't noticed it was playing. It had been playing, low, for I don't know how long. My mother's voice, young and warm and completely unguarded, filled the kitchen, singing a song addressed to someone named J., and my husband stood in the doorway hearing it for the first time.

"What is that?" he said.

"That's my mother."

"That's not—" He stopped. Listened. The voice climbed into the bridge, sure and aching, and came down the other side. "Astrid. That's not your mother."

"It is."

"Since when does your mother—" He set the folder on the counter, carefully, the way he set down anything when he was buying time. "What is this? What is all this?"

And I could have done it. I could have done the thing we were both best at. I could have said *it's nothing, I'm just sorting things out, it's been a strange week, how was your flight.* I could have handed him a river stone. Instead I reached over and turned the volume up.

He listened to the whole song. To his credit—he has his virtues, buried, but he has them—he didn't speak. When it ended, the hiss of the tape filled the kitchen like rain.

"Her name was on a marquee," I said. "In 1963. She performed under another name. She wrote these for a woman named June Calloway, and she chose not to exist instead, and she never told me, and she's dead, and I'm standing in her kitchen listening to her fall in love sixty years too late." My voice was doing something I didn't recognize. "And I'm forty-four years old and I have been doing a smaller version of the same thing my entire married life, and I didn't know until I found her tapes, and I don't know how to stop, Daniel. I don't know how to be a person who says things."

"Okay," he said slowly. "Okay. You're grieving. This is—"

"Don't." I was on my feet now. "Don't do the reasonable voice. Don't file me under grief. I found out my mother was a whole person and I realized I'm not one, and you're standing there with a folder of comps because you want to sell this house before winter, before I finish, before anyone has to feel anything about any of it. That's what you do. That's what *we* do. We sell the house before winter."

"That's not fair."

"Isn't it?" I was crying now, ugly, the way I hadn't cried at the funeral, the way I hadn't cried in fourteen years. "When did you last ask me a question you didn't already know the answer to? When did I last give you one? We have a marriage made entirely of things we decided not to say. I could inventory it. I *have* been inventorying it." I picked up the legal pad and held it out and he didn't take it, and I let it drop back to the table, and the sound it made was very small.

He sat down across from me. He took off his coat, finally, which in Daniel was an act of surrender. "The clinic," he said. "In 2016. You told me you'd changed your mind."

The kitchen went very still.

"I didn't change my mind," I said. "I changed my mind about wanting it where you could see me wanting it."

He looked at me for a long time. My mother's voice hissed on the machine, between songs now, and then—because the tape kept going—she spoke. Just a fragment, a scrap of between-song talk I'd heard a dozen times, but it landed differently with him in the room. A young woman laughing, low, and saying: *Okay, okay, one more. For J. You know which one.*

Daniel closed his eyes. When he opened them, he said, quietly, "Who was she? The woman. Do you know?"

"June Calloway," I said. "Dayton, 1963. And there's a radio program, Daniel. A late-night program out of Columbus that plays a song of hers—of *theirs*—with lyrics that aren't on any of these tapes, credited to J. Calloway. Which means someone is still alive. Which means I have maybe a little time and not much of it."

He looked at the folder of comps. He looked at the tapes. And then he said the first true thing I'd heard him say in years: "You're going to go find her. Aren't you."

It wasn't a question. It was a door, opening.

I looked at the legal pad, at item thirty-one, at all the blank lines below it waiting to be filled or finally, finally left empty.

"Yes," I said. "Tomorrow. And I need you to either come with me or let me go, but I can't do the third thing anymore. There is no third thing."

He didn't answer right away. Outside, the porch light clicked on—her timer, still keeping her time—and somewhere in the walls the old house settled around the two of us and the tapes and the truth, like it had been waiting sixty years for someone to say something out loud in this kitchen.

Daniel reached across the table and turned the pad toward himself, and read it, all of it, every item, while I sat there with my heart going like a girl's at a microphone in 1963.

Then he picked up the pen.
