## 5. Anselm at the Piano Bar

The Blue Meridian still had its sign, though the *B* had gone dark years ago and the neon now read *lue Meridian*, which Anselm had decided was either an accident or the most honest thing in the old quarter. A blue meridian. The line you crossed when you left yourself behind and kept going anyway.

He arrived at seven, as he always did on Thursdays, because Thursdays were when Mirabel Lockridge came.

Not that she came for him. She didn't know he existed—or she knew him only as the man at the gate, the one who had said the wrong name to her face and then stood there like a fool while forty years fell out of his mouth all at once. He'd rehearsed that moment differently. In the rehearsal, he said it gently: *You have her hands. You're the Verrone girl.* And in the rehearsal, Mirabel—no, not Mirabel, the girl's name was Mirabel but the woman at the gate was someone else entirely, some daughter-shaped stranger wearing grief like a borrowed coat—in the rehearsal, the woman did not look at him the way you look at a dog that has wandered onto your property.

He took his usual seat at the far end of the bar, the stool with the split vinyl that bit through his trousers if he sat wrong. Forty years of sitting wrong. He could find that stool blindfolded.

"Usual?" asked Denny, who had tended bar here since before the rerouting notices went up, since before the quarter started dying in earnest, since maybe before Anselm himself had stopped being Verrone. Denny was seventy if he was a day and had the memory of a man who had chosen, professionally, to forget most of what he saw.

"Ginger ale," Anselm said. "And whatever's quiet."

Denny almost smiled. "Everything's quiet on Thursdays."

That was the point. That was the whole architecture of the evening. On Thursdays the Blue Meridian emptied out by nine, the last regulars shuffling home past the boarded pharmacy and the nail salon that had replaced the butcher, and by ten the room belonged to the piano and whoever was drunk enough or lonely enough to sit near it. For three decades the piano had been played by a rotating cast of hired men who knew eight songs each and played six of them badly. Before that—

Before that, the piano had been played by a young man named Anselm Verrone, who had a brother with a violin and a wife with dark hair and a future so ordinary it glowed.

He looked at his hands on the bar. They were an old man's hands now, spotted and knuckled, but they still remembered everything. That was the cruelty of hands. You could burn a document, bury a letter, seal a door, and your hands would go on knowing.

* * *

She came in at 7:40, shaking rain off her coat, and Anselm did what he always did: he turned his face toward the back-bar mirror and watched her there instead, in reverse, small and silvered and safe behind glass.

Mirabel Lockridge. Twenty-nine, maybe thirty. She had her mother's jaw and her father's—no. He corrected himself, as he corrected himself every week. She had Lena's jaw. There was no other parent worth mentioning. Whatever the birth certificate said, whatever name the girl carried like a suitcase she hadn't packed, the jaw was pure Verrone, and the way she scanned a room before committing to a chair—that was Lena too, that animal accounting of exits, learned from somewhere, from some year Anselm had never been told about.

She ordered tea. Of course she ordered tea; the Meridian's tea was famously terrible, hot water and a bag of dust, and she drank it anyway, both hands around the cup, looking into it like a tuner looking down a soundboard.

He had learned things about her, these past weeks. Not by following her—he wasn't that man, he told himself nightly, though the distinction between watching and following felt thinner every Thursday—but by proximity, by the slow sediment of small observations. She worked out of a canvas bag with brass fittings, older than she was. She walked with her weight slightly forward, a listener's walk. Twice he had passed the Lockridge house on Delancey Row and heard, through the open windows, a piano coming back to life note by note under her hands, and he had stood across the street like a mourner at a closed casket until a neighbor's curtain twitched and he made himself move.

Tonight she wore her mother's coat. He was nearly sure of it. The camel wool, the missing button replaced with a mismatched one—Lena had lost that button in this very bar, winter of '81, laughing about it, and Anselm had sewn on the replacement himself because Tomas couldn't thread a needle and thought it was hilarious that his brother could.

The girl drank her terrible tea and pulled a notebook from the canvas bag and began writing, and Anselm sat very still and rehearsed.

* * *

This was how it went, every week. He built the confession in pieces, the way you'd build a fire in wet weather—one dry sentence at a time, cupped carefully, ready to catch.

*I knew your mother before she was your mother.*

No. Too much, too fast. She'd stand up and leave and never come back, and then he would have spent his one remaining connection to Tomas—a connection he hadn't even earned, a connection that was really just the accident of blood running in a stranger thirty feet away—on nothing.

*I loved your mother.*

Also true, also useless. Love confessed late is just an invoice presented after the goods are ruined.

*Your mother had another name. So did I. We were married eleven months in a house on Delancey Row, and my brother died in it, and she took the blame so I wouldn't have to, and I let her, and I have been paying rent on my cowardice ever since.*

There it was. The whole thing, laid end to end, maybe ninety seconds spoken aloud. He had timed it once, walking along the canal, saying it to the water. Ninety seconds. He had had forty years to say ninety seconds' worth of words, and every week he sat in the Blue Meridian drinking ginger ale and saying none of them.

Because here was the thing he could not get past, the reef his rehearsal always wrecked on: the truth would cost him the girl. Not her anger—he could survive anger; anger was a form of attention, and God knew he'd earned decades of its absence. It was worse than that. If he told her, she would stop coming to the quarter at all. She would settle the house, sell it sight unseen to the clearance developers, take the last bus out, and be done—with the neighborhood, with the name, with him. Why wouldn't she? Who stays in a place that turns out to be a crime scene? Who keeps a house where a man burned?

And the house was the only place Tomas had ever lived. The only place his violin had ever sounded. When they tore down Delancey Row—and the notices were already stapled to the poles, he'd read them, four months, maybe less—Tomas would die a second time, the final time, the time that counted, because the first death at least had left a room, a wall, a scorch shadow under the paint that no one had ever sanded out.

So Anselm did the arithmetic he hated. Every week he chose the girl's ignorance over the girl's trust, because ignorance kept her here, and here was the last address his brother had.

Guilt sharpened into patience. Patience curdled back into guilt. He drank his ginger ale and watched her in the mirror and called it love, because the alternative word was surveillance, and he was not ready for that word yet.

* * *

At nine o'clock the last regulars drained out, and Denny began stacking glasses with the particular loudness of a man signaling closing without saying it. And then—the part Anselm had come for, the part that had gotten him through the door every Thursday for a month—the kid on the piano took his break.

The kid was maybe twenty-two, a music student named Rafferty who played for drink money and treated the Meridian's upright like a chore. During his breaks the piano just sat there, lid down, holding its breath. And twice now, on those breaks, Mirabel Lockridge had drifted over to it.

Tonight was the third time.

Anselm held himself very still on the biting stool. She lifted the lid the way he imagined she lifted everything—delicately, diagnostically—and pressed a single key. Middle C. She tilted her head. Pressed it again. Then she reached inside, quick as a pickpocket, and adjusted something—a felt, a pin—and pressed it again, and even from the bar Anselm could hear the difference, the note settling into itself like a man finally sitting down after a long day.

She was tuning the Meridian's piano for free, on a Thursday night, in a dead bar in a dying quarter, and she didn't know—she could not know—that she was touching the exact instrument her mother used to lean against while Anselm played "La Vie en Rose" badly and Tomas pretended not to conduct from the corner table.

Lena used to say the piano was flat in October and sharp in June, that it breathed with the seasons like a living thing, and Anselm had told her no, pianos don't breathe, they're wood and wire, and she had looked at him with that expression—the one that meant *you are technically correct and completely wrong*—and said, *Then why does it sigh?*

The girl played a scale now, testing, and the sound went through Anselm like a needle finding an old scar. Not because she played well—though she did, mechanically, the way tuners do, every note given its fair due—but because of the phrasing at the top of the scale, the little hesitation before the turn, a hesitation Lena used to make humming in the kitchen. Genetics is a thief, he thought. It robs the dead and dresses the living in their clothes.

He should leave. This was the weekly verdict: he should stand up, put money on the bar, and walk out into the rain and never come back, and let the girl inherit her silence clean.

Instead he heard himself say, quietly, to Denny: "Send her a real tea. On me. Tell her the stuff from the urn isn't fit for—" and stopped, because *fit for people* was fine but *fit for her mother* was insane, and Denny was already looking at him with those bartender's eyes that missed nothing and judged less.

"On me," Anselm finished. "Don't say who."

"She'll know," Denny said. "Only two customers watch her tune a piano like it's church. One of them's me."

But Denny took the tea over anyway, and Mirabel looked up, and for one unguarded second she smiled at the kindness of it—a small smile, tired, real—and Anselm felt it land in his chest like a key turning in a lock he'd bricked over.

Then she did something new. She closed her notebook, capped her pen, and looked directly across the room at him. Not at the mirror. At him.

"You're the man from the gate," she said. Her voice carried in the empty room without effort, a voice trained to fill spaces. "The one who called me—"

"Don't," he said. Then, because the word had come out too hard, too much like a slammed door: "Please."

She studied him. He watched her do it, the professional assessment, the way she'd assess a piano brought in from a flood—what's warped, what's salvageable, what's lying about its condition. He had never been looked at so thoroughly by anyone but Lena, and it undid something in him that had been holding for forty years.

"You knew my mother," Mirabel said. Not a question. She'd done her own arithmetic; he could see the sum in her face. "Before the funeral. Before any of this. You knew her."

"I knew her," Anselm said.

"For how long?"

The honest answer was *eleven months and forty years*. The answer he gave, because he was a coward with excellent timing: "A long time ago. When we were young."

"What was her name?"

And there it was. The reef. The question he had rehearsed a hundred answers to and had no answer for, because every true answer was a detonation—*Verrone, her name was Lena Verrone, she was my wife, she is in the walls of your house*—and every false one was another brick on the pile that was already crushing him.

In the mirror behind the bar, Anselm saw himself: an old man in a wet coat, mouth open, silence pouring out.

"The same name," he said finally, "as everyone has, when they're young. The one they give up." He stood, put a folded bill under his glass, more than the tea cost by a factor of ten. "Ask your neighbors. Ask Mrs. Okonkwo. She remembers more than I wish she did."

"Why won't you just tell me?"

"Because," Anselm said, and the truth slipped out ahead of the lie the way water finds a crack, "some names are load-bearing. Take them out and everything comes down."

He was at the door when she spoke again, and her voice had changed—lower, steadier, the voice of someone who had spent the day listening to a house testify.

"There's a room in my mother's house," Mirabel said. "Sealed shut. Behind the parlor wall. The radiators know about it—they've been telling me all week. Bricks and plaster over a doorway, and nobody seals a room unless they're hiding something in it or hiding from it." She turned on her stool to face him fully. "Was it yours?"

Rain hit the window behind him. Somewhere down the block the 47 bus groaned through its route, headlights sweeping the wet glass, the last bus, the last season, the last of everything.

Anselm stood with his hand on the door of the Blue Meridian and understood that his patience had run out—not because he had grown brave, but because she had grown close, and the confession he had been rationing for forty years was now standing between them in the room like a third person, waiting to be introduced.

"It was ours," he said. "Mine and your mother's. And my brother's. And it's the reason one of us is dead."

He pushed out into the rain before she could ask which one.
