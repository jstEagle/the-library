## 6. The Sealed Parlor

Sleep did not come. It came near, circled the bed like a cat deciding whether to stay, and left.

At three in the morning Mirabel gave up on it and went downstairs in her mother's old cardigan, which she had been wearing for two days now without deciding to. The house was cold in the way houses get when no one has argued with them about temperature for a while. She lit the kitchen lamp and stood there listening to the radiators tick through their night shift, and she thought: I could drive back to the city tomorrow. I could put the house on the market with a phone call. I could be a woman who inherited a house she never wanted and sold it before it could tell her anything.

Then the radiator in the hall knocked three times, paused, knocked twice more—the phrase from last night, the one that didn't belong to any pipe logic she knew—and she heard herself say out loud, to an empty hallway, "All right. Show me."

She had worked on enough instruments to know that a sealed thing is not a silent thing. A piano with the lid closed still breathes through its seams. A room walled off is not gone; it is only holding its note. And this house had been holding something for forty years, and it was tired of holding it alone.

* * *

The map, if you could call it that, was made of heat.

Mirabel walked the ground floor at four a.m. with her palm flat against every wall, the way she'd check a soundboard for dead spots. The front rooms answered normally—cool plaster, even temperature, nothing to say. But along the east wall, past the pantry door, behind the coat hooks where her mother had hung the same gray coat for as long as Mirabel could remember, the plaster was warm. Not radiator-warm. Warm like skin under a bandage. Warm like something on the other side was alive.

She fetched the good flashlight from her tool case and got down on her knees at the baseboard. There it was: a seam in the wallpaper, so fine she'd have missed it if she hadn't spent twenty years reading the grain of spruce and maple. The paper had been hung over the seam deliberately, matched carefully, but paper stretches and plaster doesn't, and after four decades the lie had begun to show at the edges. She ran her thumbnail along it. Behind the paper, drywall—or rather, over drywall, because underneath the newer surface her knuckle found the hollow knock of lath and plaster, the old construction, the original bones.

Someone had built a wall inside a wall. Someone had closed a room the way you close a coffin.

"Okay," she said again, quieter this time, because the house felt like it was listening and she didn't want to seem ungrateful.

She went out to the van for tools. Standing in the driveway in the blue-black hour, breathing air that smelled of wet leaves and diesel from the depot two streets over, she caught herself checking the gate—the gate where Anselm had stood, where he'd said *Verrone* like he was handing her something heavy and expecting her to hold it. The street was empty. Somewhere a dog was making its one complaint of the night. She took the pry bar and the mallet and went back inside before she could talk herself into waiting for daylight, because daylight would mean neighbors, and neighbors meant questions, and questions meant admitting she was about to demolish a piece of her childhood home on the advice of a radiator.

* * *

She started where the wallpaper seam ended, low, near the floor, where damage would be invisible and repairable. Three taps with the mallet to seat the pry bar, then her weight behind it.

The house objected. The whole frame groaned once, a long creak running from the foundation up through the joists like a spine straightening after decades of slouch, and dust sifted down through the flashlight beam, and Mirabel stood very still with the bar in her hands and let the house finish its sentence.

Then she pulled.

The first foot of drywall came away in a sheet, trailing wallpaper like shed skin. Behind it: lath, plaster, horsehair insulation gone gray and soft as ash. She worked methodically, the way she'd strip a piano down to its plate—top layer, then next, no hurry, because whatever was in there had waited forty years and deserved to be met properly. Sweat ran down her back despite the cold. The cardigan came off. Her hands, which had been shaking since the funeral—not visibly, never visibly, just a fine tremor in the left hand that no client had ever noticed—went steady. This was the strange mercy of work. Give grief a task and it will stand quietly in the corner and wait.

By six, by the time the sky outside the kitchen window turned the color of dishwater, she had opened a hole two feet wide, and the flashlight showed her the rest: an interior door, painted shut, its knob green with verdigris, set in a frame that had been framed-over with rough pine studs. Beyond the door's fogged glass pane—a parlor. Furniture shapes. White sheets over everything, gone yellow, hanging like failed ghosts.

She sat back on her heels in the wreckage of plaster and let the vertigo come.

Because here was the thing she couldn't make fit: she had grown up in this house. Every room of it. She had played hide-and-seek in these halls until she was twelve, had mapped every closet, every cubby, every space under the stairs where a child could fold herself small. Children are the best surveyors of a house; they measure it with their whole bodies. And there had never been a door here. There had been a wall with coat hooks and her mother's gray coat, and behind the coat, wallpaper, and behind the wallpaper—

Behind the wallpaper, her mother had kept a room the size of a life.

Mirabel thought about the word *sealed*, which is what her mind kept offering, and how sealing is what you do to a letter, a verdict, a tomb. And she thought about her mother's hands, which had been soft and capable and had taught Mirabel to hold a tuning hammer, and she tried to imagine those hands spreading paste on the back of wallpaper, smoothing it over a doorway, pressing out the air bubbles, working by lamplight maybe, maybe crying, maybe humming—and the image was so intimate and so terrible that she had to put the pry bar down and breathe.

"You lied to me in stereo," she told the house. "You and her both."

But the house, she was beginning to understand, had only ever been the instrument. Her mother had been the player.

* * *

The door itself surrendered easier than the wall. The paint seal cracked along the jamb with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and then the swollen wood gave all at once and the door swung inward six inches and stopped, and a smell came out that stopped Mirabel's heart for one full beat.

Camphor. Old paper. Rosewater gone sour. And under it, faint as a fingerprint, wood smoke—the kind of smell that gets into plaster during a fire and never fully leaves, that surfaces on humid days for decades afterward. She had smelled it once before, in a client's house in the north hills, and the client had said, casually, *oh, that's from the fire, '79,* and moved on, and Mirabel had moved on too, because that's what you do with other people's fires.

This fire had no date. This fire had no story. This fire had been living eight feet from her bedroom for her entire childhood.

She pushed the door wide.

Dust lay over everything like a first snow that had fallen indoors and never melted. Sheets covered a sofa, two armchairs, a low table. On the far wall, curtained windows held a gray pre-dawn light that made the room feel submerged. Against the left wall stood an upright piano under its own sheet, and Mirabel's feet carried her to it before her mind agreed to go, because her feet were twenty years trained and they knew a covered piano the way a dog knows a covered bone.

She pulled the sheet off.

A Brandt & Söhne upright, 1920s, rosewood veneer crazed into a spiderweb pattern, ivory keys yellowed to the color of old teeth. She lifted the fallboard. The keys were filthy but intact. She pressed middle C. The hammers rose and fell; the strings were dead, slack, tuned down some catastrophic interval or simply abandoned—but the action worked, the mechanism remembered its job, and the note that came out was less a pitch than a sigh, a wooden exhalation, the sound of an instrument that had been holding its breath since before Mirabel was born.

On top of the piano, stacked square and neat, protected under a sheet of plastic that someone had cut from a paint drop cloth: sheet music.

She carried it to the window, tilted the curtain aside, and read by the strengthening gray light.

Schubert. A book of Chopin nocturnes. A folder of loose pages, popular songs from another decade, the kind of music played in hotel bars and neighborhood lounges by men who wore vests and rolled their sleeves. And on every page, annotations. Two hands.

One hand she knew instantly, the way you know a voice through a wall. Her mother's—small, upright, economical, the same hand that had written grocery lists and birthday cards and, once, a note in Mirabel's lunchbox every day of fourth grade. Fingering marks above the staves. Little vertical strokes for breath. In the margins, in pencil faded to ghost-gray, dynamics: *softer here. let it ache.*

The other hand was larger, looser, slanted hard to the right as if the writer were always leaning toward something out of frame. It wrote in ink, blue-black, confident. It wrote chord substitutions in the margins. It wrote, above the Schubert, a single word in a language Mirabel didn't recognize—something with a doubled consonant and an accent she couldn't place—and beneath the Chopin, in English, in that same leaning hand:

*Lena — slower. You rush the ending because you're afraid of it. Don't be afraid of it. — A*

Lena.

Mirabel stood very still. Outside, the first bus of the morning hissed past at the end of the street, and its sound came through the glass thin and far away, a world going on without permission.

Her mother's name was Dolores Lockridge. Had been Dolores Lockridge. Dolores on the funeral cards, Dolores on the deed, Dolores on the hospital bracelet Mirabel had clipped off her wrist two months ago with small scissors and kept in her coat pocket without knowing why. No one had ever called her anything else. No aunt, no parish priest, no drunk uncle at Christmas had ever slipped and called her Lena.

And yet here was a hand Mirabel recognized as surely as her own heartbeat, writing *Lena* across forty-year-old sheet music, being corrected, being coached, being told *don't be afraid of the ending* by someone who signed himself with a single letter and leaned.

Anselm. It had to be Anselm. The Verrone girl, he'd said, and Mirabel had assumed he meant her, had felt the floor tilt under the weight of a name aimed at her chest—but maybe he hadn't meant her at all. Maybe he'd looked at her face and seen the wrong woman standing there, a woman forty years younger, a woman named Lena who rushed her endings.

Grief, Mirabel discovered, can acquire a second corpse. Her mother had died three weeks ago; that grief was fresh-cut, still bleeding. But this—this was older, colder, a death that had happened decades ago and gone unreported. The woman in these annotations, the woman who played Chopin and rushed her endings, was already gone when Mirabel was born. Whoever Dolores Lockridge had been before she sealed this room and hung her gray coat on the hook and became a different person entirely—that woman had died in here, and no one had held a funeral, and no one had tuned the piano.

Mirabel sat down on the dusty bench—it held, solid, patient—and played the dead middle C again, and again, letting the flat wooden tone fill the sealed room, an autopsy note, a single struck bell for the unknown dead.

That was when she saw the drawing.

It was on the wall beside the piano, low, at a child's height, done directly on the plaster in crayon and colored pencil: a house with a crooked chimney, three stick figures holding hands in front of it—one tall, one medium, one small—and above them, laboriously, in the wobbling capitals of someone five or six years old, a name. Not a signature. A label, the way children label things to make them real, to claim them.

*LENA VERRONE.*

Three figures. One name. And the small figure, the child figure, had been drawn with Mirabel's own stubborn cowlick of hair, the one she'd fought her whole life, rendered here in crayon forty years ago by a hand that was not hers.

Or was it?

She pressed her fingers to the wall below the drawing, into the dust, and the dust was cold, and the room around her seemed to tilt slowly, gently, like a boat coming loose from its mooring. Two names. Two women. One face. A child who drew herself and called herself by a name that had been erased before she could read.

Somewhere upstairs, or in the walls, or in her own skull, a radiator knocked three times and paused and knocked twice more.

Mirabel took out her phone and photographed everything—the annotations, the drawing, the piano's serial number, the leaning blue-ink hand saying *don't be afraid of the ending*—and her hands were shaking now, visibly, uselessly, and she let them.

Then she heard it: the scrape of a shoe on gravel, out front, at the gate. Deliberate. Unhurried. The sound of a man who had been waiting a long time and knew exactly what he'd find when the wall came down.

She looked at the sealed door, at the hole in the wall behind her, at the child's crayon name drying on the plaster, and she went to answer it.
