## 1. The Inventory of Unsaid Things

The bell above the door of Letter & Ash had a crack in it, so that every customer announcement came out slightly wrong — a note that wanted to be cheerful but arrived instead as a small, apologetic cough. Zephyrine Hartwell had considered replacing it for six years and had never done so. There was something honest about a bell that couldn't quite commit to joy.

She was at the counter at nine-fifteen on a Tuesday in October, grinding coffee she would not drink, because the smell of coffee made the shop feel inhabited. The radiator under the window knocked twice, thought about it, and knocked again. Outside, the Rue du Flandre was doing its morning arithmetic: bicycles against trams, a man arguing gently with his dog, the bakery exhaling its first bread of the day into air already grey with drizzle.

The shop was narrow and deep, like a throat. Shelves of paper ran back into shadow — laid sheets, airmail stock, cards with dead kings on them, cards with no kings at all. A desk by the front window held her writing kit: three pens, one of which worked; a blotter stained with the ghost of somebody else's love letter; a jar of nibs she kept for customers who believed, wrongly, that fountain pens were romantic rather than treacherous. Above the desk hung the only decoration she permitted herself: a framed map of Brussels from 1958, when the city still had the decency to be embarrassed about itself.

At nine-thirty, her first client arrived. His name was Mr. Peeters, he was seventy-eight, and he came every second Tuesday to dictate a letter to his son in Toronto.

"Zephyrine." He settled into the client chair as though it were a throne he'd been promised. "I've thought about what I want to say."

"You said that last time."

"And I meant it last time. This time I have *thought* it." He produced a folded piece of paper from his coat pocket, opened it, and looked at it upside down for a long moment before turning it around. "Number one: I am not angry."

She wrote: *I am not angry.*

"Number two: I am a little angry, but only in the way a man is angry about weather."

She wrote that too, and did not smile until she'd finished the word *weather*, because Mr. Peeters could smell condescension the way cats smelled rain.

This was the work. People came to Letter & Ash because there was a sentence lodged in them like a fishbone, and they could not get it out alone. She charged by the page and nothing by the hour, which meant she sometimes spent forty minutes helping a woman decide between *I forgive you* and *I forgive you, but*, and she never once regretted it. There was a version of herself, years ago, who would have found this work unbearable — all that feeling, handled at such close range. But grief, she had learned, was mostly a clerical problem. It required filing. It required correct addresses. It required someone to sit across from you and write down what you actually meant, because what you actually meant was almost never what you first said.

Mr. Peeters dictated for an hour. The letter was four pages and contained, buried in the third paragraph, the true thing: *Your mother would have known how to talk to you, and I am not her, and I am sorry for the years I let that stand as an excuse.* He read it over twice, went quiet, and then said, "Yes. That's it exactly," in the voice of a man setting down something heavy.

He paid in exact change, counted from a coin purse, and left. The bell coughed behind him.

Zephyrine sat for a moment with the letter in her hands. Then she slid it into an envelope, addressed it in her best hand — the hand people paid for, the upright, unbothered script of a woman who had never lost anything — and set it in the outgoing tray beside eleven other envelopes.

Twelve letters going out today. Thirteen if you counted the one that wasn't there.

* * *

The postcards lived in the bottom drawer of the counter, beneath the spare ribbons and the dead pens, wrapped in a tea towel that had once belonged to the kitchen of a house that no longer existed. There were ninety-one of them now. She knew the number the way some people knew their weight or their debts: precisely, and without wanting to.

They were addressed, all of them, to *Mevrouw M. Hartwell, Rue Brogniez 47, Anderlecht.* Rue Brogniez 47 had been demolished eight years ago, along with the rest of the row, to make room for a development that had itself since gone bankrupt, so that the lot stood empty behind hoarding — a rectangle of weeds and broken brick where a house used to insist on itself. The postal service had returned the first card within a week, stamped *ADRESSE INCONNUE* in red ink that looked, to Zephyrine, faintly triumphant. After that she stopped mailing them. She simply wrote them, dated them, and put them in the drawer, where they accumulated like sediment, like rings in a tree, like the geological record of a woman talking steadily to nobody.

She took out the towel-wrapped bundle on Tuesdays and Thursdays, after closing, when the light through the window went the colour of weak tea. Today was Tuesday.

She chose a card from the box of blanks — this one had a photograph of the Atomium on it, absurdly, the great steel molecule grinning over the city like a monument to optimism — and uncapped the pen that worked.

*Dear Mama,* she wrote. *It rained all weekend. I bought a coat I don't need. The chestnut tree on the corner is dropping its fruit and the schoolchildren are collecting them in bags bigger than they are, which seems to me the correct order of priorities.*

She paused. This was the part where the card always became difficult — not the beginning, which was easy, domestic, safe, but the middle, where a normal correspondent would ask a question.

There were so many questions. They had backed up behind each other for thirty years, waiting their turn, and then her mother had died four years ago and the queue had dissolved all at once, unresolved, like a crowd when the lights go up. *Who was the woman in the photograph? Why did we never go back to Anderlecht? Why did you stop speaking Marols when you thought I couldn't hear the difference? What happened in 1994? Why was there a stranger crying on our doorstep the year I turned nine, and why did you close the curtains?*

She wrote none of these. Instead she wrote: *I found your recipe for waterzooi in the tin above the stove and made it wrong. The chicken forgave me. I hope that's hereditary.*

Then, as she did on every card, she signed it, dated it, and added the line she had added to all ninety-one:

*P.S. — Still here. Still asking nothing. Write when you can.*

She blotted it, tucked it into the bundle between number ninety and number ninety-two-in-waiting, and rewrapped the towel. Ninety-two unsent postcards to a house that was rubble. If she ever showed them to anyone — and she had shown them to no one, not the notary, not the occasional friend, certainly not Bram from the café, though Bram noticed everything — they would look less like correspondence than like evidence. Exhibit A: daughter, unable to finish a conversation begun too late.

The trouble with being good with words, she thought, screwing the cap back onto the pen, was that everyone assumed you were good with the ones that mattered.

* * *

At noon, Madame Colinet came in to order fifty wedding invitations for a granddaughter Zephyrine suspected, from certain details offered at length, did not entirely exist yet — the fiancé's name had changed twice in three months — but business was business, and hope was hope, and Zephyrine sold her the thick cream stock with the deckled edge and did not point out that the date given was a Sunday in February on which, per Madame Colinet's own account, the church would be closed for restoration.

In the afternoon a young man asked her to write a resignation letter "that burns bridges elegantly." She talked him down to a bridge left standing but lightly guarded. An elderly gentleman wanted the words *thank you for everything* translated into French, Dutch, English, and German for a retirement plaque, and grew tearful over the German, for reasons he did not explain and she did not press. A child of perhaps ten pushed through the door on a dare, stared at the shelves of paper as though they might be alive, and fled.

By five the light had gone the colour of stronger tea, and Zephyrine flipped the sign, swept, and made the inventory.

She kept the inventory in a green ledger, and it was not only paper stock. Every shopkeeper has her superstitions; hers was this: alongside the quantities of envelopes and ink, she listed the day's unfinished sentences. Not verbatim — she was discreet, professionally and otherwise — but in a private shorthand only she could read. *Peeters: anger/weather/son/Toronto — sent.* *Colinet: wedding/hope/doubtful — ordered.* *Retirement plaque: gratitude ×4 languages — delivered.* And at the bottom of every page, the standing entry that never changed, the one she wrote even on days when nothing else happened:

*Hartwell, M., Rue Brogniez 47: outstanding.*

Outstanding. The word did double duty and she let it.

Tonight she lingered over the entry longer than usual. October did that. Her mother had died in October, four years ago, on a night so ordinary that Zephyrine had afterward resented the weather for not marking it — no storm, no drama, just a Tuesday much like this one, a phone call, a hospital corridor the colour of porridge, and then a life reduced to a plastic bag of effects: glasses, a hairbrush, a bus pass, a key ring with one key that fit nothing Zephyrine ever found.

And in the bag, folded into quarters, a photograph she had seen maybe five times in her childhood and never once been allowed to ask about. Three young women outside a brick house, arms linked, squinting into sun. Her mother on the left, unmistakable even at twenty-five — that stubborn chin, that half-smile that suggested she was laughing at a joke she hadn't yet decided to share. On the right, a girl Zephyrine didn't know, tall, dark-haired, caught mid-grin. And in the middle, a third woman whose face Zephyrine had studied for thirty years without ever being able to name, though something in her posture — the way she leaned into Margriet Hartwell as though leaning were a language they both spoke — had always made her feel like an intruder looking through a window at a family that wasn't hers.

Her mother had burned papers before she died. Zephyrine had found the ash in the grate, and among the charred edges, one fragment of a photograph — the same brickwork, the same doorway — and had understood that whatever the picture held, her mother had wanted it gone and failed, or wanted it gone and relented, and either way had taken the explanation with her.

Zephyrine closed the ledger. Outstanding, she thought. Everything outstanding.

* * *

She stayed late, as she often did, not because there was work but because the shop after hours was the closest thing she had to a room of her own inside her own life. She made tea she let go cold. She read the same paragraph of a novel three times. Through the window, the street performed its evening shift: lights coming on in the café opposite, Bram hauling in the chalkboard sign, the drizzle giving up and starting again out of principle.

It was a good life, she told herself, and mostly believed it. She was competent; competence was a kind of company. Strangers trusted her with the sentences they couldn't say, and she gave them back polished, and they left lighter, and the bell coughed them out into the street, and she remained — the fixed point, the address that worked, the woman at the desk who was never the subject of any letter she wrote.

But competence, she had learned, was also a hiding place. You could spend years inside it. You could furnish it nicely.

She was reaching for her coat when she saw, through the glass, a figure standing across the street, motionless in the lamplight, looking at the shop. An old woman, small and straight-backed, wearing a coat too heavy for the season and a expression too fierce for the hour. She stood there long enough that Zephyrine stopped moving. Long enough that the moment acquired weight, the way moments did when someone was deciding something.

Then the old woman crossed the street with the unhurried certainty of royalty crossing a courtyard she owned, and the bell above the door gave its cracked little cough, and she was inside, filling the narrow shop the way weather fills a valley.

She looked at the shelves. She looked at the desk. She looked, last and longest, at Zephyrine, with pale eyes that seemed to be checking the goods against an invoice drawn up long ago.

"You are the one who writes," the old woman said. It was not quite a question. Her voice was low and gravelled, with an accent underneath the words like a stone under moss — Flemish, but older than the Flemish Zephyrine knew, rougher, a dialect from somewhere specific.

"I write letters for people, yes," Zephyrine said. "If you'd like to send one—"

"I don't want to send anything." The old woman planted herself in front of the counter. "Sending is finished. Sending is for people who still have someone at the other end." She set both hands flat on the counter, and Zephyrine noticed they were steady, and strong, and spotted like the backs of old leaves. "I want to learn. You will teach me."

"Teach you?"

"To write. Properly. In my mother's tongue." The old woman lifted her chin, and for a moment the lamplight caught her face fully, and Zephyrine felt the floor tilt very slightly, the way it does on a boat — because there was something in that face, something in the set of the jaw and the angle of the lean, that she knew. Knew from a photograph in a plastic bag. Knew from thirty years of studying a stranger's posture through silver nitrate.

The old woman was watching her with those pale eyes, missing nothing.

"Well?" she said. "Are you the one, or must I keep walking?"
