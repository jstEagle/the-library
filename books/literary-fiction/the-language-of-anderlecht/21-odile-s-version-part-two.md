## 21. Odile's Version, Part Two

The tape recorder sat on the kitchen table like a small black bird that had flown in from the future and didn't know what to do with itself. Odile had bought it at the flea market on Place de la Vaillance, from a man who swore it still worked, though it ate one cassette in three. She had bought it because her doctor used a word she hated — *documented* — and because some mornings she woke up and could not remember whether Margriet Hartwell was alive or dead, and the not-knowing was worse than either.

She pressed record. The little wheels turned.

"My name is Odile Verstraeten," she said in Marols, then again in French, then again in Marols because the French version sounded like a stranger talking. "I am going to tell what happened. Not tonight. Tonight I will tell the part before."

She made herself coffee she wouldn't drink. The flat smelled of yesterday's stew and the lavender sachets she stuffed into every drawer against a dampness that no sachet in Flanders could defeat. Outside, the tram sang its wire-song down the Rue Brogniez, and somewhere below, Bram was dragging his shutter up with the same three groans he'd been making for forty years, as reliable as church bells and half as cheerful.

"The part before," she said to the tape. "That's where you have to start, if you want it true. Everyone wants to start at the fire. The fire is the easy part. Fire is honest. It burns what's there and leaves ash, and ash doesn't argue with you."

* * *

It started, as most of Odile's disasters did, with a man who talked beautifully about money.

His name was De Vos — "the fox," which should have been warning enough — and he came into her life the way debt always comes: politely, through a friend of a friend, wearing a good coat. It was 1992. Anderlecht was changing; the slaughterhouses were closing, the young were leaving for cheaper walls in Dendermonde and Vilvoorde, and Odile Verstraeten, fifty-eight years old, widowed twice over by bad luck and worse men, owed money to people who had stopped being polite about it.

"I'll manage," she told everyone, which is what she'd said since she was nine years old, when managing meant going hungry so her sister could eat first.

Her sister. There it was. Lien had emigrated to Canada in 1968 and sent postcards for two years and then nothing but a Christmas card, unsigned, every December, like a ghost keeping its accounts current. Odile had written to her exactly once after the silence began — a letter asking, in effect, *what did I do* — and had never mailed it. It lived in the bottom drawer of the dresser with the other unsaid things, growing heavier each year, until the drawer stuck and she had to lift it shut with both hands.

So when De Vos sat in her kitchen explaining interest rates with the patience of a priest, she listened. And when he mentioned, offhand, that there were gentlemen in Antwerp who paid serious money for old correspondence — family letters, the older the better, signed, dated, with provenance — she laughed.

"Who would want my rubbish?" she said.

"Not yours," said De Vos, with the gentle cruelty of his profession. "But your friend's. The Hartwell papers. Old Brussels families kept everything, you know. Letters from before the war. Letters from Canada."

The kitchen went very quiet. Even the kettle seemed to hold its breath.

Lien's letters. Margriet kept them in the cedar chest in the back room of the house on the Rue Wayez — the house they'd all three grown up around, where Margriet had lived since her marriage fell apart, where the three girls had scratched their names in the hearth brick when they were children and sworn an oath in Marols that Odile could still recite in her sleep, syllable by syllable, like a rosary.

Margriet kept everything. That was the thing about Margriet. She was the keeper. She kept the letters and the photographs and the recipes and the grudges, all filed in that cedar chest with the brass hinges, and she would open it on winter evenings and read aloud sometimes, laughing at Lien's terrible spelling, crying once or twice when she thought Odile wasn't looking.

"She'd never sell them," Odile said. "Not for any money."

"No," agreed De Vos. "But you're not selling them. You're lending them. For appraisal. And appraisals take time."

Odile told him to get out. He left his card. The card stayed on the mantelpiece for five weeks, white and patient as a bone.

* * *

Here is what Odile Verstraeten wanted the tape to understand, and what she found hardest to say: she did not do it in one decision. Nobody does. You do it in installments, the way you pay off a debt, except this debt ran the other way.

First she took only three letters. "For appraisal." Lien's earliest ones, from 1968, full of Halifax fog and misspelled English. She told herself she would have them back within the month. She wrapped them in a tea towel and carried them across town feeling like a woman carrying stolen bread in occupied times — guilty, and light, and horrified at how light.

The Antwerp man — a collector named Vandenbroucke, soft-spoken, gloved, with hands like a surgeon and eyes like an accountant — turned the pages and made a sound low in his throat that Odile recognized, because she made the same sound over good lace.

"Do you know what these are worth?" he asked.

"No," said Odile, which was true, and also the last completely true sentence she spoke to him for a year.

He offered her money. Not enough to clear the debts — never enough, that is the arithmetic of debt, it is a well, not a hole — but enough to make the well feel shallower for a few months. Enough to stop the phone calls. Enough to buy back, for a season, the right to walk down the Rue Wayez with her chin level.

She took the three letters' money. Then, four months later, when the well deepened again, she took more. She invented reasons to visit Margriet — to help with the curtains, to bring rabbit for the pot — and while Margriet napped or stood at the stove, Odile sat by the cedar chest with its brass hinges and chose. She became, without ever saying the word to herself, a thief. A connoisseur of her own treachery. She learned which letters mattered least to Margriet on the surface and most to the collector underneath. She learned to fold paper back along its old creases so the chest looked undisturbed.

And the worst of it — she made the recorder hear this, made it hear twice — the worst of it was that Lien's letters got easier to take each time. Because each one she sold was one less voice reminding her that her own sister had stopped writing. She was selling her grief and calling it income.

By the spring of 1994 there were perhaps thirty letters gone. Perhaps more. She had stopped counting, the way you stop counting drinks.

* * *

Margriet nearly caught her twice.

Once, in February, Margriet went to the chest for a letter she wanted to reread — one where Lien described learning to skate on a frozen lake, terrified and delighted, "like a cow on ice, but happy" — and it wasn't where she remembered it being. Odile watched her friend kneel at the chest, turning papers with those quick clever hands, and felt her whole life narrow to the width of a cedar plank.

"It'll turn up," Odile said. "You've too much order in here, that's your trouble. Everything filed so neat even God can't find it."

Margriet laughed — that laugh, the one from the photograph, head tipped back — and let it go. That laugh forgave her, and the forgiveness was worse than accusation would have been. Accusation you can fight. Forgiveness you have to carry.

The second time, Margriet didn't laugh. She stood holding the chest lid open for a long moment and said, quietly, "Someone's been reading my mother's letters. The smell changes, you know. Paper remembers hands."

Then she looked at Odile, and Odile looked back, and lied. Lied in Marols, which is the worst language to lie in, because it has no room in it for softness — every word lands like a stone on ice. And Margriet believed her. Or wanted to. Or filed the doubt away in that cedar chest of a mind she kept, next to everything else.

* * *

The night of the fire, Odile had gone to the house to return something. That was the truth she'd never managed to say plainly, not to Bram, not to the insurance men, not to the priest who took her confession in 1997 and died before absolving her properly. She had taken a letter that afternoon — the last one, the one she'd sworn was the last, Lien's final letter before the silence, the one where she wrote *I forgive you both for whatever it was, life is too short and Canada is too cold* — and she was bringing it back. Slipping it into the chest, restoring the balance, buying back one gram of her soul at market price.

She was still on the stairs when she heard Margriet come home early.

There was no place to go. She stood in the back room like a burglar surprised by daylight, the letter in her hand, and Margriet walked in, and saw her, and saw the open chest, and understood everything in the space between one heartbeat and the next. Odile watched it happen — watched thirty years of friendship reorganize itself in her friend's face, watched love turn over like a fish showing its belly.

"You," Margriet said. Just that. In Marols, just that one word, and it contained the whole inventory.

What followed was not a conversation. It was a demolition. Margriet pulled letters out of the chest and held them up like evidence at a trial — *this one, and this one, and this one* — and her voice climbed until the neighbors' windows lit up along the street, and Odile stood in the middle of it taking it, because what else was there to do, and because some cowardly part of her thought: *at least she doesn't know about the money. At least the selling stays mine.*

They screamed at each other in the street, later — Bram would remember that, half the street remembered it — and the word that recurred, the word the whole quarter heard through glass, was *betrayed*, thrown back and forth like a stone neither woman would drop.

And then the lamp. Margriet had left a lamp burning near the window — she always did, for the neighbors coming home late, an old habit of the row — and in the storm of leaving, in the slamming and the shouting, something knocked it, or a curtain drifted into it, or — Odile never knew, and the not-knowing was its own punishment, assigned nightly — and the curtain caught, and the old dry house, ninety years of paper and varnish and grief, went up like a struck match.

Nobody died. Thank God, thank God, thank God — nobody died. But the cedar chest burned, and with it the rest of the letters, the photographs, Margriet's mother's recipes, the whole archive of a family, gone in an hour. And Margriet stood in the street in her coat watching it burn, and Odile stood beside her, and neither of them spoke, because there are no words in any language for *I did this* and *you did this* standing side by side in the same mouth.

Margriet moved away within the month. She never accused Odile publicly — never told the neighborhood, never told her daughter, never told anyone, which Odile understood eventually was not mercy but the deepest possible punishment: erasure. To be wronged so thoroughly that the wronged party declines even to mention you. Thirty years of silence, polished smooth as river stone.

And Odile kept the secret, and the debts eventually killed De Vos's patience and then De Vos himself, heart attack in a casino in Namur, and the letters sat in Antwerp behind glass, and Odile grew old in the last house on the row, alone with a dialect that was dying faster than she was.

* * *

The cassette clicked toward its end. Odile realized her coffee had gone cold hours ago. On the table, the recorder's little wheels had stopped.

She thought about rewinding, listening to what she'd said. She didn't. Some things you say once and trust to the machine.

Instead she got up, went to the dresser, and opened the bottom drawer — the one that stuck, the one she had to lift with both hands. Inside lay the unsent letter to Lien, thirty years old, thin as a wafer. She held it a long time under the lamp.

Then she set it on the kitchen table, next to the recorder, where anyone coming in the morning would see it first.

Because there was one thing more. One thing she had not yet said to the tape, one name she had not yet spoken aloud, and her courage, like her memory, was running out of afternoons.

Tomorrow, she told herself. Tomorrow she would say the daughter's name.
