## 23. Confrontation at the Hearth

Zephyrine had rehearsed the confrontation on the tram, on the walk from the stop, and twice more on the stairs of Odile's building, which smelled of boiled cabbage and other people's decades. She had rehearsed it the way she taught her clients to rehearse difficult letters: know your first sentence, know your last sentence, and pray about everything in between.

Her first sentence was: *I found the catalogue.*

Her last sentence was: *Tell me it isn't true.*

Everything in between, she suspected, would be a disaster.

She knocked. From inside came Odile's voice, sharp as a dropped pan: "It's open, I'm not dead yet."

The flat was dim, the curtains half-drawn against an afternoon that couldn't decide whether to rain. The hearth wall — the old brick chimney breast that ran up through the middle of the room like a spine — was crowded now with word cards. *Venster.* *Haard.* *Vergeven.* Taped at angles, some curling, some fresh, each one written in Odile's furious schoolteacher hand. It looked less like a home than like a life raft lashed together out of vocabulary.

Odile sat in her chair by the hearth, a blanket over her knees despite the mild weather, a cup of coffee going cold beside her. She looked up, and for one unguarded moment her face did something complicated — recognition, then relief, then something that might have been dread — before settling into its usual imperiousness.

"You're late," she said.

"I'm not. I said Thursday."

"Thursday was yesterday."

"Thursday is today, Odile."

A pause. Odile looked at the window as if consulting it. "Well," she said. "Then you're early for Friday. Sit down before you ruin the whole week."

Zephyrine sat. On the table between them lay the tape recorder they'd begun using for the lessons, its red light off, patient as a cat. Zephyrine's hands were shaking. She put them in her lap like misbehaving children.

* * *

Odile watched the girl sit down and knew, the way you know weather coming over the Kempen, that something was wrong. Margriet's daughter had her mother's way of holding her shoulders when she was about to say something brave and stupid. Like carrying a full pot of soup across a slippery floor: you could see the disaster coming and admire the determination.

She'd been having a strange afternoon. That morning she'd woken up certain it was 1991 and that she was late for a shift at the textile warehouse, and had gotten as far as putting on her work shoes before the flat rearranged itself around her and told her otherwise. The shoes were still by the door. She'd been looking at them when the knock came.

And there was something else, a splinter in the day. A word she'd lost. Not a Marols word — those she guarded like teeth — but a Dutch one, ordinary, the word for the thing you do when you've done wrong. She could see the shape of it. Four letters? Five? It began with a sound like an apology.

She decided not to need it.

"Well?" she said. "You're sitting there like a lawyer. Say what you came to say or open your books. We're doing the words for weather today, and you still say *regen* like a Fleming from a textbook."

Zephyrine opened her bag. Her hands came out with the catalogue page instead of the grammar exercises, and once it was out there was no putting it back, the way there's no putting back a glass you've already knocked toward the edge of the table.

"I found this," Zephyrine said. "Researching dialect collections. A private archive. Look at the entry, Odile. Look at it."

Odile looked. Her eyes went to the page and her face went absolutely still — not blank, Zephyrine noticed, but still, the stillness of someone standing very quiet in a house where they've heard a floorboard creak upstairs.

*'Correspondence of the Verstraeten-Hartwell families, 1960–1994,'* the entry read. *'Including letters of Lien Hartwell, final series.'*

"My name is on it," Odile said at last. "Verstraeten. And Hartwell." She looked up, and her eyes were clear, terribly clear, and for a moment Zephyrine thought: *now, now she'll tell me.*

"That's my family," Zephyrine said. "Hartwell. My mother. My aunt Lien. These are our letters, Odile. Our letters are sitting in a collector's cabinet in Antwerp, and your name is on the catalogue, and I want to know how they got there."

* * *

The word came back to Odile all at once, at the worst possible moment, arriving like a debt collector: *vergeving*. Forgiveness. No — not quite. The word she'd lost was smaller and heavier. *Bekentenis*. Confession. Six letters doing the work of a guillotine.

She could feel two selves in the chair, the way you feel two weathers fighting over one sky. One of them was eighty-one years old and tired of carrying a sack of bricks up the same hill every night for thirty years. That self wanted to say: *Yes. Yes, it was me. I sold them. I sold her sister's last letters because the bank was at the door and I told myself they were only paper, and I have been only paper ever since.*

The other self was nineteen years old, standing in the street outside a burning house while Margriet screamed at her in Marols, and that self wanted to say: *I don't know what you're talking about. Who let you into my flat? Where is my coffee?*

Both selves spoke.

"It wasn't me," Odile said. "I never touched those letters."

And then, almost in the same breath, quieter, to the cold coffee rather than to Zephyrine: "They were going to take the house, do you understand? In '94. The bank. They were going to take the house."

Zephyrine felt the floor tilt. "So it *is* true."

"I didn't say that."

"You just said—"

"I said the bank was at the door! There's a difference between a bank being at the door and a person selling letters!" Odile's voice rose, cracked, and for a moment she sounded like a much younger woman, or a much older one. "Everyone acts like paper is sacred. Paper is paper. People have to *live* somewhere."

"The letters were Lien's last words to my mother. Lien died thinking — whatever she thought — and my mother never got to answer her, because her answers are in a display case in Antwerp with a catalogue number."

"They're not in a display case," Odile snapped. "De Vos kept them properly. Acid-free folders. He was a barbarian about money but a saint about paper." She stopped. Her mouth closed with an audible click.

Zephyrine leaned forward. "You know his name."

"I read the catalogue," Odile said, "in the newspaper. Years ago. Anyone could have read it."

"In the newspaper."

"People read newspapers, girl. Even in Anderlecht."

Zephyrine sat back. Her rehearsed first sentence had done its work; the conversation was now officially beyond anyone's control, a boat with both oars over the side. She tried her last sentence instead.

"Tell me it isn't true," she said. "Look me in the eye and tell me, and if you can, I'll burn this page and never mention it again."

* * *

Odile looked her in the eye. That was the cruelty of it — she had always been able to look people in the eye; it was the one sport she'd never lost her form in.

But the fog chose that moment to come in.

It didn't announce itself. It never did. It was simply that halfway through assembling the sentence — *I, Odile Verstraeten, swear to you* — the middle of it went missing, the way a word goes missing, the way a street you've walked for sixty years goes missing, and what came out instead was:

"Margriet used to sit exactly where you're sitting. Right there. With her feet on my table, which I forbade weekly." Her voice had gone soft, conversational, addressing someone across thirty years. "She said, *Odile, if you ever lie to me I'll know it by your hands,* because I talk fine but my hands are honest, she said. Honest hands. Ha."

"Odile." Zephyrine's voice was careful, the voice you use near an edge. "I'm not Margriet. I'm Zephyrine. Her daughter."

"I know whose daughter you are." The fierceness came back like a match struck. "You think I don't know? You have her chin. You have her terrible habit of asking questions like accusations. She did that too. *Odile, where were you. Odile, why is the money gone. Odile, how could you.* As if I were a drawer she could open and inventory."

"Was the money gone?"

"I never said—" Odile pressed her knuckles to her mouth. Above her head, taped to the brick, the word card *vergeven* trembled slightly in the draft from the window. Forgive. Someone had written it there, and Zephyrine understood suddenly that the cards weren't only for remembering language. They were a fence around a field. Everything Odile was afraid to lose was pinned to that wall, and forgiveness was pinned there too, like a coat someone hoped would fit again someday.

"Please," Zephyrine said. "I'm not here to punish you. I'm here because I spent my whole life not asking my mother questions, and she died, and now the only person alive who knows the answers is sitting in that chair refusing to give them to me. Do you know what that's like? To be the last one left out of the story?"

Something moved behind Odile's eyes — the fog parting, or thickening, impossible to tell.

"Yes," Odile said. "I know exactly what that's like."

"Then tell me."

"I sold them."

The words fell into the room like a plate hitting tile. Zephyrine didn't move. Didn't breathe.

"To De Vos. The letters. Lien's letters, Margriet's letters, the ones from before the war even, family things, I boxed them up one Tuesday and took the tram to Antwerp and he paid me cash and I bought us eighteen months in this house." Odile's hands were flat on the blanket now, palms down, as if steadying something. "Eighteen months. And then the city changed its mind about the debt anyway, and I kept the house, and I lost—" She stopped. Started again. "I lost the only person who ever called me *Odi*, which no one else was permitted, which she wasn't permitted either technically, but she did it anyway, because she was Margriet."

"Odile—"

"And then the fire," Odile said, and her voice changed, went dreamy, went elsewhere. "That same year. God's punctuation. She stood in the street and screamed at me in Marols and everyone heard, the whole street heard, and I stood there and let her, because every word was true." She blinked. Looked at Zephyrine. Looked at the catalogue page as though seeing it for the first time. "What is this?"

Zephyrine's heart dropped. "The catalogue. The letters. You just told me—"

"Told you what?"

"That you sold them. You confessed. Thirty seconds ago."

"I did no such thing." Odile drew herself up, and the imperiousness reassembled itself around her like armor being buckled by invisible hands. "I have never sold anything in my life except fabric, and at fair prices. What is this paper? Whose handwriting is this?"

* * *

This, Zephyrine thought, sitting very still, is what it is to argue with someone who is disappearing. You land a blow and watch it pass through her like light through water. You get a confession and a denial from the same mouth inside the same minute, and both of them sincere, and you cannot cross-examine a tide.

"Odile," she said gently. "Look at the card on the wall. Read it to me."

Odile glared. But she looked. Her eyes found *vergeven* among the cards, and something in her face sagged, the fight going out of her the way air leaves a cushion when someone sits down.

"Forgive," she read. Then, in Marols, low, almost to herself: *"Vergeven is nie vergeten. 't Is betalen met wat je nog hebt."*

"Translate," Zephyrine said. "That's the rule. You made the rule."

Odile was quiet long enough that the rain finally made up its mind and started against the window.

"Forgiving isn't forgetting," she said. "It's paying with what you have left."

"Did Margriet forgive you?"

"I don't know." And this, at least, sounded like the truth — steady, unglamorous, arrived at honestly. "She died before I could ask. That's the joke of it, girl. Thirty years I've been writing her the apology in my head, and she never once wrote back, and I can't even remember now whether that's because she wouldn't or because I—" The sentence frayed. She looked down at her hands, spread on the blanket. "My hands are honest," she said, to no one. "She said so."

Zephyrine wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, angry at herself for crying first, and reached across and covered one of those honest hands with her own.

"Come to the shop tomorrow," she said. "Not for a lesson. Just come. There's something I found in Antwerp — in the letters. Something of hers. About you."

Odile's chin came up. For a moment she was entirely present, entirely fierce, entirely herself — the woman who had outlasted a bank, a betrayal, a burning, and now, daily, her own mind.

"If it's bad news," she said, "bring pastries. If it's good news, bring two kinds."

But after Zephyrine left, Odile sat alone in the lamplight with the rain on the glass, and looked at the word cards on her wall, all forty of them, and understood that one was missing. She got up, checked every card twice, checked behind the chair, checked under the rug, growing more frantic with each empty corner of brick.

The word for the thing you do when you've done wrong was gone again.

And taped to the wall, in her own handwriting she had no memory of writing, was a card she had never seen before, in careful block letters:

*MARGRIET — IK HEB HET GEDAAN.*

Margriet. I did it.

She stared at it for a long time. Then she took it down, folded it small, and put it in the pocket of her cardigan — over her honest hands — and turned off the lamp.
