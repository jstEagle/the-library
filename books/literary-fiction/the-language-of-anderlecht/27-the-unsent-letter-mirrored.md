## 27. The Unsent Letter, Mirrored

The archivist at the Verbeke Foundation had gone home an hour ago, but she'd left me the key to the reading room and a warning: *the heating switches off at six, miss, and so does the light, and I don't come back for either.* So I sat in the gathering chill with a box of other people's unfinished sentences, wearing my coat indoors like a penitent, and I told myself I was only staying because the tram was slow on winter evenings.

That was a lie. I stayed because of the folder in front of me.

It was thin — four sheets, maybe five — labeled in the collector's careful hand: *Hartwell, M. — drafts, unsent.* The foundation catalogued everything, even failure. Especially failure, it seemed. A man who bought letters by the kilo understood better than anyone that the ones never posted were often the heaviest.

I had already read Lien's last letter twice. I had already read my mother's marginalia — the furious scrawl in the margin, *how could you let her take them, Odile, HOW* — and then, three folders later, this. The drafts. The after-fury. The part nobody warns you about, where anger gets tired and starts writing polite letters it never sends.

The first sheet was dated eleven months after the fire.

---

*Dear Odile,*

*I have started this letter nine times. Bram's wife tells me you still walk past the shop on Tuesdays. I have taken to going out the back on Tuesdays.*

*I am not writing to forgive you. I want that clear from the first line, in case you are reading this and hoping. I am writing because Lien's letters were the last of her voice I had, and you sold my sister's voice, and I need you to know that I know exactly what you took, down to the envelope weight.*

*That is all this letter is. An inventory. You taught me that word, actually — inventaris — when we were girls and we counted your grandmother's spoons. I have made an inventory of what you took and I find the list is longer than paper.*

*M.*

---

My hands were not steady. I blamed the cold.

Because there it was — *inventaris* — sitting in my mother's handwriting like a stone I'd been stepping over my whole life without looking down. My mother had written in Marols. Not much, not fluently; here and there a word surfacing in her Dutch like a fish rising in a canal. And every time it happened, as a child, I'd asked where she'd learned it, and every time she'd said *around, you pick things up,* in the flat voice she used for questions she intended to survive rather than answer.

She'd picked it up from Odile Verstraeten. From counting spoons in a kitchen in Anderlecht before I was born.

I turned the page.

---

*Dear Odile,*

*Bram says you were seen at the lot where the house was. Standing there. I don't know if that's true or if Bram is a gossip with a heart, which he is. If it's true, then we stood in the same ashes at different hours like two women taking shifts at a grave neither will admit to visiting.*

*I have thought about what I would say if you knocked. I rehearse it while washing up. In the rehearsal you apologize and I am gracious, which shows you how fictional the exercise is, since I have never once been gracious in my life.*

*The rehearsal always ends the same way. You say his name — the collector's name — and explain the debts, and I say the debts were yours but the letters were ours, and then one of us leaves. In the rehearsals it's usually you. In reality I suspect it would be me, slamming the door, because I slam doors. You always said I slammed doors like a woman settling an argument with architecture.*

*You said that in Marols. There is no way to say it in proper Dutch that keeps the joke. This is the unforgivable thing, do you understand? Not the money. That you took the only language where my sister was funny.*

*M.*

---

I set the page down on the table very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

*Two women taking shifts at a grave neither will admit to visiting.*

I was thirty-one years old and I had been standing at that same grave for years — the empty lot on the Rue Wayez where my mother's childhood house had burned and been demolished and been replaced by nothing, by a fence, by weeds with municipal ambitions. Every Sunday night for years I wrote a postcard to that address. *Dear Mama, the shop did well this week. Dear Mama, a man came in wanting a love letter to a woman in Ghent who has already left him, and I wrote it anyway, because hope is not my department but postage is. Dear Mama, it rained. You'd have hated it. You'd have loved complaining about it.*

Thirty postcards a year, give or take. Addressed to a demolished house. Never mailed, because there was nothing to mail them *to* — no door, no slot, not even a ruin, just a postal code hovering over bare ground like a ghost with paperwork.

I had thought of it as my private strangeness. My little madness, kept behind the counter of Letter & Ash next to the ribbon tins. Something broken in me specifically, some flaw in my particular grief.

And now here was my mother, twenty-nine years dead of everything except what mattered, doing the identical thing. Writing to Odile Verstraeten across a silence wide enough to drive a hearse through. Draft after draft. Nine beginnings. Rehearsals while washing up. A woman standing in her own kitchen composing conversations with someone who wasn't answering, and filing them in a drawer instead of sending them, because sending them risked an answer, and an answer risked an ending, and an ending —

An ending meant the friendship was truly over. As long as the letter was unwritten, or half-written, or written and unsent, it was still *ongoing*. Still possible. The unsent letter is not a stopped clock. It's a clock you've covered with a cloth so you don't have to watch it.

I laughed. It came out wrong in the empty reading room, too loud, too close to the other thing. Somewhere a radiator ticked like it agreed with me.

Because look at the mechanics of it. Look at the family resemblance. My mother wrote unsent letters to a living woman across town. I wrote unsent postcards to a dead address across the canal. Same gesture. Same cowardice dressed as devotion. Same cloth over the clock. I had inherited it the way you inherit a nose, or a way of holding a cup — invisibly, completely, without anyone handing it over.

*Two generations writing to people who stopped answering.*

Except — and this was the part that made me sit forward until the chair complained — except my mother hadn't stopped answering. She'd stopped *sending*. There was a difference, and the difference had a drawer full of drafts in it.

I turned to the third sheet. It was undated, and the handwriting had changed — looser, older, the pen pressed harder, the way she wrote in the last years, when her hands had opinions of their own.

---

*Odile,*

*The doctor says things now in the tone he uses for weather. I have decided to ignore him, which is my right, and also my entire medical strategy.*

*I saw a girl today outside the bakery who laughed exactly the way you laugh — that awful honking sound you made when something was truly funny, like a goose being told good news. I nearly spoke to her. Imagine. Accosting strangers for their laughs.*

*Lien's birthday is Thursday. I am telling you because there is no one else alive who knows the date matters. This is the practical problem with outliving a quarrel: your witnesses die off, and then the quarrel is just you, alone, being right in an empty room.*

*I have decided I was not entirely right. I am not saying what you did was small. It was enormous. But I have begun to suspect that enormous wounds and enormous friendships come from the same place, and that I would rather have had the wound than never have had the friend. This is a new thought. It is uncomfortable, like new shoes. I am walking around in it anyway.*

*If you knock, I will make coffee. Bad coffee. You'll complain. Some things hold.*

*M.*

---

No date. No indication whether she ever got as far as the envelope. Knowing my mother — knowing the door-slamming, the argument-settled-with-architecture — I doubted it. The draft lived in a drawer in a house that burned down in a fire I was four years old for, and whatever happened between these two women afterward went with it, into the smoke, into the ground, into the silence my mother maintained so thoroughly that her own daughter grew up thinking Odile Verstraeten was nobody at all.

But the last line. *Some things hold.*

I read the whole folder again, start to finish, slower. And what I saw this time, what I couldn't unsee, was the shape of it. The arc. Fury first — the inventory, the accounting, the wounded arithmetic. Then the middle drafts, where the fury ran out of fuel and what was underneath it started showing through: the jokes remembered, the shared words, the Tuesday walks past the shop. Then this last one — the goose-laugh stranger, the birthday, the bad coffee offered in advance. A woman talking herself toward forgiveness one draft at a time, in the only language available to her, which was the language of letters never sent.

She was doing what I do. Except she'd gotten further than I ever have. My postcards never moved an inch in all their years — they circled the same small pond of shop news and weather. My mother's drafts traveled. They argued with themselves. They changed their minds on paper, which is the only place some minds will change.

And she'd done it without ever pressing send. Without ever giving Odile the chance to say yes or no. Which meant that somewhere in Anderlecht, presumably, Odile Verstraeten had spent thirty years not knowing any of this existed. Believing — if she believed anything — that Margriet Hartwell died still counting the inventory against her.

While all along, in a drawer, my mother had been setting out bad coffee in advance.

*Oh, Mama,* I thought, with a tenderness that felt suspiciously like anger. *You absolute door-slammer. You couldn't just knock? You had to rehearse knocking for thirty years?*

But that was the inheritance, wasn't it. That was the whole estate, willed to me intact: we Hartwells — or whatever I was, a Hartwell by blood and something else by habit — we would rather write to the ashes than risk the living person saying no. We addressed our hearts to demolished houses because demolished houses can't refuse delivery.

I looked down at my bag, where my current postcard lay — Sunday's edition, written last night at the kitchen table. *Dear Mama, I've been learning Marols from a terrible old tyrant named Odile Verstraeten, who reminds me of no one, absolutely no one, and who called my accent "a crime with vowels." More soon. Your Zephyrine.*

I'd written that. I'd written *reminds me of no one* to a woman whose best friend I was secretly apprenticed to, in a language I was learning in secret, in a flat I visited three afternoons a week under a truce that forbade the only question that mattered. I had inherited the cloth-over-the-clock and I was using it daily, professionally, with flair.

Well. Enough.

Not enough to fix anything — I wasn't naive, and the reading room was getting properly cold now, my breath faintly visible, the archivist's six o'clock long past. But enough to name the thing. Naming it felt like finding a word in Marols that Dutch doesn't have: once you have it, you start seeing the thing everywhere, and you can't unknow it.

The inheritance wasn't the grief. The grief was mine, earned fair. The inheritance was the *method*. The unsent letter, passed hand to hand like a recipe. And methods can be refused. Methods can be — what was the word Odile barked at me in my second lesson, when I apologized for a mistake?

*Verbeteren.* Corrected.

I gathered the folder, made my copies on the archivist's machine with the reverence of a forger, and left the sheets exactly as I'd found them, squared to the box's edge. Outside, Antwerp was doing its evening impression of a painting — gulls, lamplight, a tram sighing around the corner full of tired people going home to their own drawers full of unsent things.

I walked to the station composing, for the first time in years, a postcard I actually intended to deliver by hand. Not to the lot on the Rue Wayez. To a third-floor flat above a shuttered tailor's, where a lamp burned late these days, and where an old woman was losing a word a day and didn't know that somewhere in an archive, proof existed that my mother had been walking toward her for thirty years, one draft at a time.

Tomorrow was a lesson day. I would bring the pastries, yes. And underneath the pastries, folded small, I would bring the last draft — *if you knock, I will make coffee* — and put it on the table between us, and finally ask the forbidden question with evidence in my hands.

I had the tram timetable in my pocket. What I did not yet know — what wouldn't reach me until the following afternoon, when I climbed the stairs to the third floor and found the door unlocked and the flat empty and the word cards fluttering on their tape like startled birds — was that Odile Verstraeten had gone out into the fog without her coat, without her keys, and without, for the first time in eighty years, her memory of the way home.
