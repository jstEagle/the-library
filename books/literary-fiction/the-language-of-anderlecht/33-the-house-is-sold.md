## 33. The House Is Sold

The morning Odile signed the house away, she woke before the birds and made coffee for three.

Zephyrine knew something was different before she was fully awake herself — she had been sleeping in the guest room since the fog episode, since the morning Bram had called her at seven with his voice stripped of all its shopkeeper's cheer and said only, *She's at the lot. Come now.* Since then the flat had acquired the particular hush of a place being watched over: word cards on every surface, a bell on the front door that Odile had not objected to, Zephyrine's shoes positioned by the bed each night pointing toward the exit like small compass needles.

But that morning there was coffee, and there was Odile at the stove in her good blouse — the green one with the collar that Zephyrine had only ever seen her wear to the doctor's — and there was a third cup set out on the table, empty, facing the window.

"Sit," Odile said. "It's getting bitter."

"Who's the third cup for?"

"Margriet took hers black. You take yours like syrup with a little coffee in it. Sit."

Zephyrine sat. She did not say, *You haven't called her Margriet in three weeks.* She did not say, *Yesterday you asked me twice whether I was the postwoman's daughter.* She drank her syrup-with-a-little-coffee-in-it and watched the light come up over the rooftops of Anderlecht, and waited, the way you learn to wait with Odile — as if the morning were a tide and all you had to do was not drown in it.

"I've decided about the house," Odile said.

Zephyrine set down her cup very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

"The developer," Odile continued, "the one with the teeth — Verhaegen, his name is, like a brand of tractor — he came back yesterday. Bram let him in, which I'll be having words with Bram about, but never mind. He stood in my kitchen with his folder and his little pen that clicks, and he said, *Madame Verstraeten, the offer expires Friday,* as though the house were a carton of milk." She snorted. "I told him the house has stood since 1887 and would outlast his offer, his firm, and possibly his species."

"And then?"

"And then he clicked his pen and left, and I sat here all evening, and I thought about the walls." Odile turned her cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, a gesture Zephyrine had seen her make a hundred times without ever asking what it meant. "A house isn't the rooms, Zef. Any fool can build rooms. A house is what the walls have heard. And these walls have heard everything I have."

"Odile—"

"I'm selling," she said, flat and clear, in the voice she used for dictating addresses, for correcting grammar, for the word *no* in any of its forms. "Bram is right, the doctor is right, you are right, and I hate all three of you equally for it. I can't manage the stairs. I put the kettle on the wrong ring last week and stood there watching it melt because I couldn't remember what was wrong with the picture. I am not going to burn down like—" She stopped. The sentence went where sentences went with Odile now, into a pocket, and she picked up a different one as if nothing had happened. "The flat Bram found me has a lift. A lift, Zef. I've lived seventy-nine years without one and I intend to spend what's left riding up and down in it purely out of spite."

Zephyrine laughed, and it came out wet, and she pretended it hadn't.

"But," Odile said, and held up one finger, and the finger was a wall, a border, a clause written in iron, "there is a condition. One condition, and if the tractor-man doesn't accept it, he can build his flats around the house and be damned."

"The hearth wall," Zephyrine said.

Odile looked at her for a long moment — and it was one of the lucid looks, the ones that arrived like weather, unforecastable and total, in which the whole of Odile Verstraeten stood present behind her eyes, every year of her, the girl and the woman and the fading woman, all at once.

"The hearth wall," she agreed quietly.

* * *

The signing happened in Odile's front room on a Thursday, because Odile refused to sign on a Wednesday — "nothing good has ever come of a Wednesday, look at the word, it doesn't even know what it wants to be" — and because she refused to sign at the notary's office, since "I will not conduct the funeral of my own house in a waiting room with plastic chairs."

So the notary came to her: a young man named De Backer with kind eyes and a briefcase that he kept touching as though checking on a sleeping child. Bram came too, in his good coat, smelling faintly of the café's fryer, standing by the window with his hands folded like a man at a christening. The developer, Verhaegen, arrived ten minutes late in a coat that cost more than the flat Odile was moving to, and Zephyrine watched Odile take his measure across the room and decide, visibly, to be merciful.

The papers were spread on the table where Zephyrine had first translated her postcards, where the word *vergeven* had been written and crossed out and written again. Odile read every page. All of them. De Backer, to his credit, did not rush her, though his pen-clicking counterpart across the table began to develop a small twitch around minute forty.

"Page nine," Odile said at last, tapping the clause. "Read it aloud. I want it in the room."

Verhaegen cleared his throat. "*The purchaser shall preserve, in situ and in perpetuity, the brickwork of the original hearth wall of the ground-floor residence, including all inscriptions presently contained therein, and shall incorporate said wall into any future construction such that it remains visible and accessible—*'"

"Faster," Odile said. "You're savoring it like it's your idea."

"*—accessible to the public,*" Verhaegen finished, and clicked his pen, and looked up with an expression that Zephyrine, watching closely, identified as a man discovering that a signature could cost him something and finding, to his own surprise, that he didn't entirely mind. He was not a villain. He was a developer with a mother, Zephyrine had learned — a mother in a care home in Vilvoorde who, he'd mentioned once, embarrassed, still sang to him in a dialect he couldn't understand. Zephyrine had thought of that conversation more than once since.

"The wall stays," Odile said. "The names stay. Whatever else you build, you build around it, and you put a light on it, because a hearth without a light is just a hole in a house." She picked up the pen De Backer offered her. "My mother hung washing on that wall. My father burned his hand on it and swore in three languages. And when I was nine years old, a girl moved in across the row who scratched her name into the soft brick by the flue because she said a name should live where the fire lives, and I scratched mine under it, and we were never, either of us, alone again."

The room was very still. Bram had turned his face to the window. De Backer had stopped touching his briefcase.

Odile signed. Her hand, which in recent months had developed a tremor that made her furious, went steady at the crucial moment — Zephyrine watched it happen, watched the pen move through the long Flemish chain of her name, Verstraeten with all its letters accounted for — and when she finished she set the pen down with the small definitive click of a lamp being switched off.

"There," she said. "Sold. Now someone make tea before I change my mind, and Verhaegen, if I ever find a plasterboard sheet over my wall, I will haunt you in Marols, and you won't understand a word of it, which will make it worse."

* * *

The clearing of the house took a week, and Zephyrine stayed for all of it.

They worked in the mornings, while Odile was at her steadiest, packing the flat's life into boxes that Bram labeled in his enormous carpenter's handwriting. It should have been unbearable and often was, but there was an unexpected gentleness in it too, because Odile treated the whole enterprise as a lesson — everything had to be named as it was wrapped.

"Marols for *cupboard*?" she demanded, holding up a teacup.

"*kast*," Zephyrine said. "But the small one, the *kasteken*."

"And the cloth?"

"*doek.*"

"*Doek.* Again."

"*Doek.*"

"Your *oe* is getting fat. Say it thin. A *doek* is a thin thing; you're pronouncing it like a mattress."

"You're moving out of your house of fifty years and you're correcting my vowels."

"I am moving out of my house of fifty years," Odile said, wrapping a teacup in newspaper, "which is exactly why your vowels will be correct, because when I am gone, someone in this city has to say these words properly, and it is evidently going to be you, God help us both."

They found things. Of course they found things — a house that old is an archaeology of itself. A ration card from the war behind a loose skirting board. A child's shoe, single, inexplicable, in the chimney flue, which Odile held up and examined and pronounced, with great satisfaction, "Lien's. She lost it in 1948 and swore the house ate it. Margriet never let her forget it." Zephyrine took the shoe and held it a moment too long, and Odile watched her hold it, and said nothing, which was its own kind of grammar.

On the fourth morning they reached the hearth wall.

It was smaller than Zephyrine had imagined, though she couldn't have said what she'd imagined — something grander, perhaps, something to match the weight it carried in Odile's stories. It was just a stretch of brick beside a bricked-up flue, smoke-darkened at the top, the mortar gone soft and crumbling in places, the kind of wall a developer's survey would call *character feature, retain if practicable.* But Odile had made Verhaegen write *in perpetuity* into the contract, and Zephyrine understood now that she had not been being dramatic. She had been being precise.

The names were low on the wall, at the height of a nine-year-old's reach. Scratched into the soft old brick with whatever a girl of nine could find — a nail, a hairpin, a key. *MARGRIET*, and beneath it, in a different, wobblier hand, *ODILE*. The letters were shallow, weathered, half-swallowed by decades of paint and repainting, but they were there, unmistakable, the way a heartbeat is unmistakable under a coat.

Zephyrine crouched down in front of them. She had known they were there — Odile had told her, had told her more than once, the story wearing smoother with each telling — but knowing and seeing were different countries, and she crossed the border between them now with her throat tight.

"They're small," she said.

"Everything true is small," Odile said, behind her. "It's the lies that come in large sizes."

Zephyrine ran her finger into the groove of the *M*. The brick was cold. Somewhere under her fingertip was 1947, was two girls and a fire and a whole future none of them could have written. She thought of her mother's hands, which had kneaded bread and signed school forms and, once, in Zephyrine's memory, gripped a doorframe so hard the wood dented — she had never known what that day was about, and now she knew, and the knowing sat in her chest like a stone in a shoe. Margriet Hartwell had scratched her name into this wall believing she would grow old in sight of it. Instead she had grown old three streets away and then thirty years away, and the wall had kept her name the whole time, patient as brick, asking nothing.

"Odile," Zephyrine said, still crouching, "come look at them with me."

There was a silence behind her, long enough that she began to turn — and then Odile was there, lowering herself with a grunt onto the crate Bram had thoughtfully positioned, her good green blouse catching the light from the window. She reached out and put her whole palm flat against the wall, over both names, the way you'd warm your hands at a fire.

"She had the deeper letters," Odile said. "I was jealous of that for years. She pressed harder. She did everything harder — laughed harder, sulked harder, forgave—" The word stopped. Odile's palm stayed on the brick. "She was better at forgiving than I was. That was the whole tragedy, if you want it in one sentence, and I don't, so make the tea."

But she didn't move her hand for a long time, and Zephyrine didn't move either, and the two of them crouched at the hearth like women at a graveside that wasn't a graveside, at a fire that had gone out and somehow not gone out, while the light moved slowly across the floor of the room where everything had happened.

* * *

It was Bram who found the third name.

They were on the last morning, the boxes gone, the flat a hollow of echo and dust-ghosts on the floorboards where furniture had stood. Odile had been taken ahead to the new place by a neighbor — a mercy, Zephyrine suspected, arranged between Bram and the neighbor without a word passing, the quiet logistics of people who love someone — and Zephyrine was doing the final sweep with a roll of packing paper under her arm when Bram called from the hearth.

"Zef. Come here. Bring the good light."

She came. He was crouched where she had crouched, but lower, his phone's torch angled along the brick at a raking slant, and in that slanting light, where the flat light of the previous days had shown nothing, there was more.

A third name. Smaller than the other two, squeezed beneath *ODILE* as if the writer had arrived late to the wall and taken what space remained. *LIEN.*

And beneath Lien's name, in letters even smaller — a child's careful, laborious letters, the letters of someone who had to think about every stroke — a phrase in Marols, scratched with something fine, a pin perhaps, the words half-lost in the grain of the brick but legible, legible, Zephyrine's heart going hard against her ribs as she read them:

*Wat tuss'n ons gebroke is, blift ons.*

"What does it say?" Bram asked. "I can hear the shape of it but it's older than my grandmother."

Zephyrine didn't answer at once. She was crouched in the cold light with her finger an inch from the brick, not touching, and she was hearing her own voice from weeks ago, from a lesson, from a morning when she had asked Odile how you say *it's ours* in Marols and Odile had said the dialect doesn't really say *mine* and *yours*, it says *ours* or it says nothing. She was hearing the dialect's whole architecture rearrange itself around this one scratched sentence, this oath, this thing three girls had sworn into a wall before any of them could know what they were swearing.

"It says," Zephyrine said, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect, "what is broken between us stays ours."

Bram exhaled slowly, the way men do in churches. "They were children."

"They were children," Zephyrine agreed. "And then they weren't, and they forgot." She took out her phone and photographed the wall — the names, the phrase, the raking light, all of it, from every angle, twice. "Or they didn't forget. Maybe they just couldn't read it anymore. The paint, the years. You'd need a fire's own light, low and sideways, to even see it was there."

"You'll tell her?"

"I don't know." Zephyrine stood. Her knees ached; she was thirty-four and her knees ached, and the wall in front of her had been standing since 1887 and would now stand, by contract, in perpetuity, holding three girls' names and one broken, unbroken sentence. "I've learned not to decide things for her. Even the merciful things. *Especially* the merciful things."

Bram grunted, which from Bram was a whole paragraph. He clicked off his torch, and the third name folded itself back into the brick, invisible again to anyone who didn't know to look — but known now, and photographed, and carried.

Zephyrine took one last look around the empty flat. The boxes were gone; the echo had moved in. On the kitchen table someone — Odile, the last morning, before the neighbor came — had left the third cup, the one for Margriet, washed and turned upside down to drain, in the exact center of the table, as if the house should know she'd been expected.

Zephyrine turned it right side up before she left.

That evening she sat in her shop, Letter & Ash, long after closing, with the lamp on and the photograph of the three young women propped against the till, and she wrote in her notebook the phrase from the wall, letter by letter, the way Odile had made her write everything — *write it thin, write it true* — and beneath it the translation, and beneath the translation a question she had been circling for days without landing on:

*If the oath was always there, in the wall, in the language itself — then what exactly had they been waiting for?*

Her phone buzzed. Bram: *She's settled. Asked twice if the wall was signed for. I said in perpetuity. She said good, and then she taught the care nurse how to say good in Marols. Sleep, Zef. Tomorrow's a Wednesday, nothing good will come of it.*

Zephyrine smiled, and put the phone down, and looked at the notebook, and at the photograph, and at the phrase.

*Wat tuss'n ons gebroke is, blift ons.*

What is broken between us stays ours. Not *was* broken. Not *stays broken*. Stays *ours* — present tense, shared possession, the dialect refusing, even in its grammar, to let a wound belong to only one person.

Tomorrow, she decided. Tomorrow she would tell Odile about the third name. She would bring the photographs, the raking light, the whole small true thing.

She had no idea yet what Odile would do with it.
