## 39. Postcards, Mailed

The box had lived under the counter of Letter & Ash for so long that I'd stopped seeing it. It was a shoebox once — some long-dead pair of brogues, the cardboard gone soft at the corners, the lid held shut with a rubber band that had perished into a brittle brown ghost of itself. Inside were postcards. Hundreds of them. Every one addressed in my own hand to a house that no longer existed.

*Margriet Hartwell, Rue Brogniez 47, Anderlecht.*

I had written the first one three days after the funeral, standing at the counter after closing, because I did not know what else to do with my hands. The house had been demolished years before she died — she had outlived it by two decades, the way you can outlive a limb and still feel it itch — but I wrote to it anyway. *Dear Mama. It rained today. A man came in wanting a love letter to a woman in Ghent who has already left him twice.* And so on. For thirty years. Rain, customers, the price of stamps, the smell of the canal in August. Nothing that mattered. Everything that mattered.

I never mailed a single one. There was no address to mail them to. That was rather the point, though I hadn't understood the point for most of those years. You write to a demolished house because the alternative is writing to a person who can no longer answer, and a demolished house at least has the decency not to pretend it might.

Now I sat on the floor of my own shop with the box open in my lap, and Odile's voice was in my head saying, in her drill-sergeant Marols, *Zeg het eens goed — say it properly — grief is a verb, girl, it wants an object.*

She had said no such thing, of course. Odile did not dispense wisdom; she dispensed corrections. But somewhere in the last year the corrections had begun to sound like wisdom, the way a stone worn smooth by a river begins to look like sculpture.

The care home was in Jette. Two tram lines and a change, forty minutes door to door, which is why I had visited every day for the first month and then negotiated myself down to three times a week with the ruthless efficiency of a woman renegotiating a treaty she'd signed in a moment of weakness. Odile had settled in the way a cat settles into a new house: by pretending she had always owned it. Within a week she had reorganized the communal bookshelf by height, which she claimed was alphabetical, and appointed herself unofficial censor of the television volume.

Her memory was mostly gone now. The doctors had been precise about it in the way doctors are, with their arithmetic of decline, and the arithmetic had proven generous in one direction and cruel in the other. She forgot Bram's name. She forgot the name of the prime minister, which Bram argued was no loss. She forgot, on a Tuesday in March, that her mother had been dead for fifty years, and grieved her all over again, fresh, and I sat with her while she wept for a woman I could not comfort her about because I had never met her.

But the language held. That was the miracle nobody at the home quite knew what to do with. When the fog took everything else, Marols stayed — the way a hearth stays warm after the fire has gone down. She spoke it to the nurses, who smiled and understood nothing. She spoke it to the window. She spoke it, sometimes, to me, correcting my grammar mid-sentence with a fierceness that made the other residents look up from their jigsaws, and when I got it right she would nod once, gravely, like a queen conferring a small honor, and my whole week would be made.

So I had started writing the postcards again. New ones. One a week since January.

They were not addressed to Rue Brogniez 47. They were addressed to *Odile Verstraeten, Kamer 12, Residence De Lichtung, Jette.* And they were written in Marols, badly at first, then less badly, then — I will permit myself this — rather well. Because here is the thing I discovered, sitting on the floor with thirty years of unsaid things in a dead shoebox: a language does not want to be studied. A language wants to be used. It wants errands. It wants gossip. It wants to be asked to carry the groceries.

And I had three decades of groceries.

* * *

It took me four evenings to sort the box.

I spread them across the shop floor in rows, knee-deep in my own handwriting, and divided them the way you divide anything inherited: by feel. There were the early ones, stiff with formality, addressing my mother as though she might be taking notes. There were the middle years, when the postcards turned into arguments — I could see us fighting across the decades, me accusing her of silence, her silence answering. There were the last ones before she died, short and gentle and full of questions I still had not asked, and those I set aside in a separate pile because they hurt to touch even now, the way you don't press on a bruise just to check.

Bram came in halfway through the second evening, ostensibly to buy a fountain pen he did not need — his fourth this year — and stood over the wreckage of my floor with his hands in his pockets.

"You're finally sending them," he said.

"I'm deciding whether to send them."

"Zephyrine." He picked up one at random, read it upside down without shame — a shopkeeper's privilege, reading other people's mail — and put it back gently. "You have spent thirty years writing letters to a hole in the ground. There is a living woman in Jette who lights up — *lights up*, I've seen it — when the post comes, because for six months now something has arrived for her that isn't a bill or a solicitor's reminder. Send them."

"Some of them are angry," I said. "Some of them are — private. Some of them aren't even about her. They're about Mama."

"So translate them first," Bram said, shrugging. "That's your whole trade, isn't it? Saying the unsayable in a form people can receive." He tapped the box lid with his shoe. "Besides. You told me yourself what Odile says about waiting. In her language there's no future tense for missing someone. Only constructions of waiting." He looked at the rows of cards, all that patience stacked in piles. "Well. Here's thirty years of waiting, girl. Waiting wants a destination."

He bought the pen. He did not need it. I gave him a discount anyway, for wisdom rendered, and he pretended not to notice, which is how friendship works between Belgians of a certain age.

* * *

Translating the postcards taught me things about my mother I had not expected to learn from my own hand.

Because here is what happens when you take thirty years of French-inflected Brussels Dutch and force it through the narrow gate of Marols: you cannot hide. The dialect has no room for evasion. It is a language built in small houses by people who lived close together, and it assumes — it *requires* — that you will say the true thing, because everyone will know the false thing within the hour anyway. My polite postcard sentences collapsed under translation. *I hope you are well* became something closer to *the lamp is lit for you, come home.* *I miss you* became the construction Odile had taught me in my second month, the one that has no future tense at all, only a standing present: *I am in the waiting of you.* Ik ben in 't wachten van u. As though missing were a place you could live, and my mother had simply moved out of it without leaving a forwarding address.

I found, in the middle pile, a card from 1998 that I had entirely forgotten writing. *Dear Mama. Today a woman came into the shop speaking a language I didn't recognize, and I thought of you, and I hated you a little, and then I made her tea.* I stared at it for a long time. I had no memory of the woman. I had complete memory of the hating-a-little. That was the year the silence between us had been longest, and I had carried it around like a coat too heavy to take off and too expensive to throw away.

I translated it into Marols. The hatred translated strangely — softened, almost, the way a shout sounds different through a wall. In Marols, *I hated you a little* came out sounding like *you were in my thoughts against my will,* which is perhaps the more honest sentence. Perhaps the more loving one.

I set it aside. Not every card needed mailing. Some needed only to be read once more, by the person they'd been meant for all along, which was me.

* * *

The first card went out on a Monday in April.

I wrote it at the counter after closing, on one of the proper picture-postcards I sold tourists — the Grand Place at dusk, gilded and absurd — because if I was going to do this mad thing I was going to do it beautifully. In careful Marols, checking my grammar against the notebook where I kept Odile's corrections like scripture:

*Beste Odile. Vandaag heb ik uw kastenkaarten opgehangen in de winkel. Het woord voor haard staat boven de schoorsteenmantel, waar het thuishoort. De klanten vragen wat het betekent. Ik zeg hun: dat is de taal van hier, van iemand die er nog woont. Tot de lamp voor u ontstoken is. — Zephyrine*

*Dear Odile. Today I hung your word-cards in the shop. The word for hearth is above the mantelpiece, where it belongs. Customers ask what it means. I tell them: that is the language of here, of someone who still lives in it. Until the lamp is lit for you. — Zephyrine*

I mailed it Tuesday morning, and then spent Wednesday in a state of low-grade panic that I have otherwise only experienced awaiting medical results. What if the grammar was wrong? What if she couldn't read anymore? What if the nurses opened it, or lost it, or — worst of all — what if it arrived and meant nothing to her, one more piece of paper in the fog?

Thursday, Bram called the home — he had charmed the receptionist months ago, in the way Bram charms everyone, slowly and with pastries — and reported back.

"She read it aloud," he said. "Twice. To the nurse, and then to the man at the next table, who is deaf, but that didn't stop her. Then she corrected your word order."

"My word order was fine."

"Your word order was fine," Bram agreed solemnly, "and she corrected it anyway. Zephyrine, the woman hasn't corrected anyone's grammar since February. The staff were nearly in tears. They're asking if you'll make it a regular thing."

"One a week," I said. "Every week. For as long as—"

I stopped. Bram waited, kindly, for me to find the end of the sentence, and when I didn't, he supplied it himself, gently:

"For as long as the lamp needs lighting. Yes. We know."

* * *

So it became practice, in both senses of the word: the thing I did repeatedly, and the thing I was getting better at.

Every Sunday evening I sat at the counter with a postcard and the week's raw material — what the shop had seen, what the canal smelled like, which customer had cried over which letter — and I rendered it into Marols. The dialect reshaped everything it touched. Gossip became chronicle. Complaint became proverb. Once I wrote about a pigeon that had gotten into the shop and refused to leave, and by the time the sentence had passed through Marols it had become a small fable about stubbornness and home, and I posted it wondering whose fable it really was. Odile's, probably. Everything stubborn and homeward was Odile's.

And she answered. Not every week — the fog took the pen from her more often than any of us liked — but often enough. Her replies came on whatever paper the home provided: the back of an activity schedule, a page from a large-print puzzle book, once, gloriously, the margin of a menu. Her handwriting swung between the elegant schoolroom script of her youth and a wilder scrawl that wandered off the edge of the page like a sentence losing its nerve. The grammar was always perfect. Even on the bad days, when she called me Margriet and asked why I'd stayed away so long, the grammar was perfect. The language was the last room of the house still standing, and she kept it swept.

One reply, in May, contained a single line in the middle of weather-talk: *Uw moeder schreef ook zo. Ze dacht dat niemand het merkte.* *Your mother wrote like this too. She thought no one noticed.*

I sat with that for a long time. Then I put it in the drawer with the photograph, and the oath-rubbing from the hearth wall, and the tape recordings, the whole growing archive of a family I had assembled out of fragments — except that it wasn't a family of the dead anymore, was it. Half of it was alive and eating lunch in Jette, complaining about the soup.

* * *

In June I mailed the hard ones.

I had been saving them, the way you save the last of anything. The angry cards from the middle years. The grieving ones from the end. I took them to the home in person, a bundle tied with string, because some things should not travel alone in the post.

Odile was by the window in the day room, doing what she did most afternoons now: watching the courtyard with the expression of a woman supervising workmen she had not hired. When she saw the bundle she held out her hands for it immediately, imperiously, the old command returning to her fingers before her face.

"What is it?"

"Letters," I said. "Old ones. They took a long time finding their address."

She untied the string with great ceremony. She read. The day room went on around us — the television, the jigsaw man, a nurse's laugh down the corridor — and Odile read thirty years of my unsaid things, in my careful Marols, her lips moving.

At the angry ones she snorted. At the grieving ones her mouth went firm, the way it did when she was holding something steady by main force. And at one card — the one from 1998, the one about hating my mother a little — she stopped entirely, and looked up at me, and for a moment the fog cleared the way it sometimes did, a whole clean hour of her, the woman from the photographs, the woman who had scratched names in brick.

"She never showed me these," Odile said.

"They weren't hers. They were mine."

"To whom?" she asked — and I saw her understand it a half-second before she finished asking, saw the question turn over in her face like a card being played.

"To you," I said. "Eventually. It took me a while to find the address."

Odile looked down at the postcards in her lap, all that waiting finally delivered, and she did something I had seen her do exactly once before, at the hearth wall, with her hand flat against the brick. She pressed her palm down on them, the whole stack, as if testing whether they were real, whether they would hold weight.

"Well," she said at last, in Marols, gruff as gravel. "Then the waiting is over, and it's about time. Your grammar has improved." A pause. The fierce eyes, wet and unashamed. "*Ik ben in 't wachten van u geweest. Nu niet meer.*"

*I have been in the waiting of you. No longer.*

We drank the terrible institutional tea, and she corrected my pronunciation of the word for Thursday, and outside the window the courtyard went gold with evening, and I understood that this — this counter-ritual, this weekly arrival, this language traveling between two living women — was what all those years of writing to a burnt address had been training me for. Grief, it turns out, is not a thing you finish. It is a thing you convert. Into practice. Into postage. Into a lamp lit every Sunday evening at a counter in Brussels, for a woman in Jette who reads by it.

* * *

I mailed the last of the old cards — the final one, the one from the funeral week, *Dear Mama, I don't know where to send this* — on the last Friday of June. I had rewritten it, of course. The address was new. The addressee was new. But the last line I kept exactly as I had first written it, thirty years ago, because it had turned out, all along, to be true:

*I will keep writing until someone answers.*

Someone had.

I was walking back from the letterbox, lighter than I had felt in years, when my phone rang. Bram. He never called in the morning; morning was for the shop, and for gossip conducted in person, where it belonged.

"Zephyrine." His voice was strange — careful, the voice he used for delivering hard things wrapped in soft things. "The care home just phoned me. Odile's asking for you. She's—" a breath, the sound of a man choosing words the way you choose steps on ice. "She's having one of her clear mornings. A long one, the staff say. And she keeps saying one thing, over and over, in Marols. They wrote it down for me, but my Marols is yours, so you'd better come."

"Bram. What does she keep saying?"

There was a pause, and then he read it out, slowly, the way you read something sacred or alarming, and possibly both:

"*Haal de recorder. Ik heb haar iets te zeggen, en ik wil het zeggen terwijl ik het nog kan.*"

*Bring the recorder. I have something to tell her, and I want to say it while I still can.*

I was already running for the tram.
