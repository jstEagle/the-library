## 15. The Word for Betrayer Has No Soft Form

The sentence sat on the kitchen table of the empty house like something that had crawled there to die.

Gideon had written it out three times that evening—once in the Flats tongue, once in a literal English rendering so stiff it read like a police report, and once in what they privately called the *conference version*, the one with all the edges sanded off. The conference version was the one they'd been trained by thirty years of contracts to produce. *Vera Crane provided information to authorities regarding the residency status of a neighbor, for which she received compensation.* It was accurate. It was also a lie wearing accuracy's clothes.

They crossed it out.

The lamp hummed. The house ticked around them, cooling, settling, the sounds of a body relaxing after a long illness. Outside, Milton Flats went dark in stages—the corner store's sign first, then Marisol's porch light, then the streetlamp at the end of Delancey that had flickered since Gideon was nine and still flickered now, stubborn as everything else here.

The problem was not the confession. The problem was the grammar.

Gideon had spent the afternoon doing what translators do when a text resists them: they had gone back to the structure of the language itself, hunting for a way through. And what they found—or failed to find—was the thing Mrs. Adeyemi must have known when she recited the sentence over tea, watching Gideon's face with those patient, terrible eyes.

The Flats dialect had no soft form for betrayal.

It wasn't that the word didn't exist. The word existed—*shkava*—and Gideon had known it since childhood, had heard it spat across market stalls and hissed between sisters and shouted once, memorably, from a second-floor window down at a husband who had sold a family's boat out from under them. *Shkava.* It came from the old country word for a knife turned in the hand, the blade rotated so it faced your own people. Every child in the Flats learned it the way children learn *hot*: by touching it.

But English had given Gideon a whole wardrobe of gentler garments. *Betrayal* could be dressed as *a lapse in loyalty*. As *failing someone*. As *doing what you had to do*. As *complicity*, which sounded almost bureaucratic, almost forgivable, a sin of paperwork. There were novels—Gideon had translated three of them—in which betrayal was rendered as tragedy, as circumstance, as the sad arithmetic of survival. English would let Vera off the hook in eleven different ways before lunch.

The Flats tongue offered none of that. In the dialect, there was *shkava* and there was nothing adjacent to it. No word for *betrayal with mitigating circumstances*. No word for *the lesser treachery*. When Gideon tried to construct a softened phrase—*she did a shkava-thing but under pressure*—the language simply refused to carry it. The construction collapsed. It was like trying to whisper in a drum.

Because the Flats tongue had been built by people who could not afford ambiguity. That was the realization that arrived somewhere past midnight, sitting in Vera's kitchen with cold tea going bitter in the cup. Four immigrant tongues had braided themselves together in six square blocks, spoken by people whose papers were thin, whose leases were oral, whose whole existence depended on knowing exactly who stood where. In a neighborhood like that, language couldn't waste itself on euphemism. Euphemism was a luxury of the secure. The Flats said what things were because misnaming them could get you deported, evicted, drowned.

And so the dialect had kept one brutal, honest word, and Gideon—who had made a career of finding the graceful equivalent, the phrase that preserved meaning while sparing feeling—was going to have to stand up at an auction full of strangers and say it.

Say it about their grandmother.

* * *

They began practicing at two in the morning, because sleep had become a country they no longer held a visa for.

The living room was stripped now—curtains gone, rug rolled, the furniture that remained sheeted in white like a room full of ghosts waiting politely to be introduced. Gideon stood in the middle of it, facing no one, and spoke the sentence aloud.

Their voice cracked on the third word.

Not from emotion—from mechanics. Thirty years of disuse had left scar tissue in the throat, in the palate, in the specific musculature of the Flats vowels, which lived further back in the mouth than anything Boston had ever asked of them. The sentence came out wrong, flattened, anglicized, a tourist's version of a native's grief. Gideon heard it and flinched. If they said it like that in front of the community, it would be worse than silence. It would be mockery wearing memory.

So they did what they'd done at nine years old, standing at Vera's elbow with a pencil stub: they slowed down and started again.

"*Shkava,*" they said, alone, just the word. Letting it sit in the room. The knife turning in the hand.

The house took it without comment. Somewhere in the walls, a pipe knocked twice, or didn't.

Again. Slower. They paid attention to the initial consonant, that swallowed *sh* that Vera had drilled into them—"from the back of the teeth, little crane, not the lips, the sound comes from where the truth comes from"—and this time it landed closer. The word filled the sheeted room. Gideon felt it move through their chest like a stone dropped into a well, counting seconds for the splash.

Then the full sentence. Vera's sentence. The one Mrs. Adeyemi had carried for forty years like a coal she'd agreed to hold.

*I informed on Adaeze Adeyemi's brother so they would not take my house, and I took their money, and every word I taught you was bought with it.*

That was the shape of it. Not the conference version. Not the legal version. The whole, unsoftened, grammatically merciless thing—and the last clause was the part that broke Gideon open every time they reached it, because Vera had built the confession into the very act of transmission. Every lullaby, every borrowed word, every lesson at the sewing desk: purchased. Gideon's fluency itself was the proceeds of the crime. They were not the heir to the secret. They were the secret's spending.

They said it again. Their voice steadied and then failed in a different place. Again. The windows had gone from black to the deep blue-black of four a.m., and Gideon's throat was raw, and they kept going, because somewhere in the repetition the sentence stopped being a performance and started being a weight they were learning to lift correctly—with the legs, with the whole body, not wrenched up by the back.

At some point they stopped practicing the sentence and started practicing the word. Just *shkava*. Fifty times. A hundred. Saying it until it lost its horror and then, strangely, saying it past that point, until the horror returned but changed shape—until it was no longer the horror of the word but the horror of the thing, which was, Gideon understood dimly, the entire point of a language that refused euphemism. The Flats tongue had engineered its vocabulary so that you could not numb yourself to it. You could only say it and feel it, or not say it and carry it.

English, Gideon thought, would have let me practice until it meant nothing. My grandmother's language makes sure it always means everything.

* * *

Dawn came gray through the bare windows, and Gideon fell asleep at last in Vera's armchair, upright, the notebook open on their knees like a sleeping child.

And they dreamed in the old tongue.

It had been—Gideon counted, waking, disoriented—no, they didn't want to count. Years. Decades. The dreams of their adulthood had been conducted entirely in English, even the anxious ones, even the ones about the Flats; the dreaming mind had accepted the terms of the exile along with the waking one. But this dream was saturated, wall-to-wall, in the braided speech of the neighborhood. In the dream they were nine, and the market was loud, and Mrs. Okafor—young, laughing, her hair wrapped in blue—was calling across the stalls for someone to bring her scale weights, and the fruit seller answered in the Portuguese-inflected register, and someone else layered the Yoruba cadence over it, and young Gideon stood in the middle translating for a customer who had no face, translating flawlessly, effortlessly, the way water is wet, and Vera was beside them writing in the notebook, writing and writing, and the notebook's pages were the sky, and the sky's pages were torn—

Gideon woke with the word already in their mouth.

"*Shkava.*"

Said it once more, hoarse, into the gray morning light, and found that it fit now. Not comfortably. Nothing about it would ever be comfortable. But it fit the way a key fits a lock it was cut for—not pleasant, but true, and opening.

They understood then what the practice had actually been. Not rehearsal. Surrender. All night they had been searching for the graceful exit from the sentence—for some translation that would preserve the crowd's tenderness toward Vera, preserve Theo's fragile peace, preserve Gideon's own last clean image of the woman who had loved them—and the language had refused every exit, herding them back to the plain brutal center of it, until Gideon finally stopped looking and simply stood inside the sentence and let it be what it was.

Some sentences could not be carried in pieces. You carried them whole or you put them down forever.

Gideon put the kettle on. Through the window, Delancey Street woke up in its diminished way—a truck backing, a porch door, Marisol's curtains twitching with the unmistakable rhythm of a woman checking whether the Crane lights had burned all night. They had. Let her see.

Tomorrow was the auction. Tomorrow there would be a podium, a microphone, Whitlock's proxy with its confident paddle, Theo arriving with whatever he intended to do with that photocopied page, the whole scattered remnant of the Flats watching to see whether the translator would translate or deflect. Gideon's hands were steady. That surprised them most of all. Thirty years of the deflection smile, thirty years of counting past twenty in a dead language's shadow, and now, on the eve of the costliest sentence they would ever speak, their hands were quiet as closed books.

They rinsed the cup, set it upside down on the counter—Vera's habit, done without thinking, inheritance of a harmless kind—and gathered the notebook.

One more time, they told themselves. One more reading, aloud, top to bottom, before it belonged to everyone.

They opened to the first page, and Vera's handwriting waited for them like a doorway—

and Gideon found, with a small shock of recognition, that they knew the word for doorway after all.
