## 5. Theo Measures the Span

The crack on Pier 3 had grown by four millimeters since March.

Theo knew this the way other men knew their golf handicaps or their cholesterol numbers — with a proprietary intimacy that was almost tender. He knelt on the inspection platform in his orange vest and hard hat, calipers open like a heron's beak, and he measured the thing again because measuring it again was what he did. Four millimeters. Hairline to hairline. The concrete wept a little rust at its lower lip, staining the pier like an old tear track.

"Still growing?" asked Dev, his junior inspector, crouched beside him with the clipboard.

"Four millimeters since spring."

"That's bad, right?"

"It's not good." Theo closed the calipers with a soft click. "It's also not a surprise. This span's been telling us for ten years what it wants. Nobody listens to bridges until they're shouting."

Dev wrote *4 mm* on the clipboard as if writing it down were the same as doing something about it. Theo didn't blame him. Theo had spent thirty years learning that documentation was the prayer of engineers — you recorded the crack, you filed the report, you recommended monitoring, and somewhere in that liturgy you convinced yourself that noticing was a form of action.

He was good at noticing. It was the rest of the sentence where he tended to trail off.

* * *

That evening he came home to a house that smelled of lemon and something else — something warm and floury, like bread that had been thought about rather than merely baked. Mireille was in the kitchen, which was itself notable; she'd been moving through their rooms lately the way water moves through a pipe, present but without any particular interest in the journey.

She stood at the counter with her back to him, chopping parsley, and she was humming.

Theo hung his jacket on the hook — second hook from the left, always, because the first one's screw had loosened in 2019 and he'd never gotten around to tightening it — and stopped.

The humming wasn't a tune he could name. It wasn't anything from the radio. It had a shape he recognized the way you recognize a face across a crowded train platform: not the person, exactly, but the angle of them, the way they hold their head. Minor key, descending, then a small stubborn climb at the end like someone insisting on a point. It looped. She reached the end of it and went back to the beginning without pausing, the way you'd re-read a letter you couldn't quite believe.

His grandmother's kitchen had smelled like this. Flour and camphor and the ghost of woodsmoke even though the stove had been electric since before he was born. His grandmother had hummed exactly this way — not performing the song, but keeping it, the way you keep coins in a pocket, turning them over with your thumb.

"Hey," he said.

Mireille's humming stopped mid-phrase. Not faded — stopped, cut clean, like a wire snipped. She turned with the knife still in her hand and smiled the smile she'd been issuing lately, courteous and load-bearing.

"You're home early."

"Bridge let out early." He set his bag down. "What are you making?"

"Parsley thing. From the magazine." She gestured with the knife at a cookbook splayed open, spine cracked, holding itself hostage under the fruit bowl. "It's probably terrible."

"It smells good."

"It smells like I'm trying," she said, and laughed, and the laugh had a small crack in it too, four millimeters wide, and Theo heard it and did what he always did with cracks.

He noted it. Filed it. Recommended continued monitoring.

* * *

Over dinner — the parsley thing was fine, actually better than fine, which surprised them both into a brief genuine conversation about lemon zest — Theo tried to locate the humming in his memory the way he'd locate a fault line on a survey map.

His grandmother, Alba, had lived in the old neighborhood, the one the city had renamed twice and finally just paved over. Rue des Saules, then Willow Street, then a number. Her kitchen had been the center of gravity of his entire childhood: low ceiling, yellow light, a table that wobbled on a folded matchbook. She'd spoken English to him, careful and accented, and another language to herself — to the pots, to the radio when it played something she disapproved of, to God, presumably, who she addressed in the same tone she used for the pots.

When he was seven he'd asked her what language that was. She'd said, *"Our language,"* and ruffled his hair, and given him a plum, and that had been the end of the curriculum. Children accept these answers. Children are excellent at not asking follow-up questions; it's a skill they later lose and spend decades mourning.

Alba had died when Theo was nineteen. At the funeral there'd been relatives speaking the language fluently, rapid and musical, and Theo had understood nothing except once, when a great-aunt looked at him across the coffin and said a single word — soft, two syllables, rising — and whatever it meant, it made his chest hurt for a week. He'd never found out what it was. He'd never asked anyone. There had been, he realized now with the clarity of a man holding calipers up to his own life, approximately forty years available to him in which asking had been possible.

He hadn't asked. Asking was a kind of drilling, and drilling into a structure you suspected was compromised — well. You might find out the whole thing was held up by habit and paint.

"So," Mireille said, refilling his water glass, "how was the bridge?"

"Growing."

"The bridge?"

"The crack." He caught himself. "Four millimeters since March. Which doesn't sound like much—"

"But it is," she said, "when you're a crack."

They looked at each other. Something flickered there — humor, actual shared humor, the rare element — and for a moment the kitchen felt like a room two people lived in rather than a space two people maintained. Then the moment passed through them like traffic through an intersection, and Mireille began stacking plates, and Theo began the operation he performed nightly: cataloguing his wife's small departures.

She'd stopped finishing sentences. That was the big one. She'd start — "I was thinking maybe I'd—" and then simply abandon the clause, letting it die on the table between them, and he'd learned not to retrieve it because retrieving it required questions and questions required answers and answers, in this house, had a way of becoming structural.

She'd started going to the library on Tuesdays. She came back with books he never saw her read.

And now she hummed in his grandmother's language, or something so close to it that the difference was within tolerance, and she had stopped the instant he walked in, the way you stop humming when you realize you're being observed.

He said nothing.

He was aware, even as he said nothing, that saying nothing was a choice with a weight and a direction. Silence isn't neutral; silence is a material, and every silence you install bears load. He'd spent his career explaining this to city councils: you can't remove a wall and expect the roof to understand. But in his own house he'd been quietly removing walls for years, one non-question at a time, and the roof had held, mostly, and he'd called that success.

"You're staring at your plate," Mireille said gently. "The plate hasn't done anything."

"I'm admiring the plating."

"Theo."

"Mm?"

"Nothing." She carried the plates to the sink. Over the running water, very quietly, almost inaudibly, she started humming again — the descending phrase, the stubborn climb — and this time she didn't stop when she noticed him listening. She just kept it going, a little louder, a little more deliberate, like someone leaving a door open on purpose.

Theo sat at the table and listened to his wife speak, wordlessly, in a dead woman's voice, and felt the specific vertigo of a man standing on a span he has personally certified as sound while hearing, underneath his feet, a note he cannot identify.

* * *

Saturday morning he drove out to the depot to pick up equipment, and on the way back he took the long route — past the old neighborhood, which he never did, which he told himself he never did.

It was barely recognizable. The bakery was a phone store. The church was condos with a fitness center. But the river was the river, brown-green and patient, and the old pedestrian bridge still stood downstream of the new one, decommissioned, fenced, waiting for the budget line that would demolish it. He'd inspected it himself, years ago, before they closed it. Sound bones, he'd written. Fatigue in the decking. Recommend closure. Recommend monitoring.

He pulled over near the fence and sat in the truck with the engine ticking.

His father had driven buses out of this neighborhood. Route 12, the loop that connected the immigrants to the town proper, the artery the city had severed in '68 with a shrug and a rezoning. Theo had grown up on stories about the route — his father's stories, which were all weather and passengers and never, ever anything else. His father could tell you which stop had the mean dog and which had the pretty widow and which had the man who paid in exact change every day for eleven years, and if you asked him anything about the last night the route ran — anything at all, even something small, even the weather — his face would do a thing. Not close. Worse than closing. It would go *load-bearing*. You could see the structure take the weight, distribute it, hold.

Nobody in the family asked twice. That was the arrangement. Nobody had ever said *let's agree not to ask*, because agreements require acknowledgment, and acknowledgment requires words, and words were the one material the Delacroix men refused to spec. The arrangement had arranged itself, the way cracks arrange themselves along stress lines — invisibly, inevitably, and only visible after the failure.

His sister Renée called it "the family talent." *We don't ask,* she'd say, raising her wine glass. *It's genetic.*

Theo looked at the old bridge. A heron stood on the abandoned decking like an inspector who'd skipped the paperwork. Below it the river carried its usual cargo — leaves, light, the reflections of things no longer there.

Mireille was humming a language he half-knew. His wife, who drifted through their house like a tenant reading the terms of her lease. And the language was *this* language, or adjacent to it, from *this* neighborhood, and she had started humming it three weeks ago, right around the time she'd started going to the library on Tuesdays, right around the time the sentences started dying unfinished.

Coincidence was a load-bearing assumption too. He'd seen engineers rely on it. He'd seen what happened after.

His phone buzzed. Dev, with a photo of Pier 3 taken from a new angle, the rust stain longer than yesterday, or possibly the same length and photographed more honestly. *Thoughts?* the text said.

Theo typed: *Monitor. Re-measure in two weeks.* Then he put the phone face-down on the passenger seat and sat there another full minute, looking at the bridge, doing the thing he did best and worst.

Noticing.

At home, Mireille would be at the kitchen table with her library books, and if he came in quietly he might catch her humming again, and if he asked — one question, a small one, a pilot hole, nothing structural — she might answer, and the answer might be nothing, and the nothing might be everything.

He turned the key. The engine caught.

He drove home the short way, and he did not ask, and he noticed himself not asking with a precision that would have shamed a far less honest instrument, and the house, when he got there, smelled faintly of chalk.
