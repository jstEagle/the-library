## 9. Vocabulary of Guilt

The county archive lived in the basement of the old customs house in Marlow Bay, three towns down the coast road, behind a door that announced itself with a brass plaque so tarnished it read more like a warning than a welcome: RECORDS — SOME OF THEM.

The archivist was a man named Pell who had the complexion of someone who had made peace with fluorescent lighting decades ago and now considered sunlight a vulgar rumor. He looked up when Wren came in on a Tuesday morning in late October, took in the rain jacket, the canvas satchel, the general air of a man who measured things for a living, and said, "Structural?"

"Family," Wren said. "1970s."

Pell nodded as though this were the saddest category there was, which, Wren would come to learn, it was. "Kellwick?"

"How did you—"

"Everyone who comes down here asking about the seventies is asking about Kellwick." Pell pulled a ledger toward himself with the unhurried gravity of a man turning the page on a story he'd read many times. "The strike. The drowning. The company that wasn't a company for very long after. You'll want box forty-one through forty-six. Sign here. Don't use pens near the photographs. And if you find anything cheerful in there, let me know — I've begun to doubt it exists."

* * *

The archive room smelled of cold paper and older cold paper. Wren had expected to feel like a burglar in his own history, but what he felt instead was something closer to the sensation of standing on a bridge deck at night, alone, listening to the water say the same thing over and over beneath him. Patience. The archive had all the patience of water. It had been waiting thirty years for someone to come looking; it could wait another hour while he found his nerve.

He started with the council minutes, because minutes are where institutions confess without meaning to. Kellwick Parish Council, 1978–1981. He read about drainage disputes, a ferocious argument over the placement of a bus shelter (two pages), and the annual Christmas whist drive results recorded with more care than any human event of the period. Then, March 1979:

*Messrs. Harrow & Vane, agents for Corran Marine Ltd., attended regarding industrial action at the fishery. Council noted the company's assurance that all safety provisions at the harbour gates remain fully operational.*

Fully operational. He wrote the date in his notebook and sat with it. Somewhere in this building was the document that would tell him whether *fully operational* was a lie told once or a lie maintained.

Box forty-three held the correspondence. Company letterhead, blue ink, the confident slanted hand of men who had never doubted a form in their lives. Requisitions. Wage schedules. A memo about the cost of replacing padlocks versus re-keying existing ones, which under other circumstances would have been darkly funny.

And then, on a Thursday afternoon with rain ticking against the high window, he found it.

A single sheet, onionskin carbon copy, the original long gone to whatever drawer dead companies keep their originals in. Dated the fourteenth of March, 1979. Nine days before Tomas Okwuosa went into the water.

*RE: Harbour gate security during current action. Be advised that per instruction of Mr. G. Yarrow, Site Operations, the inner and outer safety gates are to be secured outside working hours by chain and padlock, keys retained solely by management. This measure to remain in force until further notice. Workers requiring access must apply to the foreman's office.*

Signed below in a hand Wren knew better than his own — because he had seen it on birthday cards, on permission slips, on the flyleaf of every book his father ever owned, that looping, slightly theatrical capital G that Gerard Yarrow had practiced as a boy and never bothered to grow out of.

Wren did not feel what he had braced himself to feel. There was no vertigo, no nausea, none of the dramatics he'd half-expected from himself. What he felt was the specific, professional horror of an inspector who has opened up a component everyone assumed was sound and found the crack running clean through it. Not deterioration. Deliberate. Installed. His father hadn't failed to prevent the drowning. His father had built the mechanism of it, ordered it, initialled the requisition for the padlock — he found that one too, twenty minutes later, £4.60, approved G.Y. — and then gone home to his family and taught his son how to skip stones.

He sat in the basement until the light through the window went from grey to the greyer shade that means evening, copying the order out word for word in his notebook, his handwriting getting smaller as he went, as though he could shrink the thing by writing it small.

Pell appeared at the door with two cups of tea, one of which he set down beside Wren without being asked. "Found your cheerful document yet?"

"No."

"Mm." Pell looked at the onionskin page, then away, granting it privacy. "You know what I always think, down here? Everyone in these files was somebody's dad." He straightened a stack of folders that didn't need straightening. "Archive closes at six. But I'm not saying I'd notice if a man needed an extra half hour with a photocopier."

* * *

He drove back up the coast road in the dark with the copied order folded into his breast pocket, where he could feel it against his chest like a splint on a break that hadn't been set. Twice he pulled over — once at the layby above Whin Point, once just past the Kellwick sign — and sat with the engine ticking and considered, seriously and at length, driving past the village altogether, past the bridge, past the grey house, all the way to somewhere with motorways and anonymity.

Both times, he started the engine again and drove the last few miles.

Because here was the thing he couldn't explain to Mara on the phone, couldn't have explained to anyone: he didn't want to stop seeing Edele. That was the discovery underneath the discovery. Under the guilt and the duty and the historian's compulsion there was a simpler, warmer, more damning fact — Thursday mornings had become the fixed point of his week. He liked the walk up from the bridge. He liked the way she said *sit* like a queen granting a favor. He liked that she thought he was merely a bridge inspector with an unusual appetite for grammar, and that the person she saw when she looked at him was uncomplicated, unburdened, a blank page she was filling with beautiful words.

Every lesson he attended now was built on the folded paper in his pocket. He understood that. He understood it the way you understand a load rating: intellectually first, and only later, horribly, in your body.

* * *

Thursday. Rain again — Kellwick's weather operated on a principle of commitment rather than variety — and the grey house smelled of woodsmoke and the seed cake Edele baked, she claimed, for the gulls, though no gull had ever been observed receiving any.

"You're late," she said, which meant he was four minutes early, which meant she'd been watching the road.

"The archive kept me."

Something flickered across her face — quick, gone. "The archive," she repeated. "In Marlow Bay. The customs house basement. My father used to take me there when I was small. He said a people's papers were its second memory." She poured the tea. "The first memory is the language. The papers are for when the first one goes."

"What happened to them? Your family's papers?"

"Burned, mostly. After Tomas. My mother fed the stove with them all winter, very methodically, like she was cooking grief." She handed him his cup. "Which is why it amuses me that you spend your Thursdays off among other people's paperwork. You're a man who trusts documents. I can hear it in your consonants."

"I don't know what my consonants sound like."

"Careful," she said. "Everything sounds like something. In Vessic we say *kessa venn* — literally, 'the mouth keeps the shape of what it holds.' Your mouth is holding a filing system, Mr. Yarrow."

He laughed, and hated how easy it was, how good it felt, this laughter purchased with concealment.

* * *

The lesson that day was water. It seemed fitting in a way she couldn't know and he couldn't bear.

"*Senn*," she said. "Water. Simple enough even for an engineer. But watch — *senn-ora*, still water, water that waits. *Senn-kai*, moving water, water that travels. *Senn-durra*—" she paused, and her voice did the thing it always did when it approached her brother without warning, dipping and steadying itself like a wader on a rock, "—water that has taken someone. We don't say drowned. There is no passive victim in it. *Senn-durra*: the water has carried. As if the sea were given credit for the work."

"And is it?" Wren asked. "Given credit?"

Edele looked at him for a long moment over the rim of her cup. Ninety-one years old and her eyes were still the sharpest instruments in the room, sharper than his gauges, his calipers, his little yellow stress meters. "That," she said, "is a question with a padlock on it. I can hear one. You've brought a locked question into my kitchen and dressed it in grammar."

"It's just a question about vocabulary."

"Vocabulary is never *just* anything. Every word in Vessic is a small verdict." She set down her cup. "But fine. Keep your lock. Locks open eventually; they're only ever borrowed time. Now — repeat after me. *Senn-kai.* Moving water."

"*Senn-kai.*"

"Better. Your accent is improving. Another month and you'll sound less like a man reading a shipping forecast and more like a man talking to the sea, which is the entire point."

* * *

He fell into a rhythm that should have shamed him and instead sustained him. Mondays and Fridays at the bridge, taking measurements for a report he was increasingly certain would end his career. Tuesdays in Marlow Bay, pulling threads — the foreman's office logbook, a witness statement from a deckhand named Prewitt who had signed it and then, judging by the smudged erasure marks, wished he hadn't. Thursdays at the grey house, learning a dying language from a woman whose brother his father had helped cage behind a chained gate.

He kept the copies in a waterproof map case under the passenger seat of his car, which he understood was both melodramatic and sensible, and which he checked every morning the way some men check a wound.

The strange part — the part he turned over at night in the cottage bed, staring at the ceiling — was that the deception and the devotion grew from the same root, watered from the same can. He came to the lessons partly to hide, yes. But he also came because Edele was the funniest, fiercest, most entirely alive person he had ever met, and because Vessic was doing something to him that no one, including Mara, had managed in years: it was giving him words for the inside of his own chest. *Unaccompanied by the sea.* He had been that his whole marriage and had never once had a term for it. Now he had a term. Terms were dangerous. Terms were how cracks got named, and named cracks had to be reported.

"You've gone somewhere," Edele said one Thursday, catching him mid-sentence. They were on kinship terms — Vessic had eleven words for cousin and he was failing at all of them — and his mind had drifted to the photocopied order sitting thirty feet below them in the map case, in the car, in the rain.

"Sorry. Load calculations."

"Liar," she said comfortably. "But a polite one, so I'll allow it. Where were we — ah. *Vessa.* To carry. Now, *vessa* has children, as all strong words do. *Vessa-mir*, to carry a burden. *Vessa-lu*, to carry a child. And *vessa-tome*—" 

She stopped.

Not the way she'd stopped over *senn-durra*. This was different. Her cup was halfway to the saucer and stayed there, suspended, and for three full seconds Edmund Okwuosa's sister was not in the kitchen at all. She was somewhere else, in some other year, and her face was doing arithmetic with grief.

"Edele?"

"*Vessa-tome*," she finished, setting the cup down with exaggerated care, the care you give fragile things, "to carry together. Two people, one weight. It's the root of our word for forgiveness, but we'll come to that another day. It deserves its own lesson. It deserves—" she smiled, and the smile was slightly wrong at one corner, like a picture hung a degree off true, "—it deserves a student who's ready."

"I'm not sure I know what ready looks like."

"No," she agreed. "But you will. Ready usually arrives wearing something you weren't expecting. In my experience it tends to knock twice."

She refilled his tea and moved on to the eleven cousins, and Wren wrote everything down in his notebook in his small careful hand, directly beneath, though she couldn't see it, the transcribed order that bore his father's name — the same name, he realized with a lurch he disguised as reaching for the cake, that he had never once spoken aloud in this house.

Outside, the tide was coming in under the old iron bridge, patient as an archivist, carrying everything toward the shore.

Two knocks, she'd said.

He had maybe a week.
