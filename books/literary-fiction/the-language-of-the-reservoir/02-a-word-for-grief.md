## 2. A Word for Grief

The community center on Delancey smelled like floor wax and old radiators, which meant it smelled like every building Coral worked in, and she moved through the recital crowd with the ease of someone who had already catalogued the room. Radiator in the corner by the stage: knocking on cold starts, air-bound. The one near the coat rack: silent, probably a closed valve, someone's small mercy or small cruelty. She could have fixed both before intermission.

She hadn't come for the recital. She'd come because Mrs. Okonkwo from the third-floor walkup on Ridge Street had begged her — *my son is playing, you must come, he will be so nervous* — and because Coral had never learned to refuse the mothers of children whose pianos she tuned. There were maybe forty people folding metal chairs into rows, a banner over the stage reading SPRING SHOWCASE in letters that had been blue once, and a punch bowl nobody was guarding.

She took a seat in the back row, where the radiator ticked like a patient clock, and let the noise of the room wash over her. This was the part of her work nobody understood: she spent all day inside other people's sound. Houses told you what they were if you listened. A house with a baby sounded different from a house with an argument. A house where someone had died kept a particular hush, a frequency below hearing that you felt in your sternum rather than your ears. Her grandmother's house had been holding that note for three years now, and Coral lived inside it the way you live inside weather.

Mrs. Okonkwo's son played a Chopin mazurka with the stiff courage of a boy who had practiced past fear into numbness. He flubbed the same passage twice and recovered both times, and Coral found herself leaning forward, hands twitching against her knees, wanting to tell him: *it's not a mistake if you arrive somewhere on purpose.* Then a girl of about ten played a piece Coral didn't recognize — something modal, wandering, with a melody that kept circling back to a note a half-step too low, grief folded into the geometry of it — and the woman beside Coral went very still.

Coral noticed because stillness in a crowd is rare. Everyone else was shifting, coughing, checking phones. But this woman sat as though the music had reached into her chest and taken hold of something. She was elderly, small-boned, wearing a headwrap the color of deep water and a cardigan that had been mended at both elbows with visible care. Her hands lay open in her lap, palms up, the way people hold them when they've given up guarding anything.

When the applause came, the woman didn't clap. She pressed her lips together and looked down at her open hands, and Coral saw — quickly, unmistakably — that she was crying without any of the machinery of crying. No shaking. No sound. Just water arriving, the way condensation arrives on a cold pipe.

The program moved on. A teenager sang a song from a movie. Someone's toddler escaped down the aisle and was retrieved. And Coral kept glancing sideways at the old woman, feeling the strange pull she sometimes felt toward instruments that wouldn't stay in tune no matter what she did — the sense that something was asking for help in a language she almost spoke.

* * *

Intermission. The crowd thinned toward the punch bowl. Coral stayed in her seat, telling herself she was resting her feet, until the old woman turned and looked directly at her.

"You were listening," the woman said. "To the second one. The girl."

"It was beautiful," Coral said. "I don't know the piece."

"Neither do I." The woman smiled, and her eyes were still wet but her voice was dry and certain. "That's why I'm crying. It isn't from anywhere anymore. Somebody made it up out of almost nothing. That's how languages die — not all at once, but one person making things up because they can't remember the real thing."

"I'm sorry," Coral said, because there seemed to be nothing else to say.

"Don't be sorry. Be curious. You're a tuner, yes?" The woman nodded at Coral's hands. "You carry the case. And you sit like a tuner. You listen with your whole spine."

"How can you tell?"

"Because I do the same thing with voices." She extended a hand, unhurried. "Adaora Eze."

"Coral Okafor."

The name landed on Adaora Eze like a stone dropped into still water. Coral watched it happen — watched the ripples move across the old woman's face, the recalculation behind her eyes.

"Okafor," Adaora repeated. "Your people are Igbo."

"My grandmother was. I'm—" Coral did the shrug she always did. "American, mostly."

"Mm." Adaora was not fooled and did not pretend to be. "And your grandmother. Is she living?"

"She died three years ago."

Adaora's hand came down and covered Coral's wrist, light as a bird landing. "Then listen to me carefully, tuner girl, because I am going to ask you something strange, and I want you to answer honestly. Do you know any songs? From her. Songs she sang when you were small. Anything at all."

"No," Coral said. It was automatic, and it was true, and it was also — she realized even as she said it — incomplete. "I mean, I don't know any *songs*. She hummed, sometimes. In the kitchen. When she thought I wasn't listening."

"Hummed." Adaora's grip tightened fractionally. "Hummed what?"

"I don't know. I was a kid. It was just — humming. The way people hum."

"The way people hum," Adaora agreed, "is never just humming. Sing it."

"I can't sing it. I don't remember it."

"You don't remember it the way you don't remember your address. Your mouth remembers it even if your mind has misplaced it." Adaora released her wrist and folded her hands again, patient as furniture. "Sing it. Or hum it. Whatever comes. Don't decide first. Deciding is how we lose things."

Every rational part of Coral wanted to laugh this off, to stand, to go get a cup of that unguarded punch. She was a private person in a public room holding a stranger's gaze, and everything in her training said *decline politely, disengage, keep your interior sealed.*

Instead she heard herself say, "It'll be nothing."

"Everything is nothing until it isn't," said Adaora Eze. "Go on."

And Coral hummed.

She hadn't known she was going to. The sound simply left her — four notes, then a turn, then two more notes descending, and then the phrase again, slightly changed, the way a river repeats itself without ever stepping in the same water twice. Her throat knew the shape of it. Her breath knew where to rest. She hummed eight bars of a melody she could not have written down under oath, and while she hummed it she was suddenly, violently, in her grandmother's kitchen: steam on the window, the smell of stockfish and crayfish, the radio off, her grandmother's back to her, broad and unbending, humming exactly this while the pot murmured and the afternoon went gold outside the glass.

The kitchen had been torn out and redone by the flipper who'd sold Coral the house. But the melody was intact. Nobody had renovated that.

She stopped. The room came back — folding chairs, punch bowl, the teenager tuning a guitar badly somewhere near the stage.

Adaora Eze had gone white around the mouth.

"What," Coral said. "What is it?"

"Say your grandmother's name."

"Nneka. Nneka Okafor. Why—"

"Where did she come from. Not Nigeria. Before Lagos, before the city. Where."

"I don't — she never said. There was a town she mentioned once, I think, but I couldn't pronounce it, so I stopped asking—"

"*Ọnịcha Ọnà,*" Adaora said, and the word came out of her like something long stored, dusted off, held to the light. "Or close to it. Say it back."

Coral tried. Mangled it. Tried again. On the third attempt something clicked into place in her mouth, some arrangement of tongue and breath, and Adaora closed her eyes as if a pain had peaked and passed.

"That lullaby," Adaora said quietly. "Four notes, then the turn, then the fall. Do you know what it says?"

"It doesn't say anything. It's humming."

"It says *sleep, my child, the river is watching over you, and the river does not forget.*" Adaora opened her eyes. They were dry now, and terribly bright. "It is a cradle song from Ọ̀nà. Not Igbo — related, cousin to Igbo, the way a cousin is related, but its own language, its own tones, its own way of breaking a heart. My mother sang it. Her mother sang it. And I have not heard it from another living mouth in thirty-one years."

The radiator behind them knocked twice and went quiet, as if it too were listening.

"I don't understand," Coral said. "If it's a language, there would be — speakers. Communities. There are millions of Nigerians in this city alone—"

"There are Yoruba. There are Igbo. There are Efik, Edo, Tiv, Hausa, a hundred tongues, and God bless every one of them. But Ọ̀nà was small to begin with. One river valley. A few towns. And the ones who left, left to survive, and to survive they became Igbo, became English, became *anything else*, because being Ọ̀nà got you nothing but a slower job and a faster death." Adaora's voice stayed level; only her hands betrayed her, closing slowly into fists inside their open rest. "My husband is dead. My brothers are dead. My cousins' children speak English and Igbo and French. I am seventy-eight years old, Coral Okafor, and I am the last fluent speaker of Ọ̀nà on this earth, and tonight a stranger hummed me a cradle song I buried with my mother."

The hall felt suddenly enormous and empty around the two of them. Somewhere a chair scraped. The teenager struck a chord.

"I hummed a lullaby," Coral said. "That's all. I don't speak anything. I barely speak English out loud, if you want the truth."

"I know. I watched you at the recital. You sit at the edge of rooms. You listen beautifully and say nothing." Adaora leaned forward, and her voice dropped into something that was not quite pleading and not quite command but was heavier than either. "So here is what I came here to find, though I didn't know its face until you said your name. I need one student. One. Not a linguist with a tape recorder and a thesis — I have met them, they take the language away in pieces and publish it like a butterfly pinned to board. Not a heritage class of thirty children who learn hello and goodbye and forget me by June. One person who will take the whole weight of it. Who will let it live in their mouth until it is theirs, and then — when I am gone — decide what it becomes."

"Why me? You just met me. You know nothing about me."

"I know your grandmother hummed Ọ̀nà in her kitchen while she made soup, in America, alone, for years, and never taught you one word of it." Adaora's voice gentled, which somehow made it worse. "Which means she carried it in secret, which means it cost her something, which means you have been breathing it your whole life without knowing. Child, the language is already in you. It's just asleep. I am not asking you to learn something foreign. I am asking you to wake up something that has been waiting."

Coral stood up. She didn't decide to stand; her body did it, the way it had done the humming, some older authority overriding the committee of her caution. She picked up her tuning case because her hands needed occupation.

"I tune pianos," she said. "That's what I do. I fix things that are broken and I leave. I'm not — I'm not a student. I'm not anybody's last hope. I have a house full of boxes I haven't opened and a piano I haven't touched in three years, and I don't know why I'm telling you this except that you asked me to sing and I did, and I don't do that either."

Adaora Eze looked up at her from the metal chair, small and unmoved, and smiled the smile of someone who has just watched a lock turn.

"A piano tuner who owns a piano she cannot play," she said. "Of course. Of course that's who the language sends." She rose, slowly, joints announcing themselves, and took Coral's forearm in both hands — the grip of a woman steadying herself, or anchoring someone else. "I am not asking you to answer tonight. I am seventy-eight; I have learned that urgency frightens people into saying no. Come to my apartment. Thursday. Listen to me speak for one hour and translate nothing, promise nothing, decide nothing. If you leave and never come back, I will have had one hour of speaking Ọ̀nà to ears that carry it, and that alone is worth the bus fare."

"I didn't say I'd come."

"You hummed, didn't you?" Adaora released her arm and stepped back, gathering her bag, settling her headwrap with two precise movements. "Something in you answered before you could refuse. I heard it. So did you."

She walked toward the door, unhurried, and Coral stood in the middle of the folding chairs with her tuning case in her hand and the lullaby still ringing in her sternum — four notes, the turn, the fall — and understood with terrible clarity that she had just been given the address of the one room in the city where her silence would not hold.

Thursday. She would go Thursday. She was already going; she could feel it in her throat, the way you feel a note the piano hasn't played yet.

Behind her, the teenager began his song, and hit the wrong key, and arrived somewhere on purpose.
