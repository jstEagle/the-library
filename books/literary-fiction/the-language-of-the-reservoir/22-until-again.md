## 22. Until Again

The house had learned my name.

I don't mean that the way a lonely person means it, talking to radiators, naming the creaks. I mean that somewhere over the winter the house stopped treating me like weather and started treating me like a resident. The pipes announced me now instead of complaining. The floorboards in the hall had a rhythm they saved for my step alone, a little three-beat phrase, and when Tomás came over on Thursdays the house played a different one for him, lower, more skeptical, and I had decided not to take it personally.

A year since Adaora died. A year since the Sunday morning when the tapes kept turning and she didn't, when I sat on the floor beside her bed with my hand on her wrist and said the goodbye word out loud, twice, once for her and once for my grandmother, and my throat did not close. It had taken me eleven months to say that plainly, even to myself: my throat did not close.

Now it was October again, the same season she had found me humming in the community center kitchen, and I had nine students coming at two o'clock, and I was labeling cassette boxes at the kitchen table with a cup of tea going cold beside me.

* * *

The recordings had outgrown the shelf.

Adaora's tapes filled two crates now — the originals from her apartment, dense with labeled jars and decades of careful handwriting, plus the sessions from the hospital, my old radio equipment humming on the bedside table while nurses learned to say good morning in Ọ̀nà and Adaora, propped on pillows, corrected their tones with the patience of a woman who had waited fifty years for anyone to ask. Then the hospice sessions. Then the last one, the Sunday morning, which I had not planned to record and which the machine recorded anyway, because I had left it running out of some superstition I couldn't name, and which I had listened to exactly once and would listen to again someday, but not yet.

And beside them, a third crate, growing: my students.

That was the part Adaora had demanded, the term she'd set when she was still strong enough to set terms, gripping my wrist with a strength that surprised us both: *No private museum pieces. If you learn it, you owe it forward. A language kept in one throat is a language already buried.* I had hesitated then — I couldn't even claim my own house, I'd told her, what business did I have promising anything to strangers — and she had looked at me with that expression, dry and bottomless, and said that a house and a language were the same problem, and the solution to both was to stop treating yourself like a tenant.

So: nine students. Every Thursday evening in my grandmother's front room, the sealed piano open at last, its strings freshly tuned by my own hands, though I still let it sit mostly silent because the class was about voices, not keys. Nine people of varying ages and no common thread except that they had each, for their own reasons, answered a flyer I'd put up at the community center with more nerve than I'd felt: *Ọ̀nà language class. All welcome. The last speaker trusted me with it. I intend to be trustworthy.*

The first session, six of them had come. By the second month, nine. They were not good at it. Neither was I. Adaora had told me in the final lesson that my pronunciation would falter for years and that the faltering was the lesson — *the mouth learns the way the house learns, slowly, by being lived in* — and so I faltered in front of them every week, deliberately, audibly, and something in the room loosened each time I did. They faltered back. We were a room full of wrong tones, and it was the most fluent I had ever felt.

Every session, I recorded them. Twenty minutes at the end, the same protocol I'd used with Adaora: greetings, then vocabulary, then a song, then — this was my addition, and I liked to think she'd have approved — each person saying one true thing about their week, in Ọ̀nà, badly, and then again in English, so that someday, some listener with the tapes and no memory of us could line the two versions up like parallel texts and find the door between them.

Today's box held last Thursday's tape. I wrote the label in both scripts — English on the left, Ọ̀nà on the right, the characters my grandmother had used in her unsent letters, the ones I'd taught myself from her hand, stroke by stroke, until my writing looked like a child imitating a grandmother, which is exactly what it was — and then I sat with the pen hovering, because the true thing Marcus had said last week deserved a better label than *week report*.

He'd said: *I am learning this language because my mother stopped speaking to me for six years and I want to be the kind of person who can say the first word.* In Ọ̀nà it had come out with the tones all wrong, blessing where he meant grief, and nobody had laughed, and he'd said it again, and the second time it landed.

I wrote: *Marcus — first words.* In both scripts. Capped the pen. Made new tea.

* * *

The package to Enugu had become a monthly ritual, and like all rituals it had accumulated its own liturgy.

I packed it at the kitchen table: two copies of each new tape, one for Chidi's family archive and one for the university library where a young linguist named Nkem had started, cautiously at first and then with the hunger I recognized from my own mirror, transcribing everything. Chidi had written back after the first package in his careful English — *We did not know her voice survived. We did not know she had kept speaking to us for fifty years into a silence we thought was hers* — and then, three letters later, he had sent something that stopped my hands for a full afternoon: a photograph of his daughter, Amara, nine years old, standing in front of a blackboard on which she had copied, in wobbling chalk, the characters from my grandmother's letters. *She asks if the letters are from a story,* Chidi wrote. *I told her they are from a story, and the story is ours.*

The Thursday class now opened with whatever Amara had sent: a drawing, a recorded sentence, once a full song she'd learned wrong and taught back to us wrong, so that the wrongness traveled across the ocean and became, by repetition, a tradition. Adaora would have called that corruption. Adaora would also, I thought, have been the first person in the room laughing, because she had spent her last year telling me that a language is not a vase you protect but a river you can't step in twice, and rivers that don't move are called something else.

I sealed the box. Wrote the address. Weighed it in my hand — tapes, a letter, a jar of the honey Tomás bought from the market on 9th because Chidi had mentioned once, in passing, that his grandmother had loved it — and set it by the door with the other outgoing things.

Then I sat down to write the letter that went inside, because the letter was the part I never rushed.

* * *

Dear Chidi,

The class is nine now. I know I said eight last month; a woman named Ruth joined after hearing us through the window, which the house leaves open now even in October, which tells you what the house has decided about us. She is sixty-four and she told me she has spent her whole life being the one who remembers things nobody asked her to remember, and when she heard there was a language with no one left to remember it, she said, finally, a job worthy of me.

I laughed until I had to sit down. I think you would have too. I think your great-aunt — our grandmother, I am still practicing the word *ours* — would have said something dry and perfect and I would have missed half of it and pretended I didn't.

We recorded the greetings again. Ruth's tones are better than mine. I have stopped being embarrassed about this. Adaora told me near the end that the teacher's job is not to be the best voice in the room but to make room for better ones, and I am telling you this so you understand that when I say Ruth is better than me, I am reporting a professional triumph.

The tapes enclosed are sessions nine through twelve. Nkem will find the vocabulary list growing slower now — we have covered the words for water, fire, door, grief (I can say it; it took me a year and I can say it), the names of birds, the song Adaora sang the first day I met her, which I now know was not a lullaby but a work song, and which I had been humming my whole life without permission or knowledge, the way you inherit a house you've never claimed. We are starting on jokes. Jokes are the hardest thing I have ever tried to do in any language, including English, including silence. Adaora said you are not fluent until you can be funny, and I intend to be funny in Ọ̀nà before I die, and I am telling you this as a witness so that someone can verify it happened.

Amara's song has entered the curriculum. I have labeled it, in the archive, as *traditional, variant: Enugu, via a nine-year-old, via a Thursday evening, via a house that used to be silent.* I hope the linguists forgive me. I hope Adaora doesn't, actually. I hope she corrects my tones from wherever she is, which I believe she does, nightly.

The house is full on Thursdays. You should hear the radiators when everyone is talking — the whole building seems to join in, pipes and voices, a chorus with no conductor. My grandmother sealed the piano for reasons I will never fully know, and I opened it for reasons I am only now able to say out loud: because a sealed instrument and an unspoken language are the same grief wearing two coats, and I am done carrying either one.

Tomás says hello. He is here most Thursdays. He fixed the boiler in this house the way he fixed the one at the center — with swearing, with genius, and with the radiator and me talking over each other until we found a key we could both live in. We are not what we were at twenty-six. We are something slower and stranger and I am not going to explain it in a letter, because some sentences should be spoken in the wrong tones first, and corrected, and said again.

Until the next box. Tell Amara the blackboard photograph is pinned above my desk, and that her characters are better than mine were at the start, and that I am not being kind, I am being accurate, and that accuracy is a form of love I learned from a dying woman in a hospital bed who corrected a nurse's good morning until it was perfect, and then closed her eyes like a queen.

The word I end with is the word she gave me, the one that means not *goodbye* but *until again* — the one my throat refused for a year and now carries like a key I finally turned:

Àtúnwọ̀, Chidi. Àtúnwọ̀, Amara. Àtúnwọ̀, all of you, all of us, across the water and the years and the two silences that turned out to be one.

— Coral

* * *

I read it twice, folded it, and tucked it into the box on top of the tapes, and then I stood at the kitchen window with my tea and listened to the house.

Two o'clock was an hour away. The radiators had started their morning song — Tomás had balanced the system in the spring, and now they didn't clank so much as hum, a low chord through the walls, and I had caught myself, more than once, tuning it by ear, adjusting the valve in the back hall by a quarter turn because the chord wanted its third a little brighter. A year ago I treated this house like rented space. Now I tuned it. There was a word for the difference and Adaora had taught it to me in the second month, and I said it to the kitchen sometimes, quietly, just to feel the shape of it: *home* was not the word. The word was older and meant something more like *the place that answers back.*

On the table, the three crates sat in their row: Adaora, Adaora, the students. Someday there would be a fourth crate — I had already bought it, it was in the cellar, empty, patient — for whatever came after the nine. Ruth's granddaughter had started sitting in on the last half hour. Marcus's mother had come to observe last week and had stayed for two hours and had said, on the tape, in Ọ̀nà, one true thing about her week: *I am in the same room as my son and we are both speaking.* And Amara, via video call on the laptop propped against the teapot, had begun asking for homework, which no nine-year-old in recorded history had done, and which I took as evidence that the language had decided to survive and had chosen strange, stubborn vessels to do it in.

I rinsed my cup. Set out the chairs — nine, plus the rocking chair by the window that nobody sat in, which was Adaora's, in the way that a chair becomes someone's after enough Thursdays of being left for them. I opened the piano lid, more from habit than need, and pressed one key, middle C, and let the house hold the note.

Then the doorbell rang — Ruth, early, always early, carrying a tin of something — and behind her the sound of the others coming up the walk, voices overlapping, the pipes picking up the chorus, and I went to answer the door with the letter's last word still warm in my mouth, saying it before I said hello, saying it to all of them at once, wrong tones and all:

*Àtúnwọ̀.*

Until again.

They said it back.
