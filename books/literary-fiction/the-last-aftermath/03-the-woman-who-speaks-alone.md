## 3. The Woman Who Speaks Alone

The basement of the conservatory smelled like paper and cold stone, like a library that had given up on being visited. August took the stairs slowly, mop handle in one hand, the other trailing the rail, counting steps out of habit—fourteen, same as twenty years ago, though everything above had been renovated twice since. The ledger in her head opened itself: *Basement level. Fluorescent hum, second fixture flickering at the far end. Water stain shaped like a country no one has named, spreading along the northeast wall since at least 1998.*

She catalogued as she went because cataloguing was how she kept the world at arm's length. A room fully described could not ambush you. A stain measured, a floor mapped, a silence noted and filed—that silence belonged to her, not the other way around.

Her route that night ended in the sub-basement archive, a corridor of steel shelving where the conservatory stored what it preferred to forget: programs from defunct festivals, faculty files of the departed, reel-to-reel tapes labeled in handwriting that had gone out of style with the century. Nobody came down here. That was why she'd saved it for last. That was why she'd saved it for every night this week, actually—the way you save the room you're afraid of for the end of the shift, telling yourself it's efficiency.

Except the concert hall was upstairs, and she still hadn't cleaned it. Four nights now. She told herself the schedule simply hadn't allowed it.

*Noted,* said the ledger. *Filed under avoidance.* And then, as it always did, it closed before she could read what was written underneath.

She heard the voice before she saw the woman.

It came from deep in the stacks, past the row marked F–K, a low, unhurried murmur—not speech exactly, or not any speech August knew. The syllables were round and deliberate, each one given its full weight, the way you'd set down eggs. There was a rhythm to it, almost musical, a rising phrase and a falling answer, question and response, except there was no one there to respond. The shelves answered only with their own quiet.

August stopped at the end of the aisle.

An old woman stood beneath the flickering light with a clipboard against her hip, small and straight-backed, silver hair cut short and practical, cardigan the color of wet slate. She was facing the shelving the way a conductor faces an orchestra, and she was speaking to it—to the empty shelves, to the boxes of dead recordings—as if expecting them to reply. Her voice carried without effort. It didn't need volume; it needed only certainty.

*Vessic,* August thought, and then corrected herself, because the word arrived unbidden and she distrusted anything unbidden. She knew the name the way everyone at the conservatory knew it, the way you know the name of a mountain range you've never climbed: Ilse Vann, the archivist, keeper of the sound collection, forty years in the building, the last fluent speaker of a language with no country left to hold it. Students treated her like a ghost story. *If you get lost in the basement, the speaking woman will find you.* August had assumed the story was mostly story.

But here was the woman, and she was speaking alone, and the language moved through her like water through a channel worn so deep it could hold nothing else.

August should have withdrawn. That was the protocol of invisibility: retreat before contact, reclassify, reschedule. She had a mop. She had a reason. But the old woman's voice did something the Chopin étude had done two nights ago—it reached into her chest and turned a key she thought she'd welded shut. Not the melody itself. The *aloneness* of it. August knew what it was to perform to a room that couldn't answer back.

She stood there too long. That was the mistake.

The woman stopped mid-syllable. Didn't turn so much as become aware, the way animals become aware, all at once and without visible motion. Then she rotated on her sensible shoes and looked directly down the aisle at August, and her eyes were pale gray and absolutely unsentimental, and they went through the janitor's uniform, through the pulled-back hair, through the twenty years, like light through dirty glass—dimmed, but arriving.

"August," the woman said.

Not *excuse me*. Not *can I help you*. Just the name, plain, in a flat American accent with something older underneath it, and August felt the floor tilt a degree, the way it does on a ship when you've convinced yourself you're on land.

"You're mistaken," August said. "I'm the night custodian."

"Mm." The woman looked at her hands, at the mop, at the laminated ID clipped to her collar, which said A. COLDHARBOUR in block letters because August had requested initials, had requested them specifically, and some clerk had complied without curiosity. "They let just anyone push that mop," the woman said. "That's what I like about this place. No standards left."

"I don't know what you—"

"Gussie."

The nickname landed in the middle of the aisle between them and lay there, and August could not step around it. No one had called her that in twenty-two years. Her mother had coined it, her father had sung it across the breakfast table off-key on purpose, her sister had used it as a weapon and then retired it along with everything else from that year. It had been buried so thoroughly that hearing it now was less like memory than like exhumation—the name climbing out of its own grave, blinking, asking why it had been put away.

"Nobody calls me that," August said. Her voice came out lower than intended.

"No," the woman agreed. "Nobody does. That's rather the point." She tucked the clipboard under her arm and came down the aisle toward her, unhurried, and up close she smelled of pencil shavings and camphor and something faintly herbal, like tea brewed from a plant that grew nowhere anymore. "Ilse Vann," she said, though she offered no hand. "You'll have heard the ghost stories. I'm pleased to report most of them are true."

"I'm working," August said. "I need to shelve—there's a cart. I'm behind."

"You're not behind. You've been standing there for four minutes enjoying my recitation, and before that you stood outside the door for another three deciding whether to come in. You move quietly for a large woman with equipment." Ilse Vann tilted her head. "Pianists move quietly. It's the pedaling. You learn to listen through your feet."

The fluorescent tube at the end of the aisle buzzed, stuttered, held. August gripped the mop with both hands like a railing.

"I don't play," she said.

"No," Ilse said. "You don't. That's the first honest thing anyone's said to me all week, and I include myself." She turned back to the shelf she'd been addressing, ran one finger along a box spine, and read the label aloud—but not in English. The syllables again, round and weighted, and this time August caught the structure of it: the label text, translated, spoken to the box in its own inherited tongue, a courtesy paid to a recording no living person would ever hear. When she finished, she looked back over her shoulder. "Do you know what I was doing just now?"

"Saying words to empty shelves."

"I was keeping appointments." Ilse said it without self-pity, the way you'd state a maintenance schedule. "Every recording in this collection is a speaker who can no longer be interrupted. Someone has to talk to them or they forget they were ever people. Language is not vocabulary, Gussie. Language is the agreement that someone is listening. Break the agreement and it doesn't matter how many dictionaries you bury in a basement. It's dead, and the shelves know it, and frankly the shelves are getting smug about it."

"My name is August."

"It is now." Ilse faced her fully, and there was something in her expression that August recognized from mirrors—the look of a person conducting an inventory of damage. "Twenty years ago I sat in the fourth row of the concert hall upstairs and watched a seventeen-year-old girl stop playing Tchaikovsky in the middle of the second movement and sit there with her hands in her lap while eleven hundred people learned what a silence sounds like when it has a body. I have thought about that silence more often than I think about most music. Do you know why?"

"Stop." The word came out sharp enough to echo off the steel shelving. August's heart was going hard and shallow, the old arrhythmia, the one the doctors had called stress-related, the one her family had agreed—without agreeing, without ever saying it—to never mention again. "Whatever you think you're doing, I clean floors. That's the whole job. I don't do conversations, I don't do memories, and I especially don't do whatever this is."

"What is this?" Ilse asked mildly. "Name it."

"A woman talking to boxes."

"And you stayed to listen for seven minutes." The old woman smiled then, and it transformed her face entirely—wicked, quick, decades younger. "A custodian would have left. Curiosity is a terrible thing in someone trying to disappear. It leaks."

August turned and pushed the cart toward the far stacks, giving herself work, giving herself distance. She could feel the woman watching her the way you feel weather change. The ledger in her head scrambled for entries: *Sub-basement. One occupant. Knows too much. Avoid Row F–K indefinitely.* But the entries wouldn't stick. They slid off, because the truth underneath them had already been spoken aloud in a dead language's accent: *Gussie*, and the fourth row, and the silence with a body.

Her father had sat in the second row that night. She remembered that with terrible clarity—remembered his hands folded on his knee, remembered the exact angle of his head when the music stopped, remembered thinking, in the long falling seconds before the hall erupted, *he isn't surprised*. She had spent twenty years not thinking that thought. It had cost her more than most people spend on lives.

"The conservatory is a hundred years old next spring," Ilse said to her back. "Did you know?"

"I don't follow the news."

"They're planning a gala. In the hall. Everyone who was anyone will be dug up and dusted and displayed." A pause, precisely calibrated. "I mention it only because I am old, and old women mention things. We can't help it. It's how we practice."

August closed her eyes briefly. The mop water sloshed. Somewhere above them, faint through concrete, a piano was being played badly by someone practicing at ten at night, and even that sound seemed to know her name.

"I have to finish my rounds," she said.

"Of course." Ilse Vann returned to her shelf, lifted a reel-to-reel box, and held it against her chest like a child. But as August pushed the cart toward the stairwell, the voice followed her, conversational, almost idle, pitched to carry exactly as far as it needed to:

"Come back Thursday, Gussie. I'll be here. I'm always here. It's the only appointment I keep that talks back."

August climbed the fourteen steps without counting them, which frightened her more than anything the old woman had said. At the top she stopped, one hand on the door, and realized she was breathing the way she used to breathe backstage—shallow, counted, controlled—and that somewhere below her, in the dark among the shelves, a language with one speaker left was waiting patiently for its next student, and had apparently already chosen her.

*Thursday,* said the ledger, in a hand that was not hers.

She wrote it down anyway, later, on the back of a supply requisition form, and hated herself for the steadiness of her own handwriting.

* * *

Thursday came the way Thursdays do—without permission.

August told herself she was taking the sub-basement route because the east stairwell was being waxed. She told herself the cart needed inventorying. She told herself a great many things while descending fourteen steps she did not count, and none of the things held, and at the bottom, in the flickering light at the end of Row F–K, Ilse Vann was already waiting, seated now on a folding chair she must have brought down herself, with two cups of tea steaming on an upturned crate beside her.

Two cups.

"I took the liberty," Ilse said, "of assuming you'd pretend to be surprised."

August stood at the mouth of the aisle with her mop like a soldier who'd forgotten which war she'd enlisted in. The old woman patted the second crate. On the shelf beside her lay a single index card, blank side up, waiting.

"Sit down, Gussie," Ilse Vann said. "Before your curiosity leaks all over my floor. I'm told it's very hard to clean."
