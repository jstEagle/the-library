## 6. First Lesson, Refused

The office was on the third floor, east corridor, and the work order said only: *Vann, I. — deep clean, do not discard papers.*

Do not discard papers. As if anyone would. As if anyone else came up here at all.

August stood in the doorway with her cart and let the room introduce itself. A single window, filmed with grime, letting in a gray that made everything look like it was already being remembered rather than seen. Shelves to the ceiling. Boxes stacked in the corner with dates on them going back further than August had been alive. A desk buried under index cards, and a chair with a blanket folded over its back like someone expected to be cold for a long time.

She had said no. Two nights ago, in the basement, among the empty shelves, Ilse Vann had asked and August had said no, flatly, the way you close a door with your body still in the room. *I clean here. That's all I do here.* And the old woman had looked at her with something that wasn't disappointment—disappointment would have been easier, disappointment you could carry out with the trash—and said, in that accent that put the weight at the front of every word, "You said no the way my daughter used to say no. Like a person practicing."

Then she had gone back to her syllables, reciting to the shelves, and August had pushed her cart away down the corridor with her heart going like a metronome set too fast.

So: she had said no. And now she was here, on a Tuesday, cleaning the office of a woman whose request she had refused, and she could not have explained to anyone—including herself—why the work order had felt like an invitation, or why she had taken it off the board before Marisol could claim it.

The ledger, she told herself. This was just the ledger. Room by room, stain by stain, the inventory she kept in her head of everything the conservatory had let go to rot. Third floor east had been on the list for two weeks. That was all this was. That was all any of it was.

She put on her gloves and started with the window.

* * *

The glass came away in gray sheets. Underneath, the city arranged itself in its ordinary distances—rooflines, a crane, the river holding the light the way a cupped hand holds water. August worked the squeegee in long strokes and tried to keep her mind on the strokes.

It did not stay there.

*Return,* the old woman had said, or the word that meant return, the word August had heard her reciting to the empty shelves before she'd known anyone was listening. *Aeneth.* August had looked it up—no, that wasn't true. There was nothing to look it up in. Vessic existed in no dictionary she could find, in no database, and when she'd asked Marisol about the archivist on three, Marisol had shrugged and said, *Vann? She's been here forever. She does the special collections. Nobody talks to her.* Nobody talks to her. As if that were the natural state of things, the arrangement of the world, and August had felt a shameful kinship with it.

She cleaned the window and did not think about the word. She was very good at not thinking about things. She had been practicing for twenty years and she had gotten so accomplished at it that the not-thinking had a kind of architecture, corridors of avoidance with doors that closed silently behind her, and she could walk the whole structure of her own mind without once opening the wrong one.

The concert hall was on the list for Thursday. She had already decided she would trade it. Marisol wanted Fridays; Marisol could have Thursdays. There was a system. The system worked.

She moved to the shelves.

* * *

Dusting the shelves took an hour, because the shelves were a lie. They looked like the shelves of any archive—spines, boxes, labels—but the labels were in a script August didn't recognize, thin vertical strokes crossed with marks like birds' feet, and when she pulled a box to dust beneath it, the contents were not books but cards. Thousands of cards. Handwritten. Each one a word.

She held one up to the gray light.

The front had the bird-track script. The back had, in a small, precise, old-fashioned hand: *selleth — the shape a room keeps after everyone has left it.*

August put the card back in the box with a care that surprised her, the way you put down something that might be asleep.

She dusted the next box. Also cards. The next. Cards. An entire language, it seemed, filed like a herbarium, each specimen pressed flat and labeled, the whole of it dependent on one woman's handwriting to mean anything at all. August thought: *when she dies, this becomes decoration.* She thought it in the flat inventory voice she used for stains and broken fixtures, and then she heard what she'd thought, and stood very still in the dust-smelling aisle between the shelves.

When she dies, this becomes decoration.

She had known the woman for six days. She had refused her four days ago. And some part of her, some part she hadn't authorized, had already begun doing the arithmetic of loss.

She finished the shelves. She did the floor. She did the baseboards, which had a gray felt of dust on them like the room had been wearing stockings. And then there was only the desk, and the desk was the trap.

* * *

She saw that immediately. That was the thing about Ilse Vann—you learned fast that nothing about her was accidental. The desk surface was covered in index cards, dozens of them, laid out in no order August could find, face down. Every other surface in the office was organized to the point of devotion; the cards on the desk were scattered like a dropped deck. It looked like mess. It was bait.

August stood over the desk with her dust cloth and did not touch the cards.

She cleaned around them. She cleaned under the desk, the chair, the legs of the lamp. She wiped the blotter's exposed margins. The cards sat there, face down, patient as stones, and August understood that the woman had left them out for her—had known, somehow, that August would be sent up here, or had arranged the sending, or had simply set the table and waited, the way you set a table for someone you know will come, because they always come, because hunger is a schedule too.

*Do not discard papers,* the work order said. The work order was in Ilse's handwriting. August recognized it now from the backs of the cards.

She could leave the cards exactly where they were. That was the correct move. The disciplined move. She had said no; the no had been clean; a no that stayed no was the only kind worth having. Her whole family had been built out of nos that stayed nos. Nobody said the thing, and because nobody said the thing, nothing had to be true, and because nothing had to be true, everyone could keep setting the table, year after year, for a meal no one would eat.

She wiped the desk's edge. One card had shifted, or had been placed, half off the stack, its corner hanging over the edge like a word left unfinished.

August looked at the window. The gray had thickened toward evening. Down in the practice rooms someone was playing scales, ascending, patient, the sound arriving through the floors like water through stone.

She turned the card over.

*aeneth*

That was all the front said, in the bird-track script, and the back, in the small precise hand, said: *return. also: regret. the word does not distinguish. a thing you come back to is a thing you are sorry for; the language assumes this is one motion.*

August read it three times. The scales below her climbed and climbed and never arrived.

*Return. Also: regret.*

Her father had come back to the house the last year, after the hearings, after the name in the papers had stopped being news and become furniture. He had sat in his chair by the window the way he'd sat there all her childhood, and the family had arranged itself around his sitting there, and no one had said *why are you back* or *what did you do* or the question, the real one, the one the family had been built to never build. And August, seventeen, had watched him sitting there and had felt—what? She had never found the English word. She had tried them all. Guilt was too heavy, too much hers. Regret was too light, too much his. Longing wasn't it either. It was a thing you came back to, over and over, a room you kept re-entering, and it was sorry, and it was home, and the language she'd been given had no word that could hold both at once, so she had held neither, and had put it down, and had walked away from the whole house of it.

*aeneth.*

The word did not distinguish.

She was still holding the card when she understood that she was holding the card, that her gloves were off—when had she taken them off?—that the dust cloth lay on the blotter and her bare thumb was resting on the handwriting as if it were braille, as if she could read the woman through the ink.

She put the card down. She picked it up. She put it down again.

Then she pocketed it.

It was theft. She knew it was theft; the word for it was theft, and the language she'd been given was very clear about that. But the card had been left face down on a desk in an empty office in a corridor where nobody talks to her, and it had been left, August was almost sure, the way you leave a door unlatched—*aeneth*, a thing you come back to—and she slid it into the chest pocket of her work shirt, over the left side, where it lay against her like a small warm weight, like a coal, like a name.

She finished the office in silence. She did not dust the desk again.

* * *

The cart's wheels squeaked on the third-floor linoleum, and August pushed it toward the service elevator and let herself, for the length of the corridor, imagine the conversation she was not going to have.

*I took one of your cards.*

*I know,* the old woman would say, not looking up. *I counted.*

*I said no.*

*You said no like a person practicing. I told you.*

*I'm not your student.*

*No,* Ilse Vann would say, and here August's imagination failed her, because she could not decide whether the old woman would say it with victory or with grief, and the not-knowing was its own kind of answer.

The elevator came. She rode down through the building's strata—third floor, archives, the dead language filed like pressed flowers; second floor, practice rooms, the scales finally stopping, replaced by a Chopin étude played badly by someone who was not her and would never be her; first floor, the lobby with its marble and its donor plaques, the century of the place engraved in brass. She stood in the elevator with her hand flat over her pocket and felt the card there, and felt, beneath the card, the other thing, the older thing, the question her family had arranged itself around, and for the first time in twenty years the two weights did not feel like the same weight.

That frightened her more than either one alone.

The doors opened on the lobby. Marisol was at the sign-in desk, eating sunflower seeds and doing the crossword. "Three east go okay? Vann's a weird one. She called down this morning, wanted to know who was assigned to her office."

August's hand did not move toward her pocket. Twenty years of practice. "Why?"

"Wanted to know if it was the new girl." Marisol filled in a square without looking up. "Said, tell her the desk is dusty."

August stood very still. Somewhere above them, three floors up, an old woman was sitting in a chair with a blanket over its back, in a room she had deliberately not had cleaned, beside a desk she had deliberately left scattered, having counted her cards and found one missing, and having called down not to complain but to leave a message in the only language she had left: *the desk is dusty.* Come back. The desk is dusty. A thing you come back to.

"Tell her," August heard herself say, and her voice came out level, which cost her something, "I'll be up Thursday."

Marisol shrugged and returned to her crossword, and August pushed her cart out into the night, through the service door, into the cold, with one stolen word over her heart and a standing appointment she had not agreed to, and did not cancel, and knew—already knew, in the place where the ledger was kept, in the room of corridors and silently closing doors—that one of the doors had just failed to close behind her, and that she could hear, faintly, through it, someone reciting syllables to empty shelves, and that she had stopped, in the corridor of her own mind, and was listening.
