## 15. The Break-In of Memory

The lesson that Thursday began with the word for *threshold* and ended with Ilse Vann looking at August Coldharbour as though she were a stranger who had wandered into the wrong basement.

They had been working for an hour. August had her notebook open, her pencil worn to a stub, the grammar of Vessic spread across the page like a map of a country she was learning to walk in. Ilse sat across from her in the archivist's office, hands folded on the desk beside a tape recorder she had brought out of a locked drawer but not yet turned on.

"Say it again," Ilse said. "Slowly."

"*Soveth kaelin.*"

"And what does it do?"

"It names the actor." August had learned this much by now, learned it in her bones. "It says: I crossed."

"Says who crossed."

"I crossed."

Ilse nodded, satisfied, and for a moment her eyes held the particular brightness they took on when the language moved through her cleanly—August had come to think of it as the look of a river running unobstructed. Then the brightness guttered.

Ilse's gaze slid off her. Not away—off, the way water slides off waxed cloth. She looked at August's face, then at the notebook, then at her own hands, and her brow creased with the mild, courteous puzzlement of a woman trying to place someone at a funeral.

"I'm sorry," Ilse said. "Have we—forgive me. Are you here about the Hollis bequest? The papers came in last week, I haven't finished—"

August's pencil stopped moving.

"Ilse."

"Yes?" Polite. Patient. A stranger's patience.

"It's August. We're in the middle of a lesson. You're teaching me Vessic."

Something flickered behind Ilse's eyes—a fish turning under ice, there and gone. "Vessic," she repeated, tasting it. "That's—" And then, softly, with wonder rather than fear: "I know Vessic."

"You're the last one who does."

"The last one," Ilse said, as if confirming an appointment. Then she frowned again, and looked at the door, and said, "You should go, dear. I have a student coming."

The words went through August like a needle finding bone. *I have a student coming.* As if August were the interruption, the delay, the thing standing between Ilse and the lesson that mattered.

She gathered her notebook. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; inside, something was howling. She stood, and Ilse watched her stand with that same courteous blankness, and August made it four steps toward the door before—

"*Aeneth.*"

August stopped.

Ilse had said it quietly, almost to herself, looking at nothing. But when August turned, Ilse's eyes had changed. The ice had broken. The river ran.

"Aeneth," Ilse said again, and now her voice had its full weight, its terrible precision. "Return. Regret. Both at once, always both—you pocketed the card three weeks ago and you still carry it in your left coat pocket, you check it on your breaks, you think I don't see." She leaned forward. "August Coldharbour. Sit down."

August sat. Her legs made the decision without consulting her.

"How long," August managed, "has it been like that?"

"Like what?" Ilse asked, and for one vertiginous second August thought the break-in had happened again—but no, Ilse's mouth curved, dry as paper. "Like that. I see. Yes." She looked at her hands. "It comes and goes. Lately it comes more than it goes. The doctor has a word for it. I told him his word is English and English is generous with words that mean nothing." She lifted her chin. "But you should understand what just happened, because it will happen again, and next time I may not come back from it. When I forget, I forget forward. I lose the most recent things first. Do you know what that means?"

August shook her head.

"It means you are the most recent thing." Ilse said it flatly, factually, the way she said everything. "My daughter is sixty years deep. My husband, forty. The conservatory, thirty. You are two months old. So when the tide goes out, you are the first thing it takes." She met August's eyes. "And the language—I have kept it so long, carried it so carefully, that it sits deeper than any of it. Deeper than my daughter's name. Some nights I cannot tell you where I was born, but I can decline every verb in Vessic. It is the last country I live in. And it is sinking too. Slowly. Like everything else."

The tape recorder sat between them on the desk. August looked at it.

"You brought it out," she said.

"I did."

"You didn't turn it on."

"No." Ilse's fingers rested on the machine without pressing anything. "Because once I record the last sentence, it exists outside me. And once it exists outside me, I am permitted—by whatever keeps the books in me—to let go of it. I have seen what happens to people after they finish their work, August. They fold up like chairs." Her jaw tightened. "I am not ready to be furniture."

"Then don't record it."

"Don't record it, and it dies with me. In a hospital room or a memory ward or wherever they put women who misplace decades. Recorded, it survives me. Unrecorded, I survive it—for a little while." She laughed, short and bitter. "There is your *aeneth*, child. Every choice in this room means both things at once. I thought you'd have learned that by now."

August looked at the recorder. At the woman across from her, who had called her by a dead girl's name once already, who confused decades, who was losing herself forward while August sat there hoarding her own past backward. Two women guarding ruins from opposite directions.

"What sentence?" August asked. "The last living one. You've mentioned it twice now. What is it?"

Ilse was quiet for a long moment. The boiler ticked somewhere in the walls—the building's old heart, keeping its uneven time.

"It isn't a proverb," she said finally. "It isn't wisdom. People expect dying languages to be full of proverbs, little embroidered pillows of wisdom. No. It's a sentence my grandmother said to my mother, and my mother said to me, the night before we each left home. It's a sentence spoken at thresholds. And it has never been written down, because Vessic was never fully written—it lived in mouths, and mouths close." She drew a breath. "It is the last sentence in the world that anyone can say correctly. And I am the only mouth it lives in."

"Then say it to me. Now. Before the machine, before anything. Say it and I'll hold it."

Ilse looked at her for a long time. Something moved across her face—not the forgetting, something older. Fear, maybe. Or the shape fear leaves after it has lived in a house for years.

"If I say it to you," she said, "and you carry it wrong—if you carry it even slightly wrong—then the wrong version becomes the last version. Forever. There is no one to correct you. There is no one to correct me anymore either, do you understand? For forty years I have been the only proofreader of my mother tongue. Every drift, every slip, every lazy vowel—I have no way of knowing it's a drift. I might be speaking a beautiful corruption and calling it Vessic." Her voice thinned. "So no. I won't say it into the air and hope. I'll say it into the machine, where it can't be argued with. And I'll teach it to you syllable by syllable until your mouth makes the same sounds as mine. That is the only honest transmission left. Machine and mouth. Nothing else."

"When?"

"Soon." Ilse pressed her lips together. "Not today. Today I forgot your name an hour ago and my mouth isn't trustworthy. Tomorrow, maybe. Or the day after. I'll send for you."

"Ilse—"

"I said soon." And then, softer, with the first crack August had ever heard in her: "I need one good night. One night where the tide stays out and I can find all of it, the whole sentence, end to end, the way my mother gave it to me. Let me have that. Then I'll spend it."

August walked back up through the building in the grey of early morning, past the practice rooms where someone was failing beautifully at a Rachmaninov prelude, and she understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that the race had started whether they'd agreed to it or not. That somewhere in the basement an old woman was holding a sentence the way you hold a door against wind—and that the wind was already inside the house.

* * *

Ilse sent for her on Saturday.

The note came up with the morning mail cart, folded into a cleaning-supply invoice where no one would look: *Come at midnight. Bring nothing. —I.*

August came at midnight. The conservatory at that hour was a held breath—piano strings cooling, radiators knocking their slow code, the whole vast building dreaming its repertoire. She let herself into the archive office with the key Ilse had given her a week earlier, pressed into her palm without comment, and found the room transformed.

Every shelf had been cleared. The index cards, the accession ledgers, the boxes of Hollis bequest papers—all of it stacked against the far wall under a dust sheet, as if the room had been stripped for surgery. In the center, under the single desk lamp, sat the tape recorder, and beside it a glass of water, and beside that Ilse Vann, upright in her chair, dressed as if for church.

"You came," Ilse said. "Good. Sit. We have perhaps two hours before my tide turns—I can feel it tonight, the way you feel weather in a scar. Tonight the water is low. Tonight I can see the whole country." She tapped the recorder. "Press the button when I nod. Not before. And August—" her voice dropped, went grave, "—whatever happens tonight, whatever I say afterward, however far I drift, you do not stop the recording and you do not leave the room. Promise me."

"I promise."

"Say it in Vessic."

August searched her memory for the grammar that named its actor. "*Kaelin soveth,*" she said slowly. "I promise. I cross."

Ilse closed her eyes briefly. "Close enough," she whispered. "One day it will be exact. Now."

August pressed the button. The reels began to turn with a small, patient sound, like something breathing.

"My name is Ilse Vann," Ilse said to the machine, clear and formal, a voice addressing posterity. "Born in Velika Morava province, in a house that no longer stands, in a language that almost doesn't. What follows is the last living sentence of Vessic, given as my mother gave it to me, on the night I left home, in the year—"

She stopped.

August's stomach dropped. "Ilse?"

"No." Ilse raised a hand, sharp. "No, it's here, it's here—" Her eyes had gone wide, fixed on the middle distance, and her lips moved silently, retrieving, hauling on a rope hand over hand. "The year. The year my mother—no. No, the year doesn't matter, the year is English, the year is a cage—" She gripped the edge of the desk. "Listen. Don't speak. If I lose the thread, don't speak, don't offer me anything, silence is a banked fire, silence keeps it—"

August said nothing. The reels turned.

Thirty seconds. A minute. The lamp hummed. Somewhere above them a door boomed shut, and neither of them moved.

Then Ilse exhaled, long and shaking, and when she spoke again her voice had changed—lower, rounder, carrying an accent August had never heard from her, an accent from underneath all her other accents, from the bottom of the well.

"*Sovennath kae ilen varo seth,*" she said. "*Sovennath kae ilen varo seth.*"

She said it a third time, slower, carving each syllable. Then she opened her eyes and looked at August, and they were wet.

"Do you hear what it does?" she whispered. "Six words. Listen to the grammar. *Sovennath*—what remains. *Kae*—when. *Ilen*—the speaker. *Varo*—goes. *Seth*—" her voice caught, steadied, "—*seth* is the word that has no English twin. It means the speech itself. Not the words. The speech. The living act of saying. What remains when the speaker is gone—is the speech. Not the memory of it. Not the record of it. The speech. It means that a language is not a possession. It is an event that keeps happening as long as someone is happening it." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, impatiently, as if tears were an administrative error. "My mother said it to me meaning: you are leaving, I am staying, but what I have given you goes with you, and so I am not lost. And I have spent seventy years understanding that she also meant: I am giving you the burden, and I am sorry, and I would do it again."

The reels turned. Ilse straightened in her chair.

"Now," she said, "we begin. I will say it once more, whole. Then syllable by syllable, and you will repeat each one, and I will correct you, and you will hate me, and we will continue. By dawn your mouth will make these sounds. And then—" she paused, and for a moment the fear crossed her face again, plain as daylight, "—and then it will live in two places. Which is one more place than it lives in now."

She said it once more, whole. Then she broke it open.

"*So-ven-nath.*"

"*So-ven-nath.*"

"Your *nath* is lazy. Again. *Nath* is a door closing, not a door drifting. *So-ven-NATH.*"

"*So-ven-NATH.*"

"*Kae.*"

"*Kae.*"

Hour by hour, syllable by syllable, the sentence passed between them, and August felt it settling into her mouth like a stone into a riverbed—strange at first, then native. Outside, the sky over the practice-room windows went from black to iron to the pale bruise of nearly-morning. Neither of them noticed. Ilse's voice grew rougher as the hours went on, and twice she paused mid-syllable with a frown, groping—and twice she found it again, hauling it up from the deep water, gasping, furious, alive.

On the eleventh repetition, near dawn, she stopped abruptly.

"That's enough," she said. "Stop. Don't say it again tonight."

"Why? Did I—"

"Because it's right. Your mouth has it. And because—" she looked at the recorder, still turning, and her face did something complicated, "—because I can feel the tide coming in. Early today. I can feel it." She reached out and pressed the stop button herself, gently, the way you close a coffin. "Go home, August. Sleep. Come back tomorrow evening and we'll begin teaching your hands the notebooks. There are fragments in my files, other pieces, a vocabulary of nine hundred words that exist nowhere else, and if the sentence is the heart, the rest is the body, and I intend—" 

She stopped. Mid-word. Her eyes went to August's face and the courtesy came down over them like a shade.

"I'm sorry, dear," Ilse said pleasantly. "Have we—forgive me. Are you here about the Hollis bequest?"

August sat very still. On the desk between them, the reels of the recorder stood quiet, holding six words in magnetic ribbon. Holding them the way a jar holds smoke.

"The lesson's over, Ilse," August said softly. "You should rest. I'll see you tomorrow evening."

"Wonderful," said Ilse Vann, the last speaker of Vessic, smiling at a stranger in her own office. "Do give my regards to whoever sent you."

August gathered her notebook with hands that were not quite steady, and walked out of the basement, and did not let herself cry until the stairwell, because the building had ears and some of what remained of Vessic was still walking around inside it in her mouth.

By evening she would learn why Ilse hadn't answered her door.
