## 17. The Empty Chair

The call came at six in the morning, which was wrong on its face because Ilse Vann did not use telephones. August learned this in the worst way: the voice on the other end belonged to a nurse at St. Ambrose, a woman with the flat kindness of someone who delivered bad news on a schedule, asking for "the student" — and August had to stand in the boiler room with her mop dripping onto her boot and say, yes, that was her, she was the student.

*Student.* The word arrived like a key turning in a lock she hadn't known was there.

She took the bus because her hands were not steady enough for anything requiring judgment. Through the window the city did its morning arithmetic — bakeries opening, men hosing sidewalks, a dog tied outside a pharmacy watching everything go by with the patience of the abandoned. August counted streetlights to keep from counting worse things.

At the hospital she gave Ilse's name at the desk and was directed to the fourth floor, where the corridor smelled of antiseptic and overcooked carrots, and where a nurse with a clipboard told her, gently, that Ms. Vann had been found confused in the archive stacks the previous evening, dehydrated, disoriented, insisting she had an appointment.

"She kept saying she couldn't be late," the nurse said. "For a lesson."

August's throat closed. She managed: "Is she—"

"Sleeping. She has good hours and bad ones. The doctor will talk to you about the rest." The nurse looked at her more closely then, the way people do when they are trying to place a face against a memory of news. "You're family?"

"No," August said. Then, because the grammar of this new language would not permit evasion: "I'm her student."

---

They let her in at nine.

Ilse looked smaller than a person was supposed to look, folded into the hospital bed like a letter into an envelope, her hands — those hands that had moved through the air all winter, conducting syllables out of the silence — lying still on the blanket as if set down carefully by someone else. An IV line ran into her arm. Her white hair was uncombed, which August had never seen, and the sight of it undid something in her chest that she had been holding shut since the phone call.

But when August sat in the chair beside the bed, Ilse's eyes opened, and they were clear. Blue-gray, sharp, appalled at their surroundings.

"Gussie," she said. The childhood nickname no one used anymore. "They've put me in a room with no books. I want it noted that this is barbarism."

"You're in a hospital."

"I'm aware. I've been fighting them since Tuesday." Her eyes tracked the window, the light, came back. "Tuesday. Is it Tuesday?"

"It's Thursday."

"Thursday," Ilse repeated, tasting it. Something flickered behind her face — a door opening onto a long dark corridor. "Then we've lost two days."

"We haven't lost anything. You need to rest."

"I need," Ilse said, with the exact intonation she used in the basement when correcting August's pronunciation, "to finish the recording."

The word landed in the room and stayed there. August had heard about the recordings only in fragments — Ilse's project, decades old, reels and cassettes and finally a digital recorder she'd bought secondhand, capturing vocabulary, songs, the grammar she carried alone in her skull. But there was one thing, Ilse had said once, that could not be recorded in pieces. One thing that had to be spoken whole or not at all.

"The last living sentence," August said quietly.

"The last living sentence." Ilse closed her eyes. For a moment August thought she'd fallen asleep, but her lips were moving, shaping sounds too faint to catch, rehearsing against the inside of her own skull. When she opened her eyes again they were wet and furious. "I got halfway through it Wednesday night. In here. Before they sedated me." She lifted one hand off the blanket, an inch, a gesture of terrible smallness. "Halfway, Gussie. A sentence is a living thing. Half of it is a corpse."

"There'll be other chances. When you're stronger—"

"When I'm stronger." Ilse laughed, a dry rustle. "Child, I have watched my mind lose whole decades the way a tide takes a beach. It does not negotiate. It does not return what it takes. On Monday I called my daughter to tell her supper was ready. My daughter has been dead eleven years." She said it without self-pity, the way you'd report weather. "I know what's coming for me. What I don't know is whether the sentence comes with me or stays behind. That part is up to us."

*Us.* August looked at the empty water pitcher, the blank walls, the machines keeping their patient vigil, and felt the full weight of the pronoun settle onto her shoulders.

"The recorder," she said. "Where is it?"

"In my office. Third drawer of the desk, under the index cards. There's a cassette — the green case. And the notebooks." Ilse's grip found August's wrist, surprisingly strong. "The notebooks matter more. Everything I ever wrote down, I wrote it wrong on purpose."

August stared. "Wrong on purpose."

"A language dies when it can be read by strangers." Ilse's voice dropped, conspiratorial, the archivist surfacing through the patient. "I built errors into every transcription. Substitutions. Reversals. A cipher only I could undo — and now, apparently, only we." Her hand tightened. "Bring me the green cassette. Bring me everything. And Gussie—" the clarity in her eyes wavered, just slightly, a candle feeling a draft, "—hurry."

---

* * *

The basement office looked ransacked, though August knew it wasn't. The conservatory's administrators had come with their clipboards and their boxes, cataloguing the effects of an employee who might not be returning, and their tidy violence was everywhere: files squared into stacks, the reading lamp unplugged and coiled, Ilse's shawl folded with institutional precision on the back of her chair.

The chair itself was empty. August stood in the doorway and could not make herself walk past it.

In the end she didn't. She went around, along the wall, the way you skirt a grave.

The third drawer held what Ilse had promised: the digital recorder with its worn buttons, and beneath a stratum of index cards — *aeneth*, in her own handwriting, meaning both *return* and *regret*; August touched that one and put it back — a cassette in a green plastic case labeled in fading ink: *FOR THE END. NOT BEFORE.*

She also took the notebooks. Eleven of them, cloth-bound, their spines cracked, dating back — judging by the first one's flyleaf — to 1963. She carried them up to the night janitor's closet that served as her office, spread them across the folding table under the fluorescent hum, and opened the first page.

It was gibberish.

Not entirely — she recognized the alphabet Ilse had taught her, the lean characters that mapped sound so much more faithfully than English ever managed. But the words made no sense. Sentences began and refused to finish. Nouns appeared where verbs should live. Every few lines a marginal note in English: *check p. 40. no — see 12. WRONG, fix later.* And *later* had never come, or had come and been undone, or had come disguised as another error stacked atop the first.

August sat back. Somewhere above her, a piano was being practiced badly — scales, halting, a beginner's unevenness — and the sound filtered down through the building's bones like rain.

Her phone buzzed. A text from Theo:

*heard ms vann is in st ambrose. is it true?*

She stared at it for a long time. The old ledger in her head — the one that catalogued stains and rooms and unsaid things, the one that had governed twenty years of her life — whispered its familiar counsel: *say nothing, need nothing, owe nothing.*

She typed: *Yes. Can you come tonight? I need help.*

Three dots appeared instantly, vanished, reappeared. Then: *already on the bus.*

---

Theo arrived at ten with two coffees and a backpack full of graph paper, and stood in the doorway of the janitor's closet taking in the notebooks with an expression August recognized from mirrors: hunger held carefully in check.

"You're sure you want me seeing this?"

"Ilse built a cipher into everything she wrote. Two heads untangle faster than one." August slid a notebook toward him. "And I can't do it alone."

"Why not?" He asked it without malice, which was the problem; Theo asked things the way children ask things, straight at the center. "You clearly read music, you clearly know languages, you clearly—"

"Theo."

"Sorry." He sat down. He didn't look sorry. He looked like someone who had decided to be patient, which was worse.

They worked. It went slowly, then less slowly. The cipher, once you suspected its logic, began to confess: Ilse reversed certain syllable pairs, substituted others according to a scheme keyed to dates — the notebook from 1974 used one substitution table, 1988 another, each table hidden in plain sight as an "errata list" in the back matter. Whoever decoded one book had won nothing; each volume demanded its own key, and the keys themselves were written in Vessic, in sentences whose meanings shifted depending on which earlier errors you'd already corrected.

"It's beautiful," Theo said at midnight, awed despite himself. "It's horrible. It's — she made the language defend itself."

"She made *herself* defend herself." August rubbed her eyes. "Think about what she lived through. Wars, borders, everyone who spoke it scattered or dead. She watched people ask her to perform her language like a trick. So she wrote it down in a form only patience could open." She paused. "Only love, maybe. Patience and love are nearly the same labor."

Theo glanced at her — quickly, then away — and said nothing, which was its own kind of fluency.

By two in the morning they had reconstructed perhaps a third of the relevant material: the phonetic framework of the final sentence, its opening phrases, the grammatical scaffolding. The middle remained locked in a notebook from 1991 whose errata page referenced a table "in the blue folder," and there was no blue folder in the office. August had searched twice.

"The hospital," Theo said suddenly. "If she knew she was going in — if she grabbed things—"

"They wouldn't let her take files." But August was already reaching for her coat. "Her handbag, though. They'd have logged it. Personal effects."

They looked at each other across the table littered with graph paper and cold coffee, and August understood that the girl who had once walked out of a concert hall and out of her own life was now, at fifty-three, about to break into a dying woman's belongings at three in the morning, and that she felt no reluctance at all — only a clean, unfamiliar purpose, like a note finally finding its pitch.

"Come on," she said. "Visiting hours start at eight. We're going to be first in line."

---

* * *

St. Ambrose at dawn was quieter than the conservatory, which should not have been possible and yet was. They rode the elevator up with a cart of breakfast trays and a nurse who assumed they were students on clinical rotation, and neither of them corrected her.

Ilse was awake. Worse — Ilse was *lucid*, propped against her pillows with the fierce brightness of a bulb before it blows, and when August came through the door carrying the notebooks, the old woman's face did something complicated: relief and grief and pride arriving all at once, none of them able to speak first.

"You went into my office," Ilse said.

"You told me to."

"I told you to bring the cassette. These—" her eyes moved over the stack in August's arms, "—these are thirty years of traps. Did you cut yourself?"

"A little. Theo helped." August set the notebooks on the overbed table. "We're missing a substitution table. 1991. You wrote that it was in a blue folder."

Something moved behind Ilse's eyes — a searchlight sweeping a shoreline. "Blue," she repeated. "Blue. The correspondence file. The one with Marta's letters." Her daughter's name landed in the room and both of them flinched around it. "No. No, that's not — the blue folder is the *grammar*. Why would I put the grammar with—"

"Ilse." August pulled the chair close and sat, taking the old woman's hands in hers. They were cold. "Look at me. We don't need the whole grammar. We need one table. One page. Do you remember where it is? Anywhere. A copy. Anything."

Ilse's hands turned over and gripped back, hard. For a moment her focus held — August could feel her mind working, could almost hear the shelves of decades being climbed. Then the grip loosened by degrees, and the light in her eyes diffused, and she said, in a voice gone suddenly distant and gentle:

"Marta, sweetheart, supper's ready. Wash your hands."

August did not let go. Across the bed, Theo had gone very still.

"Ilse. It's Gussie. Your student. I'm here."

A long silence. The machines kept their small faithful time. Then Ilse blinked, and the present returned to her face like a tide coming in, and she looked down at their joined hands with an expression of utter clarity.

"He's in the handbag," she said. "The table. Folded inside the cover of my prayer book, because no administrator on earth searches a prayer book." Her mouth curved, dry as paper. "Sentimental of me. Useful, too. Ask the charge nurse for her personal effects — you're listed as next of kin."

"I'm not your next of kin."

"You are now. I told them this morning." Ilse released her hands and settled back against the pillows, exhausted by the effort of staying, but her eyes stayed fixed on August's with the gravity of a final instruction. "Gussie. Listen. The sentence is not finished because a sentence like that one cannot be finished by the speaker alone. That was always its design. It's a sentence with a door in it. I was waiting — all these years, I told myself I was preserving, but I was *waiting* — for someone to walk through."

August's chest hurt. "Waiting for what?"

"For the language to choose." Ilse smiled, and closed her eyes. "Go home. Sleep. Come back at noon and we'll begin. And Gussie—" her voice was already fraying at the edges, sleep pulling her under, but the words came clear, "—when they offer you the stage, and they will, because institutions love a resurrection story more than they love a person — decide *before* you're standing in the lights what you will and will not give them."

August sat without moving until her breathing evened into sleep. Then she gathered the notebooks, and Theo held the door, and in the corridor he said, quietly:

"What stage? What's she talking about?"

August looked at him. Down the hall, a bulletin board announced, in letters of celebratory gold:

**THE CONSERVATORY CENTENNIAL GALA — ONE HUNDRED YEARS OF MUSIC — HONORING OUR LEGACY**

Below it, a smaller card, freshly pinned: *Director's Office: meeting requests for gala programming.*

"I don't know yet," August said. Which was true. But her hands, as she signed the visitor log, were perfectly steady, and some part of her — the part that had spent twenty years learning silences — already recognized the shape of what was coming for her, the way you recognize weather by the smell of the air.

They had until Saturday.
