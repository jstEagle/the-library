## 4. The Auction Catalogue

The envelope sat on my kitchen table for two days before I opened it.

I told myself this was scheduling, not avoidance. I had pianos to tune — the Halloway upright on Tuesday, the church's aging Bechstein on Wednesday, a baby grand in a solicitor's office that had gone sour in its middle register and needed an hour of my undivided attention. Work is a kind of shelter. I have built most of my life inside it. There is always another piano, another room, another set of hammers waiting to be voiced, and as long as my hands are occupied my mind has permission to be elsewhere. Thirty years of practice. I could tune out anything.

But envelopes don't have strings to pluck or pins to set. An envelope just sits there, being what it is.

It was thick, cream-colored, with a printed return address: Merrow & Finch, Auctioneers and Valuers, Ashcombe Road. My name and Ivy's, jointly, in a font that tried very hard to look like handwriting. Someone at the firm had done their research. Or Ivy had given them my address, which was more likely, and which meant she'd held the paper with my name on it in her hands before passing it along into the post, and hadn't called, hadn't written *Dear Silas* herself, hadn't added so much as a Post-it. Just forwarded me my own inheritance like a piece of admin.

I opened it on Thursday morning, standing up, with coffee going cold beside me, because I'd learned by then that if you sit down with a thing like that it gets comfortable and refuses to leave.

Inside was the auction catalogue. Glossy, heavy stock, twenty-eight pages. The Fairweather Glasshouse Estate: freehold land, structures, and residual contents, to be sold at auction on the fourteenth of June. A map. Terms of sale. A schedule of lots. And photographs.

I want to say I looked at the photographs last. That would be tidy, and I am nothing if not tidy. But the truth is my eye went straight to them, the way a tongue goes to a broken tooth. You can't help it. The body wants the damage confirmed.

There were six photographs. The first three were recent — taken, I guessed, within the last month, from the angles a surveyor chooses: wide, flat, uninterested. The glasshouse in these pictures was exactly what I remembered leaving it as, which is to say a ruin wearing a disguise of neglect. Panes missing in their hundreds, the remaining glass fogged white with thirty years of grime. The iron ribs of the roof exposed like the carcass of some enormous animal that had lain down in the field and been picked clean. Ivy's bindweed — no, not Ivy's, the field's bindweed — climbing the south wall in green ropes. In one photo a crow sat on the ridge beam, and I remember thinking the photographer must have been pleased about that. Ruin photography loves a crow.

Then the fourth photograph.

The caption said: *The Fairweather Glasshouse, c. 1991. From the collection of the late R. Penhale.* And I understood, before my eyes even finished taking it in, that this was the week. Not the day — nobody photographed the day, or if they did, those pictures didn't survive into county archives — but the week. The light was high summer. The tomatoes were staked and heavy. Everything in the frame was still standing, still glazed, still whole.

My father built that glasshouse the year I was seven. Two full summers of weekends, him out there with his levels and his string lines, humming — he hummed constantly, tunelessly, a habit I inherited without meaning to and have never once caught myself doing. He built it from salvaged window frames and secondhand glass, and when it was finished our mother filled it the way other women fill a kitchen. Seedlings in trays on every bench. Tomatoes up the strings. Her hands in everything, soil to the wrists, all summer long.

I stood in my kitchen holding the catalogue at arm's length for a while, the way you hold something hot.

Because here is the thing about memory that no one tells you until it ambushes them: it isn't a record. It's a garden. Things get planted in it, and things get pulled out, and after enough seasons you can no longer tell which plants came up wild and which ones you put there yourself on purpose. For thirty years I had kept a particular bed of that particular summer weeded down to almost nothing. I knew the shape of what I'd removed. I knew where the gaps were. I watered around them carefully, the way you water around a grave.

And then a photograph arrives, taken by a dead neighbor, filed in a county archive, printed by strangers who wanted only to show off a pretty building — and the weeds come back all at once, rooted in fact, dated, undeniable.

*

I sat down eventually. The coffee was beyond saving; I made fresh and didn't drink it.

In the photograph, the glasshouse ran the length of its plot, white ribs and green interior, the door propped open with the brick my father always used. Our mother stood near the open door in a cotton dress, mid-motion, slightly blurred — she had been walking toward whoever held the camera, and the shutter caught her between steps, so that she seems in the picture to be arriving forever. She was laughing. Or her mouth was open in a way that could be laughter; at this size, at this distance, through this much glass and time, it could also have been her calling out to us, telling us to come in, wash your hands, dinner's nearly ready. I chose, looking at it, to believe it was laughter. I noticed myself choosing. That's the strange part — that you can watch your own memory being manufactured in real time, and do it anyway.

I was not in the photograph. Neither was Ivy. Children are rarely in the pictures adults take of gardens; children are the reason the pictures exist but never the subject. We were somewhere behind the camera, or behind the house, or up in the apple tree whose branches crowd the top-left corner of the frame. Absent, and therefore safe. Photographs of that summer always seemed to leave me out, and I had come to think of it as the natural order: the boy who survived was also, conveniently, the boy who wasn't in the picture.

I looked at my mother for a long time. Then I looked past her, because that too was habit — look at the person, then look past the person, see what the background holds. A tuner does this with rooms. The piano is the client's story; the room is the truth. So I read the background the way I'd read a stranger's front room: the bench along the north wall, the watering cans, the coil of hose, the stack of seed trays under the staging, the bicycle leaning against the gable end—

The bicycle.

My father's bicycle. Black, heavy-framed, a basket on the front he used for seed sacks and trips to the hardware shop. It leaned against the gable end of the glasshouse at a slight angle, front wheel turned out, exactly as it leaned every day of my childhood. I knew that bicycle the way I knew the pitch of middle C. Seeing it was like hearing a familiar chord: no surprise in it, only recognition. *Yes. That's right. That's how it was.*

And then, further back — half-hidden behind the tomato rows, at the far end near the cold frames, where the light falls off and the film goes soft and grey —

A second bicycle.

*

Here is what I did next, and I record it precisely because it shames me: I closed the catalogue.

Not threw it down. Closed it, gently, aligned the covers, set it on the table, and walked into the hall to find my toolbag, because the Halloway upright was booked for eleven o'clock and Mrs. Halloway did not tolerate lateness, and work is a shelter, and I have lived in that shelter for thirty years and know every corner of it.

I tuned the Halloway upright. It's a 1962 overstrung, walnut case, played mostly by grandchildren at Christmas and mostly badly. I tuned it well. I always tune well; it's the one promise I've never broken. While I worked I thought about nothing, which is a skill, and which costs more than people imagine. Between movements — between the unisons and the octaves and the slow climb of the treble — the image surfaced anyway, the way a drowned thing surfaces when you're not watching the water. Second bicycle. Far end. Cold frames. Soft grey light.

Whose?

I knew whose. That was the problem. Some part of me that had spent three decades not knowing it knew, knew immediately, the way you recognize a voice through a wall before you've parsed a single word.

I finished the Halloway. I packed my tools. I drove home the long way, unnecessarily, and made lunch I didn't eat, and at two o'clock I opened the catalogue again, and this time I made myself look properly, the way I make myself listen to a piano that's been badly treated — patiently, without flinching, note by note.

*

The second bicycle was a woman's frame. Step-through. Lighter than my father's, with a rear rack, and a basket — no, not a basket, a child seat bolted over the rear wheel. I zoomed in with my reading glasses an inch from the page, and the halftone dots broke apart and reassembled and broke apart again, and still the facts held: step-through frame, rear rack, child seat.

Ours. Mine and Ivy's. One of us rode on that seat and one of us ran alongside, and we took turns, and the turns were negotiated with a seriousness that would have shamed the United Nations. Our mother pedaled and we argued and the lane went by, and that is the entire content of the memory, and it is a good memory, and I had thrown it away.

That's what I mean about curated amnesia. People imagine forgetting is passive, a fading. It isn't. It's gardening. Every summer of my adult life I went through the beds of that year and lifted out anything that might grow toward the fire — the heat, the shouting, the smell of smoke that I will not describe here because describing it gives it back to me — and I lifted out, indiscriminately, everything planted near it. The good things came up with the bad. The bicycle rides. The smell of tomato leaves on my fingers. My mother's hands. All of it, cleared, because grief doesn't weed selectively and neither did I.

And now here was the photograph, doing what photographs do, which is testify. Not the fire. Not the night. Just the week before — an ordinary Saturday, apparently, warm enough for cotton, the tomatoes staked, two bicycles against the glasshouse, one of them carrying a child seat that meant someone small was loved enough to be strapped in and pedaled home.

I sat with it until the light moved across the table.

Then I did the arithmetic I had been refusing to do since eleven o'clock that morning. If the photograph was the week before the fire — and the archive said it was — then the second bicycle was still there, or should have been, in the weeks after. Bicycles don't walk away. Somebody rode it or somebody left it or somebody took it, and in any case somebody would have seen it, handled it, known it. The fire reports listed our father's bicycle as recovered from the lean-to, scorched, tires melted to the rims. I know this because I read the reports once, aged nineteen, in a library, standing up, and never let myself read them again. They did not mention a second bicycle. I'm certain they didn't. I would have remembered — no. I wouldn't have. That's the whole point. I had spent thirty years making sure I wouldn't.

So either the report was incomplete, or the bicycle was already gone before the fire, or — and here my hand actually shook, here I set the catalogue flat on the table and pressed both palms down on it like a man steadying a lid — or the bicycle belonged to someone who was there that summer, in that garden, often enough to leave a bike against the wall, and whom I had edited out so thoroughly that a woman's bicycle in my own childhood garden looked, for one full morning, like a stranger's.

I thought of my mother, blurred mid-step, mouth open, arriving forever.

I thought of the cold frames at the far end, where she kept the cuttings, the things she was propagating in secret, the little glass houses within the glass house.

I thought: *who else's bike parks at our wall?*

And underneath the question, quieter, older, in the voice of the boy who hid among the seed trays while voices rose below: *you already know. You've always known. You just agreed, with yourself, never to hear it.*

The catalogue lay open on the table, glossy and indifferent, selling our ruin to strangers. Lot 1: freehold land. Lot 2: structures. Lot 3: residual contents. No lot for a second bicycle. No lot for the truth.

I got out my phone. I looked at Ivy's number, unchanged for eleven years, last exchanged messages: logistics, the funeral of an aunt neither of us had loved, three years ago. My thumb hovered. Outside, a car went past, and somewhere down the street a radiator ticked as a house cooled, saying the things houses say when no one listens.

I didn't call. Not yet. Instead I found a sheet of manuscript paper — I keep it for noting beat rates, the slow wobble of an out-of-tune string — and at the top I wrote the date of the photograph, and beneath it, in the margin where I usually mark cents sharp or flat, I wrote:

*Second bicycle. Whose?*

And below that, because the boy in me who listened through floors finally got a word in:

*Ask Ivy.*

* * *

That night I dreamed of the glasshouse, whole and glazed and summer-bright, and in the dream I was small and riding pillion on the woman's bicycle, my cheek against corduroy, and I woke with my face wet and no memory of crying, and the catalogue open on the table where I'd left it, my mother laughing — or calling — in the high summer light, and the far end of the picture soft and grey and patient, waiting for me to look again.

I put on the kettle. I did not look again. Not yet.

First, there was someone I had to call.
