## 10. Nell's Garden

Nell Okafor worked out of a converted garage on Wren Street, the kind of building that had once housed a man who fixed lawnmowers and now housed a woman who fixed landscapes. I knew this because Ivy told me, and because the whole town seemed to know everything about Nell Okafor these days—the woman from London with the grant money and the plans, the woman who wanted to buy a ruin and call it a garden.

I stood outside her door for four minutes. I know because I counted, the way I count when my hands need something to do that isn't shaking. Four minutes is long enough to walk away. It's also long enough to notice that you're not going to.

She opened the door before I knocked. She'd seen me through the window, probably. People like Nell see you coming.

"Silas Fairweather." She said it like she was confirming a tuning fork against a known pitch. "I hoped it would be you."

"You hoped."

"I don't chase people who don't want to be found. But I leave doors open." She stepped back. "Come in. The kettle's on."

The garage had been gutted and rebuilt—drawing boards, a long table scarred with craft-knife cuts, rolls of paper standing in a bin like rolled-up sleeping bags. Two mugs already sat on the table. She'd made tea for two before I'd decided to come. That should have frightened me more than it did.

"I called your shop," she said. "Your answering machine says you're available Tuesdays through Fridays."

"It says that."

"Today is Wednesday."

"So it is."

She poured the tea without asking how I took it. Milk, no sugar. She'd guessed right, or she'd asked someone—Ivy, maybe, or Mrs. Calloway at the post office, who has never in her life withheld information voluntarily. I took the mug and sat where she gestured, on a wooden chair that had been placed facing the big drawing table, which was covered by a sheet of tracing paper weighted down with brass washers.

Under the tracing paper, I could see lines. Straight ones, mostly. And curves that I recognized before I understood why I recognized them.

"That's—" I stopped.

"The original glasshouse plans," Nell said. "County archive. Your father filed them himself, 1978. His signature's on every sheet." She lifted the corner of the tracing paper so I could see the title block: F. FAIRWEATHER, GLASSHOUSE & WORKS, drawn in a hand so precise it looked printed. "He was good. Better than good. Some of these details are things I'd have to look up in modern references, and he just *knew* them."

"He taught himself," I heard myself say. "Out of books. He ordered them from a catalogue. My mother used to complain about the cost until she saw what he could grow in January."

I hadn't said a full sentence about my father to anyone in thirty years. It came out of me the way heat comes out of pipes when the system kicks on after a long summer—unbidden, slightly embarrassing, impossible to recall.

Nell didn't pounce on it. She just nodded, as if I'd confirmed something she'd already suspected, and pulled the tracing paper away.

* * *

What lay underneath stopped my breath for a moment, and I want to be honest about that, because honesty is the thing I've practiced least.

It was the glasshouse. Not a picture of it—a translation of it. The footprint of the building rendered as garden beds, laid out exactly along the old foundation lines. Where the propagation benches had run along the north wall, there were low raised beds. Where the central path had been, a gravel walk. The potting shed became a small shelter of some kind—an open pavilion, its roof drawn as slats, letting light through in stripes the way the old glass roof had let it through in panes.

And where the boiler room had been—the room I ran from, the room behind the door—there was a circle. A planted circle, marked on the plan with a symbol I didn't recognize, and beside it, in Nell's small architectural handwriting, a single word: *names.*

"What's this?" I asked, though I knew. I wanted to hear her say it.

"The memorial." She came around the table and stood beside me, close enough that I could smell chalk and cut stems on her hands. "A bed of white flowers. Snowdrops first, then narcissus, then white camassia—it'll carry through spring into early summer. Around the edge, a low wall. Bronze plaques set into it."

"For whom?"

"For whoever the town wants to remember." She said it carefully, the way you set down something heavy on a surface you're not sure will hold. "But I thought—for the Fairweathers. For your mother. If the family agreed."

"My mother's name is on a stone in St. Brannoc's churchyard," I said. "Has been for thirty years."

"Yes. And people visit it, I'm sure. But they visit graves alone, Silas. This would be different. This would be a place where the story lives outside the ground." She turned to face me fully. "That's what I actually came here to ask you about. Not the bid. Not the land. The story."

The kettle clicked off in the silence that followed. Neither of us moved toward it.

"Nell—"

"I'm going to ask you plainly, and I apologize in advance, because I know plainness can feel like an attack to people who've spent their lives being polite." Her voice stayed level, unhurried. "What happened that night? In the glasshouse. The night of the fire."

There it was. Thirty years of deflection, and she walked straight through all of it in one sentence, the way water goes through old mortar.

I smiled. I felt myself do it—the specific smile, the one I'd built over decades, the one that opens doors and closes conversations. Warm at the edges, apologetic at the center. *You poor thing, asking me that. Let me spare us both.*

"It was a long time ago," I said. "A terrible accident. My mother died, my father never spoke again, and my sister and I grew up around the hole where our family used to be. There isn't much more to tell than that."

I watched the smile do its work on her face. I watched her receive it politely, completely, and file it away as exactly what it was.

"That's a summary," she said. "Not a story."

"They're often the same thing."

"No." She shook her head slowly. "They're almost never the same thing. A summary is what you give someone so they'll stop asking. A story is what you give someone so they'll understand." She picked up her tea, drank, set it down. "I've designed memorials before. Small ones. A bench for a drowned boy up in Cumbria. A grove for a midwife who delivered half a county's children. In every case, the family gave me the true shape of what happened—not the newspaper version, not the comfortable version—and the garden came out of that shape. You can't build honest ground on top of a polite lie. It shows. Plants know. People know."

"And if the family prefers the polite version?"

"Then I build the garden anyway, and it will be beautiful, and it will be hollow, and everyone who walks into it will feel the hollowness without knowing what it is." She looked at me steadily. "I'd rather not do that. Not for this place. Not for your mother."

"You didn't know my mother."

"No. But I know what she grew." She crossed to a shelf and took down a folder, opened it on the table between us. Photographs—old ones, county archive photographs, the glasshouse in better days. And there in the foreground, sharp even through the grain: rows of seedlings under the benches, and on the staging, terracotta pots with labels written in a fine upright hand. "Someone kept records. Someone labeled every variety. Someone ran that glasshouse like a cathedral runs its candles—one at a time, every day, whether anyone was watching or not." She tapped the photograph. "That person deserves a true thing built over her. Don't you think?"

My mother's handwriting. I'd know it anywhere, upside down, underwater, in the dark. I had spent thirty years making sure I never had to look at it.

"I think," I said, and my voice came out level, professional, the tuner's voice, the voice I use on clients when they ask if the piano can be saved, "that you're very good at what you do, and that the town is lucky to have you bidding."

Nell held my gaze for three seconds. Then she smiled—not my kind of smile, but a real one, tired and fond and utterly unconvinced.

"There it is," she said softly. "The smile. My grandmother had one just like it. Nigerian woman, came here in '61, worked in a hospital where nobody asked her name for eleven years. She could make that smile in her sleep. It said *I am fine, please stop seeing me.*" She closed the folder. "It's a remarkable piece of work, Silas. Genuinely. It takes decades to build a smile like that. But it's still a wall, and walls are my business, and I know one when it's standing in front of me."

I stood. Put down the untouched tea. My legs did their job; my voice did its job; the smile held its position at parade rest.

"Thank you for showing me the plans," I said. "The beds along the north wall—that's right, that's where the light was best. She'd have approved of that."

"Will you help me?" Nell asked, at the door. Not *will you sell*, not *will you endorse*. *Will you help.* As if the answer were already yes and we were only negotiating the terms.

"I'll think about it."

"You won't," she said, kindly. "Thinking is another thing you're excellent at instead of feeling. But that's all right. I'll ask again. I'm very patient. I plant things that take seven years to flower."

I got two steps down Wren Street before I realized I was still smiling. Standing alone on a pavement in the cold, smiling at nobody, the armor holding its shape around nothing.

Behind me, through the open door, I heard Nell speaking—not to me, into a phone, casual as anything:

"Ivy? Yes. He came. He saw the plans." A pause. "No. He's not ready yet. But he will be—someone's going to have to tell him about the ledger."

* * *

I stopped walking.

The street went quiet around me, the way rooms go quiet when a pipe freezes—you can feel the pressure building behind the silence.

*Ivy.*

Ivy, who answered the phone in clipped syllables and talked about solicitors and timelines. Ivy, who had not mentioned any ledger. Ivy, talking to Nell Okafor about me, about us, about whatever it was they thought I wasn't ready to know.

I turned back toward the garage. The door was closing. Through the narrowing gap I caught one last image: the plan on the table, the circle of white flowers, the word *names* written in a steady hand beside a room where a door had once been locked.

I stood on the pavement with my smile still fixed in place, and for the first time in thirty years, I wondered what it was hiding that I didn't already know.
