## 12. Ivy Breaks

I had planned to stay three days in Marlow Bend. Four, at the outside. Enough time to sign what needed signing, argue with Silas about the auction, and get back to Leeds before anyone at work noticed I'd gone soft.

It was day nine when I found myself standing outside the glasshouse at seven in the morning with two coffees going cold in my hands, waiting for my brother to arrive, knowing that today I was going to tell him. I hadn't decided to tell him. That was the strange part. There was no moment where I weighed it and chose. It was more like a tooth that had been loose for thirty years finally deciding it was done hanging on.

Silas came up the lane on foot, hands in his coat pockets, moving the way he always moved—like a man trying not to disturb the air around him. Even as a boy he'd walked like that. I used to think it was shyness. Now I understood it was a whole philosophy of existence: take up no space, make no sound, be no one's reason to look up.

"You're early," he said.

"So are you."

He took the coffee I held out. Our fingers didn't touch; he'd made sure of that, angling the cup so I could let go without contact. Thirty years of practice in a single gesture.

We went inside. The glasshouse smelled the way it always had—wet stone, cold iron, and underneath it something green and stubborn that shouldn't have survived but did. Some kind of moss on the north wall. Mum would have known its name. She knew the name of everything that grew.

* * *

We worked through the morning without talking much. That was the other thing about Silas—you could spend a whole day beside him and never once be in a conversation. He cleared debris from the eastern beds with a care that bordered on devotional, lifting each shard of glass as if it might still be part of something. I hauled broken staging into a pile by the door and tried not to look at the corner where the seed ledger had been, because every time I looked at it I felt the thing in my chest shift, the loose tooth working looser.

It was nearly noon when he said, "Ivy," in a voice I didn't recognise, "why did you really come back?"

I set down the armful of rotted timber I was carrying. Dust rose off it and hung in the light.

"What do you mean, really?"

"You could have signed everything by post. You told me so yourself, twice. You stayed." He wasn't accusing me. He was tuning me, the way he tuned pianos—listening for the note that rang false. "You keep looking at that ledger like it owes you money."

"It doesn't owe me anything." My voice came out wrong. Too high. He heard it. Of course he heard it; he heard everything, that was his whole tragedy, he'd built a life out of hearing things people didn't say to him.

"Ivy."

"Don't say my name like that. Like you're being gentle. You've never once in your life been gentle with me, you've just been quiet, and everyone mistakes it for the same thing."

He flinched. I saw it land. And some vicious, exhausted part of me was glad, because for once I'd made a sound in him instead of the other way round.

"Tell me why you came back," he said again, quieter now.

And there it was. The tooth, hanging by nothing.

* * *

Here is what I need you to understand about being sixteen, because everything else follows from it and if you don't understand it you'll judge me, and I have been judged already, by the only judge who mattered, which was myself, nightly, for three decades.

At sixteen you believe you are the only competent person in a world run by fools. At sixteen you believe your parents' marriage is a machine that has broken down and that you, uniquely, can see where the gears have jammed. At sixteen you believe that love is a problem with a solution, and that the solution is information. If they only knew. If someone would only say it out loud. Everything is fixable if the right person says the true thing at the right time.

That's what I believed. I want that on the record. Not as excuse—as context. Because I have spent thirty years hearing the word *betrayal* in my own head and never once the word *child*, and I am so tired, Silas, I am so tired of carrying it as betrayal when I carried it in as a child carrying water for a fire she thought she was putting out.

* * *

The night before the fire, I came home late from Bethan Pryce's house. Half eleven, maybe later. The kitchen light was on, which it shouldn't have been—Dad went to bed at ten like a man keeping an appointment with death itself—and through the gap between the door and the frame I saw Mum at the table with her back to me, and she was packing.

Not tidying. Packing. I knew the difference even then, because Mum tidied the way other people breathed, constantly and without thought, and this was different. This was deliberate. She had a canvas holdall open on the chair—the big one, the one we used for camping, the one with the broken zip she'd fixed with a safety pin—and she was putting things into it with both hands, folding each item flat before it went in, the way she folded seed packets at the end of the season. Methodical. Final.

There was a envelope on the table next to her elbow. White. Thick-ish. I remember thinking it looked heavy for paper.

I should have gone up to bed. Any sensible person would have gone up to bed. But I was sixteen, and I stood in the dark of the hallway with my coat still on and watched my mother pack a bag to leave us, and the world tilted on its axis and did not tilt back.

She must have felt me there—mothers do, or maybe the floor creaked, maybe I wanted her to know, maybe some traitor part of me wanted to be caught so I wouldn't have to carry it alone even then. She turned. And here is the detail that has kept me awake for thirty years, the one I have turned over ten thousand times like a stone looking for what's underneath:

She didn't look frightened. And she didn't look ashamed.

She looked at me the way she looked at seedlings that had come up too early, in a cold snap—tender, and resolute, and sorry, and certain. She put one finger to her lips. Then she crossed the kitchen and took my face in her flour-rough hands and kissed my forehead and said, "Go to bed, my love. Whatever happens tomorrow, none of it will be your fault. Do you hear me? None of it."

And I nodded. And I went to bed.

And then I got up again, because I was sixteen, and I believed love was a problem with a solution.

* * *

I told Dad.

I want to be precise about how it happened, because precision is all I have left of that night and I have hoarded it like a miser. It was the next evening. Dad was in the workshop under the glasshouse, doing whatever he did down there in the winter dark—I never knew, none of us ever knew, he kept that room the way Silas keeps his whole self. I went down the steps and stood at the bottom and my heart was going like a bird in a box, and I said, "Dad. Mum's leaving us. I saw her packing. There was an envelope. She's going somewhere and she isn't taking us."

I remember his back. He was bent over the bench and he didn't straighten up. His shoulders stopped moving, that's all. Like a clock stopping.

"How long have you known," he said. Not a question. A measurement.

"Since last night."

Stillness. Then he said, "Thank you, Ivy," in a voice I had never heard him use—not with us, not with Mum, not with anyone. Flat and grateful and terrible, a voice like a door closing on a room where something has just been decided. And he reached out without turning around and squeezed my hand once, hard, and said, "Off you go now," and I went, believing—I actually believed—that I had done the right thing. That I had handed the adults the information and the adults would fix it. That love was a problem with a solution and I had just posted the answer.

Two hours later the glasshouse was burning and our mother was dead inside it and the door was locked, and I was seventeen weeks past my sixteenth birthday, and I have not slept a full night since.

* * *

I don't remember telling Silas any of this. That's the honest truth of the hour after I started. I remember the beginning—standing in the wrecked glasshouse with timber dust on my sleeves, saying *the night before the fire, I saw her packing*—and I remember the end, which was sitting on an overturned seed tray with my face in my hands saying *and then I told him, and then I told him, and then it burned*, and between those two points there is nothing, just a white space where three decades came out of me in what Silas later said was eleven minutes.

Eleven minutes. Thirty years, eleven minutes. There's a lesson in that about interest rates.

When I finally looked up, Silas was sitting very still on the staging beside me. Not touching me. He never touched anyone; I'd forgotten how much that used to hurt until it hurt again. But he was looking at me, actually looking, and his eyes were wet, and his mouth was doing something complicated, and when he spoke his voice cracked in the middle like a plate.

"You were sixteen."

"I was sixteen."

"You were a child."

"I told him, Silas. I'm the reason he went up there that night. I'm the reason the argument happened. Whatever happened with the lamp, whatever happened with the door—it happened because I—"

"No." He said it fast and hard, harder than I'd ever heard him say anything. "No. Stop. You don't get to do that. You don't get to hand yourself the sentence he should have served."

"He never meant to lock the door."

"I know," Silas said. "I know he didn't. I've known for thirty years and I've said it to no one, and neither have you, and look what it's done to us. Look what it's done." He dragged a hand down his face. "Ivy. Listen to me. Listen with the ears I've got and you haven't. Dad went up to that glasshouse because you told him the truth. What he did when he got there—that was his. The lamp, the lock, the argument—that was his and hers and theirs. You gave a man a message. You didn't build the fire."

"But if I hadn't—"

"If you hadn't, she'd have left anyway. She had an envelope, Ivy. She had a plan. You didn't start that. You just found out early." He paused, and then, gentler, in the voice he used for pianos: "Do you know what I did that night? I hid. I sat in the potting shed and listened to them argue and I did nothing, absolutely nothing, because hiding was safer than being seen. One of us told the truth and one of us hid, and we've spent thirty years each believing we were the guilty one. Don't you see? We were children. Both of us. Children in a house coming apart, doing what children do."

I was crying by then, properly, the ugly kind, the kind I hadn't allowed myself since the funeral where I'd stood next to my brother and watched him perform composure like a professional and decided, at sixteen, that grief was another thing I'd have to carry alone because he clearly couldn't hold any of it.

"She kissed my forehead," I said. "Before I went to bed. She said none of it would be my fault. She knew, Silas. She knew what telling him might do, and she kissed me and absolved me in advance, and I threw it away because I was sixteen and I thought I knew better than her."

"You didn't throw it away." He crouched down in front of me, which cost him something—I could see it cost him, proximity, being seen seeing me. "You set it down for a while. That's all tonight is. Setting it down."

"It doesn't feel lighter."

"It won't. Not yet. Wounds feel worse when the pressure comes off. Ask any piano—no, don't, that's a stupid comparison, forget I—" and then, to my astonishment, my brother the ghost, the man who had made invisibility into a career, laughed. A small cracked sound, but a laugh. "Thirty years, Ivy. We've been in the same silence for thirty years, each of us guarding the other from a truth that would have freed us both."

"The ledger," I said, because it was rising in me again, the tooth turning, the next thing. "There's an address on the last page. Two towns over. She was already gone in her head, Silas. Before any of it. Maybe the fire didn't take her from us. Maybe she'd already—"

"Don't." But softly. "Let her be a person. Persons leave. Persons plan. It doesn't unmake the seedlings or the humming or the way she knew the name of every green thing on that wall. Let her be a person who was leaving, and a person who died, and both of those can be true."

* * *

We sat in the ruin until the light went copper and then grey. Somewhere in the middle of it he put his hand on my shoulder—one pat, brief, catastrophic, the first time my brother had touched me in over a decade—and I let it happen and didn't comment, because commenting would have scared him back into his shell and I understood the shell better now than I ever had.

When we finally gathered our coats, he stopped at the doorway and looked back at the darkening glass, and he said, almost to himself:

"There's something I have to do. In the records office. Something I found in the auction photograph." He glanced at me, and for the first time since childhood I watched my brother decide, deliberately, to be seen. "A second bicycle, Ivy. In the background. Taken the week before the fire. I need to know whose it was—and I think I need you with me when I find out."

The cold came up through the concrete and into my bones, and thirty years shifted their weight again, and I heard myself say:

"Then we'd better go tomorrow."
