## 18. Tuning the House

The gate still stuck at the same point, two inches from closed, needing a lift and a shove. Thirty years and the hinge hadn't learned anything new. Silas gave it the lift, the shove, and stood in the dark listening to the glasshouse take his weight off the gravel.

He'd told Ivy he'd be here tonight. She'd wanted to come with him — he could hear it in the pause before she said *whatever you need* — but this was not a thing you did with a witness. This was rehearsal. And you didn't rehearse in front of an audience.

Nell had left the site unlocked for him. A key on a nail by the door, a note in her square handwriting: *Take all night. The machines don't come till eight.* Underneath, smaller: *Whatever you say in there is yours. I won't ask.* He folded the note into his jacket pocket, where it sat against his chest like something warm-blooded.

Inside, the ruin smelled of wet stone and cold iron and, faintly, impossibly, of tomato vines — though that was memory, he knew, memory leaking through the walls like damp. The moon came through the broken panes in pieces. Where the roof had failed, the sky was whole.

He set his bag down on the old potting bench, which someone — Nell's crew, probably — had cleared and swept. On it: his pitch pipe, a torch, a thermos of coffee gone lukewarm already. And beside them, wrapped in a tea towel, the seed ledger. He'd brought it without deciding to bring it. His hands had made the decision somewhere between the car and the gate.

*Not yet,* he told it. *First we work.*

Because that was the thing about tuning: you didn't start with the hard notes. You started with the ones that answered easily. You earned your way toward the dissonance.

He took out the pitch pipe and blew a low C into the dark.

The note went out thin and round and came back changed. That was what no one understood about empty rooms — they weren't silent. They were full of answers waiting for the right question. In a stranger's front room, the radiators asked him questions all day: *who lived here, who argued here, who died in the back bedroom in '09?* He'd spent thirty years answering those questions professionally and never once asking one of himself.

Tonight he was going to ask.

He blew the C again and walked the length of the north wall, holding the pipe up to the heating pipes where they ran along the brickwork, dead for decades, dry as bone. But even dead pipes had opinions. The C found a resonance in the section near the old boiler flue — a hum, low and reluctant, like a man agreeing just to end the conversation.

His father's pipe. Of course. Even now, even rusted through, that stretch of iron held the pitch of his father's humming — the tuneless, half-remembered hymns he'd droned while stoking the boiler at five in the morning, back when the glasshouse was alive and the family inside it was too.

"Still flat," Silas said aloud. His voice startled him. It had been hours since he'd spoken, and the room took the words and kept them.

He moved along. The east range, where his mother's benches had been, gave nothing back at first. He tried the D. Nothing. The E. Nothing. Then, near the vent where she'd started her seedlings every February, the E caught — a bright, brief shimmer of resonance, there and gone, like a bird landing and leaving.

Silas stood very still.

It wasn't her. He knew that. It was a column of air in a corroded pipe, vibrating at a frequency that happened to match a reed in a piece of brass. Physics. He was a tuner; physics was his trade; he would not insult the dead with mysticism.

And yet he blew the E again, softer, the way you'd knock on a door you weren't sure you were welcome behind.

Again the shimmer. Again the leaving.

"All right," he said. "I'm coming to it."

*

He worked for two hours before he let himself stop.

That was the discipline his father had beaten into the trade — well, not beaten; demonstrated, endlessly, in that patient humming way of his, before everything. *A room tells you its pitch before it tells you its problems, son. Listen first. Fix second.* Silas mapped the ruin note by note, the way he'd map any house: the west wall sang a quarter-tone sharp, warped by heat it had taken in some fire or other — not the fire, an earlier one, a small one, a forgotten one; houses had many deaths before their last. The south windows, what remained of them, rattled sympathetically at B-flat, a loose pane keeping time like a metronome nobody had asked for. The cistern in the corner answered everything with a hollow gurgle, the room's stomach, still hungry after all these years.

By midnight he had the whole ruin in his head, its resonances charted, its dead pipes tuned in theory if not in fact. It was, he realized, the most thorough job he'd ever done, and there was no one to invoice, and no one to hear it, and that was the point. Some work you did because the room deserved it. Because the room had waited long enough.

He sat on the potting bench and drank the lukewarm coffee and looked at the ledger in its tea towel.

Here was what he knew now, what the last weeks had given him whether he wanted it or not: his mother had meant to leave. There was an address in her handwriting on the last page of the seed ledger, two towns over, and beneath it a list begun and abandoned — *what to take* — three items long, the third unfinished, the pen lifting mid-word. She had been sitting right here, probably, on this bench or one like it, planning her escape among the seedlings, and then she hadn't escaped, and then she was dead, and for thirty years Silas had built a monument to her out of silence, and the monument was wrong. She wasn't a saint under glass. She was a woman with a plan and a train timetable and a husband who hummed off-key, and she had been frightened, and brave, and ordinary, and she had died before any of it mattered.

You couldn't say goodbye to a monument. Monuments didn't need anything from you. But a woman — a woman might have needed to hear from her son sometime in the last thirty years.

"Ivy says I should talk to you," he said to the dark.

The room held the words the way it held everything: without judgment, without reply.

"She thinks I've been rehearsing this my whole life. She might be right. She usually is, which is insufferable." He turned the thermos in his hands. "I used to think if I stayed quiet long enough, the past would lose my scent. Like a dog at the edge of a property. You just stand still and eventually it wanders off." He laughed, short and unamused. "Thirty years, Mum. It never wandered off. It just got better at waiting than me."

The B-flat pane rattled in a gust. Wind, not answer. He chose to take it as punctuation anyway.

*

Sometime past one, he unwrapped the ledger.

He hadn't meant to read it again — he knew the entries now the way he knew a piano's faults before touching a key — but his hands opened it to the last page the way they'd open to a familiar chord. The address. The unfinished list. *Clothes. The good photographs. My—*

My what. My mother had died mid-word, in ink, thirty years before she died in fact, and Silas had spent half his life refusing to read that sentence, and now he read it nightly, and it broke differently each time.

"You were going to tell me," he said. "Weren't you. Not Dad. Me. Kids get taken along; kids get carried." He swallowed. "And I'd have gone. That's the part I can't — I'd have gone with you, Mum. I wouldn't have looked back. I was eleven and I'd have left my own sister behind without a thought, because you asked."

The words came out of him like water finding a crack, and once the crack was open, more followed.

"And then the fire, and everyone decided what I was. The boy who survived. The boy who got out. Nobody asked how, because the answer was ugly — I hid. I heard the whole thing from inside the cold frame and I hid, and hiding worked so well I made a career of it. Tuning pianos. Going into strangers' houses and being nobody. Being furniture with excellent hearing." He wiped his face with the heel of his hand. "You'd have hated that. You always said I had your ears and Dad's stubbornness, and I took both and made a cowardice out of them."

The E-pipe shimmered when the wind moved through it. He refused to call it anything.

"But here's what I came to say, and I'm going to say it tomorrow too, out loud, with witnesses, God help me. Here it is." He stood. His knees cracked; he was fifty-one and the ground was cold. "You didn't abandon us. You were trying to save yourself, and that was allowed. It should have been allowed. And Dad didn't kill you — the lamp did, and the lock did, and a bad night did, and none of them meant to. And Ivy didn't do it by telling. And I didn't do it by hiding. We were children and a marriage and a locked door, and the fire doesn't take appointments." His voice cracked on the last of it. "Nobody was guilty, Mum. Everybody was just there. And I'm sorry it took me thirty years to say so, and I'm sorry you died mid-word, and I hope wherever you got to, the light was better than this."

He stood in the ruin with his chest heaving, and the glasshouse did what glasshouses do, which is hold warmth badly and hold sound perfectly, and his voice came back to him off the remaining panes, thinner, older, forgiven-sounding — his own words returned to him like a stranger's kindness.

He sat back down. He blew the low C one more time, and the whole ruin answered at once, every pipe, every pane, the cistern, the flue, a great ragged chord of decay and persistence, and he let it ring until it faded on its own, uncorrected, untuned, true.

"There," he said quietly. "In tune. More or less."

*

He must have slept, because he woke grey-lit and stiff, the ledger under his cheek like a pillow, and for one merciful second he was eleven and the glasshouse was alive and the smell was real tomatoes.

Then he heard it: tyres on gravel, voices, the low diesel idle of machinery arriving early.

He checked his phone. 7:12. Nell's note said eight. But crews came early; crews always came early; the world did not wait for a man to finish saying goodbye.

He gathered the ledger, the pipe, the cold thermos, and walked to the door, and stopped with his hand on the frame.

Tomorrow — no, today — he was supposed to stand here and speak. He'd done it once already, alone, in the dark, and it had nearly killed him, and it had saved him, and now the machines were here ahead of schedule and the words felt used, spent, like a match struck twice.

Through the doorway he saw a figure by the gate, raising a hand — Ivy, early too, scarf pulled up against the cold, and behind her, unmistakably, the excavator on its low-loader, and beside Ivy a second figure he didn't expect, small and upright in the morning light.

His father's care home driver's jacket. His father himself, wrapped in blankets, looking at the glasshouse for the first time in thirty years.
