## 19. What Ivy Needed to Hear

I had rehearsed the drive to Silas's flat three times in my head before I ever made it, and all three rehearsals ended with me turning around at the roundabout outside town and going back to my hotel, back to my laptop, back to the tidy fiction of my life where nothing from Fairweather Glasshouse had ever needed saying out loud.

The fourth time, I took the turn.

His flat was above a shuttered bakery on Wren Street, the kind of building that smelled permanently of old bread and cold brick. I'd been there once before, two weeks ago, when we'd finished clearing the glasshouse and he'd made tea in a kitchen so bare it looked like a stage set waiting for actors. One mug each. A kettle with a chipped spout. A piano in the corner of the sitting room — his own, an upright with a worn fallboard, the only object in the whole flat that seemed loved.

I stood outside the street door for four minutes. I know because I watched the clock on my phone tick over, the way I used to watch the clock in the school corridor while Silas was off somewhere being invisible and I was being the visible one, the responsible one, the one who told.

That word again. *Told.* Thirty years and it still sat in my chest like a swallowed stone.

I pressed the buzzer.

* * *

He opened the door already holding two mugs, as if he'd known. Maybe he had. My brother heard things through walls the way other people heard them through open windows. He'd told me once, standing among the dead pipes of the glasshouse, that every house had a pitch, that you could put your hand on a radiator and feel what the room had been feeling for years. I'd thought it was a tuner's affectation then. Standing in his doorway now, watching him register my face before I'd said a word, I understood it was something else entirely. He listened because listening was how he'd survived. And maybe — this was the thought that had kept me up half the night — maybe he'd been listening to me all along, across all those silent years, hearing the thing I never said the way you hear a note held too long under the rest of the chord.

"You look terrible," he said, and stepped aside to let me in.

"Thanks very much."

"I mean it kindly."

"I know." I took the mug. "You always did. That was the problem. You were kind in the way furniture is kind. Just there. Never asking anything."

He didn't flinch. He set his own mug down on the windowsill and waited, and I realized with a small, humiliating jolt that he was doing it again — making space, being furniture — and that I had come here precisely to break him of it.

"I need you to say something," I said.

"Okay."

"Not yet. Let me get there. If you're patient at me right now I'll lose my nerve."

Something moved behind his eyes — amusement, maybe, or recognition — and he picked his mug back up and drank, deliberately, theatrically, giving us both something ordinary to do.

I walked to the piano. I don't know why. Perhaps because it was the one thing in the room that wasn't him, and I needed somewhere to put my hands. The fallboard was down. I lifted it. The keys were yellowed ivory, clean, tuned — of course they were tuned; he probably tuned them weekly out of some compulsion, the way other men checked locks.

"Do you remember," I said, "the summer I turned sixteen?"

I felt rather than saw him go still.

"The summer Dad rebuilt the cold frames," he said quietly. "You got your bicycle. The blue one."

"The blue one." I laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "God. Yes. I'd forgotten the bicycle. Isn't that obscene? I forgot the bicycle but I can't forget the—" I stopped. Pressed my palm flat against the keys without pressing any of them down. "Silas. I have to tell you the whole thing properly. Not the version I gave you in the glasshouse, not the summary. All of it. Because I've been carrying it since I was sixteen years old and I am fifty-one next month and I cannot carry it into another decade."

He set his mug down. This time he came and stood beside me at the piano, not touching me, not filling the silence, just present, the way he'd been present in the wreckage when we found Mum's ledger — steady as a wall you could lean against if you chose to.

So I told him.

* * *

It was the third of October. I know the date because it was four days before the fire and I had written it in my diary, which I still have, in a box in my attic in Leeds, though I've never been able to open the box since. I was sixteen and furious about everything the way you are at sixteen — furious at school, at the weather, at my skin, at the particular injustice of being the daughter who was noticed and therefore the daughter who was asked to do things. Set the table. Answer the phone. Go and tell your mother dinner's ready.

Go and tell your mother.

That was the sentence that ruined everything, and it was such a small sentence. It should be illegal for a sentence that small to ruin anything.

I went up the garden path to the glasshouse at ten past six in the evening. The light was going amber the way it does in October, coming through the glass panes sideways, and Mum was inside at the potting bench with her back to me, and she didn't hear me come in because she was crying.

Not sobbing. Crying the quiet way adults cry when they think no one is there — shoulders moving, one hand pressed flat on the bench beside the seed trays, head bowed like she was praying, except she wasn't praying, she was packing. There was a suitcase open on the floor behind her. The brown one, the good one, the one she kept on top of the wardrobe for holidays we took once every three years. Half full. Folded clothes. And her jewellery roll, the green velvet one Gran left her, which she never took anywhere except holidays.

And the ledger. She was copying something out of the seed ledger onto an envelope. I couldn't see what. I didn't stay long enough to see.

She heard me then — the door hinge, or my breath, I don't know — and she turned around, and her face did something I have spent thirty years trying to describe and have never managed. It wasn't guilt exactly. It was more like a door closing. She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, very fast, businesslike, and she smiled at me, and she said, "Ivy love, I was just sorting through some things," and her voice was steady, and I was sixteen, and I wanted so badly for everything to be normal that I let her lie stand there between us like a piece of furniture, and I said, "Dinner's ready," and I walked back down the path.

And here is the part I have never said to anyone, not to the counsellor in my twenties, not to Richard, not to myself at three in the morning: I didn't decide to tell Dad. I want that understood. I didn't sit down and weigh it and choose. I was walking back to the house and he was in the workshop doorway with his pipe unlit, the way he always stood when he was avoiding going inside, and he said, "Where's your mother?" and it was completely automatic, the answer just fell out of me like a coin from a torn pocket: "She's packing a suitcase in the glasshouse."

Automatic. The way you say *fine* when someone asks how you are. I didn't even understand what I'd done until I saw his face change.

Then I spent four days watching the house hold its breath. Dad said nothing to Mum — or I thought he said nothing; later I understood he'd said plenty, in his way, in silences and closed doors and the particular way he stopped humming. Mum was brighter than usual, terrifyingly bright, making our favourite meals, laughing at the radio. Now I know what that brightness was. Then I thought it was guilt. I thought: she knows I know. I thought: I have broken something by seeing.

And on the fourth night there was the argument, and the lamp, and the fire, and my mother did not come out, and my brother did, and I stood on the lawn in my dressing gown with the heat pushing at my face and I thought — I have thought it every day since — *this is because of me. I opened my mouth. She packed the suitcase because she was leaving, and Dad knew because of me, and the argument happened because of me, and she died because her sixteen-year-old daughter couldn't keep her mouth shut for five minutes.*

I told all of this to Silas in his flat above the bakery, standing at his piano, and my voice held until the last part and then it didn't, and I gripped the edge of the keyboard to keep myself upright, and no notes sounded, and somehow that was worse than if they had.

* * *

When I finished, the flat was so quiet I could hear the bakery's old extractor fan ticking somewhere below the floor.

Silas didn't move for a long moment. Then he reached past me and played a single note — middle C, soft, barely struck — and let it ring until it faded into nothing.

"What was that?" I asked, wetly.

"Giving the room something true to listen to." His voice was rough. "Sorry. Habit. Go on—"

"There's no more. That's all of it. That's the whole ugly machine." I turned to face him. "Four days. I had four days to take it back and I didn't even know I needed to. And then she was gone and Dad went silent and everyone in town decided it was an accident, full stop, and I let them decide it, because the official version was the only version where I wasn't—"

"Don't finish that sentence."

"You don't get to tell me what sentences to finish. Not this one. Not today." I was shaking. I let myself shake. "Because here's what I need, Silas, and I need you to actually say it, not nod at it, not play a note at it, not be furniture. I need you to say that telling Dad didn't kill her. Out loud. In words. Because I have needed someone to say it for thirty years and I have never once asked, because asking would make it real, and real things have to be survived, and I was so tired of surviving."

He looked at me for a long time. My brother, who had hidden in the glasshouse while our parents argued, who had learned to be unseen because being seen was dangerous, who had built a whole life out of tuning other people's rooms and slipping out of them — he looked at me the way he must have looked at pianos, hearing everything underneath.

And then he shook his head, and my heart dropped through the floor—

—and he said, "No."

"No?"

"No. I won't say it like that. Because it isn't true, and I'm not going to hand you a comfort that's a lie, not after everything." He took a breath. "Here's what's true. Listen. Dad already knew. Before you said a word. Nell showed me the plans, Ivy — Mum had been writing to a solicitor in Ashcombe since June. June. Three months before you saw that suitcase. She'd opened a bank account. She'd viewed a room to rent. Dad knew, or suspected, or had worked it out from a hundred small silences, because he lived with her and he was not a fool, whatever else he was. What you gave him that evening wasn't the knowledge. It was permission to stop pretending he didn't have it. That's all. That's everything you did. You were a child who repeated a fact, and the fact was already true."

"But the argument—"

"The argument was thirty years of marriage arriving at its final appointment. It would have arrived with or without you. It might have arrived anyway, on some other night, over some other trigger — a bill, a birthday, a burnt dinner. You didn't build that fire, Ivy. You weren't even in the house. You were a girl in a dressing gown on a lawn, watching something you couldn't have stopped, and you have been serving a sentence for a crime nobody committed." His voice cracked on the last words, and he let it. "And I'll tell you the other true thing, since we're spending truth tonight. I hid in that glasshouse for years listening to them fight, and I told myself staying silent was keeping safe. But some part of me believed it too — that if I stayed quiet enough, small enough, the bad thing wouldn't happen. And it happened anyway. We were both children performing magic we didn't have. Your words didn't kill her. My silence didn't save her. Do you hear me?"

"Yes," I whispered.

"Say it needs saying properly, then." And he took both my hands in his — my brother, who touched people the way other people handled glass — and he looked straight at me, and he said it, plain and slow, like a man laying stones:

"Ivy Fairweather. Telling Dad what you saw did not kill our mother. You were sixteen. You were a child. You carried this alone for thirty years and it was never yours to carry. I'm saying it out loud so it exists in the air, in this room, in the world. It was never yours."

* * *

I don't remember deciding to cry. I remember the sound I made, which was not dignified, which was the sound of a hinge thirty years rusted finally giving way. I remember that Silas held on to my hands the entire time, and that at some point the piano behind us caught a draught from the window and sounded a faint cluster of notes, high and dissonant and then resolving, like the house itself exhaling.

And I remember the weight. That's the only word for it. Everyone says *weight* about grief and it becomes a cliché, but this was physical — a stone I had swallowed at sixteen shifting, loosening, and then, as he finished speaking, simply not being there anymore. I breathed in and my ribs moved further than they had in decades. I actually laughed, mid-sob, at the sheer mechanics of it.

"It's gone," I said stupidly. "The stone. It's actually gone."

"It'll visit," he said. "But it doesn't live here anymore."

We stood there a while longer, two middle-aged orphans in a bare flat above a dead bakery, holding hands over an untuned piano — no, a tuned one, everything of his was tuned — and slowly the crying ebbed, and I wiped my face with the heel of my hand, Mum's gesture, and this time it didn't hurt to notice that.

"Right," I said finally. "Well. That's that, then."

"That's that."

"I have one more thing." I fished in my bag and brought out the envelope I'd been carrying for two weeks, ever since the clearing, ever since we found the ledger. I'd copied the address from the last page before Silas claimed the book. "Ashcombe. 14 Marlow Lane. I wrote to the letting agency that handles the house. They keep records going back forty years, apparently. A woman rang me yesterday." I handed him the envelope. "There was a tenant at that address in the spring after the fire. She stayed eleven months and then moved to the coast. Her name was Margaret Fairweather."

Silas went absolutely still.

"She didn't die in the fire, Silas. I know we know that. But she also didn't vanish. She lived eleven months in the house she'd chosen, alone, and then she moved to Whitstable, and the agency forwarded one letter for her, years later, and it was returned unopened, and they filed it." I tapped the envelope in his hands. "This is a copy of the forwarding record. And the woman at the agency says there's a Mrs. Fairweather still listed at a care home in Whitstable. Independent living. Age ninety-one."

My brother sat down slowly on the piano stool, the envelope in both hands, looking at it the way he must once have looked at the auction catalogue, at the photograph, at the second bicycle — the past asserting itself against everything he'd curated away.

"She's alive," he said.

"She's alive," I said. "And she's ninety-one, and she's two hundred miles away, and neither of us has spoken to her since we were children who thought she was dead to us." I sat down beside him on the stool, shoulder to shoulder, the way we hadn't sat since the blue bicycle. "So. Are we going?"
