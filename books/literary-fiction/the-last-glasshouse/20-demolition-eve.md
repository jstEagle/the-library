## 20. Demolition Eve

The kitchen of my rented flat has one window and it faces a brick wall, which is the kind of detail I used to choose on purpose. For thirty years I have lived in rooms that asked nothing of me—rooms with no history, no glass, no pipes worth listening to. Tonight the room feels less like armor than like a waiting room. Tomorrow morning at seven, the machines come.

I have known since Ivy's phone call on Tuesday that I would write something down. That's what I do with words; I put them where they can't escape me. Tuning forks in felt rolls, tools in fitted cases, sentences in notebooks. Everything in its place, everything silent until needed.

So after dinner I cleared the table, set out a fresh pad—the cheap kind, spiral-bound, the kind I use for client invoices—and sat down to write my mother's eulogy, thirty years late.

The pen wouldn't start.

Not literally. The pen worked fine. It was the first sentence that refused to arrive. I wrote *Dear Mum* and crossed it out—she wasn't dear, not exactly, she was fierce and tired and smelled of tomato leaves and she had been planning to leave us, and none of those things fit under *dear*. I wrote her name, *Marion Fairweather*, and that looked like a headstone, so I crossed it out too. I wrote *What happened that night* and stopped, because the pad suddenly seemed very white and the kitchen very quiet, and I understood that whatever I was about to do, I could not do it as an exercise.

I got up and made tea I didn't want.

* * *

Here is what I know about drafts, because it's what I know about pianos: a draft is a recording. A recording is a fixed thing. You can play it back a thousand times and it will be identical every time, and there is comfort in that—I have made my living from instruments that hold their pitch—but a recording is also a way of never having to say anything again. Press play. Walk away. The voice does the work while you stand behind it, hands in pockets, invisible.

That was the trap, and I nearly walked into it with my eyes open. I could feel how easy it would be: write the speech, perfect it over tonight the way I'd perfect a voicing, then tomorrow stand in the ruin and read it aloud in my careful tuner's voice, flat and true, hitting every note exactly as written. Nell would nod. Ivy would cry or not cry. And I would have performed the goodbye without ever quite being present for it—a man reading his own words instead of a man saying them, the paper between us like the glass between the growing house and the world.

My mother spent her life putting living things behind glass. Seedlings, cuttings, the tender green beginnings of things she wanted to protect from frost. She believed glass was care. I learned from watching what happened when the glass broke that sometimes it's also a cage, and sometimes the thing you're protecting dies inside it anyway, and all your careful transparency amounts to a box with a lid.

I went back to the table and wrote anyway. Not because I'd changed my mind but because I needed to find out what I actually knew.

* * *

The first real sentence came at half past nine.

*I was in the glasshouse when the lamp went over.* That's not strictly accurate—I've spent weeks now assembling the night with Ivy, testing each memory against hers, and the truth is messier than one clean sentence. But the sentence was a door, and once it opened the rest followed, page after page, my handwriting deteriorating from its invoice-neatness into something ragged and slanting downhill, the way handwriting goes when the hand stops supervising it.

I wrote about the argument below, the voices rising through the floor vents the way heat rises, my father's low and my mother's sharp and both of them saying things I only now understand were said by two frightened people and not by monsters. I wrote about the lamp—the paraffin lamp my father carried because the power to the glasshouse had been cut off years before, a detail I'd forgotten and recovered from a county record like pulling a splinter—and how he set it on the bench beside the seed trays because his hands were shaking, and how nobody locked the door, and how the door simply stuck, swollen in its frame, and how a stuck door became in the town's telling a locked door, and in my father's silence a confession, and in mine a wound I kept licking for three decades.

I wrote: *He didn't lock us in. He couldn't get us out. Those are different sentences and I have spent my whole life confusing them.*

I wrote about Ivy at sixteen, going down the stairs to tell our father what she'd seen—Mum packing a case, the address in the ledger, the plan—and how a child's report of a fact became, in the combustion of that household, a spark. And how Ivy has carried the arithmetic of it ever since: *if I hadn't told, the argument wouldn't have happened, if the argument hadn't happened, the lamp wouldn't have been lit, if the lamp—* and so on, backward through causation like a row of dominoes standing in ash. I wrote what I finally believe, which took me four tries to phrase: *You told the truth. The truth didn't kill her. The night did, and the night belongs to no one, least of all a sixteen-year-old girl in her dressing gown.*

I wrote about my mother's last entry in the seed ledger. *Flat 2, 14 Coronation Road, Ashcombe.* Her handwriting steady, practical, the handwriting of a woman writing down a bus route. Not a woman planning a dramatic exit. A woman planning a Tuesday. I wrote: *She was leaving him, not us. I know the difference now. It took me thirty years and a stranger with a garden plan to teach me, but I know it.*

And near the end, my pen slowing, I wrote the part I have never said to anyone, the part I've been circling since chapter one of this whole miserable education:

*I survived because I hid. That was my whole strategy, aged eleven, and I never retired it. I hid in other people's front rooms for thirty years, tuning their pianos, hearing their houses breathe, knowing everything and saying nothing. People thought I was discreet. I was just still hiding. Tomorrow the machines are coming to knock down the last place I ever hid, and I want to walk out of it before they do.*

Then, underneath, the actual goodbye, the words for her:

*Mum. I don't forgive you for going quiet on us, and I don't blame you for wanting to go loud. You were a person. I made you into a monument because monuments don't leave. I'm sorry. Your tomatoes were the best in the county and everyone said so. I'm going to say your name out loud tomorrow in front of witnesses, and then I'm going to stop surviving you and start remembering you, which is a different job with better hours.*

I read it through twice. It was rough. Some of it was embarrassing. The tomato line might be the worst sentence I've ever written and also the truest, which is usually how you can tell.

It was midnight. The pad held six pages, and every word of it was ready to become an artifact.

* * *

I want to explain the burning properly, because Ivy will ask—you know how she asks, that sideways look, the question disguised as a change of subject—and because I want to have the answer straight in my own head before I sleep.

I took the pages out to the yard. There's a steel bucket out there that came with the flat, left by some previous tenant against the day they'd need to burn something, and I've always found that oddly comforting, a piece of equipment waiting for its purpose. I tore the pages off the spiral one at a time. The night was cold and clear, the kind of October dark where the stars look sharpened, and my breath hung in front of me like something I was about to say.

I struck the match and lit the first page from the bottom corner, the way you'd light a fire in a grate, and watched the flame climb toward my fingers.

Here's the thing about burning paper: it doesn't destroy the words. I know that sounds mystical but it's mechanical. The words are already in me. Six pages of my own handwriting, read twice—I could recite most of it right now, and tomorrow, standing in the ruin with Nell and Ivy watching, I won't need the paper because the paper was never the point. The writing was how I found out what I knew. The burning is how I make sure I have to carry it.

A preserved text can be lost, stolen, misread, quoted against you. A spoken word exists only in the air between the speaker and the listener, and then it lives where all living things live—in memory, in other people, imperfect and unfixable. My mother kept her seedlings behind glass and lost them anyway. I am not keeping this behind anything.

The pages went quickly. Paper burns faster than you expect, faster than grief, faster than thirty years. The ash lifted on the small wind and some of it came back and settled on my sleeve, grey flakes, and I let them stay. Somewhere in that ash was the tomato line. Good. Let the tomatoes go into the ground. That's what she would have done with them.

I stood at the bucket until the last edge of orange died, then tipped the ash out onto the soil by the wall. Brick wall, poor soil, nothing will grow there. Doesn't matter. It isn't a garden. It's just somewhere for the words to be.

* * *

Inside, I packed my tool case for the morning, because ritual is ritual and my hands need work even when my heart needs words. Tuning fork, mutes, felts, the pitch pipe. Then I stood at the case for a long moment and took the pitch pipe back out and set it on the table.

Tomorrow I'm going to blow an A into the dead pipes of the Fairweather Glasshouse, one last time, and listen to what comes back. I did it alone, two nights ago, and the pipes answered with that broken harmonic I've heard my whole life, the flattened fifth, the note the house has been holding since the fire. I used to think it was damage. I still think it's damage. But I've decided damage is also a kind of voice, and tomorrow I want to hear it once more before the excavators take it away—not to fix it, not to tune it, just to hear it, the way you'd want to hear a person's voice one more time even though you know the conversation is over.

Ivy arrives at six. We'll drive out together in her ridiculous immaculate car, past the auction signs coming down, past the neighbors' curtains twitching at the hour, and we'll stand in the ruin while the sky goes from black to that bruised grey that precedes everything. Nell will come at seven with her plans rolled under her arm, and she'll stand witness without asking anything of me, which is the greatest gift anyone has given me in thirty years.

And I will say it. Out loud. No paper. My voice will shake—I know how it shakes, I hear it in clients' voices every week when they talk about their dead—and I will let it shake, because a shaking note is still a note, and my mother spent her life among plants that bent in the wind and stood back up.

The pad sits on the table now, six torn stubs spiraling off it like a broken spine. The bucket is empty and cooling in the yard. In eight hours the machines arrive, and for the first time in my life I am not hiding from something that's coming for me.

I'm walking toward it, empty-handed, with the words already in my mouth.
