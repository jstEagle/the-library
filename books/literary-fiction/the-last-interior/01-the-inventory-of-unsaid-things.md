## 1. The Inventory of Unsaid Things

The house smelled the way it always had — dust and lemon polish and something underneath both, a mineral coldness like water over stone — and I stood in the hallway for a full minute before I could make myself go further in.

Three days before the funeral. That was the arithmetic I'd been given: my brother Daniel had booked the crematorium for Saturday because it was the only slot before the demolition surveyors came Monday, and grief, in our family, has always been a scheduling problem. He'd asked me to come early and "deal with the personal effects," which is what you say when you mean: touch everything she touched, so I don't have to.

I am a translator by trade, which means I am professionally acquainted with the fact that nothing survives its crossing intact. I should have been prepared for the house. I was not. Houses are worse than sentences; at least sentences admit they're provisional.

I set my bag down on the hall table, next to the bowl of keys that no longer opened anything but themselves, and began, as one does, with the kitchen.

* * *

My mother's name was Ilse Vessin Eastwick, though the first name appeared on almost nothing after 1974, and the second name only on things that could be taxed. To the neighbors she was Mrs. Eastwick. To Daniel and me she was Mother, delivered flatly, like a word in a language we'd agreed not to inflect. She had come to this country at twenty-two with two suitcases and a consonant system the locals found amusing, and within a decade she had sanded herself down to an accent so mild you'd have to be listening for it, and I was the only one who listened, and even I had stopped hearing it years ago.

Here is what I mean when I say I'm losing my mother tongue: last month, working on a commission — a slim, vicious novel by a man who writes about lakes — I reached for the word for the particular silence that falls between people who have decided not to discuss something. In English we have "elephant in the room," which is a joke wearing a metaphor's coat. In her language there is a word — four letters, a soft middle, a word I said ten thousand times before I was nine — and I sat at my desk and it would not come. I could see its shape the way you can see the shape of a face through frosted glass. I could draw its outline. The thing itself had gone.

This happens weekly now. It happens the way tide happens, or erosion: imperceptibly, then all at once, then you're standing on new coastline wondering where the old one went. My mother never taught me to read in her language. She spoke it on the phone twice a year, to a woman named Marta whose voice sounded like gravel being poured into a tin cup, and when she hung up she would stand at the kitchen window for a while with her hands folded, and neither Daniel nor I ever asked. Asking was not a thing our house did. Our house did casseroles and report cards and a form of love so heavily mortgaged that none of us could ever find the principal.

So: the kitchen. I washed two cups that were already clean, because that is a thing grieving people do and I refuse to pretend otherwise. Then I went upstairs, because the study was hers and the study was where the paper lived, and paper was the problem. Forty years of paper. Bills, recipes cut from magazines, Daniel's primary school drawings of horses that looked like furniture, my own university acceptance letter kept, inexplicably, in an envelope marked RECEIPTS 1989–1991.

The notebook was under the receipt envelope. As if filed. As if she'd known exactly how boring a label would keep it safe.

It was a school exercise book, the cheap kind, marbled cover gone soft at the corners, and across the front, in her small upright hand — the hand that wrote shopping lists and cheque stubs and nothing else, ever — she had written: *Inventory.*

Not *diary.* Not *notes.* Inventory. A word for stocktaking. For goods held.

I sat down on the floor of her study with my back against the bookshelf, which is not dignified, and I opened it.

* * *

The first entry was dated March 3rd, 1976 — six months before I was born.

*"Today I did not tell him about the money."*

That was all. No name for the him. No amount. Just the sentence, dated, and beneath it a blank space, as if the entry were a specimen pinned to a card and the white around it was part of the exhibit.

I turned the page. June 1976: *"Today I did not tell him I dream in the old language."*

August: *"Today I did not tell Petra—"*

And then a line struck through so hard the pen had torn the paper, and beneath the ruin, smaller: *"Nothing. Nothing today."*

I want to describe accurately what happened in my chest at that moment, and the honest description is: nothing dramatic. A click. The click of a lock you didn't know was a lock, turning, in a door you'd walked past your whole life. Grief, up to that point, had been a wet heavy coat I was carrying. It became, in that second, a lens. Everything sharpened. I read on.

The entries ran through decades. They were not confessions — that's the crucial thing, the thing that makes the notebook unlike any diary I've ever handled. Confession implies a debt owed to someone. These were more like... customs declarations. Here is what I carried and did not declare. Each entry a single sentence, each dated, each meticulously unelaborated:

*"Today I did not tell Daniel why the dog had to go."*

*"Today I did not tell them about the winter."*

*"Today I did not tell Vera she was right."*

*"Today I did not tell the priest. Today I did not tell the doctor. Today I did not tell anyone that I am afraid of the sound the furnace makes."*

Some days the entry was simply: *"Nothing unsaid today,"* and these were rarer than solar eclipses. Twice, in forty years.

And here is the part I still cannot get warm: the margins. Because the margins were not my mother's handwriting.

They were mine.

Small round letters, pencil, the fat-nibbled pencil of a child who presses too hard. Beside the entry about dreaming in the old language, age perhaps seven, I had written: *what is the old language?* And beside the entry about the dog: *I liked the dog.* And beside one from 1984 that read *"Today I did not tell them what I saw at the harbor"* — a single word in my childish hand, in her language, a word I no longer know, though I could feel it in my mouth like a stone I'd once kept under my tongue for luck.

She had shown me the notebook. When I was a child, she had let me read it — or I had found it, and she had let me believe I'd found it, which may be the same act of generosity — and I had written in it, and then, apparently, I had forgotten. Completely. The way you forget a language. The way you forget anything you were given no permission to keep.

I sat on the floor of the study until my legs went numb, reading. Outside, a neighbor's hedge trimmer droned on and off like a faulty heart monitor. The light moved across the carpet in the slow way light moves in houses that are waiting to be demolished, which is to say: with an air of having better things to do.

There were gaps in the inventory. I noticed them the way a translator notices a lacuna in a manuscript — professionally, hungrily, with a rising pulse. Pages torn out, close to the spine, neat tears. The dates around the gaps clustered somewhere in my ninth year, though I couldn't yet have said why that mattered. I noted it the way you note a wrong note in a piece of music you thought you knew: a small cold finger on the back of the neck.

The final entry was not in English.

It was near the end of the book, dated eleven days before she died, and it was one long sentence in her language — a sentence that ran on past every grammatical boundary I could remember, tenses folded into each other like hands, clauses hinged at angles the language doesn't officially permit. I read it three times. I understood maybe a third of it. Words surfaced and sank. There was a word I was nearly sure meant *kept,* or possibly *held,* or possibly something else entirely, some third thing English keeps no shelf for. The rest receded as I reached for it, the way fish recede in cloudy water, and I was left holding the shape of a sentence instead of the sentence, which is, I suppose, the condition of my entire life.

I copied it out onto the back of a pharmacy receipt, letter by letter, careful as a forger. My hand shook on the fourth word and I had to start again.

Then I closed the notebook and sat with it against my sternum and listened to the house do its evening settling — pipes ticking, a door somewhere breathing on its hinge — and I thought: she catalogued everything she never said, and she kept it in the house, and the house comes down in six weeks, and nobody living knows what any of it means except me, and I am losing the words at roughly the rate the bulldozers will lose the walls.

Grief, I've decided, is mostly a translation problem. Something enormous and original gets said once, in a language only one person speaks, and then that person dies, and the rest of us spend our lives producing versions. Versions of her. Versions of that winter she never mentioned. Versions of a sentence I can't finish reading.

I put the notebook in my bag. I did not put it back under the receipts. Whatever filing system she'd intended, the archive was open now.

* * *

Daniel called at nine, while I was eating toast standing up, which is the Eastwick family posture for all significant meals.

"Did you find the will?" he said. No hello. We communicate the way submarines do.

"I found a notebook."

"A notebook." I could hear him deciding how much energy to spend on this. "Is it financial?"

"It's a list of everything she never told us."

A pause long enough to drive a truck through. Then, carefully: "Petra. Don't do the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you turn her into a project. Where you translate her. She was a difficult woman who bought the same curtains twice and died before anyone could ask her a single direct question, and now you're going to spend six weeks excavating meaning out of a shopping list—"

"It's not a shopping list."

"—and you're going to call it grief, and it's going to hurt you, and I'm going to have to watch." His voice cracked slightly on *watch,* and I understood that underneath the ledger-keeping bluntness of him there was a boy who had also loved her badly and been loved badly back, and that his method of survival was demolition — sign the papers, level the site, build over it — while mine was archaeology. Neither method works. Both cost the same.

"I'll see you Saturday," I said.

"Saturday," he said, and hung up, and I stood in my mother's kitchen holding a phone and a notebook and a dead language, and outside the streetlight came on the way it has come on for fifty years, indifferent, punctual, lighting a house that had exactly three days left of being anybody's.

I opened the notebook one more time, just to the first page, just to check that it was real. *March 3rd, 1976. Today I did not tell him about the money.*

And there, in the margin, in pencil, in handwriting too adult and too hurried to be mine — handwriting I did not recognize at all — someone had written a single word in the old language.

I didn't know it. But I knew I'd seen it before, recently, somewhere I couldn't place — and I knew, with the certainty you get sometimes about faces in crowds, that whoever had written it might still be alive enough to ask.
