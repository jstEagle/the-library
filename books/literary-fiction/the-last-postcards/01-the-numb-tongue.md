## 1. The Numb Tongue

The scallops arrived at nine minutes past eight, and Beatrix Dravenmoor knew before the waiter set down the plate that she would have to lie about them.

She knew by the way he presented them — the little ceremonial tilt of the dish, the reverent pause, the word *seared* offered like a blessing. She knew by the price printed on the menu she'd memorized an hour earlier in the coat check line. And she knew, most reliably, by the fact that when she lifted the first scallop on her fork and placed it on her tongue, she tasted nothing at all. No butter, no brown crust, no brine of the sea it had been pulled from that morning off the coast of some town whose name she would now be expected to spell correctly in print.

She tasted the temperature of it. Warm. That was all. Warmth, and the faint suggestion of texture, like chewing on a memory of food.

"Everything to your liking so far, madam?"

Beatrix smiled the smile that had been photographed for three magazine profiles and one embarrassing billboard in the early years. It was a smile that required no sincerity, only architecture. "The scallops are extraordinary," she said. "I'll want a moment with them."

The waiter glowed and withdrew, and Beatrix sat alone at the corner table of Maison Lumen with a plate of food she could not taste and a notebook full of sentences she had not yet invented, and she felt the shame settle over her the way evening light settles over a room — gradually, then completely, until there was no corner of her that wasn't touched by it.

Fifteen years. Fifteen years of this.

She picked up her glass of Sancerre and swished it across her tongue the way the wine courses had taught her, the way her readers believed she did, the way her column — *Dravenmoor's Table*, syndicated in nine papers, quoted on menus from Portland to Providence — required her to. She could describe the wine perfectly. Gooseberry, wet flint, the green snap of cut grass. She had a vocabulary for pleasure that could make a stranger's mouth water from across a continent. She had once made a bistro in Queens famous with a single sentence about their roast chicken. She could do all of this blindfolded, which was fortunate, because in every way that mattered, she was.

She swallowed and felt nothing but the cold trail of liquid going down.

* * *

The condition — she refused to call it that, even privately; *condition* implied a diagnosis, a doctor, a conversation she would have to have with someone — had arrived the same week her father died. She remembered the exact meal, because she had written about it, because she wrote about everything, because writing about things was how she avoided having them. It was the tasting menu at Aubrac, eleven courses, the night before the funeral. Her editor had moved heaven and earth to get her the reservation; even grief, he'd said gently, was no reason to miss Aubrac.

She had eaten all eleven courses. She had taken notes between them. She remembered thinking, somewhere around the seventh course — a squab so celebrated that people flew in from Chicago for it — that the food had gone quiet. Not bad. Not dull. *Quiet*, as if someone had turned down the volume on her tongue the way you turn down a radio when the phone rings, and then forgotten, somehow, to turn it back up.

She assumed it was grief. Everyone assumes grief is temporary; that is grief's great public-relations triumph. She gave it a month. Then a season. Then a year. She saw a doctor for an unrelated matter and did not mention it. She saw a dentist who asked, casually, whether she'd noticed any changes in taste, and she lied with the fluency of a woman who lied for a living, and the dentist wrote *no significant findings* in a chart that would outlive them both.

By the second year she had stopped waiting for it to return and started building a life around its absence, the way a town rebuilds around a dried-up well. She learned to eat with her eyes first. She learned to read a kitchen by its sounds — the hiss that meant a proper hard sear, the wet slap that meant a sauté pan was crowded and the food would steam instead of brown. She learned to interrogate servers with the tenderness of a priest taking confession: *Tell me about the farm. Tell me how long it hung. Tell me what the chef was trying to do.* People loved telling her these things. People glowed. And from their glowing, from their adjectives and their pride and the way their voices lifted on the word *house-made*, she could assemble a review the way a forensic artist assembles a face from witnesses' descriptions — accurate, often beautiful, and entirely secondhand.

It had made her, if anything, a better critic. That was the cruel joke of it, the joke she told herself at two in the morning when the shame woke her: she had become the most trusted palate in America on the strength of a palate that had died with her father. Her readers trusted her because her praise was rare and therefore valuable, and her praise was rare because she could not be seduced — not by truffle oil, not by a chef's reputation, not by the little free desserts that arrived unbidden at the tables of critics. Pleasure could not bribe her. She was incorruptible the way a dead woman is incorruptible.

Tonight she would praise the scallops. They deserved praise; she could see that. The crust was the color of good toffee, crosshatched by the pan, and they had been set on a smear of cauliflower purée so pale and deliberate it looked like moonlight poured on the plate. Around her, the dining room hummed with the sound of people actually tasting things — the little involuntary *mm*, the closed eyes, the hand reaching across a table to steal from a partner's plate. Beatrix watched them the way a deaf composer watches an orchestra: with expertise, with longing, with a professional's ear for what she could no longer hear.

She opened her notebook and wrote: *The scallops at Maison Lumen arrive like small golden arguments for the existence of God.* Then she crossed it out, because it was too good, too easy, the kind of line that got framed and hung in the restaurant's entryway, and she had a reputation to protect. She was, she reminded herself, the critic who had closed two restaurants with single paragraphs. Her praise had to be earned in blood. That was the brand.

She wrote instead: *The scallops are seared with a confidence that borders on arrogance, and the kitchen gets away with it.* True, probably. Verifiable by anyone with a working tongue. She would build the review out of such stones — architecture, service, the room, the wine list, the tragic bread course — and by the time she was done, no reader would ever suspect that the woman writing it had eaten the entire tasting menu the way a blind woman walks through a gallery: by touch, by faith, by the testimony of others.

* * *

At home, past midnight, the apartment was cold in the particular way of a place where one person lives and has stopped pretending to heat all the rooms. Beatrix hung her coat, fed the cat — a fat, judgmental creature named Cardinal, acquired during her marriage and retained, like the good china, in the settlement — and stood in the kitchen eating saltines over the sink.

This was the other ritual, the shameful twin of the public one. The great critic, standing in her socks at midnight, eating saltines she could not taste, because she was hungry. She was always hungry. That was the part no one warned you about, that the tongue could die and the appetite live on, that the body would keep asking for a pleasure it could no longer receive, like a widow setting a second plate out of habit. She ate the saltines and felt the salt prickle faintly against her lips — the only sensation left to her, salt on the outside, salt where the tongue ends and the world begins — and she thought, not for the first time, that she had become a review of herself: beautifully written, technically flawless, and empty where the flavor should be.

The answering machine blinked. Her editor, Marcus, at 11:40 p.m., because Marcus had never in twenty years respected the boundary between a deadline and a life.

"Beatrix. It's Marcus. The Lumen piece is due Thursday, but — bigger thing. Call me tomorrow, don't call me tonight, I know it's late. The twentieth anniversary of the column is coming up and the paper wants a retrospective. A big one. Your greatest reviews, the stories behind them, the whole arc. *Fifteen Years at the Table* — that's the working title, don't make that face, I can hear you making it. Think about it. Call me tomorrow."

She stood in the dark kitchen with a saltine halfway to her mouth and felt the retrospective settle over her like a shroud. Fifteen years. They would want the squab at Aubrac, of course. They would want the story of the night she reviewed the most celebrated meal of her career, the one she'd eaten the week her father died. They would ask her what it tasted like, on camera, for the podcast. And she would say something perfect — she was always perfect — and the lie would be printed and archived and read aloud at whatever luncheon they gave her, and it would become, through sheer repetition, true.

She washed down the saltines with water and went to her desk, where the Lumen review waited in its open file like a patient. She wrote for an hour. She was good at this; she had to give herself that. She could make a reader taste a scallop she herself could not, could summon butter and sea and the caramel bite of the crust out of nothing but observation and craft, and when she read the draft back she felt the old flicker of pride, quickly followed by the old ash of shame, the two of them arriving together now, inseparable, like twins.

*The cauliflower purée is so restrained it functions as an apology for every over-sauced plate in the city,* she wrote, and knew it would run, and knew it was a kind of fraud, and filed the fraud under *craft*, the way she had for fifteen years, the way her mother — she thought suddenly, the thought arriving unbidden, smelling of cigarettes and pencil shavings — her mother would have understood completely.

Her mother, who had hummed. That was the strange place her mind went, standing at the window at one in the morning with Cardinal winding around her ankles. Not her mother's face, not her mother's voice on the phone, not the funeral or the house or the will that had left everything to Julian — but the humming. Her mother in the kitchen at Dravenmoor House, hands in flour, humming a melody Beatrix had never heard anywhere else, a melody her mother stopped the moment anyone else entered the room, the way a bird stops singing when you open the door. She had asked her about it once, as a girl. Her mother had said, *It's nothing, darling, it's just a thing I do,* and had gone on kneading, and had not hummed again for the rest of that visit.

Beatrix hadn't thought of the melody in years. Now it surfaced whole, note for note, the way childhood things do — not remembered but *replayed*, a recording stored in some room of her she hadn't entered since. She stood at the window humming it herself, quietly, testing it against the dark, and it was exactly right, every interval where her body expected it to be, and she felt — for the first time in longer than she could calculate — something move in her that was not shame and not hunger and not grief. Something older. Something with a shape.

She went to bed. She did not sleep. At six a.m., giving up, she made coffee and turned on the kitchen radio, because silence at dawn had become unbearable somewhere in the last year and she had not examined why.

The host's voice was bright and half-awake, introducing a morning session: *a young band out of Montreal, recorded live in our studio, songs from their debut record — and stick around, because their lead singer has a story about the last track that I don't fully believe, but you'll want to hear it.*

Beatrix sipped her coffee, which tasted like temperature and bitterness, the two honest flavors left to her.

And then, through the speakers, over the sound of chairs and laughter and the tuning of a guitar, the band began to play — and the melody rose out of the morning like a hand out of dark water, and Beatrix Dravenmoor stood motionless in her kitchen with the cup halfway to her lips, listening to a song that had supposedly never been written down, that her dead mother had sworn existed nowhere but in her own head, played by strangers who could not possibly know it, note for note, every interval exactly where her body expected it to be.
