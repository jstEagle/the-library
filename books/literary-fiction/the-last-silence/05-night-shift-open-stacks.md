## 5. Night Shift, Open Stacks

The library at three in the morning was the only honest room in the city.

Gideon had believed this for thirty years, and he still believed it now, though the belief had begun to feel like a load-bearing wall with a crack running through it. The stacks didn't lie. The books sat where the catalog said they would sit, spines out, patient, indifferent to whatever a person needed them to be. A patron at 3 a.m. wanted a book on grief and you found it for him and there was no performance in that, no smile required, no story to keep straight. The Dewey decimal system had never once asked him to swear to something untrue.

He pushed the cart down the 300s — sociology, economics, the sciences of how people fool themselves in groups — and let his hands do the work while his mind did something else entirely.

Marisol's card was in his breast pocket. He could feel it there like a splinter worked too deep to dig out with fingers. *Rafael Valderrama, Inmate No. 44719, Soledad.* He hadn't opened the box she'd left with him. It sat in his locker downstairs, wrapped in brown paper, tied with string, looking for all the world like something a person could simply return unopened. That had been the deal. One box. Nothing more. He'd agreed to it the way you agree to hold a hot pan — knowing you'll drop it, just not yet.

But the box wasn't where the trouble started. The trouble started earlier that evening, when he'd been reshelving the reference requests and found the slip of paper in his own handwriting, dated eleven days ago: *Municipal Archives — insurance files, 1968–1974. Request pulled for patron.* A patron who never came. A request he had no memory of making, except that his handwriting was unmistakable, the small severe capitals he'd used since childhood, since the years when a boy learned to write carefully because careless writing got questioned.

Eleven days ago. Before the funeral. Before Marisol said the name aloud on the sidewalk like a key turning in a lock he'd painted over decades ago.

Had some part of him already been digging? Had the silence started leaking before anyone cracked it?

He finished the 300s. He shelved the 900s. Then he did what he had spent thirty years not doing, which was walk to the archive room on the sub-level and unlock it with his own key.

* * *

The municipal insurance files were stored in gray archival boxes arranged by year, and the year he wanted was 1971, and his hands were steady until they weren't.

He told himself this was due diligence. A librarian verifying a record. If a stranger claimed a historical injustice, the professional response was documentation, nothing more. He was not investigating his family. He was cataloging.

The fire report from the Valderrama Street building ran forty-one pages, most of it boilerplate — construction type, occupancy class, the dry vocabulary of loss. Two fatalities. Cause listed as *undetermined, suspected incendiary.* Suspect named in the attached police cross-reference: Rafael Valderrama, tenant, second-floor rear unit. Motive cited: eviction proceedings pending.

Gideon read it twice under the green desk lamp, and both times his eye snagged on the same thing, the way a tongue returns to a chipped tooth.

Point of origin.

The fire investigator's diagram showed it plainly, a red star penciled on the second floor — but not in Rafael's unit. Not in the rear. The star sat in the front stairwell landing, the common hall, the space between apartments. And the narrative section, in the flat procedural prose of men who write reports at the end of long careers, said: *Origin appears to be at or near the base of the interior stairwell, second floor. Accelerant residue consistent with kerosene detected on treads of stairs two through four.*

Kerosene on the stairs. Not in an apartment. Someone had carried fire up those stairs, or poured it down them, and the report's author had noticed this and then, apparently, stopped noticing it. Because the next paragraph pivoted without transition to Rafael's evictions, his temper, a neighbor's statement about shouting matches, as if the geography of the fire itself were an inconvenient detail best absorbed into a more convenient story.

Gideon sat very still in the green light.

Second floor. Common stairwell. And here was the fact that arrived in his chest like cold water finding a crack: access to that building after ten p.m. was through two doors, each with a keyed lock, and the keys to the building's interior — he knew this the way you know your own heartbeat, from a childhood spent being handed things and told to hold them — the keys belonged to the family. His family. His mother kept them on a nail behind the kitchen door of the apartment on Alameda, five iron keys on a loop of wire, and she counted them the way other women counted rosary beads.

No one but family had keys to the stairs where the fire began.

He turned pages with hands that had gone foreign to him. There was a supplemental inspection, six weeks post-fire, noting that the second-floor landing had been swept and mopped before investigators' second visit — *cleaning inconsistent with condition of rest of premises* — and no record of who had done the cleaning. There was a claim settlement paid to the ownership entity, and the ownership entity was a name he had never heard spoken at any table he'd ever sat at, and when he traced it through the property filings two boxes over, the signature on the incorporation papers was his uncle Emilio's, in the looping confident hand Gideon remembered from birthday cards.

Emilio, who had died rich and mourned. Emilio, whose photograph still hung in Amparo's hallway, silver-haired and smiling, a man the family toasted every Thanksgiving whether he was alive to toast or not.

Gideon closed the box. He did not open Marisol's package. He sat in the archive room until the lamp buzzed, doing arithmetic he didn't want to finish, and the sum kept coming out the same: the boy on the stairs had seen what he'd seen, and everyone else had always known it.

* * *

"You always did work nights like a man hiding from his own house."

Amparo's voice came from the top of the archive stairs, and Gideon did not startle, because startling was another thing the family had trained out of him early. She descended slowly, one hand on the rail, wearing her funeral coat even though the funeral had been three weeks ago — she owned a coat the color of endings and wore it like armor against ordinary weather.

"How did you find me?"

"I called the main branch and asked what time the night librarian took his dinner." She came to the bottom of the stairs and looked at the table: the open box, the report, the diagram with its red star. "You didn't eat tonight."

"No."

"No," she agreed softly. "You've been down here reading about the fire."

It wasn't a question, so he didn't answer it. He watched his sister take in the scene — her brother, the gray boxes, the evidence spread out like a confession — and he watched her face do something he had never seen it do in sixty-one years of knowing her. It went blank. Not calm. Blank, the way a house goes dark room by room when someone is moving out.

"That woman put you up to this." Amparo picked up the first page of the report without really reading it, holding it the way you hold something dead. "The Valderrama girl. She came to the funeral like a vulture and now she's got you pulling files in the middle of the night—"

"She didn't pull these files, Amparo. I did. Eleven days ago. Before I ever met her." He stood. "I found the request slip in my own handwriting and I don't remember writing it. Isn't that something? Some part of me has been trying to get back into this room my whole life."

"Then some part of you needs to be locked out." She set the page down with exaggerated care, aligning its edge with the table's edge, and he recognized the gesture — their mother's gesture, the straightening of objects when the world inside was tilting. "Gideon. Mijo. Listen to me. Whatever you think you're going to find in these boxes, it isn't there. The fire was investigated. A man was convicted. Courts don't make mistakes like that."

"Courts make exactly mistakes like that when nobody with standing stands up." His voice came out low and strange to him. "The fire started on the stairs, Amparo. The common stairs. Kerosene on the treads. And the only people with keys to that building were us."

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight and grain, like old fabric.

"You've been digging up graves," she said finally, and her voice cracked on the last word, which frightened him more than shouting would have. "Thirty years, Gideon. Thirty years we kept it buried, and Tomás is barely cold, and you're down here with a shovel."

"Then tell me why it needed burying." He came around the table. "Tell me the official version. Tell me what Mother made us all agree to. Because I'm starting to think you know the shape of the hole, Amparo. I'm starting to think you've known the shape of it your whole life, and you've just never asked what made it."

"I was fourteen," she said, too fast, too flat. "I was fourteen and I remember the fire and I remember Rafael Valderrama screaming in the street and I remember Mother counting the keys afterward, over and over, like she was praying with them. That's all I know. That's everything."

But her eyes had gone to the diagram. To the red star. And they had stayed there one beat too long, the way eyes stay on a face you're trying not to recognize.

"Amparo." Gideon kept his voice gentle, the voice he used with patrons at 3 a.m., the ones carrying their own private fires. "Why did we change our name? Nobody ever answered me. Not once in fifty years. You were there when Mother burned the documents in the sink. You held the screen door. What were we running from, if not the fire? The fire was public. The fire was in every paper. Changing our name protected us from nothing — unless we weren't running from the fire at all."

His sister looked at him across the archive table, across the gray boxes and the red star and thirty years of practiced silence, and for one naked moment she looked less like the woman who had raised him half herself than like a girl of fourteen standing at a screen door, watching her mother feed the family's history into the drain and strike the match.

"Gideon," she said, and her voice was almost tender, almost pleading, "some questions you ask and the answer takes something from you that you don't get back. I'm asking you — as the last person who loves you without conditions — don't ask this one."

And that was when he understood, with a certainty that felt less like discovery than like remembering, that his sister was not protecting him from the truth.

She was protecting herself from having to watch him find it.

She left the way she'd come, coat whispering on the concrete stairs, and Gideon stood alone in the green light among the records of the dead, and above him, faint through the floor, he heard the library's great clock begin to count toward four.

He thought of the boy on the back stairs. He thought of the kerosene smell, sweet and wrong, like flowers rotting. He thought: *I saw someone.*

And the memory, coaxed by the red star and his sister's fear, began — slowly, terribly — to come home.
