## 5. Demolition Date

The notice came on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult. Tuesdays were the days I transcribed wills at the county records office, the days most given over to the dead and their tidy arrangements, and it was on a Tuesday that the state decided to arrange my brother's grave.

I found it stapled to the fence post at the edge of the lot—the fence that had gone up the summer after the fire, chain-link and municipal gray, and never come down. A sheet of paper, A4, laminated against the weather. *AVIS PUBLIC*. The town's seal at the top, the tricolor, the motto I had never once read closely. Below it, in the flat bureaucratic French of people who have never had to say a hard thing out loud:

*In accordance with Resolution 2019-44, the parcel known as Lot 7, Rue des Saules, is scheduled for clearance and regrading in preparation for the construction of municipal parking facilities. Clearance to commence on or about the 14th of June. Objections must be filed in writing with the Office of Public Works within thirty (30) days of posting.*

Fourteen June. Six weeks.

I stood at the fence for a long time. The lot behind it had not changed in eleven years, which was itself a kind of change—the way a wound that refuses to close becomes its own feature of the body. Charred foundation stones. The ghost of the front steps. Weeds that had made their peace with ash and grew anyway, stubborn and gray-green, the only things in town that had never been asked to forget anything. In the far corner, mostly swallowed by bramble, the foundation of the boathouse, which had burned too but burned lower, and which the demolition crews in the first frantic week had been ordered—ordered, I understood now, though I hadn't understood it then—not to touch.

*Objections must be filed in writing.*

I thought about who had filed the resolution. I thought about whose signature sat at the bottom of Resolution 2019-44, and what it had cost, and what it purchased. A car park. Forty spaces, probably. A place where the town could leave its cars while it went about the business of never thinking about the Joubert house.

I took a photograph of the notice with my phone, because that is what we do now instead of tearing things down, and then I drove home with my hands at ten and two, very carefully, the way you drive when part of you wants to drive through something.

* * *

At the records office the next morning, I pulled the file. This is the small superpower of my profession: I know where the town keeps itself. Lot 7, Rue des Saules. The parcel history was thin in the way that thin things are deliberately thin. Deeded to Auguste Joubert, 1981. Structure destroyed by fire, 2014. Structure demolished under emergency order, 2014—signed, A. Joubert, presiding magistrate, recused in name only, I'd have testified, if anyone had ever asked me to testify to anything. Parcel retained. Taxes paid, every year, by an account I had always assumed was my father's and now understood was my father's *arrangement*. And then, dated eleven weeks ago, a transfer request: parcel conveyed to the Municipality of Saint-Aubin-les-Bains for consideration of one euro, for the purpose of public improvement.

One euro. My brother's grave had been sold for one euro, and I hadn't even been notified, because technically the parcel belonged to my father, and technically my father was of sound mind, and technically the family had never contested any of it. Eleven years of technicalities. The whole architecture of the silence was built out of technicalities, each one load-bearing, each one signed.

The clerk who processed the transfer request was a woman named Odile who had worked the counter since before I was born. I asked her, casually, who had initiated the conveyance.

She looked at me over her glasses with the mild pity clerks reserve for people who ask questions the paperwork has already answered. "The mayor's office, chérie. It's all there. Delacroix's office. Very standard. Public improvement, community benefit." She stamped something. "They're doing the whole east end. New lighting, new paving. It'll be lovely."

"It'll be a car park."

"It'll be *progress*," she said, and smiled, and I understood that Odile was not a villain. Odile was a gear. The whole town was gears, and the machine they turned had been running so long that no one could hear it anymore except the people it had been built to grind.

* * *

I drove to my father's house without deciding to. That is how these things work in our family—motion precedes decision, and decision, when it finally arrives, is always dressed as something else. A visit. A Sunday lunch. A son or daughter dropping by.

He was in the garden, of course. Auguste Joubert at seventy-four was still a handsome man in the way that portraits are handsome, all composition and lighting, his white hair cut every three weeks by the same barber who had cut it when he was a magistrate. He was pruning the roses with the small gold-handled shears I'd bought him for his sixtieth birthday, back when I still believed gifts meant what they said.

"Inès." He didn't look up. He always knew my footsteps; I used to think it was love. "The 'Mme Isaac Pereire' is trying to take over the fence again. You'd think after forty years it would learn its place."

"Father. There's a notice on the lot."

The shears paused. Just for a moment—a half-second, a held breath—before they resumed their work. If I hadn't been watching for it, I'd have missed it. I had been watching for it for eleven years without knowing that was what I was doing.

"So I'm told." Snip. "A car park. Well. Progress." He said the word the way Odile had said it, and I understood where she'd learned it, or where the whole town had learned it, the word passed down like a recipe. "It's an eyesore, that lot. It always has been. People avert their eyes when they drive past. You can see them do it."

"People avert their eyes because they were taught to."

"People avert their eyes because averted eyes are comfortable, and comfort is the only civic virtue anyone has ever actually practiced." He straightened, pressing a hand to his lower back, the performance of an old man, though his hands were steady on the shears. "Would you like coffee? Camille sent over a galette yesterday. It's from the good bakery, the one on the square, not the one that's gone downhill."

"There was a public notice, and I wasn't informed. The parcel was in your name. You sold it for a euro."

"I donated it. The euro is a formality. It looks better in the ledger." He set the shears on the stone bench and finally looked at me, and his eyes were warm, they were always warm, that was the terrible thing about my father, that his warmth was real and also a tool, the way a fire is real and also burns down the evidence. "Inès. Sit down. You've got that look. Your mother had that look, before you were born, when the well ran dry and everyone said we'd have to leave the valley. It's the look of someone who thinks the world can be argued with."

"The world can be *filed with*. Thirty days for objections. I'm filing one."

He was quiet for a moment. The garden was very quiet around him—bees, the neighbor's radio playing something with accordions, the ordinary sounds of a town that had agreed on its own story. "And what will you say in your objection? That you have feelings about a foundation? The office will file it in the drawer where feelings go, and the bulldozers will come in June, and in July there will be forty spaces and a nice new lamp, and people will park there and lock their cars and walk into town and buy bread, and no one—*no one*, Inès—will think about what was there before, because no one thinks about what was there before now." He picked up the shears again. "That is not a conspiracy. That is mercy. I've told you. The town needed to heal."

"The town needed to *forget*. Those aren't the same thing, and you know it, because you're the one who taught me the difference. You made me learn it. You made me learn the whole vocabulary—*recusal*, *prejudice*, *burden of proof*. You sat me at that table when I was nine and made me decline nouns, and now I decline everything you say."

He smiled. It was a good smile. It had been disarming judges and juries and my mother for fifty years. "You always were my best student."

"That's not a compliment anymore, Father."

"No," he said, softly, and for a moment—just a moment—the magistrate left his face, and something older and tireder looked out from behind it, something that might, in a better light, have been grief. "No, I suppose it isn't." Then the magistrate came back, the way weather comes back, and he gestured with the shears toward the house. "Coffee. Galette. Come inside and tell me about the wills. There was one last week, old Marchetti, left everything to his cats—tell me you got that one."

I didn't go inside. I stood in his garden, among the roses he pruned so beautifully, and I understood that I was looking at the man who had arranged my whole life the way he arranged this garden—everything cut back, everything in its place, everything blooming on schedule over ground that had been cleared of something first.

"Six weeks," I said.

"Five and a half," he said, and pruned.

* * *

That night I took out the postcards.

Eleven years of them—the drafts, I mean, the ones I'd written before mailing, because I kept copies in a shoebox, in the same handwriting I'd used since I was seven, the handwriting Etienne used to say looked like a spider had survived a flood. I spread them on the kitchen table under the lamp. *Dear Etienne. The lilacs came early this year. I transcribed a will today in which a woman left her house to her sister and her anger to no one, though I don't think that's transferable.* Postcard after postcard, one a month, one hundred and thirty-two of them, each one a small act of refusal, each one addressed to a house that had stopped existing before the first one was ever stamped.

And now the ground they were addressed to was going away. That was the thing I couldn't get past, sitting there in the lamplight. Not the car park—cars could park on anything, cars park on graves all over the world. It was the *grading*. I'd looked up the word in the technical annex, because of course there was a technical annex, my father's fingerprints were on it. Clearance and regrading. They would strip the topsoil. They would take the foundation stones. They would level the boathouse foundation that the first crews had been ordered to leave, and they would bring in fill from somewhere else, clean fill, innocent fill, and the ground itself would stop being the ground it was. Whatever was in that soil—whatever the fire had left, whatever the eleven years of weeds had held, whatever the town had buried and my father had preserved by the simple expedient of never letting anyone near it—would be scraped up and hauled away, legally, with permits, in daylight, to somewhere I would never find.

The last physical evidence, vanishing legally, finally. My father had spent eleven years keeping that lot untouched, and I had spent eleven years thinking it was sentiment. It wasn't sentiment. It was *custody*. He had been keeping the ground the way you keep a letter you haven't decided whether to burn—because the day it's destroyed, the choice is made for you, and some part of him, some part buried deeper than the magistrate, had not been willing to let the choice be made.

And now it was being made. By Delacroix's office. By Resolution 2019-44. By a euro and a stamp and a laminated notice, and five and a half weeks.

Unless someone filed an objection.

I sat with that. The lamp hummed. Outside, the town did its nighttime work of being quiet. And I thought about what my father had said—*the drawer where feelings go*—and I thought: he assumes I'll write feelings. He assumes I'll write about lilacs and grief and a brother, and it will go in the drawer, and the bulldozers will come, and he will have been right one more time, which is the thing he loves most in the world, being right, being right the way other men are loved.

So I wouldn't write feelings.

I got a fresh sheet of paper, and at the top I wrote: *Objection to Resolution 2019-44, Re: Lot 7, Rue des Saules.* And beneath it, in my spider-survived-a-flood handwriting, I began to make a list. Not of feelings. Of facts. The emergency demolition order of 2014 and the sections of the lot excluded from it. The conveyance executed without notice to all heirs. The question of who had ordered the first crews to leave the boathouse foundation standing, and under what authority, and *why*. Each fact a stone. Each stone a foundation. I didn't know yet what I was building. I only knew that for eleven years I had been writing to a house, and the house was gone, and the ground was next, and if the ground went, then everything Etienne was would exist only in a shoebox and a drawer and a song on the radio that no one in this town would admit to hearing.

I wrote until two in the morning. When I finished, the list had filled three pages and ended, in the margin, with a question I hadn't planned and didn't cross out:

*Who benefits from the timing?*

Because that was the thing I kept circling back to, the splinter in all of it. Eleven years the lot had sat untouched, an eyesore everyone averted their eyes from, and no one had ever once proposed a car park. And then—eleven weeks ago, a conveyance. Eleven weeks ago was when the song had started playing on Radio Saint-Aubin, a song in a key only one dead man knew. Eleven weeks ago someone in the mayor's office had heard it too.

Somebody else thought the song was a confession. Somebody else was cleaning up before anyone could read the ground.

I put the list in an envelope, and I put the envelope in my bag, and I lay down without sleeping, and in the gray hour before morning I was already dressed, because there was one more fact I needed before I filed, one more name, and it belonged to a woman at the post office who had never once asked me a question in eleven years, and who had, I was suddenly, coldly certain, been keeping something of her own.
