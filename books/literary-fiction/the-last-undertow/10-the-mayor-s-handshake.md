## 10. The Mayor's Handshake

The call came at seven in the morning, which was the first thing wrong with it.

Mayors did not call at seven in the morning. Mayors called at eleven, after their staff had vetted the day, or at four, when they wanted to be seen working late. Seven in the morning meant someone had not slept, and that someone was me.

"Alain Delacroix," my assistant had said, as if I needed an introduction to my own name. "He'd like fifteen minutes. Today if possible."

I stood in my kitchen holding the phone and looking out at the river, which was doing what it always did—moving without going anywhere, carrying everything downstream and delivering none of it. Fifteen minutes. As if this were a ribbon-cutting. As if there were a version of this conversation that fit inside fifteen minutes.

I said yes because saying no would have been a message too, and messages were what I could no longer afford to send carelessly.

* * *

Town hall at nine smelled of floor wax and cut flowers, the way it always did before anything official happened. Delacroix kept fresh flowers in his office year-round—white lilies, mostly—and I had never once thought about what that cost until now, standing in his doorway while he came around his desk with both hands extended, as though we were old friends separated by circumstance rather than by everything.

"Ines Joubert." He said my name carefully, the way you set down something breakable. "Thank you for coming. Please—sit. Coffee? Tea? Martine makes a coffee that could raise the dead."

"I've had coffee," I said.

"Of course. Of course." He sat across from me, knees together, hands folded on them like a man in a waiting room rather than a man behind a desk. He was smaller than he looked in the paper. That was the thing about men who arranged themselves for photographs—they were always slightly less than their images, a suit with the person half-ironed out of it. His hair was silvered at the temples in a way that looked maintained rather than earned. His cufflinks caught the light: small, gold, unremarkable, expensive.

"You'll forgive the early hour," he said. "I'm an early riser myself. Always have been. There's a particular clarity to the morning, don't you find? Before the town wakes up and starts needing things."

"The town needs things all night," I said. "It just doesn't say so."

He laughed—a good laugh, warm, practiced, and underneath it something flickered and went out, quick as a match in wind. "That's very much your brother in you, I think. The wit."

I let that sit between us. He watched it sit there and understood, correctly, that I was not going to pick it up for him.

"Ines." He leaned forward, and the practiced warmth dropped away—not entirely, but enough that I could see the machinery under it, gears turning, a man doing arithmetic in real time. "I'm going to speak plainly, because I believe you're someone who values plain speech, and because the town has valued discretion for a long time, and I think discretion has perhaps served us unevenly."

"That's one way to put it."

"It's the only honest way." He spread his hands. "You've been asking questions. I want to be clear—I'm not here to tell you to stop. I'm here because I'd rather answer questions than have them asked behind my back. There's a difference between transparency and ambush, and I'd like us to choose transparency."

"And the song?"

His face did something very small. A muscle near the eye. "The song is a beautiful piece of music," he said. "It's gotten a great deal of attention. Which is why I think the moment is right—for recognition. For memory done properly, at last." He opened a folder on his desk, squared its edges against the wood, a gesture so fastidious it made my skin prickle. "The town has never honored Etienne. Not really. A plaque in the school gymnasium doesn't honor anyone; it absolves the plaque-hangers. I'd like to do more."

He slid a document across the desk toward me. I didn't touch it.

"A seat on the municipal heritage board," he said. "Paid—it's a stipend, modest, but real. And alongside it, a named fund. The Etienne Joubert Fund for Local Music. Instruments for children, lessons, a small festival in the square each summer. Your brother loved music. Let the town love it in his name." He smiled, and it was almost a real smile. "Let him belong to everyone, Ines. That's what he wanted, isn't it? To be heard?"

There it was. Not a bribe—the word never appeared, would never appear, would be deniable in any court in the country—but a bribe dressed so beautifully in recognition that refusing it would look like grief curdled into spite. A seat at the table. A stipend. My brother's name spoken aloud in official rooms instead of whispered in doorways. Everything I had wanted for eleven years, offered by the man who had helped bury the reason I wanted it.

And he knew it. That was the terrible craft of it. He wasn't buying my silence with money; he was offering me belonging, which is the one currency a person who has mailed postcards to a burned house cannot easily refuse.

"You've thought about this," I said.

"I think about this town every day," he said. "Every single day. It's the privilege and the burden of the work." He paused, and when he spoke again his voice had changed register—lower, almost confiding, the voice of a man letting you see the cost of things. "Ines, can I tell you something off the record? Not as mayor. Just as—someone who was young once, and stupid, and loyal to the wrong people in the wrong ways."

I said nothing. He took it as permission.

"This town runs on arrangements. Not corruption—arrangements. There's a difference, or there was supposed to be. An arrangement is when two people who want different things agree to want the same thing for a while. Nobody lies, exactly. Things are simply... not said. And the not-saying holds up bridges, keeps factories open, keeps families whole." He looked at his hands. "The trouble with arrangements is that they outlive the people who made them. They become the weather. You inherit a sky you never chose, and you keep it, because what else do you do with a sky?"

"What happened the night of the fire?" I asked.

The question landed in the room like a dropped glass. He didn't flinch—that would have been too easy, too legible—but everything in him went still, the stillness of a man listening to determine how much of the building he's standing in has already fallen.

"I was twenty-six years old," he said quietly. "I idolized Auguste Joubert. We all did. He was the smartest man any of us had ever met, and he was kind to those of us who weren't from anywhere. Do you know what that's worth, kindness from a man like that, when you're nobody?" He shook his head slowly. "Whatever happened that night, whatever decisions were made—I was a boy standing in the shadow of a giant. If the giant turned left, I turned left. That's not an excuse. It's a confession of smallness."

"So you're telling me you were small."

"I'm telling you I've spent thirty years being useful to this town in exchange for never having to be large about one night." He met my eyes then, and I will give him this: he didn't look away. "You can hate me for it. I've hated myself efficiently enough for both of us, most days."

For a moment—just a moment—I believed him. That was the seduction, and I felt it work on me like warm water: the sense that here, finally, was an adult willing to say the unsayable, that power might be capable of shame, that the system contained within itself the seed of its own accounting. He was offering me not silence but companionship in guilt. It was almost better than truth. It was almost family.

Then I remembered Sylvie's drawer full of returned postcards. Eleven years of them, kept by a woman who couldn't destroy them and couldn't send them. The town was full of drawers like that. Full of people keeping what they couldn't use and couldn't burn. And here was the mayor of all those drawers, offering me lilies and a stipend and a seat at the table where the sky got decided.

"The fund," I said. "Who administers it?"

"The heritage board. With oversight—" he gestured vaguely, generously, "—from your family, naturally. You'd chair the naming committee."

So I would administer my brother's ghost, under supervision, with a budget. I would become part of the arrangement. The thought arrived with a physical sensation, a settling, like a bone sliding back into a joint it had been out of for years—and that frightened me more than anything else in the room, because it felt good. Belonging felt good. Being offered something felt good. Eleven years of one-way correspondence and here, at last, was a letter that answered back.

I could take it. I could take the board and the fund and the stipend, and I could do genuine good with the money—instruments in children's hands, Etienne's melody played by a dozen kids in the square every summer—and the town would exhale, and the file would close, and the song would become heritage instead of testimony. That was the offer. That was always the offer. Not money. Continuity.

"I need to think," I said.

"Of course." Relief moved through him like weather through wheat—he controlled it, but I saw it, and seeing it told me everything the conversation hadn't. He was terrified. The polished man with the lilies and the cufflinks was a man standing in a burning house negotiating with the fire. "Take all the time you need. Though—" and here he smiled again, rueful, human, "—the demolition notice has already gone out, as I'm sure you know. Six weeks. The lot clears either way. The fund doesn't depend on the land, Ines. Memory doesn't need soil."

Memory doesn't need soil. I stood, and he stood, and he walked me to the door with his hand hovering near my elbow, not quite touching, guiding without gripping—the whole man in miniature.

"One more thing," I said at the threshold. "Marisol Vane. The singer. She learned that song somewhere. Have you spoken with her?"

Something crossed his face then that I will be thinking about for a long time. Not fear exactly. Recognition—the look of a man hearing a name he'd hoped had drowned.

"Miss Vane is a talented performer," he said. "The town has been generous to her over the years. Very generous." He held out his hand. "She knows what generosity costs."

I took his hand. It was dry, cool, perfectly steady—which surprised me, given the morning call, given the flowers, given everything. Only at the very end, as our grip parted, did I feel it: the faintest tremor, quickly mastered, a man's pulse knocking once against my palm like something locked in a box, asking to be let out.

"Think about the fund," he said. "Etienne deserves it. Whatever else is true, Ines—he deserves to be remembered well."

"He does," I said. "That's the problem. Everyone keeps deciding what remembering him well looks like. Nobody's asked him."

I left. Behind me I heard him pour himself a drink at nine-forty in the morning, the liquid hitting the glass with the precision of a man who had done it many times before and intended, for as long as possible, to keep doing it invisibly.

* * *

Outside, the town was conducting its ordinary morning—bakers, school buses, the hardware store rolling up its shutters—and I walked through it carrying the weight of how nearly I had said yes. Not to the money. To the table. To the chair with my name on it, the warm water of being included, the end of being a tenant in my own life.

At the corner by the pharmacy, Marisol Vane was posting flyers for her Friday set at the bar. She saw me and stopped, flyer in hand, and neither of us spoke, and in that silence I understood that she had also been offered something—or warned of something—and that whatever Delacroix had said to me, he had already said a version of it to her, in a room with lilies, with his hand hovering near her elbow, not quite touching.

She folded the flyer in half, and then in half again, until it was too small to read.

"Friday," she said. "Eight o'clock. I'm singing the second verse."

Then she walked away, and I stood in the middle of the town that had raised me and buried my brother, holding nothing, declining nothing, and understanding that the next move was no longer only mine—and that Friday was three days closer than six weeks.
