## 4. What the Mother Kept

The care home was called Windrose, which Coral had always thought was a name invented by someone who had never actually been outside in bad weather. It sat on a rise above the town with its back to the sea, as if it had turned around to avoid looking at the water, and its garden furniture was the kind that came in flat boxes and required an Allen key and a certain resignation.

Coral visited on Thursdays. She had chosen Thursday for no reason she could defend—perhaps because it was the day her mother had always done the accounts, sitting at the kitchen table with a pencil behind her ear like a carpenter of numbers—and now Thursday had become simply *the day*, and Marguerite expected it, or expected something, in the way that a plant expects morning.

She signed in at the desk, where a young man with a lanyard and the serene competence of someone twenty-two years old greeted her by name.

"Your mum's having a good day," he said. "She's been singing."

"Singing," Coral repeated.

"Something in—well, I don't know what language. I assumed French? She has that sort of face."

Marguerite did have that sort of face. She had spent forty years letting people assume French, or Italian, or a general Continental elegance, without ever correcting them, which Coral now recognized as a kind of professional habit—the habit of a woman who kept one whole self in reserve.

"She sings beautifully," Coral said, which was true, and also a deflection, and she noticed herself doing it, the way you notice your own hand reaching for the light switch in a familiar room.

* * *

Her mother was in the sunroom, in the chair by the window that she had claimed in the first week and defended since with the quiet ferocity of a seagull over chips. She was small these days—not shrunk exactly, but edited, the way a manuscript is edited, whole clauses removed until only the essential sentences remained. Her hair was still white and still defiantly waved, done every Tuesday by a woman named Bev who charged extra for opinions and gave them anyway.

"Coral," Marguerite said, pleased and precise. "Thursday."

"Thursday," Coral agreed, and kissed the papery cheek, which smelled of talc and, faintly, of the sea, though the sea was half a mile away and downhill.

They did the ritual things. Coral produced the biscuits—ginger nuts, because her mother had once said, decades ago, that ginger nuts were the only honest biscuit, everything else being performance—and they ate two each and discussed the weather, which was doing something dramatic out over the water, great bruised clouds shouldering in from the southwest.

"The garden's flooding again," Marguerite said. "By the wall. Where the mint is."

"I'll mention it to them."

"Don't mention it to *Denise*. Denise will put gravel down. Gravel is what people do when they've given up on a garden." She said this with such force that a nurse passing through glanced over, amused.

It was going well. It was, by the standards of the last two years, a very good visit: her mother lucid, funny, anchored. Coral knew better than to relax entirely—these days were weather, not climate—but she let herself sit in it, the way you sit in a patch of sun knowing the cloud is coming.

Then, while Coral was peeling the cellophane off a second packet of biscuits, her mother began to hum.

It took Coral a moment even to register it as language. The tune was one she knew in her bones and could not have sourced—a falling, rocking melody, three phrases, the last one turning upward like a question. A lullaby. Her mother used to hum it; Coral had carried it all her life the way you carry a scar's shape without remembering the wound.

But today her mother wasn't humming. Today she was singing words.

The words were soft, half-swallowed, worn smooth as pebbles. And they were not English. They were not French.

Coral went very still, the biscuit packet forgotten in her lap.

*Kerra whey, kerra low,* her mother sang, to the window, to the weather, to some child who was not in the room and had never been in the room. *Mor vyan, mor vean, gwel an ster'n eul.*

The sounds went through Coral like a current through wet sand. She knew them. Not their meaning—she didn't know their meaning—but their *shape*. She had heard this language before, long ago, in fragments, in corners: a word muttered over a burnt pan, a phrase exchanged once with old Mrs. Tregose down the lane when they'd both thought the children were out of earshot, a blessing of sorts her mother whispered over the washing line as if the clothes might hear and be comforted. A private weather of syllables that Coral had filed, all these years, under *mother's noises*, alongside the way she clicked her tongue at horses and hummed when she was anxious.

Nobody had ever told her it was a language. Nobody had ever said: this is Kernew, this is the old speech of this coast, this is mine and I am keeping it from you.

Or perhaps nobody had needed to say it. Perhaps the keeping *was* the message.

"Mum," Coral said carefully. "What's that song?"

Her mother stopped mid-phrase. For a moment she looked at Coral with an expression Coral couldn't read—something guarded moving behind the mildness, a door closing gently rather than slamming.

"It's just a song," Marguerite said. "A silly song."

"What are the words?"

"You wouldn't know them." She smiled then, the social smile, the one she'd deployed at parents' evenings and funerals, flawless and load-bearing. "More tea?"

There was no tea. There were biscuits and there was rain starting against the sunroom glass, and there was her mother, offering her a cup of nothing, changing the subject with the same smooth motion she'd used Coral's entire childhood to steer conversations away from money, from grief, from the summer Coral was nine and something happened that the family had folded up so small and put away so deep that Coral sometimes doubted it had happened at all.

"Yes," Coral said. "Lovely."

* * *

She stayed another hour. They talked about Denise and the gravel, about Graham's last visit (her mother's account of it was oddly vague—"he was here, he brought his folder"), about whether Nell had got the hedge sorted, about a documentary on otters that Marguerite recommended with real passion. All of it pleasant. All of it surface. Coral found herself listening underneath the conversation the way you listen for a second melody in a piece of music, waiting for her mother to slip again.

She didn't slip again. But at the door, when Coral kissed her goodbye, Marguerite held her hand a moment longer than usual and said, clearly and distinctly:

"*Dala dywans.*"

The words meant nothing to Coral. The tone meant everything—it was the tone of *be careful crossing the road*, of *ring me when you arrive*, of every small benediction her mother had ever issued. An instruction wrapped around a tenderness.

"What does that mean?" Coral asked.

Her mother looked at her for a long moment. The afternoon light caught the white hair, the faded blue eyes, and somewhere in those eyes was a flicker—not confusion, not the disease, but something older and more deliberate. A look like a woman checking that a drawer is locked.

"It means drive safely," she said.

And Coral, professional translator, thirty years of carrying other people's meanings across borders intact, heard the translation for what it was: not wrong, exactly, but a decoy. *Drive safely* was what the words might mean. It was also very much not all they meant. You could feel the surplus in them, the way you can feel a parcel is heavier than its label claims.

* * *

In the car park the rain had arrived properly, drumming on the roof, and Coral sat with the engine off and her hands on the wheel and let herself think the thought she'd been holding at arm's length all afternoon.

Her mother had a secret language.

Not had *had*—had *has*. Present tense. The dementia was eating the recent rooms of her mind first—the names of nurses, the location of her glasses, the difference between this Thursday and last Thursday—but the old rooms stood. And in the oldest room of all, deeper than English, deeper than the family, her mother was singing lullabies in a dying Cornish tongue to children who weren't there.

Here was the thing Coral couldn't get past: her mother had never sung those songs to *them*. To Coral, to Graham, to Nell. Three children, hundreds of bedtimes, and not once—not once that any of them could remember—had Marguerite sung in anything but English. She'd sung "Twinkle Twinkle" and "Morningtown Ride" and a hymn or two with the enthusiasm of a hostage. But the other song, the real one, the one in the old words—she'd kept that behind glass, like good china, like photographs of people nobody would identify.

Which raised a question Coral had never thought to ask, because she hadn't known there was a question: *who* had her mother sung it to?

The lullaby had an audience in her mother's mind. Coral had heard it today, heard the way the song was addressed—to someone small, someone loved, someone specific. You don't sing to nobody. Even lost in the fog, her mother's voice had that particular tuned quality, the frequency of speaking to a child.

And the suspicion arrived then, quietly, the way suspicions do—not with a bang but with a small cold settling, like finding a stranger's coat already hanging in your wardrobe.

The language wasn't a heritage. Heritage gets handed down. Heritage is offered, taught, made a gift of at the appropriate moment—here, this is yours, this was your grandmother's. This language had been *withheld*. Curated. Kept the way you keep a hiding place: known only to yourself, useful precisely because no one else can follow you into it.

Her mother hadn't failed to pass it on. Her mother had *refused* to.

And if a woman builds a hiding place, Coral thought, staring through the rain-blurred windscreen at the grey sea beyond, it stands to reason she had something to hide—or something to hide *from*. Or someone. A family arranges itself around silences the way water arranges itself around stones. She'd learned that much from thirty years of translating other families' letters, their legal documents, their furious reconciling emails. Every silence has architecture. Someone built it.

Graham would say it didn't matter. Graham would say—Graham had said, many times, in his reasonable portfolio-manager voice—that the past was a sunk cost and the family's job was forward returns. Nell would say it was lovely that Mum still sang, and change the subject before the subject changed her.

But Elowen Marsh's advertisement had said *a student of patience*, and patience, Coral was beginning to understand, was simply the art of waiting for locked drawers to open.

She started the car. On the passenger seat lay the notebook where she was beginning to keep her own ledger—not of words spoken, like Elowen's, but of words withheld, hers and everyone's. She opened it at a fresh page and wrote, in her tidy translator's hand:

*Mother knows Kernew. Never taught us. Sings it to someone. Find out to whom.*

Below it, after a moment, she added:

*Ask Elowen.*

The rain eased as she drove down toward the coast road, and the sea, when it appeared between the hedges, was the colour of hammered tin. Somewhere along that coast, Coral felt with a certainty she couldn't yet justify, was a cottage where a song had first been sung—and someone besides her mother who remembered to whom.
