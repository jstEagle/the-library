## 8. The Sister Who Smooths

Nell Fairweather kept a drawer in her kitchen that everyone in the family knew about and no one ever mentioned: the drawer of small mercies. Inside it lived birthday cards bought a year early, batteries of every size, a spare house key for each sibling, a laminated list of Graham's phone numbers (work, mobile, the office line he pretended not to have), and a tin of shortbread that had been there so long it had achieved a kind of geological permanence. When anything went wrong—a missed flight, a forgotten anniversary, a diagnosis—Nell opened the drawer and something in it was always exactly right.

This was not an accident. Nell had spent fifty-eight years becoming the kind of person whose kitchen contained solutions to problems other people hadn't noticed yet. She was the middle child, which meant she had been born into a finished argument between two larger personalities and had made her life's work out of standing between them with a tray.

She thought of herself as a diplomat. Her children, when they were being unkind, called her an ostrich. Her husband, David, who was a patient man with a golfer's temperament—nothing bothered him for more than four holes—had once said, gently, over dishes: "Love, you don't keep the peace. You keep the *quiet*. There's a difference."

Nell had dried a plate very thoroughly and said, "Someone has to."

That was on a Tuesday. On Thursday she learned about Coral.

* * *

It was Graham who told her, because Graham told her everything, in the tone of a man reading out weather warnings. They were at the care home, in the little visitors' room with the plastic fern that nobody watered because it was plastic, which Nell found quietly funny every single time she looked at it. Marguerite was having her afternoon nap, or what passed for one—the staff said she slept like a woman packing a suitcase in her dreams, always half-ready to go somewhere.

"Coral's been visiting," Graham said. He arranged his hands on his knees the way he did before delivering bad portfolio news. "Twice this month. The staff log shows it."

"Good," said Nell, automatically, because someone had to say good.

"She hasn't been inside that building in nine years, Nell. She didn't come to the Christmas thing. She didn't come when we moved Mother's things." He ticked these off not cruelly but precisely, the way he'd itemize a fee structure. "And now she turns up twice in three weeks? Without telling either of us?"

"Maybe she's telling us now," Nell offered. "By way of you finding out."

Graham gave her a look. It was the look he'd given her since 1974, when she'd eaten his last Rolo and told him it had expired. "I'm going to call her."

"Don't," said Nell, and then, because Graham's calling was worse than anyone else's shouting, "Let me. I'll ring her tonight. She'll talk to me."

He considered this. Nell watched him weigh it—her usefulness against his suspicion—and felt the familiar small ache of being useful rather than trusted, and decided, as always, to be grateful for the useful part. "Ask her what she wants," he said. "Mother's care is settled. We have a plan. I don't need Coral renegotiating terms from the outside."

"We don't have a plan," Nell said mildly. "You have a plan. The rest of us have opinions we've agreed not to have."

"That's the same thing."

"It really isn't, Graham."

But he was already looking at his phone, and she understood that the conversation was over in the way their conversations with Graham ended—not concluded, but adjourned.

* * *

Nell did not ring Coral that night. This was not avoidance; it was strategy, though if you'd pressed her she'd have admitted the border between those two countries was poorly marked and mostly unpatrolled. Instead she rang on Saturday morning, after her walk, when the light came through her kitchen window in a way that made even the mercy drawer look cheerful, and she made herself a coffee and sat down with the phone in both hands like a bird she was afraid of startling.

Coral answered on the third ring. "Nell."

"Nelly-Fairweather-Extraordinary," Nell said, deploying the childhood nickname like a flare. "How are you? Are you eating? You sound thin."

"I sound thin?"

"Thin people sound thin. It's a known phenomenon. David says I sound rounder every year and I've decided to take it as a compliment."

There was a pause on the line, and Nell pictured her sister: the careful face, the translator's face, the one that held still while other faces moved. Coral had always been like a lake in the early morning—beautiful, exact, and impossible to swim in without going cold.

"Are you ringing about Mother?" Coral asked.

Well. There it was. Nell took a sip of coffee and chose her footing the way she chose her words on icy pavements: deliberately, with a slight bend in the knee.

"I'm ringing because I'm your sister and I miss you," she said, which was true, "and also because Graham has developed a twitch," which was truer, "and I thought I'd get ahead of it."

"He found out about my visits."

"The staff log is apparently a public document now. He read me the entries like a magistrate." Nell turned her mug a quarter-turn on the table. "So. Twice in three weeks. That's more than Christmas, Easter, and the funeral of Auntie Pat combined, and Auntie Pat left you her brooch."

"I've been busy," Coral said.

"Busy doing what?"

Another pause. Longer. Nell heard, faintly, the sound of a kettle being filled on Coral's end—a stalling noise, a domestic white flag. Coral had never once lied to Nell outright. She simply went quiet and let the silence do the lying for her, and Nell had learned, over decades, to accept the arrangement, because the alternative was asking a second question, and second questions were how families caught fire.

"You know," Coral said finally, "things. Work. And I wanted to see her."

"Wanted to," Nell repeated, and hated herself for the note of surprise in it, because what did it say about all of them that a daughter wanting to see her mother was remarkable? But there it was. Wanting had never been much use in their family. Wanting was what you did before you learned better. Their family ran on duty, and duty was Graham's department, and smoothing was hers, and Coral had taken the third job nobody advertised: leaving.

"Yes," Coral said. "Wanted to."

"Well," said Nell, warmly, plugging the gap before it widened. "She'll love that. She does love seeing you, even when she can't quite—" She stopped. Even when she can't quite place you. Even when she calls you by your mother's sister's name and asks whether you've brought the dog. "Even when she's having a foggy day."

"How is she?"

"Same. Some days she's sharp as anything, tells the carer her cardigan's the wrong shade of blue, very Marguerite. Other days she's somewhere else entirely." Nell hesitated. Here was the thing she'd actually rung to say, sitting under the small talk like a coin under a sofa cushion. "Actually—that's partly why I'm ringing, love. Not to interrogate you. Just... a heads-up."

"A heads-up."

"There was an afternoon last week. I was there, doing her nails, and she started singing. That little song—you know the one, the sea one, the one she used to hum when we were small and she thought we weren't listening." Nell turned the mug another quarter. "Except this time she sang the words. Actual words. In that language. Kernew. And then she looked straight at me and asked me something in it, proper question, expecting an answer, and I just—I smiled and changed the subject. Told her the nails needed another coat."

"What did she ask?"

"I don't know, do I? I don't speak it. Nobody speaks it. That's rather the point of it, isn't it—it was her secret language, her and whoever she borrowed it from, and we were never invited." Nell heard her own voice come out with an edge on it and smoothed it flat again, quick as ironing. "Anyway. My point is, she's surfacing things. Old things. And Dr. Okafor says agitation sets her back, and I know you'd never want that, none of us would, so I thought—if you're visiting, maybe just... keep it light. Keep it now. Crosswords, photographs, that sort of thing. Don't go digging up the old summers or asking her about—"

She stopped herself. Too late; the sentence had already shown its shape. Asking her about *what*, Nell had almost said, and her whole body flinched from the noun the way you flinch from a door you know sticks.

There was a silence on the line that had texture to it. Nell recognized it. It was the silence of Coral thinking, and Coral thinking had always frightened her slightly, the way deep water frightens people who only ever paddle.

"About what, Nell?" Coral asked, very evenly.

"About nothing," Nell said briskly. "About the past generally. It's not good for her. That's all I'm saying. Keep it light."

* * *

Afterward she washed up, though the washing-up was done, and reorganized the mercy drawer, moving the shortbread forward as though promoting it. David came in, read the room with the ease of long marriage, and put the kettle on without comment.

"Graham's going to make this a war," she said to the window. "Coral's going to make it a mystery. And I'm supposed to stand in the middle holding biscuits."

"You could try just asking Coral what she's actually doing there."

"And if the answer's something awful?"

David leaned against the counter. "Then you'll know something awful, and knowing's never once killed you, Nell. It's the not-knowing that keeps you up."

"That's not fair. I sleep beautifully."

"You sleep beautifully because you've never once let a question finish forming."

She laughed—she couldn't help it; he delivered these things so mildly, like passing the salt—and threw a tea towel at him, and for a moment the kitchen was warm and simple and contained no sisters at all.

But that night she lay awake doing what she did instead of sleeping, which was tidying. Mentally. She went through the family the way she went through the drawer, checking each item, confirming each thing was where she'd put it. Graham: controlled, controlling, fine. Mother: fading, but fading in an orderly fashion, well-fed, well-rostered. Coral: distant, brilliant, fine at a distance. Herself: fine. Everything filed. Everything smooth.

Only—one item wouldn't sit flat. It was the summer. It was always the summer, the one they never named, the one that had happened when Coral was eleven and Nell thirteen and Graham seventeen, down at the coast, in the cottage with the green door. Something had happened there—something with Marguerite at the center of it, something the family had folded itself around ever since, the way a tree grows around a fence post—and Nell had seen a piece of it. A small piece. A piece she had carried for forty-five years in the same pocket where other people carry keys.

She had told no one. She had smoothed it over so long ago and so thoroughly that most days she genuinely couldn't feel its edges anymore. Most days.

She lay in the dark and thought about Coral's voice on the phone—*about what, Nell?*—so level, so patient, so much like a person laying a sentence out on a table and waiting to see if it would hold weight.

And she thought, with a sensation like the first cold drop through a ceiling stain: *She's digging.*

The next morning, Nell opened the mercy drawer, took out the tin of shortbread, and—on impulse she didn't examine—moved it to the back, behind the batteries, where she wouldn't have to look at it.

Then she picked up the phone and dialed Coral's number, because whatever her sister was digging for, Nell intended to find it first.
