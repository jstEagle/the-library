## 15. The Last Speaker's Bargain

The cough arrived in October the way tide arrives—first as rumor, then as fact.

Coral noticed it during a lesson on the conditional mood, that lovely Kernew construction for things that might have been but weren't. Elowen had been explaining how the language folded regret into its verbs, tucking it away like a letter into an envelope, when she stopped mid-sentence and turned her face toward the window to cough. It was a small sound at first, apologetic almost, a polite clearing of the throat. By November it had opinions of its own. By December it had a schedule.

"You're not dying," Coral said one gray afternoon, setting down her tea with more force than she intended. "You're ninety. You're allowed a winter cough."

"I'm ninety-one," said Elowen, "and I've been dying since 1954, like everyone else. The only difference between me and you is that I've had longer to get used to the idea." She wiped her mouth with the back of her hand, briskly, as though the cough were a servant who'd overstepped. "Now. Where were we? The conditional. *Bûsen.* Say it."

"*Bûsen.*"

"Again. You say it like an apology. It isn't one. The conditional doesn't apologize. It simply states what might have been and moves on. Unlike some people I could mention."

Coral said it again, and Elowen nodded once—the smallest possible concession—and looked out at the sea, which was doing its December impression of hammered tin.

The cottage smelled different now. That was what frightened Coral most, though she couldn't have said so aloud. For months the place had smelled of woodsmoke and salt and the particular dusty sweetness of old paper, Elowen's whole house being in some sense a library that had learned to make tea. Now there was something underneath it, faint and medicinal, the smell of a body working harder than it wished to. Vicks on the mantel. A doctor's leaflet folded on the kitchen table beside the grammar exercises, its corners already curling, unread.

Elowen had stopped going down to the water in October. In November she'd stopped going up the stairs before dark, sitting instead in the chair by the fire until sleep took her there, so that Coral began arriving to find her already awake, already dressed, already seated, as though she'd been waiting all night for the lesson to begin. Perhaps she had.

"Your sister telephoned me," Elowen said abruptly, in the second week of December.

Coral's teacup paused halfway to her mouth. "Nell?"

"The smooth one. She wanted to know if I understood what a responsibility I was taking on, teaching an old woman's secrets to a stranger." Elowen's eyes were bright and entirely untroubled. "I told her I wasn't taking anything on. I was giving something away, and it was nobody's business but mine and God's, and God had stopped returning my calls years ago."

"What did Nell say to that?"

"She laughed. Then she got quiet. Then she said, 'She'll tell you nothing worth telling,' and hung up." Elowen smiled at the fire. "A very revealing sentence, that. Worth more than half your lessons. *Dhe'n tan,* then. To the fire. We were on the conditional."

But Coral couldn't find the conditional after that. Her mind kept circling back to Nell's voice on the other end of Elowen's telephone, doing what Nell always did—not forbidding, never forbidding, just smoothing, laying her palm flat over the surface of things until they appeared calm. *She'll tell you nothing worth telling.* As if worth were the danger. As if the family's survival depended on everything remaining worthless.

* * *

It was the notebook that changed everything—or rather, it was the day Elowen asked Coral to fetch the notebook herself.

Until then it had lived in the drawer of the writing desk, produced ceremonially at the start of each lesson and returned to darkness at the end. But that Tuesday Elowen's hands were bad—the cold had gotten into them, she said, though Coral suspected it was more than cold—and she nodded toward the desk and said, "Second drawer. The green one. Mind the moths; they have expensive taste."

Coral opened the drawer and found the notebook where it always was, but beside it now lay two others, fat and older, their spines cracked, their covers furred at the edges. And beneath those, a shoebox. And beneath the shoebox, letters tied with string gone soft as thread.

She stood there a moment with the green notebook in her hand, feeling the way she imagined an archaeologist feels when the trowel goes through topsoil and finds not rubble but a floor—a deliberate floor, laid by someone who meant to be lived upon.

"Bring them all," Elowen said behind her. "Go on. You may as well see the estate while the estate is still above ground."

There were seven notebooks in total. Coral carried them to the fire like an armful of sleeping birds and set them on the table, and Elowen, with the air of a woman about to conduct a particularly tedious audit, opened the oldest one.

Inside, in a hand that shook only slightly, were columns. Dates. Words. Tally marks.

"What is this?" Coral asked, though she already knew. She had known since the night she'd first seen the green book, and had chosen—as her family had taught her—to know without knowing.

"It's my ration book," said Elowen. "Every word of Kernew I've spoken aloud in the last eleven years. Date, word, to whom, occasion." She turned a page. "You'll notice January of the year before last is nearly empty. Bad month. Laryngitis. I was furious about it—wasted words are wasted forever, you understand. There's no bank where you can deposit them and draw them out later. Speak a word and it's spent. Hold it and it dies with you. Either way it's gone. The only question is whether it went somewhere."

Coral looked at the columns. Thousands of entries. *Good morning* to the postman, who thought it was Welsh. *Thank you* to a gull, ironically. Whole conversations logged with herself, alone in this cottage, spending the language penny by penny so it would last exactly as long as she did—no longer, no sooner. Eleven years of a woman speaking a language almost exclusively into the air, keeping meticulous accounts, hoarding breath the way Coral's family hoarded silence.

"Oh," Coral said. It came out very small.

"Yes," said Elowen. "*Oh.* Well observed. Now you see why I chose you. Not because you loved the language—I could have found someone who loved it. Love is cheap; every fool loves something. I chose you because you keep ledgers too. I saw it the first hour you sat in that chair. You count what you don't say the way I count what I do. We're the same animal, you and I, just facing opposite directions." She closed the oldest notebook and rested her hand flat upon it, and Coral saw that the hand trembled now even at rest. "Which brings us to business."

"Business?"

"The bargain. Sit down, girl, your hovering is blocking the fire."

Coral sat.

* * *

What Elowen proposed was simple, the way avalanches are simple.

Everything. That was the offer. Not the lessons—Coral had been having lessons, pleasant twice-weekly installments, vocabulary in exchange for confession, the gentle drip of a language through a keyhole. What Elowen was offering now was the whole of it: the notebooks, the grammar complete, the stories, the songs Marguerite hummed without knowing their names, the jokes that didn't survive translation, the curses, the lullabies, the names for weather that English hadn't bothered to invent because English had never needed to live this close to the water. And underneath all of it, at the bottom of the box, wrapped in oilcloth: the summer. The cottage. The accident. The account of what happened, in the language built for saying it, from the only witness still living who had been there and remembered.

"All of it," Elowen said. "Every word I have left. I estimate—" she tapped the current notebook— "fourteen thousand words of speech remaining, give or take a hard winter. I intend to spend every one of them on you. I will die fluent, girl, or as near to it as makes no difference, and you will be standing there holding the lot."

Coral's heart was doing something complicated and painful. "And in exchange?"

"In exchange you promise to speak it."

"That's it? I promise to speak it?"

"That's everything," said Elowen. "That's the entire price. You will speak Kernew aloud. To other people. On purpose. You will not write it down and put it in a drawer. You will not learn it perfectly and then hold it, polished and private, until you die and it dies with you, which is what you would do—I can see you doing it already, you're doing it right now, planning the drawer." She leaned forward, and the firelight caught the fierce wet brightness of her eyes. "I am the last speaker of a language, Coral Fairweather. Do you understand what that means? It means that when I stop talking, a hundred thousand mornings of somebody's grandmothers calling somebody's children home for supper—that's finished. Every joke anyone ever laughed at in it. Every argument, every apology, every 'come here' and 'go away' and 'be careful on the rocks.' Eleven centuries of people being human at each other, and it all lives in this skull"—she knocked her own head with two fingers, sharply, as though knocking on a door she hoped someone would answer—"and this skull has a leaky roof and a short lease. So. I can spend it on you, or I can bury it with me. Those are the options. There is no third option. There is no careful option. There is no option where you learn it quietly and decide later. Later is where languages go to die."

The fire popped. Somewhere out on the water a buoy clanked its idiot bell, patient as ever.

"And the summer," Coral said. Her voice sounded strange to her, far away. "The accident. You'd give me that too."

"I'd give you that *first*," said Elowen. "It's the price of admission, in a sense. You can't have the language without it. It's load-bearing."

"Why?" Coral heard herself ask. "Why does my family's worst summer matter to a language?"

"Because," said Elowen, "your mother didn't hide from you children in English. Think about it. Anyone can lie in English. Your mother kept her secrets in Kernew because Kernew is the only language she knew that couldn't say *it wasn't anybody's fault.* No passive voice, remember? Someone must always act. Someone must always be named." She settled back into her chair, suddenly looking every one of her ninety-one years, and pulled a blanket over her knees with the unhurried dignity of a queen drawing up a treaty. "Your family arranged itself around a sentence, child. A sentence in my language. If you want the language, you'll have to go through the sentence. That's not cruelty. That's just architecture."

Coral sat with it. Outside, the light was failing the way December light fails—all at once, like a lamp switched off rather than a sun going down. She thought of her ledger, the great silent inventory she'd kept for thirty years: *the thing Graham said at the funeral; the thing Nell didn't ask; the thing Mother sang instead of explained; the thing I saw from the stairs and filed under later.* She had built her whole life the way Elowen had built these notebooks—one careful entry at a time, mistaking preservation for living, mistaking a full ledger for a full life.

And now an old woman was offering her everything, and the price was the one thing Coral had spent thirty years not paying: to open her mouth and let the contents be witnessed.

"If I say yes," Coral said slowly, "you'll teach me the summer. And then I'll have to say it. To Graham. To Nell. To Mother, if she can still hear it."

"To Graham. To Nell. To your mother, who I suspect has been waiting thirty years for someone to hear it in the original." Elowen's voice gentled, which was somehow worse than the fierceness. "I know what I'm asking. I'm asking you to do the thing you translate for strangers every day of your life and have never once done for yourself. Carry meaning across. From one country to another. From then to now. From silence to speech." She reached across and took Coral's hand—her grip still strong, astonishingly strong, the grip of a woman who had hauled nets or boats or simply her own stubborn self through nine decades. "The cost of the gift is exactly what you fear most. That's not a flaw in the bargain, dear. That's how you know it's a real one."

They sat a long time. The fire worked. The buoy clanked. Coral became aware that she was crying, quietly, in the undramatic way she did everything, tears that made no sound and demanded no witness—which was, she supposed, precisely the problem.

"When?" she said at last.

"Tomorrow," said Elowen. "We begin tomorrow. Grammar first—you can't carry a heavy sentence in weak arms. Then the stories. Then—" she patted the oilcloth bundle at the bottom of the shoebox, which Coral now realized she had seen all along, had perhaps been meant to see all along— "then the summer. Fourteen thousand words, girl. We shouldn't waste the daylight."

Coral stood. At the door she paused, coat half-on, and turned back.

"Elowen. Why me? Truly. Not the ledger business. Why me?"

The old woman looked at her for a long moment, and something moved behind her eyes like a fish under ice.

"Because in 1961," she said, "a woman stood in my kitchen holding a baby, and she was crying, and she spoke to me in Kernew—the last time anyone ever spoke to me in Kernew out of need rather than nostalgia. And do you know what she said?" Elowen smiled, terribly. "Neither do you. But you were the baby. Tomorrow we'll find out together."

Coral walked home along the cliff path in the dark, the wind shouldering her like an old friend with bad news, and below her the sea said the one thing it knows how to say, over and over, in a language with no passive voice at all.
