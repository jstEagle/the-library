## 1. Borrowed Words

The word for *threshold* was gone.

Lena Okafor discovered this at 11:47 on a Tuesday night in March, somewhere between the penultimate chapter of *La maison des vents* and the last glass of a bottle of Rioja she had opened, ostensibly, to celebrate. The novel was finished — her translation of it, that is; the French original had been finished by a dead man from Marseille who had never once in his life been asked to explain himself in two languages. Her version was clean. It was, if she was honest with herself, the best work she had done in three years: the wind in the house rendered as breath rather than weather, the mother's deathbed scene carrying its weight without buckling. She had read the final paragraph aloud to the empty flat, testing the cadence the way a jeweler taps a stone against her teeth, and it had rung true.

And then, closing the manuscript, stretching until her spine cracked like kindling, she had thought — idly, the way one thinks of anything at midnight — *I should call Mama and tell her I'm done.*

Mama had been dead for fourteen months.

The thought arrived without pain, which frightened her more than grief would have. Grief, at least, was a language she still spoke. What came instead was something colder and more administrative: the recognition that the reflex itself was dying, that the part of her wired to report the day's accomplishments to Adaeze Okafor was finally being decommissioned, cell by cell, like a phone line quietly cancelled after years of disuse.

She stood in her kitchen — small, white, efficient, a kitchen designed by someone who assumed cooking was an occasional emergency — and waited for the wave. It didn't come. Instead, another absence surfaced behind the first, the way fish rise when the water goes still.

*Threshold.*

The Ọ̀ṣun-Ilé word for threshold. She could not find it.

This was not unusual, exactly. This was the condition of her life now, the slow tide going out of her, grain by grain. There were words she had already surrendered without ceremony — the word for the dry season's particular dust, the word for the sound a goat makes when it is annoyed, the word her mother used for the specific quality of light at six in the evening that made everything look forgiven. Lena had noticed their departures the way you notice a neighbor has moved away: not on the day itself, but weeks later, when you reach for them and find only the shape they left.

But *threshold*. That word had lived in her body. It was the word of her childhood's most repeated instruction — *wipe your feet, cross properly, do not let your heel drag on the* — and it was the word of the doorway she had stood in, twelve years ago, refusing to enter her mother's hospital room in anything but English. If any word deserved to survive the erosion, she would have thought it would be that one.

She set down her glass and tried to summon it the way she summoned French and Spanish and Portuguese, the languages she carried professionally, fluently, effortlessly — the way a courier carries packages belonging to other people. In French: *seuil*. In Spanish: *umbral*, which had always struck her as beautiful, a word with a shadow built into it. In Portuguese: *limiar*. In Yoruba, the great neighboring tongue she'd studied at SOAS because Ọ̀ṣun-Ilé had no department, no dictionary, no orthography beyond what missionaries and linguists had cobbled together: *ọ̀nà ìwájú*, roughly, the way forward.

But Ọ̀ṣun-Ilé — her mother's tongue, the tongue of four villages and perhaps forty thousand speakers, a language small enough to hold in two hands and slipping through them anyway — nothing. A blank. Not even the courtesy of a false cognate. Just the space where the word had been, smooth as a river stone worn featureless.

"Come on," she said aloud, to herself, to the flat. "You know this."

She didn't.

She poured the rest of the wine down the sink, which felt like a punishment she hadn't earned, and went to bed without turning off the lamp in the kitchen, so that all night the flat kept a small lit doorway open over nothing.

* * *

In the morning the absence was still there, patient as furniture. Lena did what she always did with gaps: she worked around them. She answered emails. She sent the finished manuscript to her editor with a note that was warm but brief, professional competence deployed like a well-tailored coat. *Attached — apologies for the delay on ch. 14, I think the second pass has solved the tonal problem in the funeral scene.* She attached an invoice. She drank coffee standing up, looking out the window at the plane tree on Haverstock Road that was just beginning to consider spring.

It was while she was drafting a follow-up email about rates for a possible Catalan project — Catalan, a language she had learned entirely from books and a summer in Girona, learned at thirty-one with almost embarrassing ease, as if her mind were grateful for any language that wasn't the one it was losing — that her phone rang.

The screen said: **AUNT NGOZI**.

Lena looked at it for four full rings. Calls from Ngozi were rare enough to be events, and events from that side of the family required preparation the way dives require checking the depth of water. But something — the hour, the wine-less clarity of morning, or perhaps the missing word sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone — made her answer.

"Auntie."

"Lena." Ngozi's voice came through compressed and slightly delayed, the satellite lag inserting a half-second pause before every reply, so that conversations with her aunt always felt like speaking across a river. "You are well?"

"I'm well. Working. You?"

"We are managing." This was Ngozi's universal answer, covering health, wealth, weather, and the state of the nation. "Your father's brother sends greetings."

"Uncle Emeka. Tell him I said thank you."

"He will not hear it, but I will say it." A pause, filled with the faint roar of Lagos — generators, traffic, a life being lived loudly at the other end of the world. "Lena. I am calling because of a thing."

Lena sat down at her kitchen table. "A thing."

"A thing concerning your mother."

The generator-roar swelled. Somewhere behind Ngozi a child was singing, off-key and unbothered, a song Lena almost recognized and then lost, the way you lose a fish's shape in dark water.

"What thing?" Lena asked.

Ngozi's voice changed register — dropped lower, slower, moving into the careful mode she used for matters of weight. And here was the cruelty of it, the thing Lena could never explain to colleagues who admired her six working languages: she understood her aunt perfectly when Ngozi spoke English, and barely at all when Ngozi forgot herself and slid home. And Ngozi always, eventually, slid home. It happened the way rivers happen. Twenty seconds into any serious conversation, the English would thin and the Ọ̀ṣun-Ilé would come up underneath it like groundwater, and Lena would be left clutching nouns.

"—because the man is old now," Ngozi was saying, "and his people have written to me twice, and I have told them there is nothing to discuss, but they are not people who accept—"

"Sorry — Auntie — slow down. Who wrote? Whose people?"

A pause long enough to be its own sentence. When Ngozi resumed, it was in English again, flattened, deliberate, each word placed like a stone in a crossing.

"It is not a matter for the telephone."

"Ngozi—" Lena almost never used her aunt's first name; it landed between them like a dropped plate. "Is someone sick? Is someone dead?"

"No one is dead." Another pause. "Not yet."

The child in the background sang on. Lena gripped the edge of the table. Fourteen months since the funeral in Enugu, since the red earth and the canopies and the women ululating in a language she understood at the speed of weather, and she had thought the estate of her mother's secrets was settled — the flat sold, the trunk shipped to London, the accounts closed. Apparently the estate had a wing she had never been shown.

"Auntie, please. You can't say 'a thing concerning my mother' and then stop."

"I can," Ngozi said, not unkindly. "That is exactly what I can do. Listen to me, Lena. Your mother was my sister and I loved her, and because I loved her I kept certain silences, and because I keep them still, I am telling you: leave it. Some questions are debts. When you pay a debt you must have the money. Do you understand me?"

"I understand the words," Lena said, which was true, and also the most honest thing she could offer, because understanding — real understanding, the kind that included everything Ngozi wasn't saying, the freight beneath the grammar — was precisely what she no longer had. "I don't understand what they're for."

"That," said Ngozi, "is why I am telling you to leave it."

The line crackled. The child's song ended mid-phrase. For a moment Lena heard only the ocean-sound of the connection, two continents breathing at each other.

"There is a man," Ngozi said suddenly, quickly, as if the words were escaping custody. "From before. From Enugu, from before your father. He is asking after—"

And then the line dissolved into static, into the particular electronic purgatory of international calls, and by the time it reconstituted itself Ngozi was saying, in firm, closed English: "—so give my regards to your work, and eat properly, and call me at the end of the month."

"Auntie. The man. What man?"

"The end of the month, Lena."

The call ended. Lena held the phone against her ear for several seconds after the silence set in, listening to the ghost-hum of the terminated line, as if the missing half of the conversation might leak back through.

*A man. From before. From Enugu, from before your father.*

Her mother had had a life before her father. Lena knew this the way children know their parents were once young — abstractly, without evidence. There had been photographs in the shipping trunk, unopened since it arrived in London fourteen months ago and pushed, still taped shut, into the corner of her bedroom like a piece of furniture awaiting a decision. She had not been able to open it. She told friends it was because the flat was small. She told herself it was because she was busy. Both statements were true and neither was the reason.

She got up, walked into the bedroom, and stood in front of the trunk.

It was a good trunk, iron-banded, her initials once carved into the lid and worn nearly smooth: L.O., legible now mostly by faith. Her mother had crossed an ocean with this object. Inside it, presumably, lay the ordinary archaeology of a life — cloth, letters, photographs, the sediment of a woman Lena had known for thirty-four years and understood, she was beginning to suspect, not at all.

She did not open it. She put her palm flat on the lid instead, the way you'd check a sleeping animal, and felt the cool wood, and stood there long enough that her tea went cold in the other room.

*His people have written to me twice.*

Whose people?

*He is asking after—*

After whom? After Ngozi? After the family? After—

The thought arrived whole, unbidden, absurd, and refused to leave: *after me.*

"No," Lena said aloud, to the trunk, to the flat, to the absent aunt across five time zones. "No one is asking after me. No one has asked after me in years."

Which was true, and which was also, she realized as she said it, the loneliest sentence she had ever spoken in her own kitchen.

* * *

She worked for the rest of the day, because work was the room she retreated to when every other room was on fire. She proofread galleys. She declined a conference panel. She wrote 400 words of a review of a Norwegian novel about snow and silence, and caught herself, twice, reaching for images from a childhood that belonged to a different climate — the harmattan haze, the way sound traveled differently in hot air — and cutting them, because a reviewer's job was fidelity to the text, and the text knew nothing of harmattan.

At seven she made jollof rice from a recipe card in her mother's handwriting, which she followed the way one follows a letter from the dead: literally, reverently, tasting at each stage for a memory that the recipe couldn't quite deliver. Her mother had cooked by hand and by ear, adjusting for the mood of the peppers, the hunger of the house. The card said *add seasoning to taste*, and Lena's taste, she was discovering, had also been eroding — she kept adding salt trying to reach a flavor she could describe but no longer produce. The rice was fine. It was fine the way her translations were fine. Everything she made was fine. Nothing she made was hers.

After dinner she opened her laptop intending to answer one more email, and instead found herself typing into the search bar: *Ọ̀ṣun-Ilé language*.

The results took less than a second and filled less than a page. A Wikipedia stub, four paragraphs, last edited three years ago. A linguistics paper from 1987, scanned badly, on tonal patterns in "the Ọ̀ṣun-Ilé cluster (Lower Niger)". An Ethnologue entry listing speaker estimates: 38,000, status *threatened*, field notes last verified 2009. A YouTube video, eleven minutes, shot on a phone at some cultural festival in Onitsha — women in matching wrappers dancing to a drum, singing something Lena could follow only in fragments, catching every fourth or fifth word like stones from a fast stream. Forty thousand speakers. By the arithmetic of such things — the arithmetic she knew too well, having translated three novels about dying languages without once letting the subject touch her own life — there might be twenty thousand now. Fewer. The language was going out the way the tide went out of her, and somewhere in Lagos a man whose name she didn't know was writing letters to her aunt, and somewhere in a bedroom corner a trunk held the photographs of him, and the word for *threshold* was simply gone, and no search engine had ever indexed it.

She closed the laptop.

Then she opened it again, and did the thing she had been circling for fourteen months, the thing she had told herself she would do when the flat was more organized, when work was quieter, when she was stronger. She walked into the bedroom, knelt, and cut the tape along the trunk's seam with a kitchen knife, one long tearing rip that sounded louder than it was.

Inside: the smell. That was first, before anything visible — camphor and dried pepper and old cloth, the smell of her mother's wardrobe, of Enugu, of a house that no longer existed, rising out of the box like a genie with no interest in granting wishes. Lena sat back on her heels and breathed it until her eyes stung.

Under the smell: fabric, folded in the tight ceremonial squares her mother favored. A bundle of letters tied with ribbon gone brittle. A photograph album with a cracked spine. And at the bottom, wrapped in a cloth bag that had once held rice —

Lena lifted it out. Inside the bag: a cassette tape, standard ninety-minute, its plastic yellowed with age. Across the label, in her mother's unmistakable hand — the hand that had written the recipe card, the hand that had signed Lena's school forms, the hand Lena had last held through a hospital glove — in blue biro, in capital letters:

**DO NOT PLAY.**

Lena sat on the floor of her flat in London, holding the forbidden object in both hands, and outside a siren rose and fell on Haverstock Road, and the plane tree moved in a wind she couldn't feel, and somewhere across the ocean a man was asking after someone, and her aunt had said *leave it*, and the word for *threshold* was gone but she was kneeling at one now, at the border of whatever came next, unable to recall the word for the place where she stood.

She turned the cassette over. There was nothing on the back. Of course there was nothing on the back. Her mother had never needed to say things twice.

"Not yet," Lena told her — the first words she had addressed to her mother in months, and in English, still, always, the borrowed language, the safe one. "But soon."

She put the cassette on her desk where she would have to look at it every morning, propped against the lamp like a small dark headstone, and she did not sleep, and in the morning she began, very quietly, to look up flights to Lagos.
