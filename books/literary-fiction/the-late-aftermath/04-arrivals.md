## 4. Arrivals

The plane came down through harmattan haze, and Lagos rose to meet it like something half-remembered from a fever — a sprawl of rust and gold, rooftops the color of old blood, the lagoon glinting dully beneath a sky the color of skimmed milk. Lena pressed her forehead to the window and felt the old, shameful flutter in her chest, the one that had no name in English and that she had once known the Ọ̀ṣun-Ilé word for and had let it go the way you let go of a balloon you are too tired to hold.

*Ìrọ̀rùn*, her mother would have said. The feeling of returning. The feeling of coming down.

Or maybe not. Maybe the word meant something else entirely — ease, or the softening of light at dusk. That was the treachery of it. She could no longer check herself against anything. The dictionary of her childhood had burned down room by room, and she had stood by with a bucket that never filled.

The plane touched. The cabin filled with applause — Lagos always applauded its own arrivals, as if every landing were a small resurrection — and Lena did not join them. She sat with her hands folded, listening to the languages bloom around her. Yoruba, Igbo, pidgin, French, a scatter of others she could name but not follow. And somewhere in it, threaded through the announcements, the security chatter, a woman two rows up scolding her son — fragments of Ọ̀ṣun-Ilé. Not much. A word here, a cadence there, the particular rising lilt at the end of a question that made every question sound like a song asking to be sung back.

Her body knew it before her mind did. Her spine straightened. Her breath went shallow. Twelve years of London had taught her to hear the language as background noise, and now it was everywhere at once, alive, indifferent, going about its business without any regard whatsoever for the fact that it was dying inside her.

*Ìrọ̀rùn.* She held the word up to the light and found it cracked.

---

Murtala Muhammed Airport was a river with a single current, and the current was noise. Men in faded uniforms shouted destinations. Porters materialized at her elbows with hands out. A fan somewhere turned without conviction. The air hit her the moment she stepped through the doors — thick, sweet, diesel and roasting corn and something floral she couldn't name, heat pressing on her skin like a hand that remembered her.

A driver held a placard: *MISS OKAFOR*. She had not asked her aunt to send anyone. That, too, was a message. Ngozi had not come herself, and had not pretended otherwise.

The drive into the city took two hours in traffic that was less traffic than a long argument between vehicles, and through the window Lagos unspooled its endless argument back — billboards in three languages, churches with names like prophecies, a boy of maybe eight balancing a tray of sachet water on his head and weaving between cars with the serene authority of someone who had never once been lost. Hawkers tapped the glass. *Water. Water. Cold water, madam.* Their pidgin was a music she understood perfectly, and that was its own small humiliation: she was fluent in the language of the road and illiterate in the language of the house.

At a red light, two women crossed in front of the car, talking. Lena caught four words. Five. Her heart lunged at them like a dog at the end of a chain — *ọmọ mi, jọ̀wọ́, ẹ jọ̀* — my child, please, let us — and then the light changed and the sentence went with them, unfinished, and she sat back and pressed her palms flat to her thighs.

This was the thing no one had warned her about, or perhaps they had warned her and she had refused to hear: that grief is not only for the dead. That a language can die in you while its body walks around outside, healthy, loud, laughing, selling water at intersections, and that every time you hear it you attend a funeral with no body and no date.

The driver glanced at her in the mirror. "First time, madam?"

"No," she said. Then, because the truth was larger than the lie: "A long time ago."

He nodded as if this explained everything, which it did. "Na so. Lagos no dey forget person. Na person dey forget Lagos."

Lagos doesn't forget you. You forget Lagos.

She looked out the window so he wouldn't see her face.

---

Ngozi's house stood in Surulere behind a wall the color of dried clay, its gate painted a green that had once been festive and was now mostly a memory of festive. A mango tree leaned over the compound wall, dropping its fruit onto the neighbor's side with what Lena chose to read as quiet malice. The house itself was exactly as she remembered it from the one visit of her childhood — the one summer her mother had brought her back, when Lena was nine, the summer she had learned more Ọ̀ṣun-Ilé in six weeks than in the six years before or the eighteen after. She remembered the veranda. She remembered the smell of eucalyptus smoke. She remembered, with a suddenness that stopped her on the threshold — *threshold*, there, the English word arriving obediently where the other one should have stood — a woman's laugh from inside that was not Ngozi's and was not her mother's and that belonged to no one she could name, and it frightened her how much a laugh could be home.

Ngozi came out onto the veranda drying her hands on her wrapper. She was smaller than Lena remembered, or Lena had grown; her face was a map of her mother's face with different roads drawn on it, harder roads, roads that went somewhere. Her eyes did the thing that Lena had spent her whole life dreading in older relatives' eyes: the quick inventory, the measuring of the child against the memory of the child, the visible arithmetic of *what has become of her*.

"Lena." Just the name, in Ngozi's accent, which bent it into something between *Lay-nah* and a question.

"Aunty Ngozi." She said it in Ọ̀ṣun-Ilé — *Ànty Ngòzí* — the greeting her mother had drilled into her, the rising respect-note on the second syllable. It came out rusty but whole, and she watched it land on her aunt like a stone dropped into a well whose depth had changed.

Ngozi did not answer in kind. "You found the house."

"The driver found the house."

"Good. Come inside before the mosquitoes find you. They are punctual."

The sitting room was cool and dim, the curtains drawn against the heat, the furniture covered in lace doilies that defied both gravity and taste. A calendar from a funeral home — *IN LOVING MEMORY* — hung on the wall, three years old, its dead person a stranger. On the sideboard, in the place of honor, stood a framed photograph of Lena's mother, Adaeze, taken perhaps a decade ago, her smile carrying that particular patience Lena had spent her adult life trying to translate and had never found the English for.

Lena stood in front of it longer than politeness allowed.

"She hated that photo," Ngozi said behind her. "She said her chin looked like a hammer. Sit down, Lena. You look tired. Did you eat on the plane? Don't lie to me, I can see the answer in your face. There is rice. It is not fresh but it is food."

"I'm not hungry."

"You are always not hungry. That is an English disease." But she said it without warmth, the way you state a fact of geography, and she did not go to the kitchen, and the rice remained theoretical.

They sat across from each other with the photograph between them like a third person at a table. A ceiling fan turned its slow, useless circles. Somewhere in the compound a generator coughed to life, died, coughed again, and settled into its grumble, and under that grumble the neighborhood went on: children, a radio, a man calling a name that was not answered.

"You came," Ngozi said at last.

"You're surprised."

"I'm not surprised. I'm noting it." Ngozi folded her hands in her lap. Her nails were filed short, practical, the nails of a woman who had done her own work all her life. "Your mother said you would come. Before the end. She said, *Lena will come when I am gone, because that is when Lena comes to things.*" She let that sit in the air between them, unsoftened. "She was not angry when she said it. That is what I want you to know. She said it the way you say the weather."

Lena's throat tightened. "Aunty, I came because—"

"I know why you came." Ngozi raised one hand, and the gesture was so exactly her mother's that Lena's sentence died in her mouth. "The radio. Your friend in London called my friend in Yaba, who called me. This is how news travels in our country, Lena — faster than light, and just as reliable. You heard a song."

"You know about the song."

"I know about the song the way I know about rain. I was there when it fell."

The fan turned. The generator grumbled. Lena made herself breathe. "Then you can tell me what it was. What it meant. Why she never—"

"No."

The word came out flat and final, a door closing in a house with a thousand doors. Ngozi leaned forward, and for the first time since the veranda her eyes were not doing arithmetic; they were doing something older and more tired.

"Lena. Listen to me. I will say it once, and then we will eat, or not eat, and you will sleep, because you have the face of a woman who has not slept since London. There are questions that are like debts. You understand debts? A debt is not only money. Some debts, when you go to collect them, you find that the interest has been growing in the dark all these years, and what you thought was yours to collect will bury you. Some questions are debts better left unpaid. Your mother paid hers. She paid with her whole life, quietly, without complaining to God even once, and I watched her do it. I will not stand here and let a girl who would not speak to her at the end come and reopen the account."

The words went in like needles, each one finding a place Lena had not bothered to guard, because she had not known it needed guarding.

"You think I don't know what I did," Lena said. Her voice came out low, unsteady. "You think I flew six hours to be told—"

"I think you don't know what you did." Ngozi's voice did not rise. That was the terrible thing about her; nothing about her rose, everything simply stood. "You know the shape of it. You don't know the weight. There is a difference, and the difference is a whole life, and it is not mine to give you." She stood, smoothed her wrapper, and the audience was over. "The room at the back is made up. Your mother's room, when she stayed. I changed nothing in it. You will want to be alone with it, and you will be alone with it, and in the morning we will talk about ordinary things — the traffic, your work, whether you are eating — and you will not ask me again about the song."

"Aunty—"

"Good night, Lena."

She went out. Her footsteps crossed the compound, unhurried, and a door closed somewhere with the softness of a woman who had been closing doors her whole life and had gotten very good at the sound.

---

The room at the back was small and clean and smelled of camphor and closed time. A bed with a crocheted spread. A wardrobe with a mirror gone cloudy at the corners. A window looking out onto the mango tree, and beyond it, over the wall, a slice of the neighbor's roof and a strip of sky going the deep bruised purple of Lagos dusk.

Lena set her bag down and did not unpack. She sat on the bed with her elbows on her knees and listened to the house settle around her.

The language was everywhere. That was the thing she had not been prepared for — she had thought she was coming to find a song, a man, a truth, some discrete object she could take back to London and set on her desk and translate at her own pace. But the language was not an object here. It was the air. Through the window, the neighbor's family was arguing about something — a goat, a debt, a daughter-in-law, it was impossible to tell — and the argument had the music of her mother's kitchen, the same rising and falling, the same way anger and affection shared one melody and distinguished themselves only by volume. A child was being called in to dinner in a voice that rose at the end like a question that already knew the answer. A radio next door played highlife, and under the horns a woman's voice sang words Lena's ear caught and her mind could not hold, like water through a net with a hole exactly the size of her childhood.

She had spent twelve years in London believing she had lost the language the way you lose a thing — misplace it, set it down somewhere, a scarf on a train. Sitting in her mother's old room in Surulere, she understood for the first time that she had not lost it. She had buried it. And buried things do not die; they wait, and they keep, and they change the ground above them.

Her phone buzzed. A message from the radio producer in London, the one who had traced the broadcast for her, the thread she had followed across an ocean:

*Spoke to my contact in Lagos again. The composer's name is Tunde Adewale. He kept a music shop in Yaba for years — may still. Careful with this one, Lena. People say he's not what he was. Memory like a tide, they told me. Comes in, goes out.*

Lena read it three times. Outside, the purple had gone to black, and the generator had finally surrendered, and in the sudden quiet the neighborhood's night sounds came up through the floor like a pulse — voices, a dog, the far-off hymn of a night church, and everywhere, woven through all of it, the language, alive, indifferent, going on without her.

*Yaba,* she thought. *A music shop. Memory like a tide.*

She looked at the cloudy mirror across the room, and in it her mother's room held her mother's daughter like an accusation it had been keeping safe.

In the morning, Ngozi would offer her rice and the weather. In the morning, Lena intended to be gone before the rice.

She did not sleep. She lay in the dark translating the neighbor's argument word by word, catching one phrase in ten, and the ones she caught she held onto like rungs, climbing toward a morning she was already dreading — because she had heard the warning in her aunt's voice, and had understood it completely, and had understood, just as completely, that some debts you do not get to choose.

You inherit them. Or they inherit you.
