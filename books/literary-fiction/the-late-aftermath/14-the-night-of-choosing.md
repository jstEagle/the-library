## 14. The Night of Choosing

The night before Adaeze's wedding, Tunde Adewale sat on an overturned crate behind her mother's kitchen and tuned a guitar that did not belong to him.

It was borrowed — everything in his life that year was borrowed — from Sunday Okon, who ran the instrument repair stall near the motor park, and who had said, pressing it into Tunde's hands with the gravity of a man handing over a newborn, *Play it like it's yours. That's the only way anything gets played well.* Tunde had taken the instruction literally. He had slept with the guitar beside him for three weeks, its body rising and falling against his ribs like a second heartbeat, and in that time he had written eleven songs, ten of them forgettable, and one that would not leave him alone.

The lullaby. He had written it for a woman who was not yet a mother and never would be his, and he did not know yet that this was what it was. He thought of it as a working song, unfinished, a thing he would complete when the world arranged itself more kindly.

The world did not arrange itself kindly. The world arranged itself into a wedding.

Behind the kitchen wall, he could hear the women of the household moving through the last preparations — the scrape of a pot, the low argument of voices deciding how much pepper was too much pepper for a stew that would feed two hundred people tomorrow, the particular laughter of Adaeze's youngest cousin, a girl who laughed the way water spills, all at once and everywhere. And beneath all of it, occasionally, when the other sounds fell away: Adaeze herself, being fitted, being fussed over, being congratulated, her voice rising and falling in the polite registers of a woman learning to live inside her own good fortune.

*Good fortune.* Tunde pressed his thumb against a fret and felt the string bite. He had been saying that phrase to himself for two months, since the day Ngozi — Ngozi, who was not yet the granite woman she would become, who was then only a sharp-eyed girl of nineteen with her sister's interests stitched into her chest — had come to the shop where he repaired radios and told him, without preamble, *My sister is marrying Chief Okafor in December. You should not come near the house again. I am telling you this because I like you, and because I would rather you hear it from me than from my father's boys.*

He had asked her, *Does she know you're telling me this?*

Ngozi had looked at him with something that was almost pity. *She knows what she knows. Go and live your life, Tunde. She is going to live hers.*

But here was the thing he could not make Ngozi understand, the thing he could barely make himself understand: he had not come to the house tonight to plead. Not exactly. He had come because the song was finished — had finished itself, actually, at four in the morning three days ago, the last verse arriving whole and unbidden like a bird through an open window — and because a finished song is a debt, and he could not carry the debt into the rest of his life without paying it. If she married Chief Okafor with the song unsung, he would be singing it at forty, at sixty, in some other town, to some other woman, and the song would always be reaching past whoever heard it toward a kitchen in Ọ̀ṣun-Ilé country where a girl once asked him what the highlife bands in Lagos were like, and he had said, *Loud, and full of themselves,* and she had laughed and said, *Like you, then,* and he had fallen in love in the space of that laugh, the way you fall — not gradually, not with ceremony, but all at once, the ground simply opening.

He tuned the last string. The night was warm and smelled of woodsmoke and frying onions and, underneath, the green smell of the plantain trees at the back of the compound. Somewhere a dog was complaining about the moon. He waited.

He did not have to wait long. He had known Adaeze for two years, and in two years he had learned her rhythms the way a fisherman learns a river — where she ran shallow and quick, where she went deep and slow, where the current pulled without mercy. He knew that she could not sleep the night before any large event. He knew that on such nights she came out to the back of the kitchen to breathe, because the kitchen was the one room in her mother's house where she was allowed to be tired, and the back of it, behind the water pots, was the one place where she was allowed to be nothing at all.

She came around the corner with a lantern in her hand and stopped as if she had walked into glass.

"You shouldn't be here." Her voice was low, urgent, but she did not raise the lantern, did not call out, did not turn back inside. Tunde noted all of this the way a drowning man notes the shape of the shore.

"I know."

"My father's boys are sleeping in the front room. Chike is awake — he's always awake when there's meat in the house."

"I know that too." He stood, the guitar in his hands. "I came to finish something. Then I'll go, and you'll never see me again. I swear it on my mother's grave, Adaeze. I swear it on the church."

She looked at him for a long moment. The lantern trembled slightly in her grip, and the light trembled with it, so that his shadow shivered against the kitchen wall like a thing trying to get free. Then she set the lantern down on the upturned pot she used as a stool, deliberately, the way one sets down a decision, and folded her arms.

"Sing it, then."

* * *

He sang it badly, at first. His hands were shaking. The first verse — the one about the moon keeping watch over the sleeping compound — came out thin and uncertain, and he heard every flaw the way a father hears flaws in a child, and he almost stopped. But then he looked at her face, and her face did a thing he would carry for the rest of his life, through Lagos and London and the long gray corridors of his own disappearing mind: her face opened. Not softened — *opened*, like a door, like a hand, like the sky over the lagoon when the harmattan haze lifts and you can suddenly see how far everything goes.

He sang the second verse. The third. And then the last verse, the one that had arrived at four in the morning three days ago, the one he had not written so much as received:

*Sleep, my own, the road is long,*
*and I will not be there when you wake —*
*but the song will walk beside you,*
*and the song will not forget.*

He had written it, he realized as he sang it, about exactly this. He had written his own absence into a lullaby. He had composed the shape of the rest of his life without knowing it, the way a man composes his own eulogy in a fever.

When he finished, the compound was silent in a way that felt held, suspended, as though even the dog and the moon were waiting. Adaeze had her hand over her mouth. When she took it away her eyes were bright and furious at once, and she said, in Ọ̀ṣun-Ilé, the language they had built between themselves out of her mother's tongue and his father's and the pidgin of the market, the language that belonged to no one else on earth:

"What is it called?"

"It doesn't have a name yet."

"Then you'll name it after me."

"It's a lullaby. It's for a child."

"Then it's for the child I'll never have with you, and you'll name it after me anyway, and when you sing it to someone else's children you'll be singing my name and you won't even know it." She said it fast and hard, the way you say a thing to keep from weeping, and then the weeping came anyway, silently, her shoulders shaking without a sound, because sound would bring Chike and Chike would bring the boys and the boys would bring everything down.

Tunde crossed the space between them. He did not touch her. He stood close enough that she could have touched him, and he said the thing he had come to say, the plea, the whole plea, and it was smaller and more ordinary than any song:

"Come with me. Tonight. There's a lorry leaving for Lagos at four. I have an uncle in Surulere, a room, a floor at least. I have nothing, Adaeze. I have a borrowed guitar and eleven songs and my two hands. But I have nothing *else*, and everything I have is yours, and tomorrow you'll be Chief Okafor's wife and I will never—" His voice broke and he hated it, hated the crack in it, the smallness. "I will never sing to you again. I'll sing your name into strangers' children for the rest of my life. Is that what you want? Is that the life?"

And Adaeze — who in her daughter's imagination would become a villain, a stone, a closed door, a woman who chose a chief's compound over a musician's borrowed guitar — Adaeze sat down on the water pot and put her face in her hands, and when she looked up at him her face was the loneliest he had ever seen a face be, and she said:

"You think I don't know what I'm choosing? You think I haven't counted it? Tunde. *Tunde.* Listen to me." She stood. She took his hand — the first time she had touched him in three months, and her hand was cold, cold as a woman's hand in a story about ghosts. "My father owes Chief Okafor. Do you know that? Do you know why the wedding is so fast, why it's December and not next year? The farm went under in the bad rains. The debt is the size of a house. The bride price is the size of the debt. I am not a daughter marrying, I am a debt being paid, and if I run tonight, they will not come for me. They will come for Ngozi. She is nineteen. She is *nineteen*, Tunde, and she will stand where I'm standing tomorrow, and she will not even have a song."

Tunde felt the ground open under him, the same ground that had opened when she laughed, opening again, this time downward.

"I didn't know," he said. "I would have — I would have gone to your father, I would have—"

"With what? With the guitar?" She said it without cruelty, which was worse. "With eleven songs? My father would laugh you off the land, and then he would move the wedding up a month out of spite. There is no version of this where you save me. There is only the version where you save yourself, and the version where we both drown, and I have spent three months learning the difference, and it is the hardest arithmetic I will ever do."

"Then let me drown with you."

"No." She said it softly, and it was the softness that made it final. "Because you would drown *loudly*, Tunde. You would drown in songs. And I would spend the rest of my life in a compound where every song on the radio is a knife. No. Here is what I want, and you will do it because you love me, and this is the last thing I will ever ask you: sing the song somewhere far from here. Sing it so well that it travels. Sing it to children who will never know my name, so that it stops being mine and becomes everyone's, because a thing that belongs to everyone can't be taken from me. Can't be buried with me. Do you understand? I am not refusing you. I am *spending* you. It's not the same thing. God knows it is not the same thing."

And then — this was the part he would lose first, when the fog came, the part he would spend his last lucid mornings trying to hold — she reached up and touched his face, once, with the back of her fingers, the way you touch a child you are leaving with someone else at a gate.

"And when you sing it," she said, "sing the last verse wrong. Change one note. So that if I ever hear it — from a market radio, from a passing band, from anywhere in this whole wide world — I'll know it's you, and not a ghost. I'll know you lived. I'll know you kept the promise."

* * *

He kept it. God help him, he kept it for fifty years.

The lorry left for Lagos at four in the morning and Tunde Adewale was on it, sitting in the dark among strangers' yams and strangers' luggage, holding a borrowed guitar that Sunday Okon had told him to play like it was his own. He did not look back at the compound. He had promised. He sang the lullaby once, silently, inside his own skull, changing the note in the last verse — flattening it, a small wound in the melody, a signature — and he felt the song leave his body and become a thing in the world, and he understood that he had just become a father to something that would outlive every plan he had ever made.

He heard, years later, secondhand, that the marriage had been hard in its early years and then not hard, that Chief Okafor had turned out to be neither a monster nor a saint but a tired man with a big house, that Adaeze had become a woman known for her stillness, her competence, the way she could stand in a room full of noise and be a quiet place in it. He heard she had a daughter. He heard the daughter's name — Lena — and he wrote it in the back of a notebook and then, for years, did not look at it, the way you don't look at a letter you can't bring yourself to open.

He never went back. She never sent for him. Between those two silences a whole life fit, the way a whole ocean fits between two shores that can see each other on a clear day.

* * *

Now the shop was dim, and the afternoon light came through the window in dusty blades, and his granddaughter was saying his name for the third time.

"Grandpa. Grandpa. The woman is here again. The one from the other day — she came back, she's standing by the highlife records, and she keeps looking at you like—"

"Like what?" said Tunde Adewale, and his hands, without him, had already found the guitar, and were already, without him, finding the shape of the first chord of a song he could no longer fully remember — a lullaby, a debt, a name — with one note in the last verse that he had flattened on purpose, fifty years ago, for a woman who had asked him to sing her into strangers' children, and whose granddaughter was standing six feet away from him, and whose name, her name, the young one's name, was on the tip of his tongue, dissolving, dissolving, like sugar in water.
