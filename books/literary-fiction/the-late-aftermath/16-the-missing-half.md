## 16. The Missing Half

The studio smelled of dust and solder and old carpet, the way all small studios smell, as if sound itself had a body that shed skin into the corners. Lena sat with the cassette case open on her knees — the plastic shell cracked along one hinge, the label in her mother's hand curling at its edges: DO NOT PLAY. The ink had faded to the brown of dried blood.

She had played it once already, three days ago, and had not slept properly since. Her mother's voice, young and unbroken, singing Tunde's lullaby into a microphone in some room that no longer existed. Then the speaking voice, low and hurried, saying *I have to tell you what I did* — and then the tape's thin hiss, the sentence amputated mid-breath, as if the machine itself had flinched.

"You've been staring at that thing like it might apologize," said Femi, the studio's owner, a friend of a friend of the radio producer who had first led her here. He was younger than her by a decade, with kind, impatient hands.

"Is there any way to know how much is missing?"

He took the cassette, turned it over, held it to the light. "Side B was never recorded on, or it was erased, or—" he paused, weighing whether to say it, "—or someone cut it. There are splices. Look." He tilted it toward the lamp. A seam ran across the tape like a scar. "Someone recorded over part of this, or removed a section and rejoined it. Amateur work. Careful, though. Whoever did it wanted it to look whole."

Lena felt the floor tilt slightly, the vertigo she'd been living inside since the radio. *Her past exists without her permission.* Now even the evidence of it had been edited by someone else's hand.

"Who would have had this?" she asked, though she already knew. The trunk had come from Ngozi's house. Ngozi had packed it, sealed it, shipped it to London after the funeral with a card that said only: *Your mother's things. She wanted you to have them.* No mention of the cassette. No warning about the label. Just the words, and the silence around them.

Femi shrugged. "You'd have to ask whoever gave it to you."

---

Ngozi's house in Surulere sat behind a wall topped with broken glass, the gate painted a green so deep it looked almost black in the evening shade. Lena had been there twice since arriving in Lagos — once for the formal welcome that had felt more like an audit, once to ask about Tunde Adewale and been told, in a voice like a closed door, that some questions were debts better left unpaid.

Now she stood at the gate again, and the cassette burned in her bag like a coal.

The young man who kept the gate recognized her and let her through without calling ahead, which meant Ngozi had told him to. That, too, was an answer of sorts: her aunt had been expecting this visit, or something like it, for thirty years.

Inside, the compound was quiet except for a radio murmuring highlife from somewhere deep in the house, and the smell of pepper soup and camphor and old wood. Ngozi was on the veranda, shelling beans into a steel bowl, her reading glasses pushed up into her gray-threaded hair. She did not look up when Lena came out to her.

"You walk like your mother," Ngozi said. "Fast, like the ground owes you money."

"Aunty."

"Sit. If you're going to interrogate me, at least sit while you do it. Standing people ask cruel questions."

Lena sat. A hen crossed the yard with the unhurried arrogance of things that own their ground. Ngozi went on shelling, the beans dropping into the bowl one by one, each with a small dry knock.

"I played the cassette," Lena said.

The shelling stopped. Only for a moment — half a breath — then resumed. But Lena had spent twelve years translating other people's hesitations, and she knew what lived inside that half-breath.

"There was a message," Lena said. "On the tape. My mother started to say something and then the recording just—ends. And Femi says the tape was spliced. Cut and rejoined. Someone removed part of it."

The beans knocked into the bowl. Knock. Knock. Knock.

"Ngozi." She used the name bare, without the honorific, and saw her aunt's shoulders set. "Was it you?"

The older woman put down the bowl at last. She took off her glasses, folded them, set them on top of the bowl with the precision of a woman putting down a weapon rather than picking one up. When she turned, her face was not cold. That was the shock of it. Lena had spent weeks reading Ngozi's coolness as contempt, and now, up close, in the failing gold light, she saw what it actually was: the strained neutrality of someone holding a door shut against a flood.

"Your mother sent me that tape," Ngozi said, "eleven years before she died."

Lena's breath left her.

"She sent it with instructions. Written instructions." Ngozi rose, went inside, and came back with a biscuit tin — the old blue kind, Danish butter cookies long eaten, the tin holding sewing things or letters or, in every Nigerian household Lena had ever known, secrets. She opened it and drew out an envelope gone soft with age, its flap resealed many times, the paper worn at the corners from handling.

"She wrote: *Keep this where Adaeze will never find it. Give it to Lena when she can hear it.*"

The name hit Lena like a hand. Not her name — her mother's. Adaeze. She had heard it only at the funeral, spoken by strangers, and had not known until Lagos that it was the name her mother had been born with, the name that existed before the marriage, before England, before Lena herself.

"When she can hear it," Lena repeated. "What does that mean? What does that *mean*, Aunty?"

"It means your mother knew you," Ngozi said simply. "Better than you knew yourself, maybe. She said — I'm reading from memory now, but I read that letter so many times I could recite it in my sleep — she said, *My daughter has stopped hearing me. She answers me in English even when I speak to her in our tongue. She will not take this from my mouth. So keep it. One day the anger will crack, and when it cracks, she'll be able to hear. You'll know. You'll hear it in her voice.*"

The veranda seemed to sway. Lena gripped the arm of the chair. Somewhere beyond the wall, a generator coughed to life, and the radio inside the house warbled and steadied.

"So you decided," Lena said, and her voice came out strange, flattened, "for eleven years. You decided when I could hear."

"No." Ngozi's voice sharpened, and for the first time Lena heard the fear under it. "I decided nothing. I obeyed. Do you know what it is, to hold a thing like that? Your mother dying in London, and me sitting here with her voice in a tin, waiting for a day I couldn't see coming? Every Christmas I watched for you. Every phone call. Listening for the crack." She laughed, short and bitter. "It never came. Twelve years, Lena. You were a wall. A beautiful, successful, English-speaking wall."

"And then?"

"And then three months ago you called me asking about Tunde Adewale." Ngozi picked the glasses back up, put them on, took them off again — hands that needed occupation. "And I thought: there it is. The crack."

---

They went inside, because Ngozi said some things should not be said where the walls had ears and the walls in Surulere had many. In the back room, among stacked plastic chairs and a wardrobe that predated independence, Ngozi unlocked a drawer with a key she wore on a string around her neck — Lena had noticed it before and taken it for a crucifix or a locket.

From the drawer she lifted a second cassette. Identical shell. No label at all, except a date written small on the corner: a year Lena recognized as the one before her birth.

"This is the rest," Ngozi said. "What was cut off yours. She made two copies — one to send to me whole, one—" her mouth twisted, "—one she mutilated herself, before sending, because she said if it ever reached you too soon, it should reach you incomplete. She said a wound like hers shouldn't be handed to a child all at once."

"Why keep it hidden at all? Why not give me both after she died?"

"Because the second tape isn't just a confession." Ngozi held it out, and Lena saw that her aunt's hand trembled — this woman who had faced her down twice with glacial calm, trembling now over a ribbon of magnetic tape. "I listened to it once, the week it arrived. Once, in thirty years. And I will tell you why I locked it away, and you can hate me for it, and I will accept the hate, because I earned it."

She drew a breath that seemed to come up from her feet.

"On that tape, your mother asks forgiveness. From you. From God. From Tunde. She names everything — the night before her wedding, the choice she made, why she made it, whose debts she was paying. Everything you've been digging for in Lagos, she says it in her own voice, in our language, and it is—" Ngozi stopped, pressed her lips together, started again. "It is terrible and it is holy and I could not bear it. And at the end she says: *Tell Lena the truth when she can hear it. But tell her also that I asked forgiveness and I did not receive it, because I never asked the right person while they were alive to give it. Let her learn from that if she can. Let her not wait twelve years like her mother waited thirty.*"

The room was very quiet. The radio had been switched off; the generator hummed through the walls like a held breath.

"She asked forgiveness," Lena said slowly, "and didn't receive it. From whom?"

"Who do you think?" Ngozi's eyes were wet and furious. "From you, child. From you. She asked forgiveness from a daughter who wouldn't answer her in her own language. On the tape. Eleven years before she died. She knelt — I wasn't there, but you can hear it, you can hear her kneeling in her voice — and she asked a girl of twenty-something to forgive her, knowing the girl would never hear it, and she sent it anyway, because that is what we do with the prayers we cannot say out loud. We record them. We hide them in tins. We wait."

Lena's legs gave and she sat down hard on one of the plastic chairs. It groaned under her.

All those years. All those Sunday phone calls conducted in careful English, her mother's Ọ̀ṣun-Ilé met with flat British vowels until, eventually, her mother stopped offering the language at all — and Lena had catalogued that retreat as surrender, as guilt, as the slow confession of a woman who had something to hide. She had built a whole architecture of blame out of her mother's silences. And underneath the architecture, all along, a woman on her knees, asking.

"The night at the hospital," Lena whispered. "The last time. She spoke to me in Ọ̀ṣun-Ilé and I answered in English and she—" she remembered it now with terrible new clarity: her mother's face going still, not wounded exactly, but *confirmed*. Like a diagnosis arriving. "She knew. She'd already asked. On the tape. And I refused her again, live, in person, without ever knowing I was refusing."

"Yes."

"And you let me fly home thinking—"

"I let you do what your mother instructed," Ngozi said, and now the steel came back, the old coldness, and Lena understood at last that it had never been coldness at all. "Because I promised a dying woman. Because she said, *if you give it to her too early, she will break, and I will not be there to help her carry it.*" Ngozi crouched, awkwardly, joints cracking, until her face was level with Lena's. "Do you understand what I am telling you? The silence was not only yours. It was not only mine. It was hers. She built it brick by brick, the same as you built yours, the same as I kept mine — three women, one wall, each of us laying bricks on different sides of it, thinking we were protecting the others."

Lena looked at the cassette in Ngozi's palm. Unlabeled. Thirty years old. Containing her mother's voice, kneeling.

"Play it," Lena said.

"Not here." Ngozi closed her fist around it. "Not tonight. Tonight you eat pepper soup and you sleep, because tomorrow—" she paused, and something moved across her face that Lena had never seen there before, something that might have been tenderness trying to remember its shape, "—tomorrow I will go with you. To the studio. To listen. I have owed that tape an audience for thirty years, and I find I don't want to sit alone with it anymore."

---

That night, in the guest room that still held traces of her mother's girlhood — a faded poster of a highlife band, initials carved into the window frame, *A.O.*, and beside them, carved by a different, hungrier hand, *T.A.* — Lena lay awake and listened to the compound settle.

*T.A.* Carved into wood by a boy who had been disowned for loving a woman promised elsewhere. Beside the initials of the girl who chose duty, and sang his lullaby once, and hid her apology in a biscuit tin for three decades.

*When she can hear it.*

Lena pressed her palm flat against the windowsill, against the carved letters, and thought: *I can hear it now, Mama. I think I finally can.*

Tomorrow, her mother's voice would finish the sentence it began on the spliced tape. Tomorrow, the missing half.

But sleep, when it finally came, brought no comfort — only the lullaby, running over and over in the dark of her mind, and beneath it, faint as a heartbeat, a phrase she hadn't yet placed, in her mother's voice, repeating: *ask her, ask her, ask her* — and Lena woke at dawn with the certainty that the tape was not the end of what was hidden, but the beginning, and that somewhere between Ngozi's tin and Tunde's fading memory lay the last piece neither of them had dared to name.

She dressed quickly. Downstairs, Ngozi was already waiting, the blue tin under her arm, the key around her neck.

"Ready?" her aunt asked.

"No," Lena said. "Let's go anyway."
