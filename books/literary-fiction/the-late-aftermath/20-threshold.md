## 20. Threshold

The call came on a Tuesday, which felt wrong somehow. Tunde Adewale should have died on a Sunday — a day of rest, a day of song, a day when the whole neighborhood would have been home to hear the news travel door to door like a hymn. But death keeps no liturgical calendar, and so it was a Tuesday morning in London, grey as unwashed wool, when Lena's phone lit up with Ngozi's name and she knew, before answering, what the voice on the other end would say.

She let it ring four times. Not out of dread. Out of a strange, superstitious courtesy — the way you pause at a doorway before entering a room where someone is sleeping.

"Aunty." She said it in Ọ̀ṣun-Ilé. *Ìyá mi.* The word came easily now; that was the strangest part of all of it, how the language had come back not like a flood but like a tide, patient, covering her ankles, her knees, until one morning she looked down and could no longer see the ground she'd been standing on for twelve years.

Ngozi's voice was steady in the way that cost something. "He went in his sleep. Fúnmi found him at dawn. He had dressed himself properly — his good agbada, the white one. He was lying with his hands folded on his chest like a man waiting to be called into a photograph."

Lena closed her eyes. In the dark behind them: the music shop in Yaba, dust motes turning in the light from the doorway, Tunde's hands moving over guitar strings with the certainty his mind no longer always gave him. *Play it again,* she had said, a hundred times, and he always had, even on the days he couldn't remember her name but could remember every note.

"Was it—" She stopped. Started again. "Did he know, at the end? Who anyone was?"

There was a silence long enough to hold an entire marriage, an entire betrayal, an entire forgiveness.

"On his last good morning," Ngozi said, "he told Fúnmi that a visitor was coming from across the water. That she would know the song. He asked her to make sure the guitars were tuned."

* * *

Lagos received her back without ceremony. The heat pressed against her skin like a palm, familiar now, almost affectionate. At Murtala Muhammed Airport the announcements tumbled over each other in English and Yoruba and that third language, her mother's language, spoken by porters and taxi drivers and two women arguing cheerfully about the price of tomatoes — alive, unrecorded, indifferent to whether any single speaker survived. This was what she had come to understand in these months: languages did not die the way people died. They died the way rivers dried — slowly, invisibly, and then all at once, and then not at all, because rain always came somewhere.

Fúnmi met her outside the arrivals gate. Tunde's granddaughter was twenty-three and built like a question mark about to straighten itself, and she had inherited her grandfather's habit of looking at people as though memorizing them.

"He left something for you," she said, instead of hello. "But not yet. He was very clear. *Not yet.* He said you'd know when."

"When will I know?"

Fúnmi shrugged, and for a moment she looked so much like the old man that Lena's throat closed. "He said you were a translator. He said translators always know when a thing is finished."

* * *

The house in Yaba smelled of kola nut and old wood and, beneath everything, faintly, of guitar strings. The wake was already in its second night when Lena arrived, the compound full of chairs borrowed from three neighboring houses, women in wrappers of every color sitting in rows, men standing at the edges holding drinks they weren't drinking. A highlife band played under a canopy strung with bulbs, and the singer's voice rose and fell with that particular Lagos grief that never quite decides between mourning and celebration.

Lena sat where she was put, near the front, and let the language wash through her. She understood nearly all of it now. The eulogies. The proverbs. An old man describing Tunde as *a man who kept other people's songs safer than his own money*. A woman — someone's grandmother, someone's memory — recalling the young Tunde of the sixties, the one with the guitar and the reputation, who had loved a girl from a family that would not have him, and who had gone away to Lagos and turned the losing into music.

"You," the woman said suddenly, finding Lena's face in the crowd with unsettling accuracy. "You are Adéwálé's daughter. You have her chin."

*Adéwálé.* Her mother's name, spoken aloud here, in this compound, after all these years. Lena nodded. She could not speak. The woman took her hand anyway, held it in both of hers, dry and strong as bark.

"He wrote your mother's name into a song once," the woman said. "Everyone pretended not to notice. That was the whole generation, you understand? We heard everything and we said nothing, and we thought we were being loyal, and really we were just afraid." She patted Lena's hand and released it. "Sing tomorrow. He tuned that guitar for you. Fúnmi told us."

* * *

She barely slept. In the guest room, under a mosquito net glowing faintly in the streetlight, she lay awake doing what she had done every night since the recording sessions ended: running the lullaby through her mind, note by note, word by word, checking it the way a jeweler checks a setting.

*Ọmọ mi, sun, oògùn ònà ti paré.*
My child, sleep, the medicine of the road is finished.

Tunde had written the first verse the night before her mother's wedding — the night she chose duty over him, the night she stood in her father's kitchen with her hands flat on the table and said *no* in the steadiest voice she owned, because her family's survival was balanced on the decision and love, however enormous, however real, could not feed seven mouths or redeem a father's debts. He had written the song because it was the only way he had to say goodbye without saying it. And then, years later, after learning of a child born across the ocean — a child named for a river goddess, a child he had never met — he had written the second verse. The one addressed to a girl he would never hold.

*Ẹni tí ó fi ọ̀rọ̀ sílẹ̀, ọ̀rọ̀ yóò gbé e lọ.*
The one who leaves words behind — the words will carry her.

Lena had translated it four times before she got it right. The first versions were too literal, the second too pretty, the third too clever. The fourth she had simply sung aloud, alone in Tunde's shop at dusk, and known immediately that it was finished — the way you know bread is done by smell, by some sense older than technique.

And there was the altered phrase. The wrong note. The thing she had heard him play incorrectly that first day in the shop, before either of them knew the other — the flattened seventh where the melody wanted a major, the small wound in the song that she had carried out of that shop like a stone in her shoe. It had taken weeks to understand: the alteration wasn't error. It was grief. He had changed the phrase the night he learned of her mother's death, and his hands had refused ever afterward to play it the old way. His memory had begun eating names and faces and years, but the grief-note it preserved perfectly, the way amber preserves the insect.

At the funeral, she would sing it as he had last played it. Both verses. The wound included. You do not correct a man's mourning. You carry it forward.

* * *

The burial was at mid-morning, in a small cemetery behind a church whose paint had surrendered decades ago to the weather. The grave diggers worked with unhurried dignity, red earth coming up in clods that steamed faintly in the sun. The pastor spoke. The choir sang two hymns. And then Fúnmi appeared at Lena's elbow, and without a word pressed the guitar into her hands — Tunde's guitar, the old one, the one he sometimes forgot he owned and sometimes, in the fog, forgot how to play until his fingers remembered for him.

The compound of mourners quieted in stages, like a lake settling after a stone.

Lena stood. Her wrapper — Ngozi had chosen it, indigo, her mother's color — felt suddenly heavy, ceremonial, a costume for a role she hadn't auditioned for. She looked out at the faces: strangers and almost-strangers, the woman with the bark-dry hands, the old men in agbadas, children perched on walls, Fúnmi with her chin lifted, and Ngozi at the edge of the gathering, arms folded, eyes bright and unreadable, the keeper of debts finally watching one get paid.

"I never met him as my mother's friend," Lena said, in Ọ̀ṣun-Ilé, and heard her own voice steadier than she expected. "I met him as a stranger in a photograph. Then as a teacher. Then — I don't have the word for what he became. Maybe our language doesn't have that word. Maybe that's why we keep making new songs."

A ripple of assent moved through the crowd, soft as wind through maize.

Then she sang.

The first verse came out of her whole body — not performed but released, the way you release a bird you've been keeping. *Ọmọ mi, sun.* My child, sleep. Her voice was not beautiful in the professional sense; it cracked on the second line and she let it crack, because Tunde had taught her that the crack was where the truth lived, that a perfectly smooth song was a locked door. The melody walked forward, sure-footed, and when it reached the place where the wrong note waited she did not flinch. She bent the phrase exactly as his hands had bent it, the flattened seventh falling into place like a scar becoming a feature of the face, and she heard — she was certain she heard, though she would never be able to prove it — a collective intake of breath from the older mourners, the ones who knew the original, recognizing the grief and honoring it.

Then the second verse. The one for the child.

*Ẹni tí ó fi ọ̀rọ̀ sílẹ̀...*

Her voice thinned on it. This was the verse addressed to her, written by a man she had argued with in a music shop, translated for, recorded, forgiven, buried. Singing it over his grave was like reading your own letter after the sender has died — every word arriving too late and exactly on time.

And that was when it happened.

An old woman's voice joined hers — low, half a beat behind, uncertain of the words but certain of the tune. Then a man's, deeper, finding the harmony line that Lagos voices find instinctively, the way water finds its level. Then the choir, abandoning their hymnal discipline. Then the children on the wall, singing the syllables wrong and the melody right. The song moved through the crowd the way fire moves through dry grass, not because anyone led it but because the tune had been waiting in them, dormant, the way a language waits in the grandchildren of its speakers — and Lena stood in the center of it with the guitar going soft under her hands, singing less and listening more, feeling the song pass through her and out of her and into thirty living mouths, forty, and understanding that this was the only immortality her mother's language had ever needed or wanted: not archives, not cassettes labeled DO NOT PLAY, not the careful scholarship of daughters. Just this. A circle of people who needed sleep, or comfort, or courage, receiving it in a melody that refused to die.

The last note hung in the heat. Somewhere beyond the wall, a generator coughed and caught. Life, rude and persistent, resuming.

Ngozi crossed the red earth to her. For a moment the old woman said nothing — she simply reached out and touched the guitar, the way you touch a child's head in blessing. Then, quietly, in Ọ̀ṣun-Ilé:

"Your mother heard this song once. Only once, in the kitchen, the night before her wedding. She hummed it for years afterward and never told anyone where it came from." Her voice roughened. "I think she was waiting for someone to bring it back to her. I think she was waiting for you."

"She never asked me to come," Lena said. "On the tape. She started to say it and the tape ran out."

"The tape ran out," Ngozi agreed. "Life did not." She squeezed Lena's shoulder once, hard, and turned away, and Lena saw that she was crying without any change in her posture, the tears simply falling from a face that had decided decades ago not to perform its feelings — the same face, Lena realized, the same discipline, that her mother had worn in the hospital bed while her daughter chose English.

* * *

That evening, the compound emptied by degrees. The borrowed chairs went home. The band packed its amps. Fúnmi swept the red dust into neat piles, and Lena sat on the step of Tunde's shop with the guitar across her knees, playing nothing, just letting her fingers rest on the strings.

Fúnmi came and sat beside her. For a while they watched the streetlights come on down the road, one by one, unreliable and stubborn.

"He made me promise," Fúnmi said at last. "The last good morning. He was completely himself — sharper than he'd been in months. He dictated a letter and made me write it down, and he sealed it, and he said: *Give this to Lena when the song has left her hands.*"

Lena turned to look at her. "When the song has left her hands?"

"That's what he said. I asked him what it meant and he smiled — you know that smile, like he knows the ending of a film you haven't seen — and he said, *She'll understand. Translators always know when a thing is finished.*"

From inside her wrapper, Fúnmi produced an envelope. Heavy paper, sealed with wax, TUNDE'S careful hand — no, not careful anymore; the handwriting of a man writing against time, urgent and legible both, the letters of someone who knows this may be the last lucid hour and refuses to waste a single one of them.

On the front, her name. *Lena.* And beneath it, smaller, in Ọ̀ṣun-Ilé, a phrase she read twice before her heart allowed her to translate it:

*For the one who carries the song across the water.*

The evening air was warm on her face. Down the street, faintly, from a house she couldn't see, someone was humming — the lullaby, already changed slightly, already becoming theirs, the way all living songs change. The song had left her hands. It belonged to the mouths now.

Lena took the envelope. Her fingers trembled — not from grief, she realized, but from the particular vertigo of standing at a threshold, hearing the latch lift, knowing that whatever was written inside would send her home changed, again, and that home itself would have to be rebuilt around it.

She broke the seal.
