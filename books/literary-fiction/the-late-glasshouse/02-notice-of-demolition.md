## 2. Notice of Demolition

The letter came on a Tuesday, which meant nothing at the time and everything afterward.

Xanthe found it on the hall floor of Glasshouse at half past seven in the morning, having come down in socks to listen to the house wake up. This was a ritual older than most of their habits: stand in the entrance hall with the lights off and the radiators ticking, and let the building tell them what kind of day it intended to have. The east wing's pipes always spoke first—a thin metallic complaint as the boiler pushed heat through forty metres of iron that had been installed before either of the World Wars and had opinions about it. Then the west wing answered, slower, deeper, the groan of a system that had been patched in 1974 by a man named Cyril who had used the wrong gauge of copper throughout and never told anyone.

Today the house sounded tired. Not broken—Xanthe would have heard broken; they had spent thirty years learning the difference between a house that was failing and a house that was merely old—but tired, the way an old dog sounds when it settles onto its side with a long exhale. The radiators in the morning room were clicking in their irregular rhythm, three ticks and a pause, three ticks and a pause, like a clock that had forgotten what it was counting toward.

The letter lay on the flagstones just inside the door, white against the grey stone, too clean for the room it had landed in.

Xanthe picked it up without hurry. Post was rare at Glasshouse and had been for years: the occasional utility statement addressed to Vane Estates Ltd, care of the agent in Ashbourne; once, memorably, a seed catalogue for a Mrs. E. Vane that had arrived eleven years late, forwarded through three addresses, its corners soft as cloth from handling. Xanthe had put that one on the hall table and left it there for a month before filing it in the drawer with the others—the drawer that held the estate's whole correspondence history, or what Xanthe had been permitted to keep of it.

This envelope was different. Heavy paper. A solicitor's name embossed in the corner: Hargreaves & Pell, Derby. And no stamp, which meant it had been hand-delivered, which meant someone had driven out here, up the rutted lane, past the fallen wall and the rhododendrons grown to the size of small weather systems, specifically to place this letter on the floor of a house everyone said was empty.

Xanthe stood in the cold hall holding it and did not open it. This was not superstition, exactly. It was more that Xanthe had learned, over decades, to read the weight of things before their contents. A piano told you its troubles through the case before you ever lifted the lid: bulging panels meant damp, a sour smell meant moth, a lid that sat crooked meant someone had leaned on it who shouldn't have. Letters were the same. You could feel urgency in the thickness of an envelope, dread in the crispness of the fold.

This one felt final. It felt like a door closing somewhere far away, with Xanthe on the wrong side of it.

They carried it into the kitchen, set it on the scrubbed pine table beside the tuning tools laid out for the day's work—mutes, felt strips, the tuning hammer worn to the shape of their grip—and made tea. The kettle took its time. The kitchen radiator hissed its low winter note, the one that meant air in the line again, the bleed valve on the third floor needing attention. Xanthe noted it automatically, added it to the list that lived permanently behind their eyes: third-floor bleed valves, the sticking sash in the blue bedroom, the damp patch in the library ceiling that needed the gutter cleared before autumn finished its work. Thirty years of lists. The house gave them work the way other houses gave people nothing, and Xanthe had built a life inside that exchange so gradually they could no longer see its shape from the inside, the way you cannot see the shape of the water you swim in.

Then they opened the letter.

*Dear Sir or Madam,* it began, which was already wrong, because whoever wrote it knew perfectly well who lived here, or believed no one did, and both possibilities were insulting in different directions.

*We write on behalf of our client, Vane Estates Limited, in respect of the freehold property known as Glasshouse, Ashbourne Road [parish omitted], Derbyshire.*

*Our client has resolved to dispose of the above property. Planning consent has been granted for demolition of the existing structures and redevelopment of the site. We are instructed to give notice that vacant possession is required on or before the 14th day of December next, being a period of six weeks from the date of this letter.*

*We understand that a caretaker may be in occupation under informal arrangements of long standing. We regret that we are unable to recognise any such arrangement as creating rights of occupation. Our client is prepared, as a gesture of goodwill, to make a payment of £2,000 in respect of relocation expenses upon receipt of vacant possession.*

*We should be grateful if you would acknowledge receipt of this letter and confirm your intention to comply.*

*Yours faithfully,*

*P. Hargreaves*

Xanthe read it twice. Then a third time, standing at the counter with the tea going cold beside them, while the kitchen radiator hissed and clicked and went on saying what it had always said, indifferent to everything.

Six weeks.

* * *

The strange thing—not the strangest thing, but the first strange thing—was how ordinary the rest of the day remained.

Xanthe had a tuning booked in Bakewell, a Bechstein upright belonging to a schoolteacher named Mrs. Ollerenshaw who insisted on annual tunings whether the piano needed them or not, because her late husband had always had it done and she saw no reason to break the sequence now he was gone. Xanthe drove the twelve miles in the van with the letter on the passenger seat, face-up, like a passenger itself, and tuned the Bechstein, and drank the offered tea, and admired the garden through the window when invited to admire it, and smiled the smile that ended conversations when Mrs. Ollerenshaw began to ask about family, about whether Xanthe had anyone, about how it must be lonely work, all that travelling alone.

"It suits me," Xanthe said, which was true, and had been true, and was perhaps still true, though the word *suits* had begun to sound different in the mouth, like a coat that fits but has somehow changed colour overnight.

The smile worked as it always worked. Mrs. Ollerenshaw beamed, patted the piano's cheek as though it were a horse, and let Xanthe go. Xanthe packed the tools with the economy of thirty years' practice—every tool had its place in the case, worn smooth by repetition—and drove home through the grey afternoon with the wipers going and the letter riding shotgun.

Home. The word arrived unbidden and Xanthe examined it the way they'd examine a suspect note on a piano: pressing it gently, listening for falseness. Glasshouse was not home. Glasshouse was an arrangement. That was what Xanthe had told themselves for three decades: an arrangement, informal, unwritten, between themselves and a family that had mostly ceased to exist as a presence in the world. Old Mr. Vane—Eleanor's husband, Gerald—had made it before he died: keep the house sound, keep the pipes from freezing, keep the roof on, and live in the east wing rooms and take what tuning work the county offered. No rent mentioned. No wage mentioned. Nothing signed. Gerald Vane had shaken Xanthe's hand in this very kitchen and said, "You'll look after the old girl, then," meaning the house, and Xanthe had said yes, and that had been the entire contract, sealed with a handshake and a cup of tea, and it had held for thirty years because nobody had thought to break it.

Now somebody had thought to break it. Somebody had gone to a solicitor, and paid for heavy paper, and instructed a hand-delivery, and granted planning consent—which took months, Xanthe knew, months of applications and surveys and notices pinned to parish boards—and all of that machinery had been turning somewhere out in the world, in offices Xanthe had never seen, while Xanthe bled radiators and tuned pianos and kept the old girl sound.

While you were keeping the house, someone was unkeeping it.

The van turned in at the gate—the gate that hung crooked because the post had rotted and Xanthe had reported it twice to the agents and received no reply either time—and crunched up the drive under the bare plane trees. The house rose ahead of it in the mist, long and low and grey-stoned, its glass conservatory frontage catching what little light remained. People asked sometimes why it was called Glasshouse, expecting a nursery, a hothouse, some Victorian extravagance of iron and panes. It was simpler than that: the south face was nearly all window, a wall of glass installed by some confident ancestor who believed the sun would go on shining forever onto his family and his fortune. Both beliefs had failed. The glass remained, dirty at the edges, a few panes clouded with condensation between their layers, and every afternoon the house caught the last light and held it briefly, glowing like something lit from within, before the sun dropped behind the ridge and the glow went out.

Xanthe parked, sat a moment with hands on the wheel, then got out and walked the length of the south front, as they did most evenings, checking the glass by eye. A habit indistinguishable from devotion, if you were inclined to call it that.

The house looked the same. That was the thing. Six weeks from demolition and it stood there doing what it had always done, breathing its heat through its iron veins, ticking and settling, entirely unaware—or entirely unsurprised, which amounted to the same thing. Houses don't know their futures. Only the people in them do, and only sometimes.

* * *

That night Xanthe did not sleep well, and the house kept them company in its usual way.

They had lived in the east wing rooms for so long that their own sounds had merged with the house's: the particular creak of the fourth stair, the way the bedroom radiator knocked twice before settling at two in the morning when the boiler cycled down, the whistle of wind at the corner where the guttering had a gap no roofer had ever come to fix. Xanthe lay awake cataloguing these sounds the way a musician lies awake hearing a piece they can no longer stop playing. Two thousand pounds. Relocation expenses. As if the life being relocated were a quantity of furniture, measurable, portable, worth a round figure.

Where would the sounds go? That was the question that kept surfacing, absurd and enormous. Every house had its voice—you learned that in the first year of tuning, if you had ears at all, and Xanthe had ears the way other people had lungs. A house's voice was the sum of its water and its heat and its timber and its stone, all of it moving, all of it speaking, and after thirty years Xanthe could hear Glasshouse's whole biography in an hour's quiet standing: the 1890s confidence of the main stack, the wartime austerity of the patched flue, the optimism of the 1970s copper, the slow failures of the last decade that no one had funded repairs for because no one was left who cared. The house was an archive that happened to also be a building. And they were going to knock it down and put—what? The letter didn't say. Redevelopment. New builds, probably. Executive homes with underfloor heating and combi boilers, houses with no memory and no need of a tuner's ear, houses that would speak only in the flat present tense of plastic pipe.

And where did that leave the person whose whole self had been built out of listening?

Xanthe got up at three and went down to the kitchen in the dark and made tea they didn't drink, and stood at the window watching the mist move across the parkland, and tried to feel what they were supposed to feel. Grief, presumably. Fear, certainly—six weeks, and then what, a bedsit in Ashbourne, a van, the same rounds, the same smile, minus the anchor. But underneath the fear was something else, something that troubled them more because it refused to resolve: relief. A thin, shameful thread of it. Because the arrangement, the loose unwritten arrangement, had also been a cage, hadn't it? Thirty years of service rendered to an absent family, loyalty paid out year after year to an account that never acknowledged the deposit. They had stayed because staying was easier than leaving, and called it duty, and the house had let them call it that, had hummed its warm agreement around them, complicit in the fiction.

Now the choice had been taken away, and some part of Xanthe—small, buried, disowned—was almost grateful.

They hated that part. They listened to it anyway.

* * *

In the morning, practicality reasserted itself, as it always did. Xanthe was a person who fixed things; the instinct operated even when the thing was their own life, even when the fix was beyond them.

They sat at the kitchen table with the letter flat before them and made notes in the margin of a tuning ledger, because there was no other paper to hand and because writing in ledgers was what their hands did. Facts. Dates. The 14th of December. Vacant possession. £2,000. Hargreaves & Pell, Derby. Vane Estates Limited.

Vane Estates Limited. Xanthe turned the name over. In thirty years they had dealt with the family perhaps six times: Gerald, before his death; a solicitor twice; a nephew once, briefly, some years ago, a man in a good coat who had walked through the ground floor with his hands in his pockets and said "God, it's dark in here" and left within twenty minutes. Eleanor Vane, the last mistress, had died—no. Had she? Xanthe searched the memory and found it strangely soft-edged. There had been a funeral, surely, or news of one. There had been a period when the letters stopped coming, when the agents stopped replying, when the arrangement simply continued on inertia, Xanthe working and living and reporting to no one, sending invoices to an address that may or may not have existed, cashing cheques that arrived with metronomic regularity from an account that never queried a single item. Someone had been paying. Someone, somewhere, had been signing off on the boiler servicing and the gutter clearing and the caretaker's existence for years without ever once asking a question.

Who signs cheques for a house they've abandoned? Who keeps a caretaker they've forgotten?

Unless they hadn't forgotten. Unless the payments were not neglect but something else—an annuity, a penance, a debt being serviced quietly across the decades. Xanthe had never wondered about it before. Wondering had seemed dangerous, the way looking too closely at a load-bearing wall seems dangerous: better not to know what holds you up, in case knowing makes it fall.

But the ground was falling anyway. Someone else had decided that. So Xanthe sat in the cold kitchen and, for the first time in thirty years, allowed themselves to wonder openly, the wonder coming slowly at first and then all at once, like water finding a channel.

Vane Estates Limited. Trustees, probably. Families like that dissolved into trusts the way sugar dissolves into tea—invisible, everywhere, sweetening nothing. And trusts had beneficiaries, and beneficiaries had names, and names could be found. Xanthe had no idea how one went about finding them—solicitors, presumably, or the Land Registry, or asking Dev—

No. Xanthe stopped. The name surfaced from nowhere and meant nothing yet, a face half-remembered from some future that hadn't happened. They set it aside. There would be time for people later. First, the facts.

They wrote at the bottom of the page, in the small precise hand they used for pitch notations:

*Who wants it gone, and why now?*

Because that was the question, wasn't it. Houses fell into ruin on their own schedule; they did not acquire planning consent. Consent meant application, fees, surveys, patience. Someone had wanted this enough to spend money and wait. Someone had wanted Glasshouse gone quickly and quietly, before anyone who might object noticed there was anything to object to.

And the only person living in the way—the only witness, the only keeper of whatever the house might have to say about the matter—was a piano tuner with a practiced smile and no legal right to the floor beneath their feet.

Xanthe looked up at the kitchen ceiling, at the faint brown bloom of the damp patch they'd been monitoring for four years, and felt the house around them the way you feel a sleeping animal: its warmth, its weight, its utter trust.

"All right," they said aloud, to the radiator, to the pipes, to the old girl herself. "Let's find out who's doing this to you."

The house ticked back, three ticks and a pause, and said nothing it hadn't said before. But upstairs, in the east wing, in the room Xanthe had slept in for thirty years, there stood a wardrobe that had always been heavier than its contents required—and in six weeks it would all have to come out anyway, everything, every drawer and cupboard and cavity, before the machines came.

There was packing to do. And packing, Xanthe would learn, means opening.
