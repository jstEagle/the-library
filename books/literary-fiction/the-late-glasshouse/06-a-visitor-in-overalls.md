## 6. A Visitor in Overalls

The van arrived on a Tuesday, which Xanthe would later think of as the last ordinary day, though at the time it seemed only another grey entry in the ledger of a grey month. They heard it before they saw it—the particular complaint of an overworked alternator labouring up the drive, a sound the gravel reported to the house and the house reported through its bones to the soles of Xanthe's feet as they stood in the scullery with their hands in a sink of cold water.

Glasshouse had not received a visitor in eleven months. The postman did not count; he came to the gate and left his offerings in the box like tribute left outside a temple whose god had long since stopped answering. The Vane family's solicitors sent letters that required no face. The occasional rambler strayed up from the footpath, took one look at the façade—its windows blind with grime, its stone the colour of old teeth—and turned back, satisfied or disappointed, Xanthe never learned which.

But this was a van, and vans meant work, and work meant someone had decided something about Glasshouse without consulting the house itself.

Xanthe dried their hands on the towel that hung by the sink, a towel gone thin as communion paper, and went out through the back passage into the pale light.

* * *

Dev Okonkwo had been surveying buildings for nine years, and in those nine years he had developed a private taxonomy of the people who answered doors. There were the owners, who wanted to tell you everything; the tenants, who wanted to tell you nothing; the agents, who wanted to tell you what they'd been told to tell you; and then there was a fourth kind, rarer, whom he had never managed to classify—the ones who opened the door as if they were the building itself, as if the knock had travelled through them rather than to them.

This one was the fourth kind.

They were perhaps fifty-five, though age sat strangely on them, worn smooth rather than deepened, like a pebble in a stream. Grey hair cut short and practical. Clothes that had once been good and were now merely serviceable. And eyes—Dev noticed eyes professionally; you learned to read whether a person would let you onto their roof before you asked—eyes that looked at him the way you might look at weather: assessing, unhurried, already deciding how much of you to let in.

"Morning," Dev said. "I'm looking for the caretaker. Or—" he consulted his clipboard, though he didn't need to; he had read the file twice on the drive over, "—Xanthe Marchetti?"

"You've found them." No handshake offered. No invitation either, but no door closed. The figure stood in the frame the way furniture stands: present, load-bearing, going nowhere.

"Dev Okonkwo." He held up the lanyard with its photograph of himself looking younger and more optimistic. "Chartered surveyor. I'm carrying out a pre-demolition condition survey for the trustees. Should take three days, maybe four. I'll need access to all floors, the cellars, and the roof space if it's safe."

Something moved behind those eyes—not surprise exactly. Confirmation, maybe. As if a sound the house had been making for weeks had finally resolved into words.

"Six weeks," Xanthe said. It wasn't a question. It was the sentence they had been living inside since the letter came, testing its walls.

"The notice says forty-two days from issue," Dev said carefully. He had learned, over the years, to be careful with the arithmetic of demolition around the people who lived inside it. "I'm just the man who measures."

"Yes," said Xanthe. "You're just the man who measures." And there was something in the way they said it—not hostile, not warm, but weighted, like a coin placed deliberately on a counter—that made Dev want to say something else, something unprofessional, something like *I know what it is to be reduced to your function*. He didn't say it. He clipped his lanyard on, shouldered his bag, and waited.

After a moment, Xanthe stepped back from the doorway. Not an invitation. An acquiescence.

"Mind the third stair on the west flight," they said. "It sings."

* * *

It sang. Of course it sang. Everything in Glasshouse sang, if you knew how to listen, and Xanthe had spent thirty years learning the score.

They followed the surveyor through the house at a distance calibrated to be neither welcoming nor obstructive—two paces behind and slightly to the side, the position Rosa had taught them before she'd taught them anything else. *Never in front*, she'd said, straightening young Xanthe's collar in the mirror of the servants' hall, her hands smelling of beeswax and lavender water. *In front is where they look. Behind is where they don't. We live behind.* Xanthe had been seven years old and had understood it the way children understand theology: completely, and without knowing they would spend a lifetime practising it.

Dev worked the way water works—finding every low place, every crack, pooling briefly in each corner before moving on. He tapped walls with a knuckle and listened. He photographed the damp bloom on the morning room ceiling, the staircase balustrade gnawed by decades of hands, the conservatory where the glass had begun its slow surrender, pane by pane, to gravity and frost. He wrote in a small notebook in handwriting so compressed it looked like iron filings arranged by a magnet.

And he asked questions.

That was the trouble. Other surveyors—there had been two, years ago, assessing insurance nobody expected to pay out—had asked about access and hazards and nothing else. This one asked, while running his torch along the panelling of the drawing room: "Who laid this floor? It's herringbone oak. That's not standard for a house this period—it's earlier, or salvaged."

"I wouldn't know," said Xanthe, which was true in the way most of their answers were true: technically accurate, spiritually evasive.

"And the radiators." He crouched at one, the great Victorian column radiator in the library, its cast iron flaked with paint like the skin of a moulting reptile. "These are original, but they've been modified. Someone's re-plumbed half the system themselves. Hand-bent pipe, non-standard fittings. Thirty years of work, I'd guess. Maybe more."

Xanthe said nothing. Their silence had a texture the surveyor apparently could feel, because he glanced up, and for a moment their eyes met across the dusty gold light falling through the library window, and Xanthe understood that this man heard things too. Different things. But he listened with his whole body, the way they did, and that made him dangerous in a way the solicitors and the wreckers were not.

"It's good work," Dev said, standing. "Whoever did it loved this house. You don't hand-bend thirty years of pipework for a building you're indifferent to."

"The pipes needed bending," Xanthe said. "I bent them."

It was the longest speech they had made to a stranger in years, and it cost them something. They turned away, ostensibly to open the shutters wider, and felt the practiced smile assemble itself on their face—ready, deployed, the drawbridge going up.

But Dev had already turned back to his notebook, writing, and whatever he wrote he kept, and Xanthe found themselves wondering, with an unease that had no honest name, what it was.

* * *

By the second afternoon, Dev had filled forty pages and formed a theory he could not put on any clipboard.

The theory was this: the house was not empty. It was inhabited the way a shell is inhabited by the sound of the sea. Everywhere he went, he found evidence of maintenance so constant and so tender it amounted to devotion—sash cords replaced, gutters cleared, rot arrested mid-advance like a battle frozen at its turning point. And everywhere he went, he also found evidence of the maintainer's presence in small involuntary ways: a chair drawn up beneath the fuse box at exactly the height of a person sitting watch; a mug ring on the cellar steps' third tread, where someone drank tea in the dark waiting for a damp reading; a pencil mark on the boiler room wall, a tally, hundreds strong, counting something—days? readings? visits?

He had surveyed condemned hospitals with more life in them than most houses. But this house was alive, and it was alive in one direction only: toward the quiet person who moved through it ahead of him like a current through water, opening doors, standing aside, answering in single sentences, and watching him with those eyes that missed nothing and gave nothing back.

On the afternoon of the second day, he found them in the music room.

He hadn't meant to go in. It wasn't on his schedule until Thursday. But the door stood ajar, and through the gap he could see the piano—a Bechstein, grand, its lid closed, its case the colour of strong tea—and above it, on the wall, photographs in frames furred with dust except for two bright rectangles where frames had recently hung and been taken down.

Xanthe was standing at the window with their back to him, and they were not doing anything. That was the strange thing. They were simply standing, looking out at the overgrown garden, and the quality of their stillness was so complete that Dev felt he had interrupted not a moment but a state of being—as if the person were a note being held, and his entrance the release of the pedal.

"Sorry," he said. "Door was open."

"It usually is," Xanthe said, without turning. "Rooms you stop using, you stop closing."

Dev stepped inside. The room held its breath. He had walked through a hundred grand rooms in a hundred dying houses and none of them had ever felt like this—like a lung that remembered breathing.

"That piano's tuned," he said, because it was the only thing he knew to say, and even as he said it he knew it was wrong, or wrong-shaped.

"No," said Xanthe. "It isn't. It hasn't been tuned in thirty-one years."

"But you're a piano tuner. It says so in the file. Marchetti, resident caretaker, engaged for tuning and maintenance, 1994–"

"My mother tuned it last." Now they turned, and the light from the window caught their face, and Dev saw that the practiced smile was gone, and what was underneath it was not hostility but something older and more tired: grief that had been lived in so long it had become architecture. "She tuned it the week before she left. Then she didn't come back, and I decided the house could keep her pitch."

Dev looked at the piano. Closed lid. Dust on the case—but not on the keys' fallboard hinge, he noticed. Not on the hinge. Someone touched that hinge often enough to keep it clean. Someone opened it, maybe. Looked. Closed it again.

"What was she like?" he asked, and immediately regretted it, because it was not a surveyor's question, and he watched Xanthe register that fact—the slight shift of weight, the recalculation, the invisible drawbridge beginning to rise.

"She was invisible," Xanthe said. "Like me. It's a family trade."

And then, before he could answer, they did something he would think about for months afterward. They crossed the room, lifted the fallboard's hinge with one finger—a gesture so habitual, so worn into the body, that it looked less like an action than a reflex—and let it fall again with a soft wooden click, a full stop at the end of a sentence.

"The cellars flood after rain," they said, voice level again, professional again, the door of the person closing as the door of the conversation opened. "If you're going down tomorrow, go before noon."

* * *

That night Dev sat in his van in the layby outside the estate wall with a thermos of coffee going lukewarm beside him, and wrote in his notebook—not measurements, for once, but questions.

*Who tallies days on a boiler room wall? Who keeps a dead woman's piano at her pitch for thirty years? Why does the file on this place have a hole in it?*

Because the file did have a hole. He'd noticed it the first evening: the ownership trail went strange in the late eighties, documents missing, a transfer recorded but never registered properly, the trust's papers referring to "the arrangement" with a vagueness that lawyers only used when vagueness was the point. And the staff records stopped abruptly in 1989. Rosa Marchetti, housekeeper. Last entry: wages paid to date of leaving. Nothing after. No forwarding address. No reason. In thirty years of paperwork, Dev had learned that the most important facts were always the ones the paper was built to avoid.

He looked up at the house. From the layby, past the broken gates and the rhododendrons grown vast and feral, Glasshouse showed its south face against the bruised evening sky, and one window on the upper floor was lit—a small yellow rectangle, steady, patient.

Someone was awake in there. Someone was keeping the light on for a house scheduled to die, the way you keep talking to a person in a coma, not because they can hear you but because stopping would be a kind of murder.

Dev wrote one more line in the notebook, underlined it, and closed the book.

*Ask her—or them—about 1989.*

* * *

Inside, in the small lit room that had once been the housekeeper's sitting room, Xanthe sat with the box of letters on their knees and did not open it.

They had not opened it in four days. They had carried it from room to room as the surveyor's footsteps moved overhead, through the floors, along the corridors—had set it down on tables and windowsills and finally on their own bed, where it sat now like a sleeping animal they were afraid to wake. Because the letters were addressed in their mother's hand, and their mother's hand was a country Xanthe had emigrated from at fourteen and never returned to, and the surveyor with his listening body and his wrong-shaped questions had come within a finger's width today of making them say things out loud that had never been said aloud, and the house—

The house was restless tonight. Xanthe could hear it. A pipe knocking in the east wing, irregular, arrhythmic, the way pipes knocked when a valve somewhere was failing—or when the wind found a gap it hadn't found before. They had tuned their ear to this house for thirty years and it had never lied to them, and right now it sounded like a throat clearing before speech.

Outside, far off down the drive, a van door closed. The surveyor, sleeping in his van like a faithful dog, refusing even to leave.

Xanthe looked down at the box. At the topmost envelope, at the familiar slanting hand that had written shopping lists and birthday cards and, once, a note left on the kitchen table that had ended a childhood: *Gone to see about work. Mind the house. Mind Aldous. Be invisible.*

*Be invisible.*

The surveyor had looked at them today—really looked, the way nobody looked at invisible people—and the world had not ended. The floor had held. Something in Xanthe's chest, some muscle clamped tight for three decades, had shifted by a fraction, painfully, like a joint remembering motion.

They picked up the top letter. Turned it over. The seal, broken long ago by someone else's hands, gaped like a small dark mouth.

From the east wing, the pipe knocked again—twice, pause, twice—and Xanthe, who knew every language the house spoke, heard it suddenly as what it might have been all along.

Not a warning.

A knock. From inside.
