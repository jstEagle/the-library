## 14. Intercepted

The invitation arrived on cream card, embossed at the crest, and Cass understood it immediately for what it was: not a courtesy but a summons. *The Vane Charitable Trust requests the pleasure of Miss Cassandra Grey's company at its autumn dinner, Ashcombe House, Saturday the fourteenth.* Her grandmother had gone pale when she saw it—pale in that particular way of a woman who has spent decades learning which envelopes are dangerous—and had said only, "You don't have to go."

Which was, of course, precisely why Cass went.

She dressed carefully, in the armour of respectability: a dark green dress her mother called severe, low heels she could stand in for hours, her hair pinned back so there would be nothing soft about her face to apologise for. In the taxi up the long drive to Ashcombe House she rehearsed nothing. She had learned already that Marcus Vane did not respond to rehearsal; he responded to improvisation, and he was better at it than anyone she had ever met.

Ashcombe was everything Glasshouse was not. Where Glasshouse sagged and wept and let its damp into every conversation, Ashcombe stood dry and certain, its Georgian façade washed by floodlights, its gravel raked into lines so perfect they looked drawn on. Inside, the rooms were warm in a way that felt administered rather than lived in. Waiters moved with the silence of people who have been trained out of existing. And everywhere, on every surface, photographs: the Vane family across two centuries, arranged in silver frames like evidence for a defence.

Cass found Marcus near the fireplace in the drawing room, holding a glass of something amber and barely drinking it. He was smaller than she'd expected—that was the first shock. Photographs had made him monumental; in person he was a neat, compact man of perhaps seventy-five, silver-haired, with his sister's same grey eyes set in a face that had decided, somewhere along the way, to be pleasant about everything. He wore his charm the way other men wore watches: visibly, and to tell the time by.

"Cassandra." He said her name as though tasting it. "Or do you prefer Cass? Your grandmother was always Eleanor. Never Ellie. She thought diminutives were a form of theft."

"I prefer Cass."

"Then Cass you shall be." He smiled. It was an excellent smile. "You've been busy. I hear you're writing a family history."

"Family histories," Cass said. "Plural. There seem to be several versions."

"There always are." He gestured her toward the window, away from the fire, away from the ears of the two trustees who were watching them with the studied blankness of men paid to see nothing. "That's what makes them interesting. Shall we?"

It was not a question. She walked with him to the window, where the floodlit lawn fell away into darkness, and understood that she had been manoeuvred exactly where he wanted her: visible to the room, isolated from it, framed.

"Young women who dig into old families," Marcus said pleasantly, "usually want one of two things. They want to belong, or they want to burn. Either is fine, you know. I've accommodated both. But the ones who cause real trouble—" he turned the glass slowly in his fingers, watching the light move through it, "—are the ones who can't decide which they want."

"And which do you think I am?"

"I think you're your grandmother's granddaughter." He said it kindly. That was the horror of it—the kindness. "Eleanor also couldn't decide. She wanted the truth and she wanted the family, and when the two came into conflict she chose the family, and then she spent fifty years being sorry about it in Whitby. I'm offering you a faster route to the same place."

"The truth isn't a route."

"No," he agreed. "It's a bill. And someone always has to pay it." He took a small sip at last. "Here's mine, since you've come all this way. Rosa Marchetti was a liar and a thief, and my brother's children will not grow up thinking otherwise. The trust exists to protect this family's name, and the name is the only thing I have ever been asked to protect. You may write whatever you like about the houses and the horses and the bad marriages. Leave the rest."

Cass felt her heartbeat in her teeth. "You're asking me to falsify history."

"I'm asking you to curate it. Everyone does. Your grandmother did. Aldous did—he was the best of us at it, actually, thirty years of curation without a single tremor. Even Rosa curated; she left before anyone could ask her anything." He set down his glass on the sill with a small precise click. "You imagine power is about hiding things. It isn't. It's about deciding what everyone agrees happened. And that, my dear, is simply inheritance. I inherited it, as my father did, as you—" the smile again, warmer now, more terrible, "—as you may yet."

*As you may yet.* She heard it land exactly as he intended: not a threat, an offer. A door held open. Join the agreement, and be one of the people to whom things are agreed.

"I found the letters," Cass said quietly.

Something moved behind his eyes—not fear, she thought, but recalculation. A man redoing arithmetic.

"Letters are paper," he said. "Paper burns. Or it sits in boxes, and boxes get mislaid, and no one reads them because reading them would serve no purpose. Purpose, Cass, is the whole art. Give any document a purpose and it becomes dangerous; take the purpose away and it's just sentiment." He straightened her shoulder strap where it had slipped—a gesture so intimate, so casually proprietary, that she stepped back before she could stop herself. He noted the step. Filed it. "Your access to the archives at Ashcombe has been reviewed, incidentally. There were concerns about confidentiality. I'm afraid Dr. Pryce will have to suspend your reader's ticket."

"My grandmother's papers are in those archives."

"Your grandmother's papers are trust property. As is her correspondence, her photograph albums, and, I believe, a piano." He said the last word lightly, but Cass caught it—the faintest hitch, a man touching a bruise through clothing. "Everything belongs to someone, in the end. Usually to me. Do enjoy the dinner—the lamb is meant to be very good. And Cass?" He was already turning away, already rejoining the warmth of the room, already becoming again the pleasant uncle of the photographs. "Whatever Xanthe Marchetti told you—they're persuasive, I know, they have their mother's way of making silence sound like honesty—remember that they want something too. Everyone who comes asking about Glasshouse wants something. Ask yourself what."

He left her at the window. The dinner was long and gilded and she tasted none of it. She watched him work the table like a conductor, drawing laughter from the solicitor, deference from the developers, a fond anecdote from old Mrs. Pryce—and understood, sitting in the candlelight with her hands folded, that she was looking at the mechanism itself. Not a villain in a story. An heir. Corruption passed down like the silver, polished each generation, used without thought.

* * *

They revoked her ticket by email on Monday morning, three sentences, cc'd to nobody. By Wednesday the archivist, a young woman named Priya who had been unfailingly kind to her for eight months, met her eyes across the reading room with genuine misery and mouthed, *I'm sorry—I had no choice.*

"I know," Cass said aloud, and watched two other readers pretend very hard to be reading.

But Marcus had made one error, and it was the error of a man who had never been invisible: he believed that controlling the archive controlled the past. He had spent his life among catalogued things—papers in acid-free folders, photographs in numbered sleeves, everything locatable, therefore everything seizable. He had never considered what lived outside the folders. The letters in Xanthe's possession. The memory of a dying butler in a care home outside York. A house itself, standing damp and defiant with six weeks left on its notice, keeping its own records in its pipes.

Cass sat that evening in her flat with her laptop open and the family history document glowing on the screen—all eight months of it, the careful chapters, the sourced paragraphs—and deleted nothing, changed nothing, but added a new heading at the end:

**Chapter Eleven: What Was Removed, and By Whom.**

Underneath, she typed a single line: *The following account is reconstructed from materials the Vane Charitable Trust declined to make available.*

Her phone buzzed. Xanthe.

*"Marcus called me,"* the message read. *"Offered money. I said no. Also — Dev found something in the demolition file. The order was pushed through fast. Trustee signature. Guess whose."*

Cass looked at the line for a long moment. Then she typed back: *"Come to Whitby. Both of you. Bring the last letter. We're going to need my grandmother to open her own door."*

She pressed send, and sat in the quiet of her flat, listening to the city hum below, and understood that she had just done the thing Marcus had spent seventy-five years ensuring no one in his orbit ever did: she had chosen a side, publicly, in writing, with witnesses.

Somewhere in Whitby, an old woman named Mrs. Grey was living behind a door that had stayed shut for fifty years. Somewhere in a care home, Aldous was running out of time to confess. And somewhere in a filing cabinet at Ashcombe, behind a lock Marcus believed was final, sat the original demolition order—with a date on it that predated, by eleven days, the discovery of the letters behind the wardrobe.

He had known the letters existed. He had known before Xanthe found them.

Cass closed the laptop, and began, in the dark, to plan how to prove it.
