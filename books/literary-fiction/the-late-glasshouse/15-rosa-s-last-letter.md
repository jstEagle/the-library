## 15. Rosa's Last Letter

The box had been sitting on Xanthe's kitchen table for three days before they could bring themselves to open it again.

It was not that they feared what was inside. They feared, rather, that there would be nothing inside — that the letters would simply stop, as Rosa Marchetti's life seemed to have stopped, mid-sentence, mid-thought, mid-song, and that whatever truth lived in the gap would remain a gap forever. Xanthe had spent thirty years listening to buildings tell their stories through copper and cast iron, and they knew better than anyone that some silences were structural. Load-bearing. You did not knock them out without bringing the whole wall down.

But on the fourth morning — a grey one, rain ticking against the kitchen window like a tuner testing each string in turn — Xanthe made tea they did not drink and lifted out the final letter.

It was different from the others. Thicker. The paper had gone soft at the folds, handled many times before it was ever folded, and the handwriting was less controlled than in the earlier letters, the loops of the capital R dragging like something pulled against its will. There was no date. The others had all been dated with a precision that now struck Xanthe as its own kind of grief — Rosa numbering her days like a prisoner scratching tallies. This one carried only a salutation, and even that was crossed out and rewritten.

*My dear Eleanor* — struck through.
*Eleanor* — struck through.
*Ellie.*

Xanthe read it standing up, because sitting down suddenly felt impossible.

---

*They are sending me away tomorrow. I am writing this tonight because tomorrow I will be too busy pretending to go willingly, and you know how well I pretend. You taught me that, or I taught you — I can no longer remember which of us learned invisibility from the other.*

*He came to my room on Tuesday night. You know who I mean; I will not write his name because names have power and I refuse to give him any more of mine. He stood in the doorway with his hat still on, which told me everything before he said a word. A man removes his hat to enter a church. He kept his on because he was not entering anywhere he intended to stay.*

*He offered me money. Then, when I refused it, he offered me silence, which is the same money in a different currency. He said the family name could not bear a scandal, and I asked him — God forgive me, I laughed when I asked him — whether the family name bore anything else, or whether it too was carried about by servants and set down in corners where the guests wouldn't see it.*

*He did not laugh. That is the thing I want you to understand, Ellie, more than anything else in this letter: he did not laugh, and he did not raise his voice, and he never once took off his hat. He simply explained, in the tone of a man discussing drainage, that I would leave Glasshouse on Friday morning, that my position would be described as dismissed for theft, that references would be provided to no one, and that if I stayed — if I made trouble — there were institutions for women in my condition, and he knew the men who ran them, and they owed him favours.*

*My condition. As though I had caught it from a bad drain. As though I had not been alone in that attic room every night for four months, sick into the basin, terrified, waiting for you to come back from London because you were the only person in that house whose word counted for anything.*

*I know you wrote to me. Please do not think I doubt it. Aldous gave me your letters — three of them, over those months — and I could feel in the way he handed them over that there had been others, that pages were being weighed in some invisible balance upstairs and only the lighter ones allowed through. Your brother reads our correspondence, Eleanor. Perhaps he has always read it. Perhaps that is why he waited until you were in London to come to my door with his hat on and his voice level and his offer that was not an offer.*

*You will want to fight this. I can see you doing it already — marching into his study, laying out the argument like cards. But listen to me. Listen, because I love you and because I am the only person left in that house who will say it plainly: you cannot win. Not because you are wrong but because being right is a luxury in that house, and we were never the ones who could afford it. If you fight him, he will not destroy you — he is not cruel in that way, cruelty requires attention and he has none to spare — but he will simply wait, the way stone waits, until you are married off or sent abroad or quietly folded into some other wing of the family where your opinions cannot reach the light. And I will still be gone. And the child will still be born somewhere with no name worth having.*

*So I am going. I have arranged a room in Leeds with a woman Mrs. Fairweather knows. I will take in sewing. I will tell people my husband died, which is true in every way that matters, since the man who did this to me was dead to me before the child was even a possibility.*

*Do not look for me. This is the hardest sentence I have ever written and I have written it four times now and torn up three. Do not look for me, because if you find me he will find me, and I cannot — Ellie, I cannot spend this child's whole childhood glancing at gates. Let me be nobody. Nobody is safe. I spent six years at Glasshouse learning to be seen exactly as much as was convenient, and I am very good at it now, and I intend to be magnificent at it for the rest of my life.*

*But I will write to you. I will write to this house, because houses keep secrets better than people do, and because some part of me cannot believe that you and I are finished — that everything we were to each other can be ended by a man in a hat. Write to me care of the Leeds address. Burn nothing. Keep everything. One day, when the child is grown, when it is safe, I will come back and we will sit in the glass room and drink that terrible sherry and laugh at how frightened we were.*

*Tell the piano I'm sorry. Tell it I always meant to finish learning the second movement.*

*Your friend — not servant, never servant, whatever the wages book says —*

*Rosa*

*P.S. It isn't theft, what they'll say I did. What I took was nothing. What they took — well. You can weigh that yourself, in your scales, in your glass room, and I hope the weight keeps you awake at least one night, my darling, my dear, my Ellie.*

---

Xanthe read it twice. Then a third time, standing in the same spot while the tea went cold and the rain kept its patient rhythm against the glass, and by the third reading they were no longer reading so much as falling through the words, the way you fall through ice into water that is colder than breath.

Marcus Vane. The man at the edge of every photograph. Eleanor's brother, presumed dead abroad, presumed anything at all because no one had bothered to look closely — because looking closely was a servant's habit and Xanthe had it in abundance and had never once thought to turn it on their own origin.

Their father.

The word arrived in Xanthe's mind and sat there like an object left in a hallway by someone who did not live in the house. *Father.* Xanthe picked it up, turned it over, found no maker's mark. All their life they had been the child of Rosa Marchetti, and then, after Leeds, after the coughing and the hospital and the flat above the shop, the child of no one, raised by a butler's stiff kindness in a house that paid for them the way it paid for coal — regularly, without comment, as a cost of keeping warm. Xanthe had built an entire self on that foundation: unclaimed, unclaimed, unclaimed. It had hurt, but it had been a clean hurt, an honest architecture. *She left. She died. No one wanted me enough to say so.*

And now the foundation shifted, and beneath it was not bedrock but a man's shame, poured like concrete by someone who had never removed his hat.

Xanthe sat down finally, because their legs had decided the matter without consulting them.

The strange thing — the thing they would remember afterward, in the years when this morning had softened into story — was that the devastation did not feel like devastation. It felt like tuning. Like finding the note that had been wrong in a room for thirty years, the faint sourness everyone had stopped hearing because it had always been there, and striking it once, hard, and hearing it resolve. Their whole identity engineered by another man's embarrassment. Their invisibility not a flaw in their character but a design specification, drawn up in a study by a man discussing drainage. Even Rosa's disappearance — the wound under all wounds — had been administered not by fate but by administration. A Friday morning. A description invented for the wages book. References provided to no one.

Xanthe laughed. It came out wrong, cracked in the middle, half sob, and they let it, because there was no one to hear and because the smile — the practiced smile, the smile that ended conversations — had no use here in this kitchen with no one in it. For thirty years the smile had been Rosa's inheritance, the one thing she had left them besides the tuning fork and the ear. *Be seen exactly as much as is convenient.* And Xanthe had been magnificent at it. Magnificent, invisible, employed, safe.

Nobody is safe, Rosa had written. Nobody is safe. She had meant it as armour, and she had forged it well, and she had worn it until her lungs gave out, and she had passed it to her child like a coat that would always be slightly too large.

But armour, Xanthe thought, setting the letter down with hands that were almost steady, is made by people who expect to keep losing.

The rain eased. Somewhere in the walls, a pipe ticked as the heating cycled, and Xanthe listened to it the way they listened to everything in Glasshouse — automatically, intimately, the way you listen to a sleeping animal. The house had known. That was the thought that rose last, absurd and certain. The house had held these letters behind its own plaster for fifty years, had kept Rosa's voice in its cavity the way it kept heat in its pipes, and all those decades Xanthe had walked its corridors reading its history and never once suspected that the deepest history was theirs, filed in the walls like wiring.

*One day, when the child is grown, when it is safe, I will come back.*

The child was grown. It was not safe — Marcus Vane was alive, and powerful, and had just signed the order to pull this house down over the evidence it contained — but the child was grown, and the child had an ear, and a satchel, and a box of letters, and for the first time in thirty years a reason to stop smiling.

Xanthe washed the cold tea down the sink, put on their coat, and took the last letter with them.

There was a trustee dinner on Thursday. Cass had told them the time, the place, the dress code, with the fierce practicality of someone raised to assume doors would open for her. Xanthe had said no. Xanthe had said no twice, smiling both times.

They picked up the telephone, and this time they did not smile at all.
