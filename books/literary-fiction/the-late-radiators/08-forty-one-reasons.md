## 8. Forty-One Reasons

Hazel laid the letters out on her mother's kitchen table in the order of their postmarks—or rather, in the order they would have been postmarked, had any of them ever been posted. Forty-one envelopes, each sealed, each addressed in her mother's small, upright hand to *Tomas Lindqvist*, each stamped and never cancelled. The stamps themselves formed a kind of archaeology: the old monarch's profile from the earliest ones, the newer design from the later. Thirty years of postage purchased and never used. Her mother had paid for the sending over and over again, as if the paying were the point.

She made coffee she didn't drink and began at the beginning.

The first letter was dated March 14th—the year before Hazel was born—and it took her nearly an hour to read, because the language came back to her the way water comes back to a dried creek bed: not all at once but in pools, finding its old channels, pooling in the low places of her memory before spilling onward. She read aloud under her breath, testing each sentence against her mouth like a key against a lock that might have been changed.

*Dear Tomas,* the letter began. *I have written this eleven times. I keep starting with an apology and then understanding that an apology is a door closing, and I want instead to leave everything open...*

Hazel stopped there. She looked out the window at the neighbor's fence, at the particular gray of the afternoon, and understood that she was reading a love letter. Not a confession, not yet—an opening. Her mother, at twenty-six, writing to a man who was not her husband, with a tenderness so unguarded it embarrassed Hazel to witness it, the way one is embarrassed to see parents dancing.

*I liked you best on Tuesday,* her mother wrote, *when you argued with me about the translation. You were wrong, by the way. The word means what I said it means. But I liked being argued with by someone who took the words seriously enough to be wrong about them.*

The word. Hazel went back to it three times, sounding it out, feeling for it in her mouth the way you probe a sore tooth. It was there—she could almost hold it—but not quite. A word about regret, or something adjacent to regret, some emotion English kept at arm's length because it was too specific, too shameful, too precise. Her mother had used it twice in the first letter alone.

* * *

By the fourth letter, Hazel had stopped translating in her head and started simply reading, the language rising in her like a tide that had only been waiting for permission. This was the cruelest gift of the day: after years of watching words slip away one by one—she'd lost the word for *eavesdrop* two winters ago, the word for a certain kind of pale winter light just last spring—they were returning now, all of them, summoned by necessity. The language had not left her. It had been waiting for a reason.

The letters told a story in installments, and the story was this:

Her mother, Astrid, had worked as a translator in those years—young, newly married, taking freelance work through the cultural institute downtown. Tomas Lindqvist had been a client: a furniture maker whose workshop occupied the ground floor of a building near the harbor, a man who wrote long, careful letters to suppliers and craftsmen abroad and needed them rendered into languages he didn't speak. He was forty when they met. He was married once, briefly, long ago; there were no children. He read poetry. He argued about words.

The affair, if that is even the right word for what the letters described—something slower than an affair, more like a long conversation that the body eventually joined—lasted fourteen months. Hazel counted. Fourteen months of letters written across the span of thirty years, which meant her mother had spent more than twice as long not sending them as she had spent loving him.

In the ninth letter, dated late autumn of the following year, the tone changed. Hazel read it standing up, without meaning to, as if her body needed to be ready for something.

*Tomas. They have taken you. I sat in the courtroom today and I said the words they put in front of me and you looked at me and I could not look back. I am going to say this plainly because plainness is all I have left: I lied. You know I lied. Everyone in that room knew I lied except the people whose knowing mattered.*

Hazel set the letter down. Her hands were cold. Outside, a car passed, ordinary, oblivious.

She picked it up again.

*Your lawyer asked me if I saw you near the safe that night and I said yes. I did not say yes because I believed it. I said yes because Henrik was sitting in the second row with his hands folded like a man at prayer, and when I hesitated he leaned forward, and I have spent my whole life learning to read the silences of men, and his silence said: say yes, or everything we have, everything you are, ends here.*

Henrik. Hazel's father. Henrik Marchetti, who died when Hazel was nineteen, who had been to her a mildness itself—a man who fixed radios and grew tomatoes and never raised his voice. She tried to fold this new knowledge around the shape of him and it would not fit, and then, horribly, it would. She thought of how quiet he'd become in certain company. How her mother had gone silent in his presence too, sometimes—not the silence of contentment but the silence of someone conserving herself. How, after he died, her mother had seemed to expand slowly into the space he left, like a plant toward light, and how Hazel had misread that expansion as grief.

*They gave him seven years,* her mother wrote. *Seven years for a theft I know you did not commit, because you were with me. That is the sentence I carry now: not seven years but the number of hours you were with me while they decided otherwise. I will count them. I will count them every day, and when the counting becomes unbearable I will write to you, and I will not send the letters, because sending them would destroy what remains of your life, and I have already destroyed enough of it.*

* * *

The middle letters were the hardest, and Hazel read them the way you eat when grieving—mechanically, without tasting, unable to stop.

There was a letter in which her mother described visiting Tomas in prison, once, early on, sitting across a table from him while a guard watched, both of them speaking in the flattened public language required by surveillance, saying nothing true, everything coded. *You asked about my health. I said I was well. You looked at my hands and knew I was lying about that too, because my hands have always told the truth my mouth won't.*

There was a letter from four years in, after his release, describing how she had seen him once from a bus window, standing outside his old shop—which was no longer his shop, which had been sold during the trial to pay the judgment, sold to a man named Voss whom her mother referred to only as *the buyer*, with a bitterness that survived even translation. *You looked older. You looked like a man carrying a building on his back. I got off at the next stop and walked back and stood across the street for twenty minutes and did not cross it. I have thought about that street crossing every day since. It is the smallest distance I ever failed to travel.*

And threaded through all of it, recurring like a refrain, was the thing Hazel had been circling since the wardrobe moved: her.

*I watch Hazel sleep and I think: she has your hands.* (Letter twenty-two.) *She argues about words the way you did. Yesterday she corrected my pronunciation and I laughed until I cried and she didn't understand why, and I couldn't tell her, and the not-telling has become a room I live in.* (Letter twenty-nine.) *Henrik adores her, I want to say that fairly, I need to say it fairly—he is not a monster, he is a frightened man who did a monstrous thing and then built a life on top of it the way you build on landfill, and the ground shifts sometimes and he lies awake listening to it shift, and I lie awake beside him listening to him listen.* (Letter thirty-one.)

That last one undid her. Hazel sat at her mother's table with forty-one unsent letters fanned out before her like a losing hand and wept—for her mother, for Tomas Lindqvist, for the father she had loved who turned out to be a frightened man built on landfill, and finally, most strangely, for herself: a child assembled from a lie, carried through a marriage as living evidence, loved, apparently, fiercely, by everyone involved, and deceived by all of them in the same breath.

* * *

It was dark when she reached the last few letters. She turned on the kitchen light and noticed, with a small shock, that she'd been reading in the dimming natural light for over an hour, her eyes adjusting without her permission, the way the body accommodates what the mind refuses to see.

Letter thirty-eight was short. *I saw Ilse's daughter today. Your son's girl. She has your way of standing with her weight on one hip. I wanted to tell her who I was. I bought bread from her stall and thanked her in our language and she smiled and I walked away shaking.*

So there was a son. There was a granddaughter already grown, working a market stall. Hazel did the arithmetic with numb efficiency: if Tomas had died two years ago—if the address across town still answered to his name—then somewhere in this city there were people who had lost a grandfather to a wrongful conviction, people who had inherited the ruin her mother had helped make.

Letters forty and forty-one were both dated within the last five years, and both were different in kind. Shorter. The handwriting larger, less controlled—the hand of a woman whose body was beginning to fail her, though Hazel hadn't known that yet, hadn't been told, had been managing her mother's decline from a distance of six hundred miles and a lifetime of practiced distance.

Letter forty read, in full:

*Tomas. I am dying. I don't write that for sympathy; I write it because dying clarifies, the way frost clarifies a window. Here is what the clarity shows me: I chose wrong. Not once—every day, for fifty years, I chose the silence, and I called the silence protection, and it was cowardice wearing protection's coat. Tomorrow I am going to mail this letter. Tomorrow I am going to drive to your address and put it in the box myself. Tomorrow.*

Letter forty-one was dated nine days later. It contained a single line:

*I drove past your house. I sat outside it for one hour. I am sorry. I am sorry. I am sorry.*

Hazel held that last page for a long time. The paper had warped slightly, as though it had once been damp—as though it had been held by wet hands, or wept over, or carried out to a car and back. Her mother had gotten as far as the street. Her mother had sat outside the truth for an hour with the engine running, and had driven home, and had sealed this letter, and had put it behind the wardrobe with the other forty, and had died with the whole unsent weight of it ten feet from where she slept.

And now the house was sold. Marcus had signed. In ten days strangers would own these walls, and the box would have been hauled to a landfill with the rest of the clearance if Hazel hadn't needed to patch a water stain.

She gathered the letters back into their order, squared their edges, and sat looking at the top envelope—that first one, *I have written this eleven times*—and felt the question arrive that had been forming all evening beneath the grief, patient as sediment:

If her mother had been the prosecution's key witness, then there was a record. Courtrooms keep records. Somewhere in the county archive there was a transcript with Astrid Marchetti's name in it, her mother's voice preserved in the exact shape of the lie.

Hazel stood, found her coat, and reached for the keys.

Tomorrow she would go and read what her mother had said out loud, under oath, in a room where silence was not permitted.
