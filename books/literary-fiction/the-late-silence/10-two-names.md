## 10. Two Names

The morning after I broke the east wing open, I did what any reasonable person does with a house full of ghosts: I made coffee in a kitchen that hadn't been used since the Clinton administration, and I sat at a table scarred with my brother's initials and tried to remember why I'd stopped being Lena.

It's not an easy question. That's the thing nobody tells you about names—you don't choose them so much as you survive them. Felix came to me the way a coat comes to you: off a rack, secondhand, slightly wrong in the shoulders but warm enough. I was nineteen. I needed a coat.

But Lena—Lena had been made for me by someone else's mouth.

* * *

Here is what I remember of the summer before everything, which is to say here is what I have spent fifteen years not remembering:

I was eleven. Daniel was sixteen, and he had discovered that the east wing, sealed off for reasons our father never gave and we never asked, had acoustics like a held breath. The long gallery where the light came through the tall windows in slanted bars. The little music room at its end with the good parquet, still unwarped then, still singing underfoot.

"Say something," he told me, standing me in the center of the gallery.

"Lena," I said, because that was my name, and the room took it and gave it back twice, three times, softening it each round until it sounded less like a word than a weather condition.

"There." He was grinning. He had that grin that made adults forgive him things they'd have punished me for, and made me forgive him things no one should have to forgive. "That's your name when it's happy. You can hear it."

He called me Lena the way other people hummed—absently, constantly, without deciding to. *Lena, catch.* *Lena, look at this.* *Lena, don't tell Dad.* It wasn't a name so much as a frequency, and for five years I lived inside it, a girl who existed most fully in the echo of her own name bouncing down a dead man's hallway.

Then the boathouse roof. Then the police asking questions in our kitchen with their hats in their hands. Then my father going quiet in a way that wasn't silence but architecture—a silence built to last, load-bearing—and my mother leaving within the year, and me at nineteen standing in a county clerk's office with a form and a pen, choosing a name out of thin air because I could not stand to be called by the sound of my own happiness anymore.

Felix. I picked it from a list of names I found in a library book about Roman history, which should tell you how much thought went into it. It means lucky. It means fortunate. I chose it the way you choose a password after someone's broken into your accounts: deliberately ugly, deliberately nothing, a door only I would think to knock on.

For fifteen years it worked. Felix tuned pianos. Felix left towns before anyone learned her surname. Felix was a woman with excellent hearing and no history, and if sometimes a client said a name in a certain warm register—*Felix, love, come see this*—and something in my chest lurched sideways like a floorboard giving way, well. That was the price of the coat. Coats chafe.

* * *

Present day. Halloway House, nine in the morning, coffee that tasted like the inside of a thermos forgotten in a car. I sat in the kitchen with my father's study key on a chain around my neck—I'd started wearing it there, I noticed, the way you wear a locket—and I thought about names.

Because here's what I'd found yesterday, in the sealed wing, among the correspondence and the lawyer's drafts and the deed with its torn-away final page: a letter, unsent, in my father's hand, addressed to no one, dated four years ago. Two lines.

*I never called her Lena again. I know she thinks I didn't notice. A man notices what he refuses to say.*

I read it six times. My father, who had spoken to me perhaps forty words a year for the last decade of his life, who had watched me introduce myself as Felix at my mother's funeral and said nothing, who had let the new name pass between us at every Christmas card and every strained phone call like a toll neither of us would pay—he had noticed. He had carried it. And he had written it down and put it in a drawer instead of sending it, because that was his whole method, his entire genius: my father did not withhold truth. He filed it.

I folded the letter into my jacket pocket, where it pressed against my ribs all day like a second heartbeat.

* * *

The strange thing about amputation—and I use the word precisely, because that afternoon in the county clerk's office felt surgical, felt like the clean severing of something infected—is that the body keeps reporting pain from the missing part. Doctors call it phantom limb. I called it professionalism.

Every piano I tuned for fifteen years, I tuned with half my attention, because the other half was always listening for the east wing. Every time a child ran through a client's hallway calling a sibling's name, my hands kept working while something behind my sternum stood very still, listening. I told myself I had escaped Halloway House. What I had actually done was leave the house standing and carry the acoustics with me, everywhere, permanently, like tinnitus.

And now the house had me back, and the sealed wing was open, and the letter was against my ribs, and I understood—with the flat, unhurried clarity that arrives only when grief has finished its paperwork—that I had not cut away the past at all. I had cut away myself. Lena hadn't been Daniel's invention. She'd been mine. His name for me was just the first place I heard it ring true.

You can't tune an instrument by removing strings. I knew that better than anyone alive. Pull the string, and you don't fix the note; you lose the note forever, and the instrument lists out of true, and every other string has to bend to compensate. Fifteen years of Felix, and every relationship I'd ever had bent slightly around the gap where Lena used to be. My clients got a woman who was wonderful at listening and impossible at staying. My father got a daughter who signed cards with a stranger's name. I got a coat that fit like a wound.

* * *

In the afternoon I went back up to the east wing—not to the study, not yet; the study could wait, the study was a minefield—but to the gallery, to the music room, to the places where a girl named Lena had once been audible.

The parquet had warped after all, in the decades since. Under my feet it creaked in a minor interval, two notes a semitone apart, complaining. The tall windows were gray with grime, but the light still came through in those slanted bars, dust turning slowly in them like punctuation. I stood in the center of the gallery, where I'd stood at eleven, and I said it out loud, feeling ridiculous, hearing ridiculous.

"Lena."

The room took it. Gave it back twice, three times, softer each round. The same as ever. Rooms don't grieve; that's their cruelty and their mercy. The room still knew how to say my name happily. It had been saying it to no one for thirty-five years, patiently, the way you keep a light on.

I stood there until the echo died and then some, and I will not pretend I didn't cry, because I did, but it was a specific kind of crying—the kind that isn't sadness exactly, more like the moment when a bone that's been set wrong finally gets re-broken. Hurts like hell. Necessary. The body's grim arithmetic: short-term agony traded for the possibility of straightness.

When it was over I wiped my face on my sleeve, blew my nose with tremendous dignity, and said, to the room, "All right. Both of us, then."

Because that was the decision, though I didn't yet know what it would cost or what it would demand. Not Felix instead of Lena. Not Lena instead of Felix. Both. The tuner and the girl in the echo. The coat and the body underneath it. I had spent half my life being one name and the other half being the other, and the discovery of the morning—the letter, the two lines, *a man notices what he refuses to say*—was that everyone else had known it all along. Anton at the funeral, saying *Lena* like a key turning. Mira Kask, whoever she was, whose existence proved my brother had a whole self I'd never met. Even my father, filing his refusal in a drawer. I was the only person who'd been fooled, and I'd done the fooling.

* * *

That evening I sat in the hall with the great piano—closed, forbidden, its lid locked these fifteen years—and I laid out what I knew on top of it like tools on a workbench.

A lodger's son named Anton Reval, who knew my brother and knew my name and appeared at my father's funeral like a debt coming due. A photograph promised. A girl named Mira whom Daniel had loved, apparently seriously enough that our fathers—both fathers, plural, the letter's phrasing suggested—had opposed it furiously. A deed transferring boathouse land to someone unnamed. A statement drafted by Daniel days before he died, according to Anton, hidden somewhere in this house, possibly in this piano, which my father forbade anyone to play, which my father kept locked, which my father—

Which my father had kept, I realized, the way he kept everything. Filed. Not destroyed. My father had never in his life destroyed a document; destruction required a decision, and decisions were what he paid other people to have. Whatever was in that piano, he'd known about it, and he'd locked it behind a lid rather than face it, and he'd paid three strangers for decades to keep the rest of the world from opening it either.

The lawyer. Tomorrow I would call the lawyer—Hargreaves, ancient, careful, the family solicitor since before I was born, a man who had drafted the deed and presumably removed its final page. A dying man, Anton had heard. Dying men are interesting. Professional obligation has a way of loosening its grip right around the time the grip is all that's left.

I put my hand flat on the piano's closed lid. Cold, polished, patient. Somewhere inside it, maybe, my brother's handwriting. Maybe his voice.

"Not yet," I told it, and meant: soon.

Upstairs, in the guest room that had been my room, I slept without dreaming for the first time since the funeral, and if that sounds like peace, it wasn't. It was exhaustion. But it was also, I think, the first night in thirty-five years that the house and I agreed on my name.

In the morning I would call Hargreaves, and I would ask him whose promise my father was protecting, and I suspected—I already suspected—that whatever he said would make the sealed wing look like a nursery.
