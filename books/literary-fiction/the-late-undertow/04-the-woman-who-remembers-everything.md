## 4. The Woman Who Remembers Everything

The address on the letter was a street I'd driven past a hundred times without seeing—Ozumba Mbadiwe's quieter cousin, a row of terraced shops wedged between a phone-repair kiosk and a church that advertised Tuesday deliverance. The bakery at ground level had been shuttered for years, by the look of it; the paint on the plywood over the windows had gone the grey of old chewing gum, and someone had stenciled BREAD in letters that had given up halfway through the D.

The stairs were outside, iron, and announced my weight to the whole neighborhood with each step. I counted fourteen. At the top there was a green door with a brass knocker shaped like a fish, and before I could decide whether to use it or knock with my knuckles like a normal person, a voice said, "It's open. You've been standing there for two minutes deciding about the fish."

I pushed the door open.

The flat was small and ferociously organized, which is a combination I've since learned to recognize as its own kind of argument: *I have less than you think and more than you'll ever know.* Books everywhere, but not scattered—ranked. Spines aligned like soldiers who'd been told exactly why they were standing there. A wall of cassette tapes in shoeboxes labeled in a script I couldn't read, loops and hooks and dots that looked like handwriting from a country that no longer existed on any map I owned. A kettle. Two chairs. A table with a cloth so clean it seemed to be daring me to put something on it.

Adaeze was smaller than her voice. That was the first thing. On paper—well, in a letter—she had been enormous, filling margins, underlining twice. In person she was a compact woman in a wrapper the color of dried hibiscus, seated in one of the two chairs as though the other existed purely for decoration, or for people she hadn't yet decided to permit.

"You're taller than your mother," she said. "Sit down before you block my light."

I sat. The second chair was wooden, upright, positioned at an angle that made slouching geometrically impossible. I had the sudden, absurd feeling of being back in primary school, except the teacher was eighty-something and had already read my file.

"You got my letter," she said. It wasn't a question.

"I did."

"And you took four months."

"Three," I said, and then heard myself, and stopped.

Her eyebrows went up a fraction. It was the most devastating fraction I have ever witnessed. "You counted. Good. A man who counts his evasions is at least honest about the arithmetic." She poured water from the kettle into two glasses—not tea, just water, hot, which struck me as either an oversight or a test—and pushed one across the table. "Drink. It's for your throat. You talk for a living, yes? I read your column."

Something in my chest performed a small, involuntary maneuver. "Then you know—"

"I know you wrote four hundred words about a soup last Thursday and used the word *whisper* twice." She sipped her own water, watching me over the rim. "*The broth whispers of smoke.* Broth does not whisper. Broth is a liquid. Writers whisper. Liars whisper." She set the glass down with a click. "But it was very beautiful lying. I will give you that. My neighbor's son reads you aloud at their table like scripture. They think you are a great man."

"And you?"

"I think you are a man who has never once described a taste he has tasted." She said it the way you'd note that a clock was running slow. No cruelty in it. Just observation, the way weather is observed. "Which is interesting, because I knew your mother, and she also had a way with words that arrived slightly ahead of the truth."

The name landed in the room before I could decide how to receive it. *Bisi.* I hadn't heard anyone say it out loud in years except Funmi, and Funmi said it like a bill that kept arriving.

"You knew my mother."

"I knew your mother when she was seventeen and thought she was going to be a radio presenter." Adaeze smiled, and for a moment the sharpness in her face rearranged itself into something almost girlish, some earlier draft of the woman. "She had a laugh that made other people laugh whether they wanted to or not. Very dangerous quality. Governments fear such laughs." The smile folded away again. "Sit properly. You're twisting like a man being interviewed."

I straightened. God help me, I straightened.

"Why are you here?" she asked.

"You invited me."

"I invite many people. Most have the sense not to come." She leaned forward, and her eyes were quick and brown and entirely unfooled. "I asked you to be my student. That is a serious thing. It is not a hobby, not a favor, not something you do between reviews. So before I teach you one word, you will tell me why. Why you. Why now. Why a man who cannot taste food wants to learn a language whose entire soul is flavor."

I opened my mouth.

Here is what I had prepared, because I am a professional and professionals prepare: something graceful about heritage, about roots, about the responsibility of the diaspora to its dead tongues. I had been polishing it in the car on the drive over, actually, turning phrases the way I turn phrases, and they were good phrases, they would have worked on almost anyone—

"That's not an answer," Adaeze said.

I hadn't said anything yet. I want that on the record. I had said nothing, and she waved her hand through whatever was forming behind my teeth like a woman dispersing smoke.

"I can see the speech from here. It's very well dressed. Take it off." She sat back. "Why do you want to learn?"

And the terrible thing—the genuinely terrible thing—was that I didn't know. Not in the way people say *I don't know* as a prelude to finding out. I mean the words simply weren't there. I had spent twenty years building a vocabulary for things I couldn't feel, and now, faced with a question about the one thing I might actually feel, I had nothing. I was a man with ten thousand words and no language.

"I don't—" I started.

"Take your time." She picked up her knitting, of all things, from a basket beside her chair—something blue, half-finished—and began working the needles with a soft rhythmic clack, as though my silence were a radio program she was content to listen to. "I am old. I have nowhere to be. But understand: I will wait all afternoon, and you will still have to say it."

* * *

What follows is not what I said, because I said very little. What follows is what happened inside me while an old woman knitted and the light moved across her wall of tapes and the shuttered bakery below held its breath.

I thought about the letter. *To the son of Bisi.* Not *Mr. Oyelaran.* Not *the critic.* The son of Bisi—as if my byline were the alias and this other thing were the name. Nobody had addressed me that way since I was nine years old and answering to it across a kitchen in Surulere.

I thought about how she'd known. How a stranger above a dead bakery had my address, my profession, my habits, my fraudulence, catalogued with the precision of a woman who had been paying attention for a very long time. People don't watch you that closely unless someone taught them to. Someone had told her things. Someone had talked about me, over years, in detail, to a woman who remembered everything.

My mother had talked about me.

That shouldn't have undone me. Mothers talk about their sons; it's practically the definition. But my mother had spent thirty years not talking—about the language, about my father, about Lagos, about everything that mattered—and the idea that beneath all that silence there had been a channel running, a private line from our kitchen to this flat, carrying reports on me like dispatches from a war I didn't know I was in—

"She asked you to write to me," I said finally. "Didn't she. Before she died."

The knitting needles paused. Resumed.

"Bisi is dead eleven years," Adaeze said. "And I keep my promises longer than most people keep their marriages. Answer the question."

"What question? You haven't let me—"

"*Why.*"

And because I couldn't give her the true answer, because I didn't have it, what came out instead was the closest thing to it my mouth could find:

"Because I'm tired."

The needles stopped for real this time.

"I'm tired of describing things I can't feel," I said, and heard my own voice do something it hadn't done in public or private in twenty years, which was crack. "I write about food every week. Every week. And I haven't tasted anything since I was nine—a fever, the doctors said, the nerves, it happens, you learn to live around it—and I learned, I learned so well that nobody knows, my editor doesn't know, my readers don't know, I don't even know anymore where the performance ends. I eat plain rice alone in my kitchen like a man doing penance for crimes he can't name, and then I go out and tell forty thousand people that a broth *whispers*, and they believe me, and I hate them a little for believing me, and I hate myself more for making them believe, and I don't know why your letter made me count the months, I don't know why I kept it instead of throwing it away, I don't know why I'm sitting in your chair drinking hot water on a Tuesday afternoon—"

I stopped. The flat was very quiet. Somewhere below, a generator coughed and settled.

"But I think," I said, "it's because you're the first person who looked at me and saw the whole thing at once. And I've spent my whole life being seen in pieces."

* * *

Adaeze set down the knitting. She looked at me for a long moment—not with pity, which I would have survived, but with recognition, which I nearly didn't.

"My husband used to say," she began, "that the worst hunger is not for food. It is for someone to ask you the right question and then stay in the room for the answer." She refilled my glass without asking. "You have been hungry a long time, Jonah Oyelaran. It has made you thin in places that don't show."

"I haven't answered your question," I managed. "About why I want to learn. I don't have an answer. I came here thinking I did and it turned out to be a speech."

"Good."

"Good?"

"A speech I could have gotten from a journalist. From a cultural officer. From a young man writing his thesis." She stood, slowly, one hand on the arm of her chair, and crossed to the wall of shoeboxes. Her fingers walked along the spines of the cassettes without reading them—she knew them by touch, by position, the way a pianist knows keys in the dark. "I have refused three students in five years. One cried. One argued. One wrote an article about me that called me 'a living treasure,' which is a phrase people use about things they intend to put in a museum and visit twice." She pulled a box down and set it on the table between us. "You I refuse differently."

"I don't understand."

"You don't have an answer yet. So we will begin anyway, and the answer will come, and when it comes you will tell me, and I will know if it is true because by then you will have learned enough of the language to be unable to lie in it. Ọ̀rọ̀ Ilẹ̀ is not a tongue for liars. There are too many ways to be caught." She tapped the box. "Twelve words for kinds of listening. Did I mention that in the letter?"

"You mentioned the language has no word for critic."

"It doesn't. Why would it? A critic decides. A listener receives." She opened the box. Inside, cassettes lay in rows, each labeled in that looping script, each dated in English numerals going back further than I'd been alive. "We start next week. Tuesday. Come hungry—not for food. For words. And Jonah—"

"Yes?"

"Do not bring me another speech. Bring me the rice."

I stared at her. "The what?"

"The plain rice you eat alone in your kitchen, doing penance." She was already turning back to her chair, dismissing me with the total ease of a woman who had buried a husband, a language, and several governments' worth of patience. "Bring it. We will eat together, and you will describe to me honestly what you experience, which is nothing, and we will begin from nothing, because nothing"—she sat, and picked up her needles, and did not look at me—"is where I began too."

I let myself out. The iron stairs rang under my feet, fourteen steps, and at the bottom I stood in the street looking back up at the green door with the fish knocker, feeling like a man who had gone to see about renting a room and been handed the deed to a house he didn't know he owned.

That night I ate plain rice alone in my kitchen, as always. But halfway through the bowl I stopped, fork in the air, because I understood that I was no longer eating it as penance.

I was eating it as preparation.

And on the table, propped against the salt I never used, the letter caught the overhead light—and for the first time I noticed, faintly, in the corner beneath her signature, a second line of writing in that looping script I couldn't read. Small. Deliberate. Addressed, somehow, unmistakably, to me.

I didn't know yet what it said.

I only knew that in a language with twelve words for listening, I had just begun to hear.
