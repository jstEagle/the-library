## 17. The Grammar of Forgiveness

The lesson that changed everything began, as the worst lessons did, with a trick.

Adaeze had me copying verb tables for three weeks by then—conjugations I copied faithfully into a notebook I was starting to think of as a second spine, something structural, something holding me upright. The language had tenses English didn't bother with: a form for things that happened and still hurt, a form for things that happened and were finished hurting, a form for things that would happen only if the ancestors allowed it. I filled pages. I was proud of my pages. I should have known better than to be proud around Adaeze.

On a grey Tuesday in March she took the notebook out of my hands, closed it, and set it on top of the fridge where neither of us could reach it without admitting we wanted to.

"Enough copying," she said. "Today you learn *fúyẹ́mọ̀*."

"I know that word." I did—I'd written it out forty times. "To release."

"You know how to spell it." She lowered herself into her chair with the care of a woman negotiating with her own knees. "Spelling is not knowing. In this language, some words are doors. You can draw a door on paper your whole life and never walk through one."

I waited. Waiting was a skill she'd taught me too—she rewarded patience with stories and punished fidgeting with silence, and I had learned which economy I preferred.

"*Fúyẹ́mọ̀* has no tense," she said. "Did you notice? Go back through your notebook. Every other verb bends—past, future, conditional, the form for things said in anger, the form for things said at night. But this one does not bend. Do you know why?"

"Because forgiveness isn't about time?"

She made the face she made when I said something half-right—the face of a woman tasting soup that needed more salt. "Because you cannot forgive in the past or the future. There is no *I forgave*. There is no *I will forgive*. The verb only exists in the mouth of the person saying it, now, meaning it. If you say it without meaning it, the word doesn't fail." She looked at me over her glasses. "You fail. The word simply refuses to be spoken by a liar. It sits in your throat like a stone until you either mean it or spit it out."

"That's a lot of pressure for a verb."

"That is what verbs are for." She tapped the armrest. "English lets you hide inside its grammar. You can say 'I have forgiven him' and mean nothing, and the sentence stands there like a well-dressed man at a funeral, respectable and empty. My language does not dress its words. Now." She folded her hands. "We conjugate."

"It doesn't conjugate."

"We conjugate *you*, Jonah. Sit up."

* * *

What she meant, I understood too late, was that the exercise wasn't grammatical at all.

"To say *fúyẹ́mọ̀*," she explained, "you must name three things: who released, who is released, and what wrong is being carried. The wrong must be named aloud. Not described. Named. There is a difference. Description is a wall; naming is a window. So." She leaned forward. "Your father. Say it."

"Say what?"

"The whole thing. Who releases, who is released, what is carried. In Ọ̀rọ̀ Ilẹ̀ if you can. In English if you must. But aloud, in this room, to another person."

My mouth opened. What came out was nothing—a small dry sound, like a page turning.

"Take your time," she said, which from Adaeze was not kindness but strategy. She knew I'd fill silence eventually. It was the one dishonest habit she hadn't cured me of yet.

I looked at the kitchen behind her—the dented kettle, the calendar from a Lagos bank, years out of date, that she refused to replace because "time is a colonial concept and also I like the pictures." I thought about the cassette in my bag, which I carried everywhere now like an organ I'd grown. I thought about my father's voice saying goodbye to a baby who couldn't understand him, and how I'd spent thirty-nine years understanding him wrongly instead.

"I release you," I said finally, and my voice came out strange, unpracticed, like a joint that hadn't bent in years. "Bàbá. I release you. You didn't leave us. You were taken from us, and you spent whatever was left of your life trying to carry our language somewhere it could survive, and you died doing it, and I called you a coward in my head every day of my childhood because it was easier than calling myself one for hating you." The words kept coming now, ugly and fast. "*Fúyẹ́mọ̀.* I release you. I release the wrong I carried, which was never yours to begin with."

The room went very quiet. Somewhere below us, the shuttered bakery creaked as the building settled.

Adaeze didn't smile. That was the discipline of her—she never rewarded the big moments with softness, because softness would have let me off the hook. Instead she nodded once, slowly, like a judge accepting evidence.

"Again," she said. "In the language."

And I did. Badly. My tones were wrong—I put falling tones where rising ones belonged, and she corrected me mid-sentence without mercy—but I said it, all of it, in the tongue my father had used to say goodbye, and when I finished, something in my chest unlocked with an audible feeling, less like relief than like a lock rusting open after decades.

"Now your mother," Adaeze said.

That one was harder, and she knew it. Bisi had been dead four years, and grief for the dead curdles easily into grievance, and mine had been simmering since Funmi's phone call, since Manchester, since the slow accumulation of everything Bisi had buried to keep us safe—buried so deep that her sons' inheritance had been silence, and her daughter's inheritance had been fear, and the man who loved her had become a stranger in his own family's memory.

"She lied to us," I said. "For thirty years."

"She protected you."

"She decided for us. She decided we'd rather be safe than whole, and she never asked, and she died without asking, and I—" I stopped. Breathed. Adaeze waited with the patience of a woman who had buried a husband and outlived a language and was not going to be rushed by a middle-aged critic having feelings in her kitchen.

"I release you, Mama," I said at last. "You chose the only weapon you had, which was silence, and you wielded it for us, badly, perfectly, the way mothers do. *Fúyẹ́mọ̀.* I release you. I release the anger. It was real and it was earned and it is heavy and I am putting it down."

This time Adaeze did close her eyes briefly, and I saw her lips move—not in prayer exactly, more like a woman checking a ledger against memory. When she opened them they were wet, and she pretended they weren't, and I pretended not to notice, and between those two pretensions there passed something truer than most honest things.

"And now," she said, and her voice had changed—lighter, almost casual, which is how I knew to be afraid, "the third one."

"There is no third one."

"There is always a third one." She folded her arms. "Who else is carrying a wrong in this room?"

"Nobody. This is my—these are my parents' sins, not mine."

"Is that so." She unfolded her arms and leaned back. "Jonah. For twenty years you wrote about food you could not taste and called it work. For twenty years you performed pleasure for strangers while eating plain rice alone in a flat that smells of nothing. You built a career out of a beautiful lie, and then, when the truth arrived wearing a letter addressed to 'the son of Bisi,' you nearly threw it away—and when you didn't throw it away, you spent months learning a dead man's language and a dying woman's grammar, and do you know what you have not done, not once, in all that time?"

I said nothing.

"You have not forgiven yourself. Not for the reviews. Not for the silence. Not for the years you spent hating a father who deserved your grief instead. You forgive everyone else their survival strategies, boy, but you will not grant yourself the same mercy. You stand outside yourself with a red pen." She pointed at me. "*Fúyẹ́mọ̀* cannot be conjugated in the second person. You cannot release yourself through me. The verb only works in the first."

The afternoon light had gone long and amber across the floor. I became aware of my own heartbeat, of the tape in my bag, of the notebook on top of the fridge, of all the sentences I had ever written that described nothing I felt.

"I don't know how," I said. It came out smaller than I intended.

"Of course you don't. Nobody does. That is why the verb exists—to teach the mouth what the heart refuses." She stood, joints cracking, and went to the window. Below, the street was doing its evening business, indifferent to us. "But I will tell you what I told myself, forty years ago, when I stood in a classroom where children were beaten for speaking, and I chose to keep speaking anyway, and people suffered for my choice, and I had to decide whether to drown in that or live with it. I said: the wrong is not that I survived. The wrong is only if I stop now."

She turned around.

"So. Say it, or don't. But know that the lesson is not over until you do, and I am an old woman with nowhere to be."

* * *

I didn't say it that day.

I want to be honest about that, because honesty is the only currency I have left. I sat in Adaeze's kitchen until the light died, and then I stood, and thanked her for the lesson, and collected my bag with the tape in it, and walked down the stairs past the shuttered bakery, and got in my car, and drove home, and ate plain rice alone, and did not sleep.

The verb sat in my throat like a stone, exactly as promised.

Three days later I came back. She had tea waiting, which meant she'd expected me, which meant she'd known. We worked on greetings for an hour—safe territory, the shallow water—and then she set down her cup and looked at me, and I set down mine.

"I release myself," I said. "For the reviews. For the performance. For teaching strangers to hunger for descriptions of things I couldn't feel. *Fúyẹ́mọ̀*—"

And there it stopped. The stone. Right where she said it would be. I stood in the doorway of the sentence like a man at a border with the wrong papers, and Adaeze watched me with terrible gentleness, and the word would not come, because the word cannot be spoken by a liar, and some part of me—some part that had been keeping the accounts all along—did not believe I had earned it yet.

"I can't," I said. "Not yet."

"I know," Adaeze said. "Not yet is not never. Put it down when it's ready to be put down, not before, or you'll only pick it up again heavier." She poured more tea. "Two of three is not failure, Jonah. Two of three is a man standing up."

"But the verb doesn't conjugate partially."

"No," she agreed. "It waits."

* * *

That night I dreamed in Ọ̀rọ̀ Ilẹ̀ for the first time—not fluently, just fragments, my father's voice braided with Adaeze's, a tone pattern I couldn't place repeating like a bell buoy. I woke at four in the morning with my heart going hard and wrote, without deciding to, the opening of something new. Not a review. A book. Her name at the top of the first page: *Adaeze*, spelled correctly, every mark in place.

At the next lesson I showed her the page. She read it twice, slowly, moving her lips. Then she looked up with an expression I hadn't seen on her before—something between pride and alarm, the way you might look at weather turning.

"Good," she said. "Good. Because we are running out of Tuesdays, and there are things left to teach you that cannot wait for your self-forgiveness to arrive on its own schedule." She reached for her cane. "Next week, I will tell you the rest about your father. What he carried. Where he was going. Why the photographs show a man standing apart."

"What do you mean, the rest?"

She was already moving toward the kitchen, already done with me for the day, an old woman with an archive in her head and no time left to shelve it.

"I mean," she said over her shoulder, "that you have forgiven a ghost and a grave, and you think the story is finished. It is not finished. Your father did not only run, Jonah. He carried things. And someone has to know what they were before I don't."
