## 21. Speaking for the Dead

The funeral home on Peckham Road had gotten her name wrong twice.

First on the printed program—*Adaeze Okonkwo-Bello*, a name that was almost hers, assembled by someone who had skimmed a form. Then again when the celebrant rehearsed it aloud that morning, flattening the vowels into something apologetic, something English could carry without effort. Jonah had stood at the back of the chapel with the corrected order of service in his hand and said nothing, which was his oldest skill, and then he had walked to the front and said, quietly, "It's Adéṣewá. The stress falls here." And he'd shown them, syllable by syllable, the way she had shown him eleven months ago in a flat above a shuttered bakery, tapping the table like a woman keeping time against forgetting.

The celebrant had looked at him—this tall Nigerian-English man in an ill-fitting agbada Funmi had insisted on—and nodded once, the way you nod at a stranger who turns out to be holding the map.

That was Tuesday's rehearsal. The funeral was Thursday. In between there was a night Jonah did not sleep, sitting in Adaeze's flat—his flat now, though he couldn't say it yet—with her notebooks open around him like weather.

* * *

The trouble revealed itself on Thursday itself, forty minutes before the service, in the small anteroom where the family and the elders of the community had gathered. There were more people than Jonah had expected. Adaeze had outlived most of her generation but not, it turned out, all of them: three old women from the Lagos association, a man named Pa Adebayo who walked with two sticks and a face like carved wood, a handful of middle-aged children of the diaspora who had grown up hearing fragments and understanding none of them. And Funmi, in blue and gold, magnificent and furious-looking, the way she always looked when she was frightened.

The celebrant cleared his throat. "We'll begin with the eulogy," he said. "In Ọ̀rọ̀ Ilẹ̀, as Mrs.—as Adéṣewá requested. Her instructions were specific."

Silence.

Jonah watched it move through the room like cold air. The elders looked at one another. One of the women—the one with the coral beads, whose name he would later learn was Mama Ngozi—began speaking low and fast in Yoruba, and another shook her head, and Pa Adebayo said something in a language Jonah recognized with a jolt that went through him like current: the tones were right, the music of it, but the words were only half-familiar, a cousin of what Adaeze had taught him, worn down by sixty years of disuse.

"I knew her fifty years," Pa Adebayo said finally, in English, each word set down like a stone. "I cannot do this. My tongue—" He touched his mouth. "She kept it. I let it go."

Mama Ngozi's hands were folded. "I can pray in it," she offered. "A little. Not a eulogy. She would know the difference. She would *know*."

And everyone understood what she meant: that Adaeze, who had spent her whole life listening for the difference between the true word and the approximate one, between devotion and performance, would know exactly who had stood up and given her less than everything.

Jonah felt the undertow then. He would recognize it later for what it was—the old pull toward invisibility, the sweet gravity of letting someone else stand up, of being the man in the back row with the corrected program in his pocket. He had spent thirty years writing raptures about food he could not taste; he had spent eleven months learning a language he still spoke like a man crossing ice. Every reason to stay seated arrived at once, dressed as humility.

Funmi caught his eye across the room. She didn't nod. She didn't have to. She just looked at him the way she had looked at him in Manchester, across her kitchen table, after she'd finished telling him about their father—the look that said *you already know what you have to do, brother, and I am not going to make it easier.*

Jonah stood up.

* * *

Later he would not be able to reconstruct the first thirty seconds. What remained was sensation: the floor through his shoes, the heat of his own collar, the paper trembling in his hand—not because he hadn't prepared, but because he had prepared in a language that lived in his chest now rather than his head, and his chest was shaking.

"My name is Jonah Oyelaran," he began, in English, because Adaeze had taught him that too: *start where you are standing.* "Bisi's son. Adaeze called me that before I ever met her—'the son of Bisi'—and I didn't understand until nearly the end that it wasn't an introduction. It was an assignment."

Someone in the front row made a sound, half laugh, half sob.

"She asked me to be her last student. I want to tell you what that cost, because she would want it told honestly—she believed a language died faster in polite mouths than in silent ones." He took a breath and switched.

"Ẹ kú àárọ̀." Good morning—an absurd greeting for an afternoon funeral, and he knew it, and he said it anyway, because it was the first thing she had ever taught him, in a kitchen that smelled of pepper and dust, and because the dead deserve to hear where you began. "Ẹ kú àárọ̀, mà. Mo wá. Ẹni tí mo jẹ́ ni ọmọ Bisi."

His tones were wrong. He heard them land wrong even as he spoke—too flat on the rising syllable, too careful everywhere, a student's voice, a foreigner's voice in his own inheritance. And here was the thing he had not anticipated, the thing that undid him: nobody flinched. Mama Ngozi leaned forward. Pa Adebayo closed his eyes. The room bent toward him the way plants bend toward a window.

He told her life in Ọ̀rọ̀ Ilẹ̀ as far as his grammar would carry him and fell back into English when it wouldn't—which was often, and he stopped apologizing for it somewhere in the second paragraph, because he could hear her voice: *"Do not spend your breath begging pardon. Spend it on the next word."*

He told them about the twelve words for kinds of listening. He told them how she had learned them as a girl from her grandmother, who had learned them under the ban of colonial schoolmasters who washed children's mouths with soap for speaking what they were born knowing. He told them about the recordings—two hundred hours of them now, catalogued in his flat, every verb form she could still summon, including the one he was not going to get through this speech without using.

Because that was why he had stood up. Not for the biography. Anyone could read the biography. He stood up because there was a structure in Ọ̀rọ̀ Ilẹ̀ that existed for releasing old wrongs—a verb form so particular, so deliberate, that Adaeze had made him practice it for six weeks before she allowed it into ordinary conversation, saying: *this is not a word you say carelessly. This is a door you open on purpose.*

He had used it twice in his life. Once in her kitchen, for his father. Once, later, for his mother. Both times incomplete. Both times she had listened with her head tilted, patient as water, waiting for the third conjugation he could not yet say—the one for himself.

He said it now, standing over her coffin, in front of elders whose tongues held more of the language than his ever would.

"Mà, mo fọwọ́ sí mi," he said. *Mother—I release myself.* His voice cracked on the final tone and he let it crack. "I forgive myself. For the years I stayed quiet. For the reviews I wrote about meals I couldn't taste, the beautiful words I spent describing nothing. For every time silence felt safer than truth. You told me once that a man practicing invisibility is still practicing something—that it takes work, that it's a choice renewed daily. I choose differently now. I'm choosing it out loud, in your language, badly, in front of everyone, because you taught me that badly and out loud is the only way anything true gets said."

The room was absolutely still. Somewhere a phone buzzed and was silenced with a violence that made several people jump.

Then Mama Ngozi rose.

* * *

He had braced for correction—he had been bracing for correction his entire life, in kitchens and newsrooms and school corridors, the flinch before the verdict. So when the old woman came toward him with her coral beads swinging, some ancient machinery in him braced for the blow.

What she did was take his elbow.

"Ọmọ Bisi," she said. *Child of Bisi.* And then, gently, in the mixed broken-and-beautiful way of the diaspora, she corrected him. The vowel in the second phrase—open it wider, like this. The tone on the penultimate word—it rises, it asks, it doesn't state. She demonstrated. She waited.

"Say it again," she said.

He said it again. Wronger, if anything, because his throat had closed.

"Again."

Again.

"Better," she said, which was a lie, and both of them knew it was a lie, and it was also somehow completely true. Then she turned to the room. "This boy speaks our language like a man carrying water in his hands—some spills, but he carries it." Laughter, wet and grateful, moved through the chapel. "Who among us does not spill? Who among us has not let the bucket sit empty for thirty years?" She looked at Pa Adebayo, who did not look away. "He stood up. That is the whole of it. He stood up."

One by one, the others came. An elder corrected his greeting and he repeated it. A young woman—he never learned her name—showed him the proper way to speak Adaeze's praise-name, and he stumbled through it twice, three times, while four hundred people watched, and something happened in him each time that he had no vocabulary for in either language: being corrected without being diminished. Being imperfect in public and finding the public still holding him up.

He thought of the exposé. He thought of the withdrawn nomination, the colleagues who had discovered urgent business elsewhere, the column he no longer had. He had confessed in print and been punished for it, and he had believed, for a dark stretch of weeks, that visibility was simply a slower form of execution. But this was different. Here, imperfection wasn't fraud. Here, the attempt itself was the offering. Adaeze had known that. She had chosen him *because* he was half-gone—not despite it—because a man who has lost everything false has room for what's real.

At the podium, he turned back to the coffin one last time.

"Adéṣewá," he said, and got the stress right, and heard the room receive it like a bell struck cleanly. "Ẹ ṣé. Thank you. You said a language dies in polite mouths. Yours was never polite. Mine is learning." He paused. "Kó si irẹ́. Rest well. Your words are awake. I heard them this morning in my own mouth, and they tasted—" he stopped, because the word arrived unbidden, the word he had been chasing his whole career through other people's kitchens, "*they tasted like beginning.*"

* * *

Afterward, in the car park, under a sky doing its grey London best, Pa Adebayo came to him on his two sticks and stood very close, close enough that Jonah could see the language still living behind his eyes like fish under ice.

"The archive," the old man said. "Her recordings. And your father's papers, if they exist. What will happen to them?"

"They'll be digitized. There's a linguist at SOAS who's agreed to help. And—" Jonah hesitated. "And I'm starting a class. Saturdays. Children, mostly. Diaspora kids who've never heard it spoken except at funerals."

Pa Adebayo studied him for a long moment. Then he said something in Ọ̀rọ̀ Ilẹ̀—slowly, deliberately, enunciating like a teacher—and waited.

Jonah parsed it. *When*—no. *If the student is ready.* No. He worked through it again, tone by tone, and felt his face change as the meaning assembled itself.

*"The teacher learns the lesson twice,"* he translated slowly. *"Once to keep it, once to give it away."*

The old man smiled for the first time. "Saturday," he said, in English. "What time?"

Behind them, Funmi was laughing at something Mama Ngozi had said—the real laugh, the one from childhood, the one their mother's silence had buried and their father's exile had nearly finished. Jonah stood in the car park holding a funeral program with the correct name printed on it, and reached into his pocket for his notebook, because a sentence had just arrived, whole, the opening line of the book he had been afraid to start—

—and his phone buzzed in his other hand. An unknown number. A message preview he read without meaning to:

*Mr. Oyelaran — this is Dr. Ifeoma Eze at the University of Ibadan. We believe we've found your father's remaining papers. You will want to sit down.*
