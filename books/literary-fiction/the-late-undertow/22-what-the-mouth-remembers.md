## 22. What the Mouth Remembers

The children arrive on Saturdays the way weather arrives—scattered at first, then all at once.

Jonah has learned to expect them by ten-fifteen, though the class officially begins at eleven. They come in ones and twos up the narrow stairs of the community centre on Rye Lane, Peckham, trailing parents who linger at the door with the particular anxiety of people depositing something precious into unfamiliar hands. A girl named Zainab, eight years old, whose grandmother speaks three languages and whose father speaks none of them well. Twin brothers from Croydon who fight over the window seat. A teenager called Marcus who came the first week only because his mother made him and now arrives early to help Jonah arrange the chairs, pretending he doesn't.

Twelve children. Some weeks nine. Once, gloriously, fourteen, when a visiting cousin from Houston joined for a single Saturday and taught them all a hand-clapping song her own grandmother had used, and Jonah wrote down every syllable before she flew home.

He teaches them the way Adaeze taught him: standing, moving, unembarrassed. The language lives in the body first, she always said. You do not learn Ọ̀rọ̀ Ilẹ̀ with your eyes. You learn it with your feet, your hands, your mouth making shapes it has never made. So the children greet each other across the room in call and response, their voices overlapping and colliding, wrong in a hundred small ways that are, Jonah has come to believe, the only right way to begin.

*Ẹ kú àárọ̀*, he calls.

*Ẹ kú àárọ̀*, they answer, most of them mangling the vowels, one of them—Zainab, always Zainab—landing it clean as a bell.

There is no word for *critic* in Ọ̀rọ̀ Ilẹ̀. There are twelve words for kinds of listening. Jonah teaches them four before breakfast, so to speak—the listening you do with your ears, the listening you do with your whole skin when someone is angry, the listening you do to the dead, the listening you do to a room that is about to change. The children invent gestures for each one. Marcus's gesture for listening-to-the-dead is so solemn, so unexpectedly beautiful—a slow lowering of both palms, like setting something heavy onto water—that Jonah borrows it permanently and uses it himself now, every time, and thinks of a boy from Croydon every time he does.

Adaeze would have hated the borrowed chairs. She would have loved everything else.

Her photograph sits on the shelf above the whiteboard, the one from the flat above the shuttered bakery, the one where she is laughing at whoever held the camera, daring them to be dull. Below it, in a frame Funmi sent from Manchester, a photograph Jonah had never seen until last spring: Bisi, young, head thrown back mid-laugh, and beside her a watchful man looking not at the camera but at her, as if she were the only fixed point in a shifting world. His father's face. Not cold. Afraid, and guarding what he loved.

The children never ask about the photographs. They ask about everything else. Why does the language have no word for goodbye, only words for *until*? Why are there six ways to say rain? Can you swear in it—please say you can swear in it—and Jonah, who swore an oath of dignity to Adaeze's memory, tells them there is a word for calling someone a goat that has eaten your homework, and watches twelve faces light up like struck matches.

Some Saturdays, after the parents collect them, he sits alone in the emptied room for a few minutes before locking up. He says the greetings once more, quietly, to nobody. It is not prayer exactly. It is closer to watering something.

* * *

The book is forty thousand words long and refuses to end, which Jonah understands is its way of staying alive.

He works on it in the mornings now, at the kitchen table, because the study still feels like the room where he performed. *The Last Student*, he is calling it, though Funmi votes for something else and his agent votes for something else again and Jonah has learned, at fifty-three, to hold his own line gently. It is not a memoir. He tried writing it as a memoir and the sentences came out wearing cologne. It is a book about Adaeze—her war, her husband lost to a road that took no responsibility, her students who quit when the grammar got hard, her thirty years of keeping a promise to a woman named Bisi who chose silence over safety's opposite. And it is a book about a language that colonial schoolmasters beat out of children's mouths and that survived anyway, the way water survives, finding the low places.

Writing it is the hardest work he has ever done, harder than the confession, harder than Manchester.

Because the old craft is useless here. For twenty-five years Jonah Oyelaran built cathedrals out of words describing meals he could not taste, and the craft was real even if the experience was borrowed—he knows this now, holds both truths without dropping either—but the craft was also a kind of armour, and armour does not bend. To write about Adaeze he has to write plainly. Short sentences. No rapture. When he reaches the night he played the cassette—his father's voice saying goodbye to a baby who could not understand, *sleep well, small one; I go so that these words will live; forgive me for the shape of my leaving*—he writes it in under two hundred words and then sits with his forehead on the table for a long time, because plainness, it turns out, costs more than ornament ever did.

His editor, Priya, reads the draft and calls him at seven in the morning.

"It's good," she says, and pauses, and he braces, because he knows that pause. "It's better than good. It doesn't sound like you."

"I know," Jonah says. "That's the point."

Another pause. "The old readers will hate it."

"The old readers believed me about food for twenty-five years. They can survive one honest book."

She laughs, surprised, and he hears her write something down, and he understands that somewhere in that sound his career is being rebuilt on different foundations—not wider than before, but deeper, load-bearing in a new way.

He is stuck, currently, on the final chapter. He knows how it ends—with salt, with grief, with the beginning of forgiveness—but knowing an ending and earning it on the page are different countries, and he cannot find the ferry. Adaeze would tell him to stop trying to steer. *The language moves*, she said once, exasperated, tapping his notebook. *You keep trying to carry it like luggage. Put it down. Walk beside it.*

So he puts it down. He walks beside it. On Thursdays he records himself reading passages aloud in Ọ̀rọ̀ Ilẹ̀, badly, and plays them back, and corrects, and records again, and the flat fills with a language that was nearly buried twice—once by empire, once by fear—and is now, at least in this one kitchen, stubbornly alive.

* * *

The review publishes on a Friday, in the magazine that fired him and rehired him, because Priya argued for it with the ferocity of a woman defending family.

It is eight hundred words. It is about a small Nigerian restaurant in Deptford run by a widow named Mrs. Okonkwo, who came to Jonah's Saturday class for two weeks last autumn before deciding she was too busy, but who feeds him whenever he appears, unasked, unprompted, sliding a plate across the counter with the authority of a woman who has decided your opinions no longer matter, only your appetite.

He ate the meal. That is the first sentence of the review, or nearly: *I ate this meal, which for me is not a small thing, and I will explain why, briefly, and then I will tell you about the soup.*

He tells them why. Four paragraphs, no more—the fever at nine, the silence at the table, the twenty-five years of borrowed pleasure, the confession, the cost. He does not dramatise. He does not apologise twice. Then he writes about egusi soup the way he should have written about every meal of his life: honestly, specifically, from inside the body. The way the ground melon seeds thicken against the roof of the mouth. The bitterness of the bitter leaf arriving late, like a correction. The stockfish that tastes, to him now, unmistakably, of patience—of someone soaking and rinsing and waiting, days of attention folded into a single spoonful.

*I spent decades describing flavours I could not feel,* he writes near the end. *I owe those years a debt I can never repay except by telling the truth now, while I can, about what tasting actually is. It is not luxury. It is memory arriving through the mouth. Mrs. Okonkwo's soup tastes like a kitchen I was carried through as a child, before the fever, before the silence, a kitchen I did not know I remembered until tonight.*

The comments section, that sewer, behaves strangely. People write about their own silences—the son who never told his father he loved him before the diagnosis, the woman who performed happiness at weddings for a decade, a man who admits he has been reading restaurant reviews for years without ever once tasting what they described, and loving them anyway, and feeling seen now, and slightly betrayed, and glad. Priya forwards him a letter from a food scientist in Toronto who lost her smell to a virus and wept reading it on a train.

Jonah reads the letters twice and does not reply to any of them yet. He is learning that some responses need to steep.

Mrs. Okonkwo, informed of the review, shrugs magnificently. "So now you can taste," she says. "Come Saturday anyway. The children need feeding."

He comes Saturday. He brings jollof rice, his mother's recipe, cooked from memory and from a card in Adaeze's handwriting that he found, months after the funeral, tucked into the back of her Yoruba-Ọ̀rọ̀ Ilẹ̀ dictionary, annotated in her fierce marginal script: *For Jonah. Tell him Bisi measured nothing. Neither shall he.*

* * *

The funeral was in April. It is October now, and the mornings have turned, and Jonah stands in his kitchen on a Sunday with the windows fogged and the radio off, doing what he does every Sunday now, which is cooking.

Bisi measured nothing. Neither shall he.

Scotch bonnets—two, because he is brave, one and a half because he is honest. Tomatoes blistered under the grill until their skins surrender. Onions sweated down soft and gold. The parboiled rice, the stock, the bay leaf, the thyme, the long covered wait on low heat that is the entire secret and cannot be rushed and cannot be faked, which is perhaps why he loves it: a dish that punishes performance and rewards only presence.

He has made it eleven times since April. The first three were disasters—burnt at the bottom, raw at the top, salt forgotten entirely, which felt like a joke someone else was telling. The fourth was edible. The seventh was good. Tonight, the eleventh, he lifts the lid and the steam rises up into his face and—

Salt.

Not flavour exactly. Sensation. Grief made physical, grief made welcome, the taste of every table he sat at silent, every word swallowed, every year of elegant avoidance—and underneath all of it, unmistakable, undeniable, the beginning of something that is not grief at all.

He serves himself a plate. He eats standing at the counter like a man who has somewhere to be, though he has nowhere to be at all, nowhere but here, this kitchen, this body, this mouth that remembers.

Outside, London goes on being London. In Peckham tomorrow there will be chairs to arrange and greetings to mangle and a boy from Croydon with solemn hands. On the desk upstairs, a book waits, patient as water, for its ending. Somewhere a language with twelve kinds of listening continues, in twelve kitchens, in nine mouths, in one girl named Zainab who lands the vowels clean as bells.

Jonah eats his mother's jollof rice alone on a Sunday evening, and it does not taste like loss.

It tastes like beginning.
