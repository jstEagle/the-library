## 1. The Hours Nobody Claims

The library closed at eight, but Mara Oyelaran's shift began at nine, when the building had already exhaled its last patron and the cleaning crew was pushing their wide gray mops through the reading room like slow boats through a harbor. She arrived the way she did everything: without announcement, through the staff entrance on Delancey Street, badge pressed to the reader with a practiced flick of the wrist that didn't break her stride. The turnstile clicked. Somewhere above her, in the dark of the stacks, a ventilation duct ticked as it cooled.

Nine years she had worked here—no, seven. Seven years, and still she counted them wrong sometimes, the way you miscount stairs in your own house because your feet know the way better than your mind does. Seven years of nights. The day staff called her *the night girl* even though she was thirty-one and had a master's degree in library science framed on the wall of an apartment they had never seen. They called her that not unkindly. That was the thing about being invisible: nobody was cruel to you, because cruelty required noticing.

She hung her coat on the hook behind the circulation desk—the second hook, always the second, because the first belonged to Dayna from Acquisitions who came in at six a.m. and would find a strange coat there like an omen—and logged into the returns system. Forty-one items checked in overnight through the book drop. She could tell before she opened the bin what she'd find: the usual census of the city's sleeplessness. Insomnia memoirs. A dog-eared collection of Chekhov stories with a bus transfer used as a bookmark, dated three weeks ago. Someone had returned *The Shipping News* with a coffee ring on the cover shaped so precisely like a planet with a moon that Mara stood holding it for a moment longer than shelving required, thinking about orbits, about things that circle and circle and never land.

She shelved in order. 813.54. 863.3. Biography, then poetry, then the large-print westerns that a man named Mr. Halloran came for every Tuesday at eleven a.m., which meant he came during the hours she was asleep, which meant in seven years she had never once seen his face, only the wake he left in the collection: books pulled forward an inch, spines askew, a faint smell of wintergreen. She knew him by his absence. It occurred to her, reshelving *Lonesome Dove* for perhaps the two-hundredth time, that this was how most of the people in her life existed now—as disturbances in a field, evidence of passage, never the passing itself.

Her phone buzzed against her hip at 10:40. She knew without looking. It was the scheduled buzz—not a message but a reminder, one she'd set herself two years ago and kept renewing like a subscription to a magazine she never read: *Text Bisi.*

Bisi. Her sister. Four hundred miles away, married now, a son Mara had met once, at six months old, when he'd gripped her finger with the astonishing strength of the very young and she had cried in the parking lot of a Cracker Barrel and driven home through the night telling herself the crying was about nothing, about tiredness, about the light.

Mara took out her phone. In the blue glow of the returns screen she drafted, as she had drafted a thousand times:

*Hey. Saw a kid today at the bus stop wearing your old green jacket, the one with the missing button. Made me think of—*

Delete.

*How is Sam walking yet? I saw a video Mom posted. He looks—*

Delete. Mom. There was a word that ended every draft like a dropped glass.

*Bisi. I've been meaning to tell you—*

Tell her what? That was the problem with the unsent texts. They all converged on the same sentence, the one she had been circling for seven years the way the coffee ring circled its planet, and she could no longer see the shape of it clearly, only the stain it left.

She put the phone face-down on the desk. Outside the tall windows of the reading room, the city performed its nightly argument with itself—a siren rising and giving up, a couple laughing too loudly on the corner, the neon of the pawn shop across the street buzzing its one pink syllable. The central library sat in the middle of it all like a held breath.

* * *

At midnight she made her rounds. This was the part of the shift she loved, if love was the right word for a feeling so close to grief that they might have been twins separated at birth. The building at midnight belonged to her entirely. She walked the stacks with her cart, checking the quiet, and the quiet checked her back. In the local history room, the microfilm machines stood like sleeping animals. In the periodicals section, yesterday's newspapers lay in their wooden cradles, headlines already going soft around the edges, news becoming record becoming dust.

She passed the biography section and slowed, as she always did, at the D's. There was a book there—she had shelved it a dozen times—about a jazz singer who had lived two lives, one public and one hidden, and whose secret had come out only after her death, in letters found in a trunk. Mara had read it standing up, over three nights, hiding it inside the shelf when the security guard made his hourly pass. She had read it the way you press on a bruise. Not because it hurt exactly. Because it confirmed something.

Everyone has a trunk, she had thought. The question is whether anyone opens it while you're alive to watch.

Her own trunk was small enough to carry in a coat pocket: a name she'd stopped using at twenty-four, a name she'd started using at twenty-six, and seven years of silence between her and everyone who had known her by the first. When she left home, she hadn't run dramatically. She'd packed two suitcases, taken the 47 bus to the train station, and texted her mother from the platform: *I'm sorry. I can't be what you need.* Her mother had texted back a single word—*God*—and then nothing, and the nothing had continued for months until it hardened into policy, and the policy into geography, and now her mother lived four hundred miles away in a house Mara had seen only in photographs her sister sent and then, eventually, stopped sending.

Her father had died when she was nineteen, which meant he was spared everything, which meant she missed him with a ferocity that surprised her at odd moments—like now, shelving biographies at midnight, finding his hands suddenly superimposed on hers, big palms, careful with book spines, turning pages at the corners the way he'd taught her, *so the paper doesn't remember where you've been.* But paper remembers. Paper remembers everything. That was the whole trouble with libraries, and the whole reason she loved them: they were buildings built specifically to defeat forgetting, and she had made her life inside one while constructing herself as a woman designed to be forgotten.

The security guard, Okafor, found her at 1:15 in the 900s, standing in front of a book about the history of Nigerian immigration to the coastal cities, not reading it, just standing.

"Oyelaran," he said, using her name fully, formally, the way he always did, and she felt the small complicated pleasure and pain of it. "You're doing the stare again."

"What stare?"

"The one where you're shelving books but you're actually somewhere else." He leaned on his flashlight like a cane. "Where do you go?"

"Same place everybody goes," she said. "Nowhere."

He laughed, shook his head, moved on down the aisle, his footsteps fading into the carpeted hush. He was a good man, Okafor. He asked questions he didn't expect answered. That was the etiquette of the night shift: you offered each other the forms of human connection without the contents, like serving empty plates at a banquet, and everyone pretended to eat.

* * *

At 2 a.m., the building made its deepest sound, the one visitors never heard: a settling, a long structural sigh, as if the whole weight of the books—four hundred thousand volumes, all those remembered lives—pressed down through the floors and into the foundation and the foundation said, *I have you, I have you.* Mara sat at the circulation desk under the one lamp she allowed herself and did the returns log, and between entries she opened her phone again.

This time she didn't draft to Bisi. She scrolled instead to the contact labeled *Mom* and looked at it the way you look at a locked door in a house you used to live in. Last message received: fourteen months ago. A photograph of a casserole. No caption. She had responded with a thumbs-up, because there was no emoji for *I am your child and I am still alive and I am still yours even though we both pretend otherwise,* and the thumbs-up had felt like a lie told with the body, and she had hated herself mildly for a week.

Underneath the casserole photo, older messages surfaced like sediment. *Call me back.* *Your aunt Funke says hello.* *Did you eat?* And once, devastatingly, at 11 p.m. on a Sunday two years ago: *I dreamed about you. You were little. You were happy. Call me.* Mara had typed *I'm happy too, Mama* and deleted it, because it wasn't true in the way her mother needed it to be true, and typed *I miss you* and deleted it, because it was true and truth was the one currency their family no longer accepted, and finally sent nothing, and the nothing had sat there between them ever since, growing, taking up space in the conversation like furniture neither of them would move.

A draft began assembling itself in her chest, unbidden, the way drafts did at 2 a.m.:

*Mama. I work nights at the library. I have a plant that refuses to die. I take my coffee black now, the way Dad did. I have a name you gave me and a name I chose and I am so tired of carrying both like luggage through every hour of every day. I am not asking you to understand. I am asking you to call. Just call. Say anything. Say the weather. Say the casserole was good. Say—*

She locked the phone. Set it down. Pressed her palm flat against the cool wood of the desk, grounding herself in the grain, in the present tense, in the smell of old paper and floor wax that was, whatever else it was, home.

The lamp hummed. Somewhere in the fiction stacks a book had been left spine-up, open, abandoned mid-sentence by some daytime reader, and she got up to close it, to mark the page with a slip of acid-free paper, to honor the interruption. The sentence where the stranger had left off read: *and she understood that the house had been keeping her secrets longer than she had been keeping them herself.*

Mara closed the book gently. Paper remembers where you've been.

* * *

The last hours went the way they always went. She straightened the chairs in the reading room so they faced forward, obedient, ready for tomorrow's strangers. She watered the ficus by the reference desk, which was dying slowly and with dignity, dropping one yellow leaf per day like a man leaving a trail of breadcrumbs toward an exit he'd never take. At 4:30 she ate half a sandwich at the desk, watching the sky over the pawn shop go from black to the color of a bruise healing.

And at 5:50, she executed the maneuver she had perfected over seven years, the choreography of non-encounter. Dayna from Acquisitions arrived at six sharp. Mara heard the elevator before she saw it—the particular rattling ascent of the freight car—and she gathered her coat, killed her lamp, and slipped into the stairwell, descending two flights to the staff exit as Dayna ascended. They had never once spoken. They had shared a building, a payroll cycle, a coat rack (two hooks apart) for seven years. Sometimes Mara imagined introducing herself—*Hi, I'm Mara, I work nights, I think we're colleagues*—and the imagining was so embarrassing, so nakedly lonely, that she always aborted it halfway through, the way she aborted the texts.

Tonight, though, in the stairwell, something small happened. On the landing between floors, pinned to the corkboard where the fire drill notices lived, someone had posted a flyer. City transit notice, official letterhead, a map. Mara's eye caught on the number before anything else: **47**.

*SERVICE DISCONTINUATION NOTICE. Effective [date], Route 47 will be permanently rerouted...*

She stood on the landing with her hand on the rail. The 47. The bus she had ridden out of her mother's neighborhood with two suitcases and a heart full of unsayable things. The bus that ran, still, at the far edge of the city, out past the river, into the old district—the streets where she grew up, where her grandmother Iya Agnes still lived in the house with the blue door, or had lived; Mara hadn't called since Christmas, and the Christmas call had lasted ninety seconds and contained no information whatsoever. The district they'd been talking about tearing down for years, the condemned blocks, the boarded storefronts she avoided thinking about by never going, never looking, keeping the whole geography of her childhood sealed in amber at the end of a bus line.

Six weeks, said the notice. Six weeks and the route would be gone, rerouted, and the old neighborhood would recede another degree beyond reach, and someday there would be no bus at all, and then no street, and then—

Her phone buzzed. Not the scheduled reminder. An actual call, at 5:52 in the morning, from a number she almost didn't recognize and then recognized completely: her aunt Funke, her mother's sister, who never called, who had called her exactly twice in seven years, both times about funerals.

Mara looked at the ringing screen. The stairwell was cold and smelled of concrete. Above her, faintly, the elevator rattled upward with Dayna inside it, ascending into the ordinary day.

She answered.

"Auntie?"

"Mara." Funke's voice was stripped down, formal, the voice of someone holding themselves together with both hands. "Mara, I'm sorry to call like this. It's your grandmother." A breath, crackling down the line from four hundred miles away. "It's Iya Agnes, omo mi. She's gone."

Outside, through the wired glass of the exit door, the first bus of the morning hissed past the library, heading east, toward the river, toward the 47's route, toward the blue door, carrying its handful of half-asleep passengers into a day that had already, without their knowing it, changed shape.

"I'll come," Mara heard herself say. "I'll come home."
