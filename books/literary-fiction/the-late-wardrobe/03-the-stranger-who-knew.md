## 3. The Stranger Who Knew

The church smelled of lilies and floor wax, the way churches always did when they were preparing to say goodbye to someone they had only half known. Mara stood at the back of the sanctuary with her coat still buttoned, watching the pews fill with faces she could not attach to names and names she could not attach to faces. Seven years away will do that. Seven years of unanswered calls and returned Christmas cards will turn your own blood into a congregation of strangers, all of you dressed in black, all of you pretending the distance was weather.

She had told herself she would stand at the back. She had rehearsed it on the bus—because of course the 47 had carried her here too, groaning up the hill through neighborhoods that had changed their storefronts twice since she'd last looked—and she had rehearsed the whole choreography: arrive late, stand apart, sing nothing, leave early. A funeral is a place where nobody questions a woman who keeps herself at the edge of things. Grief makes its own privacy.

But then Aunt Funke had seen her.

Funke had been standing near the casket with the particular upright exhaustion of a woman who has spent three days on the phone with mortuaries and insurance companies and relatives overseas, and when her eyes found Mara across the sanctuary, something moved through her face—not welcome exactly, not surprise either, but a kind of grim confirmation, as if Mara's arrival were one more item on a list of things that had gone wrong this month. She crossed the aisle without hurrying. She took Mara's hands. Her grip was dry and strong and smelled faintly of the menthol rub she had used on her knuckles for as long as Mara could remember.

"You came," Funke said.

"I came."

"She would have wanted—" Funke stopped, pressed her lips together, started again. "Sit with us. Front. Don't hide in the back like a debt collector."

So much for the choreography. Mara let herself be led down the aisle past rows of turned heads, past women in gele headwraps the colors of storm warnings and flags, past men whose colognes arrived a half-second before their handshakes. She sat where Funke put her, third pew from the front, close enough to see the grain of the casket's wood, far enough that Iya Agnes's photograph above it resolved into the face Mara remembered: the firm mouth, the small gold cross that had hung at her throat every day of Mara's childhood, the eyes that had always seemed to be reading a document held just over your shoulder.

*Agnes Oyelaran*, said the order of service. Just that. No middle name, no honorific beyond the *Iya* everyone had used aloud, the Yoruba word for mother that the neighborhood had given her because she had mothered half of it—the boys with nowhere to sleep, the girls running from whatever they were running from, the ones the church forgave and the ones it didn't. *Iya Agnes*. Mother Agnes. As if the whole district had been her parish and every lost soul her congregation.

Mara had loved her grandmother the way you love a lighthouse: from a distance that made the light seem steady, never close enough to feel the heat of the lamp or hear the machinery grinding inside it.

The organ began. The choir sang. Mara mouthed the words of hymns she hadn't sung since she was nineteen, since the last Sunday she'd sat in a pew beside her mother and felt the words curdle in her mouth, each verse a small negotiation she was losing. Beside her, Funke wept quietly and efficiently, dabbing her eyes with a handkerchief that appeared and disappeared like a card trick. On Funke's other side sat Mara's cousin Tunde, grown now into a man with a soft jaw and a wedding ring, who glanced at Mara once, did a visible double take, and then stared very hard at his order of service for the remainder of the first hymn.

*Yes,* Mara thought. *It's me. Yes, I know. Yes, seven years.*

The pastor spoke about Iya Agnes in the way pastors speak about the recently dead: warmly, generically, with the confidence of a man summarizing a book he had skimmed. She had been a pillar. She had been generous. She had feared the Lord. He did not mention the halfway house on Adeyemi Street, though half the pews had been filled by people who had slept under its roof. He did not mention the forty years she had kept the lights on in that building after the city stopped funding it, paying the electric bill out of her pension like a woman feeding a fire she refused to let die. He did not mention, and Mara noticed herself cataloguing everything he did not mention, the way you notice the missing rooms in a house you once lived in.

Then, during the eulogy—a cousin from Lagos, reading haltingly from a phone—it happened.

The doors at the back of the sanctuary opened. Not slammed; opened, with the careful excessive quietness of someone who knows he is late and hopes the hinges will forgive him. Mara turned, along with half the congregation, because a door opening during a eulogy is the closest thing a funeral gets to drama.

A white man stood in the doorway.

He was perhaps fifty-five, tall and stooped, with thinning hair combed in a style that had given up sometime in the nineties. His suit was gray and wrong in every particular—too broad in the shoulders, too short in the sleeve, shiny at the elbows, the kind of suit that had been bought for someone else or for some other body, decades ago, and had been waiting ever since for an occasion important enough to wear it. His tie was knotted with the desperate precision of a man who had practiced in a mirror. Under his arm he carried a flat parcel wrapped in brown paper, and he held it the way people hold things they are afraid of dropping and more afraid of setting down.

He stood there a moment, blinking in the light, and then he came down the aisle.

Mara watched him walk. There was something in his gait that snagged her attention before her mind could name it—he walked like a man moving through a house he knew blindfolded, past furniture he could navigate by memory even in the dark. He nodded to people in the pews. And here was the strange thing: they nodded back. Not all of them. But enough. An old woman in purple touched his wrist as he passed. A man near the front—Mara recognized him vaguely, someone from the old neighborhood, a face from church suppers—gripped his shoulder briefly, two beats, and let go.

He reached the front. He stood before the casket for a long moment, longer than politeness required, his head bowed, the brown-paper parcel clutched against his chest. From where she sat, Mara could see his lips move. Not praying, she thought. Or not only praying. Talking. The way you talk to someone you have talked to ten thousand times, the way the dead can still be addressed if you've had enough practice.

Then he turned around and embraced Aunt Funke.

Funke went stiff as a fence post. Mara saw it happen—saw her aunt's arms rise halfway and hover, saw her face perform the terrible arithmetic of a woman deciding, in public, in real time, whether to return an embrace or refuse one. She settled for patting his back twice, the way you might calm a large dog.

"Brother Desmond," the man said, loud enough that the eulogy-reader faltered. "I'm so sorry. I'm so sorry she's gone."

"Yes," Funke said. "Thank you."

And then he said the thing. Mara heard it the way you hear a glass crack in another room—quietly, unmistakably, with the whole shape of the break arriving before the sound does.

"How is the family bearing it? All of you—all of you *Oyelaran-Adeyemis*—she spoke of you every single day."

The name dropped into the sanctuary like a stone into a well.

*Oyelaran-Adeyemi.* Hyphenated. Double-barreled. A name with a hinge in the middle, joining two halves that had no business being joined—or every business, depending on which story you'd been told. Mara felt it land in her own chest with a physical weight, because she knew that name. Of course she knew it. It had existed at the edges of her childhood the way a scar exists at the edge of a family photo—visible if you looked, unmentioned if you didn't. Her grandmother's full legal name, the one on the deed to the house on Adeyemi Street, the one her mother had stripped down to plain *Oyelaran* the year they moved across town, the way you strip wallpaper off a wall before painting over it. Nobody used it. Nobody had used it in thirty years. It belonged to the old neighborhood the way the condemned buildings did: structurally unsound, scheduled for removal.

Around Mara, the congregation performed its silence. It was a skilled silence, practiced, the silence of people who had spent decades not saying a thing and had gotten very good at the not-saying. But Mara heard the seams in it. She saw Tunde's wife lean toward Tunde and whisper behind her program. She saw the old woman in purple nod slowly, as if the name were a hymn she'd forgotten she knew. And she saw Funke flinch.

It was not a large flinch. It was the smallest possible movement—a contraction around the eyes, a tightening of the jaw, the kind of thing you would miss entirely unless you had spent your life studying the faces of people who were hiding something, which Mara had, because she had grown up in a house where hiding things was the primary domestic art. Funke flinched the way a person flinches when a stranger says their childhood nickname in a supermarket. Not pain. Recognition. The flinch of a locked door hearing its own key rattle.

"I'm sorry," the man said again, to the room at large now, aware of the hush he'd caused but misreading it, or choosing to misread it. "Forgive me. I know—I know what I'm called. She called us that. Both of us." His voice caught on *both*, and he steadied himself against the end of the pew. "I'm Desmond. I stayed with her. At the house. On Adeyemi Street."

"The halfway house," someone said, not a question.

"Yes, ma'am. Thirty-eight years ago. I was fourteen." He smiled, and the smile cracked something open in his face—grief and gratitude fused into an expression too complicated for a funeral, too honest for a church. "She took me in when nobody else would have me. She was—" He stopped. Looked at the casket. Looked at the photograph above it, at the firm mouth and the gold cross. "There isn't a word big enough," he said finally. "I've been looking for one for three days. There isn't one."

The Lagos cousin resumed her eulogy, reading with the strained brightness of a woman determined to get to the end of her paragraph. The moment passed, the way moments do in churches, absorbed into liturgy. The man—Desmond—slid into an empty pew near the front, the brown parcel balanced on his knees, and did not look at anyone else for the rest of the service.

Except once.

Mara felt it before she confirmed it, the way you feel sunlight move onto the back of your neck. A warmth, a specificity, a direction. She kept her eyes on the order of service—on the psalm, on the printed dates bracketing her grandmother's life like bookends—and let her attention slide sideways, down the angle of her own shoulder, across the aisle, to the gray ill-fitting suit.

He was looking at her.

Not glancing. Looking. With the unhurried, deliberate attention of a man confirming something he had already suspected, or searching for something he had been promised he would find. His eyes were pale blue and red-rimmed and absolutely steady, and they held hers for three seconds—long enough for courtesy to expire, long enough for the look to become a statement—and in those three seconds Mara understood, with the cold clarity that sometimes arrives in crowded rooms, that he had come to this funeral for her. Or not for her, precisely. For something that ran through her. Something he expected to find written on her face the way the hyphenated name was written somewhere in her blood.

She looked away first. She hated that she looked away first.

* * *

The burial was quick, the ground already half dug, the cemetery perched on the hill above the old district so that from the graveside you could see the neighborhood spread out below—the water towers, the church steeples, the cranes. So many cranes now. Demolition equipment parked in lots like patient animals. From up here the district looked less like a place than like a sentence someone had decided to strike through, and Mara stood at the edge of the mourners while the pastor read the committal and watched a bulldozer, absurdly, go about its work two streets down, chewing at the corner of a block she had walked a thousand times.

Desmond stood at the other edge of the group, alone, the brown parcel still under his arm. He had not brought flowers. He had not signed the guest book, or if he had, he'd done it in handwriting too small to read. He stood the way Mara stood—at the perimeter, inside the grief and outside the family—and she recognized the posture with an uncomfortable jolt. It was her own posture. It was the stance of a person who belongs to a thing without belonging to the people who own it.

Afterward, at the reception hall—jollof rice and fried plantain and pound cake, the smell of the old neighborhood reproduced faithfully in a rented room—Mara stationed herself by the coffee urn and watched him work the crowd. He knew more people than she did. That was the humiliating arithmetic of it. Seven years away, and a white man in a borrowed suit knew her grandmother's world better than she did: he knew which deacon had driven Iya Agnes to dialysis, he knew the name of the woman who'd run the corner store, he knew the nicknames, the feuds, the recipes. He laughed at the right places in other people's stories. He touched shoulders. He was, Mara realized, doing what she had spent her entire adult life refusing to do: staying.

Funke found her at the urn.

"You're leaving," Funke said. It wasn't a question. Funke had a gift for stating Mara's intentions before Mara had finished forming them, a gift that had made adolescence unbearable and was, apparently, immortal.

"After the cake," Mara said.

"He shouldn't have said the name." Funke poured coffee she didn't drink, holding the cup like a prop. "In there. In front of everyone. Whatever he thinks he is to her, that wasn't the place."

"What is he to her?"

Funke's cup paused midair. "A boy she helped. One of dozens. Half this city ate at her table."

"That's not what I asked."

"Mara." Her aunt set the cup down. Up close, in the reception-hall light, Funke looked older than she had at the church, or maybe just more tired, the tiredness no longer performing competence. "You've been gone seven years. You don't get to come back and start asking questions like you paid rent here the whole time. There are things in that house that are handled. Were handled. Will stay handled."

"Handled," Mara repeated. The word tasted scorched, though she couldn't yet have said why.

Her aunt's mouth opened—to answer, or to end the conversation—and at that moment a shadow fell across the coffee urn, and Desmond was there, holding a paper plate with a slice of pound cake he clearly had no intention of eating.

"Sister," he said to Funke, gently, and then turned to Mara, and up close she could see that his eyes were wetter than they'd been in the church, that he was holding himself together with visible effort, thread by thread. "I'm sorry to interrupt. I've been trying to find my courage all afternoon." He shifted the plate to his left hand and extended his right. "Desmond Hale. Your grandmother was—" He stopped. Started again. "I need to talk to you. When the time is right. Not today. Today is for her." He reached into his jacket with his free hand and produced a business card, soft-edged, slightly bent, as if it had been carried in that pocket for years waiting for an occasion. "Whenever you're ready. Whenever you want to know."

Mara took the card. Their fingers didn't touch, but the card was warm from his pocket, and that warmth felt like a message.

On it, beneath a phone number and an address on a street she hadn't thought about in years, were four typed words:

*OYELARAN-ADEYEMI FAMILY ARCHIVE.*

Behind her, she heard Funke's breath catch—a small, sharp sound, the sound of a locked door hearing its key again—and when Mara looked up from the card, Desmond was watching her with those steady red-rimmed eyes, and he nodded once, almost imperceptibly, the way you nod to someone across a room who you both know is carrying the same secret.

"I'll leave the light on," he said, and walked away through the crowd, the brown parcel under his arm, toward the door, toward the hill, toward the condemned district where the bulldozers were already eating.
