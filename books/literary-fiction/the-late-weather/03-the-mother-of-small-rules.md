## 3. The Mother of Small Rules

The summons came the way Odile Fairweather's summons always came: not as a request but as a weather event, something you dressed for and braced against and afterward found yourself describing to other people in the tone of a survivor.

*Thursday. Four o'clock. Don't be late; I haven't got late left.*

That was the whole message, delivered by Casper over the phone in his careful middle-brother voice, the voice he used when he was delivering bad news wrapped in logistics. Felix had stood in the staff room of the library at seven in the morning, still smelling of the night's dust and old paper, holding a mug of coffee he'd forgotten to drink, and heard himself say, "I know," as if anyone had asked him anything.

He spent the intervening days doing what he always did with dread: shelving it. There was a place for it on the night shift, between the 700s and the man who came in every Tuesday to read books about lighthouses without ever checking one out. Dread kept well in the dark stacks. It didn't mildew. It just waited, patient as a bookmark.

On Thursday afternoon he took the bus across the city instead of the train, because the bus took longer, and because there was a version of him — a younger version, a version who had once believed that time could be negotiated with — who thought that arriving late to a thing might soften it. He knew better now. His mother had been softening nothing for seventy-one years.

* * *

Odile lived in a third-floor walk-up above a shuttered piano shop in the north of the city, an apartment she had moved into after selling the family house with the ferocity of a woman burning a bridge behind her. She had never explained why she sold it. None of them had ever dared ask. The apartment smelled, as it always had, of bergamot and camphor and underneath both something faintly medicinal, the smell of a body being maintained against its own objections.

She was in her chair by the window when he let himself in, the chair she had claimed like territory years ago, angled so she could see the street and the door at once. She had been beautiful the way winter is beautiful — all structure, no mercy — and even now, folded into a cardigan the color of wet slate, with the oxygen line running from the tank beside her chair to her nose like a tether she pretended not to notice, she retained the architecture of command. Her hands were the tell. They lay in her lap too still, spotted, the knuckles swollen like knots in old wood. Hands that had ruled four children and one drowned town's worth of opinion, gone quiet.

"You're early," she said, which was her way of saying *thank you*, which was her way of saying nothing at all.

"The bus was fast."

"Nothing about you has ever been fast." She looked him over the way she looked over everything: inventory first, affection later, if at all. "Sit. You look like you sleep in a library."

"I work in a library."

"At night. Like a bat." She waved at the teapot on the side table. "Pour. Two cups. I'm allowed tea again, the doctor says, as if it were ever the doctor's tea."

He poured. The ritual of it steadied him — the weight of the pot, the small percussion of lid against rim. This was how they had always spoken, he and his mother: through objects, through errands, through the grammar of things done for each other instead of said. He set her cup in her reach and sat across from her, and for a while neither of them drank, and the radiator ticked, and somewhere below a shutter creaked in wind that hadn't reached the upper floors yet.

"Imogen called," Odile said at last.

"Did she."

"She wants me to meet a hospice nurse. A *consultant*." The word came out of her mouth like a pit spat from fruit. "As though I were a property to be assessed."

"Aren't you?" Felix said, before he could stop himself.

Her eyes came around to him slowly, and he saw the flash in them — the old lightning, undimmed by illness — and then, worse, he saw it bank itself down into something almost like humor. "You always did have Imogen's mouth and none of her nerve."

"She'd say the same about me."

"Imogen doesn't say much of anything anymore. That's what worries me." Odile lifted her tea, drank with visible effort, set it down. Her hand trembled on the return journey and she covered the tremor by smoothing her skirt, a sleight of hand she'd performed so long it had become invisible even to herself. "Casper tells me you're still doing nights."

"Fifteen years in April."

"Fifteen years." She repeated it flatly, weighing it. "A grown man, shelving books in the dark. Your father would have said—"

"My father would have said whatever you told him to say."

The silence that followed had teeth. Felix watched his mother decide, visibly, whether to spend energy on the wound or save it. She saved it. She was conserving everything now; he understood that. A failing body teaches a certain economy, and Odile had always been a natural economist of feeling — spending only where the return was guaranteed.

"There are arrangements to discuss," she said.

"Funeral arrangements?"

"Don't be morbid, it's tedious. Arrangements." She let the word sit there, capitalized, load-bearing. "Regarding the estate."

Felix set his cup down with more care than the gesture required. "You've made a will. Lawyers make wills. That's the entire point of lawyers."

"My lawyer makes my will. I make my terms." She turned her face toward the window. Below, a delivery truck was double-parked, and its driver was arguing with no one. "There are documents. In Lowmark. In the house."

The word *Lowmark* landed in the room like a stone through glass. Felix felt the old cold come up through the floor of himself — the salt smell of it, the foghorn at three in the morning, the cannery whistle, Theo's laugh going out ahead of him down the dock stairs. He had not been back in fifteen years. Neither, he was fairly sure, had Ada. The house had been sealed like a tomb nobody would fund, kept by a caretaker Odile paid and never visited, and the whole family had agreed — without ever agreeing, the way families do — that Lowmark was a country they had collectively renounced citizenship in.

"There's nothing in that house but water damage and ghosts," he said.

"There's a desk."

His heart did something unhelpful. "Mother."

"In the study. The writing desk. It's locked, and I have the key, and when I'm done with it, someone will need to open it, and it won't be Casper, because Casper opens everything and understands nothing, and it won't be Imogen, because—" She stopped. The sentence went where her sentences about Imogen always went: into a cul-de-sac of unfinished judgment. "It should be you."

"Why?"

"Because you read," she said, as though this were an answer, and perhaps to her it was. "Because you finish things. Because you're the only one of my children who ever learned to wait."

"I waited because you taught me that wanting things gets them taken away."

The words were out before he could weigh them, and this time she did spend the energy. She turned back to him fully, and the oxygen line swung, and her eyes — gray, his eyes, the family's eyes, the color of the sea off Lowmark in November — fixed on him with an attention that made him feel eleven years old and caught at something.

"Who took things from you, Felix?"

"You know who."

"Say it."

"You did." His voice stayed level. Fifteen years of night shifts teach a man to keep his voice level in the dark. "Everything I wanted, you found a reason it was wrong. The university. The girl. The—"

"The girl." Something moved across her face, quick as a fish under ice. "Ada."

"I didn't say a name."

"You didn't have to. You never had to. That was always your problem." She drew a breath that cost her; he heard the effort in it, the rattle beneath, and hated how much he cared. "You loved her the way you love those books of yours. From the shelf. Never taking one down."

"That's not fair."

"No," she agreed, unexpectedly. "It isn't. Very little is." She closed her eyes for a moment, and in that unguarded second she looked suddenly, shockingly mortal — a small old woman in a chair, tired all the way down. When she opened them again the general was back, but thinner, like paint worn to primer. "Theo is dead fourteen years. You've mourned him properly, better than any of us. And she's mourned him properly. And the two of you have spent fourteen years being *proper* at each other across a city, and you want to stand there and tell me nothing was lost."

"We were never—"

"I know what you were never. I was there. I was there the summer before the boat, watching you watch her across my own dinner table, and I was there after the funeral, watching you carry her coat like it was made of glass." Her voice had risen and now it fell, deliberately, the way she lowered flags. "I am dying, Felix. Do you understand what that means? It means I no longer have time for the polite version of anything."

He looked at his hands. They were shaking slightly, and he folded them, another inherited gesture, another sleight of hand.

"What's in the desk?" he asked.

"Terms."

"Whose terms?"

"Mine." She smiled, and it was the smile he remembered from childhood verdicts — the smile that preceded sentences. "You'll hate them. That's how you'll know they're mine."

"Odile," he said, using her name the way the three of them did when pushed past *Mother*, "whatever scheme you've built in that house, whatever you've locked in that desk — we won't do it. Whatever it is. Casper will fight it, Imogen will burn it, and I will shelve it in the dark and forget it. We learned from the best."

For a long moment she said nothing. The radiator ticked. Down on the street the delivery driver gave up and drove away, and the quiet that followed was enormous.

Then Odile Fairweather began, very softly, to laugh — a dry, rusted sound, two notes, no more, ending in a cough she rode out with her chin up like a woman refusing to be seen drowning.

"Oh, my boy," she said, when she had breath again. "You think I don't know that? You think I built it without knowing exactly what each of you would do?" She reached out and, with the last of her traveling strength, laid her ruined hand over his. Her skin was cool and papery and it broke his heart in a place he'd boarded up years ago. "I've known you all your life. I built the lock to fit the key I already put in your pocket."

He pulled his hand free — gently, gently; even now, gently — and stood.

"I have a shift," he said.

"It's three in the afternoon."

"I open early."

At the door he stopped, because some part of him — the part that had carried her coat, the part that finished things — made him turn. She was watching him from her chair, small against the window light, and for one unguarded instant her face held something he had never once seen on it in forty-odd years and recognized anyway, because he'd been wearing its twin in mirrors his whole life.

"Mother," he said. "What happens if we just refuse? Whatever it is. What happens then?"

She considered him. Outside, the wind had finally climbed to the third floor and pressed at the glass.

"Then Lowmark goes to men with bulldozers," she said, "and everything in that house goes with it. Including the truth." She picked up her tea as though the conversation were concluded, as though she had not just detonated something in the center of him. "Four o'clock Thursday next week. Don't be late. I haven't got late left."

* * *

He walked. He didn't remember deciding to walk, but his feet had apparently convened their own committee, and they carried him west through the afternoon crowds, past the market stalls folding up, past the ferry terminal where the dawn-shift workers were streaming out in their orange vests — and he slowed, without meaning to, scanning the gate the way he always scanned it, the way a compass scans north, and there she was.

Ada Voss, coming off shift, hair tied back, hi-vis jacket unzipped over a sweater he recognized because he'd catalogued its repairs over the years like entries in an archive. She was laughing at something a coworker said, and the laugh was the same laugh, and the sight of it hit him the way his mother's words had hit him an hour ago: *you loved her the way you love those books. From the shelf.*

She saw him. Of course she saw him; she always had. Her hand rose halfway — a wave, aborted, converted into adjusting the strap of her bag — and he raised his own hand and did the same thing, and they passed within ten feet of each other on the terminal concourse, and she said, "Felix," and he said, "Ada," and the two syllables crossed in the air between them like ships in fog, signaling correctly, passing safely, carrying nothing.

He walked on toward the library with his mother's voice in his ears — *I built the lock to fit the key I already put in your pocket* — and it was only three blocks later, standing at the crosswalk with the light changing twice around him, that he understood what she had actually told him.

Not the terms. Not the desk. Not the documents waiting in the drowned house where his brother died.

What she had told him was that she had known. All those dinners, all those summers, all those years of him practicing restraint like a second language — she had known, and she had said nothing, and now, at the end, she was planning to make him pay for the silence. Or reward it. With Odile, he thought, stepping off the curb as the light changed a third time, the two had never been distinguishable.

Behind him, at the terminal, Ada Voss stood a moment longer than she needed to, looking after a man walking away at the exact speed of someone fleeing.
