## 12. The House That Kept Score

The key was warm from Ada's hand by the time they reached the door.

She had carried it the whole way from the car — four hundred meters of gravel and salt wind, Felix a half-step behind her with the flashlight he'd insisted on buying at the last open shop in town, as if the house might not have electricity, as if it might not have anything. The fog had come in off the water while they drove, thickening by degrees until Lowmark existed only in pieces: the cannery's rusted spine, the chapel's leaning cross, then nothing, then the house, materializing out of the white like something surfacing to breathe.

It looked smaller than she remembered. Houses did that, she supposed, when you stopped being the person who had lived in them.

"You don't have to go first," Felix said.

"I know." She fit the key into the lock anyway. It turned with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and the door swung inward on air that had been holding its breath for six years.

* * *

Dust sheets over everything. That was the first thing — not the smell, though the smell came next, camphor and cold ash and the particular mineral damp of a house that had given up arguing with the sea. Odile must have sent someone before the end, or perhaps years ago, to throw sheets over the furniture like a stage crew striking a set that would never be struck properly. The shapes underneath were familiar and wrong at once: the armchair where Theo's father had sat every evening of Ada's married life, now a pale hump; the long table where they'd eaten Sunday dinners, a sleeping animal; the piano, sheeted so completely it looked like a body under a shroud.

Ada stood in the doorway and let the house look at her.

Houses kept score. She had learned that young, in her mother's apartment where the water stains on the ceiling mapped every winter her father didn't come back. This house had been keeping score longer than she'd known it. Every scratch on the banister was a Christmas tree dragged inside. The darkened rectangle on the wallpaper above the sideboard was where Odile had hung the portrait of Felix's grandfather, taken down after some argument no one would explain to Ada, its ghost still hanging there, still disapproving. The third stair creaked. The kitchen window stuck unless you lifted it from the left. The floorboard outside the bathroom groaned in E-flat, and Theo used to play it with his heel when he got up in the night, deliberately, so she'd know which direction he was going.

She walked in. Her feet remembered the E-flat board and avoided it, and then, absurdly, she went back and stepped on it just to hear it groan.

"Ada?" Felix's voice from behind her, careful.

"Testing," she said. "Making sure it still works."

"It works."

The coat was on its hook.

She saw it before she meant to — the hallway ran straight back from the door to the kitchen, and there on the pegs beside the door, third hook from the left, hung Theo's oilskin jacket, dark green gone almost black with age, the collar up the way he always left it because he said a collar that wouldn't stand up on its own wasn't worth owning. Six years of coastal damp should have rotted it. Should have slid it off the hook and pooled it on the floor like something dead. Instead it hung there with an insolent patience, sleeves slightly swaying in the draft from the open door, as if he'd only just stepped out to check the mooring lines and would be back in ten minutes wanting tea, wanting to tell her about the weather coming in, wanting —

"Ada."

"I see it, Felix."

He came up beside her. He didn't touch the coat and he didn't touch her, and she understood both refusals as forms of kindness. He was looking at it the way you look at a grave marker you haven't visited: with guilt, with relief that someone else has been keeping the flowers watered.

"He'd have hated this," she said. "Being left hanging. He always said coats belong to houses, not closets."

Felix made a sound that was almost a laugh. "He said that?"

"Constantly. He'd hang his coat on whatever was nearest. Fence posts. Other people's chairs. I found it once on the head of the lighthouse statue." She heard herself talking, steady, practical, the voice she used at the ferry terminal when the fog rolled in and passengers demanded answers about schedules no one could promise. *The crossing is delayed. We'll update you shortly.* A voice for other people's waiting.

The desk was at the end of the hall.

She hadn't let herself think of it directly on the drive — had thought around it, the way you think around a tooth. It stood against the wall beneath the window where the light came in best, Odile's writing desk, walnut, brass-handled, the drop-front locked. She had watched Odile sit at it a thousand times, pen scratching, letters going out to lawyers and cousins and the editors of small journals, and never once, in all the years Ada had been in this family, seen it open when Odile wasn't sitting there. After the accident, Odile had locked it. Ada knew because she'd tried it — once, drunk on funeral sherry, needing somewhere to put her rage, and the drawer front hadn't given, and Odile had appeared in the doorway and said, without heat, *Not that one, dear,* and Ada had understood that the sentence contained an entire continent she wasn't invited to explore.

And now here was the key in her pocket, and here was the desk, and between them stood six years and a dead woman's instructions.

"Not yet," she said, and was surprised to hear it aloud.

Felix nodded. "Not yet."

* * *

They worked through the ground floor the way archaeologists work, or the way people defuse things. Felix pulled dust sheets and folded them into white squares, methodical, and Ada followed with the windows, fighting the catches, letting the sea air in to argue with the camphor. Neither of them spoke much. Speaking felt like it would cost more than it bought.

But the house kept handing her things.

The kitchen: the blue enamel kettle still on the stove, and she could hear — actually hear, in the skull behind her ears — the sound it made coming to boil, that rising whistle Theo had claimed sounded like his mother calling him home. The calendar on the wall had stopped at June of that year, someone's handwriting — Imogen's, she thought — marking a birthday on the seventeenth. The summer before the accident had been the good one. That was the cruelty of it, the thing she could never make anyone who hadn't lived it understand: the last summer was the best one. Theo had been lighter that year. He'd laughed easily. He'd started swimming again in the mornings, out past the breakwater, and come back dripping and pleased with himself like a boy. He'd said, twice, in words she could still reproduce exactly, *I feel like something's about to change, Ads. Something good.*

The seventeenth of June was marked on the calendar. The accident was in October. Between those two dates lay the whole of what she'd lost, and the house held all of it in its rooms like water held in cupped hands, leaking slowly through the years but never emptying.

She opened the cupboard above the sink and found the mugs. Their mugs. Hers was the chipped one with the wading bird, the glaze worn pale where her thumb had gone for eleven years. She took it down and held it and did not cry, and the not-crying was the hardest physical labor of her life, harder than hauling lines, harder than the dawn shifts, harder than standing at a graveside in rain while strangers sang.

"You're doing the thing," Felix said gently from the doorway.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you hold everything perfectly still so it can't hurt you."

"And you're doing the thing where you watch me do it instead of saying anything true."

He considered that. Fair, his face said. He leaned against the doorframe, and the afternoon light through the window caught the gray at his temples, and she thought — not for the first time, but with new sharpness, here in this house where thinking such things was dangerous — that he had gotten handsome the way harbors get beautiful: by enduring. Fifteen years she'd known him. Fifteen years of Sunday dinners and funerals and ferry-platform waves, and in all that time he had never once asked her for anything, and she had spent most of those years telling herself that this restraint was respect, was decency, was the mark of a good man who loved his brother's memory too much to —

To what. To want. She couldn't even finish the thought indoors. Some sentences were outdoor sentences.

"There's a room upstairs I've never been in," Felix said. "Odile kept it locked my whole childhood. Casper swore there were tax records in it. Imogen swore it was where Odile wrote the letters to the newspaper."

"What did you swear?"

"That it was where she kept us. All of us. Every version." He smiled faintly. "Children are idiots."

"Children are accurate," Ada said. "Go on. I'll finish down here."

He hesitated at the stairs — she saw him weigh it, saw him decide to give her the room rather than the company, another of his refusals that read as kindness — and then his footsteps went up, avoiding the third stair out of old habit, and the house settled around her alone.

* * *

She found herself in the parlor, in front of the sheeted piano, and she pulled the sheet off because she had to know if it was real.

Real. Odile's piano, the one nobody played except at Christmas, when Imogen would hammer out carols with the confidence of the untrained. Ada sat on the bench — the bench was cold through her jeans — and lifted the fallboard. Inside, on the music stand, someone had left a piece of sheet music open. Chopin, a nocturne, dog-eared. And in the margin, in pencil, in Odile's narrow slanted hand: *Theo, slower here. You rush when you're happy.*

You rush when you're happy.

Ada pressed her palm flat against the keys without sounding them. Theo had played? Theo, who claimed music gave him hives, who sang only in the boat and only badly and only when he thought no one could hear? When — and the question arrived whole, unwelcome, wearing its own answer — when had Odile sat at this piano teaching her eldest son a nocturne, and why had Ada never known, and what else about the man she'd married had been conducted in rooms she wasn't in?

Grief, she was learning, was architectural. It wasn't a wave, whatever the pamphlets said. It was a building. You moved through it room by room, and each room had been furnished without your consent, and some rooms you could enter and some stayed locked, and the whole structure had been built by other people — the dead, the living, everyone who'd ever touched the walls — and you inherited it whether you signed for it or not. This house was her grief built at full scale, walkable, and she was moving through it now touching the load-bearing walls, testing which ones would hold.

The light was going. Through the parlor window the fog had thinned and the harbor showed itself: the marina, mostly empty, the breakwater, the black skeleton of the old pier, and beyond it, on its rock, the lighthouse, dark, its gallery rail furred with gull lime and salt bloom. Six years dark. The town had petitioned twice to have it automated; the funding had fallen through twice. So it stood there like a held breath, and Ada looked at it and thought of the second item on Odile's list — *restore the lighthouse archive* — and wondered what an archive was doing in a lighthouse, and what it had been keeping, and for whom.

Everything in this house was a document. She was beginning to understand that. The coat was a document. The calendar was a document. The pencil note in the Chopin was a document, and the E-flat floorboard, and the chipped mug, and the locked desk most of all — the desk was the master document, the index, the place where the house kept its accounts, and Odile had sealed it and then, in death, handed Ada the key like a dare.

*Come find out what I knew,* the key said. *Come find out what I did with what I knew.*

From upstairs came the sound of Felix opening something heavy — a trunk, maybe, or a door swollen shut finally giving way. Then silence. Then his footsteps crossing the floor above her head, slow, stopping.

Then, faintly, the sound of a man sitting down on the floor of a locked room in his dead mother's house.

Ada stood in the parlor with her hand still on the silent keys. She could go up. She could knock, or not knock, and sit on the floor beside him in whatever Odile had kept up there, two orphans of the same architecture, and say something true at last in a house that had heard so little of it.

Or she could go to the desk.

Her hand went to her pocket. The key was there, warm again, warmer than the room, warmer than it had any right to be. Through the window the lighthouse stood black against the last of the light, and below it, far out, the sea was already working itself up for weather — she could read it the way she'd learned to read it at the terminal, the changed color, the wrongness in the swell — the season turning early, storms coming ahead of schedule, the late weather arriving late or early or whenever it pleased now, keeping no one's rules.

Neither, she thought, should we.

She crossed the hall. The desk waited under the window, its brass handle dull with six years of salt air, and behind her, upstairs, Felix was saying something to the empty room in a voice too low to carry — one word, maybe two, a name, possibly hers, possibly his brother's — and Ada fitted the key to the lock, and her hand was shaking, and she did not stop it.

The lock turned.

The drop-front swung down toward her like a hand opening, like a mouth, and inside, in the lamplight-gray of the dying day, she saw paper. Stacks of it. Journals. Bundles tied with string. And on top, squared neatly, addressed in Odile's hand to the two of them together — *For F. & A., who will not thank me* — an envelope thick enough to hold a great many pages, or a very few terrible ones.

Downstairs, the front door, which she had closed behind them, drifted open on its own weight, and the sea air moved through the house, lifting the sleeve of Theo's green coat in a slow wave, as if the dead were gesturing: *go on, then. Read.*
