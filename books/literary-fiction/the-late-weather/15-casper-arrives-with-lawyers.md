## 15. Casper Arrives With Lawyers

Casper Fairweather had learned everything he knew about power from watching his mother set a table.

Odile placed knives at angles that suggested consequence. She seated the difficult relatives where the light was unkind. She could make a person feel like a guest in their own childhood home by handing them the wrong fork, and she could do it smiling, and she could do it while loving you — that was the part Casper had never managed to explain to his wives, both of them gone now, both of them having used the word *suffocating* once each, carefully, like setting down something breakable.

So when Casper arrived at the Lowmark house on a Tuesday in late September with two lawyers and a folder the color of old money, he chose his seat first. He took the chair at the head of the kitchen table, the one Odile had died sitting near, and he arranged the folder square to the table's edge, and he waited for his brother and Ada Voss to come down the stairs like children summoned by a smell of burning.

The lawyers were named Prentiss and Okafor. Prentiss was older, gray at the temples, with the patient face of a man who had watched many families eat themselves. Okafor was younger and carried a laptop like a shield. Casper had found them through a golf acquaintance in the city, and he had paid the retainer out of the account that used to be his daughter's college fund before his third business failed, though no one here would ever know that, and he intended to keep it that way.

He heard them before he saw them — Felix's low voice, Ada's quieter one, the particular rhythm of two people who had spent fifteen years not finishing sentences with each other. Casper straightened his cuffs. He had dressed for the occasion: charcoal suit, no tie, because a tie said *litigation* and he wanted this to feel, at least for the first ten minutes, like family.

It did not feel like family for even the first ten seconds.

Felix came into the kitchen still holding a paint scraper, flecked white at the knuckles, and stopped when he saw the table. Ada came in behind him carrying two mugs of coffee, saw the lawyers, and set the mugs down on the counter with a small deliberate click, as if returning weapons to an armory she'd hoped was closed.

"Cas," Felix said. "You didn't call."

"I don't call ahead for family business." Casper gestured. "Sit. Please. This doesn't need to be unpleasant."

"Then why does it look like it?" Felix leaned the scraper against the doorframe. He hadn't shaved. There was plaster dust in his hair, and Casper thought, with a bitterness that surprised him, that his little brother looked happier filthy in this drowning house than he had ever looked clean anywhere else.

Prentiss opened the folder. "Mr. Fairweather. Ms. Voss. I'll be brief." He wasn't brief; men who promised brevity rarely were. He laid out the estate's structure in the flat voice of someone reading coordinates: the marriage clause, the one-year window now ticking past its second month, the developer interest in the waterfront parcel, the valuation figures that made Casper's chest tighten with a hunger he recognized from auctions. Then he reached the part Casper had rehearsed on the drive down, three times, in the rearview mirror.

"My client wishes to contest the will's validity on several grounds," Prentiss said. "Chief among them, questions regarding undue influence exercised upon the decedent during her final months, and the circumstances under which certain parties gained access to her and to these premises."

Casper heard it happen — the sentence landing, the silence after it, the way sound behaves in a room where something has just broken. He kept his eyes on the folder. That had been the plan. Let the lawyer say it; stay out of the blast radius.

But Ada looked at him. That was the thing about Ada Voss that had unnerved him since she was nineteen years old and walked into Theo's life like weather: she didn't flinch, didn't perform hurt, didn't give you anything to work with except attention. She looked at Casper the way she used to look at tide charts, and she said, quietly, "Which parties?"

"Ada—" Felix started.

"Which parties, Mr. Prentiss? I'd like it said plainly. My husband is dead eleven years and my mother-in-law is dead six weeks, and I have been accused of nothing yet, so let's hear the accusation while it's fresh."

Okafor shifted. Prentiss, to his credit, did not blink. "My client's position is that the marriage clause reflects arrangements discussed between yourself and the decedent prior to its drafting, and that your continued residence here—"

"My *continued residence*—" Ada laughed once, without any humor in it at all, a sound like a door slamming in another room. "I arrived nine days ago. Ask the neighbors. Ask the fog. I'm sleeping in my dead son's— " She stopped. Casper saw her hand flatten against the counter, steadying herself on the house itself.

"She means she's been living here while the will is pending," Casper said, "which is precisely the appearance of a problem."

"The appearance." Felix turned from the doorway. Until then he'd been very still, the way he got still, the way their mother used to say he went *library-quiet*, but now the stillness had heat coming off it. "Say what you're actually accusing her of, Casper. Out loud. In front of the hired help."

"They're not the hired help, they're—"

"You brought lawyers to your dead mother's kitchen and sat in her chair. Say it."

And there it was, the moment Casper had driven four hours to avoid and had engineered anyway, because some part of him — the part that had been eight years old when Felix was born and had never entirely forgiven the world for producing him — wanted it said. Wanted it dragged into the light so it could be disproven, or proven, or anything other than the shapeless dread he'd carried since Wren put that key on the funeral-home table and called them *the Fairweather-Vosses* like reading names off a monument.

"My position," Casper said, each word placed like Odile placed knives, "is that Ada has spent fifteen years positioned exactly where a woman would need to be standing if she intended to inherit this family's entire estate. She married Theo. Theo drowned. She never remarried, never left, stayed close enough to burn, and now conveniently the will surfaces requiring exactly one thing from her — a wedding she's had fifteen years to want."

The room went very quiet. Outside, gulls argued over the roofline of the cannery. The refrigerator hummed its idiot hum.

"That's what you drove here to say." Felix's voice had dropped into a register Casper had heard maybe twice in his life, both times before something got broken permanently. "In front of her."

"In front of everyone. That's rather the point of—"

"Fifteen years." Felix crossed the kitchen, unhurried, terrifying. "She spent those fifteen years raising a grief the size of a house. She sent flowers when Imogen's divorce went through. She drove four hours when your daughter was in the hospital — do you remember that, Casper? Because I remember it. I remember her sitting in that corridor all night because you couldn't leave the waiting room, you were too busy being angry at the doctors. She came to Mom's birthday every single year knowing Mom was cruel to her, knowing Mom blamed her for Theo, ate the terrible cake, thanked her for it. Fifteen years, and you think she was *positioning*."

"Felix." Ada's voice, low. "Don't."

"She's right, don't—" Casper stood up too fast, his chair shrieking against the linoleum, and the ugliness rose in him all at once, the whole sedimentary layers of it: the money gone, the marriages gone, the sense that everyone else had been given a life while he'd been handed a ledger. "Don't defend her like that. Don't stand there with dust in your hair defending her like it's *your* name she'd be taking."

"It isn't about the name."

"Isn't it?" Casper heard himself go on, helpless now, past the last exit. "Because Wren said Fairweather-Voss, and you know what I thought? I thought, of course. Of course Mom wrote them into a contract together. Of course she did. She always liked Ada better than any of us, and Felix — Felix has been in love with his brother's wife since before the casket was closed, since before the boat, maybe since the wedding, maybe since before the wedding, and every one of us has watched him orbit her for fifteen years like we're all too polite to say the word—"

"*Casper.*"

"—so forgive me if I decline to pretend this clause fell out of the sky. Forgive me if I notice that the two people who benefit are the two people already half-married in everything but paperwork, grieving in stereo, restoring lighthouses together like a couple in a—"

Felix hit him.

Not hard. That was somehow worse — a short, closed-fisted blow to the shoulder, more shove than strike, the kind of blow brothers exchange at fourteen, and it knocked Casper back into Odile's chair, and the folder slid off the table and fanned across the linoleum like a losing hand. Okafor made a noise. Prentiss did not move at all, which told Casper the man had seen much worse than this and billed by the hour regardless.

For a moment nobody breathed.

Then Felix stepped back, looked at his own fist as if it belonged to a stranger, and said, in a voice gone suddenly hoarse and small, "That's not — that's not what this is. That's not what it's ever been."

And Ada said nothing.

That was the worst of it, Casper thought afterward, driving back up the coast road with the folder empty and Prentiss's retainer burning a hole in his conscience: Ada said nothing. She stood by the counter with her coffee going cold and watched Felix deny it — deny *it*, whatever it was, the whole fifteen-year weather system of it — and her face did something complicated and then did nothing at all, and Casper, who had grown up fluent in the language of things unsaid, read the silence perfectly and hated himself for it.

Because it wasn't a denial. It was a woman listening to a man lie on her behalf, and letting him.

* * *

They regrouped at the Anchor, the bar at the top of the harbor road that had been failing gracefully since 1987. Prentiss drank club soda and took notes. Okafor ate chowder and said nothing, having learned in four hours that Fairweather family meetings were best survived like weather events — indoors, away from windows.

"You understand," Prentiss said, "that an undue influence claim requires evidence. What you gave us today was a scene. Scenes don't file well."

"What I gave you," Casper said, staring into his whiskey, "was the truth."

"Your version of it." Prentiss capped his pen. "Here's what I observed, for whatever a professional observation costs you. The librarian loves her. Has loved her long enough that it's structural, load-bearing. The widow — I've sat across from a hundred schemers, Mr. Fairweather, and they check the exits. She checked your brother. Twice. When he hit you, she checked *him*, not the door, not the money, him." The lawyer finished his soda. "If I were building your case, I'd need something better than a man defending a woman too honestly."

"Too honestly," Casper repeated.

"He revealed more than he meant to. People do that when the accusation touches something true. It cuts both ways." Prentiss gathered his papers. "Get me the codicil. Get me correspondence between your mother and her attorney. And get me something on the Voss woman that isn't a feeling, or we withdraw the challenge and you settle for whatever the probate court gives you, which will be less than the developers are offering, and we both know it."

After they left, Casper sat alone at the Anchor's sticky table while the afternoon went amber outside the window. The harbor below was doing its slow tidal breathing. Somewhere down there was the mooring where Theo's boat had been, the night it mattered, the night Casper had stood on this same dock with salt wind in his teeth and told his brother about the frayed line — told him plainly, twice, and watched Theo untie anyway, laughing, saying he had somewhere to be, saying it like a man with a secret worth drowning for.

Casper had carried that night for eleven years the way you carry a stone in a pocket: not heavy enough to put down, too heavy to forget. Everyone thought his anger at Ada was greed. Let them. Greed was simpler. Greed didn't require explaining that he had warned Theo, that Theo had smiled, that the line had parted in the black water like it had been waiting.

His phone buzzed. A text from Imogen, the first in weeks:

*heard you brought lawyers to the house. casper. tell me you didn't say something unforgivable.*

He typed and deleted three replies. Finally he wrote:

*too late.*

The reply came fast, three dots appearing and vanishing, appearing again — his sister composing herself at the far end of a signal, the way she'd been composing herself since the storm. Then, at last:

*I'm coming to Lowmark. There are things about that night none of you know. About what Theo was doing, and why. Don't contest anything until I get there. Please. Just — wait for me.*

Casper stared at the screen until it dimmed, then darkened, then showed him his own reflection: a man in a good suit, alone in a failing bar, holding a phone full of words that sounded almost like a confession.

Outside, the first clouds of the season were stacking up over the water, gray and organized, like something arriving on schedule after a long time deciding to.

*Wait for me,* she'd written.

Casper ordered another whiskey and tried to remember a single autumn, in his entire life, when anyone in his family had ever managed to wait for anything.
