## 5. First Words

The community hall smelled like a church that had given up on God and taken up carpentry instead. Sawdust, cold varnish, the ghost of a thousand coffee urns. Tomas Vinter had left the front door propped open with a brick, which struck me as either an invitation or a dare, and I stood in the doorway for a full minute deciding which.

"You came," he said, without turning around. He was at a folding table near the far wall, arranging papers with the deliberation of a man setting a table for royalty who might not show up. "I gave it fifty-fifty."

"Fifty-fifty?"

"Grief is unpredictable weather." He looked up then, and something crossed his face — not surprise exactly, more like recognition arriving late to its own appointment. "You have her way of standing in doorways. Like the room has to prove itself before you'll enter it."

I didn't answer that. I'd learned in the week since the funeral that people who said things like that about my mother were either lying or hurting, and often both, and either way the correct response was silence. Silence was the family currency. I was rich in it.

The hall was worse inside than out. Plywood over half the windows, chairs stacked in pyramids like some civilization had fled mid-meeting. A stage at one end with a curtain so faded it had gone from red to the pink of old skin. Somewhere above us water dripped with the patience of a thing that intended to be here long after we weren't.

"Sit anywhere," Tomas said. "Except there." He pointed at a folding chair directly across from him. "That's the student's chair. It's earned nothing yet."

"Where should I—"

"That one's fine." He gestured at a chair three feet to the left of the student's chair. Then he seemed to hear himself, and his ears went pink. "That was stupid. Sit in the chair. If you're going to sit, sit in the chair."

So I sat in the chair, and he sat across from me, and for a moment neither of us did anything, which apparently was part of the lesson too.

"I should tell you my terms," he said. "One hour a week. You do what I ask between lessons, even if it seems pointless. Especially if it seems pointless. And you don't ask me why I'm doing this until I'm ready to tell you."

"That's three terms."

"Yes. Well. The Kavreni never were good at arithmetic. We counted in fives because we had five fingers, and everything past five was 'many,' and many was enough." He said this lightly, but I noticed his hand had gone flat on the table, pressing down on the papers as if they might blow away indoors. "Do you accept?"

"I don't know what I'm accepting."

"No," he agreed. "Nobody ever does. That's rather the point of covenants." He slid a single sheet of paper across the table. On it was a word written in careful pencil: *vessunna*. "Today you learn one word. Just one. If you learn it properly, it will take the whole hour."

It didn't look like much. Seven letters, a double consonant, the kind of word you'd skim past in a novel.

"What does it mean?" I asked.

"'Return,'" Tomas said. "To come back. To go home again." He watched me write it down, copying the letters into my notebook with the mechanical obedience of a schoolgirl, and waited until I finished before he continued. "But here is what your dictionary will not tell you, and what every other language lets you get away with forgetting. In Kavreni, *vessunna* is incomplete by itself. It is grammatically impossible to say 'I return.' You must say what you return *from*. Not where you're going — everyone knows where they're going, that's the easy part, that's just geography. The grammar demands the place you left. The thing you turned your back on."

He let that sit. Outside, the dripping found a new rhythm, faster, like it had opinions about the lesson.

"So if I wanted to say 'I return,'" I said slowly, "I'd have to say... I return from—"

"From somewhere. From someone. From something you abandoned, or that abandoned you, or that you simply walked away from because walking away is what humans do instead of flying." His eyes were pale gray, the color of the harbor in February, and they did not blink enough. "The verb has a slot in it, Yara. A slot that must be filled. You cannot decline to fill it. There is no tense, no mood, no polite construction that lets you leave it empty. The language refuses."

"The language refuses," I repeated. "Like it's alive."

"Like it's honest," he said. "Which most people find worse."

* * *

We spent forty minutes on pronunciation, which I will spare both of us. My mouth was built for English and Norwegian, languages that keep their feelings in the front of the face. Kavreni kept its feelings further back, somewhere near the soft palate, in a region I apparently had never exercised. *Vessunna* came out of me as *vessuna*, then *vessuma*, then once, catastrophically, as *vesseña*, at which Tomas closed his eyes and murmured something that I suspected was a prayer or a curse and refused to translate.

"Again," he said, for perhaps the ninetieth time.

"*Vessunna.*"

"Better. Again."

"*Vessunna.*"

"Now use it. Full sentence. Tell me you are returning."

I opened my mouth. And here is the thing I had not anticipated, the trap laid not by Tomas but by the grammar itself, by seven letters and a rule: I could not finish the sentence. Because the slot was there, waiting, patient as the drip overhead, and everything I might put into it led back to the same place.

*I return from the house on Vardø Street, from the sealed letters, from the rooms I own and don't belong in.*

*I return from the hospital corridor where she told me not to come to the funeral, and I came anyway.*

*I return from her.*

My throat closed around all of them at once. I set down my pencil with exaggerated care, the way you do when the alternative is throwing it.

Tomas watched me struggle with the expression of a man who has seen this exact drowning before and knows better than to grab for the flailing arms. "You can't say it," he said. Not unkindly. Almost clinically.

"It's a stupid rule."

"It's the only honest rule in the language." He leaned back, and the folding chair complained under him. "Every Kavreni sentence about coming home is also a confession. You want to say *vessunna*, you pay the toll. You name what you left. My grandmother used to say the word was invented by women waiting on the pier — that no man got to say 'I'm back' without saying what he'd done with the years, because the women had counted every day of them." He smiled faintly. "Linguists say that's folklore. Linguists have never waited on a pier."

"I don't know what to put in the slot," I said, and heard how young I sounded, how exactly like a child refusing to name which sibling broke the lamp.

"You know perfectly well what to put in the slot."

And that was when I understood — really understood, in the body, below the wry commentary where I usually lived — what I had agreed to. Not lessons. Interrogation. This gentle old man with his ledger and his dying language had handed me a verb shaped like a keyhole, and the only thing that fit through it was my mother.

"She's dead," I said. My voice came out flat, factual, the tone I'd used with the funeral director. "You can't return from a dead person. There's nowhere to return *to*."

"You can't return *to* her, no." Tomas's voice dropped, went almost tender, which was somehow harder to bear than the grammar. "But *vessunna* doesn't ask where you're going. It asks what you left behind. And you left her behind, Yara. We all did, in our ways. Some of us years ago. Some of us—" he stopped himself, and I saw the sentence go back down his throat like a fish pulled off a hook. "Some of us are still working on it."

"What were you going to say?"

"Pointless question. You accepted my terms. No asking why until I'm ready." But his hand had gone to his breast pocket again, the same gesture from the bus stop, touching whatever flat thing lived there, and I filed the gesture away with the other evidence I was quietly accumulating against him: the ledger glimpsed at the stop, the way he'd recognized me, the fifty-fifty odds he'd given my arrival. This man knew things about my mother. He was rationing them out like a man watering a plant during a drought, drop by drop, terrified of flooding it.

I picked the pencil back up. "If I say it," I said, "if I actually complete the sentence — what happens?"

"Nothing happens," Tomas said. "That's the miracle of it. Nothing happens, except now it's been said, in a language with maybe one speaker left, and it exists outside your chest for the first time. Things said in Kavreni stay said. The language is too small to lose track of anything."

I looked at the word on the page. *Vessunna.* Seven letters. A tollbooth disguised as vocabulary.

"My mother," I said finally. The word cost more than I expected; I felt it leave me like blood leaving a cut. "I return from my mother. *Vessunna* — from Astrid." I didn't know the construction, couldn't have declined it properly, probably mangled every case ending in the language's long memory. It didn't matter. The slot was filled. The sentence stood there on the table between us, small and complete and unbearable.

Tomas did not congratulate me. He took off his glasses, which I would learn was his version of weeping, and polished them on his cardigan for longer than any lens required.

"There," he said, when his voice worked again. "Now you've spoken Kavreni. First words. Most people start with 'hello.'"

"Hello would've been easier."

"Hello lies," he said. "It pretends you're meeting for the first time." He put his glasses back on and became briskly, deliberately practical, gathering his papers, and I understood the lesson was over whether the clock agreed or not. "Next week, the word for 'leave.' You'll find it wants to know where you're going. The two verbs are married, in Kavreni. They argue constantly."

At the door, with the brick propping it open and the harbor wind coming through smelling of diesel and salt, he stopped me with one more thing.

"Your mother's first word was the same one," he said. "*Vessunna.* She chose it herself, out of the whole grammar. I asked her why, and she said—" He paused, and I watched him decide, in real time, how much truth the hour could hold. "She said it was the only word in any language that made you carry what you carried."

Then he closed the door gently, and I stood on the steps of the condemned hall holding a notebook with one word in it, and my phone buzzed in my pocket. Uncle Halvard. *Family dinner Sunday, sweetheart. We haven't seen you since the funeral. Come hungry, leave heavy* — that last part was a joke he'd been making for thirty years, the kind of joke that functions as a lock.

I looked at the harbor, at the water going that particular late-afternoon color I didn't yet have a word for, and thought: *vessunna*. I return from my mother. And next Sunday I would sit at a table with the people she'd returned from too, though none of us had ever been allowed to say it.

I answered the phone. "I'll be there," I said, and put the word for return in my pocket like a stone.
