## 7. The Ledger of Names

A language dies the way a harbor freezes: not all at once, but in rings, from the edges inward. First you lose the fishermen's words for ice — *skarn*, thin ice that lies; *vesskarn*, honest ice; a third word for the ice that sings at night, which no one has needed since 1974. Then you lose the words for mending nets, then for arguing about weather, then for blessing a boat, and finally you are left with what every dying language keeps longest: the words for mother, for bread, for come home.

I had been counting my losses in a ledger since before I understood that was what I was doing. A green clothbound book, bought in 1961 from a stationer who has since become a parking structure. In it I recorded every conversation held in Kavreni, every lesson given, every student taken and lost. My father would have called it vanity. My father called most things vanity, right up until he called them nothing at all, because by then the words had gone out of him like heat out of an open door.

The ledger sat on the table between us now, in the community hall where the radiators knocked like someone politely asking to be let out. Yara Eriksen had been coming to me for six weeks. One hour, Thursdays, as agreed. She arrived always ten minutes early and left always five minutes late, which told me more about her than any of her Kavreni did. Grief keeps its own schedule, and hers ran long.

That Thursday she had asked the question I had been waiting six weeks for her to ask, though I had hoped she'd take longer, the way you hope the doctor will take longer reading the scan.

"What is this book?"

I could have lied. At seventy-eight, lying is one of the few exercises I can still do sitting down. But there is a rule in teaching, older than any grammar: the student eventually asks the question the teacher is afraid of, and whatever you do in that moment decides whether you have a student or merely an audience.

"It's a ledger," I said. "Of names."

"Names of what?"

"Of everyone I have ever spoken Kavreni with. Deliberately, I mean. Not counting arguments with God, which I conduct in Kavreni because He has no excuse for pretending not to understand."

She almost smiled. She had her mother's smile, or rather her mother's refusal of a smile — the way the mouth considers it and files it away for later. Six weeks I had been watching Marta's face do arithmetic behind Yara's eyes, and I had said nothing, because some recognitions should be allowed to arrive on their own feet rather than being carried in.

"May I look?"

I turned the ledger around and pushed it across the table. There is a certain relief in surrendering a thing you have guarded too long. It is the same relief, I imagine, that a man feels setting down a piano he has been carrying up stairs for forty years, mixed with the sudden terror of all those landings behind him.

* * *

The early pages were crowded. That is the word — crowded, the way the old quarter itself had once been crowded, three families to a stove, four opinions to a fish. In 1961 I had taught eleven students. Dockworkers' children mostly, and two girls from the cannery who learned faster than anyone and used their fluency chiefly to insult each other with a precision English cannot furnish. Kavreni insults were engineered like the boats: small, watertight, capable of surviving open water.

I watched Yara turn the pages. Her finger moved down the columns the way a customs officer's moves down a manifest, looking for the discrepancy.

Each entry followed the same form. Name. Year begun. Year ended. And then a final line — the last word the student had spoken to me in Kavreni, recorded because I am a sentimental man with a filing system. Most entries ended in *farvel* — goodbye — or in *imorgen*, tomorrow, which was the crueler one, because *imorgen* was nearly always a lie. Students wrote *imorgen* and then moved to the mainland, married people whose mouths had other shapes, decided that a language spoken by fewer people than worked in a single shift at the cannery was a poor investment of a young life. They were not wrong. Being not wrong is the specialty of this century.

"Why do you write down their last word?" Yara asked.

"So they're never entirely finished," I said. "A sentence stopped mid-word is still grammatically alive. Ask any linguist. Or any widow."

She turned another page. The crowds thinned. By 1970 there were four students a year, then two, then one, and the handwriting grew larger, as handwriting does when it has only itself for company. Entries began ending not in *farvel* but in unfinished words — *hjert-* with the pen pressed hard enough to tear the paper, *kom tilb-* abandoned halfway. Once, in 1979, simply a smudge where a word had been written and then furiously erased, the paper worn thin as a confession.

"You kept teaching," she said. "Forty years. Even when—"

"When the hall emptied? When the city redrew the map and the buses forgot us? Yes. A man who stops counting his listeners becomes a man talking to himself, and I already do that in two languages. Three, if you count Norwegian, which my father would insist we don't."

"And these last ones." Her finger had reached the final pages. "There are so few."

"There are three names left," I said. "All crossed out. And one blank line."

I had shown no one the blank line. It sat at the bottom of the last full page, ruled and waiting, and I had refused it for years the way you refuse a chair offered at a funeral — politely, firmly, because accepting it would mean admitting the service was for you.

Yara looked at the crossed-out names. Sanna Eriksen, crossed out 2019 — her cousin, though I doubted she knew Sanna had ever sat where she was sitting, chewing the same consonants. Old Bergliot from the ferry office, crossed out the winter her mind went soft and she began answering me in Swedish, sweetly, apologetically, as if Kavreni were a coat she'd lent out and couldn't remember to whom. And the third name, crossed out just last year: Jon Petter, who died with the word for *harbor* in his mouth, according to his daughter, who reported it to me the way you report a casualty.

"That leaves—" Yara counted, and I watched her arrive at the arithmetic I had lived inside for a year. "It leaves you. You're the only one who speaks it."

"I am the only one who answers himself in it," I corrected. "Which is a different profession."

"But if something happens to you—"

"Then Kavreni joins the ice-words and the net-mending words and the blessing-the-boat words, and the harbor district becomes exactly what the developer's brochure says it is: an underutilized waterfront opportunity." I took the ledger back from her. "Which is why you are here on Thursdays, Miss Eriksen. Not for your benefit. For the language's. It needs a body. Any body. Yours happened to be available and stubborn."

I said it lightly. Lightness is the last tool in the kit and I use it until the handle wears smooth. But she wasn't listening to the lightness. She was looking at the page again, at the years, and I saw her finger stop moving, and I knew — the way you know a storm is coming not from the sky but from the gulls going quiet — that she had found the year.

* * *

1987 occupied a single line, and the line was the fullest in the book.

*Marta Eriksen. Begun 1983. Ended—*

And then nothing. No year. No last word. Just the dash, drawn long and level, a horizon with no sun over it.

Every other entry in forty years had its closing. Hers had a wound.

"She was your student," Yara said. It wasn't a question, but it wasn't steady either. "My mother."

"Marta Lindqvist, then," I said. "Before she was an Eriksen. Before she was anything the family had plans for."

"How long did she study?"

"Four years. 1983 to—" I stopped. The sentence had walked me to the edge of the cliff it always walked me to, and I stood there looking down at the water as I had stood there a hundred times, alone, at three in the morning, rehearsing the fall.

"Tomas." She used my first name for the first time. Students always think it's a small permission. "How good was she?"

I laughed, briefly, without much humor in it — the laugh of a man who has just been asked how tall the mountain was that fell on him.

"Good," I said. "Good is the wrong instrument. You want a word with more range. In four years she did what students do in twenty. She heard the tones before I taught them — the rising grief-pitch and the falling shame-pitch, which sound identical to outsiders and are opposite in every way that matters. She dreamed in Kavreni by the second winter; she told me so herself, embarrassed, as if she'd been caught doing something improper. She translated a ballad — the *Vessel-Lament*, the one about the boat that returns crewed by nobody — and her translation made me cry, which I informed her was unprofessional, and she informed me that the poem was unprofessional first."

Yara had gone very still. Stillness runs in that family like a trade skill.

"Then why did she stop?"

And there it was. The question underneath the question, the one the whole family had arranged itself around like furniture arranged around a stain. I put my hand flat on the ledger, on the long dash, the horizon with no sun.

"Because she finished," I said.

"Finished what?"

"The work she came to do."

I closed the book. I heard myself do it — the small green clap of covers meeting — and understood that I had just done the thing I had promised myself, in the shower, in the night, in the margins of every sleepless hour since Yara Eriksen first appeared at the dead-end bus stop wearing her mother's face, that I would not do. I had opened the door and shut it in the same breath. Forty years of keeping a ledger of everything said, and here was the one entry I had kept as silence.

"You're doing it," Yara said quietly. "Whatever it is. You're doing the thing my family does."

"And what is that?"

"Stopping mid-word." She nodded at the ledger in my hands. "You write down everyone else's last word. You keep theirs alive. But yours—" She stopped herself, and I saw her choose, deliberately, to finish the sentence anyway, and something in me rose toward her like a tide answering a moon it didn't know it had. "Your entry for her doesn't end. It just stops. Like everyone in my family talks. Like Halvard at dinner. Like my grandfather's letters, which are all dates and no names. Like—" She exhaled. "Like my mother, for thirty years, whenever the harbor came up."

Outside, the wind found the broken window on the north side and played it, one low note, the note the hall had been playing since 1998. The radiators knocked twice and gave up.

"Marta didn't abandon the language," I said.

The words were out before the committee convened. That is how the truth leaves a man after forty years of quarantine — not announced, but escaped, slipping past the guards while they change shift.

Yara leaned forward. "What did she do, then?"

I looked at the ledger. I thought of a promise made in a hospital room in 2004, to a woman smaller than her own voice, holding my hand with a grip that belied the machines, saying: *Let them have the quiet, Tomas. Let them keep it. Someone has to lose gracefully in this family, and I've had practice.* I had promised. I keep promises the way I keep ledgers — badly, incompletely, but past all sense.

"I can't tell you," I said.

"You just said she didn't abandon it."

"I said what I could say. The rest isn't mine to give. It was hers, and she—" I set the ledger down between us, closed, a green box with a life inside it. "She spent it the way she wanted. That is the whole of what I owe you on the subject, and more than I owed myself."

Yara sat back. I expected anger — the Eriksens do anger beautifully, lacquered and load-bearing. Instead she did something worse. She nodded slowly, the nod of a person adding a room onto a house she'd thought complete, and said:

"The letters in her house. After she died, I found sealed envelopes. Dozens of them. Labeled with dates, no names, no addresses. Just years written on the front in her handwriting." She was watching me the way you watch a lock. "They start in 1983."

The radiators chose that moment to knock again, three times, like a fist on a door, like a knock I had been waiting forty years to hear and had built my whole quiet life on never hearing.

"They end in 1987," she said.

I did not answer. I am an old man and I know the value of a held breath. But my hand, without consulting me, had come to rest on the ledger's cover, directly over the year 1987, the way you press a hand over a wound, or over a door, unable to decide which.
