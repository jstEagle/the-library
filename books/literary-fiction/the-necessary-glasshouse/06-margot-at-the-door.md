## 6. Margot at the Door

Margot had been driving since four in the morning, and by the time the Ohio line slid past her windshield the light was doing that thing she hated — going flat and silver, like solder before it flows. She'd left Portland in a hurry she hadn't admitted to anyone, not even to herself. She'd told her landlord two weeks. She'd told her studio partner she had family business. She'd packed three boxes of tools, a change of clothes, and the pane she'd smashed on the night the letter came, wrapped in a towel, because she couldn't explain why but she couldn't leave it either.

The broken pane rode in the passenger seat like a hostage.

She'd driven all night because sleeping in a motel somewhere outside Boise felt worse than driving, and driving felt worse than thinking, and thinking felt worse than remembering, and there was nothing between Boise and Cleveland but the kind of country where your mind has nowhere to hide. So she drove. She drank gas-station coffee that tasted like hot pennies. She listened to static where stations should have been. And when the sun came up over the flats she thought: *I am thirty-eight years old and I am driving home because my father is selling my mother's glasshouse out from under the ground she's buried in.*

Not under. Near. Vivian Underhill lay in the Halloway cemetery, up the hill from the ravine, which Margot had never once visited and did not intend to visit now. She had a rule about it. She had rules about everything, which was what happened to children who grew up in houses where the truth had been quietly removed, like a load-bearing wall, and everyone agreed to walk carefully around the space where it used to be.

Her hands were steady on the wheel. Her hands were always steady; that was the trade she'd made with herself at nineteen, when she'd apprenticed in a glass shop in Tacoma and learned that lead came doesn't care how you feel, it cares whether your cuts are true. Glass work had saved her life in the way that only precision can save a certain kind of person. You could not lie to a pane of glass. It either fit or it didn't. There was no smile you could give it.

Which was maybe why she was dreading the house so much. Because the house was full of people who smiled at things.

* * *

Halloway hadn't changed, or had changed in the way small towns do, which is to say every difference was a wound dressed as improvement. The diner was a coffee franchise now. The hardware store was a vape shop with a hand-painted sign apologizing for itself. But the water tower still said HALLOWAY in letters gone the brown of old blood, and the road out toward the house still ran along the top of the ravine, and Margot's stomach still dropped the way it had when she was eleven years old and the school bus took this same curve and some kid would say something about the Underhills' glasshouse and go quiet in a way that wasn't respect.

She turned onto the gravel drive at six in the evening, having been on the road for thirty-eight hours, and saw Theo's rental car already there, parked too neatly, aligned with the edge of the gravel as if the gravel had tolerances.

Of course it was there. Of course he'd beaten her. Theo beat everyone to everything; it was how he avoided arriving.

She sat in her car for a moment with the engine ticking down. Through the windshield the house looked exactly as it had in her memory and wrong in every particular — smaller, grayer, the paint holding but tired, the porch rail sagging by degrees so slight you'd need an instrument to measure them. Theo probably had an instrument. Behind the house, past the dead garden, she could see the roofline of the glasshouse, low and green with ivy, catching the last of the light like something drowned just under the surface of a pond.

She got out. Her legs were wooden. Thirty-eight hours of driving had gotten into her knees and hips and wouldn't come out.

The front door opened before she knocked.

Theo stood in it, backlit by the hallway bulb, and for one unguarded second his face did something complicated — relief and dread fighting to a draw — before the famous smile arrived and smoothed everything flat. The smile. God, the smile. Their father had it too, or the seed of it, and their mother had never once worn it, and Margot had spent her whole adult life cataloguing the ways that smile functioned: it ended conversations, it forgave without asking permission, it closed doors so gently you thanked him for it.

"Theo," she said.

"Margot." He looked at her the way he looked at bridge decks, she thought. Assessing load. "You look—"

"Don't." She lifted a hand. "Whatever the word is, don't."

He stepped aside to let her in, and she passed him close enough to smell hotel soap and highway exhaustion, and neither of them touched the other, and both of them noticed.

* * *

The hallway smelled the same, which was the cruelest part. Old wallpaper paste and lemon oil and underneath it, faint as a watermark, chlorine — from the years their mother had kept a cold plunge tank in the glasshouse annex for the orchids, some Victorian nonsense she'd read about in a book, and roses, always roses, because Vivian Underhill had believed a house should announce its intentions at the door.

Their father was not downstairs. A note on the kitchen counter in Aldous's upright architect's hand: *Gone to the county office re: permits. Back by eight. M — your room is made up. T — the boxes in the garage are labeled by room.* No greeting. No question about the drive. Just logistics, laid out like a man laying track.

"He left a note," Margot said.

"He left notes."

"For us. Both of us. Like we're tenants."

"We are tenants." Theo poured water from the tap into two glasses without asking if she wanted one, and set it in front of her, and she drank because her body demanded it even while her pride objected. "Thirty days' notice. He's had the estate lawyer draw up inventory sheets. Numbered. There's a clipboard."

"There's a *clipboard*."

"On the fridge. Magneted."

She laughed — one sharp note, no humor in it, more like a sneeze of the soul — and set the glass down. "God. He's turned Mother's death into a project plan."

"He turned everything into a project plan. This is just the last one."

And there it was, she thought. Day one, hour one, and they were already doing it — talking about Aldous in the third person, in the past tense of grievance, circling the thing they weren't going to talk about. She could feel the shape of it between them like heat off a road. *The night Mother died.* Neither of them named it. Neither of them ever named it. In thirty years they had built around it the way a tree builds around a fence wire, swallowing it, growing crooked over it.

"How was the drive?" Theo asked, and it was such a transparently procedural question that she almost admired it.

"Long."

"You should eat something."

"I will."

"There's soup. He made soup."

She stared at him. "Father made soup?"

"He follows her recipes." Theo said it evenly, but Margot caught the flicker, the half-second where his eyes went to the window, to the glasshouse roofline visible past the dead garden. "There's a card file. Alphabetized."

Vivian's recipe box, alphabetized by a man who had never in Margot's memory cooked a single meal while his wife was alive. She filed it away next to the other small wrongnesses, the collection she'd been keeping since childhood without knowing what it was for: the photograph albums missing their final year, the way Father spoke of Mother in flawless polished sentences like a eulogy rehearsed for decades, the locked annex door she'd tried once at fifteen and never again.

"I'm going to see it," she said.

Theo's cup stopped halfway to his mouth. "The house?"

"The glasshouse."

"It's locked."

"I know it's locked. I want to stand in front of it. Is that allowed under the clipboard regime?"

Something moved through his face — she watched him decide against naming it — and he set his cup down with a small precise click. "It's structurally unsound. That's what he says. Ivy's gotten into the frame, the glazing bars are rotten, there's a risk of collapse."

"And you believe him? You, who hit bridges with little hammers for a living?"

"I haven't assessed it."

"But you *looked*."

A pause. Long enough to be an answer. "I looked," Theo admitted, and Margot felt the first hairline crack of the week open between them, not wide enough to see through yet, but there. He had looked. He had looked and seen something that made him pause, and he was choosing not to say it, and that choice was the most familiar sound in the world to her. It was the sound of their entire childhood.

"Well," she said. "When I've slept, I'm going to look too."

* * *

She did sleep — three hours, in her childhood bed, under a ceiling she'd once counted cracks in during insomniac nights, and woke at eleven with the disoriented conviction that she was seventeen and late for something. The house at night had its own weather. Floorboards spoke in the voices of people who weren't there. Downstairs she heard Theo moving, methodical, the soft scrape of tape guns, and she went down and found him in the dining room surrounded by flattened boxes, working through the china cabinet with the efficiency of a man defusing something.

"You're packing at midnight."

"Couldn't sleep." He didn't look up. "Jet lag works differently when it's grief."

"That's not jet lag."

"No," he agreed. "It isn't."

She stood in the doorway watching him wrap their mother's good plates — the ones with the blue rim, the ones that had come out only twice a year — in newsprint, each one tested against his palm for soundness before wrapping. He was good at it. He was good at everything that involved handling objects instead of feelings, and she loved him for it and hated him for it in the same breath, because she was the same, they were the same, two children raised to process loss through manual labor.

"Theo." She pulled out a chair and sat astride it, arms folded on the backrest. "We should agree on terms."

Now he looked up. "Terms."

"For the next two weeks. You and me, in this house, with him." She ticked fingers. "One: we pack fast. Two: we sell faster. Three—" and here she made herself hold his eyes, because this was the important one, the one that mattered, the one she needed him to sign onto the way you need a countersignature, "—we feel nothing. Nothing gets discussed. Nothing gets reopened. We do the job, we take our checks, we go home. Agreed?"

Theo studied her for a long moment. Outside, wind moved through the dead garden, rattling the stalks, and somewhere far off — or maybe not far off, maybe closer than she wanted it to be — something loose in the glasshouse creaked against its ivy.

"You know that's not how it goes," he said finally. Quietly. Not cruel. Almost gentle, which was worse.

"It's how I need it to go."

"I know." He wrapped another plate. The newsprint crackled. "Okay. Pack fast, sell faster, feel nothing. I'll take the deal."

But he said it the way he signed inspection reports, she thought. Certifying a structure he had doubts about.

They worked until two in the morning, side by side, not touching, not speaking of anything that mattered, wrapping twenty years of a family into newsprint and tape. It was, Margot thought as she carried a box labeled KITCHEN #4 to the stack in the hall, the most honest conversation they'd had in a decade — two people agreeing to build a wall together, each privately aware the foundation underneath it was already failing.

At the bottom of the china cabinet, under the false board their mother had installed and nobody had ever mentioned, Theo's hand stopped.

"Margot," he said. "Come look at this."
