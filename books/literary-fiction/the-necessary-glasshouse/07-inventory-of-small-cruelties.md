## 7. Inventory of Small Cruelties

**Theo**

The boxes had been labeled before either of us arrived. That was the detail I kept returning to, the way a man keeps returning his thumb to a chipped tooth: *KITCHEN — MISC*, *VIVIAN'S STUDIO — SORT*, *BOOKS — DONATE*. My father's handwriting, block capitals ruled with the same steel edge he'd used on my geometry homework thirty years ago, each letter identical to its neighbors, no letter allowed to have a bad day. He had labeled boxes for an estate that wasn't yet an estate, in a house where my mother's coffee cup still sat in the drying rack, and he had done it with the calm of a man packing for a business trip.

Margot noticed too. She said nothing about it, but I watched her turn one of the labels toward the light like she was checking a signature on a painting.

We worked in silence for most of the first morning, and the silence was not empty. It had texture. It had the weight of every silence we'd ever shared in this house, all of them stacked like sandbags against something neither of us would name. Margot wrapped china. I carried boxes. We were good at this, at dividing labor so precisely that no task required us to touch the same object at the same time.

The house smelled of chlorine and roses, same as always — the sunken parlor where Aldous kept a small indoor pool through my childhood, long drained now but never resurfaced, its chemical ghost soaked into the plaster, layered over with the rose potpourri Vivian had simmered in every room from June until frost. Two smells that should have canceled each other out and instead had married, gone stale together, become the smell of home itself. I'd forgotten until I walked in. The body keeps an inventory the mind refuses to audit.

"Attic first," Aldous said, appearing in the doorway with a clipboard. Of course a clipboard. "Then her studio, then the bedrooms. The auctioneer wants a preliminary inventory by Friday."

"The auctioneer," Margot said, "has a name?"

"Whitcomb. He comes highly recommended." Aldous wrote something on the clipboard. "Start with the attic. There's a ladder issue — mind the third rung."

He said it the way he said everything, as though the house were a project already ninety percent complete and we were contractors who'd shown up late. Then he went back down the hall to his study, and I heard the door close with the soft, deliberate click of a man closing a door.

The attic ladder's third rung was fine. I checked. It's what I do.

* * *

**Margot**

You can tell everything about a family by what they keep in the attic, and ours had kept weather. Rain gear from three decades, none of it current. A child's sled in a town that gets maybe two real snows a year. Boxes of Christmas ornaments wrapped in newspaper so old the headlines had gone the color of weak tea. And photographs — hundreds of them, albums and shoeboxes and loose prints sliding around in a hatbox like fish in a bucket.

I sat cross-legged on the floorboards and started sorting them into decades, because sorting is what I do instead of feeling, a trade I learned in this very house. Theo hauled boxes up behind me and stacked them by the dormer window, where the light came through gray and even, good light, restorer's light. Old habit: I assessed it automatically. North-facing, diffused, no glare. You could match a piece of antique glass under light like that. You could see exactly what was missing.

That was the thing about the photographs. Once I started looking, I couldn't stop seeing it.

Here was 1978: Vivian Underhill at the county fair, laughing, a ribbon pinned to her blouse for the dahlias. Here was 1981: the four of us on the porch steps, Aldous's hand on her shoulder, me gap-toothed, Theo scowling at the camera with the expression he still wore at forty-three. Here was 1984, 1985, 1986 — picnics, holidays, the glasshouse going up panel by panel in the background of a dozen shots, my mother in every frame with dirt on her knees and that look she had, like the world was a seed packet she couldn't wait to open.

And then nothing. Not less. Nothing.

The last photograph of my mother in that attic was dated August 1987. She died in October 1989. Twenty-six months, and the record simply stopped, the way a heart monitor stops — not fading, stopping. No birthdays. No harvest festivals. No pictures of the glasshouse finished, though she'd spent three years building it and it was the only beautiful thing my father ever let stand on that property. I turned the albums over. I shook the shoeboxes. I went through the loose prints twice, then a third time, telling myself I'd missed a sleeve, misread a date.

"Theo." My voice came out strange, flattened, the voice I use on clients when a piece arrives more damaged than the appraisal claimed. "When's the last picture you've seen of Mom?"

He came over and stood above me, and I watched him do what he always does — scan, count, verify. His eyes moved across the albums like instruments across a span.

"This one, I guess," he said finally, tapping August '87. "Why?"

"There should be two years more than this. Minimum. She took pictures of everything. She labeled the backs." I flipped a print over: *Theo, 6, first lost tooth, July.* Her handwriting, round and sure, every i dotted with a little circle. "She documented us like a court stenographer. And then she just — stops. Two years before she dies. Doesn't that strike you as—"

"She got busy." He said it fast, the way you set down something hot. "The glasshouse. The garden society. People get busy, Margot."

"People don't stop existing in their own family album, Theo. They get busy. They don't get *erased*."

The word came out louder than I meant it to, and it hung there in the attic dust, and Theo looked at me with an expression I recognized because I'd been looking at it my whole life across dinner tables and funeral receptions: the look of a man standing at the edge of a thought, deciding not to step off.

"We're here to pack," he said. "Not to curate."

But he didn't go back to his boxes right away. He stood there another few seconds, looking at the August '87 photograph — our mother holding a tomato the size of her fist, mid-laugh — and something moved behind his face, some load shifting somewhere deep in the structure, and then he picked up a box marked *MISC* and carried it down the ladder, and the third rung held him without complaint.

* * *

**Theo**

Downstairs, I found my father in the kitchen making an inventory of the silver, which he was doing with a jeweler's loupe, which was absurd, and I understood it was not about the silver.

"Dad. Mom's photographs stop in eighty-seven."

"Mm." He didn't look up. "Some of those albums are water-damaged. I may have discarded the worst of them."

"When?"

A pause, measured out like dosing medicine. "Years ago. After the funeral, cleaning up. It was difficult, having her face everywhere." Now he looked up, and his eyes were wet in exactly the right amount, calibrated grief, and I felt the old machinery engage in my chest, the gears that converted suspicion into guilt. "You understand, Theo. Some things a man can't look at every day."

I understood. Understanding was the family business. We were all majority shareholders in understanding.

"What about her letters?" Margot asked from the doorway. She'd come down without my hearing her; she could still move through this house like a cat, like she used to when she was sneaking out to smoke at fifteen. "People kept letters then. She had sisters. Correspondence. Where is it?"

"Burned, I expect, or thrown out with the damaged things." Aldous returned to the silver, tilting a fork to catch the light. "Your mother wasn't sentimental about paper. She was a gardener. Things grew, things died, she composted them. It was one of the healthiest attitudes I've ever known."

It was a perfect answer. That was the thing about my father's answers — they were never evasive, never fumbling, never guilty. They were complete, reasonable, and delivered without a single wasted syllable, like engineering specifications. Every answer closed like a well-machined joint. And I had spent my whole life trusting answers that closed cleanly, because a clean closure meant no movement, no drift, nothing to inspect.

I was forty-three years old, standing in my dead mother's kitchen, before I heard it: the answers weren't clean. They were *finished*. Prefabricated. Answers you build in advance, in a workshop, and carry with you, waiting for the question.

"Her ledgers, then," Margot said. "She kept garden ledgers. Ruth Kessler told me once they used to compare notes."

"I'm sure whatever papers there were will surface as we sort." He wrote on the clipboard. "Studio next. Then I'd like the kitchen done by end of day — Whitcomb's people will want access."

He left the room. At the door he stopped, half-turned, and for one unguarded instant he looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with age, tired like a man who has been carrying something heavy up many flights of stairs for a very long time.

"You're both doing well," he said. "This is going smoothly."

Then he was gone, and Margot and I stood in the kitchen among the boxed years of our mother's life, and neither of us said what we were both thinking, because saying it would make it a thing in the air, and things in the air in this house had a way of being opened like windows in January.

* * *

**Margot**

Her studio was the worst room and the best room, and I saved it for last on purpose, the way you save the worst room.

It sat off the back of the house where the glasshouse began — a converted lean-to with a potting bench and a bank of south-facing windows my father had installed for her birthday in '85, the only generous thing I ever saw him do without being asked twice. Her tools were still on the pegboard. Her gloves were still draped over the bench edge, stiff as shed skin. A trowel lay where she'd left it, handle worn to the shape of her grip, and I picked it up and it fit my hand almost exactly, because my hands are her hands, and I had to put it down again and breathe at the window for a while.

Theo worked the shelving while I took the bench drawers. Seed packets, twine, plant markers, a tin of tags in her handwriting. And in the second drawer, under a stack of catalogues, a ledger.

I knew it was hers before I opened it. Green cloth cover, corner bumped, a rubber band around it gone brittle and snapped when I touched it. Inside, columns in that round sure hand: dates, beds, varieties, germination notes, weather. *Mar 14 — tomatoes in cold frames, 6 wks early if glass holds. Apr 2 — Theo helped stake peas, complained entire time, good worker underneath.* I laughed and it came out broken. She'd written us down the way she wrote down seeds — as things she was growing, keeping records on, expecting to see bloom.

The entries ran forward through the seasons, dense and alive, and I read with my finger under the lines like a child, following her voice, until I reached the spring of 1989 — her final year, the year the photographs had already abandoned — and the handwriting changed. Not worse. Denser. The entries got longer, ran past the columns into the margins, small urgent script crowding the ruled lines like people pressing against a door. Words I couldn't make out at arm's length in the failing afternoon light. Entries dated days apart instead of weeks. And then, in September, the writing stopped mid-season. Mid-*sentence*. The last full line ended with a dash, the way hers did when she meant to continue tomorrow —

— and the next page was torn out.

Not ripped in haste. Torn close, along the spine, carefully, by someone who didn't want ragged edges. The stubs of it were still visible in the binding, a pale seam of absence running down the gutter of the book like a scar.

"Theo."

He came over. I handed him the ledger open to the torn place and watched him read backward through it, faster and faster, his lips moving slightly, and I watched the exact moment he reached September and hit the missing page. His whole body went still — not calm-still, the way he usually was, but the stillness of a man on a bridge who feels, through the soles of his boots, a vibration he cannot account for.

"It just stops," he said.

"It doesn't stop. It's stopped. Someone tore it out." I touched the stubs. "She wouldn't have. She kept records like scripture. This was her — I don't know. Her proof she existed."

"A page tears out. Books get damp, bindings fail—" But he was doing it again, scanning, counting, and I saw his eyes flick to the drawer, to the empty spaces in it, the way you notice missing teeth in a smile. He'd been cataloguing absences all day without letting himself total them. The photographs. The letters. Now this. "Where are the other ledgers?" he said quietly. "She kept these yearly. There should be ten of these. There's one."

"One," I agreed.

We stood there in our mother's studio with the light dying in the west windows, surrounded by everything she'd owned, which was turning out to be a museum of subtraction. Everything remaining, arranged. Everything personal, gone. As if someone had curated her life down to the parts that made no demands — the trowels, the twine, the cheerful public woman at the county fair — and removed every part that might speak.

From the hallway, my father's voice, pleasant and carrying: "How's the studio coming? Whitcomb will love those windows."

"Coming fine," Theo called back, and his voice was level and easy and closed like a machined joint, and I realized with a cold sliding feeling that I did the same thing, that we had both learned it in this house, that fluency.

He waited until the footsteps faded. Then he looked at me, and for the first time since I'd gotten off the plane, my brother looked at me without measuring anything first.

"Tomorrow," he said, "we do the glasshouse."

"The annex too?"

A pause. One beat, two. Somewhere in the walls the house ticked, cooling, the way houses do, the way they talk when nobody admits to listening.

"The annex especially," Theo said.
