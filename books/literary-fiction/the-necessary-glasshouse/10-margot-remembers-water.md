## 10. Margot Remembers Water

The rain in Portland came the way it always did in October — not falling so much as arriving, a gray insistence against the studio windows that made the whole city feel like the inside of a jar. Margot had learned to work through it. Rain was good for glass work, actually; it kept clients away, kept the phone silent, left her alone with the lead came and the grozing pliers and the slow arithmetic of fitting broken things back together.

She was releading a Victorian transom window — roses, of all things, faded to the color of weak tea — when she heard the letter again.

Not literally. The letter was three thousand miles away on her kitchen counter, propped against the fruit bowl where she'd stood it two days ago like something contagious. *Aldous Underhill, Executor.* The auction date circled in his tidy hand. *The property known as the Underhill Glasshouse, together with contents, to be sold at public auction.*

She'd read it eleven times. She knew this because she counted things too — not the way Theo counted, with calipers and load tables and that terrible useful precision of his, but the way restorers count: how many pieces in a shattered pane, how many firings a kiln could take before the shelf gave out, how many times a person could read four sentences before they stopped being sentences and became a door swinging open.

The rain picked up. Somewhere down the block a storm drain gulped, and Margot set down her pliers, because her hands had begun to do something without her permission.

They were remembering water.

* * *

Everyone in Halloway remembered the night Vivian Underhill died. That was the strange thing about small towns — grief there wasn't private property. It got zoned, parcelled, assigned to everyone within earshot. For thirty years Margot had carried other people's version of that night the way you carry a coat someone else chose: it fit badly, but arguing about it seemed worse than wearing it.

The official version went like this. Storm of the decade, late September 1993. Wind took a limb off the sugar maple behind the house; the limb came through the roof of the cold frame attached to the south side of the glasshouse; Vivian Underhill, who never could leave her plants alone, went out into the storm to cover the seedlings, slipped on the wet flagstone, and was struck by falling glass. She was found by her children. Death by misadventure. The coroner used a phrase Margot would later learn was boilerplate: *injuries consistent with.*

Consistent with. As if injuries were clothing and the night were a mannequin.

Margot's version had no such tidiness. Her version began with the sound.

People assumed breaking glass sounded like breaking glass — a crash, a sparkle of noise. But anyone who worked with it knew better. A pane going down made a sound first like a sigh, a kind of exhalation, the material deciding. Then the scatter. Margot had been twelve that autumn and already apprenticing herself to her mother at the potting bench, already learning which orchids liked their feet wet and which sulked, already learning that glass, properly cut, obeyed. So when the maple limb came through the cold frame roof she didn't hear an accident. She heard a sentence ending mid-word.

She had been in her room reading. Theo had been — where had Theo been? This was the part that had gone soft over the years, the way old mortar goes soft, crumbling at a touch. In the family record, both children had run out together, found their mother together, screamed for their father together. A matched set of witnesses, corroborating.

But Margot remembered running alone.

She remembered the hallway light on, which was wrong because the power had flickered earlier and Aldous had gone to check the breaker box. She remembered the kitchen door standing open to the rain, breathing cold air into the house. And she remembered the yard: black, streaming, lit from behind her by the kitchen's yellow spill, and the cold frame — the low glass structure their mother had built before either child was born, a long ribcage of panes against the glasshouse wall — opened up like something dissected. The maple limb lay across it. The seedling flats were drowned or airborne. And under the largest fallen panel, half-covered, one arm flung out across the wet stone as if reaching for a trowel —

Her mother. Under glass. In the rain.

Margot did not remember screaming. She remembered being unable to. She remembered the specific physics of that silence, how it sat on her chest, how the rain filled it up. She was twelve and her body simply declined to produce sound, and what came out instead was her mother's name shaped in breath only, *Mama*, a word with no volume, a word that dissolved.

And then — this was the part. This was the part she had spent thirty years folding smaller and smaller and putting away in the deepest drawer of herself, the part that contradicted everything the family had agreed upon, the part that had made her, at nineteen, buy a bus ticket to Portland and not come back except for funerals —

Theo was already there.

Not running out beside her. Already there. Standing in the doorway of the glasshouse itself, the little antechamber door off the potting shed, nine years old, soaked through, hair plastered flat, barefoot — barefoot, in September rain, on flagstone slick as ice — just standing. Not moving toward their mother. Not moving away. Standing in the doorway with the rain coming down behind him like a curtain and his face doing nothing at all.

That was the detail. That was the splinter she'd swallowed and carried ever since. Not horror on his face. Not grief. Nothing. A nine-year-old boy looking at his dead mother under a sheet of glass and wearing the expression of a child waiting to be told what he was looking at.

And then Aldous came out of the dark — from the direction of the garage, though the breaker box was on the other side of the house — and gathered Theo up under one arm like a piece of equipment, and said, in a voice pitched exactly between calm and command, *"Inside, both of you. Now."* And Margot had gone inside, because she was twelve and he was her father and the rain was enormous, and by the time the ambulance came she had been dried and wrapped in a blanket and installed at the kitchen table with cocoa she didn't drink, and the story had already begun assembling itself around them like scaffolding.

*You girls shouldn't have seen that,* a neighbor said, meaning her and Theo both, though only one of them had seen anything.

*The poor boy found her,* someone else said, later, at the funeral, and Aldous had nodded, and Theo had looked at his shoes, and Margot had opened her mouth — actually opened it, she could still feel the shape of the words against her teeth — and then closed it, because what would she even say? *No. He was already there. He was there before me. He was barefoot. Why was he barefoot?*

Why was he barefoot?

Thirty years, and that question had never once stopped humming. It hummed under every Thanksgiving, every Christmas card signed in Aldous's fountain pen, every phone call in which Theo described his bridges in loving decimal places while saying nothing whatsoever about himself. It hummed the night of their father's sixtieth birthday, when Margot had watched Theo perform his famous smile — the one that ended conversations the way circuit breakers ended current — and thought: *you were in that doorway. You know what you saw. What did we agree to forget?*

Because that was the thing about the family record. Records don't write themselves. Someone curates them. Someone decides which version gets laminated and passed around, and everyone else, out of love or exhaustion or fear, signs on. Margot had signed on at twelve years old because signing on was the price of staying in the house, and the house was where the surviving parent was, and a twelve-year-old does not yet know that some silences are not peace but debt.

She knew now. She'd had thirty years of knowing now, and the interest had compounded.

* * *

The rain eased toward evening. Margot made tea she didn't drink and stood at her workbench with the Victorian roses half-repaired, and let the memory finish its work, because she had learned that memory is like a stain in old glass: if you look directly through it you see nothing but distortion, but hold it at an angle, in the right light, and the whole image resolves.

At an angle, more details surfaced.

The coroner's report — she'd obtained a copy years ago, during one of her periodic flare-ups of wanting answers, each of which she'd smothered alone because Theo was unreachable and Aldous was Aldous — said *lacerations consistent with falling glazing, blunt force trauma consistent with fall.* But Margot remembered her mother's hands. She had seen them, briefly, before Aldous pulled the children away — one arm outflung, and the hands, her mother's strong gardener's hands with the dirt permanently in the knuckle creases, had been *empty*. No trowel. Their mother never went out in weather without a tool in her hand; she'd taught them both that, joked that a gardener without a trowel was just a person standing in mud. If Vivian Underhill had gone out to save her seedlings, where was her trowel?

And the seedlings themselves. The official story required her mother to have gone out *for* them. But Margot remembered the flats afterward — she'd helped clean up, or rather she'd been allowed to watch the cleanup from the kitchen window, another curated view — and the flats nearest the door, the ones a fleeing or hurrying woman would pass first, had been untouched. Covered, even. Someone had covered them. Their mother covered her seedlings before she did anything else; it was ritual, reflex. Which meant she'd had time, at the start. Time to protect the plants and then — what? Go back inside? Head somewhere else? The cold frame was on the south side. The path from it led past the potting shed, past the annex, along the ravine side of the property.

Along the ravine.

Margot set down her tea. Outside, the Portland rain kept up its gray arithmetic, and she thought about her brother driving east right now, or maybe already arrived, cataloguing bridges the way he catalogued everything, filing himself away in some inspection report of the soul marked *structurally deficient, further evaluation required.*

He'd been in the doorway. He'd been barefoot. He'd been nine.

For thirty years she had built her version of him around that image: the boy who froze, the boy whose face went blank because the feeling had nowhere to go, the boy who grew into a man who measured expansion joints because expansion joints were honest — they moved exactly as much as they were designed to move, no more, no secrets. She had forgiven him for the doorway long ago, or told herself she had. Children freeze. Children are not responsible for catastrophe. She believed that the way she believed in load paths and kiln schedules.

But forgiveness wasn't the same as forgetting, and lately — since the letter, since the auction date had started ticking down like a fuse — the two versions of that night had begun grinding against each other inside her. The family record said: two children ran out together. Her memory said: I ran out alone and found my brother already standing in the impossible place, already wet, already silent, already becoming whoever he is now.

One of those versions was wrong. Either her memory had fabricated the most formative image of her childhood — dressed it in rain and bare feet and that unbearable blankness — or the record was a forgery, and someone had spent thirty years keeping it laminated.

And if the record was forged, then the question stopped being *what did Theo see* and became the question she had never once allowed herself to ask in full daylight:

*What was Theo doing outside at all?*

Children wander. Children follow parents. Maybe he'd trailed their mother out and frozen when the limb came down — that was the benign version, the version she could live with, the version she'd lived with. But the benign version had holes in it the size of the ravine. Because Aldous had come from the wrong direction. Because the power was out and the hallway light was on. Because her mother's hands were empty and her seedlings were covered and the coroner's phrase — *consistent with* — was the phrase people use when they've decided not to look too closely.

Margot wiped her hands on her apron and crossed the studio to where her phone sat charging. On the screen, the thread with Theo, last message four days old: *Driving out Friday. Two weeks, tops.* His idea of communication — a logistics update, a shipping manifest of a human being.

She typed: *When you get there — before you pack a single box — I need to tell you what I remember about that night. All of it. Not the version we agreed on.*

She stared at it. The cursor blinked. Thirty years of family architecture stood on the foundation of that unasked question, and she was about to start pulling bricks.

She thought of her mother's hands, empty. Of the covered seedlings. Of the bare feet on wet stone.

She hit send.

Then she turned back to the Victorian roses, and picked up her pliers, and began fitting the broken pieces together — because that was the only prayer she knew, the only one glass had ever taught her: that whatever shatters can be held, if you're patient enough, in something stronger than itself.
