## 8. The Neighborhood That Isn't There

The notice had been up for eleven days when I finally let myself read it past the headline. *Service Adjustment: Route 14 Discontinued Effective [date]. Alternative service available via [route that does not go where you need it to go].* The transit authority had a gift for euphemism. They didn't discontinue a route; they "adjusted" it, the way you might adjust a pillow under the head of someone who is already dead.

I stood in front of the poster in the library lobby for longer than was professional. A man waiting for the elevator watched me with the mild alarm of someone deciding whether to offer help or call someone. I moved along. Invisibility is a discipline; even grief has to be catalogued quietly.

Here is what I knew and did not say: I had not ridden the 14 in twenty years. I knew its schedule the way you know a scar's weather — intimately, uselessly. It ran from the terminal downtown out through the east side, past the cannery that wasn't a cannery anymore, into the old neighborhood where the streets had names like saints and the buildings had been dying politely for decades until the city decided to stop being polite.

Tomás took that bus home the last night of his life. I know this because I put him on it. I stood at the stop and watched the taillights go, and I said nothing, because what I had to say was unforgivable and I hadn't yet learned that unforgivable things should still be said. That is the whole curriculum of my life, really. Everything since has been remedial study.

So on a Thursday night after my shift, instead of going home, I walked to the terminal and bought a ticket with cash, which felt appropriately clandestine for an act that harmed no one but me.

* * *

The 14 at midnight is a different animal than the 14 at rush hour. At midnight it belongs to the people the day has finished with: a nurse coming off shift with her shoes in her lap, two teenagers sharing one earbud, a man asleep with his mouth open as if mid-argument with God. I took a window seat about halfway back — Tomás always took the back, I took the window, some geometries are permanent — and pressed my forehead to the glass.

The city unspooled. And here is the thing nobody warns you about demolition: it doesn't remove the past, it annotates it. Every lot where something used to be now holds a smaller, meaner thing — a parking structure, a condo with a name like a perfume, a fenced vacancy with weeds growing through the asphalt in defiant cursive. My memory kept supplying the original buildings over the replacements, a double exposure. The cannery's brick smokestack, gone; in its place a self-storage facility whose sign promised CLIMATE CONTROLLED PEACE OF MIND, which struck me as the most honest advertising in the city, since what people store is usually their pasts.

I had brought my ledger. Of course I had. Twenty years of habit doesn't dissolve just because you've boarded a haunted bus. But when I opened it to a fresh page and wrote the date, my pen hovered. What was the entry? *Rode the 14. Saw where things used to be.* It looked so small. Grief does not fit in columns. I had built my whole system on the premise that naming a thing tames it, and here was the flaw in the architecture: some things get larger when named. Some words are owners, not possessions.

I wrote anyway. It's what I do. *Smokestack: gone. Cannery office with the green door: gone. The wall where the boys played handball: still there, shorter than remembered, everything is.*

The nurse got off at Delgado. The teenagers at the next stop. By the time we crossed into the old neighborhood proper, it was me and the sleeping man and the driver, who drove this route the way you'd drive a hospice patient — gently, without hurry, knowing the destination.

* * *

I got off at San Vicente, which was not the stop I needed but was the stop I could bear. You have to approach these things by degrees, like cold water.

San Vicente and Third had been the heart of it. There had been a bakery there — the Solà bakery, though I didn't know then that the name meant anything to me beyond bread; the world is full of circles you only see from inside them — and a barbershop, and a church whose bells rang wrong for forty years and everyone loved them for it. The bakery was a phone repair shop now. The barbershop was vacant, its window papered over with a poster for a lost cat, sun-bleached to a ghost. The church still stood, deconsecrated and for lease, its bell tower silhouetted against the sodium lights like a raised hand that never got an answer.

I stood on the corner doing the mapping. This is a librarian's grief: I cross-referenced. *Here* — the empty lot with the chain-link — *the fruit stand where Mrs. Aguilar gave Tomás free bruised plums because he was skinny and charming.* Gone. *There* — the laundromat with the broken clock frozen at 4:12 — *where I waited out a rainstorm once with a boy whose name I have honestly forgotten, which seems like a theft I committed against myself.* Still there, the clock presumably still wrong.

A woman came out of the phone shop to lock up, saw me standing there at one in the morning with a notebook, and asked if I was okay. People keep asking me that. I said yes, which is the most fluent lie I own, and she nodded like she didn't believe me and locked the door anyway, and we were both satisfied with the transaction.

Then I walked the four blocks to Alameda Street. I made myself walk them. There is no way to make this interesting; it was four blocks and each one cost more than the last.

* * *

The building was gone. I knew it would be gone. I had known it would be gone for twenty years, in the way you know a diagnosis before the doctor says it. But knowledge and standing in front of it are different currencies entirely.

Where the building had been — five stories, brick, the fire escape Tomás shinnied down to meet girls, our mother's window box with the geraniums she talked to like difficult relatives — there was now a parking structure. Three levels of open concrete, fluorescent-lit, half empty at one-thirty in the morning. A sign at the entrance read MONTHLY PARKING AVAILABLE. INQUIRE WITHIN.

I stood across the street. The ledger was in my coat pocket and I did not take it out. Some entries refuse to be written, and I have learned — am learning, slowly, at fifty-one, like a bad student — to let them stand.

I tried to reconstruct it anyway. Memory is stubborn; it kept projecting the building onto the structure like a slide show nobody asked for. *Third floor, second window from the left:* his room. He'd painted it himself, some terrible orange, and our mother complained about it for a decade and repainted nothing. *Ground floor:* the vestibule with the mailboxes, one of which had been ours, 3B, and into which, the winter before he left, I had put a letter I never mailed and then retrieved, ashamed, before he could find it. I had forgotten that letter for twenty years. Standing in front of the parking structure, I remembered it completely — the weight of it, the envelope going soft at the corners from being carried and re-carried in my coat pocket for weeks. What it said, I couldn't retrieve. Only the carrying. Isn't that always the way. We lose the content and keep the weight.

A car pulled into the structure while I stood there. A young man got out, locked it, jogged up the ramp to whatever apartment he was going home to. He passed within ten feet of me and did not see me, and I want to report that I remained invisible by choice, but the truth is I don't think I could have been visible if I'd tried. I was mostly memory myself at that point, a double exposure standing on a curb.

Here is the arithmetic I did, standing there, because arithmetic is my other native language:

Twenty years since I spoke to him. Nineteen years, eleven months, and some days since he died — I stopped counting precisely at year ten because the precision was becoming a devotion, and devotions of that kind eat you. One bus route remaining between the person I became and the person I was. Zero days until they cancel it. Well — ninety-some days. The city had given me a deadline, and deadlines, I have found, are the only things that move me. I am a creature of due dates. Apparently even my grief required one to get me on the bus.

And underneath the arithmetic, the sentence I had been avoiding for two decades, surfacing now through geography the way water surfaces through concrete, finding every crack:

*I never told him I was sorry. I never told anyone why.*

* * *

I rode the 14 back toward downtown near three in the morning. The driver was a different one now, a woman with gray braids and a radio murmuring boleros low enough to be deniable. The bus was empty except for me. She glanced in the mirror once, twice, and on the third glance said, "You look like you seen a ghost."

"I visited one," I said. "It lives in a parking structure now."

She laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of her — and said, "That's half this route, honey," and turned the boleros up slightly, as if in sympathy, and we rode.

I opened the ledger. Under the date, I wrote the entry I'd been unable to write on the northbound trip:

*Rode the 14 southbound. The neighborhood is annotated, not erased. His building is a parking structure. I remember the weight of an unsent letter and not its contents. Deadline acquired: the route closes in under three months. Conclusion: geography keeps better records than I do, and even geography is being deleted. If I am going to say anything, the window is closing at municipal speed.*

It was the longest entry I'd ever written about myself. It still wasn't aloud. It still wasn't true in the way Mireia Solà would demand truth be — spoken, witnessed, costing something. But it was a crack in the wall, and light was getting through, and light through a crack is how you find the rest of the door.

At the terminal I stepped down into the cold. Behind me the bus idled, waiting for its last run of the night, and I thought: *not its last run ever. Not yet.* There were ninety-odd runs left. I intended, for reasons I couldn't yet file anywhere, to be on more of them.

When I got home there was a message on my answering machine from the library's reference desk, forwarded from the main line, timestamped 9:47 p.m. A woman's voice, accented, unhurried, older than the machine itself seemed able to hold.

"This is Mireia Solà again. You told me to check back about the forms." A pause long enough to contain a lifetime. "I checked back. Nobody called. So I will tell you something instead of asking. The word in my language for a place that exists only in memory — there is such a word. It also means *a debt the living owe.* Think about that, Miss Quintana. I will come Tuesday."

The machine clicked off. I sat in the dark with my coat still on, smelling of diesel and old streets, and understood that the language I had been secretly studying in the closed stacks — the language with no other speakers, the language I'd told myself was professional curiosity — belonged to her. To the bakery on San Vicente. To the neighborhood that isn't there.

She would come Tuesday. And I would have to decide whether to admit I had already begun learning her words behind her back — or whether some debts, like some letters, stay carried and unsent until they rot through the pocket.
