## 13. The Cough

It began, as catastrophes do, with something small enough to fit inside a teacup.

We were in the periodicals room on a Tuesday—Mireia's preferred night because the microfilm machines hummed at a frequency she claimed was "the exact pitch of bureaucratic despair," and she liked to teach against it. She had a word on the board she'd written in chalk, a long one, all elbows: *argipen-ezina*. Unexplainable. She was using it to describe the city's transit authority.

"A word like this," she said, tapping the chalk against the board until a small white dust fell like snow that had given up on ambition, "is for things that are true but refuse to be reasonable. The reroute notice. My landlord. The way you, Silas Quintana, still write your homework instead of saying it."

"I said one sentence aloud last week."

"You said 'the radiator is loud.' That is not a true sentence. That is an alibi with punctuation."

I laughed—actually laughed, out loud, which should have been my first warning. Laughter was not in my inventory. I catalogued silences; I did not produce noise. But there it was, escaping me like a bird from a building that had sealed its windows years ago, and Mireia looked so pleased with herself that I almost forgave her for the radiator remark.

Then she coughed.

It was a small thing. A dry syllable, quickly swallowed. She waved her hand as if shooing it away and continued her sentence about the transit authority's grammar of evasion. But I had spent twenty years listening to what people swallowed, and I heard where the cough came from—not the throat but deeper, somewhere below language, in the country of the body where words have no jurisdiction.

"Water?" I offered, already moving toward the tap in the corner. Another first. In twenty years I had never fetched anything for anyone. Neutrality, I had believed, meant keeping your hands empty.

"Water would be lovely, and also unnecessary." She drank it anyway, all of it, and set the paper cup down with the deliberate care of someone placing a period at the end of a sentence she intended to stop writing.

* * *

The second cough came Thursday. The third came Friday, and by then they had stopped being guests and started being tenants.

I noticed other things too, because noticing was my profession. She paused on the stairs outside the library—two flights she used to take like a woman settling a score with gravity—and stood at the landing with her hand flat against the brick, breathing through her mouth. Her ankles, when I saw them above her shoes, had begun to hold the impression of her sock elastic, small rings pressed into flesh like tree marks. When she laughed—which she still did, often, cruelly, at my expense—the laugh ran out of air before the joke ran out of itself.

"You're swelling," I told her, on the third Friday, with all the delicacy I have ever managed, which is none.

"I'm retaining water. It's different. Swelling is what happens to the sentimental."

"That's not how bodies work."

"And you are an expert on bodies now? You who treat your own like a storage unit?"

She was right, of course. I had spent decades treating mine exactly that way—stacking boxes of unsaid things in the dark and calling the arrangement order. But I knew enough to know that the body does not accept rent forever. Eventually it evicts.

"See a doctor," I said.

"After the lesson."

"Mireia."

"After. The. Lesson." She arranged her cards on the table—we were doing vocabulary that night, the weather words, the ones for fog and thaw and the particular wind that comes off the river smelling of iron—and dealt me my hand like a dealer who suspected the whole game was rigged. "Besides, doctors are just librarians of the flesh. They catalogue symptoms and file them under later. I've been filed under later my whole life. I don't intend to start reading my own record now."

But she went, the following Monday, because her daughter-in-law in Bilbao telephoned and shouted at her in two languages until compliance seemed easier than resistance. I know this because Mireia reported it to me with the weary satisfaction of a woman describing a natural disaster she'd survived: "Nekane has the lungs of a dockworker and the guilt apparatus of an entire cathedral. Against such weapons, even I surrender."

* * *

The hospital called me Wednesday afternoon, which was strange, because I had not known hospitals had my number, and stranger still when I learned Mireia had given it to them weeks ago—"in case of emergency," the nurse said, and I felt something turn over in my chest that I chose not to examine, because examining it would mean admitting I had become someone's emergency contact, which meant admitting I had become someone.

"They've admitted her for observation," the nurse said. "Cardiology. She's asking for you, but she's also telling everyone you're her student and refusing to elaborate, so if you could—"

"I'll come now."

The ward smelled of antiseptic and overcooked carrots. Mireia sat propped up in bed looking smaller than I had ever seen her, though whether the bed had shrunk her or the illness had, I couldn't say. Her hair was unbound, silver and defiant, and she was arguing with the television in her own language, which I understood now well enough to catch: she was telling it, with some precision, that its programming was *lotsotsua*—worthless, garbage, the word you use for a thing that has failed at being a thing.

"There she is," she said when she saw me. "My heir apparent. Pull up a chair and prepare to be bored by cardiology."

"What did they find?"

"Ah. Straight to the catalogue. You are your mother's daughter, whoever she was." She patted the blanket beside her, and I sat, because refusing her had stopped being possible somewhere around chapter five of my life with her. "They found a heart that has decided, after eighty-one years of faithful service, to begin resigning. Congestive heart failure—that's the phrase. *Bihotz-gutxiegitasuna.* Say it."

"*Bihotz-gutxiegitasuna.*"

"Good. Heart-insufficiency. Literally: heart-not-enoughness. Isn't that beautiful? Other languages pretend the heart can simply fail, all at once, dramatically, like a bridge. Mine admits the truth—it was never quite enough, and now the not-enoughness has come due." She said it lightly, the way she said everything unbearable, wrapping the terrible in grammar like a pill in jam. "They want to keep me. Adjust medications. Monitor. The usual theater."

"How long?"

"Straight to the due date, no browsing." She studied me, and for once I watched her decide not to deflect. It cost her; I saw it cost her. "They won't give numbers. Doctors never do—they've learned we build calendars out of them. But I read faces the way you read marginalia, Silas, and every face on this ward is reading me a short book."

The window behind her held a slice of parking structure and sky. I looked at it because looking at her was suddenly difficult, the way bright light is difficult after years underground.

"Weeks," I said. Not a question. I had learned from her that naming a thing takes away half its power, and the other half you learn to carry.

"Weeks. Perhaps a month if I flatter the medication. Less if I continue smoking, which I do not intend to stop, so let us call it weeks." She reached over and took my hand—her grip still strong, her skin papery and warm. "Which means, my careful, invisible girl, that our schedule has changed. We will not be learning the language. We will be saving it. There is a difference, and the difference is urgency."

* * *

I went back to the library that night and did not log a single overheard fragment. The ledger lay open on the desk before me, its columns waiting—time, location, the thing said, the thing withheld—and for the first time in twenty years the whole apparatus looked like what it had always been: a mausoleum with excellent organization.

Instead I made lists. Lists of everything Mireia had taught me, cross-referenced, alphabetized, the vocabulary of a dying language arranged in the only immortality I knew how to offer: filing. If I could not stop the clock, I could at least index it.

Around two a.m., I emailed the diaspora contact again. Alazne Etxeberria, the retired schoolteacher in Toronto whom I'd tracked down through three interlibrary networks and one very confused archivist in Gasteiz. Two weeks ago she had replied with warmth—a voice memo, actually, her voice low and rough, speaking the language haltingly, rustily, but speaking it, proof that Mireia was not the last, that the word *last* might be negotiable. Mireia had listened to the recording four times and pronounced judgment: "Rusty. Imperfect. Alive. Write to her again."

So I wrote. I wrote about the lessons, the recordings we were planning, the urgency. I asked when she might come, or speak again, or simply confirm she existed beyond that one beautiful, impossible memo.

By dawn, no reply. By the following dawn, no reply. By the third day, my email bounced back with a message from a server I didn't recognize: *address no longer active.*

I tried the archivist in Gasteiz. He answered within hours, apologetic, baffled: he had lost the thread of the correspondence himself. Alazne Etxeberria had written to him last spring, then gone quiet. He assumed she had moved. He assumed everyone moved; that was rather the point of a diaspora.

I sat with that silence for a long time, in the green glow of the night desk, understanding it completely. Silence was my native tongue. I knew all its dialects: the silence of shame, of exhaustion, of a person standing at the edge of something too large and stepping backward. Somewhere in Toronto, a woman had opened the door to a dying language, felt the draft, and closed it again. I could not even hate her. I had spent fifty-three years doing precisely the same thing.

But hatred wasn't the problem. The problem was arithmetic. One speaker, failing. One student, incomplete. One diaspora contact, vanished. And a bus route with a termination date that now felt less like bureaucracy than prophecy.

* * *

Mireia lasted six days in the hospital before discharging herself, which surprised no one, least of all the cardiologist, who told her gravely that she was making a mistake, and to whom she replied, in front of witnesses, "Doctor, I am eighty-one years old and fluent in a language no one else speaks. Everything I do from here forward is a mistake. The question is only which mistakes are worth making."

He had no answer for that. Neither, I noticed, did medicine.

She came back to her apartment with a plastic bag of pill bottles and instructions she intended to follow at roughly sixty percent compliance, and she summoned me there on Sunday with a single line in a text message, the first text message she had ever sent me, typed with one finger over the course of what I estimated to be forty minutes:

*Bring the recorder. Bring yourself. Bring nothing else.*

Her apartment was exactly as I remembered it from the night of the cabinet—every surface a sediment layer of a life's attempts: reel-to-reel tapes labeled in fading ink, letters from ministries in three countries, photographs of students who had grown old and scattered and, in several cases, died. She had cleared the kitchen table, though, wiped it down, set out two chairs facing each other like an interrogation or a wedding, depending on your theology.

"Sit," she said. "We begin the recordings tomorrow. Tonight we plan them. Stories first, then songs, then the curses—I insist on the curses, Silas, a language without its curses is a museum piece, and I refuse to be a museum. Then the grammar, dictated slowly, everything I can pour out of myself before the tap runs dry."

"How many sessions?"

"As many as my heart permits. Which is a phrase I never thought I'd use sincerely." She lowered herself into her chair with a care that frightened me more than any cough had—she who had always moved like weather, sudden and total, now moving like a woman crossing ice. "And you will run the equipment, and you will ask the questions, and when I forget a word—and I will forget words now, the doctor says the poor circulation starves the memory—you will wait, and not fill the silence, because the word usually comes back if you let the silence hold the door open for it. You of all people know how to wait in silence. Finally, a use for your pathology."

It was the cruelest and kindest thing she'd ever said to me, and both at once, which was, I was learning, her native register.

"There's something else," I said. "Alazne. The contact in Toronto. Her address is dead. The archivist lost her. She's gone quiet."

I watched the news land in her face and settle there, familiar as an old tenant. She didn't rage. She nodded slowly, the way you nod at a bill you expected.

"Yes," she said. "That's how it goes. Forty years I've watched doors open and close. People write to you full of fire, and then life remembers it has other plans for them, and the fire becomes a letter in a drawer, and the letter becomes a memory, and the memory becomes a story they tell at parties: *I almost learned a dying language once.*" She turned her hands over on the table, palms up, an old woman's gesture of accounting. "I outlived everyone twice, Silas. Once when they died, and once when their interest did. I had hoped—I confess I had hoped—that you would spare me the second death this time."

"I'm still here."

"You are. And here is not nothing. Here may be everything, if we hurry." She leaned forward, and her eyes, sunken now, still had that banked-fire quality, the look of a hearth in a house about to be sold. "So we hurry. Tomorrow, nine o'clock, you bring the machine and we make the first tape. And Silas—"

"Yes."

"The homework continues. Even now. Especially now. One true sentence a day, spoken aloud. You may say it to the recorder if saying it to me is still beyond you. But I warn you"—and here she smiled, tired and terrible—"when I die, and I will die soon, and we both know it, the last thing I intend to hear from you is not 'the radiator is loud.'"

* * *

I rode home on the second-to-last bus, because the last had grown too crowded with mourners-in-advance, people riding the route now for the sake of riding it, taking photographs of stops that would soon be fiction. Through the window, the old neighborhood slid past in its new costume: the parking structure where Tomás's building had stood, the demolition fencing along the arcade, orange signs blooming on every corner like some aggressive invasive flower.

At my stop, I stood in the cold and took out the ledger. Old habit. The page was blank; I hadn't logged anything in days. Under *thing withheld*, I wrote nothing, because the list of withheld things had finally exceeded the page, the book, the system.

What I did instead was practice, under my breath, into the collar of my coat, where no one could witness it: the sentence Mireia would demand, the one I owed, the one about Tomás and the night he left and the sound the motorcycle made, or didn't make, at the intersection where I had promised to meet him and had not gone.

I got as far as his name. Just the name, spoken aloud to the dark, in my own language, which was supposed to be the easy part.

Tomorrow, nine o'clock, the recorder. Weeks, perhaps a month. A language pouring itself out faster than I could catch it, a diaspora gone silent, a bus route dying on schedule, and somewhere inside me, a sentence twenty years overdue, still waiting for a mouth brave enough to issue it.

The clock, I understood now, had never been ticking for Mireia alone.
