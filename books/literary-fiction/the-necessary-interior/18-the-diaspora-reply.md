## 18. The Diaspora Reply

The email arrived on a Tuesday, which felt appropriate. Tuesdays are the day the library receives its interlibrary loan shipments—crates of other people's urgencies, wrapped in brown paper and string—and I have always found something consoling about that. The world's unanswered requests, arriving in bulk.

I was at my desk at 2:40 a.m., doing what I do: logging the night's fragments into the ledger. A man on the 3 a.m. phone call to his mother had said, *I'm not angry, I just want you to know where things stand*, which is a sentence that contains its own opposite. A woman in the study carrels had whispered *please* eleven times over forty minutes, to no one visible, working through some private arithmetic of supplication. And beneath these, as always, my own column—the omissions ledger, the inventory of unsaid things, now running to three notebooks and a growing sense that the notebooks were judging me.

The subject line read: **Re: Solà — query re: last speaker**.

My hand actually shook. I want to be honest about that, because everything else in this account is honest, or is trying to be. My hand shook, and I sat there in the pool of my desk lamp like a person who has been praying for rain and then hears thunder and doesn't trust it.

Her name was Dr. Amara Okonkwo-Reyes. Linguistics, University of somewhere cold and far away. Her reply was four days late by any reasonable standard and twenty years late by Mireia's, and it began with an apology so carefully worded it could have been typeset:

*I apologize for the delay. Your messages went to an address I no longer check regularly—I left that institution some time ago. I confess I opened them with a certain weariness. I have pursued "last speakers" before. Twice. Both times I arrived to find either that the speaker had died, or that the speaker's family wished very much for the language to be preserved but not, if it came to it, learned.*

*But I watched the recordings you attached.*

*Tell me the old woman's name again. Tell me she is still alive when you send this. Tell me when I can come.*

I read it four times. Then I did something I am not proud of and not ashamed of either: I checked the timestamps against Mireia's hospital discharge paperwork, which I keep folded in my wallet now like a talisman, like a ticket for a journey I haven't taken yet. She had come home against advice nine days ago. Dr. Vasquez had used the phrase *weeks, not months* in a voice he probably uses for weather reports, and Mireia had thanked him warmly, as though he'd offered her a compliment.

Weeks. The email wanted dates. The email wanted me to tell her when she could come, and every date I might write down was a date on the wrong side of a door that was already closing.

* * *

I brought it to her the next afternoon, printed out, because Mireia does not trust screens and because I wanted her to hold it. There is something in the weight of paper—a request, an answer, a life's delayed correspondence—that a screen refuses to carry.

She was in her armchair by the window, wrapped in the blue shawl she wears even indoors, sorting through her cabinet of failures. That's what she calls it—not bitterly, but with the flat precision of a woman cataloguing her own museum. Recordings from 1974. Letters from a foundation that had promised funding and delivered a pamphlet. Photographs of students who had learned greetings and then lives. She was holding a cassette tape up to the light as though she could read its contents by refraction.

"Sit," she said, without looking up. "You have the face."

"What face?"

"The face of someone carrying news they've rehearsed." She set the cassette down. "In my language we have a word for a messenger who arrives having practiced their expression in a window first. It translates roughly as 'one who polishes the bad news.' Say what you're carrying, Silas. You'll drop it otherwise."

I handed her the printout.

She read slowly. I watched her lips move over the English, then stop moving altogether, and I understood that she had gone somewhere inside herself where translation happens without the mouth's permission. When she looked up, her eyes were wet, but her voice was dry as catalog card stock.

"She watched the recordings."

"Yes."

"Which ones?"

"The lullaby. The one about the river that argues with itself. And—" I hesitated. "The curse."

Mireia laughed. It turned into a cough, and she rode the cough out with the patience of long practice, one hand pressed flat below her ribs. "Of course the curse. Everyone loves the curse. It's the one thing people ask me to say twice." She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand. "Twenty years I wrote to universities. Twenty years. I wrote to linguists, to cultural ministries, to a man in Geneva whose title I still don't understand. I got form letters. I got one offer to consult on a *film*, Silas—they wanted the language for a war movie, the villain would speak it, it would sound exotic and menacing. I said no. I said no so many times that saying yes started to feel like the betrayal."

"And now?"

"Now a woman with two surnames writes back in four days' worth of delay and asks when she can come." Mireia held the printout at arm's length, studying it like a specimen. "Ask me the question you're not asking."

I didn't answer. In the ledger of my omissions, this moment would occupy half a page.

"You're asking whether hope is cruel," she said. "Whether it's kinder to let me die believing nothing was coming than to let me die believing something was coming and miss it." She folded the pages along their existing creases, precisely. "That's a librarian's question. Weighing the mercy of information. I'll save you the trouble: it isn't your decision."

"Mireia—"

"I outlived everyone I loved twice, I told you. Do you know what the third time would be?" She looked at me then, fully, and there was iron under the frailty. "Outliving the hope too. No. She comes. Whenever she comes. Even if she comes to a grave. Especially if she comes to a grave."

* * *

That night I wrote to Dr. Okonkwo-Reyes myself, and here I must confess to a small dishonesty of omission, which is after all my native genre. I told her the truth about Mireia's health—but the softened version, the version with weeks stretched toward their generous outer limit. I told her flights were possible in six weeks. Six weeks was a number I invented out of love and fear in roughly equal parts, and I have thought since about how a lie told kindly is still a lie, and how Mireia would have called it polishing the bad news, and how she'd have been right.

Dr. Okonkwo-Reyes replied within hours this time. Grant deadlines, teaching obligations, a conference she could not abandon. She could arrive in seven weeks. Seven weeks. She ended with: *Please record everything. Every day. If I arrive to find only recordings, I will build from them. But tell her—tell her I am coming to learn, not to archive. There is a difference, and she will know the difference.*

I read that sentence standing in the periodicals basement, under the humming fluorescent tube that flickers over shelf 14, and I had to put my hand against the shelving. *Coming to learn, not to archive.* Because I knew which one I had been. I had spent months taking, cataloguing, transcribing—building my careful files, my beautiful ordered notebooks, all of it preservation-shaped and none of it inheritance-shaped. Archiving is what you do to the dead. Learning is what you let the living do to you.

Mireia had known the difference from the first lesson. *Not charity,* she'd insisted. *Inheritance.* And I had nodded and gone home and written sentences instead of speaking them, filed words instead of wearing them.

* * *

I brought the second letter to her apartment on Thursday. She was weaker; I noticed it in the way she reached for the teapot and I took it from her, and in the way she let me. Two months ago she'd have swatted my hand. Now she surrendered objects like a queen distributing last gifts.

"Seven weeks," I said.

She was quiet for a long time. Outside her window, the city was performing its evening demolition of daylight—that grey hour when the cranes along the river look like gallows or prayers depending on your disposition.

"Do the arithmetic," she said finally. "Don't spare me. You've been sparing me and it insults us both."

"Seven weeks puts her here in mid-February."

"And I am—" She paused, and I braced for false modesty, and it didn't come. Mireia Solà did not deal in false modesty. "I am a woman whose heart is finishing its sentence. Mid-February is optimistic. It may be mid-January. It may be Sunday."

"So we record until then. Everything. We work like—"

"Like the building is on fire, yes, I know how archives work, I invented despairing archivists." But she smiled, and the smile cost her something and she paid it anyway. Then she grew serious, and leaned toward me, and the lamplight caught the deep lines around her mouth, the geography of eighty-some years of speaking a language almost no one could hear.

"But listen to me, Silas. Listen, because this is the part that matters and I will only have the strength to say it once or twice more." She took my wrist. Her grip was still strong; illness takes the flesh before it takes the will. "If Amara arrives and I am not here, the language does not die with me. Do you understand? It dies with *you*, if you let it. She will come to learn from whoever carries it. The question is not whether inheritance can be posthumous. The question is whether you will accept it while I can still watch you refuse."

"I'm not refusing."

"You are refusing constantly. Beautifully. With footnotes." She released my wrist and settled back. "Every sentence you write instead of speak. Every time you transcribe instead of repeat. You are building the most magnificent mausoleum for a living tongue I have ever seen. And I love you for it, and I am telling you it stops now."

The word *love* landed in the room and neither of us flinched from it, which was new, and which I will not examine further here except to note that some things, once named aloud, become load-bearing.

"Say something," she said. "Right now. Not for homework. For me."

And I—god help me, I opened my mouth, and what came out was English. *"I'm trying."*

Mireia closed her eyes. When she opened them, there was no anger in them, which was worse. "Tomorrow," she said. "We start tomorrow. The fire is lit, Silas. Come and burn with me."

* * *

I walked home the long way, past the terminal, where the reroute notices have multiplied like a species discovering an unoccupied niche. FINAL SERVICE: JANUARY 31. Someone had spray-painted beneath one of them, in letters still dripping: *NOT THE LAST WORD.* The city had already sent crews to sandblast two others. They'd missed this one, or maybe they'd decided a little graffiti was cheaper than a little truth.

I stood there in the cold with my breath making its small white arguments and I thought about posthumous inheritance. About Tomás, who taught me to ride a bike and to swear in two languages and never got to teach me anything else, because I was seventeen and proud and he was nineteen and leaving, and the last thing I said to him was a sentence I have spent twenty years refusing to translate. About Mireia, who outlived everyone twice and is now arranging to be survived by a language. About a woman with two surnames buying a plane ticket to meet a teacher who will likely be a photograph and a hard drive and a night librarian with shaking hands.

Can inheritance be posthumous? I had thought this was the question. Standing at the terminal, I understood it wasn't. The question was whether I would still be hiding in the archive when the heir arrived—whether I would hand Dr. Okonkwo-Reyes a filing cabinet and call it a legacy, or whether I would open my mouth and let the language live in me badly, imperfectly, alive.

Mireia says the blessing reserved for finished students can only be given to a voice, not to a notebook.

January 31 is coming. February is coming. Sunday is possibly coming.

Tonight, before my shift ends, I am going to take out the ledger—all three notebooks—and I am going to do what I should have done months ago. Not read it. Speak it. To the empty reading room, to the shelves, to whatever patronless dark listens at 4 a.m. Practice, Mireia would call it. Rehearsal for the terminal, for the cameras, for the goodbye I owe in a language that is not mine and is becoming mine at exactly the speed of a failing heart.

One true sentence, spoken daily. I owe her months of them.

I'll begin with the hardest: the one about Tomás, and the bus, and the night everything unsaid began.

It's 4:12 a.m. The reading room is empty. My hands are steady.

Almost.
