## 1. Inventory

The Halvard Conservatory at midnight is a different instrument than it is by day.

By day it is brass and ambition, the stairwells ringing with scales, the corridors smelling of coffee and rosin and the particular nervous sweat of people about to be judged. But after the last practice room goes dark, the building exhales. The radiators tick in their own time signatures. The marble floors hold the day's heat in their bones and give it back slowly, like a pianist sustaining a pedal past all reason. I push my cart through the east wing and the wheels make a sound I have come to think of as mine alone — a soft, two-note complaint, *squeak-hush, squeak-hush* — and in nine years no one has ever come to see who is making it.

That is the arrangement I have with this building. It lets me be no one.

I was hired eleven months ago, though the nights have a way of running together, and the woman in HR — a tired, kind-eyed man, actually, named Oskar, who did not look at my references too closely — gave me a lanyard with a photo so blurry I could be anyone. I have decided this is a feature. Lior Dravenmoor's face was, at one point, reasonably well known in certain circles: printed on recital programs, projected on the screen above the competition stage in Geneva, framed in the hallway of the pre-college division with a caption in gold lettering. That girl had cheekbones and a future. The woman on the lanyard has neither, or at least no one has looked hard enough to say.

My shift begins with the keys. There are forty-one of them on the ring, and I know each by the sound it makes turning — the practice rooms all click, the faculty offices all grind, the recital hall's lock has a stutter, two turns and a pause, like a person clearing their throat before a difficult sentence. I have never once thought of this as a waste of my training. I want to be clear about that. People assume, when they learn what I was and what I am, that I must lie awake grieving the wasted years. But there is a discipline to cleaning that resembles music more than most music does: the same devotion to sequence, the same humility before a thing that must be done completely or not at all. You cannot play half a nocturne. You cannot mop half a floor. Well — you can, but the room knows.

After the keys, the inventory.

This part is not in my job description. It is in the notebook.

* * *

The notebook is a cheap one, spiral-bound, its cover softening with the damp of my coat pocket. I bought it at the bodega on Delmore Street the week I started, meaning to keep a log of supplies — and for the first three pages, I did. *Paper towels, 12. Disinfectant, low. Replace bulb, practice room 9.* Then, on the fourth page, without deciding to, I wrote: *The clock in the second-floor corridor is four minutes fast and has been for years, and no one has ever corrected it, and everyone pretends not to be governed by it.*

After that, the notebook became what it is now: a catalogue of the building's objects and my own unsaid sentences, kept in no particular order, because order would have been a kind of ambition and I was finished with ambition.

Tonight's entries, in the order they occurred:

*The bulletin board outside the recital hall still has a poster from the spring faculty recital, curling at the corners. The cellist's name has faded faster than the others. I stood in front of it for four minutes. I did not take it down.*

*A glove, single, black, left hand, on the third-floor landing. Good leather. Someone will come back for it. I put it on the radiator shelf where lost things wait, and I thought: that glove is warmer than I am, and it doesn't even have a hand in it.*

*I heard someone practicing the Schubert B-flat in room 12 — badly, the way you play it in your second year, before your hands learn to be afraid of it. I stood outside the door until the playing stopped. I did not go in. I did not say: you're rushing the third bar, everyone rushes the third bar, the whole point of the third bar is that it resists being rushed. I said nothing. Unsaid sentence number four hundred and something.*

I number them now. It started as a joke I made only to myself, which is the only kind of joke I make anymore. The unsaid sentences. There are hundreds. Some are small — *your bow is crooked, the radiator in 9 is leaking, you left your glove* — and some are the size of cathedrals, and I will not write those here, not even in the notebook, not yet. The notebook has its own thresholds. Even a page can be a stage.

Because that is the truth I circle most nights, mopping: I did not lose the piano. I lost the speaking. Anyone who has heard a great performance knows the performer is talking — not in words, but in the thing words are a rough translation of. For seventeen years that was my language, my only fluent one. Then, one afternoon in Geneva, in front of eleven hundred people, I stopped. Mid-phrase. The way a singer on a tape stops, the way a clock stops, the way a person stops in the middle of a sentence when they finally hear what they're saying.

I have never told anyone what I heard. It is in the notebook, though. Page thirty-one, written at 4 a.m. in a hand I barely recognize as mine. I have not read page thirty-one since I wrote it. Some entries you catalogue and close, like a room you mop around rather than in.

* * *

At 2:15 I take my break in the stairwell of the west wing, because it is the one place in the building with a window that opens, and in October the cold is a kind of company. I sit on the third step with my thermos and my sandwich and I watch the courtyard, where a birch tree is losing its leaves in the unhurried way of things that are not ashamed to be seen changing.

This is when I heard the footsteps.

Not the cleaning kind of footsteps — mine are the only cleaning kind, and I know their rhythm the way I once knew the left hand of a Chopin etude. These were deliberate, descending, with the particular weight of someone who owns the stairs rather than uses them. Heels. Measured. A half-second pause at each landing, the pause of a person listening even when there is nothing to hear.

I knew the footsteps before I could name them. That is the terrible thing about the body: it keeps its own inventory. Nine years, and my spine recognized her before my mind consented to.

Nadia Voss.

My cart was ten feet away, parked by the door to the second-floor corridor, and I did not decide to move — I was simply, suddenly, behind it. Crouched. The smell of the mop water rising around me, gray and honest. Through the gap between the cart's wheels and the doorframe I watched the stairwell above, and then she came down into it, into the sodium light, and there she was.

She was older. Of course she was. But Nadia Voss wears age the way she wore her teaching clothes — precisely, without apology, a charcoal coat and a scarf the color of a storm over water, her gray hair cut short now, her posture still that of a woman who has spent forty years making teenagers sit up straighter. She carried a folder against her chest the way some people carry a small dog. She paused on the landing. She looked at the birch tree through the window, and something crossed her face that I had never seen on it in six years of lessons: an unguarded expression, brief as a fish surfacing.

She had been, for six formative years, the most important person in my life. She had hands that could correct an entire phrase by resting two fingers on your wrist. She had said, when I was fourteen, *you have something the others will have to be given* — and I had built a cathedral on that sentence, and she had stood in the wings in Geneva when the cathedral came down, and afterward she had said — what had she said?

That was the thing. I could not remember. Nine years, and what I remembered was the silence afterward. The drive home. The way she looked out the window. The way she said, finally, *you'll rest, and then we'll begin again,* in the voice of a woman reading from a manual for an appliance she did not understand. And then I never called, and she never — she sent one card, once. I still have it. Unopened. It is in a shoebox with my competition medals, which is to say: in a shoebox with the other things I could not throw away and could not look at.

Nadia stood on the landing for another moment. She was looking at the mop cart now. At the cart, or — no. Not at the cart. At the space behind the cart, which was not empty, which contained a crouching woman in a gray uniform holding a thermos, and Nadia Voss missed nothing, had never missed anything, that was the whole terror of her.

Her eyes moved across the gap. One second. Two.

I did not breathe. I had not been a performer in nine years but I still knew how to be still, stillness being the one technique that never leaves you. The mop water ticked as it settled. Somewhere below, a radiator knocked twice, like a polite request for entrance.

Then a door opened on the floor above — Oskar, maybe, or a student pulling an all-nighter — and Nadia turned toward the sound, and the moment passed over me like weather. She descended the rest of the stairs without pausing again, and the door at the bottom sighed shut behind her, and I sat on the cold linoleum behind my cart with my heart going like a beginner's scales.

I should have felt foolish. I did feel foolish. But underneath the foolishness was something I recognized with a kind of grief: relief. The relief had a taste, metallic, like the water from the conservatory's old fountains. I had hidden, and it had worked, and being no one had kept me safe, and safe felt exactly like nothing, and nothing was the thing I had chosen, and I sat there tasting my choice.

I opened the notebook. My hands were not quite steady. I wrote:

*She almost saw me. I hid behind a mop cart from the woman who taught me everything. Unsaid sentence number — I don't know the number. The one that begins: I'm sorry. The one that begins: you were there. The one that begins: why didn't you —*

I stopped writing. The pen made a small hole in the page.

* * *

The rest of the shift, I worked the east wing, and I worked it slowly, because slowness is a way of thinking. By four a.m. I had arrived, without meaning to, at the end of the corridor — the one I avoid, the one past the practice rooms where the light fixtures are older and hum at a pitch I can feel in my teeth. The corridor ends at a door I had never had reason to open: 14B.

But tonight there was a work order clipped to my cart that hadn't been there before. Oskar's blocky handwriting: *14B — deep clean, request of Dr. Ostrov, she asked for nights, said she works nights too, said please.* The word *please* underlined twice, which Oskar never did.

Dr. Ostrov. The name meant nothing to me. The linguistics offices had always been a country I had no passport for; musicians and linguists share a building the way neighbors share a wall, hearing each other's lives through it without ever meeting.

I unlocked 14B with a key I had to try three times, and the door swung open onto lamplight and paper — boxes of them, hundreds, stacked along every wall, and on a desk buried under a cardigan and a magnifying glass and a teacup with a chip in its lip, a machine I recognized only from old films: a reel-to-reel recorder, its two spools waiting like open hands.

And in the lamplight, in a wheelchair, wrapped in a shawl the color of the storm-scarf I had seen three hours earlier on a different woman — a small, ancient face turned toward me, bright-eyed, entirely awake, looking directly at me the way no one had looked at me in nine years.

"You're the night person," she said. "Good. I hoped it would be you."

I stood in the doorway with my cart and my keys and my unsaid sentences, and she smiled at me like a woman who has already read the fourth page of a certain spiral-bound notebook, and she said:

"Come in, Lior. I've been waiting to meet you since 2011."

I had not told her my name. The lanyard photo was too blurry to read. But she was already turning to the machine, already reaching for a spool, and I understood — with the particular vertigo of a person stepping off a ledge they have been standing on for years — that I was going to go in.
