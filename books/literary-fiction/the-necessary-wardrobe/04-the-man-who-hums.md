## 4. The Man Who Hums

The station was called KJUN, 1340 on the AM dial, and its transmitter stood in a field of salt grass at the edge of town like a rusted lighthouse warning ships away from land instead of toward it. Ansel had called three times before anyone picked up. The first two calls, the answering machine had played a jingle so old the tape warbled, a woman's voice promising *all the news that fits the harbor*, and then a dial tone that went on so long he began to suspect it was a recording of a dial tone, a dead line preserved like a fly in amber.

The third time, a man answered on the fourth ring, mid-sentence, as if Ansel had interrupted a conversation with himself.

"—no, you can't run the fishing report at six, the tide tables don't come in till five-forty, who told you—" A pause. "KJUN, this is Ovid."

Ansel said nothing for a moment. He was sitting in his car outside the restaurant where he'd written last night's review, the notebook open on the passenger seat, and the word *KJUN* seemed to hang in the humid air between him and the dashboard.

"The song," he said finally. "You played it. Last night. Around eleven-forty."

A silence on the other end, longer than dead air, shorter than a lie.

"Which song?" the man said, and his voice had changed—flattened, careful, the voice of a man who had been asked this question before and had learned not to like the answers.

"The one without a name. Piano and a man humming. It ends in the middle of a phrase."

Another silence. Then, quietly, almost with relief: "You heard the ending. Most people call about the beginning."

The station was a converted bait shop, and it still smelled like one—brine and two-cycle engine oil soaked so deep into the walls that no amount of carpeting or acoustic foam had ever dislodged them. A hand-painted sign over the door read KJUN 1340, THE VOICE OF THE HARBOR, though the harbor it spoke for had been shrinking for thirty years, its boats sold, its cannery a condominium with a marina facade. Ansel pushed through the door into a room that was half studio, half archive, half attic—three halves, like the town itself, which had never learned to add up.

The man behind the mixing board was sixty or so, brown-skinned, with a beard gone entirely white and a cardigan the color of weak coffee. He wore headphones around his neck like a stethoscope. He looked at Ansel the way a doctor looks at a man who has driven a long way to hear something difficult.

"You're the critic," Ovid Reyes said. "Ansel Coldharbour. I'd know the voice. You did a piece on the Oyster Cellar two years ago that made the owner cry on air when he called in. Happy crying," he added. "Mostly."

"You know my voice."

"I know voices. It's the whole job." He gestured at a folding chair that had once, judging by the logo faded on its back, belonged to a bait and tackle shop. "Sit. You want coffee? It's terrible. It's the only honest thing about this station."

Ansel sat. He did not want coffee. He wanted, with an intensity that frightened him, the thing he had driven ninety minutes to ask for, and he found that he could not say it directly. Eleven years of not-saying had atrophied the muscle.

"The song you played," he said. "Where did it come from?"

Ovid Reyes took off his headphones and set them down with the deliberation of a man buying time. Behind him, a reel-to-reel machine sat on a shelf among dozens of others, their plastic cases yellowed, their spines labeled in different inks and different decades. Ansel's eyes went to them and stayed there. Ovid saw where he was looking.

"You're the first person who's come in person," Ovid said. "Eleven years I've been getting these tapes. You're the first one who walked through the door."

"Eleven years." The number went through Ansel like a needle finding a nerve. "You've been playing them for eleven years?"

"Once a month, maybe. Sunday nights, the last hour, when nobody's listening but the insomniacs and the shrimpers coming in early." He rubbed his beard. "I call it the Unfinished Hour. Nobody's ever complained. That should tell you something about my audience."

"Where do the tapes come from?"

Ovid stood, went to a filing cabinet in the corner, and pulled open the bottom drawer. Inside, stacked flat like specimens in a drawer of a natural history museum, were cassette tapes. Ansel counted without wanting to: more than a hundred. Each one labeled in the same careful, slanted hand—dates, mostly, and occasionally a single word. *Tern. Salt. Marguerite's window.* Ansel felt the floor tilt slightly at that last one and gripped the arm of the chair.

Ovid lifted one out and held it up to the light, as though checking its age by its rings.

"They come in the mail. No return address. Postmarked from right here in town, most of them. Sometimes from the next county. Plain envelopes, no note, ever." He turned the tape over. "And here's the thing, Mr. Coldharbour, the thing I've never said on air because it sounds like madness and this town has enough of that already: these are songs nobody has written. I know because I've checked. I play music for a living; I know what exists and what doesn't. I called a friend at the copyright office once, a woman who owes me a favor, and I hummed four bars down the phone and asked her to search. She came back a week later and said, *Ovid, either you've lost your mind or you've found a genius.*"

"And you think—"

"I think a genius doesn't mail his work to a dying AM station in a town with one traffic light and never once wants credit." Ovid set the tape down. "I think these songs come from somewhere else. I've thought a lot of things over the years. I've thought the sender was a ghost. I've thought the sender was me, sleepwalking, which is the kind of thought you have at three in the morning in a bait shop." He looked at Ansel directly then, and his eyes were kind and tired. "But you didn't drive ninety minutes to talk about my theories. You recognized one."

Ansel's hands had gone cold. He put them flat on his knees.

"The one from last night," he said. "The piano one. It ends in the middle of a phrase—"

"Every one of them ends in the middle of a phrase. That's the other thing. They're all unfinished. Like the man ran out of tape, or out of time, or out of—" Ovid stopped. "You know it."

It was not a question. Ansel heard it anyway, and heard what was underneath it: *please know it, please be the one person in eleven years who can tell me what I've been holding.*

"My brother wrote it," Ansel said.

The words came out of him with a strange mechanical clarity, as if they had been stored in a separate part of him, a part that had been waiting for this room, this smell of brine, this drawer of tapes.

"Theo. Theo Coldharbour. He never wrote it down. He never recorded it. He used to hum it when he was sanding the hull of his skiff, and he'd stop whenever anyone came near, and he'd say—" Ansel's voice caught. "He'd say it wasn't finished yet. That he'd play it for everyone when it was finished. He drowned before it was finished. Eleven years ago. And last night I heard it on your station, in a car, on a highway, and I drove into a ditch."

Ovid Reyes did not say *I'm sorry for your loss*, which Ansel would later realize was the single most decent thing anyone had done for him in a decade. Instead the old man turned in his chair, threaded a tape onto the reel-to-reel with hands that shook only slightly, and said, "Which part did he stop at?"

Ansel closed his eyes. He had not let himself hear it, really hear it, in eleven years—he had spent a decade building a life with no room in it for the sound of his brother humming, and now it rose in him whole, note for note, the way a language you never officially spoke rises in you when you are dying or dreaming.

"He'd get to the fourth phrase," Ansel said. "There's a place where it should turn, where it should go up instead of down, and he'd stop. He said the turn wasn't true yet. He said he'd know it when he—" 

"—found it," Ovid finished, and pressed play.

The piano came out of the small speaker, tinny and warm at once, and then the humming, and Ansel sat in a bait shop that smelled like his childhood summers and listened to his dead brother's unfinished song in a room where a hundred more of them lay stacked in a drawer, and he understood that he had been wrong for eleven years. He had thought he was the keeper of what Theo left behind. He had built an inventory of everything unsaid and believed himself the sole archivist of a dead man's silences.

But someone had been recording them. Someone had been mailing them, month after month, year after year, to a man who played them to insomniacs and shrimpers. Someone who knew the songs no one else had ever heard. Someone who labeled one of the tapes *Marguerite's window*.

The song ended, mid-phrase, in the place where the turn wasn't true yet.

"Who sends them?" Ansel asked. His voice sounded like someone else's. "You must know. Eleven years—there has to be something. A postmark. A handwriting. A time of day the mail comes."

Ovid Reyes looked at him for a long moment, and then he reached into the drawer again, past the tapes, and came out with a rubber-banded bundle of envelopes, soft with handling, and set them on the mixing board between them like a hand of cards he'd been losing with for a decade.

"There's one thing," he said. "One thing I never followed up on, because I was afraid of what I'd find, and because a man my age learns that some doors are better left shut. But you've got a dead brother's song in your mouth and a ditch in your car's undercarriage, so I figure you're past being careful." He untied the band with fingers that had finally stopped shaking. "The postmark. The early ones, the first two years, they're all from the general post office. But the last nine years—" He slid one envelope across the board. "Look at the cancellation."

Ansel looked.

The postmark was faded but legible, the ink of a stamping machine that had been striking envelopes for decades: a date, a town, and beneath them, the words *PIKE MARINE & HARBOR CO. — STATION AGENT*.

The marina office. The marina where Theo had worked the summers of his last three years. The marina owned by Harlan Pike, the man who had stood on the dock in his good coat and told the gathered family, the police, the whole town, that it was an accident, that the water was high and the night was dark, that boys who knew boats didn't always come home from them—and had said it with such authority, such settled finality, that everyone, including Ansel, had let it be true.

The room was very quiet. Somewhere in the walls, the old transmitter hummed its constant note, a sound Ansel realized he had been hearing all his life without ever listening to it, the way he had heard everything, the way he had tasted nothing.

"Mr. Coldharbour," Ovid Reyes said gently, "you've gone the color of the mail."

Ansel picked up the envelope. The paper was cheap, the kind sold in boxes at the drugstore, the kind the marina office kept by the register for invoices. And on the front, in that careful slanted hand, his own name and address—his mother's old address, the house that was being sold on Friday, the house that still had one locked room in it that no one had opened in eleven years.

"Ovid," he said. "Has a tape ever come with a note? Just once. Just one word."

The old man was quiet for so long that the transmitter's hum seemed to fill the whole building. Then he reached into his cardigan pocket and took out a slip of paper, yellowed, folded into eighths, worn soft as cloth at the creases.

"Once," he said. "Three years ago. I've never played what was on the tape. I've never told anyone what the note said. I kept it because I didn't know who it belonged to." He unfolded it and turned it toward Ansel. "I think maybe it belongs to you."

The handwriting was Theo's.

Ansel knew it the way he knew the song—whole, instantly, unbearably. Four words, in the language the two of them had invented as boys, the language that had no official existence, the language that had died on the night Theo did, or so Ansel had believed for eleven years.

He read them. And his blood, which had been cold for a decade, went colder still, because the words were not a greeting, not a farewell, not a song.

They were a warning.
