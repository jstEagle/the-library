## 3. Harmattan Season

The plane descended through a ceiling of harmattan haze, and Lagos came up at her like a memory she'd tried to drown — brown-gold, dust-thick, the lagoon a dull pewter plate. Zephyrine pressed her forehead to the window and breathed in the recycled cabin air, because she already knew what waited outside: woodsmoke and diesel and roasting plantain and the sea's salt rot, all of it stacked in layers she could name but not feel. A sommelier's vocabulary. A dead woman's tongue.

Her editor had called it madness. *You're mid-arc on the London season, Zee. You can't just vanish to Lagos for a house sale. Send a lawyer. Send Adaeze — whoever she is — a power of attorney.* Marguerite had a gift for making cowardice sound like scheduling. Zephyrine had said something polished in reply, something about needing to see the wardrobe with her own eyes, and hung up before the polish cracked.

The truth was simpler and uglier: the box was in her carry-on. Forty-one letters in her own hand, and beneath them, in her mother's, replies she had never been meant to find and had not yet found the courage to read past the first page. She had read that first page eleven times on the flight. *So you are alive and writing,* it began, in the upright, unhurried script Zephyrine had spent twenty years failing to forget. *Good. Then you can also read this.*

She had not read further. She had ordered a second gin and watched the map crawl south.

Murtala Muhammed Airport received her the way Lagos receives everything — loudly, without ceremony, as if she were both expected and irrelevant. The heat found her at the jet bridge, not a temperature so much as a presence, a hand laid flat against her chest. She stood in the immigration queue registering smells the way a deaf woman registers music: by structure, by inference. Petrol. Fried meat somewhere beyond the glass. The particular green-rot sweetness of the drainage channel she had grown up calling *the river*, because her mother had refused to let her call it anything uglier.

Her body knew these things. That was the obscenity of it. Her body catalogued them with a connoisseur's precision and delivered nothing — no pleasure, no revulsion, just inventory. She had built a career on this inventory. *Notes of charred scotch bonnet, a whisper of fermented bean, the finish like a held breath.* Reviews written the way a portrait artist paints the dead.

The driver Auntie Bisi had sent — a silent, enormous man named Emeka who took her single bag without asking which it was — drove her through traffic that had not learned she'd been gone. Danfo buses with scripture on their bumpers. A hawker threading between cars with a tray of gala and pure water balanced on his head like a crown. The Third Mainland Bridge unspooling across the lagoon, and beneath it the water holding the whole grey sky, and Zephyrine thought: *I wrote about this once. I won a prize for a piece about this bridge, and I wrote it from a hotel in Copenhagen.*

Her mother's street had narrowed. Or the world had widened; it was impossible to say. The house still stood behind its wall, though, ochre paint gone the colour of weak tea, the bougainvillea her mother had fought with for thirty years now winning decisively over the gate. Emeka honked twice, a pattern, and a side door opened before the car stopped.

Auntie Bisi stood in it.

She was smaller than Zephyrine remembered, wrapped in a wrapper the blue of deep water, her head bare, her face doing the work of a full tribunal. She looked at Zephyrine for a long time. Long enough for Emeka to set the bag down and withdraw, for the generator next door to cycle up, for a lizard to cross the compound wall with the unhurried entitlement of a landlord.

"Zephyrine," Auntie Bisi said at last. Not a greeting. An identification, the way a coroner identifies.

"Auntie."

"You look like her." She said it the way other people say *you look terrible.* "You stand like her too. Like the ground owes you money." She stepped back from the door. "Come inside. The fan in the front room is broken and I have not fixed it, because it is not my house. Everything in this house is not my house. That is my whole life now."

The front room was exactly as it had been in Zephyrine's memory and entirely wrong — the same lace curtains gone grey, the same framed cross-stitch (*A HOME WITHOUT LOVE IS JUST A COMPOUND*), the same brown leather settee with its plastic still on the seats, preserved against a future that had never come. The air held camphor and old varnish and, faintly, from the kitchen at the back, the ghost of pepper. Zephyrine's chest did something complicated.

"You came," Auntie Bisi said, lowering herself into an armchair with the sound of a woman settling in for a long trial. "Adaeze said you would not come until the papers were ready. She said, *Auntie, my sister signs things and leaves things, that is what she does.* I told her, wait. The mother was my friend forty years. The daughter must have something."

"I came as soon as—"

"You came when the house was being sold." Auntie Bisi's voice was not loud. It had the terrible gentleness of a woman who had rehearsed this. "Twenty-two years, Zephyrine. I buried your mother and I watched the road for you and the road stayed empty. Now the wardrobe must be moved and suddenly Lagos has an airport again." She picked a piece of lint from her wrapper with great care. "Sit down. You will take water. You will not explain yourself to me, because I have heard every explanation Lagos has and yours will not be new."

Zephyrine sat. The plastic on the settee crackled under her like a reprimand.

"I found letters," she said. "In the wardrobe. Ones I wrote her. And she—" The sentence failed. She had written reviews of truffle and sea urchin and a twenty-course meal built entirely around smoke, and she could not finish a sentence about her mother.

"I know what is in the wardrobe." Auntie Bisi said it flatly, and the flatness was worse than anger. "Who do you think found them? You think the wardrobe opened itself? I was cleaning after the burial. I pulled it out to move it and the back panel was loose — your mother's doing, she hid everything behind panels, her money, her grievances." She looked at Zephyrine then, and something in her face shifted, some grief older than the argument. "She read every one of your letters. Every one. She answered every one. And then she put the answers in the box with the letters and she told me, *Bisi, if she ever comes back, give her the box. If she never comes back, burn it.*"

The fan — the broken one — ticked as it cooled. Somewhere outside, a woman was calling a child home in a voice that climbed three floors of evening.

"She never came back," Auntie Bisi said. "So I nearly burned it. I sat with the matches. I want you to know that. I sat with the matches for a whole harmattan." She rose, joints announcing themselves, and stood over Zephyrine with the blue wrapper like a flag. "But I am a coward about fire and a sentimental woman, so I kept it, and now you are here, sitting on plastic like a visitor in your own mother's house, and I will tell you the only thing I have to tell you."

Zephyrine waited.

"Your mother was not waiting for an apology," Auntie Bisi said. "She was waiting for you to cook. That is all. That was the whole of it, at the end. She said, *Bisi, if she comes, tell her the kitchen is still hers. Let her cook one meal before she goes.*" She turned toward the back of the house, toward the smell of old pepper and camphor. "The kitchen is still there. Nobody has cooked in it since she died. Adaeze cannot — she says the kitchen belongs to a ghost and she will not cook for a ghost. So. There is your inheritance, Zephyrine. A kitchen and a ghost."

She paused at the doorway, and for one moment her voice lost its tribunal and became simply old and tired and fond.

"You have her hands," she said. "I saw them when you took off your gloves at the door. She had those hands. She used to say a cook's hands remember what the mouth forgets." Then the tribunal returned. "Dinner is at eight. Adaeze is coming. I advise you to sleep before then. You will need your strength for my cooking and your patience for my company, and I cannot tell you which will be harder."

* * *

The guest room had been hers. That was the first thing. The bed under the window, the crucifix her mother had nailed above it despite the fact that no one in the house had been Catholic in living memory, the water stain on the ceiling shaped like a country that kept redrawing its borders. Zephyrine sat on the bed with the box on her knees and the harmattan light going amber through the curtain gap, and she did not open the box.

She opened the window instead.

The street came in whole. Generators and gospel music and a boy selling bread from a basin, his call rising and falling like a man negotiating with God. The smell of the evening meal moving house to house — she could name every component, onion base first, then crayfish, then the pepper hitting oil, that specific cough-inducing bloom — and she stood at the window like a woman pressing her face against the glass of an aquarium, watching the life she'd been born into swim past on the other side.

Homesickness, she thought, was supposed to be for places you remembered. Nobody warned you it could run the other direction. That a place could remember you — could hold your bedroom, your mother's friend, your mother's handwriting in a box — with a fidelity you had never earned, and that the sickness was not for Lagos at all but for the girl Lagos remembered, the one who left before she could be held to anything.

Her phone buzzed. Marguerite: *Confirming you've landed safely and that this is a brief personal matter and not a nervous breakdown. The Grayling wants 800 words by Friday. There's a tasting at Clove — I'll decline for you.*

Zephyrine typed: *Tell them I'm researching.* Then she deleted it, because it was a lie, and typed: *Tell them I'm learning to taste.* Then she deleted that too, because it was worse — it was a promise, and she had twenty years of evidence about what her promises were worth.

She put the phone face-down on the bed, beside the box.

From the kitchen came the sound of Auntie Bisi working — the rhythmic knock of a knife against a board, the rattle of a pot lid, and once, unmistakably, the dry whisper of dried bitterleaf being crumbled into a mortar. Zephyrine went still. She knew that sound. She had grown up on that sound. But bitterleaf in pepper soup — her mother had never—

The knife stopped. Auntie Bisi's voice floated down the corridor, not raised, not aimed, just left where Zephyrine would find it:

"Your mother changed the recipe the year you left. Ask Adaeze. Or better — don't ask anybody. Cook it."

Zephyrine looked at the box on the bed. Forty-one letters in her hand. Forty-one replies in her mother's, unread past the first page, sitting on top of a recipe that had been rewritten in her absence like a sentence amended by someone who had finally stopped waiting for the author to come home.

Outside, the harmattan haze was thickening, the streetlights coming on in soft halos, the whole city wrapped in dust that had traveled from the Sahara to be here, to be nowhere else. Zephyrine reached for the box, and stopped, and did not know which she feared more — what her mother had written, or that she had written it in a kitchen that no longer belonged to her.
