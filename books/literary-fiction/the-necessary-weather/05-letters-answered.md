## 5. Letters, Answered

She read them on the floor of her mother's bedroom, cross-legged on the bare boards where the bed used to be, because the kitchen table felt like a place where other things might happen and she wanted no other things. The box sat open beside her. Forty-one of her own letters on top, forty-one answers beneath, and she had spent the whole of the first night unable to touch the answers at all, as though they were a mirror she'd been avoiding for fifteen years.

Now it was morning. Harmattan light came through the shutters in dusty blades. Auntie Bisi had left garri and groundnuts on the veranda without knocking, the way people leave offerings, and Zephyrine had not eaten them. Her hands were steady in the way that hands are steady just before they are not.

She took out the first reply. The envelope said only *ZEPHYRINE* in her mother's tall, severe hand, the letters like fence posts. The postmark was Lagos, dated eleven days after the letter Zephyrine had written from a hostel in Peckham, the letter in which she had said, among other unforgivable things, that she was never coming back.

*So,* the letter began. No greeting. No name beyond the one on the envelope.

*So. You have written to tell me you are never coming back. You wrote this in English, which is interesting, because when you are angry you forget your Igbo and when you are frightened you forget your English, and I have read your letter three times and I cannot decide which you are. I think both.*

*You say I chose the restaurant over your graduation. I say the restaurant fed us. You say I chose the restaurant over you. I say the restaurant was you. Everything I did with my hands was for you and you have written to me to tell me that my hands were empty.*

*Do not write to me again until you have something true to say.*

Her mother had signed it with her full name, the way she signed complaints to the electricity company. Zephyrine set it down. Her face was wet without her permission.

She had remembered the estrangement as a silence she had chosen, dignified, mutual, a door closed from both sides. The letters told a different story: she had closed the door and her mother had stood at it, knocking, for years.

* * *

The second letter, six months later, was shorter. *I sold the blue dress you left. It did not fit me. I did not need it to fit me. I needed it out of the wardrobe so the wardrobe could hold other things.* It was not about the dress. Zephyrine understood that immediately, the way you understand a language you claim not to speak. Her mother had been clearing space — in the wardrobe, in the house, in the furious architecture of her heart — and she had announced it in the only dialect available to her, which was inventory.

The third letter softened by a degree. The fourth asked after her health in a way that was not a question. *I hope London is feeding you. Lagos is feeding me too well; Auntie Bisi says I am getting fat and I told her fat is what happens to a woman when her daughter stops breaking her heart, and she laughed at me, and I did not.*

By the ninth letter Zephyrine had stopped reading standing up. She was lying on her side on the bare boards now, letters fanned around her like a hand of cards she was losing. Her mother's handwriting changed over the years — began tall and severe, then leaned, then loosened, until by the letters of Zephyrine's thirties it had a cursive warmth that Zephyrine had never once seen in person. Her mother had grown old in this correspondence. Grown old writing to a daughter who filed her replies in a box behind a wardrobe and did not open it.

Except — and this was the vertigo, the thing that made her sit up with her hand at her mouth — Zephyrine *had* sent the letters. She remembered writing them. She remembered the walk to the post office in Camberwell, the weight of the envelope in her coat pocket, the way she had stood outside for ten minutes arguing with herself. She had posted them. She was almost certain she had posted them.

And her mother had answered every one.

Which meant the answers had come, and been taken, and hidden. Hidden by whom? Zephyrine had left Lagos at twenty-six. The flat in Peckham had been hers alone. Unless — she counted backward, appalled by the arithmetic — unless the letters had been forwarded. Unless someone in Lagos had received them and never passed them on. Unless the silence she had blamed her mother for had been manufactured, maintained, by a third hand.

She sat with that for a long time. The fan turned. Somewhere below, a vendor called out the price of something in a voice like a bell.

No. She made herself stop. There was no one left to blame who wasn't already dead, and blame was the currency she had been trading in for fifteen years and it had bought her nothing but a column in a newspaper and a palate full of ash. She put the question down the way you put down a hot pot: carefully, knowing you will have to pick it up again.

* * *

The middle letters were the hardest, because they were the kindest.

Her mother wrote about the house — the roof leaking in the second rainy season, the generator, the neighbor's goat. She wrote about Auntie Bisi's daughter's wedding in such detail that Zephyrine could see the aso-ebi, could smell the jollof. She wrote about a girl called Adaeze, first in passing — *the girl I told you about helps me at the shop now, she is quick with figures* — and then with a weight that made the name impossible to skip: *Adaeze has moved into the back room. Her people are gone, all of them, and I could not watch her go into the world alone the way you went. She is not you. I am not asking her to be you. But the house has two daughters in it now, and only one of them speaks to me.*

Zephyrine read that sentence four times. *The house has two daughters in it now.* Somewhere in Lagos, while Zephyrine was writing rapturous lies about tasting menus, a girl she'd never heard of was drying her mother's plates.

The letters never asked her to come home. That was the thing that undid her. They never said *come back, I am dying, I forgive you.* They said: *I made pepper soup today and used too much uziza. Your father used to say a woman who oversalts her soup is a woman thinking about something else. He was wrong. A woman who oversalts her soup is a woman thinking about you.*

And once, only once, near the end: *I am not angry anymore. I want you to know that. Anger is a fire that needs feeding and I am too old to fetch wood. What is left is simpler and worse. I miss you the way you miss a limb. Not as a thing you lost. As a thing that was part of how you moved through the world.*

* * *

The last letter was in a different envelope. Thicker, softer paper, the kind bought deliberately, and the handwriting had gone thin and careful, the writing of a woman conserving strength for the sentence itself.

*Zephyrine.*

*The doctor has told me what the doctor tells everyone, and I am telling you because you are my daughter and you will hear it from someone eventually and I would rather it were me. I am not afraid. I have made my peace in the kitchen, which is where I have made every peace of my life.*

*I do not ask you to come home. I know you. You will read this and you will book a flight and you will stand at the door and neither of us will know what to say, and it will be a small thing, a polite thing, and we will have spent our one meeting on politeness. So I ask you for something smaller.*

*Before you leave Lagos — whenever you come, however briefly — cook one meal in my kitchen. Not for me. I may be gone. Cook it for yourself. Use my pot. The big one, the dented one, you will know it. Follow the recipe in the blue notebook, the last version, not the one you learned as a girl. I changed it. You will want to change it back. Don't.*

*I have written to you every year for fifteen years and you have never written back, and I have decided, in the end, that this is all right. Because I know you, Zephyrine. I know that you keep everything. You kept my letters even though they hurt you — yes, I know you received them, Bisi's son carried them to the courier office in Surulere and the courier confirmed delivery, year after year, and you never once answered, and I have decided that a woman who keeps the letters she cannot answer is a woman who intends, someday, to answer them.*

*So I have kept writing. For the girl who will come back. She is you, later. She is you with more years in her. I have been writing to her all along.*

*Cook the meal. Taste it. That is all. That is the whole of what I ask.*

*Your mother,*

*Ngozi Okonkwo*

Zephyrine read it twice. Then she understood what she had just read, and read the middle of it again, and the floor tilted.

*Yes, I know you received them.*

Her mother had known. All those years, the unanswered letters — her mother had known they arrived. And she had written anyway, fifteen years of letters into a silence, on the faith that the silence was not refusal but paralysis. That her daughter was not cruel. That she was *stuck*.

And she had been right. That was the shame of it, the shame arriving not like a wave but like a change in the weather, total and atmospheric: her mother had died knowing her better than Zephyrine knew herself. Had died having correctly diagnosed the girl who left — not heartless, just frozen — and had written fifteen years of letters to the woman she would become, and had died *confident* the woman would come.

Zephyrine sat on the bare boards of the bedroom and cried the way she had not cried at the funeral, which she had watched on a video call from a hotel in Copenhagen between two reviews. She cried for the letters she'd hidden from herself, or that had been hidden, she cried for Adaeze drying plates in the back room, she cried for the blue notebook she now knew existed and did not yet have, and underneath all of it she cried with something that was not grief at all but its opposite — the terrible, tender discovery of being known.

Her mother had believed she would come back.

She had come back. Six weeks late, to a house with a sold sign, to a sister with her mother's stubbornness, to a box of answers she had never opened.

She gathered the letters in order, oldest on top, and held them against her chest the way you hold a child, or a wound. Then she heard footsteps on the stairs — Adaeze's, she knew them now, unhurried, deliberate — and a knock she did not wait to be answered.

"The developer's people called," Adaeze said from the doorway. She was holding a blue notebook. "They want to close early. Two weeks, not six." Her eyes went to the letters, to Zephyrine's face, and something in her jaw tightened. "And I found this. In the kitchen. I assume it's yours by inheritance."

She held out the notebook. On its cover, in her mother's hand: *Pepper Soup — final. Do not argue.*

"Adaeze," Zephyrine said, and her voice came out ruined. "Did you know? About the letters?"

Her sister looked at her for a long moment, and the look was not unkind, which was somehow worse.

"I knew she wrote them," Adaeze said. "I posted half of them myself." She turned and went down the stairs, and Zephyrine sat alone with the notebook in her lap and the last letter open beside her, and outside, the first fat drops of the rainy season began to hit the shutters.
