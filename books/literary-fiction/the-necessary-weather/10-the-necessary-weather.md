## 10. The Necessary Weather

The review that changed everything was four hundred words long and contained the word *cannot* eleven times.

I know because my editor counted. He called me on a Tuesday in February, his voice doing the careful thing it does when he thinks I've finally lost my mind. "Zeph. You gave the Connaught two stars and spent most of the column explaining you couldn't taste the sauce."

"I could see it," I said. "It had a beautiful shine. Like lacquer, like a piano. I described what I saw."

"You wrote that eating there felt like 'reading a love letter through glass.'"

"Is that not accurate?"

There was a silence on the line with a whole weather system in it. Then he said, "It's the best thing you've ever written," in the tone of a man admitting to a crime.

The column ran under a new banner — *The Necessary Weather* — because I'd told him I wanted to stop pretending the critic and the woman were different people, and he'd said fine, but give me something for the masthead. The letters came in after that first honest one. Hundreds of them. People who cooked for relatives who no longer tasted. People who had lost smell to surgery, to Covid, to grief they didn't have a name for. A man in Leeds who hadn't been able to taste anything since his divorce and said my column was the only food writing that didn't make him feel like a ghost at a banquet.

I answered every letter. It took entire Sundays. Adaeze called it penance; she said it the way she says most kind things, so it sounds like an accusation.

* * *

London in March is a city apologizing for itself. Rain comes down the window of my flat in thin gray ropes, and I sit at the table where the wardrobe now stands — shipped across an ocean, absurdly large for the room, smelling faintly of camphor and old cedar — and I write.

Not reviews, in the evenings. Something else.

It started as notes. Sentences I couldn't use in a column: *Her mother's kitchen had two temperatures, boiling and forgiveness.* *The sisters fought the way weather fights, without anyone winning.* I told myself it was research, that literary fiction was just criticism with a longer leash. But by April the notes had names, and the names were not quite ours — Nnenne instead of Zephyrine, Chiamaka instead of Adaeze — and by May there were forty pages, and the forty pages had a shape I recognized the way you recognize your own handwriting on a stranger's letter.

Two sisters. One kitchen. A house being sold out from under both of them while they argue about a recipe. My editor's other editor read the pages and used the word "promising," which I have decided to take as praise rather than diagnosis.

What I don't tell anyone — what I barely tell myself — is that the novel is a letter too. That every scene is addressed somewhere, even if the address is only the past.

* * *

Sundays, I cook.

This is new. For twenty years my kitchens were stages — mise en place arranged like evidence, pans hot for photographs I never took. Now I cook badly and often. I burn things. I over-salt. Last week I made jollof that Adaeze, over video, pronounced "a hate crime against tomatoes," and then stayed on the line for forty minutes telling me how her own mother — our mother — used to fix mine.

Our mother. I am still learning the plural.

The pepper soup I can make now with my eyes closed, which is fortunate, because twice I've made it with them closed, standing at the pot crying the way you cry at funerals when the hymn gets you — not sad exactly, more like wrung out. The taste comes back strongest on rainy days. I don't know the mechanism. Auntie Bisi says taste is memory with the volume up, and Bisi has never once let accuracy interrupt a good theory.

She calls every Sunday at six, Lagos time, which is five here, and scolds me about the crayfish. "You are grinding it too fine. Leave it some dignity." She sent a second jar in March with a note: *For the road. There is always a road.* She has started signing her messages "your auntie," possessive, unappealable, as if the matter were settled in court years ago and I'd simply failed to attend the hearing.

Adaeze visits in June. We have it planned like a summit — dates confirmed, expectations managed — but when she comes through arrivals at Heathrow she looks smaller than her voice and she hugs me before either of us can decide whether to, and we stand there in the corridor smelling of airplane and each other's shampoo, two women raised in the same house by the same silence, learning the same language from opposite ends.

She stays nine days. We fight twice, properly, about nothing — about the dishwasher, about whether my novel makes their mother too gentle — and both times the fight ends the same way, with one of us cooking and the other one eating, and neither of us saying the treaty out loud because treaties spoken aloud get revoked.

On her last night she stands at the wardrobe, running her hand along the wood the way you'd touch a sleeping animal. "Mummy kept her shoes in here," she says. "And her anger. And us, I think. Everything she couldn't put down."

"And the letters."

"And the letters." She looks at me. "You're going to put us in the book anyway."

"Yes."

"Make the kitchen bigger than it was," she says. "Kitchens are always smaller in memory than they were in life. Give us the room."

* * *

The rain hasn't stopped all evening. I sit with the window open a hand's width, the way I learned to in Lagos — closed windows are for people with nothing to listen to — and the sound comes in off the street, that soft static, that necessary weather.

Here is what I know now, what I would write if someone asked me to define the word *home*, which no one has, which is probably why I keep defining it anyway:

Home is not the house. The house belongs to a developer now, or will soon; there are flats going up behind its old walls with names like The Signature and The Reserve. Home is not even the city, though the city helps — Lagos lives in me now the way a song lives in you after the radio's off, playing on some internal frequency you can't switch off and wouldn't.

Home is a practice. It is Sunday crayfish ground with dignity. It is letters answered, all of them, even the ones that arrive late, especially those. It is calling your sister on Thursdays for no reason and hanging up mid-sentence because the call dropped, and calling back, and calling back again, because the calling back is the whole point, has always been the whole point.

Taste, when it returned, did not return the way I expected — not all at once, not like sight restored. It returns in weather. Some days everything is flavorless and I do not panic anymore; I set the day aside like a coat I'll need later. Other days the rain on the air outside my window carries pepper soup — utazi and goat and the slow fire patience of it — so vividly that I stand at the open window with my eyes shut and my mouth open like a child catching snow, tasting a country three thousand miles away, tasting my mother's hands, tasting the girl I was and forgave, all of it dissolved in ordinary London water.

My mother knew. That's the thing I circle back to, the small sun everything else orbits. She died knowing I'd come back. Not hoping — knowing, the way you know rain is coming from the color of the sky. She left the letters where I'd find them. She left the recipe changed, bitterleaf added, *for the ones who go* — a door held open for fifteen years by a woman who never once said come home, because she understood that some doors you cannot push people through. You can only keep them unlocked and trust the weather to do the rest.

The last page of the manuscript is due to my agent in September. In it, the sisters stand in a kitchen larger than any real kitchen, larger than memory allows, and they cook a meal together that takes the whole book to finish, and when they sit down to eat, the rain begins — not tragically, not as symbolism, just rain, the necessary kind, the kind that falls whether or not anyone is ready for it — and one of them says grace, and the grace is one word long.

I haven't decided the word yet.

But tonight, with the window open and the rain coming down in ropes and the wardrobe breathing its cedar breath into my small bright room, I think I might be close. I think it might be the word my mother's final letter ended on, the one I read forty times in that empty kitchen, the one she wrote in her careful schoolteacher's hand as if she were grading the world and finding it, despite everything, worth the ink:

*Come.*

Not a command. An invitation with the door already open. And so the last page writes itself while I sleep, and in the morning I will find it waiting, and the word will be there, and it will be mine, and it will be hers, and the rain will still be falling on the city that taught me hunger, falling on the flat where I learned to eat again, falling — softly, steadily, like an answer arriving years late and right on time —

and I will stand in it.
