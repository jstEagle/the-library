## 2. Black Clothes, Old Hymns

The church on Marigold Street had been holding funerals for the Okonkwo family since before the family changed its name, though nobody said that either. Isandro noticed it the way he noticed most things now—sideways, in the margins of his attention—how the building had absorbed them twice over: once in 2009 for his father, and again today for his mother, and how between those two dates it had held baptisms and weddings and choir practice with a patience that felt almost accusatory. Buildings remembered. It was people who agreed to forget.

He stood at the front beside Adaeze, both of them in black that fit differently than it had fifteen years ago. His suit had been bought for this occasion specifically; hers was an old one, softened by wear, the cuffs slightly frayed where her hands worried them. She kept her chin up the way their mother had taught them to keep chins up—as if grief were a posture you could hold correctly, like a bow at the end of a performance.

And there were performances here. Isandro recognized them because he had spent his whole adult life studying the craft of not being seen, and a funeral was nothing if not a theater of managed visibility. The relatives filed past in their dark clothes, each one performing a version of themselves calibrated to the occasion: the cousin who wept openly and beautifully, tears arriving exactly when needed; the great-aunt who pressed his hand and said *she's at peace now* with the serene confidence of someone who had never once been at peace herself; Uncle Terrence, who moved through the receiving line like a man who had rehearsed this too, shaking hands, nodding, saying all the right words in all the right measures.

*She went quickly,* Terrence told a woman from the second pew. *That's the mercy of it.*

Isandro heard the sentence from three feet away and understood that it was not about his mother at all. It was about 2009. It was about the other death, the one that had no funeral hymns because it had been ruled an accident and accidents don't get eulogies, they get insurance forms and a closed file. *Quickly. That's the mercy.* As if speed were the only kindness death knew how to offer this family.

He performed his own part. He shook hands. He accepted casseroles and condolences with the same expression he wore while mopping the recital hall—a pleasant, porous blankness into which other people could pour whatever they needed. *Thank you for coming. She loved you. Yes, she was ready.* He had gotten good at this. Fifteen years of invisibility will teach a man fluency in every dialect of polite deflection.

What he could not deflect was the way the euphemisms accumulated, each one landing on him like a small weight placed carefully on a scale.

*Your father would be proud of how you've cared for her.*

*At least she didn't suffer.*

*These things happen for a reason.*

*It was God's time.*

Every sentence circled the same absent center. Nobody said the word *accident*, but everyone was thinking it, the way a room full of musicians can all be thinking of the same wrong note without anyone playing it. And beneath the word they weren't saying lay the deeper silence, the load-bearing silence, the one the whole family had built its house upon: that his father had fallen from the loading dock behind the conservatory on a Tuesday night in October, and that the police report had taken eleven days to write, and that somewhere in those eleven days something had been decided—not loudly, not criminally, but in the quiet way things are decided among people who have something to protect.

Isandro had been nineteen then. He had spent the last fifteen years not thinking about it, which is to say he had thought about it constantly, in the way a man who refuses to look at a scar still knows exactly where it is.

* * *

The service began with the old hymns, the ones their mother had sung in Igbo before she'd learned to sing them in English, the ones whose melodies lived in Isandro's hands even now, dormant, like seeds in frozen ground. The organist was a heavyset man named Deacon Eze who had played at their father's funeral too, and when he struck the opening chords of "Abide With Me," Isandro watched the man's shoulders settle into the familiar shape of the music and felt something twist in his chest that he refused to name.

Music did that to him now. It arrived uninvited and left damage.

He stood when he was supposed to stand. He sat when he was supposed to sit. He mouthed words he half-remembered. Beside him, Adaeze sang—actually sang, low and steady, her voice carrying the melody the way a hand carries water across a room without spilling it. She had always been the braver singer. At nine years old she had stood up in this same church and performed a solo while their father filmed it on a camcorder that ate the tape afterward, so that the performance existed now only in memory, which is to say it existed nowhere.

Their mother's casket sat closed at the front. Isandro was grateful for the closure. Open caskets asked questions of the living that he had never learned to answer.

The pastor spoke about rest, about labor ended, about a woman who had given everything to her children. All true. None of it complete. The pastor did not speak about the fourteen years after October 2009 when Chiamaka Okafor had stopped humming in the kitchen, or the way she had flinched whenever a car backfired, or how she had never once visited the conservatory where her son worked, though it was twelve minutes from her house and she had helped raise money for its scholarship fund in the years before. Grief has an official version and an unofficial version, and funerals are where the official version gets read aloud while the unofficial version sits in the pews, dressed in black, holding its tongue.

Then came the eulogy, delivered by Terrence, because Terrence always delivered the eulogies. He was good at it. He had a voice made for podiums, warm and grainy, a voice that suggested decades of Sunday dinners and sound advice. He stepped up to the pulpit, adjusted the microphone with the unhurried competence of a man who had done this many times, and looked out at the congregation with eyes that were genuinely wet.

"Chiamaka was my sister," he began. "But she was more than that. She was the keeper of us."

Isandro listened with half his mind. The other half was doing what it always did at gatherings of the family: counting exits, reading faces, tracking the weather of the room. It was a habit he'd developed after his father died, a vigilance he'd mistaken for maturity. He knew which relatives avoided eye contact with him—the ones who had known something, or suspected something, or simply couldn't bear the arithmetic of what the family had survived. He knew which ones looked at him too directly, with pity that curdled into curiosity. Curiosity was the dangerous one. Pity you could absorb. Curiosity wanted answers.

Terrence talked about their mother's cooking, her garden, her stubborn faith. He talked about how she'd come to this country with two suitcases and a husband with a head full of music, and how she'd built a life out of less than most people start with. The congregation murmured its assent. Somewhere in the third row a baby fussed and was hushed.

"She raised two extraordinary children," Terrence said, and here his voice caught, professionally, artfully. "A daughter who became a nurse because she couldn't stop caring for people. And a son—" he paused, and Isandro felt the pause arrive like a hand on his shoulder, "—a son who gave up more than any of us will ever know."

The words landed strangely. Not cruelly. Strangely. As if Terrence were speaking in a code that only Isandro and perhaps two other people in the room could decode. *Gave up more than any of us will ever know.* Was it an apology? A warning? A eulogy for the pianist folded inside the janitor? Terrence's eyes found Isandro's for a moment—just a moment—and in them Isandro saw something he had seen there many times over the years and had never been able to name. Not guilt exactly. Something adjacent to guilt. The look of a man standing near a fire he remembers starting, or failing to stop, and watching to see whether anyone else smells the smoke.

Beside him, Adaeze's hand found his.

She gripped it hard. Harder than comfort required. Her fingers pressed into his knuckles with a force that had nothing to do with mourning and everything to do with holding on—to him, to herself, to some ledge only she could see. Isandro turned his head slightly. Her face was composed, eyes forward, jaw set, but her hand was trembling, fine tremors running through it like current through wire, and her grip said what her face would not: *Don't look at him. Don't listen to him. Stay here with me.*

He squeezed back. He didn't let go.

Because here was the thing Isandro had never said aloud, not to Adaeze, not to anyone: during the eulogy for his father, fifteen years ago, in this same church, Terrence had gripped his hand the same way. Nineteen years old, hollowed out, sitting in the front row while the organ played "It Is Well with My Soul"—and Terrence had reached over and clamped onto his hand like a man securing cargo in a storm, and had held on through the entire eulogy, and Isandro had assumed it was love. It probably was love. But it had also felt, even then, even through the fog of his own devastation, like restraint. Like a man holding down the lid of something.

Now Adaeze was doing it to him, and the symmetry was unbearable.

Terrence finished the eulogy with a blessing, and the congregation rose to sing the final hymn, and Isandro stood with his sister's hand crushing his and sang nothing and heard everything: the organ, the voices, the creak of the pews, and underneath it all, faint as a note sustained past its written length, the memory of a Tuesday night in October and the sound—he had never told anyone this, had barely admitted it to himself—of a door closing somewhere below his practice room window. A heavy door. The loading dock door. At an hour when no one should have been using it.

He had been nineteen and preparing to fail spectacularly in public, and he had heard the door, and he had thought nothing of it, and six weeks later his father was dead, and the door was the kind of detail that grief is supposed to blur but never does.

* * *

The reception was at the family house, the house on Delancey Row's quieter cousin of a street, filled now with folding chairs and paper plates and the particular acoustics of forty people trying to talk around an absence. Isandro moved through it like a waiter at someone else's party, refilling, fetching, agreeing. *Yes, she loved her garden. No, I'm holding up. Thank you, the flowers are beautiful.*

Adaeze worked the room differently. She received condolences the way a customs officer receives travelers—politely, thoroughly, with an attention that made people give up more than they intended. Isandro watched her extract, from a cousin named Beatrice, the information that their mother had called her twice in the last year crying and wouldn't say why. He watched her learn, from an old neighbor, that Chiamaka had been seen walking past the conservatory last spring, standing outside it for nearly twenty minutes, and leaving without going in. Each fact Adaeze collected and stored away with a small nod, filing it in whatever ledger she kept behind her eyes.

They had inherited different halves of their mother. Adaeze got the vigilance. Isandro got the silence.

In the kitchen, away from the crowd, Terrence found him. Isandro was washing dishes that didn't need washing, because his hands required occupation the way other men's required cigarettes.

"You did well today," Terrence said. He leaned against the counter, sleeves rolled, and for a moment he looked tired in a way the eulogy hadn't permitted. "Both of you. Your mother would have been proud."

"Thank you for speaking."

"It's what family does." Terrence watched him scrub a plate that was already clean. "Isandro." A pause, weighted. "Whatever you're feeling right now—and I know it's a lot, losing her, on top of everything—remember that some doors are better left shut. Some rooms in a house you don't go into. Not because there's anything wrong with the room. Because the house stands because of where the walls are."

Isandro set the plate down. "I'm drying dishes, Uncle Terrence."

"I know what you're doing." Terrence's voice stayed gentle, which somehow made it worse. Gentle was how Terrence said serious things; loud was how he said harmless ones. "I'm just saying it early, so you don't have to hear it late. Your mother is at peace. Let her stay that way. Let yourself stay that way." He put a hand briefly on Isandro's shoulder—warm, heavy, brotherly—and left the kitchen.

Isandro stood at the sink with the water running and understood that he had just been warned at his mother's funeral reception, and that the warning had been delivered as kindness, and that the worst part was that some exhausted part of him wanted to accept it. Shut the door. Keep the walls where they were. Fifteen years of practice had made the silence comfortable the way a cast is comfortable—rigid, protective, and slowly deforming everything it touches.

* * *

The last guests left after dark. Adaeze moved through the house turning off lights, and Isandro found her in their mother's bedroom, standing at the dresser, not touching anything, just looking at the objects arranged there: the perfume bottle, the hairbrush with silver hairs still in it, the framed photograph of four people at a beach in 2003—Terrence squinting, their mother laughing, Adaeze gap-toothed at seven, and their father, Emmanuel, mid-sentence as always, one hand raised in the gesture he used when explaining music to anyone who'd stand still.

"He grabbed my hand," Adaeze said, without turning around. "During the eulogy. Too hard."

"I know. I saw."

"Not you. Him. Fifteen years ago." Now she turned. In the dim light her face was unreadable, which meant it was fully readable, because Adaeze only became unreadable when she was deciding how much to show. "He did the exact same thing to me today that he did to you at Dad's funeral. Same grip. Same spot. I've been thinking about it all through the reception and I can't make it mean nothing."

"It means he loves us."

"It means he's afraid." She said it flatly, without drama, the way she reported lab results. "There's a difference, Isandro, and I think you know there's a difference, and I think you've known it longer than I have."

He looked at the photograph. His father's raised hand seemed to hang there, suspended between explanation and farewell. "Adaeze—"

"Don't." Her voice cracked, just once, just enough. "Don't do the thing where you smooth it over. You've been smoothing things over since you were nineteen years old. Mom smoothed things over for fourteen years and it ate her alive—I watched it eat her, I watched her stop singing, I watched her flinch at car doors, and today everybody stood up and called it peace." She breathed. Steadied herself the way she did before difficult shifts. "Someone is going to tell me eventually why our father fell off that dock, and why the report took eleven days, and why Uncle Terrence holds our hands like he's keeping us from standing up. And I'd rather hear it from you."

The house was silent around them. Outside, a car passed, headlights sweeping the ceiling, and Isandro thought of the loading dock door, heavy and final, closing on a Tuesday night.

Before he could answer—before he could decide which lie of tenderness to offer, or whether he had finally run out of them—the doorbell rang.

They looked at each other. It was nearly ten. The guests were gone; the casseroles were accounted for. Adaeze went to the door with the quick stride of a nurse expecting bad news, and Isandro followed, and through the frosted glass he saw a figure standing on the porch, tall and gaunt, holding himself very still, like a man who had waited a long time for this moment and intended to survive it.

Adaeze opened the door.

The stranger looked past her, directly at Isandro, and said a word that dropped through the warm air of the house like ice through water:

"Okonkwo."
