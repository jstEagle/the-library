## 20. Adaeze Finds the Second Witness

The archive room of the county records annex smelled like pencil shavings and cold coffee, and Adaeze Okafor had been breathing it for six hours when she finally found the name.

She had come prepared to be bored. She had brought a thermos of ginger tea, a legal pad divided into columns, two pens in case one died, and the particular grim patience she'd learned as a hospital administrator, where entire afternoons could be consumed by a single misfiled invoice and no one would ever apologize for it. She had told her husband she was "following up on some paperwork," which was true in the way that most of her lies were true—technically accurate, spiritually hollow. Her children were at her mother-in-law's. Isandro thought she was still grieving in the ordinary way, folding their mother's dresses into donation bags, letting time do its slow anesthetic work.

Instead she was three folders deep into the payroll history of Meridian Holdings' defunct subsidiary, Meridian Cultural Services, LLC—a company that had existed, according to these records, for exactly eleven years, employed between four and nine people depending on the fiscal year, and whose purpose was described in its articles of incorporation with the magnificent vagueness of all shell entities: *to provide administrative and ancillary support services.*

Ancillary support services. Adaeze wrote the phrase on her legal pad and underlined it twice. Somewhere in her chest, below the grief and beside the fury, something was hardening. It had been hardening since the funeral, since Elias Voss said *Okonkwo* over their mother's grave like a man dropping a stone down a well and waiting for the splash. Since Terrence's face went the color of old paper. Since Isandro called her, voice low, from outside Voss's building on Delancey Row, saying only: *He has tapes, Ada. He has Dad's voice.* She had sat in her parked car in her own driveway for twenty minutes after that call, hands on the wheel, watching her kitchen light burn yellow through the window, understanding that her life had quietly split into before and after and that she was now living in the after.

So: ancillary support services. She pulled the next folder.

* * *

Here is what Adaeze knew about money, and about how families like hers learned to be quiet about it:

She knew that in the spring of 2010, when she was nine and Isandro was nineteen and the world had already ended once, Uncle Terrence had appeared at their house with his good coat on and stayed late with their mother in the kitchen while the children were sent upstairs on the pretext of homework. She knew that afterward their mother had cried—not the loud crying of the funeral weeks but a low, continuous sound, like a radiator—and that when Adaeze crept halfway down the stairs, she'd seen Terrence holding their mother's hands across the table, both of them bent toward each other like people praying over a body.

She knew that they never moved out of the house on Fenwick Street, though everyone assumed they would have to sell it. She knew that Isandro's tuition debt to the conservatory was forgiven—"a kindness," Terrence called it, and their mother had flinched at the word. She knew that her mother stopped working double shifts at St. Agnes's around the same time, and that when Adaeze asked why, years later, her mother had said, *I have enough,* in a tone that closed doors.

And she knew—had always known, the way you know a load-bearing wall is cracked even if the plaster looks fine—that none of this was luck. That grief had been subsidized. That someone, somewhere, had decided the Okonkwos—Okafors, after the name change, after the paperwork Terrence filed in 2009 that made them legally into people who had never been anything else—were worth a monthly sum, and that the sum had arrived like weather, unremarked upon, for fourteen years.

Her mother's bank statements were in the cedar chest now, because Isandro had found them and because neither of them could bear to look at them alone. MERIDIAN HOLDINGS — DISBURSEMENT, the deposit lines read, month after month, year after year, $2,400, then $2,650, then $3,100 as inflation ticked upward, punctilious as rent. Fourteen years of punctiliousness. Their mother had deposited every one and spent eleven of those years dying slowly of what the doctors called cardiac arrhythmia and what Adaeze now understood had been something with no billing code.

The question that had organized itself in Adaeze's mind, the way questions do when you refuse to let them sleep, was not *who paid*. They knew who paid. The question was *who authorized*—because there is always a difference between the hand that writes the check and the will that commands it, and in any system built to protect itself, the will hides behind layers of administrative and ancillary support services.

Which was why she was here, in the annex, with her cold tea, pulling folder after folder.

* * *

The break came at 4:40 in the afternoon, forty minutes before the annex closed, in a box labeled MCS-07 / PERSONNEL (1998–2011).

Payroll registers, mostly—carbon copies of quarterly summaries, the kind of thing kept because retention schedules demanded it and no one ever looked. Adaeze had learned to read them fast: names, titles, salary bands, withholding codes. Most of the employees cycled through in a year or two. But four names persisted, and she had written them in her second column: E. VOSS, NIGHT SECURITY (terminated 2010); R. IBEKWE, BOOKKEEPER (resigned 2011); D. MERCER, FACILITIES LIAISON; and T. OKAFOR—

She stopped.

T. OKAFOR, CONSULTANT, RETAINER.

Her uncle's name. Her uncle's name, spelled with the new surname, sitting in a Meridian subsidiary's payroll register starting Q3 2010, $800 a month, code 44—she looked up code 44 in the front matter of the register, hands steady through sheer force of will—*confidential advisory services, non-disclosure attached.*

Terrence hadn't just negotiated the settlement. He'd gone on the payroll. He'd been *kept*, the way a guard dog is kept, or the way a witness is kept—close, fed, and quiet. And he had used the new family name to do it, the fresh alias, so that anyone tracing Okonkwo money would find nothing, and anyone tracing Okafor money would find only a consultant.

Adaeze sat very still. A fluorescent tube above her buzzed and steadied. She thought about Terrence's face at the funeral, the warning in his voice—*some things are load-bearing, Ada; you pull them out and the whole house comes down*—and she understood that he had meant it literally. He was in the walls. He had been in the walls for fifteen years, drawing his eight hundred dollars a month, telling himself whatever men tell themselves.

She copied the entry onto her pad, then photographed the page with her phone, then photographed it again from a different angle because she didn't trust herself yet. Then she turned back to the second column, to the other persistent name, the one that mattered more.

R. IBEKWE, BOOKKEEPER. Employed 1999–2011. Salary band modest. Withholding code ordinary. A woman who had done the actual work—the disbursements, the ledgers, the monthly rhythm of $2,400 arriving at a widow's bank—for twelve years, and who had resigned the same year the payments to the Okafors apparently restructured or ceased, and who had then vanished from every record Adaeze could find in the box.

Adaeze closed the folder and held it against her chest for a moment, the way you hold a door shut against wind.

* * *

Finding Rosa Ibekwe took nine days.

It took, in order: a request to the state licensing board for certified bookkeepers active in 2010 (forty-one names, none matching); a search of property records (nothing); a long evening with Isandro on speakerphone while he dug through Voss's clippings and she dug through obituaries, both of them half-hoping and half-dreading the word *survived by*; a dead end at a church in East Hollow where a Rosa Ibekwe had taught Sunday school until 2013 and then moved, reason unknown; and finally, on the ninth day, a hit on a professional relicensing database—Rosa Ibekwe, age sixty-eight, bookkeeper, license renewed biennially without lapse, current address a small brick duplex on Corliss Avenue with a garden visible in the street-view photograph, roses along the walk, pruned with obvious discipline.

"She's alive," Adaeze said to the phone, to Isandro, to the empty kitchen at eleven at night. "She's alive and she renews her license. Do you understand what that means? People who want to disappear let their licenses lapse. People who want to be findable keep paying the fifty-dollar renewal fee."

Isandro was quiet for a moment. She could hear him thinking, hear the caution assembling itself in him the way it always did, brick by careful brick. "Ada, if she wanted to talk, she'd have talked by now."

"People don't talk, Isandro. People get asked correctly." She was pacing her kitchen, phone pressed to her ear, looking at the dark window where her own reflection ghosted over the yard. "Voss got asked incorrectly—he got paid off and exiled, and he's spent fifteen years marinating in it. This woman processed the payments. She knows the amounts, the dates, the authorization chain. She might know whose signature is on the original instruction, and I promise you it isn't some middle manager's."

"And if she doesn't want to be found? If we scare her?"

"Then we leave and we never contact her again. I'm not going to terrorize a retired bookkeeper on her porch." She stopped pacing. Through the window, down the street, a neighbor's porch light came on and off, someone letting a dog out. "But I'll tell you what I've learned this month, brother. Everyone in this story has been waiting fifteen years for someone to ask. Voss. Mom. Maybe even Terrence. Silence isn't a choice people make once—it's a choice they remake every single day, and remaking it wears grooves in you. Somebody has to offer them the off-ramp."

Another silence. Then Isandro, quieter: "Take someone with you anyway. Take me."

"You sweep floors, Isandro. You can't be seen asking questions at a witness's house."

"I can take a personal day."

"When was the last time you took a personal day?"

The pause on the line was answer enough, and it broke her heart in the old familiar place, the place that had been breaking since she was nine years old and heard two voices on a loading dock and was told she'd dreamed it. "Saturday morning," she said. "You drive. We park around the corner. I knock. If she shuts the door, we go home and eat pancakes and never speak of it. Deal?"

"Deal," he said, and she heard in his voice the same thing she felt in her chest: hope, hardening.

* * *

Saturday came up gray and mild, the kind of October morning that couldn't decide what it wanted to be. Isandro drove with both hands on the wheel at ten and two, the way he did everything now—carefully, apologetically, as if the road might resent him. Adaeze watched the neighborhoods change outside the window: bodegas giving way to dentists' offices, then to the quiet streets near Corliss where the houses sat back behind hedges and the air smelled faintly of leaf mold and someone's woodsmoke.

They parked around the corner, as planned. Isandro killed the engine and didn't move.

"Say it," Adaeze said.

"Say what?"

"Whatever speech you've been drafting since Tuesday. Say it now so I can ignore it properly."

He almost smiled. It was strange, she thought, how much of their childhood lived intact inside their adult faces. "Just—if she asks why now, why after fifteen years, have an answer that isn't revenge. People can refuse to help revenge. It's harder to refuse to help the truth."

"It's the same thing."

"No," Isandro said, and there was something in his voice she couldn't name yet, some sediment of the conversation he'd clearly been having with himself in the dark hours between shifts. "Revenge wants the person who hurt us to suffer. Truth just wants the record to stop lying. Those overlap sometimes. They're not the same." He looked out the windshield at the wet leaves. "Mom spent fourteen years taking that money and fourteen years being eaten by it. Whatever we do, I don't want it to be for nothing again. I don't want us to trade one silence for another."

Adaeze looked at him—her big brother, the prodigy, the man who swept the floors of the building that broke him—and felt the fierce, complicated love that was the only uncomplicated thing left in her life. "Then let's go make the record stop lying."

* * *

Rosa Ibekwe opened the door on the second knock, and Adaeze knew immediately that they had not surprised her.

She was a compact woman in a cardigan the color of dried thyme, reading glasses pushed up into gray-threaded hair, and she looked at them—at Adaeze's face, specifically, and then past her to Isandro standing at the bottom of the steps—with the unhurried assessment of someone who had spent a career noticing discrepancies. Behind her, the hallway smelled of eucalyptus and old paper. On the wall, framed, hung a certificate of completion from a financial forensics seminar, dated 2014.

"You're the daughter," Rosa said. Not a question. "Emmanuel's daughter. You have his chin."

Adaeze's throat closed. Fifteen years, and this stranger said the father's first name like it was still warm.

"How—" she managed.

"The day your mother died, the obituary ran with the wrong maiden name and the wrong surviving relatives. I checked. I've been checking for a long time." Rosa stepped back and opened the door wider. "Come in before the neighbors invent something better. Kitchen's through here. I have tea already made, which will surprise you less than it should."

* * *

The kitchen table was clear except for a Bible, a crossword folded to the corner, and—Adaeze's heart stuttered—a single manila folder, squared neatly to the table's edge.

Rosa poured tea without asking preferences. Chamomile. She sat, folded her hands, and regarded them both over the rims of her glasses.

"I'm going to tell you how this is going to go," she said, "because I'm sixty-eight years old and I don't have the patience for the dance anymore. You're going to ask me if I processed payments from Meridian Holdings to your mother. I did. From June 2010 to March 2024, one hundred and sixty-eight disbursements, initially twenty-four hundred dollars, adjusted annually. I'm going to tell you they were coded as 'cultural outreach stipends,' which was a lie I typed myself, one hundred and sixty-eight times. And then you're going to ask me who authorized them, and I'm going to show you what's in that folder, and after that, young lady, you and I are going to have a harder conversation than either of us drove here expecting. Are we agreed?"

Adaeze set down her cup. Her hands were not entirely steady, but her voice was. "Agreed."

Rosa slid the folder across the table and opened it.

Photocopies. Neat, chronological, annotated in a small precise hand. The first page was a memorandum on Meridian Holdings letterhead, dated May 14, 2010—three weeks after Emmanuel Okonkwo fell from the loading dock, two weeks after the coroner ruled it accidental. RE: LONG-TERM SUPPORT ARRANGEMENT — FENWICK STREET HOUSEHOLD. The memo was brief, bureaucratic, bloodless. It established the stipend. It specified confidentiality. It designated the disbursement channel through Meridian Cultural Services.

And at the bottom, in ink, above the printed name ALDOUS CRANE, CHAIRMAN & FOUNDER, was a signature. Adaeze had seen it before—in the conservatory's lobby, engraved on brass beneath the renamed concert hall. CRANE HALL. She had walked under that name her whole childhood.

"He signed it personally," Rosa said. "Not his office. Not his foundation. Him. I know because the instruction came down with a verbal addendum that I was never to discuss the account with anyone including internal audit, and because Mr. Crane reviewed the disbursement log himself, quarterly, in person. He would sit where you're sitting, Mr. Okonkwo"—a nod at Isandro, who had gone very still—*"and he would run his finger down the column of payments and initial each page. Like a man tending a garden he was ashamed of. Or proud of. I never decided which, and I typed those pages for eleven years."

"Why?" Isandro asked. His voice came out rough. "Why would he—"

"Because your father was going to expose him." Rosa said it flatly, without drama, the way you report a figure that's been audited. "Your father worked the books for the conservatory's expansion fund—I know you may not know that part; they scrubbed his title from everything after. He found what Crane was doing with the construction financing, the inflated contracts, the kickbacks routed through Meridian subsidiaries. He told Crane he was going to the board. Two weeks later he was dead, and three weeks after that, I was instructed to open this account." She removed her glasses and pressed the bridge of her nose. "I told myself it was charity. For about a year, I told myself that. Then Mr. Voss disappeared—night watchman, saw something on that dock, suddenly reassigned to a Meridian property four hundred miles away and never heard from again—and I stopped telling myself stories. I started copying pages instead. Slowly. Over years. Because I am a bookkeeper, Mr. Okonkwo, and a bookkeeper's whole religion is that somewhere, someday, the ledger gets balanced."

The kitchen was silent except for the refrigerator's hum. Adaeze became aware that Isandro was staring at the signature, at the initials marching down the quarterly logs, at fifteen years of a man's handwriting keeping accounts on their family like a hobby.

"The harder conversation," Adaeze said finally. Her mouth was dry. "You said there'd be one."

Rosa put her glasses back on. When she looked up, her eyes were kind and absolutely unbending.

"Yes. Here it is. I will testify. I will put every page of this on the record, and I will say Aldous Crane's name in any room you put me in. But I will not do it so your family can come out of this clean." She laid one brown hand flat on the folder, on the neat columns of numbers that had been their mother's survival and their mother's poison. "Your family took this money. Your uncle negotiated it and then drew a salary to stay quiet about negotiating it. Your mother cashed every check for fourteen years. Those are facts in my ledger too, and I did not copy them for fifteen years so that a lawyer could curate them away. If we do this, we do all of it. No clean victims. Everybody's hands on the table—including yours."

Adaeze felt the words land like cold water—felt, underneath the sting, the thing she'd been building all month click into its final shape. Hope wasn't a feeling, she understood suddenly. Hope was a plan that had survived contact with the truth.

"Everybody's hands," she said. "Including ours."

"Including yours." Rosa closed the folder and rested her hand on it. "Now. Before you go—there's a name you'll need, and a date, and I want you to understand that when I give them to you, there's no walking any of it back. The name is the auditor your father tried to contact the week he died. He never reached him. The auditor reached *me*, two years ago, asking the same questions you're asking." She paused, and for the first time something moved behind her composure—something that looked, improbably, like fear, or maybe its opposite. "And the date is Thursday. Because Thursday, Mr. Crane's lawyers served a subpoena on the archives annex. Someone knows what you found. And whoever it is, they're faster than you."

Outside, the gray light was failing early, and somewhere down Corliss Avenue a dog began to bark, and Adaeze reached across the table and put her hand on the folder, on the evidence, on the beginning of the end, and held on.
