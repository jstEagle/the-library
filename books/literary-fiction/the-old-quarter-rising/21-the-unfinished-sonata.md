## 21. The Unfinished Sonata

The archive lived in the basement of the conservatory, behind a door most students walked past for four years without once wondering what it held. Isandro had cleaned that corridor every Tuesday night for fifteen years and had never opened the door himself. It wasn't locked, exactly—it required a key from the administration office, which was the same as being locked if you were a man with a mop bucket—but he had never asked for it either. Asking was a form of visibility. Asking meant explaining why.

He asked on a Thursday in late October, three days after Terrence wept at the kitchen table and confessed to fourteen years of negotiated silence, and two days after Voss pressed the envelope into his hands with something like tenderness in his ruined face. The letter sat now in a fireproof box under Isandro's bed, and he had read it four times, and each time it rearranged him like furniture in a dark room—he kept bruising against what he'd thought was solid.

But this morning he came for something else. He told the woman at the administration desk—her name was Priya; she had worked there eleven years and still called him "sir" when he brought her a package—that he needed to look through the personal effects collection, the items donated by families of former faculty. She didn't ask why. Perhaps she assumed he was running an errand for someone. That was the great convenience of his old life: no one ever imagined the janitor wanted anything.

The archive smelled of cold paper and machine oil, of decades compressed into shelving. Fluorescent tubes hummed above rows of gray archival boxes, each labeled in fading marker: faculty files, program archives, correspondence, recordings. A dehumidifier chugged in the corner like something sleeping. Isandro stood just inside the door for a long moment, letting his eyes adjust, feeling the particular hush of a room where the building kept everything it pretended to have forgotten.

The conservatory remembered everything. That was the thing about institutions—they forgot nothing, they only declined to say.

* * *

His father's effects occupied two boxes near the back, catalogued under E. OKONKWO in someone's careful hand, the dates bracketing a life: 1961–2009. Isandro knelt in front of them the way you kneel at a grave when there is no one left to perform for.

The first box held the ordinary archaeology of a teaching career. Programs from faculty recitals, his name printed in typefaces that changed with the decades. Grade books with columns of careful numbers. A coffee mug wrapped in newspaper—Isandro unwrapped it and found the logo of a jazz club long closed, the paint worn away where a thumb would rest. His father's thumb. Isandro held the mug for a while without drinking anything, which felt absurd, and then set it down gently, the way you set down something that might still be warm.

There were letters from students, thank-you notes spanning twenty years. *Dear Mr. Okonkwo, I got into Eastman.* *Dear Mr. Okonkwo, I think about your advice every time I practice.* One note, undated, written in a child's unsteady hand: *my dad says you are the best piano player in the city.* Isandro read that one twice. He did not remember writing it, but the handwriting was unmistakably his own, age eight or nine, and something in his chest folded closed around it like a hand making a fist.

In the second box, beneath a layer of tissue paper, lay the manuscript.

He knew before he touched it. Some part of him—the part that had spent fifteen years not thinking about music and failing constantly, because the body remembers what the mind forbids—recognized the shape of the folder, the weight of the paper stock his father favored, heavy cream pages bought from the shop on Delancey Row that had since become a phone repair store. The folder was labeled in his father's slanted capitals: SONATA NO. 2 — IN PROGRESS — DO NOT PERFORM.

*Do not perform.* As if performance were the danger. As if the music itself needed protecting from the world rather than the reverse.

Isandro carried the folder to the reading table under the one working lamp and opened it with hands that had gone strangely formal, ceremonious, as though he were a stranger handling another man's relics. Which, in a way, he was. The last time he had touched his father's manuscript paper he had been nineteen years old and certain of who he was.

The sonata began conventionally enough—a first movement marked *Allegro ma non troppo*, dense but disciplined, the handwriting confident, corrections made cleanly in the margins. His father composed the way he spoke, Isandro realized: economically, with sudden unexpected warmth buried in the middle of an argument. There was a second movement, slow, marked *con molta tenerezza*—with much tenderness—and here the ink grew softer, the note-heads rounder, passages rewritten and rewritten until the page nearly tore. Isandro could see the archaeology of revision, the strata of a man trying to say something exactly and almost getting there.

And then the third movement.

It stopped mid-bar.

Not faded out, not abandoned gradually the way unfinished works usually are, trailing off into sketches and fragments. It stopped mid-bar, mid-phrase, the final measure containing only the beginning of an idea—a rising figure, three notes climbing toward a fourth that was never written—and then nothing. Blank staves. Forty empty lines of staff paper, waiting, the way a mouth waits after a sentence interrupted.

The date in the upper right corner was the week his father died.

Isandro sat very still. The dehumidifier chugged. Somewhere above him, faintly, a student was practicing Liszt badly, the notes arriving through the floor like rain.

He understood, with the flat clarity that had been coming to him all autumn, that he was looking at the exact shape of his own life. A phrase begun and abandoned at its most promising point. Fifteen years of blank staves. Everyone had told him the story differently—accident, tragedy, misfortune, the scaffolding gave way—but the manuscript told the truth the way documents do, without knowing it: his father had been interrupted. Not finished. Interrupted. And whatever force had interrupted a man in the middle of writing a rising figure toward a resolution—that force had also reached forward fifteen years and interrupted his son.

*If anything happens, it wasn't the scaffolding.* His father's voice on the cassette, frightened and precise. Isandro had listened to it six times now. Each time, the fear in that voice sounded less like a man afraid of dying and more like a man afraid of leaving something incomplete.

* * *

He did not decide to go to the practice rooms. That was the strange part. Later he would try to reconstruct the decision and find there wasn't one—only a sequence of movements, as if the building itself were carrying him. He returned the boxes to their shelf except the manuscript folder, which he carried up two flights with both hands, past the corridor he had mopped a thousand Tuesdays, past the notice board where recital posters layered over each other like sediment, into the practice wing.

It was four in the afternoon. The hallway hummed with the overlapping noise of lessons, scales, the metronomic patience of young people becoming themselves. Isandro knew these sounds the way a lighthouse keeper knows the sea. For fifteen years they had been the wallpaper of his invisibility—noise he cleaned up after, noise that confirmed his place outside it.

He tried three doors before he found an empty room. The fourth door opened onto a small practice room, windowless, lit by a single caged bulb, holding an upright piano of the institutional kind—scarred, slightly out of tune in the upper register, the felt of the hammers compacted by decades of students hammering at their fears. Someone had stuck a sticker of a cartoon frog on the fallboard. Someone else had peeled it half off.

Isandro set the manuscript on the music rack. Then he stood there, hands at his sides, and looked at the instrument the way you look at a person you loved who has every reason never to forgive you.

Fifteen years. He tried to count the ways he had avoided this moment and lost track. He had learned to walk past practice rooms without hearing them, a skill that had taken years to develop and cost more than anyone knew. He had trained his ears into exile. When Mirela had asked him—weeks ago now, in the corridor, with her terrible directness—whether he played, he had said no, and the lie had been so smooth and so practiced that he had believed it himself for a full second.

His hands hung at his sides. They looked ordinary. Broad-knuckled, a janitor's hands, the nails kept short for practical reasons, a pale scar across the left index finger from a broken bottle in a recycling bin seven years ago. But underneath the ordinariness they were still his father's hands, still the hands that at nineteen had been called the finest technique the conservatory had produced in a generation, and the knowledge of that sat in him like water sitting in a wall, invisible until the day the plaster gives.

He sat down on the bench. It was too low; he adjusted it without thinking, the automatic gesture of a lifetime ago, and the automaticity of it nearly undid him. The body keeps its own archive. Nothing had been deleted. Everything had been filed.

He lifted the lid. The keys were yellowed ivory substitutes, cool under his fingers. He placed his hands in position—not for the sonata, not yet, just the neutral five-finger posture of a beginner, the first shape any pianist learns—and felt the piano answer with a faint resonance even untouched, sympathetic strings humming with the building's noise, the instrument listening back.

Then he played the opening of the first movement.

It came out rough. Of course it did. His fingers were strangers wearing his gloves; the muscle memory was intact but the fine control had silted over, and the tempo wavered, and he clipped a passage that should have sung. He stopped. Started again. Stopped again. In the old days—in the life before—he would have been merciless with himself, would have drilled the passage forty times until it obeyed. Now he found he didn't care about the roughness. That was the strangest discovery: the perfectionism that had ruled his childhood, that had made his debut at nineteen a kind of execution, that had shattered along with his hands and his nerve—all of it had died sometime during the fifteen years of floors, and what remained was something rawer and less ambitious. Not the desire to play well. Just the desire to play.

He worked through the first movement twice, slowly, stumbling, correcting, the sound leaking under the door into the hallway where students passed and paused and moved on, because a middle-aged man in a custodian's polo shirt playing haltingly in a practice room was not, apparently, remarkable enough to stop for. He was grateful for that. He was also, for the first time, aware of how much the gratitude cost.

The second movement took longer. *Con molta tenerezza.* He played it at half speed, and somewhere in the middle—the rewritten passage, the one his father had revised until the paper thinned—the music reached into him and found the grief he had been managing like a budget for fifteen years, line itemed and capped, and broke the cap open. He didn't cry, exactly. His eyes simply filled, and he kept playing, and the tears fell on the keys and he wiped them with his sleeve between phrases, and the movement came out of him sounding like what it was: a son talking to his father through the only telephone line that had never been disconnected.

When he finished it, he sat in the ringing silence and breathed.

Then he turned the page to the third movement.

* * *

The third movement was fast. Marked *Vivace, ma con ombra*—lively, but with shadow. His father had been reaching for something in it, some synthesis of the argumentative first movement and the tender second, and the pages crackled with energy, the ink pressed hard, the rhythm intricate and driving. Isandro sight-read it as far as his rusted fingers would allow, which was further than he expected, the momentum of the music pulling him along like a current.

And then he arrived at the bar where the manuscript ended.

Three rising notes. Toward a fourth that was never written.

He played the three notes. They hung in the air of the little windowless room, unresolved, asking their question. His fingers hovered over the keys where the fourth note should be, and waited—for the phrase to complete itself, for the resolution to arrive, for his father's intention to rise up out of the paper and finish what it had started.

Nothing came.

He tried again. Three notes, and then his hands simply stopped, the way they had stopped at nineteen, the way they had stopped every time in his imagination over fifteen years whenever he let himself imagine returning. It wasn't the old paralysis—not the terror, not the failed nerves. It was something else, something he recognized only slowly, sitting in the caged light with his father's unfinished question hanging in the air:

He couldn't finish the phrase because it wasn't his to finish. Not yet. The fourth note belonged to a man who had been killed before he could write it, and to write it for him—to resolve it, to make it beautiful, to make it whole—would be a kind of forgery. A comforting lie. The same comfortable lie the family had lived inside for fifteen years, the scaffolding story, the accident, the kindness of Director Reyes with his job offer shaped exactly like a muzzle.

You couldn't improvise an ending to someone else's sentence until you understood why the sentence was cut off. You couldn't resolve a phrase whose interruption was a crime.

Isandro sat there a long time. The fluorescent hum. The distant Liszt, still bad. The frog sticker peeling off the fallboard. His hands resting in his lap now, quiet, having done all they could do—which was more than he'd believed possible an hour ago, and less than the music demanded.

He thought about the letter under his bed in its fireproof box. *Never trade truth for safety,* his father had written, in the voice of a man who knew he was spending his last weeks. Isandro had read those words four times and each time heard them addressed not to some abstract future but to this exact moment: a man at a piano, hovering over an unfinished phrase, deciding whether to invent a pretty ending or to go find out what actually happened.

The truth was the fourth note. Not metaphorically. Literally. Somewhere in the machinery of Meridian Holdings and the testimony of Rosa Ibekwe and the payroll records Adaeze was pulling apart thread by thread, somewhere in Crane's brandy-warm denials and Reyes's unsent resignation, the actual final measure existed—the reason, the hand, the moment. Find that, and the phrase could close itself honestly. Anything else was decoration over a wound.

He closed the fallboard gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping child.

Then he copied out the third movement's final page—there was a photocopier in the archive, and Priya had gone home, and no one stopped him—and he folded the copy into his breast pocket, over his heart, which was a sentimental gesture and he knew it and did it anyway. The original went back in the folder. The folder went back in the box. He aligned the box on the shelf precisely where it had been, label facing out, and stood looking at E. OKONKWO 1961–2009 for a moment longer than necessary.

The dates bothered him. They bothered him more than they had an hour ago. 1961–2009, the dash between them doing its small bureaucratic work, reducing a man to a hyphen. But the manuscript said the dash was wrong. The dash implied completion, a life run to its natural end. His father hadn't ended. He had been *stopped*. And somewhere in this building's paperwork, in Meridian's ledgers, in the memory of a night watchman named Elias Voss, the reason for the stopping was still legible.

Isandro climbed the stairs out of the basement carrying the folded photocopy like contraband, which in a sense it was—the property of a dead man, smuggled back into circulation.

In the corridor on the ground floor, under the flickering light he had reported for repair twice and fixed himself the third time, he nearly collided with Mirela, coming out of her evening lesson with her violin case—no, piano, she was a pianist, he corrected himself, he had known that for years, the correction itself a small alarm bell. She looked at him, then at his breast pocket, where the folded pages made a visible rectangle.

"You've got sheet music," she said. Not a question. She had the unnerving perceptiveness of the young, the ability to see straight through the costumes adults wear.

"It's an archive document," Isandro said. "I'm returning it."

"You're carrying it *out*," Mirela said.

He looked down at the rectangle over his heart. Looked back at her. Fifteen years of practiced erasure rose in him, the smooth deflection, the smile that ends conversations—and stalled, halfway up his face, unable to complete itself.

"It's my father's," he heard himself say. "He never finished it."

Mirela studied him for a long moment, her music bag sliding off her shoulder, her eyes going from his face to the pocket and back with an intensity that made him feel, for the first time in fifteen years, fully visible—and not erased by it.

"My grandfather left a symphony half-done," she said quietly. "Nobody in my family will talk about why he stopped." She shifted her bag higher. "Maybe somebody should."

She walked off toward the dormitories, and Isandro stood alone in the corridor holding his father's interrupted voice against his chest, understanding that the question he had come downstairs to escape had followed him up into the light, and that tomorrow he would have to call his sister, and tell her what he'd found, and say the words out loud that he had only just allowed himself to think:

*We can't finish the phrase until we know who cut it off.*
