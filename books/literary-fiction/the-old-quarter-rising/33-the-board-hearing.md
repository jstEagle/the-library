## 33. The Board Hearing

The hearing room had been a lecture hall once, back when the conservatory still taught things Isandro didn't already know by heart. He recognized it the way you recognize a face after surgery—the bones familiar, everything else rearranged. The tiered seating where students had slouched through music history was now gallery seating for the public, and the lectern where Professor Amado had droned about sonata form stood empty at the front of a horseshoe of tables draped in gray cloth, behind which sat seven members of the state arts commission in their various shades of solemnity.

Isandro arrived an hour early because he could not bear to arrive on time. He wanted to watch the room fill, to see who came and how they carried themselves, and mostly he wanted to be seated before anyone could look at him and decide what they saw. For fifteen years he had perfected the art of being looked through. Now his name was in the papers—*PRODIGY'S FATHER MURDERED*—and people looked at him the way they look at a car accident they've slowed down for: with that terrible appetite.

He wore the only suit he owned, bought for his mother's funeral six weeks ago, black wool gone slightly shiny at the elbows. Adaeze had ironed it for him this morning without being asked, pressing the collar flat with a care that said more than either of them could manage aloud.

The room filled slowly. Reporters clustered near the aisle with their laptops open like hymnals. A scattering of conservatory faculty, faces he'd swept around for years, now nodding at him with careful neutrality, as if he were a colleague they hadn't quite decided to claim. Students, too—a few of them, Mirela among them, third row, her hair pinned up severely as if she'd dressed for an exam rather than a spectacle. When she caught his eye she didn't smile. She just held his gaze for a moment, steady, and then looked down at her program. It was exactly right. He could have kissed her forehead for it.

And near the back, alone, sat Aldous Crane.

Isandro had expected lawyers to surround him, had expected the old man to be wheeled in like evidence himself. Instead Crane sat upright in a navy blazer, silver-haired, hands folded on his knee, wearing the expression of a man attending a recital given by a student he was considering sponsoring. Eighty-one years old, indicted three weeks ago, out on his own recognizance, and he still occupied rooms the way weather occupies a sky. Isandro felt the old reflex move through him—the urge to lower his eyes, to become furniture—and hated it, and did it anyway, briefly, before lifting his chin again.

At nine o'clock precisely, the chairwoman of the commission, a dry-voiced woman named Okafor-Hughes—no relation, though Isandro saw several people do the arithmetic anyway—struck a small gavel and read the purpose of the inquiry into the record: allegations of financial misconduct spanning two decades, allegations of concealment of circumstances surrounding a death in 2009, and the question of whether the conservatory's governance had been compromised by its principal benefactor.

*Allegations.* The word was doing enormous work, holding up the whole morning like a load-bearing wall.

Then Crane's counsel rose to begin, and Isandro understood that the wall was going to be tested from the other side.

* * *

The lawyer's name was Perrine Duval, and she was very good, which Isandro had known abstractly and now learned viscerally, the way you learn a current is strong only when it takes your feet.

She did not defend Crane, not at first. She built a stage. She walked the commissioners through Elias Voss's employment history with the tenderness of a woman handling something fragile she intended to break: hired as night security in 2004, dismissed in 2010, no forwarding address for eleven years, gaps in residence documented across three states, a 2013 arrest for disorderly conduct in a city Isandro had never heard of, a subsequent stretch of unemployment, a documented psychiatric evaluation—entered into the record with a delicacy that made the cruelty worse, because it was so clean.

"I don't raise these facts to mock Mr. Voss," Duval said, turning toward the witness table where Voss sat gaunt and upright in the same brown jacket he'd worn to the grave. "I raise them because this inquiry deserves better than a story assembled by a man who has spent fifteen years nursing a grievance into a religion. Grief is real. Grievance is not evidence."

Voss's hands were flat on the table. He did not flinch. But Isandro, watching him from the second row, saw what the commissioners probably did not: the way Voss's thumbs pressed white against the wood, the small economy of a man spending all his strength on staying still.

Duval took him apart by inches. The tape—the cassette labeled IO-1—had no provenance. No chain of custody. A recording of uncertain date, of a voice of uncertain identity, made on equipment that could not be authenticated. "Anyone can label a cassette," she said. "I could label one this afternoon." The photograph placing Voss's father-figure—placing *Emmanuel Okonkwo*, she said the name carefully, correctly, and Isandro felt the room register it—with Aldous Crane proved nothing except that two men had once stood in the same frame; men posed together at galas, at groundbreakings, at weddings of convenience. The payroll records Rosa Ibekwe had produced were, Duval conceded, troubling—but they documented *consulting fees*, ordinary and unremarkable, the kind of discretionary disbursement every institution of this size generated monthly. "You may find the optics distasteful," she told the commission. "Optics are not crimes."

By eleven o'clock the word *allegation* had been joined by *inference*, *conjecture*, and *narrative*, and Isandro could feel the room's temperature changing—not cooling but thickening, the air taking on the weight of doubt. He watched a commissioner who had seemed sympathetic at the start now frowning at her notes. He watched a young reporter in the aisle stop typing.

This was the thing no one had warned him about—not Adaeze, not Reyes, not even Voss with his fifteen years of rehearsed testimony. That truth did not arrive in the world naked. It arrived dressed, and everyone got to argue about the clothes.

When Duval finished, the chairwoman called a recess, and Voss walked past Isandro's row without looking at anyone, his spine straight as a fence post, and Isandro thought: *he has done this before. He has been disbelieved before, professionally, repeatedly, and he came anyway.*

It was the bravest ugly thing he had ever seen.

* * *

They found each other in the corridor outside the men's room, which had become, in the way of institutional corridors, a kind of neutral country during recesses. Voss stood at the window looking down at the loading dock.

Isandro followed his gaze. Fifteen years he had crossed that dock weekly with mop buckets and recycling bins, and he had never once looked at it as a place where anything had happened. Now it lay below in the gray light, scuffed concrete and a rusted rail, ordinary as a parking lot, and he understood that this was Duval's deepest advantage: the scene of the crime refused to look like one.

"They'll eat me alive this afternoon if I go back up there," Voss said. Not bitter. Almost curious, the way he said most things.

"Then don't."

Voss turned. His face in daylight was worse than Isandro remembered—older than sixty should allow, the skin stretched thin over something that had been burning a long time. "Your sister asked me the same thing last night. Don't come back. Let the documents carry it." He smiled, and it was not a pleasant smile but it was an honest one. "Fifteen years ago I let documents carry it. I signed my statement, I took my check, I drove west, and I told myself the paper would speak for me." He tapped the window glass, down at the dock. "Paper speaks with whatever voice the loudest lawyer gives it. I'm what's left over when the lawyering's done. They need to see what's left over."

"You saw a man on that dock," Isandro said quietly. "Tell them again."

"I saw a man on that dock," Voss repeated, and for a moment his voice lost its flatness and became what it had been on the night itself, Isandro was sure of it—young, frightened, certain. "Tall. Light coat. Standing where there was no reason for anyone to stand at eleven-forty at night. And your father went over that rail four minutes later, and the man walked away calm as a man leaving work." He exhaled. "That's my whole testimony. Everything else is just the life I've lived since nobody believed it."

Down on the dock, a delivery truck backed in with a beep of good-natured machinery, and two men began unloading instrument cases, laughing about something. The world's indifference was total. Isandro found it almost restful, in a horrifying way.

"The evaluation," Isandro said. "The psych evaluation. Was it true?"

Voss considered him. Most people would have been offended. Voss seemed relieved. "True enough. You spend fifteen years as the only witness to something everyone's paid to forget, you develop symptoms." He straightened his jacket. "Your turn soon, isn't it? The Okonkwo children."

"Adaeze testifies tomorrow. Then Rosa. Then—" He stopped.

"Then you," Voss said. "Reading the letter." He studied Isandro's face with those hooded, patient eyes. "Do you know what you're going to feel when you finish reading it? Out loud, in front of all of them?"

"No."

"Neither did I," Voss said. "That's why I'm telling you. Nobody knows until they're standing in it." And he walked back toward the hearing room alone, a gaunt man in a brown jacket going to be disbelieved for the second time in his life, on purpose.

* * *

Terrence testified at two o'clock.

Isandro had begged him not to. Not loudly—they had passed the whole weekend in a truce of lowered voices, the family having learned that volume solved nothing—but at Sunday dinner, over Adaeze's jollof that tasted like apology, Isandro had said, "Uncle, you don't owe them anything. You negotiated in good faith. You were trying to keep two kids alive."

Terrence had buttered a piece of bread with terrible concentration and said, "A man my age doesn't get to choose what he owes anymore, Isandro. He just finds out what it was."

Now Terrence sat in the witness chair in his church suit, seventy-three years old, hands folded on his knee in a posture so like Crane's from the morning that Isandro's stomach turned. Under oath, he confirmed what the bank records showed: that beginning in November 2009, monthly payments had flowed from a Meridian Holdings subsidiary to an account he controlled as trustee for the Okonkwo family. Fourteen years. He confirmed the amounts. He confirmed the signature authority.

Duval rose for cross-examination, and here she changed instruments entirely. With Voss she had used a scalpel. With Terrence she used silk.

"Mr. Okonkwo-Terrence, I'm sorry—how do you prefer?"

"Terrence is fine."

"Terrence. You've raised these two children since they were nineteen and nine. That's a considerable act of love."

"It was a duty."

"And the settlement you negotiated—the one counsel for the complainants calls 'hush money'—did you understand it that way? Did anyone use that phrase with you?"

"No one used any phrase. There was a document. My attorney reviewed it. It provided for the family's support following the death of Emmanuel Okonkwo, whose passing was ruled accidental by the coroner." Each clause delivered like a man laying bricks.

"So you accepted support for grieving children, from a foundation connected to their father's professional world, after an accidental death. And fourteen years later, a journalist and a disgraced former employee have renamed that support a crime, and now you sit here while strangers suggest you helped bury a murder." Duval let the silence breathe. "Does that feel like justice to you, Terrence?"

"Objection," said the commission's counsel, mildly, and the chair sustained it, but the damage was done; Isandro watched it land in the room like pollen. Because it *was* the question, wasn't it—the one Isandro had been turning over since the night he'd found his mother's unsent letter. What did justice mean when the wronged family had eaten from the wrongdoer's hand for fourteen years and been nourished?

The chairwoman leaned forward. "Mr.—Terrence. I have one question of my own. In your own words, without counsel's help: why did you accept the payments?"

The room went very quiet. Somewhere a camera shutter clicked and was shushed.

Terrence looked at his hands for a long time. Isandro knew that look. He had seen it across Sunday tables his whole childhood—his uncle assembling sentences the way he assembled everything, carefully, load-bearing, nothing wasted. And Isandro realized, with a dread that rose through him like water filling a hull, that Terrence was deciding, right now, in real time, whether to say the true thing or the safe thing, and that fifteen years of Sundays hung on which one came out.

"There was a lawsuit coming," Terrence said finally. "From the conservatory's insurers. About the debut performance. About damages related to—there were claims the school had failed in its duty of care to a student who suffered a breakdown. The family had nothing. I had nothing. A man came to me and said the foundation wished to help, and that helping would be simpler if litigation ended." His voice stayed level. Only his hands moved, unfolding, refolding. "I signed. I told myself I was saving those children. Some nights I still tell myself that."

"And other nights?"

The question came from Commissioner Okafor-Hughes herself, soft as a knife going in.

Terrence lifted his head and found Isandro in the second row, and what passed between them in that half-second was the whole of their history—the man who had taught him to drive, who had paid for strings and sheet music, who had sat beside him at the funeral of the brother whose death he'd helped price out—and then Terrence looked away, back to the commission, and said:

"Other nights I know I sold something I had no right to sell. I just thank God the children were too young to know it was theirs."

The gavel came down for the day's adjournment thirty seconds later, and the sound barely covered the noise of the room exhaling.

* * *

Afterward the corridor was chaos—reporters calling questions, a photographer's flash strobing off the trophy case, someone from the conservatory's communications office trying to herd the commission members toward a side exit. Isandro stood against the wall by the trophy case and watched his family's private shame become public property in real time. By evening, he knew, Terrence's words would be headlines, stripped of context, argued over by strangers who had never eaten at that table. *SOLD SOMETHING I HAD NO RIGHT TO SELL.* The sentence would belong to everyone now. That was the loss inside the justice, the thing Adaeze hadn't fully believed when he'd tried to describe it and he hadn't fully believed himself until this moment: you did not get to hand the truth to the world and keep the world's hands clean. The truth came with fingerprints all over it, and everyone who touched it left their mark.

Adaeze found him there. She was pale under her composure, her notebook clutched to her chest like a schoolgirl's, and for a moment neither of them spoke. Down the hall, Terrence was being escorted through the crowd by the commission's staff, moving like a man wading upstream, and he did not look at them, and Isandro understood that he might never again, quite, look at them the same way—that something had ended today in the family that would not be renamed or rebranded or settled.

"Tomorrow it's me," Adaeze said.

"I know."

"I'm going to tell them about the dock. About being nine. About Terrence telling me I dreamed it." Her jaw set in the way Isandro had learned, over forty years, meant the conversation was already over. "And then Thursday, they call you."

"Yes."

She reached out and gripped his forearm, hard, the same grip she'd used on him at their mother's funeral, the grip that was the oldest language they had. "Whatever happens in that room," she said, "when you read it—read it like he's listening. Not them. Him."

Then she released him and walked into the flashbulbs with her chin up, and Isandro stood alone at the trophy case, looking at the engraved names of decades of students, and thought about his father's handwriting, and about how many hours remained between now and the moment he would have to give his father's voice to a room full of strangers—

—and that was when he saw the commission clerk working down the witness list, pen moving, and heard her say to her assistant, not quietly enough: "Okonkwo goes Thursday morning. First thing. Before the defense gets another full day with our witnesses."

Thursday morning. Thirty-six hours.

His hands, hanging at his sides, had begun, very faintly, to shake.
