## 4. The Drowned Girl

The thing about drinking at nine in the morning is that people stop asking you questions, and the thing about people stopping asking you questions is that eventually you start answering ones nobody asked.

I was on my third beer — or fourth; I'd stopped counting when counting stopped meaning anything — sitting on the back steps of the Calloway house where the boards sag in a way that matches a man's spine after sixty years of salt air, when she came around the side of the building. The Winterbourne girl. Vivian's daughter. She had her mother's jaw and none of her mother's caution, which I suppose is what happens when grief outruns manners.

"Mr. Calloway," she said. "Ruth said you might talk to me."

"Ruth says a lot of things," I told her. "Most of them about me. None of them flattering."

She didn't laugh. Vivian would have laughed. That's how I knew whose daughter she was before Ruth even confirmed it — not the jaw, but the way she stood there waiting for an answer like the answer owed her something.

"I'm going to Marrow Bay Cemetery this afternoon," she said. "I understand there's a grave there I should see."

* * *

Here is what I know about Lena Okafor, and I want it understood that I know it the way you know a scar — not because somebody described it to you, but because you were there when it was made.

She came to Marrow Bay in 1978 with her father, who worked the boats, and by 1980 she and Vivian were inseparable, two girls joined at the hip like a pair of scissors, which turned out to be more prophetic than anybody wanted. Lena wrote songs. Not little songs — songs. Songs that made the choir go quiet afterward, that made old Pastor Hollis take his glasses off and polish them longer than glasses need polishing. And Vivian harmonized. That was the arrangement: Lena built them, Vivian lifted them.

I was four years older and mostly useless, but I drove them places, because I had a car and they had nowhere to be that wasn't each other. I drove them to the seawall, to the drive-in two towns over, to the church on Sundays when neither of them believed a word of it but liked the singing. I was the furniture of their friendship. Furniture doesn't get asked things. Furniture just gets sat on and moved and, eventually, left behind in a house nobody lives in anymore.

In August of 1989, Lena drowned.

That's the sentence the town has been living inside for thirty-five years. *Lena drowned.* Four syllables, ruled accidental, closed like a drawer. What the sentence doesn't tell you is that the water that night was flat as a hymnal. No storm. No current worth the name. A woman who swam like she'd been invented in the ocean went under a hundred yards from shore on a night so calm the moon had a reflection you could walk on.

What the sentence also doesn't tell you is that my car was parked near the seawall that night.

* * *

The Winterbourne girl walked beside me to the cemetery because I offered and she accepted, and because I'd found, somewhere between the third beer and the fourth, that my mouth had come loose from its moorings.

"Lena Okafor," I said, when we reached the stone. It's a modest stone. The town spent more on the pastor's parking lot. "Best friend your mother ever had. Best songwriter this coast ever produced, though you'll have to take my word, because there's nothing left with her name on it."

Elodie crouched and read the dates. Born 1971. Died 1989. Eighteen years old. She did the arithmetic the way her mother used to do it — fast, and then again slower, as if hoping for a different sum.

"The radio played one of her songs," Elodie said. "Last week. They credited a man named Deane."

"Deane." I said the name like spitting out a fishbone. "Pastor Deane. He came after Hollis died. Young then. Ambitious the way a net is ambitious — everything goes in, nothing comes out."

"You knew him?"

"Everybody knew him. He knew everybody better. That was his gift." I sat down on the grass, which Ruth would've called disgraceful, and which felt appropriate. You don't visit Lena standing up. "He took over the choir. And the choir started sounding different. Better, some said. Richer. Those were Lena's songs, girl. Every last one of them. He sang them under his own name, registered them under his own name, and when folks asked, he said Lena would've wanted the music to live on. And here's the devil of it — maybe she would have. Maybe that's even true. But wanting music to live on isn't the same as letting a man wear it like a coat he bought."

Elodie was quiet a long moment. Gulls were working the harbor below, screaming their one opinion over and over.

"My mother," she said carefully. "Did she know?"

And there it was. The question I'd been walking toward for thirty-five years, arriving the way bad news always arrives — already seated in the room, waiting for you to notice it.

"Your mother knew," I said. "Your mother knew everything."

* * *

I want to be fair to Vivian. Dead women deserve fairness more than most, since they can't contest the record.

Vivian didn't steal anything. Let's establish that first, because I can feel the girl's mind sorting people into piles already — thieves and victims, guilty and clean — and the truth of Marrow Bay doesn't sort. It never has. That's the whole disease of the place.

After Lena died, Vivian changed. Not the way people say it at funerals — *she was never the same* — but actually, measurably, like a key cut new. She stopped writing. Thirty years, not one song, and a guitar in a case she wouldn't open if the house was on fire. She left town and only came back for funerals, standing at the back of the church with her coat still buttoned, leaving before the coffee. And once — once, in 1994, when I was sober enough to remember it and drunk enough to say it — I asked her why she let Deane keep the songs.

She looked at me the way you look at a man holding something breakable, and she said, "Amos. Some doors you hold shut so other people can sleep."

I asked her what that meant. She said, "You know exactly what that means. You were there."

And I did know. God help me, I did.

Because here's the part I've never said out loud, not to Ruth, not to the priest, not to the bottle, though the bottle and I have discussed it nightly: the night Lena drowned, I saw her argue with Vivian on the seawall. I was down the beach with a girl whose name I mercifully forget, and I saw the two of them silhouetted against the water, and I saw Vivian walk away, and I saw Lena stay. And I saw a third figure further along the wall, half in shadow, watching. Tall. Collar. I told myself it was nobody. I told myself a lot of things that year, the way you throw sand on a fire — enough of it and the light stops showing through.

My car was near the water because I was near the water. That's all my guilt amounts to, technically. Technically. But guilt doesn't read technicalities, and neither does a town. If it ever came out that Amos Calloway's car sat fifty feet from where Lena Okafor went into a flat calm sea, well — this town chose its story in 1989 and it will defend that story the way it defends its moorings, because if the story goes, the whole harbor drains.

So I kept quiet. We all kept quiet. Quiet is the local industry. We export it.

* * *

By the time we got back to the boarding house the light had gone long and orange, and I was sober in the way that follows drunk — every nerve exposed, every thought too loud. Elodie stopped at the bottom of the steps.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked. "Really."

I considered lying to her. I'm good at it; I've had practice. But she had her mother's eyes when they wanted something, and those eyes had never once accepted a lie from me, even when I was only lying about how much I'd had.

"Because relief," I said, "is just fear wearing a nicer shirt. I've been carrying this so long I forgot it was heavy. You asked, and it came out, and for about ten minutes I felt lighter than I have since Reagan was president." I rubbed my face. "But now I'm sitting here listening to how quiet this street is, and I'm thinking about who else is listening. Because this town hears everything, Miss Winterbourne. And it forgives nothing except silence."

"That sounds like a warning."

"It's a weather report." I nodded up the street, where the church steeple stood against the darkening sky like a finger raised for attention. "Deane's still alive, you know. Old now. Sick, they say. His boy runs the church accounts these days, and his boy guards those records like a dog guards bones. You start pulling this thread, you're not pulling it alone. There's forty years of knots on the other end, and every one of them is tied around somebody's throat."

"Then someone should untie them."

I almost laughed. Almost. It was such a young thing to say, such a Vivian thing to say, from back before Vivian learned what doors were for.

"Go home," I told her gently. "Write your book about somewhere else. Some towns drown their dead twice — once in the water and once in the story. Don't let this one do it to you."

She looked at me for a long moment, and I watched her decide — watched it happen behind her eyes like weather coming in off the water.

"Mr. Calloway," she said, "my mother kept a locked drawer for thirty years. I opened it last week. Whatever this town buried, she kept receipts."

Then she went inside, and the door clicked shut behind her with the sound of a lock turning, and I stood on Ruth's sagging steps feeling the relief drain out of me and the fear pour in to fill the space, cold and rising, the tide coming back in.

Down the street, a porch light went off. Then another. Then, at the far end, near the church, a car door closed — soft, deliberate, the way doors close when someone wants the closing heard by no one.

I stood very still and listened to Marrow Bay being quiet, and for the first time in thirty-five years, the quiet sounded less like peace and more like held breath.
