## 2. The Notice

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which felt appropriate. Tuesdays were the day the world delivered its paperwork — invoices from the county, calibration certificates for the strain gauges, a reminder that Theo's dental cleaning had been moved to Thursday. Astrid sorted the mail at the kitchen counter the way she sorted everything: by category, by urgency, by how much of herself each item would require.

The certified envelope was cream-colored and heavier than it should have been. Probate Court, County of Marion. Her name typed out in full — Ms. Astrid J. Eastwick — with a formality that made her feel like a stranger to herself.

She set it between the fruit bowl and the toaster and went to change out of her work clothes.

This was not avoidance, she told herself. This was sequencing. You didn't open a load-bearing wall before you'd shored up the ceiling. There was an order to things, and the order was: shower first, tea second, letter third. She was very good at order. Order was, if she were honest — and she tried not to be, mostly — the load-bearing wall of her entire personality.

* * *

In the shower she thought about expansion joints.

She'd spent the morning at the Halvorsen overpass with a junior engineer named Priya who asked excellent questions and laughed at all the right moments, which made Astrid feel both useful and vaguely fraudulent. They'd measured the joint openings at pier three. Everything within tolerance. The bridge was doing exactly what bridges do, which is move — a few millimeters here, a few there, breathing with temperature and traffic — and the whole art of the profession was making room for movement so nothing cracked.

"Ten years," Priya had said, crouched beside her with the calipers, "and you've never once seen a failure?"

Astrid had considered this. "I've seen plenty of failures. Just rarely where anyone's looking."

Priya had laughed. Astrid hadn't meant it as a joke.

Now, standing under water that was technically the correct temperature, she thought about how she and Theo had installed their own expansion joints years ago without ever discussing it. The way he took his calls in the garage. The way she scheduled her runs for when he watched his documentaries. The way they'd each learned, with the quiet competence of two engineers building separate structures on adjacent lots, to leave gaps where the cracking would otherwise occur.

Everything within tolerance. Nothing actually connected.

She turned off the water before the thought could develop further. Some inspections you postponed.

* * *

The tea did not help. Tea was supposed to be a ritual that steadied the hands, but her hands were steady; it was everything behind them that had gone loose. She sat at the kitchen table in her oldest cardigan and looked at the envelope the way she might look at a hairline fracture in a concrete abutment — knowing it was small, knowing it was structural, knowing that the difference between those two facts was the entire job.

Theo came home at six-fifteen, which was normal, carrying groceries, which was also normal, and he kissed the top of her head, which was normal too, and none of it registered as warmth so much as confirmation that the system was still running.

"You got mail," he said, nodding at the envelope, which sat where she'd left it like a cat that had decided to live there.

"Probate court."

He paused, grocery bag halfway to the counter. "About your mother's house?"

"The house." Not her mother's house. The house. She'd been practicing this grammatical distancing for two years and had gotten quite fluent at it. "They're moving to auction. I have thirty days to claim personal property or sign a release."

"Auction." He said it carefully, testing the word for weight. "That seems fast."

"It's been two years, Theo."

"I know. It just—" He put the milk in the fridge, giving himself something to do with his hands, a habit she recognized because she shared it. "It doesn't seem like something that should happen by certified letter."

"No," she agreed. "But most true things don't arrive politely."

He looked at her then, really looked, and for one unguarded second she saw him deciding whether to ask the next question — whether she wanted company when she opened it, whether she was okay, whether *okay* was even the right instrument for measuring what she was. She watched the question assemble itself behind his eyes and then, with the gentle efficiency of long practice, dismantle itself again.

"Do you want me to make the pasta?" he said instead.

"Please."

And that was their marriage, right there, in miniature: he offered pasta where he could not offer presence, and she accepted pasta because accepting it was easier than explaining why it wasn't enough. The meal would be good. He was a genuinely good cook. They would eat it at the table, and he would tell her about the permit dispute at work, and she would tell him about pier three, and neither of them would say anything false, and neither of them would say anything true, and afterward they would wash dishes side by side in a silence so well-engineered it had won awards, if silences won awards, which they didn't, which was probably for the best.

* * *

She opened the letter at nine, alone at the kitchen table while Theo read in the living room with the lamp on low, the particular arrangement of domestic life that let them be together and apart simultaneously, a design solution so elegant she sometimes marveled at it and sometimes wanted to take a sledgehammer to it.

*RE: Estate of Eleanor Voss (deceased). Notice of Intended Sale of Real Property.*

Her mother's name in typewriter font. Two years dead and still capable of arriving unannounced.

Astrid read the way she read inspection reports — fast, technical, hunting for the numbers. Parcel number. Assessed value. Date of auction: the twenty-third. Deadline for claims against the estate: fourteen days from receipt. A paragraph of boilerplate about interested parties. A signature from someone named Gerald P. Hutchins, Deputy Commissioner, whose handwriting suggested a man who signed four hundred documents a week and had stopped seeing them as containing people somewhere around 1998.

Fourteen days. Thirty days until the auction itself.

She calculated, automatically, the drive time to Kestrel Lane. Four hours and forty minutes if she avoided the interstate through Corvane, longer if she stopped, and she would stop, because there was a bakery in Millbrook that her mother had loved and that Astrid had driven past twice a year for twenty years without going in, and she suspected that this time she would go in, and she suspected that this was the kind of small surrender that preceded larger ones.

Three weeks, minimum. Maybe more. Sorting a house like that one — a house that had accumulated forty years of a woman's life the way river sediment accumulates, layer on layer, undisturbed — would take every day of it.

She got up and walked to the hallway closet.

The boxes were where they had always been, since the funeral, since the drive back from Kestrel Lane with the car packed full of things Wren had pressed on her at the last minute — "take some of it, take *something*, Astrid, you can't leave with nothing" — and Astrid had taken six boxes to make the leaving look like grief rather than escape, and had stacked them in the closet, and had not opened them.

Two years. Six boxes. She knew them by their contents the way she knew bridge components: box one, linens; box two, kitchen things including the blue enamel colander; box three, books; box four, photographs she could not yet look at; box five, miscellany; box six, the Christmas ornaments, which Wren had packed last and wept over and which Astrid had carried to the car without crying at all, and had hated herself for that, quietly, for two years.

She stood in front of the closet with the probate letter in her hand and did not open any of them.

This, too, was sequencing. She told herself she'd deal with the boxes at the house, where they belonged, where context would make them bearable. She told herself that opening a box of her mother's linens in a hallway in her own home, under fluorescent light, next to the vacuum cleaner, would be unbearable in a way that served no one. These were reasonable engineering judgments. She believed them approximately sixty percent.

The other forty percent knew the truth, which was that the boxes were sealed for the same reason certain bridge decks were closed to traffic: not because the structure had failed, but because no one had yet determined whether it could hold.

* * *

She called Wren the next morning, which surprised them both.

Their calls followed a protocol older than either of them could remember: brief, logistical, warm at the edges the way a reheated meal is warm at the edges. Wren lived in Portland now, worked in hospital administration, had a life Astrid knew mainly through its furniture — a new couch mentioned in passing, a promotion described in one sentence, a friend named Cassie referenced often enough that Astrid felt she almost knew her.

"They sent you the letter too," Wren said, by way of hello. Not a question.

"This morning. Yesterday, technically."

"Hutchins." Wren said the name the way you'd spit out a seed. "He called me in March to confirm I'd been notified of 'proceedings.' Proceedings. Like it's a weather event."

"It is, sort of. Slow-moving. Two-year flood stage."

Wren made a sound that was almost a laugh. Almost. Their conversations were full of these almosts — jokes that nearly landed, questions that nearly got asked, forgiveness that nearly arrived. "Are you going?"

"I have to. Fourteen days to file a claim on personal property or it all goes with the house." Astrid turned her coffee mug a quarter rotation on the table. "I'll need at least three weeks. The attic alone—"

"The attic." Wren's voice changed, flattened. "Right. The attic."

There it was, the first fault line of the call. The attic was Wren's territory — Wren had been the one up there every winter hauling down decorations, every summer moving boxes away from the roof leak their mother refused to fix, Wren who knew the attic's geography the way Astrid knew bridge decks. Astrid had left at eighteen and the attic had stayed, in some unspoken division of assets, Wren's.

"I'm not cataloging without you," Astrid said. "I want to do it together. Or— I want you to tell me what matters. What to keep out of the lots."

A pause long enough to have weather in it.

"You want my inventory," Wren said. "Of Mom's things."

"I want—"

"Because here's what I remember, Astrid. I remember packing six boxes for you at the funeral while you stood by the car. And now the court sends you a letter and suddenly it's a project we're doing together."

"Wren—"

"I'll email you a list." Flat. Final. The sound of a gate closing on well-maintained hinges. "The things that matter. Save yourself the trip."

"I'm coming anyway."

Another pause. Longer. When Wren spoke again her voice had done something complicated — softened and hardened at once, like concrete curing wrong. "Fine. Come. But Astrid? The blue room stays locked. I mean it. Whatever you think is in there, whatever you've decided it means after all these years — it stays shut."

The line went quiet in the specific way lines go quiet when both people are holding words they've decided not to spend.

"I'll see you Saturday," Astrid said.

"Saturday," Wren confirmed, and hung up.

* * *

That evening Astrid sat at the kitchen table with a legal pad and began, in self-defense, to plan.

This was her oldest coping mechanism and she deployed it without shame: columns, timelines, contingencies. *Day 1–2: travel, initial walkthrough. Day 3–5: ground floor inventory.* She wrote *contact Hutchins re: extension* and *photograph everything before Dev's team tags it* — the auction house had assigned someone named Dev Anand, according to the packet, an appraiser who would arrive Tuesday — and she wrote *boxes from closet: bring? decide* and then drew a small box around *decide* the way she boxed critical values on her field reports.

Theo came in, read the pad upside down from across the table, and pulled out a chair.

"Three weeks," he said.

"At least."

He nodded slowly. "I could come. Weekends. Or—" He stopped. Restarted, the way a sentence does when it's been edited mid-flight. "Or I could drive out the last week. Help you pack the car."

The offer was genuine. That was the thing about Theo that made everything harder — his offers were always genuine, always reasonably sized, always delivered at the correct moment with the correct tone. He was a kind man who had learned, somewhere in the last decade, to love her in units she could accept without having to feel them.

"That would help," she said. "The last week. There'll be a lot of lifting."

He smiled, relieved, and reached across the table and squeezed her hand, and she let him, and the squeeze lasted exactly as long as squeezes between people who trust each other completely and touch each other rarely.

After he went up to bed she sat with the legal pad a while longer, and at the bottom, below the columns and the contingencies, in the margin where no plan was supposed to live, she wrote one line in smaller handwriting:

*Kestrel Lane. 14 days.*

Then, beneath it, after a long moment, in even smaller handwriting, almost as if testing whether the pen still worked:

*What am I afraid of?*

She looked at the question for a long time. Then she did what she always did with findings she wasn't ready to certify: she tore the page off the pad, folded it into quarters, and put it in her jacket pocket, where she kept such things — the small sealed fractures, the readings she'd take again later, when she was stronger, or when the structure finally forced the issue.

Upstairs, Theo's light clicked off. In the closet, six boxes waited in the dark with their patient, terrible patience.

Saturday, she thought. Four days. The blue room stays locked.

She turned off the kitchen light and stood a moment in the dark, listening to the house settle around its own small tolerances, and wondered — not for the first time, but with new urgency, the way a hairline crack becomes urgent only when you notice it spreading — what else in her life was holding, and what was merely waiting.
