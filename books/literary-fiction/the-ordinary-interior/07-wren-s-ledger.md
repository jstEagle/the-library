## 7. Wren's Ledger

Wren had been keeping accounts since she was nine years old, when her mother handed her a spiral notebook and said, *You're the organized one, Wrennie. Write down what we owe people.* The first entry was $14.75 to Mr. Petrossian at the corner store for bread, milk, and a lottery ticket their mother swore was an investment. Wren had written it in careful block letters, dated it, and felt — even then, at nine — the strange adult pleasure of a debt made visible. A thing on paper could be managed. A thing on paper could eventually be crossed out.

She was forty-three now, and the notebook habit had metastasized into three binders, a color-coded spreadsheet, and a mental ledger so comprehensive it kept her awake some nights like a second heartbeat.

The current column, the one that mattered, read:

*To Astrid: twenty years of silence, unreturned phone calls (eleven), one Christmas card per year with a signature but no note, zero visits during Mom's illness.*

*From Astrid: nothing. Which is also something.*

Wren sat on the back steps of the Kestrel Lane house with a thermos of coffee going cold in her hands and watched her sister through the kitchen window. Astrid moved through the rooms the way she apparently moved through everything — methodically, carefully, touching nothing longer than necessary. She'd arrived yesterday with a suitcase and a laser measuring device, which was such an Astrid object that Wren had almost laughed. Almost.

*You're the organized one.* Except that wasn't true anymore, was it? Somewhere along the way Astrid had become the organized one too — from a distance, where organization was easy. Where grief was easy. You could catalog a dead woman's spoons from two thousand miles away if you never had to smell the house while she was dying in it.

The resentment rose in Wren like water finding its level, familiar and shameful in equal measure. She knew the shame was part of the package. She'd read the articles. *Caregiver burnout is real. Resentment is normal. You have to practice self-compassion.* She had practiced self-compassion the way she practiced everything: diligently, briefly, and with a running tally of how much time it was costing her.

Here was the thing Wren had never said out loud, not to anyone, not even to Dr. Okafor on Tuesdays at four: she hadn't stayed because she was good. She'd stayed because leaving would have meant admitting what staying cost, and Wren Eastwick did not admit costs. She recorded them. There was a difference, and the difference had eaten her alive by slow degrees for six years.

* * *

Their mother's decline had come on like weather — forecastable, deniable, then suddenly everywhere. First it was small things. The gas left on twice in one month. A grocery list that said *eggs, eggs, eggs* in increasingly urgent handwriting. Then the afternoon Wren came over and found her mother standing in the middle of Kestrel Lane itself, in her housecoat, asking a startled jogger if he'd seen two little girls. "They were just here," she kept saying. "Astrid and Wren. They were just here."

Wren was fifty-one inches tall again in that moment, holding her mother's cold hand on the asphalt, saying, "I'm here, Mom. I'm right here." And her mother had looked at her — looked *through* her, that was the terrible part — and said, "No, you're the big one. The big one went away."

The big one being Astrid. Who had gone away at eighteen and never really come back, except in December, bearing cards.

So Wren had done the arithmetic every daughter does and no daughter should have to do. Someone had to move closer. Someone had to take the power-of-attorney paperwork to the lawyer's office on Route 9 and sit in the waiting room reading a two-year-old *Field & Stream* while their mother signed things she mostly understood. Someone had to learn the difference between the good hospice nurse and the one who smelled like cigarettes and impatience. Someone had to hold their mother's hand at 3 a.m. on a Tuesday in March and lie to her — gently, over and over — that yes, Astrid was coming this weekend, yes, she'd be here soon.

That last one. God. That last one had cost more than all the others combined. Because their mother, even dissolving, even gone most days into whatever country the dying inhabit, lit up at the name. *Astrid.* She'd say it like a password to somewhere better. And Wren would nod and pour the water and adjust the blanket and add another line to the ledger.

*To Astrid: one lie per visit, approximately two hundred lies total, each one a small theft from my own mouth.*

She wasn't proud of the ledger. That was the thing nobody told you about resentment: it didn't feel righteous. It felt like drinking. It felt like a thirst that got worse the more you attended to it. Some nights, after her mother finally slept, Wren would sit in the blue room — the locked one, the one their mother had kept shut for decades until the dementia opened everything indiscriminately — and she would look at the strange objects inside and feel absolutely nothing but tired. Boxes of someone else's life. Letters she'd never read, because reading them felt like one more task, and Wren's capacity for tasks had hit zero sometime around year four.

Maybe that was the unforgivable thing, she thought now, watching Astrid's silhouette move behind the frosted kitchen glass. Not that Wren had stayed. But that she'd stopped *feeling* it somewhere in the middle. Caregiving without love is just labor. And she had clocked out emotionally years before her mother died, and had stood at the funeral dry-eyed next to a box of ashes while relatives murmured about how devoted she was, and had wanted to scream: *I'm not devoted. I'm just the one who didn't leave.*

Except Astrid had left first. That was the whole point. That was the load-bearing wall of Wren's entire grievance architecture. Astrid left at eighteen after a fight neither of them ever discussed, and everything after — the cards, the distance, the eleven unreturned calls — grew from that foundation.

And yet.

And yet there was a splinter in it, and Wren had spent two years trying to ignore the splinter, and now, watching her sister measure doorframes like the house was a bridge, the splinter had begun to work its way to the surface. Because Astrid had come. However late, however wrong, however armed with spreadsheets and measuring devices — she had gotten in her car and driven here and walked into the house that Wren knew made her skin crawl. Something had brought her. Wren didn't know what yet. But nine-year-old Wren, keeper of ledgers, knew that debts ran both directions, and she had a growing, unwelcome suspicion that her own column had entries she'd never audited.

* * *

The email had arrived on a Thursday, sitting in her inbox between a dental reminder and a newsletter from a native plant society she'd joined during her mother's illness and never quit because quitting felt like one more loss.

**Subject: Offer of Position — Field Director, Coastal Restoration Programme**

It was from a conservation trust based in Lisbon. They wanted her. They'd wanted her for two years, actually; this was the third offer, each one more insistent, each one requiring an answer within weeks rather than months. The salary was absurd. The work was exactly the work she'd trained for and then shelved — coastal wetland restoration, the thing she'd studied before life had other plans. Three years, renewable. Housing included. A photograph attached showed a gray-green estuary at dawn with a sentence underneath: *We think you'd be extraordinary here.*

Wren had read it forty times. She had drafted eleven replies. All eleven began with some version of *Thank you so much, but—*

But her mother was dead. That was the punchline, the awful liberating punchline, and Wren still couldn't say it. Her mother had been dead for two years and Wren was still performing the caregiving, still structuring her entire life around a duty that had ended, because the duty had become the only shape she knew how to occupy. If she took the job, she'd have to say the true sentence out loud: *I am exhausted. I gave six years to a dying woman and I have nothing left and I want to go somewhere the tide does the work and nobody needs me at 3 a.m.*

Saying that felt like betrayal. Saying it felt like the final abandonment, the one that would make all the earlier ones retroactively true. As long as Wren stayed exhausted and dutiful and slightly martyred, she got to keep the moral high ground, and the moral high ground was the only property she owned outright.

Her friend Priya had put it brutally over wine in April: "You're hoarding your exhaustion, Wren. It's the last thing she gave you and you won't spend it."

Wren had thrown a breadstick at her. Then gone home and cried in the bathtub for an hour, which was the closest she'd come to honesty in months.

Now she sat on the back steps with the cold coffee and the offer open on her phone, and she heard Astrid's footsteps cross the kitchen floor above her, and she thought: *she gets to leave again. She'll catalog everything, sell everything, fly back to her husband and her bridges, and I'll be the one who stays near the grave. Even now. Even dead, Mom finds a way to hand Astrid the exits.*

The unfairness of it was so clean, so perfect, that Wren almost admired it.

* * *

At noon, a van pulled up outside — magnetic signage, AUCTIONEERS & ESTATE APPRAISAL, and a young man who unfolded himself from the driver's seat with the unhurried grace of someone who had never once rushed in his life. Wren watched from the steps as Astrid answered the door. Watched her sister's shoulders do something they hadn't done since Wren arrived — drop, soften, come down from around her ears.

Wren narrowed her eyes.

She went inside ostensibly to get more coffee and actually to listen. This was also a skill the caregiving years had honed: the ability to monitor a household while appearing to do something else. She stood in the hallway with the kettle and heard the appraiser — Dev, according to the introduction that drifted down the hall — say something about a sewing machine, and heard him say it *wrong*, technically wrong, calling it a Singer 201 when any fool could see the decals were from a 66, and heard Astrid correct him, and heard Dev laugh — delighted, genuinely delighted — and say, "Okay, you're dangerous. Most people can't tell a 66 from a 201. What else do you know?"

And Astrid laughed.

Wren set the kettle down harder than necessary.

It wasn't jealousy. She wantted to be clear with herself about that. It was not jealousy. It was — she searched for the accurate term, because accuracy mattered, accuracy was the one virtue she'd never abandoned — it was *witness*. For six years Wren had been the only audience for their mother's decline, the sole keeper of records, and now here was Astrid laughing in the hallway with a stranger over a sewing machine, easy as anything, while Wren's own chest felt like a room with the furniture covered in sheets.

The kettle screamed. She let it scream for three full seconds before turning it off.

When she carried her mug into the front room, the two of them were crouched over the piano — the old upright, dead these fifteen years, three keys silent and the rest sour. Dev had lifted the lid and was peering inside like a doctor. "Moth damage in the hammers," he was saying. "Honestly? It's worth more as a story than an instrument."

"Everything in this house is," Astrid said, and there was something in her voice Wren couldn't file under any of her usual headings. Not guilt. Not avoidance. Something almost like wonder.

Wren cleared her throat. Both of them looked up. She saw Astrid's face do the thing it always did when Wren entered a room — a flicker, a recalculation, the warmth banked like a fire poked down for the night.

"Lunch," Wren said flatly. "There's soup. If either of you care."

"We care," said Dev warmly, and Wren decided, provisionally and against her better judgment, not to hate him.

* * *

That night, unable to sleep in her childhood bedroom with its water stain shaped like Portugal — *Portugal, of all things, the universe has a sense of humor* — Wren got up and went downstairs and opened her laptop at the kitchen table. The Lisbon email glowed in the dark kitchen. Third offer. Reply requested by month's end.

In the spreadsheet she kept for the estate — tab four, *Outstanding Decisions* — she added a row:

*Item: Accept/reject Lisbon position.*
*Status: Pending.*
*Blocker: Requires admission of exhaustion. Exhaustion admission requires surrendering moral high ground. Moral high ground is sole asset.*
*Proposed resolution: none.*

She stared at it. Then, below, in the notes field, she typed the thing she'd been circling for two years:

*I did not stay because I loved her more. I stayed because I was too tired to figure out who I was if I wasn't needed.*

She deleted it immediately. Then typed it again. Then closed the laptop without saving, which was its own kind of entry in the ledger — a debt acknowledged and immediately re-hidden.

Upstairs, Astrid's floor creaked. Once, twice. Pacing, or insomnia, or both. Wren lay in the dark listening to her sister's restless footsteps overhead and felt the strangest sensation: not anger, exactly, and not forgiveness, but curiosity. A thin, unwelcome, living thread of it.

*Astrid left at eighteen,* Wren reminded herself. *Whatever the fight was, whatever the reason, she chose the door.*

But the memory of that fight had always had a soft spot in it, a place where the details went blurry, and lying there in the dark Wren found herself probing that blur the way you probe a sore tooth. Astrid had run out of the house that night white as paper. Not angry-white. *Frightened*-white. Wren had been seventeen and furious and had never once, in twenty years, asked why her sister looked afraid.

Because asking would mean the ledger was wrong. Asking would mean the whole account had been misfiled.

Downstairs, faintly, she heard Astrid's door open again, and quiet footsteps moving toward the back of the house — toward the blue room, the locked one, whose key their mother had lost years ago and whose lock, Wren had noticed yesterday, now hung broken and swinging.

Wren sat up in bed.

She listened to her sister's footsteps stop outside the blue room. She listened to the long silence that followed. And the ledger-keeper in her, the nine-year-old with the spiral notebook, wrote a new entry in invisible ink:

*To investigate: what does Astrid know that I don't?*

*Interest: accruing.*
