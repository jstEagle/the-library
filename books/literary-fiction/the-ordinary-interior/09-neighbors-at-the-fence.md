## 9. Neighbors at the Fence

The knock came at nine-forty in the morning, which was too early for salespeople and too cheerful for bad news, and Astrid stood in the hallway of her childhood home holding a clipboard she didn't need, listening to the sound of someone humming on the porch.

She opened the door to a woman of about sixty wearing a cardigan the color of paprika and carrying, in both arms, a casserole dish so heavy it seemed to have opinions about its own transport. Behind her on the steps stood a tall man with silver at his temples and a folding chair under one arm, as though he'd come prepared to stay a while and had learned from experience that standing invitations were unreliable.

"You must be the Eastwick girl," the woman said. "The one who measures bridges."

"I—yes. Astrid."

"Marisol Okonkwo. This is Ted. We're number 41. You're number 39. We've been waiting for you for two years." She said this without accusation, the way one might report the weather or the arrival of a bus, and then held out the casserole dish. "Chicken and rice. Your mother's recipe. Or my mother's recipe that your mother stole, depending on who you ask, and since she's not here to defend herself, I'll let you decide."

Astrid took the dish. It was warm. That was the detail that undid her slightly — that someone had cooked this morning, in a kitchen forty feet away, using her mother's handwriting, and carried it across the fence line while Astrid had been inside cataloging doorknobs.

"Come in," she said, because there was nothing else to say.

* * *

They filled the kitchen the way water fills a basin — completely, without effort. Ted set up the folding chair and then, surveying the room, decided against sitting in it and instead leaned against the counter where the phone used to hang, which Astrid suspected was where he'd always stood. Marisol moved through the space like she owned a key to it, opening drawers she clearly knew the contents of, producing a serving spoon from the third drawer down without looking.

"The good spoons are still here," she said, delighted. "Ruthie hid them from me for twenty years. Every time I lent her something she'd return it and keep the spoon."

"She kept them," Astrid said faintly.

"Of course she kept them. Ruthie Eastwick kept everything. Didn't she?" Marisol looked at her then, really looked, with an expression that was kind and appraising at once. "You're doing the inventory. Dev told us. He's been over at our place twice this week telling us about your piano."

"It's not my piano."

"He says it's a Broadwood and it's dead as a doornail and you laughed when he played it anyway." Marisol's eyes crinkled. "He said that like it was important news. Like he'd discovered something."

Astrid felt heat rise in her face and turned toward the kettle out of pure reflex. Her mother's kettle. Her mother's stove. She filled it because her hands wanted employment.

"We were just—" she began. "It's a broken instrument. There's a comedy to it."

"There's a comedy to most things," said Ted from the counter. His voice was low and unhurried, a bass note to Marisol's melody. "That's what your mother used to say. When things went wrong, she'd say, well, Ted, there's the comedy. And we'd sit out on her porch and find it."

Astrid's hand paused on the kettle handle.

"You sat on her porch?"

"Every Sunday," Marisol said. "Seven o'clock. For eleven years. Since Frank died — you remember Frank, no, of course you don't, you were gone before Frank — since Frank died, your mother couldn't sleep Sundays, and I couldn't either, so we started having coffee in the evenings instead of mornings, and it became a thing. A fixture. The whole street knew about it. The Hendersons' boy used to cut through our yards to avoid interrupting it."

Eleven years. Astrid did the arithmetic against her own absences and arrived at a number she didn't want. Eleven years of Sundays. She had sent birthday cards. She had called on holidays, briefly, with Wren's voice tense in the background and her mother's voice pitched carefully bright, everything load-tested and structurally sound and bearing nothing.

"What did you talk about?" she asked, and hated how small the question sounded.

Ted and Marisol exchanged a glance — the glance of people who have shared a long history and can conduct entire conversations in punctuation.

"Oh," Marisol said, "everything. Nothing. The Hendersons' hedge. Whether the mailman was having an affair — he wasn't, poor man, he was just tired. Ted's knees. My sister in Tucson. Ruthie read us poems sometimes. Did you know she wrote them? Little ones. On the backs of envelopes."

"No," Astrid said. "I didn't know that."

"Well." Marisol patted the counter, as if steadying it. "Now you know some of it."

* * *

The casserole, when they finally ate it — because Marisol would not hear of anyone eating anything else, and produced plates from the cupboard above the sink, second shelf, left side, which she navigated blind — tasted like Astrid's childhood in a way that made her throat tight. Not any specific meal. Just the temperature of being fed by someone who knew what you liked before you said it.

"Your mother made this every time someone on the street had trouble," Ted said. "When the Okonkwos' roof leaked, chicken and rice. When old Mr. Prasad's wife passed, chicken and rice. When I had my hip done, chicken and rice, delivered with instructions so detailed you'd think it was ordnance."

"She liked instructions," Astrid said.

"She liked *being useful*," Marisol corrected gently. "Instructions were how she loved people. You'd know that."

Would she? Astrid looked down at her plate. She thought of her own inspection reports, her tolerance tables, her habit of reducing the world to measurements because measurements couldn't disappoint you. She thought of Theo asking, every few months, *are you all right*, and her answering *fine*, and how fine had become a load-bearing word in their house.

"She was hard on herself," Marisol went on, into the silence, softly. "Those last two years. After the diagnosis started showing. She'd forget a word mid-sentence and get furious at herself, and I'd say, Ruthie, half the street can't finish a sentence sober, and she'd laugh, and then ten minutes later she'd be furious again. But she never once — and I want you to hear this, because I think you carry things — she never once said a word against either of her girls. Not one. People fish, you know. They say, oh, my daughter doesn't call, isn't that a shame. Ruthie never fished. If you asked about you or Wren, she'd say, 'They're busy. They're building their lives. That's what we raised them to do.' And then she'd change the subject to the hedge."

Astrid set down her fork.

The grief that came up then wasn't the clean grief of loss. It was the other kind — the kind with gratitude folded into it like a fault line, the kind that says: *she was tender, and you weren't there, and strangers saw it, and they are being kind to you right now with the same tenderness, and you don't deserve it, and here it is anyway.*

"Thank you," she managed.

"Don't thank me yet," Marisol said briskly, and stood, and began clearing plates with the efficiency of a woman who had cleared these particular plates in this particular sink many times. "Thank me after you've heard about the garden."

"The garden?"

Ted chuckled from the counter. It was a deep, rolling sound, the laugh of a man who had been laughing at the same jokes for decades and found them improved by repetition.

"Your mother," he said, "grew tomatoes she refused to eat."

* * *

It came out in pieces, over tea, at the kitchen table where Astrid had eaten breakfast for eighteen years and where she now sat as a guest.

Her mother had planted tomatoes the spring after Astrid left — the year the house went quiet, though Astrid hadn't known it had gone quiet; she'd been in a university dorm learning about load distribution. Tomatoes, and beans, and a row of marigolds against the fence. And every August, when the tomatoes came in red and heavy, she would pick them, fill a basket, and walk them next door.

"To us," Ted said. "Every year. Twelve years running. And every year I'd say, Ruthie, you grow them, you eat them. And every year she'd say, 'I don't care for tomatoes, Ted.'"

"But she grew them," Astrid said.

"She grew magnificent tomatoes," Marisol confirmed. "Prize-worthy. The Hendersons asked for her secret and she gave them a different false answer each time. And then she'd bring the whole harvest over that fence and stand there watching us eat them with the satisfaction of a general reviewing troops."

"Why?" Astrid asked. "Why grow them if—"

And then she stopped, because she understood it, suddenly, the way you understand a load path — not by studying it but by seeing where the weight actually went. Her mother had needed to nurture something through a full season. Needed the daily tending, the watering, the watching-for-blight, the slow accumulation of ripeness. And she could not eat the fruit of it herself, because the fruit was not the point. The giving was the point. The handing of the basket over the fence was the point.

Her mother had spent twelve years practicing a kind of love she apparently couldn't practice indoors.

"That last summer," Ted said quietly, "when she was getting muddled — she still grew them. We'd find her out there at odd hours in her nightdress, checking the leaves. Marisol would go over and help her tie the stakes, and Ruthie would talk to her like she was sixteen years old, telling stories about girls at school, about a friend of hers—" He paused. Glanced at his wife.

"A friend," Marisol repeated, in a tone that contained an entire archive.

Astrid's spine straightened. "What friend?"

"Oh, no," Marisol said, raising both hands, paprika sleeves sliding. "No, no. That's not mine to tell. That's Ruthie's story, and she kept it close, and I only ever got the edges of it. But she talked about this friend the way people talk about weather they remember from childhood. Warm. Specific. Like it had happened to her personally and no one else."

"What was her name?" Astrid asked, and heard her own voice go careful, the way it went careful around cracks.

Marisol looked at her for a long moment. Then at Ted. Some communication passed between them, thirty-nine years of marriage compressed into a second.

"She never said a name," Marisol said finally. "Just 'my friend.' But there was a letter once. I brought her mail in during a storm, years back, and there was a letter on the hall table already opened, and I shouldn't have looked, and I only saw the top of it, and the signature was just an initial. C." She shrugged, apologetic. "I forgot about it for years. It came back to me lately, is all. Old women remember strange things."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere upstairs, in the blue room, a shoebox sat with Astrid's fingerprints on it, one letter missing from its count, and Astrid felt the guilt of it arrive precisely on schedule, punctual as a scheduled inspection.

"C," she said.

"Mm," said Marisol, and drank her tea, and watched Astrid with eyes that missed nothing and judged less than they might have.

* * *

They stayed three hours. Ted fixed the gate hinge that had been squeaking since approximately the Clinton administration, announcing tolerances as he worked — "quarter inch of play, that's generous, your mother never fixed anything she could complain about instead" — and Astrid found herself laughing, actually laughing, in her mother's yard, which felt like trespassing and grace at once.

At the fence line, gathering casserole dishes and folding chairs, Marisol took Astrid's arm. Her grip was light and her hands were strong, gardener's hands, tomato-tending hands.

"I'm going to tell you something and then I'm going to leave, because it's nearly lunchtime and Ted gets difficult if unfed." She smoothed Astrid's sleeve, an absent maternal gesture that Astrid realized, with a small internal collapse, she had been starved of for twenty years. "You're going to want to inventory this house. Box it, label it, price it, get it done. That's your nature; I can see it; it was hers too. But this house isn't a structure, sweetheart. It's a record. And records have things in them that don't fit on clipboards." She patted Astrid's arm once, firmly. "Read everything. Even the parts that scare you. *Especially* those."

Then she was gone, across the grass, calling to Ted about the hinge, and the fence between number 39 and number 41 stood between them, wooden and ordinary, having borne more traffic than any bridge Astrid had ever rated.

* * *

That evening Astrid climbed the stairs to the blue room with the letter she had pocketed days ago, the one addressed to C., and stood in the doorway without turning on the light. Dusk came through the window the way it used to when she was sixteen and the world was simpler and she had ruined it with one open door.

*Especially those,* Marisol had said.

Downstairs, the front door opened and closed — Wren, back from the solicitor's, keys jangling, footsteps pausing in the hall below. Astrid heard her sister stop at the foot of the stairs, heard the pause lengthen, and knew, with the certainty of a woman who measured things for a living, that Wren had noticed the shoebox was out of place.

Footsteps began up the stairs.
