## 1. The Inventory Begins

Three days after my mother's funeral, I stood in her kitchen holding a legal pad and a pen that didn't work, and I thought: this is fine. This is something I know how to do.

I had flown in from Chicago on Tuesday, slept badly in my childhood bed beneath a quilt that smelled of cedar and dust and, faintly, of her—the way a coat still smells of a person for about a week after they die, and then betrays you by smelling only of itself. On Wednesday we buried her, or rather we stood around while men lowered a box into the ground and a pastor I'd never met said words she would have found ornamental. On Thursday I bought legal pads, archival boxes, acid-free tissue paper, and a label maker, because grief without supplies is just standing in rooms crying, and I had never been good at standing in rooms crying.

The house in Marrow Creek had been hers alone for eleven years, since my father died, and before that it had been theirs for thirty-one, and before that it had been somebody else's entirely, which is a fact I never once considered until now, standing in it as its executioner.

That's what cataloging an estate is, if you're honest. You are not preserving anything. You are assigning prices to a life so strangers can carry it out the door in pieces.

I started in the kitchen because kitchens are where mothers live, taxonomically speaking.

*Item: one stand mixer, chrome, circa 1978, bowl dented.* She made bread every Sunday of my childhood in that mixer, and I could tell you the model number off the plate, the year KitchenAid moved production details I'd read in some article I no longer remembered reading, the exact pitch of the motor's complaint when the dough got heavy. What I could not tell you was whether she liked making that bread. Whether it was devotion or duty or just the cheapest way to feed two boys who ate like woodchippers. I stood there with the pen poised over the pad and realized I had a complete technical manual of my mother's kitchen and not one sentence of her.

I wrote: *Mixer, chrome, works.* The *works* felt like an accusation.

* * *

By noon I had done the kitchen, the pantry, and the hall closet, and I had filled four pages in handwriting so neat it looked like someone else's. That's the trick of competence—it holds you together from the outside while the inside does whatever insides do when nobody's watching. I cataloged the hall closet in forty minutes flat: umbrellas (three, one broken), winter coats (hers, mine from high school, Dale's left behind like a hostage), a shoebox of birthday candles, a vacuum cleaner older than both sons combined.

Dale had called Wednesday night, already, from Phoenix. Not to grieve—to coordinate.

"So what's the number looking like," he'd said, which meant the estate, which meant the house, which meant his third of whatever the realtor said Marrow Creek property was going for these days.

"I haven't gotten that far."

"Bram. It's a house in a town with one stoplight. It's not the Louvre. Box it, price it, list it. Six weeks, the guy said."

"The guy being the realtor you hired without asking me."

"He answers his phone. That's asking." A pause, and then, softer, in the voice he used maybe twice a decade: "You okay?"

And I'd said yes, because what was the alternative, and because Dale and I had built our whole relationship out of that particular brick—*you okay? / yeah*—a wall with no door in it.

He was the older one, Dale, by four years, and he'd left at eighteen the way people leave towns that have wronged them in ways too small to name, and he'd become a man who talked about square footage the way other men talk about weather. I loved him. I want that on the record somewhere, since I'm keeping records now. I loved him the way you love a language you can no longer speak fluently—you remember it's beautiful, you just can't get through a sentence.

* * *

The living room took me all afternoon, and it was there that the eulogy problem announced itself.

Here is what happened at the funeral: I did not speak. The pastor asked if either son wished to say words, and Dale shook his head with the finality of a man declining a second drink, and I opened my mouth and found nothing in it. Not stage fright—I've read my work aloud in bookstores to audiences of nine people, most of them lost, one asleep. Nothing to say. Worse than nothing: a head full of accurate description and zero meaning. I could have stood up and delivered an inventory. *My mother owned forty-one years of National Geographic. My mother kept her tea in tins labeled in her own hand. My mother's sofa had been reupholstered once, in 1994, in a green she chose from a swatch book while my father waited in the truck.*

So instead I'd sat in the pew doing what I do, which is compose, and everything I composed was furniture.

Now, three days later, standing in the living room with the legal pad, I decided to fix it retroactively. I would write the eulogy here, among her things, and deliver it to nobody, or to the empty chairs, or possibly to the realtor, who deserved some punishment. I sat down on the green sofa—*sofa, green, reupholstered 1994*—and began.

"Margaret Voss was born—" I stopped. Was that even true? Margaret Voss. I knew her maiden name the way I knew state capitals, recited, unexamined. Margaret Ellison, from somewhere in Ohio that nobody ever visited or mentioned. Had anyone from Ohio come to the funeral? I ran the faces of the front pews through my mind and came up with neighbors, church people, the pharmacist, Odile—

No. Not yet. There was a woman at the edge of the gathering whose face I couldn't place, dark-haired, standing apart the way you stand when you're attending a stranger's funeral on purpose. But that was a problem for later, or for never. Grief gives you permission to defer everything.

I tried again. "Margaret Voss was a woman who—"

Who what. Who kept her receipts for forty years, as I would learn the next day in the study. Who watered ferns. Who wrote her grocery lists in pencil and pressed down hard enough that you could read them from the sheet beneath, ghost-lists of meals eaten decades ago. Who laughed—at what? I searched forty-three years of memory for my mother laughing and found the sound but not the cause, the way you can hum a song and be unable to name it.

Every writer knows the difference between describing a thing and knowing it. I had spent twenty years describing things for a living—two novels, both well reviewed, neither about my family, a fact everyone politely declined to notice—and I knew exactly how to make a room breathe on the page. And I stood in my mother's actual breathing rooms and discovered I had never once written her down, not in a notebook, not in a letter, not in the margins of anything. There were no drafts. There was no version of her anywhere except the official one, the one printed on the funeral program, and the program said *loving mother and devoted member of First Lutheran*, which is the obituary equivalent of *works.*

I wrote on the legal pad, under the living-room inventory, in small letters I immediately regretted:

*She was a woman I could inventory completely and describe not at all.*

Then I crossed it out. Then I un-crossed it. Then I closed the pad and went to make coffee in a kitchen I had already finished cataloging, using a percolator I had assigned a value of eight dollars.

* * *

The house has a way of talking when you move through it slowly. Fast, it's just a house—doors, floors, the furnace knocking in the walls like a tenant demanding repairs. Slowly, it testifies. Every room held evidence of habits I had lived inside without ever seeing, the way you don't see your own accent.

In the bathroom: her hairbrush with silver coming in at the temples like frost advancing across a window. In the linen closet: sheets folded in thirds, hospital corners, a discipline she maintained for a family that had dwindled to one woman and a guest room. In the dining room, the table where we'd eaten ten thousand dinners set now with nothing, its surface reflecting the window in a gray oval, and I stood there remembering that my father had always sat facing the door and my mother had always taken the chair with the wobble and I had never, in forty-three years, asked why she always took the wobbly chair. Small thing. Everything is a small thing until you try to sum it.

I worked until the light went long and orange, and by five o'clock I had filled eleven pages and sealed six boxes, and the house was beginning to look like what it was becoming: inventory. Things losing their names as objects and gaining names as assets. The mixer stopped being the Sunday bread machine and became Lot 14. I hated myself a little for the efficiency. I kept being efficient anyway, because efficiency was the only vessel I had that could hold this much water without leaking.

At dusk I carried the last box to the hallway and passed the study door, which I had deliberately left shut all day. Deliberately, and told myself I hadn't. The study had been my father's room first and then, after he died, mysteriously, hers—she'd started sitting in there evenings, Dale and I assumed, though neither of us had ever asked her why, or what she did in there, or anything at all, really. We were men who called at holidays. We were men who said *you okay?*

The door was shut and I put my hand on it anyway, the way you touch a stove you already know is hot, and then I took my hand back and went upstairs and stood in my childhood bedroom looking at the quilt.

Tomorrow morning was the interment—the actual lowering, postponed three days for reasons of cemetery scheduling that struck me, even at the time, as a joke the universe was telling at my expense. We had buried her symbolically Wednesday; Thursday we would bury her administratively. I would stand at the grave again with the same small crowd, and the same pastor, and I still had no eulogy, and I had begun to suspect I wasn't going to get one, that the words weren't hiding somewhere waiting to be found like the good scissors, that maybe there had never been words between us and the silence was the true family heirloom, passed down intact.

I fell asleep in my old bed under the cedar-smelling quilt, and the last thing I thought, in the borderland before sleep, was a sentence I would have rejected from any character as too neat:

*I know everything she owned and nothing she was.*

But sleep doesn't check your prose for clichés. Sleep just takes you.

* * *

Friday morning I woke early, drove to the cemetery, watched the box go down into the ground a second time, and shook hands with people whose names I mostly knew. The dark-haired woman from Wednesday was there again, standing at the perimeter by the fence, not pretending to pray, watching—not the casket, me. When the pastor finished and the small crowd broke apart like a fist opening, I turned to look for her properly, and she was already walking away between the stones, unhurried, a woman leaving a place she had finished with.

I might have let it go. I was practiced at letting things go; it was practically the family trade.

But as she passed the gate she said something—not to me, not loudly, almost to the air, the way you'd speak to a grave—and the wind carried it across the grass, and it was a name. Our name. Except it wasn't. It was a surname I had never heard attached to my family in my life, spoken in the tone people use for facts, and she said it the way you'd say *Voss*, easily, like it belonged to us, like it had always belonged to us, like the name on the stone behind me was the borrowed thing.

She went through the gate and was gone, and I stood there in the cold with a legal pad under my arm full of accurate descriptions, feeling the ground tilt—not the ground, the thing I'd stood on all my life—and understanding that the inventory had just gotten much, much longer.
