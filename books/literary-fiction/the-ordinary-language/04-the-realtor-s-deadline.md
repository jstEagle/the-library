## 4. The Realtor's Deadline

The realtor's name was Petra Lund, and she arrived on Tuesday morning in a white sedan that looked like it had been washed specifically to shame my mother's gutters. She was maybe forty-five, wore a blazer the color of oatmeal, and carried a tablet in a leather case as if it were a small animal she'd trained not to bite.

"Mr. Voss," she said, shaking my hand with a grip calibrated to convey both warmth and efficiency. "Thank you for making time. I know this isn't easy."

It wasn't, but I hadn't expected her to know that, and I resented slightly how easily she said it, the way you'd say *I know traffic is bad* or *I know Tuesdays are rough*. Grief as a scheduling inconvenience. Still, she was the first person in three days to stand in my mother's kitchen and look at it like a place where something had actually happened, rather than a set of surfaces awaiting appraisal, so I forgave her.

"Six weeks," she said, before she'd even taken off her sunglasses. "Closing is the fourteenth of next month. That gives us—let me see—" she tapped the tablet, though we both knew she'd already seen— "forty-one days to get the house show-ready."

"Show-ready," I repeated.

"Cleared out, deep-cleaned, minor repairs. The roof flashing needs attention—I noticed on the drive up—and there's some water staining in the upstairs bath we'll want to disclose but also mitigate." She said *mitigate* the way other people say *bless you*, automatically, kindly, without expecting anything to change. "The good news is the bones are excellent. This is a solid house. Somebody's going to love these bones."

I looked at the bones in question: the hairline crack running up from the doorframe of the pantry, which had been there my entire life; the wallpaper in the hallway my mother had threatened to replace since 1987; the window over the sink that stuck every June and was freed every June by the same butter knife, which lived in the same drawer for that purpose alone and would now be cataloged as *kitchen utensil, one (1), butter*.

"Somebody's going to love them," I agreed, because what else does one say.

Petra walked the house with the brisk tenderness of a hospice nurse. She opened closets and closed them. She noted the water heater's age with a small sympathetic click of her tongue, as though the water heater were an elderly relative who'd had a good run. In the study—the room I had not yet begun, the room I had been circling for three days the way you circle a dog you suspect is sick—she paused only long enough to say, "This'll be the big one," and moved on. She didn't know how right she was. I didn't either, not yet, not fully. But I felt the study behind us like weather.

At the front door she turned back. "Forty-one days, Mr. Voss. I've seen families try to do this in two weekends and I've seen it take a year. The ones who rush end up throwing away things they regret, and the ones who linger—" she smiled, professional, kind— "well. Lingering isn't really an option here. Is it?"

"No," I said. "It isn't."

She left a folder on the hall table—a timeline, a checklist, a recommended hauler, a recommended shredding service. A shredding service. There was a whole industry for this, apparently. Whole fleets of men whose job was to make paper disappear so houses could become money. I thought about my mother's handwriting on envelopes I hadn't opened yet, and put the folder face-down.

* * *

Dale called at 9:40 that night, which meant it was 6:40 his time, which meant he'd finished dinner and was calling from the den, television muted, because Dale never called during anything he might miss.

"How's it going over there?" he asked, which was not a question about grief. With Dale, *how's it going* meant *how much is left*.

"Slowly," I said. "There's a lot."

"There's always a lot. That's why they invented dumpsters." I could hear him shifting, the creak of his recliner, a lifetime of evenings encoded in that sound. "Petra sent me the packet. Numbers look decent, considering. Considering the market, I mean. Considering everything."

Considering Mother, he did not say. Neither of us had managed to say it yet, in any call, in any form. We had spoken eleven times since the funeral and the word *she* had appeared perhaps twice, always in the passive construction, always about logistics: *she wanted the service at St. Brigid's, she had the plot paid off since '09*. My mother had become a set of prior arrangements. It was efficient. It was unbearable.

"The estate account covers the closing costs," Dale went on, "but I'd rather not dip into it more than we have to. So whatever you can move yourself—furniture, that kind of thing—saves us the hauling fee. And Bram, seriously, don't pay anyone to appraise the ordinary stuff. It's ordinary stuff. Take pictures, list it cheap, let it go."

"It's not all ordinary stuff."

A pause. On Dale's end, something clinked. Ice, probably. Our father's bourbon habit had skipped him genetically and landed instead as a modest nightly rye, which Dale considered a personality.

"What's not ordinary?" he asked, and there was something new in his voice—not suspicion exactly, but the accountant's instinct that somewhere a column wasn't balancing.

"I don't know yet. Papers. Letters. Things in the attic."

"Bram." He said my name the way he had when we were kids and I'd broken something of his—not angry yet, but pricing the anger. "Mom kept receipts from 1974. Dad kept warranties for appliances we donated in the eighties. That's not a mystery, that's just—people. They keep things because throwing things away feels like dying a little. You know this. Don't make it weird."

"I'm not making it weird."

"You're making it weird. You've got six weeks to empty a house and you're talking about letters like they're evidence." He laughed, short and dry. "Look. I loved her too. Okay? I did. But she's gone, and the house is going, and the sooner we're through it, the sooner we're—"

"Through it," I said. "Right."

Another pause, longer. For one strange moment I thought he might say something true—that he missed her, that he'd cried in his car after the graveside like I had, that the silence between us on the phone was the same silence we'd grown up inside and neither of us knew how to live without it. But then he cleared his throat and said, "Just keep the receipts for anything you spend. And Bram? Don't sign anything Petra sends without reading it. Realtors are fine but they work for the commission, not for us."

He hung up—or rather, the call ended in the way Dale ended calls, mid-breath, as if the conversation were a shift at a factory and his part of it was done. I sat in the dark kitchen holding the phone and understood, with the mild clarity that comes at night, that my brother and I were grieving the same woman in two different currencies, and that neither of ours was convertible into the other's.

* * *

Wednesday I made a list. It seemed like the responsible thing, the thing Petra would approve of, the thing that would convert the house back into tasks. I sat at the kitchen table with legal pads—my mother's legal pads, actually, half a dozen of them in the drawer beneath the phone, because she wrote everything down: grocery lists, mileage logs, the dates the lilacs bloomed going back fifteen years—and I divided the house into zones.

Zone One: kitchen and dining room. Low risk. Dishes, pans, the good china nobody had used since Aunt Renata's funeral lunch. Two days, maybe three.

Zone Two: bedrooms. Mine still had a poster of a sailboat I had never sailed under; Dale's room had been a shrine to order until he left at eighteen and it became a shrine to nothing, door shut for thirty years. Five days.

Zone Three: living room, hall, bath. Furniture, photographs, the piano no one played. Four days.

Zone Four: the attic. Receipts, boxes, the deed with names crossed out, the photographs of the woman who was not my mother. I wrote *attic* and then sat looking at the word for a long time, the way you look at a word you've written correctly but that has suddenly stopped meaning what it means.

Zone Five: the study.

I didn't write an estimate beside Zone Five. Some part of me already knew the study wouldn't obey the schedule, that it was the room where the house kept its actual contents, the ones that didn't weigh anything. In three days I had gone in twice, stood in the doorway once, and removed exactly one item: a single letter, addressed in my mother's hand to *E. Ferrant*, which currently lived in the inside pocket of the jacket hanging by the back door, where I could feel its weight every time I passed, the way you feel a splinter.

Forty-one days. Minus today, minus yesterday's false start, minus the day of the funeral itself, which counted for nothing. Say thirty-eight. Thirty-eight days to turn a life into inventory, and I had budgeted the study zero hours because I couldn't bring myself to write a number next to it.

I got up and made coffee I didn't want, because my hands needed a task that wasn't thinking.

* * *

Thursday, Petra called at eight a.m., cheerful as a fire alarm.

"Good news," she said. "I have a couple interested in a preview. Young couple, relocating from Duluth, they saw the listing photos go up on the MLS draft and they're champing at the bit. Would Saturday work for a walkthrough?"

"The house is full of everything we own," I said. "Every closet. Every drawer. There are boxes in the bathtub."

"That's fine, that's totally normal, buyers expect that. Honestly, a little lived-in clutter reads as *cared-for*." A pause, and then, gentler, the blazer coming off for just a second: "Bram. Can I call you Bram? Here's the thing. Houses like this—well-priced, well-located—they move fast in spring. If this couple bites, we could talk offer by Monday. Which means your forty-one days could effectively become twenty. I don't want to pressure you. But I'd hate for you to lose a good buyer because the porch furniture wasn't sorted."

I stood in the hallway looking at the wallpaper my mother had hated for thirty-seven years and thought: she never replaced it. Not because she couldn't afford to. Because replacing it would have meant deciding, and deciding meant choosing what the house would become, and I think my mother spent the last decades of her life refusing to decide almost anything, keeping the house in a state of permanent deferral, the wallpaper a monument to postponement. And now here was a woman from Duluth who wanted to walk through the postponement on Saturday and imagine her own life laid over it like fresh paper.

"Saturday works," I heard myself say.

"Perfect. Ten o'clock. Just—paths clear, lights on, and if you could have the personal papers boxed up and out of sight, that helps enormously. Buyers open their imaginations easier when they can't see anybody else's paperwork."

Out of sight. Box up the papers. I thanked her and hung up and stood there while the house ticked around me—the refrigerator's hum, the settling of old joists, the particular silence of a place where someone used to move through rooms and doesn't anymore.

Then I went to the back door, took the letter out of my jacket pocket, and held it. *E. Ferrant.* My mother's slanted, unhurried hand. The flap unsealed, the letter inside folded in thirds, forty years old and light as a moth.

Thirty-eight days. Maybe twenty. Maybe less. The countdown had a countdown now, grief stacked inside commerce like nesting dolls, and everyone—Petra, Dale, the young couple from Duluth, the whole apparatus of sale and clearing and closing—was counting down with me, patiently, professionally, toward the moment when the house stopped being hers and became a transaction.

And underneath all of it, ticking at its own private speed, was the question the letter represented, the one no checklist had a line item for: *who was E. Ferrant, and why did my mother write to her, and why did she keep the reply she never opened?*

I put the letter back in the pocket. Then I climbed the stairs to the attic, pulled the cord, and stood in the cone of yellow light with the dust turning slowly above me, and began—out of order, against every zone on every legal pad—to count what couldn't be sold.
