## 8. The Letter He Shouldn't Have

I want to tell you I agonized over it. I want to tell you I sat with the letter on my knee for a long evening, weighing the ethics of opening mail that was never mine, that was in fact addressed to a dead woman whose family my family had, apparently, done something to in a winter I'd never heard named. I want to report a crisis of conscience, a long dark night of the soul, some worthy internal struggle that would make me look better in the retelling.

What actually happened was this: I made a sandwich.

I was standing in my mother's kitchen on the ninth day, and the letter was in my jacket pocket where it had lived since the study, and I was hungry in the dumb animal way grief makes you hungry, the body insisting on its own agenda while the rest of you stands around at a funeral. I got out the bread. I got out the mustard. And somewhere between the refrigerator and the counter my hand went into my pocket and came out with the envelope, the way your hand comes out with your phone before you've decided anything, and I was standing there with a letter in one hand and a butter knife in the other, and the butter knife was losing.

The envelope was soft with handling. That was the thing I noticed first, before the address, before anything. It had been opened and closed and opened again, many times, the flap's gum long dead, the paper worn thin at the corners like a river stone. My mother had read this letter. My mother had read this letter dozens of times, and then she had put it in a drawer under a bundle of unopened replies, which meant she had kept the conversation going in only one direction for years, a woman writing letters into a silence she had maybe earned, and reading them over and over like a person rehearsing an apology she never got to deliver.

Or refused to deliver. I didn't know yet which. I still don't, entirely.

The address on the front said *E. Ferrant, Marrow Creek* — no street, no number, as if in this town a name was an address, as if the mailman would simply know. The postmark was faded to a ghost. I held it over the sink so the crumbs of thirty years wouldn't fall on the counter, which is a stupid detail, but grief is made of stupid details, it's mostly stupid details with one unbearable thing hidden in the middle of them.

I opened it.

*Dear Edith,*

*I have started this letter eleven times. I know because I found the others and burned them, and then I found I could not stop starting it, so here is the twelfth and I am sending it because Dale is asleep and Bram is at school and if I wait for a brave day it will never come, because I am not a brave woman, I am a tidy one, and tidiness is not the same thing.*

That was the first paragraph, and I had to put the letter down on the counter next to the mustard and stand there with my hands flat on the laminate, breathing, because it was her voice. Not the phone voice, not the eulogy voice, not the brisk cataloging voice she'd used on the phone when I called her every other Sunday for twenty years and we talked about weather and Dale's business and whether her hip was bothering her. This was a voice underneath all of those, a voice I had never been given clearance to hear, and it was unmistakably, devastatingly her. Wry. Precise. A woman who counted things because counting was how she kept from drowning.

Eleven drafts, burned. My mother, who saved every rubber band and every twist tie and every birthday card from 1987, had burned eleven drafts of a letter and kept the twelfth, and kept the reply she received to it unopened in a drawer for — I did the arithmetic on the postmark, and my hands went unsteady — for thirty-one years.

*I don't expect you to read it. I expect you to burn it, and you'd be right to. But I have to say it once, in ink, because saying it in my head for thirty years has not worked, the words will not stay said, they slide back into the silence every night like a drawer that won't close.*

*It was the winter of the name change. You know which winter. You have always known, and I have always known that you know, and we have built forty years on that mutual knowledge the way people build a house on a fault line, and the house stands, but you feel it when you lie down at night.*

*Your father came to us in February with nothing but the clothes that hadn't burned and your mother's cough and you in your arms, and my husband said yes, we can shelter you, and I believed him when he said it the way you believe a man who is quoting scripture, and then —*

And then there was a blot. An ink blot, or a tear that had dried and warped the paper, a small brown galaxy right in the middle of the page, and the sentence after it resumed in a hand that had gone smaller, tighter, a hand that was holding itself.

*— and then your father signed papers he should never have been given to sign, and my husband held the pen, and I watched him hold the pen, and I said nothing. That is my crime. Not the pen. The silence after the pen. Your father could not read the English in those documents, and we both knew it, and I stood in the kitchen with the kettle going and said nothing, and the kettle has been going in my head ever since, thirty-one years, and it has never once boiled.*

I read that sentence four times. *Your father could not read the English in those documents.* I read it a fifth time and the kitchen tilted slightly, the way a room does when you stand up too fast, except I was sitting, I had sat down at my mother's kitchen table without noticing, the sandwich forgotten, the letter spread out in front of me like a map of a country I'd apparently been living in my whole life without knowing its name.

My father. Gerald Voss. The man who corrected my grammar. The man who read the newspaper with a ruler under each line and believed, with his whole enormous certainty, in the sanctity of documents, in signatures, in the clean authority of paper. The man who once drove forty minutes back to a gas station because they'd charged him for a car wash he hadn't used. That man had held a pen over documents a grieving man couldn't read, and had held the silence after, for forty years, and had raised two sons on top of the silence the way you raise children on a floor you know is rotten and never tell them not to dance.

*People will tell you it was legal,* she wrote. *Gerald tells himself it was legal every night, and I think he has almost made himself believe it, which is the cruelest thing a man can do to himself, to succeed at that. The land was yours. The wages from the mill were yours, the ones they docked for the fire, the fire you did not set. I know you did not set it. I knew it then. I stood in this kitchen and I knew it and I said nothing, and Edith, I have been saying nothing for so long that I have forgotten how the other thing is done, and I am asking you, in this letter, to teach me, though I have no right to ask, though teaching me would cost you more than I have ever been worth.*

*I am not asking forgiveness. I want to be clear about that, because I have read enough novels to know that's what the letter is supposed to ask for, and I refuse. Forgiveness is yours to give and I will not angle for it like a beggar at a church door. What I am asking for is smaller and harder. I am asking you to let me say it. Once. In ink. To let the words exist outside my head, where they have been rattling around like a marble in a jar for thirty-one years, and where they have kept me company in a way I am ashamed of, because the marble is the only honest thing in the house and I have grown fond of it.*

*I enclose nothing. I promise nothing except this: if you write back, I will read it. If you burn this letter, I will know, and I will deserve it, and the marble will rattle on.*

*Yours in the only way I know how, which is badly,*
*M. Voss*

*M. Voss.* Not her first name. Even in the letter, even in the confession, even in the twelfth draft of the most honest thing she ever wrote, she had signed it with an initial and a stolen surname. I sat at the table and laughed, once, a short ugly sound with nothing in it, the kind of laugh that's just grief changing costumes.

I looked at the sandwich. I looked at the letter. The letter had won by a landslide.

Here is what I want to explain, and I don't fully understand it myself, so bear with me. You'd think that reading your dead mother confess to a moral crime would close a door. That the shame would be a wall, that you'd put the letter down and call the realtor and let the silence win the way it had always won, the way it was designed to win, generation after generation, silence as an inheritance passed down with the good silver.

But it wasn't a wall. It was a hunger.

Because in that letter my mother was more alive than she had been in any of my thirty-four years of knowing her. The woman on the phone talking about her hip was a photograph. The woman in this letter was a person — furious at herself, funny in the teeth of it, counting her drafts, naming her kettle. I had spent nine days cataloging her house and I had not found her in a single room. She'd been in the drawer the whole time. She'd been in the twelfth draft.

And the terrible arithmetic of it, the thing I kept turning over like a stone: she had written to Edith Ferrant. Odile Ferrant. The woman at the graveside who had said our name like it was a costume we were wearing. Which meant Odile was — what? Edith's daughter? Granddaughter? The letter was thirty-one years old, and the bundle of unopened replies beneath it — I went back to the study that afternoon and looked at them properly this time, with the letter's light on them, and the postmarks spanned decades, the most recent only four years old. Four years. My mother had written to Edith Ferrant four years ago. Edith Ferrant had replied four years ago. And my mother had put the reply in the drawer unopened and kept writing, and kept not reading, and I understood that too, suddenly, horribly: she couldn't read the answers. She could only afford the confession. The replies might contain forgiveness, which she'd refused to beg for, or they might contain the other thing, and either way she'd have to *do* something about it, and she was a tidy woman, and tidiness is not the same thing as courage. She'd said so herself.

So she'd written into the silence for thirty years and filed the silence in a drawer, alphabetized, and called it a life.

I stood in the study holding the bundle, and the hunger got worse. Because there were answers in my hand. Thirty years of answers, from a woman who had every right to hate us, in an envelope addressed to my mother, and my mother had never read them, and my mother was dead, and the mail had, in the crudest possible sense, been forwarded to me.

I put the bundle down. I actually did. I put it back in the drawer and closed the drawer and stood there in the study with my hands in my pockets feeling like a man who has found a load-bearing wall and is trying to pretend it's decorative.

Then I took the bundle back out.

Here is my defense, and it is a bad one, and I'm giving it anyway. She's dead. Edith Ferrant is dead — Odile had said it at the graveside, or implied it, or I had inferred it from the way she'd stood, I honestly couldn't reconstruct it anymore. The letters' owners were both gone. The letters belonged to no one, which meant they belonged to the truth, which meant — and this is where you can hear the hunger talking, the hunger always sounds like logic, that's its whole trick — which meant that reading them wasn't theft. It was probate. It was inventory. I was cataloging the house, after all. Room by room. Object by object. And a letter is an object. I had a system. I was being thorough.

My mother would have seen through that in about four seconds. She'd have looked at me over her reading glasses and said, *Bram, you always did dress up the thing you wanted in the clothes of the thing you should do,* and she'd have been right, and I'd have hated it, and that's the other inheritance, I'm realizing: not the silence, not the name. The fluency. The family talent for saying the true thing in a shape that lets you keep it.

I carried the bundle to the kitchen. The light was going, that late gray light that makes every window in an old house look like it's remembering something. I set the bundle on the table next to the mustard and the unfinished sandwich, and I picked the oldest reply off the top — the first answer, the one that came after the twelfth draft, the beginning of the thirty-year conversation my mother had with her own drawer.

The envelope was heavier than the others. Something inside it besides paper.

I turned it over. I turned it back. My hands were doing that thing hands do when the rest of you has already decided, trembling slightly, performing reluctance for an audience of nobody.

Through the paper I could feel the shape of whatever was enclosed. Flat. Small. Rectangular, with a corner that caught against the envelope's lining when I tilted it.

A photograph, I thought. Or a key.

I stood in my mother's kitchen, in the house with six weeks left on it, holding thirty years of mail that was never mine, addressed to a woman who had never read it, written by a family whose name we were apparently wearing like a borrowed coat, and I want to tell you I hesitated.

The flap came open in my hand like it had been waiting.

* * *

The oldest envelope held a photograph and a single sheet, and I'll tell you about the sheet in a minute, but first the photograph, because the photograph is the reason my hands stopped shaking and went cold instead.

It was a picture of two young women on the steps of this house. My house. The porch I'd sat on all through childhood, the railing my father repainted every other summer, the steps worn into shallow smiles by a century of feet. The two women sat close together on the middle step, shoulders touching, squinting into a sun that had set forty years ago, and one of them was my mother — younger than I had ever seen her, maybe twenty-five, hair down, laughing at something just outside the frame — and the other was a woman I had never seen, dark-haired, sharp-faced, holding a baby up toward the camera with the fierce pride of someone presenting evidence.

On the back, in a hand I now recognized as my mother's, was written: *M. and E., the good year. O. in arms.*

O. in arms. Odile. The baby on the porch steps was the woman who had stood at my mother's grave and said a name that wasn't ours, and forty years ago she had been held up toward a camera on these steps by two women who were, in that photograph, in that light, in that word on the back — *the good year* — friends.

Friends. Before the pen. Before the winter of the name change. Before the kettle that never boiled.

I sat down on the floor of the kitchen, actually sat down, my back against the cabinet under the phone my mother had never replaced with a cell because she said the cord kept her honest about how far she could wander while talking. The sheet of paper was Edith's first reply. It was short. It was not what my mother feared and not what she hoped, and I read it standing up because sitting felt like too much surrender, and this is what it said:

*M.,*

*You are right that I will not forgive you, and right that you did not ask, and I notice you managed to ask without asking, which was always your gift. Keep the marble. Rattle it. I have mine.*

*I did not burn your letter. I read it four times and then I put it in the box with the other things I am keeping for Odile, who is twenty-two now and asks questions I cannot answer and deserves answers I do not have the right to give alone. So here is my counteroffer, since you like documents so much, your family: I will not forgive you, but I will correspond with you. We will tell each other the truth in ink, and we will not ask each other for anything else, and when we are both dead our children will find the box, and it will be their turn to decide what the words are worth. That is not forgiveness. It is a ledger. I am told your husband respects ledgers.*

*Enclosed is the photograph. I want you to have it because it is the only proof that the good year happened, and I find I need someone else to hold the proof or I will start believing the bad years were the only ones.*

*E.*

*A ledger.* Thirty years of letters, and my mother had never once opened a single reply, because to open the ledger was to be entered in it, and my mother — tidy, careful, fluent in the shape of things — could confess forever as long as no one ever answered.

But Edith had designed it so someone would answer. *Our children will find the box.* She'd known. Thirty-one years ago, she had sat down and written a letter that was not to her friend but to her friend's son, a letter with a delayed fuse, and she had trusted that the silence in our family was not bottomless, that eventually someone would open the drawer, that the mail would get delivered even if it took a generation and a graveside stranger to deliver it.

Odile Ferrant, standing at the graveside, saying our other name. She hadn't come to haunt us. She'd come to collect.

I looked at the photograph again, at my mother laughing on the steps, at the baby held up like evidence, and I understood that I was holding the first page of something, not the last, and that the bundle in my hand — thirty years of a ledger neither of the writers had balanced — was addressed, in every way that mattered, to me.

Outside, the light had gone all the way. The kitchen window had become a mirror, and I saw myself sitting on the floor of my mother's house holding her secret, and behind my reflection the dark rooms went on and on, all of them full, all of them waiting to be cataloged.

The bundle had twenty-two unopened letters left.

I got up and put the kettle on.
