## 1. The Inventory

Every Sunday evening, I sit at my kitchen table with a notebook and lose words on purpose.

Not on purpose, exactly. That's the lie I tell myself so the ritual feels like work instead of mourning. What I actually do is this: I open the notebook to a fresh page, write *August Crane — Sunday* at the top like a schoolgirl dating an exam, and then I try to remember my grandmother's kitchen. Not the room — the language of it. The word for the chipped blue bowl she kept lemons in. The word for the sound the screen door made when it caught the wind and slapped shut. The word for the particular gray of the sea in October, which was not the same as the gray of the sea in November, and for which there existed — I am nearly certain — a single, specific word.

Most Sundays, the page stays mostly blank.

Tonight the notebook is open to its forty-third page, and I have written down three words, and two of them I'm not sure about anymore. There's a stage in losing a language where you can no longer trust your own memory of it, where every recovered word arrives with a question mark attached, like a refugee with dubious papers. Is that really the word for *gull*, or is it the word for *heron* that I've dressed up as a gull because I needed it to be something? My dictionary — the one real dictionary, the heavy one with the cracked spine that came over in a suitcase in 1951 and has outlived everyone who packed it — sits across the table from me like a judge. I avoid its eye.

The third word I wrote tonight is *kamin*. Stove. That one's solid. That one I'd bet money on, or at least a modest dinner. My grandmother stood at her kamin every morning of her life and made coffee so thick you could stand a spoon in it, and she called me by a name that wasn't August, a diminutive, a small soft thing, and I cannot — this is tonight's discovery, logged and dated — I cannot remember what it was.

I knew it six months ago. I know I knew it because I wrote it on page thirty-one, next to the date, and then underlined it twice, the way you underline things you're afraid of losing. But when I went back to check just now, the ink had faded into a smudge of itself, or my memory of reading it had already overwritten whatever the actual letters were, and all I could see was the shape of a name without the name inside it. A footprint where the bird used to be.

This is how it goes. Nobody warns you that forgetting a language isn't like forgetting facts. It's more like watching a coastline erode. You don't notice any given morning's loss; you notice only that the beach is narrower than it used to be, and that some mornings the water reaches places it never reached before, and that you've started standing farther back from the edge without deciding to.

People ask me about it sometimes — people at dinner parties, when they learn what I do for a living. A translator! How romantic. And which languages? And I say the true thing, which is: the ones I learned on purpose are fine. It's the one I was born into that's going. Languages you choose stay because you keep choosing them, daily, deliberately, the way you stay married. The language you're given doesn't ask to be chosen. It assumes it lives in you rent-free, and then one day you come home and find it has moved out and left no forwarding address.

My childhood language has no forwarding address. It has a suitcase somewhere, maybe. It has a cracked-spine dictionary and a dead woman's handwriting and a coastal town I haven't visited in eleven years, and it has me, sitting at a table in a city apartment with a notebook full of question marks, conducting an inventory of everything I'm losing in the order I lose it.

There's a professional term for what I do with the notebook, or there would be if anyone else did it. Linguists call the phenomenon attrition. First-language attrition: the erosion of the mother tongue through disuse. They study it in emigrants, in adoptees, in children moved too young. They measure it in controlled studies with flashcards and fMRI machines. What they don't tell you, what no study captures, is the texture of it — that losing a word feels less like misplacing an object and more like a small death in the family that nobody else has heard about yet, so you have to carry the news around alone, grieving someone no one else knows existed.

*Grietas.* No — wrong language entirely. See? Even my griefs get their wires crossed now.

The phone rings at 9:47, which is late enough that I know before answering that it will be bad news. Good news keeps business hours. Bad news calls after nine and uses your full name.

"August." My mother's voice, pitched in the key she reserves for occasions — funerals, weddings, phone calls from lawyers. "Your grandmother passed this afternoon."

"I know," I say, and then hear how that sounds. "I mean — I don't know how I know. I just did."

A pause on her end. In the background, a television murmurs, which means my father is home and has not taken the phone, which means this call is being performed for an audience of one, me, and that the performance has been rehearsed.

"The funeral is Saturday," she says. "In the town. Obviously." As though I might have assumed we'd hold it here, in the city, among strangers. "You'll come."

It isn't a question. My mother does not ask questions about logistics; logistics are settled, and your attendance is a fact you discover about yourself, the way you discover weather.

"Yes," I say. "Of course."

"She'd have wanted—" my mother begins, and stops. This is new. My mother never stops mid-sentence; sentences are load-bearing structures in her architecture and she knows it. "She'd have wanted the family there. All of us."

All of us. The phrase lands strangely, and I note the strangeness the way I note everything these days, cataloging it, filing it. All of us means my parents, my brother Daniel if he can be extracted from his life in Zurich, great-aunt Vera if Vera is still — well. Vera is a separate category of existence and always has been. All of us does not include, apparently, anyone outside the immediate perimeter, though my grandmother's town was once full of people who claimed kinship of one kind or another. Eleven years away and I couldn't name half of them anymore. Couldn't name them in either language.

"There will be arrangements to discuss," my mother adds, and something in her tone — a hairline crack under the lacquer — makes me think *arrangements* means more than flowers and hymns. "Saturday, August. Don't be late."

She hangs up before I can say anything else, which is also traditional. In my family, conversations end the way chess games end between players who both saw the checkmate coming four moves ago: abruptly, and with dignity preserved.

I sit with the phone in my hand for a while. Outside my window the city does its evening arithmetic — buses sighing, a siren stitching north to south, someone below laughing at something I'll never hear properly. None of it touches me. None of it ever quite touches me, and I've built a whole life on that fact, a career even. Translation is the perfect profession for a person who lives one glass pane removed from every room she stands in. You are present at everything and party to nothing. You carry other people's meanings across borders and hand them over intact, and no one asks what you yourself meant, because the answer would require a language neither of you speaks.

My grandmother spoke it, though. Whatever it was — the untranslatable residue, the thing underneath the words — she spoke it fluently, and she tried to teach it to me every summer of my childhood, and I was a bored child of the city with a Walkman and better things to do, and I let it slide off me like rain off wax. She never scolded me for it. That's the part that hurts now, writing it down. She never once said *you'll be sorry*. She just kept talking to me in her language, patiently, year after year, as if the words were seeds she was confident would take eventually, and I let her believe it, and maybe she did believe it, right up until the summer I turned nineteen and didn't come back.

Page forty-three. I look down at it. Three words, two doubtful. Forty-three Sundays of this. At the current rate of loss, I calculate — and I do calculate, because arithmetic is the last comfort of the frightened — I have perhaps four years before the notebook fills with nothing but dates and my own name, and then five years after that before I forget why I started it.

Unless I go back. Unless I go back to the place where the words live, where they're still in the air, in the salt, in the mouths of old women on benches. Language attrition reverses with immersion; the literature is clear on this. Go back to the coast, the papers say, and the tide comes in again.

I have spent eleven years not going back.

*

I pack on Friday night, which is unlike me — I am a Saturday-morning packer, a person who believes luggage should be assembled calmly, with lists — but sleep isn't happening anyway, so I stand in my bedroom pulling sweaters off shelves and listening to the city hum its one long note. I pack black clothes, obviously. I pack the dictionary. I don't decide to pack the dictionary; my hands simply put it in, the way your hands put a photograph in, and I let them, because arguing with your own hands about grief is a losing game.

At the bottom of the suitcase, folded around tissue paper I never remove, is a dress I bought eleven years ago for a visit I canceled. It still smells faintly of this apartment, of cedar and dust, of the self I was when I believed I'd go back the following summer, and the following one, and the following one. There is always a following summer. That's the beautiful lie of exile, even voluntary exile, even exile that consists merely of a four-hour train ride you decline to take: there is always a following summer, until there isn't, until the phone rings at 9:47 and a voice trained for occasions tells you the account is closed.

On the train Saturday — no, I'm getting ahead of myself. On Friday night, after the packing, I make one last attempt at the ritual, off-schedule, unsanctioned. I sit on the edge of my bed with the notebook and try to summon my grandmother's voice saying goodbye. Because that's what tomorrow is, technically, and it seems important to arrive having practiced.

Her goodbyes were long productions. She had a farewell for leaving the house, a different one for leaving the town, and a third, gravest kind, for leaving her — a whole liturgy of parting words, each with its own intonation, its own little rise and fall, and I stood through hundreds of them as a child, kissing air near her cheek, absorbing none of it, understanding all of it in the way children understand, which is completely and wordlessly.

Say it, I tell myself now. Just one of them. Any of them.

Nothing comes. The page stays empty. Somewhere in my skull there is a locked room with her voice behind the door, and I have lost not the key but the belief that the door exists.

And then — I want to report this accurately, because accuracy is the only inheritance I have left — and then, just as I close the notebook, one word surfaces. Unbidden. From nowhere, or from everywhere, from the salt water of the place itself reaching four hundred kilometers inland to tap me on the shoulder:

*Doma.*

It means — it meant — *tomorrow*. Or possibly *hope*. Or possibly both at once, because in that language, in that drowned country of a language, those may have been the same word all along.

I write it down. I underline it twice.

In the morning I will board a train to a town whose name I can still say, barely, in a language I am losing, to bury the woman who planted all of it in me and never once said I'd be sorry. I will stand in a church among relatives who feel like acquaintances and acquaintances who feel like strangers, and I will perform a grief I genuinely feel but cannot locate, and I will not yet know — how could I know — that the ground under all of it is hollow, that beneath the polite machinery of my family's sorrow there runs a fault line with a boy's name on it, and that a stranger is already in town, waiting at the wake, holding a postcard with my grandmother's handwriting on it and a word I haven't seen in twenty years.

But that's tomorrow. Tonight there is only the notebook, and the lamp, and the word *doma* drying slowly on the page like a footprint at the tide line — proof that something walked here once, and might walk here again, if the water cooperates.

I turn off the lamp. The city hums. Somewhere east, past the dark hills, the sea is doing what the sea does, keeping everything, telling nothing.
