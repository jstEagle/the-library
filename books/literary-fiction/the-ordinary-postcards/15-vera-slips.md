## 15. Vera Slips

The trouble with a memory is that it doesn't leave all at once, like a guest who thanks you at the door. It leaves the way the tide goes out — in small thefts you don't notice until the boat is sitting on its keel in the mud.

I noticed on a Tuesday. Or possibly a Thursday. The days have begun to arrive out of order, like postcards from a careless friend. I was sitting in my chair by the window — I am always sitting in my chair by the window; it is the one position from which I can see both the road and the sea, and I have never trusted either of them to behave unobserved — and I reached for the word for the thing you put under a teacup. A saucer. No. Not *saucer*, that's the other language, the heavy one, the one that came later and sat down on everything like wet wool. I wanted the old word, the small silver one, and it wasn't there. There was a hole where it had been, shaped exactly like itself.

I said *saucer* to the empty room and felt like a traitor.

Then August arrived, and for a while the holes didn't matter, because she brought words with her the way some people bring weather.

* * *

She had been coming every day that week. I want to say she came because of the box, but the truth is I don't entirely know why young people do anything anymore, including the things they think are their own idea. She would sit on the sofa with her legs folded under her like a girl at a séance, and she would read me what she'd translated the night before, and I would correct her — not because she was wrong, exactly, but because translation is like cooking: everyone follows the same recipe and no two dishes come out the same, and mine is better.

That morning she read me the card from 1981, the one with the gulls. Tomas wrote about the gulls. He wrote about them the way only someone who has crossed water writes about birds — as if they were colleagues, as if they might carry messages if properly bribed.

"You've made him too polite," I told her.

"He *was* polite."

"He was polite the way a cat is polite. He was watching you the whole time to see what you'd do next." I heard myself laugh, a sound like a drawer sticking. "He stole my hairbrush once. Did I tell you? He hid it in the bread tin because he knew Mother checked the bread tin and nowhere else. He wanted to be found. That boy committed crimes the way other people write letters."

August looked up. "You've never told me that."

"I'm telling you now." And then, because the tide was going out again, I added, "Am I? Have I?"

Her face did something careful. "You have now," she said, which was a kind answer, the kind people give when the honest answer is *no, and also you told me this yesterday, twice.*

I decided to accept it. A woman my age learns to take yes for an answer even when it isn't one.

* * *

It happened in the afternoon. I know because the light had gone the color of weak tea, and August had gotten up to close the curtains against it, and when she turned back around I was somewhere else entirely.

I want to be clear: I did not choose to go there. People talk about memory like it's a room you can walk into and out of. It isn't. It's a room you wake up inside, already mid-conversation, with no door marked EXIT.

"Did you eat?" I asked her. "There's fish. Your mother made fish before she left for the mainland, and she'll be angry if it goes to waste, and you're too thin, Tomas, you're all bones and elbows, sit down."

August stood very still by the window. I remember thinking she moved quietly for a boy.

"Tomas," I said, patting the sofa. "Sit. You don't have to tell me anything. You never have to tell me anything. But you have to eat."

And here is the thing I will defend until whatever is left of me stops defending anything: in that moment, he was there. Not August-with-Tomas-inside-her, not some confusion of names. Him. Nineteen years old, salt still in his hair after all those years, that look he had of someone perpetually about to apologize for taking up space. I could smell the sea off him. I could hear him breathing the way he breathed in the first winter — carefully, quietly, like a boy who had learned that breathing loudly gets people found.

"Vera," August said gently. "It's August."

"Don't be ridiculous," I said. "August doesn't eat fish. August eats—" and the hole opened up right there, right in the middle of the sentence, a hole shaped like a food, and I sat with it, furious, while the boy watched me with his careful eyes.

"It's okay," he said. In the old language. Fluently. Effortlessly. The way water is wet.

I looked at him for a long time. Because that was the thing, you understand. That was the unbearable, miraculous thing. Forty years I had been losing that language one saucer at a time, and here it came out of this child whole, unbroken, running like a tap, and I thought: *so this is where it went. It didn't die. It just went home ahead of me.*

"Say something else," I whispered.

"What should I say?"

"Anything. Say the thing about the gulls. Say it in your own voice."

So he did. He said the gulls over the harbor were fat and insolent and had clearly never known hunger, and that he envied them their bad manners, and that someday he would be fat and insolent too, and we laughed — I laughed, an old woman laughing alone in a chair at nothing, which is the most frightening thing a person can witness and the least frightening thing a person can do — and for a few minutes the room was 1979 and the radiator clanked and Mother was shouting about the bread tin, and I was not dying, I was not forgetting, I was simply alive in the middle of everything, all of it happening at once, which is, I suspect, how it always was anyway, and we only ever pretended otherwise by calling it *time*.

* * *

When it passed — and it passes, it always passes, that's the cruelty of it, the tide comes in and takes the good things out with it — August was kneeling in front of my chair holding both my hands, and her eyes were wet, and she was speaking the old language back to me. Badly. Beautifully. Like someone playing a piano after twenty years: wrong notes everywhere, and underneath them, unmistakably, music.

"*Kus tu esi?*" she said. Where are you. As in: where have you gone. As in: come back.

"I'm here," I said, in the new language, because the old one had drained away again and left me standing on the mud. "Where else would I be?"

"You called me Tomas."

"I know who you are." I pulled one hand free and tapped her wrist, once, the way you tap a thermometer. "Don't flatter yourself. You have his chin. You've always had his chin. Nobody in this family has a chin except through him, and none of you will admit it."

"That's not true."

"It's absolutely true. Look at your mother. Look at your father. Chins like unfinished sentences." I was enjoying myself, which is dangerous, because enjoyment makes me honest. "The whole family is arranged around that boy like flowers around a stone, and you've all spent forty years pretending the garden grew that way naturally."

August didn't let go of my hands. She had gotten better at that, that week — at not letting go, at not being managed. Her mother's daughter in the ways that mattered and nobody's daughter in the ways that counted more.

"The night of the storm," she said. "The second winter. When someone knocked."

Ah. There it was. She'd been circling it for days the way gulls circle a harbor — patiently, insolently, waiting for something to surface.

"I told you," I said. "Ask me while I still remember."

"And you keep not remembering. On purpose or otherwise."

"On purpose or otherwise," I agreed. "That's the entire human condition, as far as I can tell. Everything else is decoration."

"Vera."

"Vera, Vera." I took my hands back and folded them, which is what I do instead of doors and walls. "You want to know what happened after the door. Everyone wants to know what happened after the door. Your grandmother wanted to know, and she died not knowing, or knowing and not saying, which with your grandmother was the same thing. Your father knows part of it and has spent his life sitting on it like a man sitting on a suitcase he's afraid to open. And I know—"

I stopped.

Not because I'd lost it. For once, not because I'd lost it. I stopped because I saw, quite suddenly and quite clearly, the shape of what I knew, the way you sometimes see a whole room at once when the lamp comes on — and understood that once I said it out loud, it would stop being mine. It would become hers. And then it would become everyone's. And there is a version of our family, the version that has held for forty years, that would not survive the telling.

But there is another consideration, and I will admit it plainly because I have earned plainness: I am running out of Tuesdays. Possibly Thursdays. The tide goes out whether or not the boat is ready.

"I know," I said slowly, "that the answer is not what any of you have decided it is. That's all I'll give you today. The answer is not the story. The story is a kindness we built around the answer, and kindnesses built around answers are just lies with good posture."

August sat back on her heels. "Then tell me the answer."

"When I'm sure I'll still be here tomorrow to have told it," I said. "You think I'm being dramatic. I'm being logistical. There's a difference, and the difference is that dramatic people die with clean consciences and logistical people die with finished business."

* * *

That evening she helped me to bed, which I permitted because refusing help is a luxury for people whose bodies still take their own side. At the door she paused, the way she does, and said something in the old language — a full sentence, halting, but a real one, assembled by herself, no dictionary in sight.

I made her repeat it three times. Partly to correct her grammar. Mostly because I wanted to hear it again.

"*Sa oled viimane,*" she said. You are the last one.

"Yes," I said. "Aren't I insufferable about it."

She smiled, and went, and I heard her footsteps go down the stairs and out, and the house settled into its usual silence, the silence of a family that has mistaken quiet for peace.

I lay in the dark and did inventory, the way I do now, nightly, like a shopkeeper counting stock that keeps evaporating. Saucer — gone. The word for the gap between fence posts — gone, though I could see the gap itself, absurdly, perfectly. My mother's name — present. Tomas's face — present, thank God, present. The night of the knocking —

Present.

It has been present for forty years and it will be present, I suspect, until the very last item leaves the shelf, because it is not a memory at all. It is a sound. Sounds go last. Ask anyone who has ever loved a language: you forget the words for years and decades, and then on your deathbed what rises is not meaning but music — a lullaby, a knock, a voice through a door saying please, please, in the rain.

Knock. Knock. Knock.

I lay there and listened to it, the way I have lain there listening to it since the winter I was fifty-three, and I thought: tomorrow. Tomorrow I will tell the girl. Tomorrow, while the lamp still works.

Somewhere past midnight I woke with my heart going like a bird in a box, and the room was wrong — too bright, too many shapes moving in it, voices asking questions I answered in the wrong language, in the old language, in the language of a nineteen-year-old boy standing in the rain outside a door that should have opened sooner.

They said the word *hospital* the way people say it, gently, as if the word were the problem.

"No," I told them, in two languages, so there could be no mistake. "No, you don't understand. I haven't finished. I haven't *told* her yet."

Nobody listens to old women. They write it all down, though. Every word. I made sure of that — I grabbed the nurse's sleeve and made her promise, made her swear on whatever nurses swear on, that if I started talking in the night, in the old language, about a door, about a boy, about footprints going down toward the water —

she would write it down.

All of it.

Every word, before the tide comes back for the rest.
