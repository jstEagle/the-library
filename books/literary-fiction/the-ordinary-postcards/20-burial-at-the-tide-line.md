## 20. Burial at the Tide Line

*August*

The tide was going out when we started, which Ivo said was the correct way to do it.

"You want the sea to come to you," he explained, hands in the pockets of a coat older than my mother. "Not chase after it. Chasing after the sea is how you get people drowned."

"Is that from Tomas?"

"No," he said. "That's from forty years of watching people chase after it."

We had agreed to meet at the breakwater at seven in the morning, before the town woke up and developed opinions. The funeral had been eleven days ago. The confrontation with my family — chapter nineteen of this disaster, if you're keeping score — had been four days ago, and my voice was still hoarse from asking *why was I the price* out loud for the first time in thirty-one years. My mother had wept. My father had opened his mouth twice and produced nothing either time, like a man testing a door he already knew was locked. And Vera, propped in her wheelchair with a blanket over her knees like a queen granting an audience to her own collapse, had looked at me and said, in the old language, *Now you know. Now go put them where they belong.*

*Them* being the postcards. Forty years of them, translated now, every one — the early ones full of a boy's careful handwriting, the middle ones desperate and addressed by name, the last ones found in a clinic drawer that smelled of camphor and neglect. All of them written. None of them sent. A correspondence conducted entirely with people who refused to receive it.

I carried them in a wooden box I'd found in the attic, the same box, its lid warped from decades of temperature swings it had survived better than most marriages. It weighed almost nothing. That seemed wrong. It should have weighed as much as a body.

"You're limping," Ivo observed.

"I'm walking thoughtfully."

"On one leg."

"Some thoughts are heavier than others."

He made a sound that might have been a laugh or might have been the sea clearing its throat through him. He did that — absorbed the landscape until you couldn't tell where the weathered man ended and the weather began. Seventy-some years old, built like driftwood, with the kind of face that suggested he'd been handsome once and had decided not to make a fuss about it.

We walked past the breakwater, past the line of beach huts with their paint peeling like sunburned skin, down to the stretch of gray sand below the dunes where, according to Vera's last lucid telling, a fourteen-year-old boy had washed ashore in October of 1979, half-drowned, hypothermic, speaking a language none of them knew but which — by some accident of geography and empire — my grandmother's mother had spoken too.

Here. This exact stretch, or near enough. The sea doesn't leave landmarks. That's rather the point of it.

Ivo stopped and stood looking at the water with his head slightly tilted, the way you look at someone you're trying to recognize across a crowded room.

"This is it," he said. Not a question.

"You've never been here before."

"I've been here every winter for twenty years." He crouched suddenly, pressed his palm flat against the wet sand, held it there. "Just never let myself walk down to the water. You understand? I could see it from the road. I could smell it. But if I walked down here, then it would be a place, and places can disappoint you."

"And now?"

"Now I'm too old to protect myself from disappointment. It's one of the few advantages." He straightened up, wiped his hand on his coat without looking at it. "Read me the card."

* * *

*Ivo*

She read it standing, holding the paper flat against the wind with both hands, and she read it in the old language first, the way he'd asked.

I don't know why I asked for that. Maybe because the last time I heard the language spoken aloud by someone who meant it was in a camp in the nineties, a man from two villages over, singing to himself while he mended a boot, and when they moved him on I understood I would probably never hear it again except in my own head, where it had begun to sound less like a language and more like a room I kept paying rent on.

August read it and the room opened.

Tomas's last card — the one from the clinic drawer, undated but late, the handwriting gone loose and large the way writing goes when the hand has stopped believing in the future:

*To whoever is still keeping my words: I am dying slowly, which is boring, and quickly, which is inconvenient. The doctors are kind. The walls are white. I have decided that if I cannot have my life back, I will have the sea. One time. Just to stand in front of it and say thank you and goodbye in the proper order. If anyone from that house reads this — I forgave you in the second winter. I only ever wanted you to know that.*

She translated it into English afterward, sentence by sentence, because I'd told her I wanted both, and her English version was beautiful and precise and nothing like hearing it. Hearing it was the other thing. Hearing it was the boy himself, alive for four minutes on a cold beach.

When she finished, neither of us spoke. The tide kept doing what tides do, which is everything, constantly, without applause.

"He forgave them in the second winter," she said finally. "Before the knocking. Before they turned him away. He'd already forgiven them while they were still deciding whether to feel guilty."

"That's the part that took me longest to learn," I said. "In the camps, the ones who survived best weren't the angry ones. The angry ones spent everything on the anger and had nothing left for living. Tomas forgave people the way other men smoke. Continuously. Casually. In a way that was going to kill him eventually, though not for the reason you'd think."

"You loved him."

"I loved him the way you love a brother you invented. We met on a boat that should have killed us both. After that, everything else was paperwork." I looked at the box under her arm. "You brought all of them."

"All forty years."

"Even the ones addressed to you?"

"Especially those."

"Good," I said. "A burial needs everyone present. Even the difficult relatives." I nodded at the sand between us. "Dig."

* * *

*August*

I dug with my hands, because Ivo refused to let me use the small spade I'd brought.

"Bare hands," he said. "It's not a fence post. It's a letter."

So I knelt in the cold sand above the tide line — just above it; we'd argued about this, Ivo insisting the cards should sit where the highest spring tide could reach them, me pointing out that the highest spring tide would also reach the parking lot, and him saying "then the parking lot is part of it too" with the serene authority of a man who had clearly won stranger arguments — and I dug until my fingers hit the damp layer where the sand changed color and temperature, and the hole filled slowly with seawater seeping up from below, as if the ocean had been waiting under us the whole time, listening.

Which, I suppose, it had.

I read each card once more before I laid it in. Not aloud this time. Some things you say to the crowd and some things you say to the deceased, and the difference is volume.

The early ones went in first, chronologically, the way you'd lay out a life. The boy's letters home that were never mailed because home was a word that had been confiscated along with everything else. Then the middle ones — the ones from hospitals and detention centers and a series of towns whose names I now knew in three languages — each addressed by name. To Grandmother, who wrote him back for forty years and sealed every reply. To Mother, who was a teenager when his cards started arriving and an accomplice by the time they stopped. To Father, who was a child being taught silence by a man who'd learned it from his own father, silence handed down like a christening gown that didn't fit anyone anymore.

And then the one with my name on it. Dated the year of my birth. *If you are reading this, you are the reason they could keep pretending.*

I had hidden that card from myself for a week. Slept with it under my pillow like a teenager with a love letter, if love letters came with knives in them. I had nearly burned it in the kitchen sink at two in the morning, lighter in hand, and stopped not out of reverence but because I realized burning it wouldn't destroy the sentence — the sentence was in me now, load-bearing. You can't burn a wall out of a house you're standing in.

But translating it had done something I hadn't expected. Somewhere between his handwriting and my typing, the accusation had turned into something else. Not absolution — I wasn't ready for absolution, and honestly I think he'd have been embarrassed by it — but context. He hadn't written it to wound me. He'd written it the way you leave a note on a table before catching a train: *this is where I was, this is what it cost, do with it what you can.* He'd addressed it to me because I was the future tense of the family. The only one who might still be able to say things out loud.

"August." Ivo's voice, quiet. "You're somewhere else."

"I'm everywhere else. Give me a minute."

I laid his card in last, on top, face-up, so the sea would read it first.

Then I hesitated. Because here was the thing nobody warns you about burials without bodies: you don't know the protocol. There's no liturgy for interring paper. No hymn, no handful of earth, no professional mourner checking his watch. I stood there with sandy knees and salt-stung eyes feeling like a woman who'd shown up to a wedding in the wrong century.

Ivo solved it the way he solved most things, by refusing to acknowledge that a problem existed. He simply began to speak in the old language — low, unhurried, not a prayer exactly, more like a report. Telling the sea, as far as I could follow, where Tomas had come from, what he'd survived, what he'd asked for and been denied, and that the family downstairs — meaning us, meaning the two of us standing in the wind like idiots — had finally delivered the mail. Forty years late. With apologies.

Then he stopped, and nodded at me, and I understood that I was supposed to add something.

I don't speak the old language fluently. I speak it the way you speak a childhood: in fragments, in nouns, in the names of foods and weather and parts of the body, with the verbs mostly missing. But I had spent weeks inside these postcards, and some of Tomas's sentences had worn grooves in me, and so what came out of my mouth was half mine and half his, and it was wrong in several places grammatically, and it was the truest thing I have ever said in any language:

*You wrote to us for forty years. We are answering now. The delay was ours. The words were always yours.*

Then I pushed the sand over the box with both hands, and the hole filled with seawater from beneath, and the sand settled and slumped and became, within minutes, indistinguishable from every other patch of beach, which is either the whole point of burial or the entire tragedy of it, depending on the hour.

Ivo put his hand on my shoulder. It weighed a surprising amount, like everything about him.

"There," he said. "Delivered."

* * *

*Ivo*

She cried, of course. Quietly, facing the water, the way people cry when they've decided crying is private even in company. I looked elsewhere, at the gulls, at a container ship sitting on the horizon like furniture someone forgot to move. A man my age learns when to watch grief and when to give it room. You give it room. Watching is for later, if it's wanted.

After a while she said, "What happens now? To you?"

I considered lying, out of habit. Decided against it. She'd earned the truth, and besides, lying to translators is a fool's economy — they can hear the seams.

"I'll go home," I said. "Wherever that is this year. I'll stop looking. That's the strange part — I spent forty years looking for a living man, and now that I've found a dead one, the looking is finished. There's a kind of peace in it. Like setting down luggage in a station where no train is coming."

"That's a terrible metaphor."

"It's a true one. Truth rarely travels first class." I turned my collar up against the wind. "But I'll tell you what I'd like, if you're offering. Read me your translation. All of it. Not the book — the book is for strangers. I mean before. Sit somewhere warm and read it to me the way you read it to yourself, mistakes and doubts and all. Nobody alive heard those cards but him and the people who locked them away. I'd like to hear them once from someone who unlocked the door instead."

She looked at me for a long moment, and I saw her decide something. She'd been deciding things all month — each one costing her a little family, a little certainty, a little inherited silence — and this decision cost nothing at all, which is why it showed on her face as relief.

"Yes," she said. "Come to the city. I'll read you all of it."

"And then?"

"And then," she said, turning back toward the water, toward the buried box, toward whatever the tide would do with it — take it, return it, keep it in the dark under the sand like a secret it had finally been trusted with — "and then I'm publishing it. Under my real name. Crane, Ravensworth, all of it, the whole ridiculous arrangement spelled out in the acknowledgments if I have the nerve. They can disown me or they can read it to me someday. I've finished choosing their silence for them."

The tide was coming in now. Of course it was. It had been coming in the whole time; that's the arrangement with the sea, the only honest one on this coast — you give it something, and it decides.

We walked back up the beach side by side, leaving footprints that were already filling with water, and behind us the first wave reached the place where the box lay, touched it once, lightly, the way you touch a shoulder at a funeral, and drew back to consider.
