## 14. Bidding War

### Sebastian

The courthouse steps smelled like rain and old limestone, the way they had when Sebastian was eleven and testified—no, not testified, he corrected himself, *stood*—at his father's memorial service. Everything in Harrow came back to standing somewhere you didn't want to be.

He arrived forty minutes early because arriving early was a form of measurement. He counted the folding chairs: sixty. He counted the bidders' paddles stacked on the registration table: thirty-one. He counted, without meaning to, the ways out of the building: four, one of which was probably locked since the Nixon administration.

Delia found him at 9:22, holding two coffees in paper cups so thin he could feel her hands shaking through them.

"You look like a man about to inspect something," she said.

"I am. I'm about to inspect an auction."

"Sebastian." She handed him a cup. "It's a house."

"It's a house," he agreed, which was a lie of exactly the size he'd been telling for fifteen years. It was not a house. It was the porch where Ruth taught him to shingle, the stairwell where Delia had once slid down in a laundry basket and broken her wrist and laughed the whole way to the emergency room, the kitchen where his mother had stood at the sink and hummed off-key and never once asked him what was wrong, because asking would have required him to answer.

He drank the coffee. It was terrible. He noted this in the mental ledger he kept of everything, the ledger that had ruined his marriage and possibly his life and had never once saved him from anything.

Ruth arrived at 9:40 wearing a green dress Sebastian had never seen, which meant it was old, because Ruth bought nothing new after 1994. Her hair was pinned up like a woman going to court, which, technically, she was.

"You're all here," she said, surveying them. "Good. Stand together. Auctions are won by people who look like they mean it."

"We don't have enough money to mean it," Marcus said, coming up behind them with his daughter, Poppy, asleep against his shoulder. "We have enough money to *gesture* at meaning it."

"How much do we have?" Ruth asked.

There was a silence while four adults did arithmetic they were all ashamed of.

"Eleven thousand," Sebastian said finally. "Between all of us. Eleven thousand, two hundred and forty dollars." He didn't say how he knew the exact figure. Nobody asked. They were learning not to ask him things like that; it only ever produced more numbers.

Marcus shifted Poppy to his other shoulder. "The developer's been walking around town all week in loafers that cost more than my car. He's got a binder."

"A binder," Ruth said darkly, as though the binder itself were a moral failing.

"Harrow Landing Partners," Delia said. "They want to flip it into short-term rentals. 'Riverfront charm.' That's from their website. There's a drone video."

"My house is going to be in a drone video," Ruth said, and for a moment her face did something Sebastian had never seen it do, which was crumple. Then it un-crumpled, because Ruth's face had load limits too, and she'd spent seventy years reinforcing them.

The doors opened at ten.

* * *

### Delia

Delia had sold forty-one houses in her career. She knew auctions the way surgeons know operating rooms—the choreography, the tension, the particular smell of people deciding what things are worth. She had stood beside dozens of families while strangers bid away their childhoods, and she had always believed, in the professional part of herself, that houses were houses and money was money and sentiment was a closing cost nobody could itemize.

She had believed that right up until the auctioneer said "Lot One, the Crane property, 114 Millrace Road," and her whole body went hot.

The developer was easy to spot. His name was Trent Voss—no relation, she'd checked, because she checked everything now—and he wore a blazer the color of a yacht club and bid with a little nod, economical, bored, a man flicking crumbs off a table. He'd done his homework. The Crane house sat on three-quarters of an acre above the river, and the county's new flood maps hadn't touched it, and there wasn't another parcel like it inside the town limits.

Bidding opened at eight thousand. A farmer went to nine. Voss took it to twelve with one nod.

Delia raised her paddle at thirteen.

Her hand did not shake. Fifteen years of open houses and difficult closings had trained that out of her. But her chest felt like the attic of the house itself—full of boxes nobody had opened, everything up there heavier than it needed to be.

Fourteen. Voss, nodding.

Fifteen. Her.

Sixteen. Voss, still not looking at her, looking at the auctioneer, looking at his phone, a man spending money the way other men breathed.

At eighteen thousand Delia turned around. Sebastian sat in the third row with his notebook closed on his knee, and Marcus stood by the wall with Poppy awake now and watching everything with the solemn eyes of a five-year-old who had learned that grown-ups' faces told you the weather before the weather did.

"Seb," Delia said quietly.

He was already moving. He came down the aisle and stood beside her, and the strange thing—the thing she would think about later, turning it over like a stone—was that standing next to him made her feel *smaller*, not bigger. Fifteen years of absence should have made him a stranger. Instead he was instantly, unbearably her brother, the boy who had held the ladder when she climbed onto the garage roof, who had said *I'll catch you* and then actually caught her, which was more than most people ever managed.

"Nineteen," Sebastian said, raising his own paddle, and his voice didn't shake either, but Delia heard the effort in it, the same effort she heard in bridges when she drove over them fast.

Twenty. Voss glanced at them for the first time. He smiled the smile of a man who has read the comps.

Twenty-two.

They had eleven thousand two hundred and forty dollars. They were bidding with money they did not have, on credit extended by hope, which was the worst lender in the world.

Delia raised her paddle again anyway.

### Marcus

Marcus had been to a lot of bad rooms in his life—courtrooms, hospital waiting rooms, his ex-mother-in-law's kitchen—but the Harrow County auction room achieved a special quality of badness because everyone in it had known him since he was nine years old. Mrs. Pell from the pharmacy was there. His old shop teacher. The woman who used to chase him off her lawn with a broom, now gray and gentle-looking, bidding on a dresser lot.

He stood against the wall with Poppy on his hip and watched his family spend money they didn't have, and he thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *this is my fault.* Not the auction—well, yes, the auction, that was literally his fault, he'd requested it, he'd signed papers in a diner booth with cold coffee and shame sitting across from him—but also the larger fault, the structural one, the crack running through all of them that no one had inspected yet.

Because he'd known. All these years, he'd known what Sebastian had done on that report, and he'd let him carry it alone, because carrying it alone meant Marcus never had to pick it up. That was the trick of being loved by someone quiet: they handed you your freedom wrapped in their silence, and if you didn't unwrap it, you could pretend it wasn't a debt.

"Twenty-four thousand," said the auctioneer, "do I hear twenty-five?"

Voss nodded.

"Twenty-five thousand. Going once—"

"Wait," Marcus said, and heard his own voice come out loud, too loud, the voice he used at parties and funerals and everywhere else he didn't want to be. "Wait. Sorry. One second."

He walked to the front—not to bid, he had nothing left to bid with, they'd pooled everything under Ruth's chair in a canvas tote bag like a bake sale—and he looked at Trent Voss of Harrow Landing Partners, and he said:

"That house has a porch swing with four sets of initials carved into it. Mine's the crooked M. The girl who carved hers married a man from Dayton and she still sends Christmas cards. There's a burn mark on the kitchen floor from a turkey fire in 1988 that Ruth swears was the best Thanksgiving anyone ever had. You can't put that in a binder."

Voss blinked at him. Somewhere behind Marcus, Delia made a small sound that might have been a laugh or might have been grief.

"Noted," Voss said pleasantly. "Twenty-six."

"Twenty-six thousand," the auctioneer sang. "Do I hear twenty-seven? Twenty-seven? Going once—"

Sebastian raised his paddle.

Marcus stared at him. *We don't have it,* he mouthed. Sebastian, who communicated in numbers the way other men communicated in hugs, mouthed back something that was either *I know* or *I'm sorry*, and given that it was Sebastian it was probably both.

"Twenty-eight," Voss said.

And that was where the arithmetic ended. Delia turned to Sebastian. Sebastian shook his head, once, small, the way he shook his head at bridge components that had failed final inspection. Ruth sat very straight with her hands folded on the canvas bag, and Marcus saw her lips move—counting, or praying, or doing whatever Ruth did instead of crying, which was the same thing with different grammar.

"Going once," said the auctioneer. "Going twice—"

Poppy, on Marcus's hip, reached up and patted his cheek with one small hand, the way she did when he was sad, because children always knew first.

"Sold," said the auctioneer, "for twenty-eight thousand dollars, to number fourteen."

Voss stood, buttoned his blazer, and shook hands with the auctioneer like a man concluding a mildly pleasant lunch. On his way up the aisle he paused beside their row.

"For what it's worth," he said, "the porch swing stays. We're rebranding it as *heritage character*."

Nobody answered him. He left. The doors swung shut behind him with a sound like a book closing.

* * *

### Ruth

Ruth had buried a husband, a best friend, and every reasonable expectation she'd had for her seventies, and she had learned this: grief does not hit you at the funeral. Grief hits you in the parking lot afterward, when someone asks if you need help loading the chairs.

So she held it together through the handshake with the county clerk, through Delia's arm around her shoulders, through Marcus saying *I'm sorry, I'm so sorry, this is on me,* which was true and also not the whole truth and also not something she could bear to hear just then. She held it together through the walk down the courthouse steps into the bright cold morning.

Then Sebastian appeared beside her carrying the canvas bag, because of course he'd carried it, the boy couldn't watch a woman carry anything, and he said, in his flat inspector's voice, "We lost by four hundred and seventy dollars after fees. If we'd had—" and stopped, because Ruth had stopped walking.

"No," she said.

"No?"

"No, we didn't lose by four hundred and seventy dollars." She took the bag from him and hugged it to her chest like a child's schoolbag. "We lost by twenty-eight thousand dollars, because that's what the house cost, and we never had it, and we went in there anyway, all four of us, and we stood up in front of God and Mrs. Pell and we *bid*. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

Sebastian looked at her with the expression he'd had since he was seven years old, the one that said he understood perfectly and would die before admitting it.

"Fifteen years," Ruth said, and her voice cracked on the number, and she let it, because today of all days she could afford one crack. "Fifteen years you've all been scattered like dropped change, and today—for one hour—you were a family in a room, fighting for the same thing. I have waited fifteen years to watch that happen and I will not have you turn it into a math problem."

Delia laughed—a wet, surprised laugh—and Marcus put his free arm around Ruth's shoulders, and Sebastian stood there holding nothing now, his hands empty at his sides, looking more lost than any of them.

"The house isn't gone yet," Ruth said. "Nothing's signed till the sale closes. And I've lived in this town long enough to know that deals fall through, and counties meddle, and men in yacht-club blazers don't always get what they—" She stopped herself. She was giving away too much, and there were things they weren't ready to hear, things she'd set in motion herself, and those things could keep for a few more days. "Anyway. Come on. I'm buying everyone lunch at the diner, and Marcus is going to tell me about his daughter's medical bills whether he wants to or not."

Marcus went pale. "How did you—"

"Marcus. I changed your diapers. You think I can't read you?" She started down the sidewalk, daring any of them not to follow.

They followed. Of course they followed. That was the whole point of them, Ruth thought, the thing none of them had ever quite managed to see: they always followed each other home.

* * *

### Sebastian

That night the four of them sat in Ruth's kitchen until late, and something happened that Sebastian could not have predicted and could not have engineered and therefore had no protocol for: they talked. Not about the house. About everything else. Marcus talked about the bills until his voice gave out, and Delia talked about the scholarship she'd lost, the one tied to the scandal, the future that had quietly evaporated the year Sebastian wrote his careful report, and Ruth talked about the winters she'd spent alone in a house full of other people's photographs.

Sebastian mostly listened. That was his role; it had always been his role. But near midnight, when Marcus got up to check on Poppy sleeping in the back bedroom, Sebastian found himself standing too, following him into the hallway, and the question was in his mouth before he could measure it, before he could file it, before he could do any of the things he'd spent a lifetime doing to questions like this one.

"Marcus."

His brother turned. The hallway light was the same yellow it had been when they were kids.

"That night," Sebastian said. "On the bridge. In the report." His heart was going like a jackhammer against a load rating it was never designed for. "Did you know I lied for you?"

The hallway was very quiet. Down the hall, Poppy breathed in her sleep. And Marcus looked at him for a long moment with an expression Sebastian had never seen on him before—not charm, not deflection, not the loud warm armor he wore everywhere—something older and rawer than all of that.

"Yeah," Marcus said quietly. "I always knew."
