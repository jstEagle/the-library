## 4. Out Loud

The flat had never held this many people. Thirty years of Freya's visits, and the rooms had learned to hold one woman and her silences; now eleven bodies filled the sitting room, and the walls seemed to lean in, curious. She had invited them with a message she wrote four times before sending: *Come on Saturday. I have something to read. It will not take long. It will take as long as it takes.*

She'd moved the furniture back, set out plastic chairs borrowed from the downstairs neighbour, arranged the garments on the bed in the back room like patients waiting to be seen. Forty-one of them. She had photographed every seam, every hem, every name, and the photographs lived now on two drives and in a cloud she didn't fully trust but had paid for anyway. Whatever happened tonight, the names would outlive the cloth.

Adaora arrived first, of course. She came with a covered dish of jollof rice and set it on the counter without asking where anything was, the way people do when they have decided a space belongs to them too. She looked at the chairs, at the small crowd assembling—neighbours, the pastor's wife, two of her mother's old colleagues from the hospital, a man from the Igbo community association whose name Freya had learned only that morning—and then at Freya.

"You're nervous," Adaora said. Not a question.

"I've translated treaties," Freya said. "I've stood at podiums in Geneva. I've never read my mother's handwriting out loud to strangers."

"They're not strangers."

"That's what frightens me."

* * *

She had practised all week. That was the humiliating truth of it—thirty years of English polished to a mirror-finish, and here she was at her kitchen table at midnight, mouth shaping syllables like a child's, *Ndewo, ndewo*, the greetings her mother had tried to teach her at seven and she had refused at eight because the other girls at school laughed at the music of it. Her tongue had forgotten. Her tongue was remembering. Some nights the words came out whole and she wept at the strangeness of hearing them in her own voice; some nights they came out broken and she wept at that instead.

She had decided she would not apologise for the brokenness. That, too, was a translation of sorts—of shame into something she could carry.

* * *

At seven o'clock she stood in the sitting room with the first garment over her arm, a wrapper of faded indigo relief-cloth, and the room went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when they sense a confession coming.

"My mother," Freya began, in English, because she owed them the frame before the picture, "was called Adaeze Okonkwo. Some of you knew her as a nurse. Some of you knew her as a landlady who never raised the rent. Some of you knew her as a woman who gave to the church and never told anyone why she gave so much." She paused. "I knew her as a woman who never spoke Igbo to me. I thought she was protecting me. She was. But she was also hiding."

She unfolded the wrapper. Turned it inside out. Found the hem with her thumb, the way she'd taught herself, the way her mother's fingers must have known blind.

"These clothes are made from relief cloth. From Biafra. She carried them out when she carried nothing else. And in every hem, she embroidered names." She looked up. "I'm going to read them. In Igbo, as well as I can. Some of it will be ugly. Forgive the ugly parts. The names themselves are beautiful."

The first name came out of her like a stone dislodged from a wall. *Nnamdi Eze.* The second easier. *Adanna Obi.* The pastor's wife made a small sound—she had known an Adanna Obi, or her mother had, or the name simply landed where names land in a community that lost so many it stopped counting.

Freya read. Sheltered. Fed. Hidden in the hospital laundry during the sweeps. *Chinedu.* *Ngozi.* *Olisa.* Each name a life her mother had stood in front of. She read them and felt the strange double grief of it: pride and dread in the same mouthful.

Then she reached the fourth garment. The blue dress. Adaora's dress. And she set down her notes, because she had decided she would not read this part from paper.

"Here the ledger changes," Freya said. "Here my mother wrote something else. I'm going to read it exactly as she wrote it, and then I'm going to say what it means, and neither of those things will be comfortable."

The room held its breath. Adaora sat very still, hands folded, a woman who had waited two generations for this sentence.

"She wrote: *Nke a abụghị ekele. Nke a bụ ụgwọ.* — This is not a gift. This is a debt. And then a name: *Nwadiuto Ezeani.*" Freya's voice did not waver; she had spent the week making sure it wouldn't, and the effort of it ached in her jaw. "Nwadiuto Ezeani was Adaora's grandmother. During the war, my mother took her identity papers—her real name, her real history—to survive a repatriation sweep. She meant to return them. She told herself she meant to return them. Nwadiuto died in 1971, stateless in a camp, because the woman carrying her name was in Enugu, alive, employed, and lying. My mother knew. She knew for fifty years. She sewed the name into this dress and she never gave the dress away, and she never said the name out loud, and she raised a daughter who didn't know the language well enough to read the confession in her own house."

Silence. The radiator ticked. Somewhere below, a door closed.

"I am not my mother," Freya said. "But I am her heir, and this is her estate, and a debt is an asset you owe. So: the flat, when it's sold, the first portion goes to Adaora's family. Not charity. Restitution. And this—" she held up the blue dress, "—was never mine to keep."

She crossed the room. Adaora stood. For a moment neither woman moved, and Freya understood that they were both translating—grief into gesture, apology into cloth, fifty years of silence into a single handoff in a small sitting room that smelled of jollof rice and old paint.

Adaora took the dress. Held it against her chest the way you hold a child, or a wound.

"Nwadiuto," she said, in Igbo, the name whole and unbroken in her mouth, and it was the first time in the evening that the language sounded easy.

* * *

She read the rest of the names after that. All of them. Her voice went hoarse around the thirtieth and the pastor's wife brought her water and the room stayed, nobody checked a phone, nobody left. When she finished, an old man from the community association stood and said, in Igbo, *Daalụ nwa m*—thank you, my daughter—and Freya answered *Daalụ, Papa*, and the words came out of her without rehearsal, without translation, like water finding an old channel.

People stayed late. They ate. They told stories about her mother that were neither legend nor indictment, just true. A woman Freya had never met said Adaeze had once walked her, pregnant and undocumented, through a police checkpoint on the strength of a borrowed name, and Freya laughed—actually laughed—because of course, of course, the borrowed name, the whole architecture of her mother's survival built out of other people's names, given and taken, and here they all were at the end, giving them back.

* * *

The last guest left at eleven. Adaora stayed to help stack the chairs.

"You know what I keep thinking," Adaora said, folding the last one. "My grandmother died with no one to say her name. Not because people didn't know it. Because saying it cost something, and everyone chose silence. My mother chose silence. I almost chose it too." She looked at Freya. "You didn't know her. You had every reason to keep the hem turned inward. And you read it anyway."

"I read it badly."

"You read it *in* the language. That's not nothing. That's—" Adaora stopped, and Freya watched her decide to say the true thing instead of the kind one. "My Igbo is better than yours. My children's Igbo will be worse than mine. That's the arithmetic we've all accepted. But tonight there were forty-one names spoken in this room in Igbo, and a woman who buried the language for thirty years dug it up and used it, badly, out loud, in front of witnesses. That's not a dying language. That's a language being carried, badly, by people who refuse to put it down."

Freya looked at the empty wardrobe through the open bedroom door. Bare wood. The smell of camphor and decades. She had come here to empty a flat in a weekend.

"Come back," she said. "After the sale. I have the photographs. We could—" she faltered, then committed, the way her mother never had, "—we could make a book. The names. Translations. Both languages. I'll do the work badly and you'll correct me."

Adaora smiled, and it was not the smile that ends conversations. It was the other kind.

"I'll bring the rice," she said.

Outside, the city went on with its ordinary noise. Inside the emptied flat, in a language diminished but no longer alone, two women turned off the lights, saying goodnight to each other, and to the names, and to the dark that finally had nothing left to hide.

* * *

Adaora walked to the bus stop with the dress in a cotton bag against her back, and the weight of it was the weight of a grandmother's arm across her shoulders, and she did not put it down once, not at the stop, not on the bus, not on the walk from the stop to her mother's house, where she would knock on the door and wake the whole family and say, in Igbo, before anything else, before hello, before explanation:

"I have something to tell you. Her name was Nwadiuto. Sit down. I'm going to start at the beginning."
