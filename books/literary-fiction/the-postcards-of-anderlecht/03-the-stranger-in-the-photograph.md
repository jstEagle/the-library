## 3. The Stranger in the Photograph

The archives on the Boulevard Pachéco smelled of dust and radiator heat, and Ada had to sign her name—her real one, the one on her identity card, the one she used for rent and taxes and nothing else—in a ledger that went back to 1974. The clerk barely looked at her. That was the thing about borrowed lives: nobody checked them.

She had come for fire records. Rue de l'Église, Anderlecht, March 1989. She'd told herself it was research. Mireille's stories needed grounding; a novelist owed her facts. But she took the stairs two at a time, and her hands were not steady, and she knew what she was doing.

The box was thin. Fires that kill no one generate little paper.

*Incendie d'habitation — une victime décédée.* Her father. She read the cause twice: faulty wiring in the stairwell. She read the list of occupants and found her mother's name, then a line that stopped her breath:

*Enfant mineure placée sous la tutelle de l'État (O.C.C.), transférée le 14 mars 1989.*

Transferred. As if she were furniture. As if she were a parcel.

The clerk photocopied everything without comment. In the corridor afterward, Ada stood under a flickering tube light and did the arithmetic she had spent thirty years refusing to do. The photograph in Mireille's shoebox—a young couple on a doorstep, a baby wrapped in white, the burned street behind them still standing then, whole, oblivious—the date penciled on the back was February 1989. Six weeks before the fire. Six weeks before the state *transferred* her.

She had assumed the family in the photograph were neighbors. Strangers whose grief Mireille had preserved by accident. But strangers don't appear in shoeboxes. And the woman's face—

Ada had spent her career studying faces, learning to write other people's love in their own handwriting. She knew how resemblance worked. The woman's jaw was her jaw. The man's crooked left eyebrow sat above her own eye every morning in the mirror.

She walked out onto the boulevard and the city kept moving around her, trams and bicycles and a man arguing into his phone, all of it indifferent, all of it continuing, while she stood there understanding that three years of postcards—*I am well, I am working, I hope the garden is green this summer*—had been addressed to a house where no one had lived since the wiring failed, to parents who could not receive them, to a child who had been carried out of that street in someone else's arms and never brought back.

She had been writing to herself. Every stamp. Every Tuesday night walk to the yellow box on the corner. All of it mail with no destination but the one she'd been fleeing.

* * *

Mireille opened the door before Ada knocked. She was in her chair by the window, the good shawl around her shoulders, and she looked at Ada's face and said, in Walloon, the word for *sit down*, soft as an apology.

"You knew." Ada stayed standing. "You've known since the first lesson."

Mireille folded her hands. "I knew your mother," she said. "Before I knew you. I knew the child who was born in that house in November, and I knew the woman who came to my door in March with a baby and no husband and a face like the end of the world. Your grandmother was my friend. Your mother was my friend's daughter. When they gave you away—" the dialect had a phrase here, *èl pitite do feu*, the little one of the fire, and Mireille said it slowly, laying it down between them like something breakable—"I was too old to take you myself. Everyone said it was better. Everyone always says it is better."

"Better." Ada heard her own voice go flat and strange. "You let me sit in your kitchen for two months. You let me learn your words. You let me tell you about the postcards." The rage arrived all at once, hot and total. "You watched me mail letters to a burned house and you said nothing. Do you know what that is? Do you know what you did?"

"I did what your mother asked me to do," Mireille said, and her voice did not rise. "She made me promise. *Let her be new,* she said. *A child of the fire should get to be someone else.*" She looked out the window at the grey roofs. "I have broken that promise every day since you knocked on my door. I am an old woman, Ada. I wanted one student before I die. God forgive me—I wanted you more than I wanted to keep her secret."

"Her." Ada laughed, and it came out wrong. "You mean me. You mean I've been ghostwriting my own life. Even you. Even this. I can't have one thing that isn't secondhand."

Then the rage turned, because rage always turns, and she aimed it down the years at a woman with her own jaw holding a baby on a doorstep, at a man with a crooked eyebrow who had wired a stairwell badly and died for it, at whoever at the O.C.C. had stamped *transférée* and gone to lunch. *You let go of me. You let them hand me off like a parcel. I wrote to you for three years and you couldn't even be dead properly—you had to be a stranger in a photograph.*

And then, last, at herself—at the woman who had chosen a life of rented words precisely so she would never have to look for her name in a ledger, who had mailed postcards to ashes because ashes don't write back, who had learned a dying language from the one person who could have told her everything and heard only lessons.

She sat down finally, because her legs gave out, and Mireille reached over and covered her hand with both of hers, and neither of them spoke Walloon or French or any language at all for a long time.

* * *

The demolition notice was waiting under Ada's door when she got home. Official blue paper. *Rue de l'Église 14, Anderlecht — démolition et déblaiement prévus le 22 du mois.* Three weeks. The city had decided, in its patient bureaucratic way, that the past was a safety hazard.

She stood in her hallway holding it and felt nothing she could name, which frightened her more than grief would have.

She went straight back to Mireille's. They had a lesson scheduled—the subjunctive of longing, Mireille called it, the phrases you use for things you want and cannot say—and Ada needed it, needed the ritual of repetition, needed anything that wasn't the blue paper in her bag.

But Mireille didn't open the door.

The neighbor from the ground floor was in the corridor, wringing her hands. "An ambulance came an hour ago. A stroke, they think. She was sitting in her chair with her cards all laid out on the table, like she was mid-lesson." The woman lowered her voice. "They took her to Saint-Pierre."

At the hospital, a nurse let Ada stand in the doorway of the room. Mireille lay small against the pillows, one side of her face slack, her good hand curled on the blanket. On the bedside table, someone had set her glasses and a folded piece of paper.

Ada recognized the handwriting. Mireille's lesson plans—she'd seen dozens, the old woman's careful lists of words like inventory. This one was half-finished. The last legible line read:

*Èl pitite do feu — li dit on:*

And then nothing. The sentence broke off mid-phrase, the way a life does.

Ada picked up the paper. Around her, the hospital went on—trolleys, murmured announcements, a television somewhere playing to no one—and she stood in the doorway of her teacher's silence, holding the unfinished lesson, knowing that whatever Mireille had meant to teach her next, she would have to find it alone.

Outside, the evening was turning cold. Twenty-two days until the rubble.
