## 11. Conversations With a Thief

The Kessler Avenue Care and Rehabilitation Center smelled of lavender disinfectant and overcooked carrots, a combination that seemed designed to make visitors forget why they had come. Mirabel signed the guest book with a false name — *M. Iversen*, their mother's maiden name, close enough to be honest if anyone checked, far enough to be safe if anyone did — and printed *Local Music History Project* in the column marked PURPOSE OF VISIT. The pen at the desk was chained to the stand, as though someone might steal it, and Mirabel thought: there's a joke in there somewhere, but it isn't funny.

The nurse at the front desk was young and kind and tired in the way hospice-adjacent people get tired, all the way down to the bone. "Mr. Vasquez is having a good day," she said, which Mirabel would learn was the staff's way of saying his heart hadn't argued with him yet this morning. "He loves visitors. He'll tell you he doesn't, but he does."

*He'll tell you,* Mirabel wrote later in the notebook they'd started carrying everywhere now, a cheap spiral thing bought at a drugstore because irony apparently had a sense of humor. *Everything is a performance you have to catch from the inside.*

Room 214 faced east. The door was half open, and through the gap Mirabel could see an old man propped against pillows with the particular architecture of someone who had learned to make even illness look intentional. He was thin in the way dying men are thin, but his hands were elegant — long-fingered, resting on the blanket like they were waiting for a piano that wasn't there. His hair was white and full, combed back. He wore a cardigan over his hospital gown, navy blue, buttoned correctly.

Mirabel knocked on the open door anyway. Manners were the weapon today; manners were the whole arsenal.

"Mr. Vasquez? Edwin Vasquez?"

The old man turned his head, and the smile arrived before the eyes did — a warm, generous, fully operational smile that lit up his face like a porch light coming on. "That's me," he said, voice roughened but musical underneath, a voice that had spent decades being listened to. "And you're either a doctor, a bill collector, or good news. Please tell me it's the third one."

"Neither, actually." Mirabel stepped in, extended a hand. "My name is Mirabel Iversen. I'm working on a local music history project — oral histories of musicians from the Milton Flats area, mostly from the sixties through the eighties. Your name came up."

Something moved behind the smile then, quick as a fish under ice, and was gone. "Did it," Edwin said. Not a question. An acknowledgment. He gestured to the chair beside the bed. "Sit, sit. Nobody comes here to talk about music. They come to talk about my blood pressure, which is boring, or my diet, which is tragic. You're already my favorite person of the week, and it's Tuesday."

Mirabel sat. Set the recorder on the bedside table — a small digital thing, visible, honest-looking. "Do you mind if I record? For accuracy."

"Record whatever you like." He waved a hand, magnanimous. "I've been recorded since 1963. It stopped bothering me around 1971." The smile again, tilted now, conspiratorial. "You want the early years. Everybody wants the early years. Nobody wants to hear about the hip replacement."

"I'd actually love to hear about all of it," Mirabel said, and meant it more than they wanted to. This was the trap of him, they realized within ten minutes: he was genuinely good company. He was funny. He remembered everything — dates, venues, the name of the bartender at the Blue Lantern who watered the whiskey, the exact month the flood took out the underpass on Route 9. He told stories the way some people breathe, easily, continuously, each one shaped and finished, each one with a punchline or a poignancy deployed precisely where it would land best.

He talked about learning guitar from a traveling salesman who wintered in Milton Flats in '58. About playing VFW halls and wedding receptions and the Sunday afternoon radio hour on WKLM, back when the signal reached three counties. About a talent show at the grange hall where he'd come in second to a girl who yodeled, "and she deserved it, she was tremendous, I've never been bitter about second place except every day since."

Mirabel laughed. Hated themselves for laughing. Wrote in the margin of their mind: *this is how he got away with it. People liked him. Liking someone feels like knowing them.*

"You grew up around here?" Edwin asked, mid-interview, turning the lens around the way skilled talkers do. "You've got a local face."

"Family's from Milton Flats," Mirabel said. "Delmore Street, originally."

The pause was small. A quarter-second, maybe less — the time it takes a man to decide which version of himself to hand you. Then: "Delmore Street! That was a good street. We had family off Delmore." He shook his head fondly. "Whole neighborhood's gone now, I hear. Condos. Progress eats everything eventually, even memory."

"Even memory," Mirabel agreed quietly.

They let him talk for forty minutes. It cost something to sit there and be charmed by him, to feel the pull of it even while knowing — knowing what Dani knew, what the fire report didn't say, what the whole family had arranged itself around like furniture around a stain. But there was strategy in it too. Gus had told them enough about the demo tape; Dani had given them the shape of the silence; the lawyer had explained what the law couldn't do. What remained was the man himself, and men like this — Mirabel understood now — could only be approached through their own instrument. You didn't confront a performer with evidence. You gave him a stage and watched what he did with it.

"So the songs," Mirabel said, when the stories had wound down to the warm afterglow phase, when he was leaning back against the pillows looking pleased with his own retrospective. "Where did they come from? Three albums' worth. That's a lot of material for one man."

Edwin's hands moved on the blanket, a small unconscious motion, fingers walking. "Oh, they come from everywhere," he said. "Life comes from everywhere. A song's just life that held still long enough to write down." He chuckled. "Some of them came easy. Some of them I chased for years. There's one — 'Kessler County Line' — I heard the melody in a dream in 1984 and lost it for two years. Woke up one morning in '86 and there it was again, whole, like it had been sitting in a drawer somewhere waiting for me." He tapped his temple. "It's all in here. Everything I ever heard. It doesn't leave."

"And the ones that came easy?" Mirabel kept their voice light, interviewer-light, curious rather than pointed. "The ones that just showed up?"

"The best ones always show up." He smiled. "You don't write your best song. You recognize it. Like a face across a crowded room." He looked at Mirabel then, directly, and for a moment the warmth in his eyes was replaced by something older and flatter and much more careful. "Why do you ask?"

"Just building the picture," Mirabel said smoothly. "For the project. Musicians always say the songs feel found rather than made."

"That's exactly right." He settled back, satisfied. "Found. That's the word."

Mirabel had planned this part for weeks. Had rehearsed it in the car outside, in the shower, into the steering wheel on the interstate. Four bars. No more. Enough to be unmistakable to the only other person alive who knew the tune, not enough to be anything you could call accusation. Rosa's song — the cooking song, the dishwater song, the one she hummed in the kitchen at 44 Delmore Street while she said *not finished yet, mija, don't rush a song, it'll tell you when it's done.* The one that had come through the radio at 1 a.m. credited to *E. Vasquez*, polished and completed and wearing someone else's name like a borrowed coat.

Mirabel hummed it.

Four bars, low, almost casual, as if thinking aloud. The room went very quiet. Somewhere down the hall a cart rattled; a television murmured through the wall; the east-facing window let in flat gray light that made everything in the room look slightly underwater.

And Edwin Vasquez's face did something complicated.

It happened in layers, fast enough that only someone watching for it would catch the sequence. First: recognition, involuntary, the flicker of a man hearing a voice he knew. Second: pleasure — genuine, reflexive pleasure, the pleasure of a musician hearing a good melody, and God help her, it *was* a good melody, it had survived twenty years and a fire and a theft and it was still beautiful. Third — and this was the layer Mirabel would carry home like a stone in the shoe — grief. Actual grief, surfacing and immediately drowned, a man mourning something for one unguarded instant before the fourth layer came down over all of it: the smile, restored, rearranged, warmer than before, which was how you knew it was armor.

"That's an old one," he said.

His voice was steady. His hands, on the blanket, had gone still — completely still, the walking fingers frozen mid-step.

"It is," Mirabel said. "It's beautiful. Do you know its history?"

"It's an old one," he repeated, gently, as if Mirabel were a student who had asked a question slightly wrong. "People hum things at me all the time. Fragments. Half-melodies. Sometimes I can place them, sometimes I can't." He tilted his head, and the charm poured back on like lamplight. "Where'd you pick that up?"

"My grandmother used to hum it," Mirabel said. "In her kitchen. She never finished it. Or she said she hadn't." A beat. "She died in a house fire in 2011. Delmore Street."

The silence that followed lasted three seconds. Mirabel counted. In those three seconds Edwin Vasquez did not look away, did not blink excessively, did not perform shock or sorrow or any of the things a guilty man might reach for. He simply looked at Mirabel with an expression of profound, courteous attention, the expression of a man giving a difficult moment its full weight, and when he finally spoke his voice was soft with something that sounded, impossibly, like compassion.

"I remember that fire," he said. "Terrible thing. Whole street mourned her. Rosa was—" he paused, and the pause was masterful, a craftsman's pause, "—she was a remarkable woman. Everyone who knew her says so."

*Everyone who knew her says so.* Present tense carefully avoided. Past tense deployed like a fence post. And the name — he'd said the name *Rosa* without asking for it. Mirabel had never mentioned a first name. Had said *my grandmother*. The recorder sat on the table between them, its little red light burning, and Mirabel watched him understand, in real time, that he had just handed over a piece of the truth the way a man drops coins from a torn pocket — unknowingly, or pretending to.

But he recovered; of course he recovered. Recovery was his native language. "I'm sorry," he said, laying his elegant hand briefly over Mirabel's where it rested on the arm of the chair. The touch was dry and light and terribly practiced. "That was a long time ago, and it was a hard time for a lot of families. If you're doing a history project, you should know that about those years — everybody lost something. Houses. People. Songs." He smiled, sad and wise. "Mostly we lost the songs. That's the real tragedy of places like Milton Flats. All that music, and nowhere to put it down."

"Someone put it down," Mirabel said. "Some of it made it onto a record. There's a song — I heard it on the radio last year. 'The Long Way Home to Kessler.' They credited E. Vasquez."

"Yes," Edwin said pleasantly. "That's mine."

"It sounds like her song."

The words landed in the room and neither of them moved to pick them up. Outside, a crow argued with another crow about something in the parking lot. Edwin's face held its warmth, but beneath it, visible now that Mirabel knew where to look, was the other thing — the flat, patient watchfulness of a man who had been keeping a secret for so long that the secret had become structural, load-bearing, something the whole house of himself stood on.

"Well," he said at last, and his voice was gentle, almost tender, "songs travel. Melodies are like that. They pass through families, through kitchens, through— " he gestured vaguely, gracefully, "—through whoever loved them. Maybe she heard something of mine once. Maybe I heard something of hers. That's not theft, Miss Iversen. That's folk music." He laughed softly. "You should put that in your project. *Edwin Vasquez says melodies belong to everyone.*"

"Do they?" Mirabel asked. "Or just to whoever records them first?"

For the first time, the smile faltered — not fell, nothing so dramatic, but shifted, the way a mask shifts when the wearer's jaw tightens behind it. He looked at Mirabel for a long moment, and Mirabel looked back, and between them passed something that had no manners in it at all, no warmth, no performance: two people acknowledging, silently, that both of them knew exactly what this conversation was.

Then he exhaled, and the charm returned, and he pressed the call button by his pillow with the air of a host concluding an evening. "You'll have to forgive me," he said. "These interviews wear me out more than I like to admit. The heart's a tyrant." He smiled his porch-light smile. "Come back sometime. I've got forty years of stories and you've barely scratched '62."

Mirabel stood, gathered the recorder, clicked it off. At the door they paused.

"One more question," they said. "Off the record. Did you know my grandmother well?"

Edwin Vasquez looked at them from his pillows, thin and white-haired and dying, with his beautiful thief's hands folded on the blanket, and he said:

"Everybody who knew Rosa well is gone, Miss Iversen. Whatever's left of her now is what people choose to carry." A pause, delicate as a held note. "Choose carefully."

* * *

In the parking lot Mirabel sat in the car for a long time with the engine off, hands shaking — not with fear, they discovered, but with the aftershock of proximity, of having sat three feet from the center of the whole dark arrangement and traded courtesies with it. He knew. He had known from the first sentence, probably from the guest book, maybe from the moment the nurse said *someone's been asking about Delmore Street* — and he had chosen, characteristically, to perform. To give an interview instead of a confession. To leave the door open exactly one inch, the way a man leaves a door open when he wants to see who pushes it.

Mirabel drove to the end of Kessler Avenue and pulled over and called Dani.

"He knows who I am," they said, when she picked up. "He knows why I came. And Dani — he said something at the end. He said whatever's left of Grandma is what people choose to carry." Mirabel stared through the windshield at the care home's brick face, at the east windows, at room 214. "That's not denial. That's a man talking about an inheritance."

There was a long silence on the line. When Dani spoke, her voice had changed — lower, steadier, the hospice-nurse voice, the voice of a woman who had decided something.

"Then it's time I showed you the photograph," she said. "The one with the notebook. Because if he's talking about carrying her — Mirabel, I think he still has her. I think he's had her this whole time."
