## 12. Rosa's Half

The letters lived in the shoebox too — beneath the fire report, beneath the melted photograph of a house that no longer existed, wrapped in a dish towel that still smelled faintly of cedar and something older, something like smoke that had given up trying to be smoke. Mirabel had known they were there for years. They had never read them. Reading them felt like opening a door in a burned house: you didn't know what you'd find standing, and you suspected most of it would be standing only because no one had touched it.

But now there was a song on the radio with Edwin Vasquez's name attached to it, and a notebook missing since 2010, and a cousin on the phone saying *you don't want to know what we all agreed not to know*, and Mirabel found themselves at the kitchen table at two in the morning with the dish towel spread out like an altar cloth and forty-one letters fanned across the wood.

They were all addressed to the same person. Not to Mirabel. To a woman named Odessa Crane, who had taught piano out of her front room on Kessler Avenue for thirty years and who had died in 2014, and whose daughter had mailed the whole correspondence back to 44 Delmore Street two years after it burned — because where else was there to send it? The return address had been crossed out by some postal worker's pen, a single decisive line through *44 Delmore*, as if even the post office understood the address was a fiction now.

Mirabel poured coffee they wouldn't drink and began at the bottom of the stack, because the bottom would be oldest, and oldest meant closest to the woman before she became the wound.

*March 9, 1987.*

*Dear Dessie,*

*Edwin says I should stop writing songs about the flats, that nobody wants to hear about bus lines and laundromats and Mrs. Okafor's dog. He says heartbreak sells but laundry doesn't. I told him he has it backwards. Heartbreak is everywhere. Laundry is specific. Anybody can be sad. It takes talent to be tired.*

*I finished the one about the laundromat. It's called "Warm From the Dryer" and it's the best thing I've ever done and I will die on this hill.*

*Rosa*

Mirabel laughed — actually laughed, alone, at two in the morning, the sound startling them in their own kitchen. They had never heard their grandmother laugh; she had died when Mirabel was nine, and what remained in memory was a lap, a smell of cinnamon and cigarettes, hands that hummed while they worked. But here was the laugh, preserved in ink, aimed like a dart at a brother who thought sorrow was a product category.

They kept reading.

* * *

The letters ran from 1986 to 2003, seventeen years, and they were not confessions and they were not diaries. They were letters between two women who had clearly spent decades finishing each other's sentences, and so half of every letter was answer to a question Mirabel would never see. But the shape of Rosa Vasquez came through anyway, the way a figure comes through fog — not all at once, but unmistakably.

She worked at the claims office downtown and hated it with a specificity that bordered on art. She raised two children mostly alone during the years her husband drove long-haul, and she wrote about this without self-pity, as logistics: *"Tommy has decided shoes are optional. I have decided many things are negotiable and shoes are not."* She saved for a guitar for four years and then bought a used Guild off a man in Braddock who threw in a case "because you made me laugh about my ex-wife." She played open mics at the VFW hall on Thursdays until the Thursday crowd got mean about women who wrote their own material, and then she stopped going, and the letter where she describes stopping is the letter where Mirabel first understood what had been taken from her — not the songs, not yet, but the rooms.

*Dessie, I'm not going back. Not because they hurt me. Because I caught myself shrinking the songs to fit the room, and a song that fits every room fits nowhere. When I get good enough, the room will come to me. That's either faith or stubbornness and I've stopped caring which.*

Mirabel sat back. The kitchen clock ticked. Somewhere down the block a car alarm went off and gave up.

*When I get good enough, the room will come to me.* And then the room had come — in the form of a man named Gus somebody, decades later, playing her melody on the radio with her brother's name stapled to it. The room had come. She just hadn't been in it.

* * *

By the fourth hour, the sky outside had gone the color of dishwater and Mirabel had arranged the letters chronologically along the table like evidence, which is what they were, though evidence of what exactly they couldn't yet say. What emerged, reading straight through, was a career happening in real time — small, regional, real. Rosa recorded three demos at a studio above a tire shop in 1991. She placed second in the county fair songwriting contest in 1994 (first place went to a man who wrote a jingle for a car dealership, a fact Rosa reported with the flat calm of a woman cataloguing injustices for later). She had a folder of songs she called "the serious ones," separate from the funny ones, and she almost never let anyone hear the serious ones except Dessie, and once, apparently, Edwin.

*You'll think I'm foolish,* she wrote in the fall of 1996, *but I sent the serious ones to Edwin. He asked. He said he knew people now, that his band was getting somewhere, that he could shop them around. I know how that sounds. I know what everyone says about him taking credit for everything since we were kids — remember the science fair, Dessie, remember the poster — but he's my brother. Family looks out. He looked out when Tom left. He looked out when the roof went. You don't keep score with family.*

*Besides, I told him: half. I said it plain. Half of whatever comes of these songs is mine, and if anyone asks, you say it plain too. He laughed and called me a businesswoman and kissed the top of my head like I was still nine. But he shook on it. Your brother shook on it too, that Christmas, when I made him do it in front of everybody. Half.*

Mirabel put the letter down very carefully, the way you set down something hot.

Half. She had claimed half out loud, in front of witnesses, fourteen years before the fire. And the family — Dani's voice came back, *we all agreed not to know* — the family had arranged itself around the arrangement, and somewhere in the middle of it was a handshake at Christmas that everyone remembered and no one mentioned.

There was more. There was always more. In 1998, a letter written on the back of a claims-office memo:

*He called it "borrowing." That's his word. Borrowing. Like I'm a library. Dessie, he played "Delmore Street Line" at the Bluebird Room on Friday and introduced it as his own and I stood in the back with my coat on and watched two hundred people clap for my bus route. My laundromat. My Mrs. Okafor's dog. And afterward he hugged me in front of everyone and said "We did it, Rosie," and what was I supposed to do, make a scene? Stand up in the Bluebird Room and shout *that's mine*? A woman shouting mine in a bar is a drunk. A man shouting mine in a bar is a songwriter.*

*I went home and I didn't cry. I want that noted. I sat at the kitchen table and I wrote a new one instead, the meanest thing I've ever written, and it's beautiful, and he will never have it. Let him starve on what he stole. I can grow more.*

Mirabel pressed the heel of their hand against their eyes. This was the cruelty of it, they thought — not the theft itself, thefts happened everywhere, every day, in every industry, men leaning on women's work like it was furniture. The cruelty was the hug. The cruelty was *we did it, Rosie*, delivered in public, converting her silence into his warmth, making her complicity the price of her dignity. He hadn't just taken the songs. He'd built a machine where every time she objected, she became the difficult one, the bitter sister, the woman who shouted in bars. And she had understood the machine perfectly, and stayed inside it anyway, because he was her brother, because he'd paid for the roof, because family.

*Family loyalty,* Edwin had called it, according to Dani. Mirabel could see now that those two words were load-bearing. The whole architecture of silence stood on them.

* * *

Near the top of the stack — the last years, 2001 to 2003 — the letters changed. Tom was gone by then, dead of the thing in his lungs that the trucking company settled out of court for less than a year of his salary. Rosa's handwriting loosened. She wrote shorter letters, denser ones, and she wrote about the notebook.

*I've started keeping everything in one place. The serious ones, the funny ones, the mean one, all of it, spiral-bound so it stays open on the piano. Forty-one songs, Dessie. Counted them last night like a miser. Some of them are twenty years old and still not finished and some are finished and still waiting. I've decided 2002 is the year. I'm fifty-eight years old and I am done waiting for rooms.*

*I found a studio. A real one, not over a tire shop. The engineer heard two songs and said — I'm writing his exact words so you can make me repeat them when I lose my nerve — he said, "Where have you been hiding?" And I said, "Milton Flats," and he laughed, and I said, "No, seriously."*

*Here's the part you'll hate. I need Edwin. Not his permission — God, no, never again his permission. His contacts. The Bluebird Room people, the label man in Harrisburg. I'm going to sit him down at Christmas and lay it out: the notebook, the recordings, the half. All of it, on paper this time, signed. He can be part of it or he can be beside it, but he will not be on top of it. I'm done being the library.*

*Pray for him, Dessie. He's going to need it more than me.*

Dated December 14, 2001.

Mirabel counted backward without wanting to. Christmas 2001. The fire report in the shoebox was dated March 2011 — nearly a decade later. So the confrontation hadn't ended in fire, not immediately. Something else had happened first. Ten years of something else — the slow grinding of a claim made and deferred, a brother who charmed, a sister who waited, a family that watched and learned to look elsewhere. The fire hadn't been the crime. Mirabel was beginning to understand that the fire might have been the punctuation mark at the end of a very long sentence.

And yet — the notebook. If Rosa had laid it all out at Christmas 2001, if the notebook existed, spiral-bound, forty-one songs, sitting open on a piano in a house on Delmore Street — then it had been in that house in March 2011, or it hadn't, and either answer was terrible. Either it burned, and forty-one songs turned to ash while their thief lived on in the next county collecting royalties. Or it didn't burn, and someone carried it out through the smoke, and that someone had known exactly what it was worth.

Dani's photograph. The songbook seen once at a family gathering in 2010, months before the fire, and never again.

Mirabel looked at the last letter in the stack. It was short. The handwriting had gone careful, controlled, the way handwriting goes when the person holding the pen is holding something else down.

*Dessie —*

*Short one. I confronted him tonight. Properly. No Christmas table, no audience, just him and me and the truth laid out flat between us like a body. I told him I'm recording in the spring. I told him the lawyer drew up papers. I told him half means half and I will spend my last dollar making it mean half.*

*He cried, Dessie. Did you know he could still do that? He held my hands and said he'd make it right, that family is the only thing that lasts, that he'd never meant to take anything, only borrow, only keep things safe. And I wanted to believe him. That's the shameful part. Fifty-eight years old and some part of me still wants my big brother to be good.*

*But I watched his hands while he talked. He wasn't holding mine. He was holding his own wrist, squeezing it, the way he used to when we were kids and he'd broken something and was deciding whether to confess or hide it.*

*I know that look. I grew up next to that look.*

*Whatever he decides to do — I want it written down somewhere that I saw it coming.*

*Rosa*

March 3, 2011.

Eight days before the fire.

Mirabel sat motionless in the gray morning light, the letter trembling faintly in their hand, the kitchen full of the smell of cold coffee and old paper. Outside, the first bus of the day sighed past at the corner — the 47, running its route toward Delmore Street, toward the chain-link lot, toward the condos that hadn't broken ground yet. Eight days. She had seen it coming and written it down and mailed it to a piano teacher on Kessler Avenue, and then eight days later the house was gone and the report said *cause undetermined* and the family closed ranks around a crying man who squeezed his own wrist.

She had left a trail. Of course she had — she was a woman who counted her songs like a miser and wrote her enemy's tells into the record. She had left it in the one place no one would look, because no one in the family read her mail, because the mail came back after the fire and went into a shoebox under a dish towel, and grief is a very effective lockbox.

Mirabel gathered the letters slowly, squared them, retied them with the string. Then they picked up the phone and dialed the number they'd sworn, three days ago, not to dial again.

Dani answered on the fourth ring, her voice sleep-roughened, guarded. "It's four in the morning, Mirabel."

"I read Grandma Rosa's letters," Mirabel said. "All of them. Dani — she confronted him eight days before the fire. She told a piano teacher she saw it coming. And there's a notebook with forty-one songs in it that nobody's seen since 2010."

Silence on the line. Long enough that Mirabel checked the screen to see if the call had dropped.

Then Dani exhaled, and when she spoke again her voice had changed — flattened, professional, the voice she probably used to deliver news at the hospice. "There's something else you need to hear first," she said. "About that night. About what I signed. Come to the diner tomorrow, and Mirabel — bring the letters. All of them."
