## 20. Credit Where Due

The studio was above a laundromat on Corbin Street, which meant the whole session smelled faintly of dryer sheets and warm cotton. Mirabel noticed it the moment they climbed the stairs — that soft domestic sweetness clinging to everything, as if the building itself had been laundered. It seemed right, somehow. Rosa had spent her whole life in kitchens and laundromats and church basements, places where women's work hummed underneath everything else like a bass line nobody wrote down.

Gus was already there when Mirabel arrived, sitting in a folding chair against the wall with his cap on his knee, looking smaller than he did behind a microphone. On air he had a voice like gravel poured slow; in person he was a thin man with a hearing aid that whistled when the room got quiet, and hands that wouldn't stop moving, turning the cap, smoothing it, turning it again.

"You're early," he said. "Musicians are never early."

"I'm not a musician."

"No," Gus said, and there was something almost gentle in it. "You're the other thing. The reason we're here." He nodded toward the booth, where a young engineer with purple-streaked hair was coiling cables with the serenity of someone who had done it ten thousand times. "That's Priya. She runs this place. Small label, small everything. That's what you want for this. You don't want a big room. Big rooms make songs into products. Small rooms let them stay what they are."

"And what are they?"

Gus considered the question seriously, the way he apparently considered everything. "Debts," he said finally. "Songs are debts somebody paid so we didn't have to."

* * *

It had taken three weeks to get here, three weeks of phone calls and emails and one long afternoon in Gus's kitchen while he made eggs he forgot to eat. The notebook lay between them on the table that day, spiral-bound, its cover soft as cloth from twenty years of handling. Edwin's handwriting filled most of it — chord charts, lyric fragments, dates in the margins — but threaded through it, unmistakable once you knew to look, were pages in another hand. Rounder. Slower. A woman who wrote the way she cooked, tasting as she went, crossing out and circling back.

Rosa's pages. Rosa's chords. And on page fourteen, the song. "Delmore Waltz," she'd titled it, though she'd never called it anything out loud. She'd just hummed it over the sink, over the ironing board, over the years, saying *not finished yet, mijo, a song isn't finished until somebody else can carry it.*

"Somebody carried it," Mirabel had said to Gus. "Just not her."

Gus had turned the tape over in his hands — the demo tape, postmarked nine days after the fire, mailed anonymously to the station in 2012. He'd played it maybe four hundred times on late-night radio without knowing what he was holding. "You know what I keep coming back to?" he'd said. "Whoever mailed this wanted it heard. Maybe Edwin mailed it himself, some kind of penance by proxy. Maybe somebody else found it and couldn't sit on it. Doesn't matter now. What matters is it's been floating around out there for twelve years wearing the wrong name, and names are the only thing the dead can't defend themselves." He'd looked at Mirabel then, and his eyes behind the bifocals were very steady. "So we defend them. That's the job. Not punishment. Correction."

*Correction.* Mirabel had turned the word over all the way home. Not justice — the lawyer had been clear about how little the law had to offer, a shrug dressed in legal language. Not revenge — Aunt Lorraine would have burned the notebook before she let it be used as a weapon. Correction. Like fixing a misprint. Like straightening a frame that had hung crooked so long the whole wall looked tilted.

* * *

Priya emerged from the booth and shook hands all around. She was younger than Mirabel expected, maybe thirty, with the unhurried competence of someone who ran a business too small to fail loudly. "Okay," she said. "Walk me through it one more time, because I want the paperwork exactly right. Co-credit. Rosa Vasquez and Edwin Vasquez, fifty-fifty?"

"Fifty-fifty," Mirabel said. "Her half goes to the Milton Flats Tenants' Fund. His half—" They paused. This was the part they'd rehearsed and still hadn't gotten smooth. "His half goes wherever he wants it to go. He signed off on the credit. That was the condition. The money was always secondary."

"He's dying," Dani had told them, back when they were still deciding. "Dying men sign things. Make sure he signs the *right* things." And he had — a notarized acknowledgment of authorship, witnessed at the care home on Kessler Avenue, his signature shaky but legible, the letters of *Rosa* formed with a care that looked almost like devotion and might have been. He hadn't apologized. Mirabel understood now that he never would, not in words. The notebook, returned. The name, restored. It was the closest thing to contrition his particular architecture could bear, and Mirabel had decided — on the last ride of the 47, with the melted hinge pressing cold through their coat pocket — that it would have to be enough. Not because he deserved mercy, but because Rosa's song deserved to come home more than Edwin deserved to burn.

"And the recording itself," Priya said, tapping her pen. "You want it live. One take. No overdubs."

"One take," Mirabel confirmed. "She never got to record it. I don't want the first real version to sound like it was built in pieces. I want it to sound like it existed all along and we just finally caught it."

Priya wrote that down, then underlined it twice. "I know a pianist," she said. "Older guy, played hotel lounges for forty years, never made a record. When I described the tune to him he went quiet on the phone and then said, 'My mother used to hum something like that.' So. There you go. Songs travel sideways." She smiled. "He'll do it for free. I'm charging you nothing for the studio. Gus is doing the liner notes. This thing wants to exist more than it wants to make money, and those are my favorite projects."

"What about distribution?" Mirabel asked. "I don't know anything about any of this."

"Streaming, bandcamp, a hundred physical copies pressed on vinyl because Gus will insist and honestly he's right, a song like this should have a groove cut into it." Priya shrugged. "And here's the part you'll like. The metadata. Every stream, every download, every radio play — the credit reads *Rosa Vasquez & Edwin Vasquez, 'Delmore Waltz,' composed c. 1987.* That string of text travels everywhere the song goes, forever. You can't subpoena a database, but you also can't unwrite one." She tapped the pen against her teeth. "Your grandmother's name is going to be attached to this song in every corner of the world it reaches until the servers rot. That's a better monument than marble. Marble doesn't stream."

Mirabel felt something loosen in their chest — not relief exactly, something older than relief. For ten years they had written postcards into an absence, words going out and nothing coming back. Now here was the opposite: a name going out into the world and *staying*, replicating, refusing to disappear. Rosa, who had been reduced to *cause undetermined*. Rosa, whose house was a chain-link lot waiting for condos. Rosa, who had said a song isn't finished until somebody else can carry it.

Somebody was carrying it now.

* * *

The session happened on a Tuesday evening in late October, the light going amber outside the studio's single window, the laundromat below emptying out as the day shift came home. The pianist's name was Theo, and he was seventy-eight and had hands like driftwood, and when Priya played him the melody from the radio recording — Gus's tape, transferred clean — he closed his eyes and let his fingers find it on the silent keys, hovering, mapping.

"It's in three," he murmured. "But it leans. Listen — it leans left on the second beat, like somebody walking with a heavy bag on one shoulder." He opened his eyes. "Whoever wrote this knew about heavy bags."

"She did," Mirabel said.

Theo nodded as if that settled everything, and sat down at the upright piano Priya kept tuned to the quarter-inch, and the red light went on.

They got it on the second take. The first was good — Theo said so himself, which from him was practically a standing ovation — but there was a hesitation in bar eleven, a breath held a half-second too long, and Theo refused to ship a hesitation. "She wouldn't have hesitated," he said. "Not in her own house."

The second take, the piano came in like weather. Mirabel stood against the wall with the notebook open on their palms, following the melody through Rosa's round, patient handwriting, and somewhere around the second chorus they stopped reading because they didn't need to. The song rose up through the floorboards of the little room above the laundromat, through the smell of dryer sheets, through twelve years of static and silence, and it sounded — this was the thing Mirabel couldn't explain to anyone afterward, tried to tell Dani and failed, tried to write in a postcard and crossed out — it sounded *finished*. Not polished. Finished, the way a sentence is finished, the way a life is finished: complete, and containing everything incomplete inside it.

When the last chord decayed into the room's quiet, nobody spoke. Gus took off his cap. Priya let the tape roll another thirty seconds just to capture the silence, because she said silences were part of recordings too, and this one belonged to Rosa.

Then Gus cleared his throat, put his cap back on, and said, "Now the hard part."

"Which is?"

"Letting people hear it." He smiled, and it was the radio smile, the gravel-voiced one, but his eyes were wet. "Twelve years I played that stolen tape and called it Edwin Vasquez. I owe a correction on air. You're going to sit next to me while I make it."

* * *

The broadcast was scheduled for a Sunday night — the same slot where Mirabel had first heard the song, pulling over on the shoulder of Route 9 with their heart hammering like a fist on a door. Gus insisted on it. "Full circle or nothing," he said. "Radio people are superstitious. We're basically sailors."

Mirabel arrived an hour early and sat in the station's cluttered break room watching the sun set over the transmission tower, drinking coffee that tasted like the machine it came from. Dani had offered to come and then, at the last minute, called to say she couldn't — she had a shift, she said, but her voice said something else, and Mirabel let her have it. Complicity inherited like eye color doesn't dissolve in one broadcast. Dani was sleeping better these days, but she still flinched at the word *statement*, and Mirabel loved her enough not to drag her into rooms she wasn't ready for.

Aunt Lorraine had not called at all. That was its own kind of statement. But two of the cousins had texted — *we'll be listening*, one said, and the other sent nothing but a photograph of Rosa, young, laughing, holding a guitar too big for her like it was a child she intended to raise. Mirabel saved that photo and looked at it now, in the break room, until the coffee went cold.

At eight-fifty-seven, Gus walked them into the studio. The on-air light glowed red. He counted down silently with his fingers, and then his voice poured out over the night — that voice like gravel poured slow, familiar to thousands of insomniacs and truckers and night-shift nurses across three counties.

"Good evening. Most of you know I don't do corrections on this program. Twenty-six years behind this microphone, and I've made my share of mistakes, but tonight I'm fixing the biggest one." A pause, professionally measured. "Twelve years ago, a tape arrived at this station, no return address, postmarked the week of the Delmore Street fire. I've played a song from it maybe four hundred times. I credited it to Edwin Vasquez, because that's what the label on the box said. What the label didn't say — what none of us knew — is that the song was written by his sister. Rosa Vasquez. Of 44 Delmore Street, Milton Flats. She wrote it sometime around 1987, in a kitchen, probably, humming it over whatever she was cooking, telling anybody who asked that it wasn't finished yet." Another pause. "It was finished. It was finished the whole time. We just weren't listening in the right room."

He turned to Mirabel and nodded, and Mirabel leaned toward the microphone, and their voice came out steadier than they had any right to expect.

"Rosa Vasquez was my grandmother. Her house burned in 2011. The fire report says cause undetermined. Everything else — the notebook, the theft, the arrangement our family made around the truth — that's a longer story, and it's not mine to tell tonight. Tonight belongs to the song. It's being recorded properly for the first time this week — one piano, one take — and it will carry both names forever: Rosa Vasquez and Edwin Vasquez. All proceeds from Rosa's half go to the Milton Flats Tenants' Fund, because the neighborhood she lived in is being torn down on schedule, and she'd have hated that more than she ever hated my uncle, which is saying something." Mirabel breathed. "This is 'Delmore Waltz.' Composed by Rosa Vasquez. Somebody's carrying it now, Grandma. You can put the spoon down."

Gus cued the track.

The piano came in like weather, leaning left on the second beat like somebody walking with a heavy bag on one shoulder, and Mirabel sat in the red-lit studio and listened to their grandmother's unfinished song finish itself over the airwaves, traveling sideways into kitchens and cars and hospital corridors, into every room where somebody was doing the work that hums underneath everything else.

Afterward, in the parking lot, Gus lit a cigarette he clearly wasn't supposed to smoke on station property and offered Mirabel one. They declined. He smoked it anyway, looking up at the tower.

"Four hundred times I played it wrong," he said. "Tonight makes it even." He flicked ash. "You know what your grandmother and I have in common? Neither of us ever got famous. Difference is, she didn't choose it." He ground the cigarette under his heel. "Fame's overrated anyway. Credit's the real currency. You gave her the real currency."

* * *

The record came out three weeks later, on a gray Thursday in November. A hundred vinyl copies, a digital release, liner notes in Gus's careful typewritten prose. Priya emailed Mirabel a screenshot of the streaming page: *Delmore Waltz — Rosa Vasquez & Edwin Vasquez.* Below it, the tenants' fund link, and beneath that, already, the first comments.

*My mother grew up on Delmore Street,* someone wrote. *I think I heard her hum this.*

*I'm a hospice nurse in Corbin County. Played it for a patient today. She said her sister used to sing it. Cried for an hour. Good crying, she said.*

*Who was Rosa Vasquez? Does anyone know more about her?*

That last comment Mirabel read five times. *Does anyone know more about her?* A stranger, asking. Wanting. The door that had been sealed since 2011 — the door Mirabel had kept shut on purpose, mailing postcards to the ashes rather than risk what answers might cost — stood open now, and people were walking through it asking questions, and the questions didn't end anything. They started things.

Mirabel sat at the kitchen table with the notebook open to page fourteen, the melted hinge from the lot on Delmore Street beside it, and began, slowly, to write. Not a postcard. Something longer. Something true.

Outside, the first snow of the season was starting, dusting the rooftops white, and Mirabel's phone buzzed on the table: a message from Dani, a photograph of the care home's front desk, and beneath it a single line of text.

*Edwin's asking for you. The nurse says it sounds urgent. He says he has something to tell you about the fire — something he's never told anyone.*
