## 6. The Address That No Longer Exists

The post office clerk on Harbour Street has a face like a closed ledger. She has stamped my postcards every Tuesday for forty years, and in all that time she has never once asked where they are going. I used to think this was indifference. Now I understand it is a mercy. Some questions are doors you do not want to open, and she has the good sense to keep hers locked.

"Room for one more," she says this morning, sliding the card across the counter.

"One more is all there ever is," I tell her, and she almost smiles. Almost. The people of this town have learned that my sentences come with weather attached, so they take them in small doses.

I walk back to the hotel the long way, past the fish market, because the long way takes eleven minutes and the short way takes four, and I have never been a man who could be trusted with spare time. Spare time is where the remembering lives.

* * *

In 1943 I was fourteen years old and I knew exactly how much a human being weighed when he was hungry. I don't mean in pounds. I mean how much space he took up in his own body — how he shrank toward his own spine like a letter being folded smaller and smaller to fit an envelope that kept getting narrower. My sister Anka had folded herself down to nearly nothing by that winter. Eleven years old and she had stopped growing upward and started growing inward, her ribs announcing themselves through her sweater like the ribs of a boat that had been left too long out of water.

Our mother was gone. Our father was gone. The war had taken them the way tide takes furniture off a beach — without malice, without ceremony, just an enormous indifferent pulling. What remained was me, Anka, and a room in the displaced persons' wing of the Hotel Meridian, which sounds grand until you learn that "wing" meant twelve families stacked into what had once been a ballroom, separated by bedsheets hung from wire.

And what remained, above everything, was hunger. Hunger is not an absence. It is a presence. It sits on your chest at night. It does your arithmetic for you. It makes every moral question look like a sum, and it always rounds down.

Ilse Brenner's family had food. Not plenty — nobody had plenty — but they had a cousin who worked the coastal farms, and twice a month a parcel would arrive, and Mrs. Brenner would divide it with a fairness that I have spent sixty years trying to describe in any language and failing. She gave us bread. That's the whole of it. She saw a boy folding himself smaller and a girl already folded, and she cut her family's bread thinner so ours could be thicker, and she did it as if it were nothing, as if fairness were simply the shape of her hands.

Anka began to grow again. Upward, even. By spring she had gained two centimetres and the colour of a living person, and I loved Ilse's family the way you love oxygen: completely, and without noticing it, because noticing only happens when it stops.

Then the ration cards came up missing.

Not ours. The administration's — a clerical shortfall, a bureaucratic hemorrhage, someone upstairs having miscounted, and the answer drifting down like ash: someone in the Meridian was holding unregistered cards, and whoever reported them would receive supplementary provisions. A kilo of flour. Sugar. A tin of fat.

A kilo of flour. I want you to sit with that number the way I have had to sit with it for sixty years. A kilo of flour, weighed against a family of five. And sitting on the other side of the scale, my sister, eleven years old, folding herself smaller every week, her wrists like kindling, asking me — not asking, really, children that hungry don't ask, they just watch you with their enormous patience — whether tomorrow there would be bread.

I told myself stories on the walk to the administration office. I still know all of them by heart; I performed them nightly for decades. *They'll only question them. They'll send them somewhere better. Someone else will report it anyway, better it comes from me, at least then I can ask about Anka's provisions.* Each story had the structural integrity of smoke, and I walked through all of them, and I opened my mouth, and I said the Brenners' room number.

I said it in the language of the administration, flat and clear, the way you read a train timetable. Fourteen years old, and I had already mastered the voice that lets you betray someone while feeling like a clerk.

They came for the Brenners on a Thursday. I stood at the ballroom window and watched Mrs. Brenner gather her children with those fair hands, watched her look around the room — not accusingly, that's the unbearable part, not accusingly at all, but the way you look at a house you're leaving, taking its measure, saying goodbye to the walls — and then they were gone, into a truck, into the machinery, and the sheet that had divided their corner from ours swayed in the draft for a long time afterward, empty, like something grieving.

Anka ate the flour. She lived. She grew up tall and loud and eventually emigrated and sent letters full of grandchildren and recipes, and she never knew the price of her centimetres, and I made sure she never would, and that silence was the second betrayal, though I did not yet have the language to call it that.

Because here is what I learned, slowly, over years, in the way you learn a language you'd rather not speak: Vessic has no word for *sorry*. My grandmother said this made us an honest people. I think it made us an accountable one. In Vessic you cannot discharge a wrong with a sound. There is only *vess-ahlin* — the accounting — a construction that names the debt, names its holder, names the fact that it compounds, and places it permanently in the debtor's keeping. You do not say sorry. You say: *this is owed, I am the one who owes it, and I will carry the weight of knowing it until the carrying ends.* It is a terrible thing to have to say. It is a more terrible thing to have no way to say it. English offered me *regret*, which is a doily. French offered me *pardon*, which is a door slamming. Only Vessic could hold the actual weight, and by the time I understood this, I was nearly the last one holding the language itself — the debt and the vessel for it, fused, alone.

So I began writing postcards.

* * *

The first one went out in the spring of 1949, to the Brenner family's last known address, a street in a town that had changed hands twice since. I wrote the sentence in Vessic, the full accounting, all of it — the act, the flour, the scale, my sister's name, the truth entire — and I mailed it, and it did not come back, and I decided this meant it had arrived.

It hadn't. I learned that years later. But by then the habit had become the architecture of my life, and I have never been able to say whether I kept mailing them because I believed they arrived or because the mailing was the only penance available, and a man will take a useless penance over none, the way a drowning man will grab driftwood even knowing it won't hold.

Every Tuesday. Sixty-some years. The address stopped existing sometime in the sixties — the street demolished, the district renumbered, the Brenners scattered into whatever fates were waiting for them — and I mailed to it anyway. The postal service, bless its granite heart, kept accepting them. Perhaps they sit in a dead-letter office somewhere, a room of undeliverable accounts, my sentence repeated thousands of times in a language no sorter could read. Sometimes I imagine some future archivist opening that room and finding it and thinking: here lived a madman. Let them think it. A madman is at least a man who noticed the weight.

People ask — the ones brave enough — why not simply stop? Why not let the debt lapse, the way debts do, the way everything does? And I tell them: because in Vessic, a debt doesn't lapse. It transfers. It waits. Ilse Brenner carried my sentence inside her life whether she knew it or not; her children inherited the shape of it without knowing why their mother went quiet at certain words. The accounting doesn't end when the address disappears. It ends when it is spoken, aloud, to the ones who now hold the other side of it. Or it ends when the speaker dies — and then, my grandmother would have said, it does not end at all. It becomes a stone in the river. Everyone downstream drinks around it.

For decades I thought I would die with the sentence unspoken, and I had made a kind of peace with that — the peace of a man who has stopped expecting rescue. And then, three weeks ago, through the wall of Room 213, I heard a piano tuner working, and beneath her tuning I heard her listening — truly listening, ear pressed to the building's iron throat, hearing what the pipes remembered — and I thought: *there.* There is a person who understands that things persist in structures. That pressure leaves records. That a wrong, like heat in a radiator, keeps ticking long after the fire is out.

She refused me, of course. She smiled the smile people use to end conversations and left, and I sat in my room and felt hope arrive late, the way it always does, knocking politely at the door of a house that had already been sold.

But tonight, standing at my window with this week's postcard in my hand — the same sentence, the sixty-third hundredth repetition, my hand steadier than it has any right to be — I hear something through the wall. Faint. Rhythmic. Someone tapping against a radiator pipe, and the rhythm is mine. It is the cadence of the sentence. She is tapping my sentence on her radiator, syllable by syllable, getting it wrong in three places and right everywhere else.

I stand very still, an old man holding a postcard addressed to a street that no longer exists, and I feel the accounting shift its weight — not discharged, nothing so kind as that, but *moving*. Finding a new carrier.

Tomorrow I will knock on her door again. Tonight I write the address one more time, in ink going thin, and I think: perhaps the post office was never the point. Perhaps the sentence has been waiting all these years not for a mailbox, but for an ear.
