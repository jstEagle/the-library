## 7. A Stranger at the Door

Wren told himself he was only walking.

That was the lie he'd been polishing all week, the way he polished the conservatory's brass door handles — small, repetitive work that kept the hands busy and the mind quiet. He was only walking. The coast road happened to run past the house. The house happened to be there. Dusk happened to fall exactly when his feet stopped moving.

The auction had been eleven days ago. Eleven days of Daniel Reyes's name sitting in his chest like a swallowed stone. *Sold to Mr. Daniel Reyes.* The auctioneer's gavel had come down and something in Wren had come down with it, some load-bearing wall he hadn't known he was leaning on.

He stood now at the mouth of the gravel drive, hands in the pockets of a coat too thin for the sea wind, and looked up at the house.

It was smaller than memory had made it. That was the first betrayal. In his head the place had been a fortress, a cathedral, the whole geography of his childhood — white clapboard walls gone gray at the salt line, the wraparound porch sagging like a tired shoulder, Aunt Mei's wind chimes still hanging from the eave even though Aunt Mei had been dead four months and the chimes' paint had long since flaked to bare copper. They turned in the wind without sound, or maybe the wind wasn't strong enough yet. Maybe they were just waiting.

*Lot 47.* He could still hear the number. Numbers were how the world had learned to talk about his life. Lot 47. Case number such-and-such. Sentence: time served, restitution, community. His mother reduced to a docket entry, himself reduced to a ward of the state, and now the house itself reduced to a lot, a parcel, an asset liquidated from an estate that had nothing left to give.

The light was doing what coastal light does in October — going amber and then bruise-purple in the space of a breath. The windows caught it and held it, so the whole house seemed lit from inside, warm, inhabited, as if someone were home. As if his mother were in the kitchen with the radio on, flour on her wrists, calling his name in that half-annoyed, half-laughing voice she saved for when he'd been at the piano too long and dinner was getting cold.

*Wren. Wren. The nocturne will still be there after you eat.*

Except she wouldn't have said *nocturne*. She'd have said *that sad one*, because she never learned the names of things, only how they made you feel.

His throat did something complicated. He breathed through it.

He should go. This was the sensible conclusion, the one his practiced smile would have endorsed if smiles could talk: you've seen it, it's smaller than you remembered, the sea is the same, go home to your rented room above the laundromat and your mop bucket and your name tag that says *STAFF* in cheerful block letters. Go home. Don't stand on the public road staring at another man's house like a ghost who missed the memo about moving on.

But his feet didn't get the memo either. They stayed planted in the gravel, and the wind came off the water carrying iodine and cold kelp, and the light drained out of the windows one pane at a time, and Wren Zhou stood outside the house where his mother died and felt absolutely nothing he could name, which was its own kind of screaming.

He didn't hear the door open. He heard footsteps on the porch boards — unhurried, deliberate, the tread of a man who wanted to be heard coming — and then a voice that hadn't changed as much as the rest of the world had.

"You're blocking my driveway."

Wren turned.

Daniel Reyes stood at the top of the porch steps with a cardboard box under one arm and no expression at all. Ten years had put a lawyer's composure on him like a coat that fit too well — shoulders squared, chin level, eyes doing the careful work of being unreadable. But underneath it Wren could still find the boy from that summer, all elbows and sunburn, the boy who had sat on this same porch eating saltwater taffy and arguing about whether the tide came in faster than it went out.

Fourteen years old. Fourteen years old, and his testimony had been clean and certain and devastating, delivered in a courtroom hush while Wren sat in the gallery with an aunt's hand clamped over his own mouth.

*You're blocking my driveway.* Not *what are you doing here.* Not *get off my property.* A lawyer's opening move — technically true, deniable, a door left open in both directions.

"I'm on the county road," Wren said. "This is public."

"It is." Daniel shifted the box against his hip. "You're also standing there like you're deciding whether to burn it down."

The wind chimes finally spoke — three thin notes, copper on copper, a sound Wren's body recognized before his mind did. His mother had hung them. Aunt Mei had kept them. Some other woman would take them down or leave them to rust, and either way the sound would be wrong.

"I was just looking," Wren said.

"Everyone says that."

"Everyone who?"

"People who come by." Daniel came down two steps, then stopped, as if he'd surprised himself with his own forward motion. Up close, in the last of the light, Wren could see the fatigue under the composure — the faint maps of sleeplessness around his eyes, the box in his arms with *KITCHEN, MISC* written on it in marker. Moving boxes. Daniel Reyes was unpacking his victory alone at dusk, and had heard footsteps on his road, and had come out anyway.

"How many people come by?" Wren asked.

"A few. Neighbors. An old man who cried." Daniel shrugged, a motion carefully rationed. "They all want something. To see it. To touch it. To tell me what happened here like I don't already know."

"And what do you say?"

"I say thank you for stopping by." A pause. The sea filled it, long and gray and indifferent. "What do you want, Zhou?"

There it was. His name in Daniel Reyes's mouth, stripped of the honorifics lawyers use when they're performing neutrality. *Zhou.* Flat, careful, almost gentle, which was worse.

"I told you," Wren said. "I was walking."

"For eleven days?"

So Daniel had seen him before. Or heard about him — Mrs. Okonkwo at the corner house, probably, whose curtains had eyes. Wren felt heat climb his neck and hated it, hated being legible, hated that this man could read him across ten years and a gravel drive.

"The road goes somewhere," Wren said. "I go along it."

Daniel looked at him for a long moment. Then he did something Wren had not prepared for. He set the box down on the bottom step, sat beside it, and looked out at the water.

"Then walk later," he said. "Sit first. It's a good hour. My mother used to say the six o'clock light forgives everything."

*My mother.* Two words, tossed out casually, and Wren felt them land like stones in still water. Daniel's mother had testified too. Had held her son's hand while he described what he'd seen from the dunes. Had looked at Wren's mother across the courtroom with an expression Wren had spent a decade refusing to decode.

He should leave. Every instinct honed by ten years of practiced smiling said *leave, deflect, vanish.* Instead he heard himself say, "Your mother was wrong. Nothing forgives anything. It just gets dark."

And Daniel Reyes laughed — short, startled, real — and patted the step beside him.

* * *

From the porch, Daniel watched the man on the road the way he'd learned to watch hostile witnesses: without appearing to watch at all.

He'd known it was Wren Zhou before he reached the door. Mrs. Okonkwo had called that afternoon, breathless with the particular joy of the elderly who have gossip with actual weight to it — *the Zhou boy, Danny, the piano boy, he's been walking past every evening, I saw him from the window, he just stands there.* She'd said *the Zhou boy* the way people say *the storm of '98,* a fixed historical event with a proper name.

Daniel had told her thank you for letting me know, and hung up, and sat in the middle of his half-unpacked kitchen among his mother's china and his father's silence, and had not moved for twenty minutes.

Now here was the thing he could not explain to anyone, least of all to the man currently descending his steps with the wariness of a cat approaching a dog that has historically bitten: Daniel had bought this house on purpose. Not for the investment — his firm's partners would have choked on their espresso if they'd seen the comps. Not for the view, though the view was the kind realtors photographed. He had sat in that auction hall with his paddle in his lap and watched Wren Zhou's hand rise once, tremble, and lower, and had understood with total clarity that he was not bidding against a rival. He was bidding against a ghost, and the ghost couldn't afford him, and the only merciful thing — the only thing his conscience, that tenant who paid no rent but never left, would permit — was to win, and keep the house from strangers, and wait.

Wait for what, he hadn't let himself specify. For the ghost to come back, apparently. For the ghost to stand on the county road at dusk looking like a man reading his own obituary off a fence post.

"You knew I'd be here," Wren said. He'd taken the step, finally, but not the seat — crouched instead, forearms on knees, a posture halfway between staying and going. "Okonkwo."

"She has excellent windows."

"Ten years," Wren said, "and you buy the house. Out of everywhere on this coast. Why?"

It was the question Daniel had rehearsed answers to in the shower, in the car, in the elevator at work where the mirrored walls multiplied him into a panel of liars. *Investment. Location. Nostalgia.* All defensible. All false.

"My father grew up two towns over," he said, which was true and adjacent to the truth. "I have memories here."

"You were fourteen."

"So were you."

The words came out before he could weigh them, and he felt them land — saw Wren's jaw tighten, saw the sea wind push his hair back from a face that had gone very still. Fourteen. The summer everything happened, they had been the same age, two boys on this coast, one of them watching through the dune grass and one of them asleep upstairs, and neither of them children again after.

"That's not an answer," Wren said quietly.

"No," Daniel agreed. "It isn't."

He should give him one. He had ten years of law practice and a lifetime of his father's training in the art of the partial truth, and he deployed none of it. Instead he picked up the box marked KITCHEN, MISC, set it on his knees, and opened the flaps, and took out the thing on top, which was a mug. Chipped, faded, a cartoon whale on the side. He'd found it behind the stove that morning, wedged where it had fallen years ago and been forgotten, and he had stood holding it like an archaeologist holding a bone.

"This was yours," he said. "I think. It was behind the stove."

Wren went white.

Not metaphorically — Daniel watched the color actually drain from the man's face, watched his hand come out and hover over the mug without touching it, the way you'd reach toward a bird you were afraid of startling. The cartoon whale. The chip on the handle shaped like a crescent moon. Daniel knew these things because he had drunk cocoa from this mug in this kitchen at fourteen, and because he had spent the drive over composing a sentence that would not be cruel, and had failed, because there was no version of returning a dead woman's mug to her son that was not cruelty wearing kindness's clothes.

"I'm sorry," Daniel said. "I should have thrown it out."

"Don't." Wren's voice cracked on the single syllable. He cleared his throat, mastered himself with visible effort, and took the mug. Held it in both hands like something rescued from a fire. "Don't throw it out."

They sat in silence then, the three of them — two men and a mug — while the lighthouse at the point came on and swept its slow white arm across the water. Daniel catalogued the silence the way he catalogued everything: it was not comfortable, but it was not hostile either. It was the silence of two people circling the same wreckage from opposite shores, each waiting for the other to name what lay between them.

Because that was the thing. They had never spoken. Not once, not properly, in ten years. There had been the trial, where Wren was a child in the gallery and Daniel was a child on the stand, and afterward there had been nothing — no confrontation, no letter, no drunken encounter at a bar. Just the town's memory pressing them into the same story from opposite ends: *the boy who lost his mother, and the boy who put her away.* Daniel had carried his half of that story so long it had worn smooth, like a stone in the pocket, and he had never once examined it in good light, because he was afraid of what shape it would turn out to be.

"You hate me," he said at last. It wasn't a question. He'd earned the right to make it a statement; he'd had ten years of evidence.

Wren didn't answer immediately. He turned the mug over in his hands, thumb finding the crescent-chip, worrying it like a rosary bead.

"I used to have a speech," he said finally. "Ten pages. I wrote it when I was nineteen. I was going to find you and deliver it." A breath that might have been a laugh. "I still know it by heart."

"What's the ending?"

"'I hope you remember her face every day for the rest of your life.'"

The lighthouse beam passed over them, and in its wake the dark seemed deeper. Daniel nodded slowly, accepting the sentence the way you accept a verdict — not because it's right, but because the court has spoken.

"And now?" he asked.

"Now I mop floors at the conservatory," Wren said. "The speech seems dramatic, from down there."

It was the closest thing to honesty Daniel had gotten from anyone in months, and it undid him more than hatred would have. Hatred he could manage. Hatred had rules. This — this man admitting his ruin in a flat voice on a public road, offering it up like a receipt — this broke every protocol Daniel had built his adult life on.

"Zhou." He waited until the other man looked at him. The lighthouse swept around again, and in its light Daniel saw what he'd been refusing to see since the auction: not an enemy. A mirror. Two boys from one summer, grown into two men carrying one secret, except only one of them knew it was a secret — only one of them knew that the testimony that destroyed the Zhou family had been assembled like a piece of furniture, by a father with instructions and a boy with no tools to refuse.

*Say what you saw, mijo. You saw the car. You saw her. That's all. That's all you saw.*

He had seen the car. He had seen her. And he had seen other things too, things that had been gently, firmly, lovingly sanded away before he ever reached the witness stand, until the story fit the shape his family needed it to fit.

"Come inside," Daniel said. "I'll make coffee. We can — "

"No." Wren was already standing, the mug cradled against his chest like contraband. Whatever window had opened in him was closing; Daniel could see it happening, the practiced smile assembling itself feature by feature, a drawbridge going up. "Thank you. For the mug."

"Zhou —"

"There's an old woman on Seaview Lane," Wren said, pausing at the edge of the drive, and the smile was fully in place now, perfect and terrible, the smile that ended conversations. "Mrs. Adeyemi. She was home the night my mother was arrested. She told the police she saw a second car." He let that sit in the dark between them. "She told me yesterday she remembers more than she said. I'm going to go ask her what."

He walked away down the coast road, into the dark, a thin silhouette with a child's mug held against his heart, and Daniel Reyes sat on the porch steps of a house he had bought to protect a ghost, and understood that the ghost had just declared war on the living.

Inside, on the counter, under the box marked KITCHEN, MISC, his phone buzzed. His father's name on the screen. *Call me, mijo. Important.*

Daniel looked at it for a long time, and did not pick it up.
