## 12. Practice Rooms

The key to the east wing was supposed to be mine alone. Mr. Okafor had pressed it into my hand my first week with the apology of a man handing over something embarrassing — *the old rooms, nobody uses them, just don't let the students in, they'll break the windows* — and for two months I had used it only to sweep dead moths from the sills and check for leaks after storms. The east wing had been closed since the renovation, a corridor of boarded practice rooms left behind like a row of pulled teeth, and the conservatory's money had gone instead to the glass atrium and the recording studio with its foam-lined walls and its plaque thanking a donor whose name meant nothing to anyone.

I had told myself the key was a burden. I had told myself a lot of things.

It was a Tuesday in late October when I first opened Room 9 instead of just looking at it. I remember the day because the radiators had come on that morning for the first time, and the whole building had filled with that knocking, groaning heat, the sound of an old body remembering how to move. I remember because I had spent the afternoon in the atrium, mopping around a cluster of first-years who were arguing about whether the Chopin B minor sonata was morbid or merely honest, and I had stood there with my mop dripping, listening, feeling the old familiar ache open in my chest like a door I had nailed shut from the inside.

Room 9 still had a piano. That was the thing I hadn't let myself think about too closely. An old upright, a Zimmermann, its case gone the color of weak tea, its bench missing. Someone had covered it with a drop cloth, and under the drop cloth it had waited — how many years? — in the dark, in the cold, in the dust that lay on everything in that wing like a second skin.

I pulled the cloth off and dust rose around me, catching the gray light from the boarded window, and for a long moment I just stood there with the cloth in my hands like a man who has uncovered something he is not sure he is allowed to see.

I did not play that first night. I want to be honest about that, because the story people would tell later — the story that went around the conservatory by Friday, growing a little more ornate with each retelling — made it sound like a resurrection, a man rising from the dead in one glorious movement. The truth was smaller and stranger. That first night I sat on the floor with my back against the piano's leg and my knees drawn up, the way I used to sit as a boy under my mother's upright in the coastal house, and I listened to the building breathe. The radiators knocked. The wind found the gap under the boarded window and made a thin whistle of it. Somewhere far off, in the living wing, a student was running a passage over and over, the same four bars, getting them a fraction better each time, and I closed my eyes and let that sound wash over me the way other men let rain.

I had not touched a piano in ten years. That was not strictly true — my hands had touched keys, in hotel lobbies, in the music room of the storage facility where I sometimes took my lunch, once, memorably, in a church in a town whose name I've forgotten, when a wedding party had abandoned their rented keyboard and I had played four bars of something before catching myself like a man grabbing a live wire. But *playing* — that was different. Playing was a country I had renounced my citizenship in. You do not get to visit. That was the deal I had made with myself at nineteen, walking out of this very building with a duffel bag and a smile I had begun practicing that same afternoon, the smile that ended conversations: *you don't get to visit, and if you do, you don't get to stay.*

But my hands. My hands had not signed anything.

* * *

The second night I opened the fallboard.

I told myself I was only checking whether the instrument was salvageable, whether the strings had rusted through, whether the hammers had hardened into something useless. This was even true. It was the kind of true that a lie wears as a coat. I lifted the fallboard and the smell came up out of the piano — felt and wood and the ghost of whatever had been played on it last, decades ago — and my hands hovered over the keys the way hands hover over a stove, over a wound, over a face you used to know.

I played middle C.

The piano was badly out of tune, which was a mercy, because it meant the sound was not *my* sound, not the sound that had been recorded and broadcast and written about by people who used words like *incandescent* and *frighteningly complete*. It was a stranger's sound, a sick sound, a C that wavered like a voice about to cry. I played it again. Then the C above it. Then, because my fingers were already moving without my permission, the first four notes of something I could not name until I was inside it, and then it was too late: it was the nocturne, the E-flat, Opus 9 No. 2, the piece I had played at my final recital at nineteen, three months before everything, the piece the first-years in the atrium had been circling around that afternoon without naming, the way you circle a grave.

I stopped. My heart was going like a bird in a box.

The room was dark except for the strip of streetlight that came through the gap in the boards, and I sat there in the dark with my hands in my lap, and I said, out loud, to no one, in a voice I did not recognize: *No.*

But the thing about saying *no* out loud in an empty room is that the room hears it, and the room does not agree. The room had a piano in it. The room had me in it. The room had ten years of silence stacked against its walls like cordwood, and it was not going to let me walk out with even one log.

I went back the third night. And the fourth. By the second week I had a routine, and the routine was the most shameful and the most sacred thing I owned. After my shift ended at ten, I would buy a coffee from the machine in the basement — the machine that produced something brown and hot and only nominally coffee — and I would carry it up the back stairs to the east wing, and I would let myself into Room 9, and I would play until my hands ached, which at first was ten minutes and by the third week was two hours.

I did not play well. That has to be understood. The prodigy was gone; whatever had lived in my hands at fifteen had died or emigrated or was sleeping somewhere too deep to reach. What I had instead was worse and better: I had the memory of music without the mastery, an audience of one who knew exactly what the piece should sound like and could not make it happen. There is a particular torture in that, and a particular tenderness too. I was learning my instrument the way you learn a language you spoke as a child — the grammar still in the bones, the vocabulary rusted, the accent gone strange. Scales first. Then Bach, the two-part inventions, because Bach forgives. Then, when I could bear it, Chopin again, the nocturne, played slowly, so slowly it barely qualified as music, more like a man walking through a house he grew up in, touching the walls.

I was careful. God, I was careful. I played pianissimo. I played with the fallboard felt down half the time, muffling everything to a rumor. I timed my loudest passages to the radiator's knocking, the way burglars time their footsteps to thunder. I never turned on the light. I never stayed past one. And every morning I wiped the keys with the rag I carried for the purpose and replaced the drop cloth and locked the door and walked back down through the sleeping building as a janitor, only a janitor, a man with a bucket and a ring of keys and a smile that ended conversations.

It could not last. Of course it could not last. But I want to record here, in whatever small archive this account becomes, that for nineteen nights it did — nineteen nights of the closest thing to happiness I had felt since the coastal house, since before the coastal house, maybe since ever — and that I knew even then it was borrowed time, and I played anyway. That is the whole of what I learned in Room 9, and it took me ten years to learn it: you play anyway.

* * *

The night it ended — or began, depending on how you hold it — was the twenty-third of October, a Thursday, raining hard enough that the gutters on the east wing had backed up and water was finding its way under the boarded window in a thin silver thread. I remember the rain because it covered my sound. I remember thinking, with the reckless logic of a man halfway into the nocturne, that tonight I could play at a real volume, that the storm would keep my secret the way it keeps everything, washing it down the drains and out to sea.

I was wrong about the rain. I was wrong about the drains. And I was wrong, most importantly, about the girl.

Her name was Thea Lindqvist, though I did not learn it until later, and by then it hardly mattered, because by then everyone knew her as the girl who heard the ghost. She was a second-year, seventeen or eighteen, a cellist — I learned that later too — and what she was doing in the east wing at half past eleven on a rainy Thursday I never fully established. The official version, the one that reached me thirdhand, was that she'd been hiding from her roommate to practice, that she'd heard the piano through the wall of Room 7, that she'd stood in the dark corridor for forty minutes with her ear against the plaster, too frightened and too transfixed to move.

The truth, when she finally told it to my face, weeks later, was better than that. She'd been crying. That was all. She'd had a lesson that went badly, a teacher who told her she had plateaued, and she had come to the dead wing because dead wings are where you go when you can't be seen failing, and she had heard, through two walls and forty years of dust, someone playing the E-flat nocturne so slowly and so badly and with such unmistakable grief that she had sat down on the floor of the corridor and cried harder, and then, somehow, less.

I didn't know any of this at the time. What I knew was that I finished the nocturne — all of it, the whole piece, the first time I had managed it end to end — and that in the silence after the last chord, before I had even lifted my hands, I heard it: a sound in the corridor. Small. Human. The sound of someone trying not to breathe.

I was across the room and at the door before I had decided to move. I pulled it open and the corridor was empty, or nearly — there was a scuff of a shoe around the corner toward the stairwell, a wet footprint on the linoleum, small, and the smell of rain and, faintly, of rosin.

I stood in the doorway with my heart hammering and my hands still trembling from the music, and I told myself it might have been anything. A cat. The building settling. My own ears, playing tricks after an hour of pianissimo in the dark. But the footprint was there, and the footprint was going *away*, and whoever it belonged to had heard me.

I did not sleep that night. I lay in my room above the dry cleaner on Marsh Street and stared at the ceiling and ran the arithmetic of disaster. A student had heard a piano in a locked wing. Students talked. That was the one law of the conservatory that had not changed in ten years — I had been a student once; I knew how we had talked, how a rumor moved through those halls faster than fire. By morning, someone would be saying they'd heard music in the east wing. By evening, the story would have a shape. And the shape it would take — I knew this too, with the sick certainty of a man watching a wave build — was not *a janitor is playing piano in the old rooms.* Nobody would believe that. Nobody could believe that, because the janitor was nobody, and nobody does not play the nocturne at midnight with grief in his hands.

The shape it would take was a ghost.

I went to work the next morning braced for it. I mopped the atrium. I emptied the bins outside the recital hall. I fixed the sticking door on the second floor. And all day I watched the students the way you watch a sky that might storm — and nothing. The day passed clean. The rumor did not come.

I almost believed I'd imagined the whole thing.

It came on Friday. It came, as these things do, sideways, in fragments, in the spaces between classes, and by four o'clock it had reached the faculty lounge, and by six it had reached Mr. Okafor, who came looking for me while I was wringing out a mop in the basement and stood at the bottom of the stairs with an expression I had never seen on him before — not anger, not suspicion, but something like wonder trying very hard to look like administrative concern.

"Wren," he said. "You got a minute?"

"Sure," I said, and smiled the smile.

He didn't smile back. That was the first sign. The smile had never once failed me; it was the single most reliable instrument I owned, better than any piano, and watching it fail against Okafor's face was like watching a key turn in a lock and the door not open.

"There's a story going around," he said. "About the east wing. About music. You know anything about that?"

"Kids and their ghosts," I said. "This building's got a hundred years of them."

"Mm." He came down one more step. He was holding his glasses in his hand, which he did only when he was about to say something he'd rehearsed. "The story's got a detail in it. The girl — second-year, cellist, Lindqvist — she says the playing was Chopin. The E-flat nocturne. And she says — and I'm quoting the version that reached me, so take it for what it's worth — she says whoever was playing knew the piece. Not student-knows-it. Not teacher-knows-it." He put his glasses back on, and behind them his eyes were very kind and very tired. "She says it was played like someone apologizing to it. And Wren, there's maybe four people alive who'd play it that way, and one of them mops my floors."

The basement was very quiet. The boiler ticked. Somewhere above us a door slammed and a river of students poured out into the Friday evening, laughing, shouting, carrying their futures under their arms in black cases.

"I don't know what to tell you, Mr. Okafor," I said.

"No," he said. "I don't imagine you do." He turned to go up the stairs, then stopped, one hand on the rail, and said, without turning around, in a voice pitched just for me, under the noise of the world: "Room 9's piano was tuned in September. Work order came through my office. I signed it. I never asked myself why until this week." And then he was gone up the stairs, and I stood alone in the basement with the mop dripping onto the concrete, understanding that I had been seen — not caught, which would have been simpler, but *seen*, which was infinitely worse, and infinitely more dangerous, and something else besides, something I would not let myself name until much later.

By Monday the whole conservatory knew. By Monday it was no longer a ghost. By Monday, in the atrium and the stairwells and the practice corridors, in the voice of every second-year who had ever heard the name in a history lecture, in the hushed, thrilled, half-disbelieving tone people reserve for the return of the dead: *the ghost prodigy has come back.* They said it like a rumor. They said it like a prayer. They said it in front of me, not knowing, and I smiled the smile and pushed my mop and said nothing, while inside me the buried thing turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, like a man waking in a strange room, to remember its own name.

And on Tuesday, when I came down the back stairs at the end of my shift, there was a note tucked into the handle of my bucket. Heavy paper. A lawyer's handwriting — I recognized it now, the precise, upright, faintly old-fashioned script, because I had seen it on the auction sheet and on the letter that had come with the deed, and my stomach dropped through ten floors before I'd even unfolded it.

It was an invitation. Dinner, it said. At the house on the coast road, Sunday. *I've been cooking your mother's recipes,* Daniel Reyes had written, in that careful hand. *I think it's time we talked about her. I think you've heard the rumors about yourself by now — and I think it's time you heard mine.*
