## 20. Twenty-Two Days

The journalist's name was Ada Kwan, and she typed faster than Wren could think.

They sat in the back room of a café two towns up the coast road, the one with fogged windows and a radio that played nothing after ten. Between them on the table lay the folder: photocopies of the repair invoices, the photograph of the dented fender taken before it was hammered smooth, Ruth Calloway's signed statement dictated into Wren's phone three days ago in a care home that smelled of lavender and disinfectant. And the postcards — all forty of them — scanned and numbered in Ada's neat hand.

"Twenty-two days," Ada said, not looking up. "That's what your lawyer says. After that, the statute closes on the hit-and-run like a door." She tapped the table once. "I can hold the story until then. I can't hold it past."

Wren watched her hands move over the keyboard, quick and certain, and felt something he hadn't expected to feel: terror. Not of exposure — he had lived inside exposure his whole life, the town's eyes on his family like weather. He was terrified of the arithmetic. Every day the story sat unfinished was a day Daniel's father breathed easier; every day it ran early was a day the old man might die before anyone said his name aloud in a courtroom.

"You keep looking at the folder like it's going to bite you," Ada said.

"It already did." He turned his coffee cup a quarter turn. "It bit everyone who touched it. My mother most of all."

Ada stopped typing. She was maybe fifty, gray at the temples, with the patient stillness of someone who had waited out a great many liars. "Tell me about her. Not the case. Her."

So he told her about the postcards. How Lin Zhou had written forty letters over eleven years to a woman named R., never mailing a single one, keeping them in a biscuit tin beneath the floorboards of a house that no longer belonged to anyone in his family. How each card began *Dear Ruth* and ended *burn this*, and how Ruth had kept every instruction unkept.

"She wrote them because she couldn't say any of it out loud," Wren said. "Not even to herself. Paper let her lie a little less."

"And now you're going to make the whole town read them."

"If we go through with it."

Ada leaned back. "You've been saying 'if' for a week, Mr. Zhou. At some point 'if' becomes the story."

He knew. God, he knew. The deadline wasn't just legal; it was moral, and it ticked in his chest at night like a second heart.

* * *

Daniel found his father sitting up in bed with the television muted, watching the numbers scroll.

The hospice bed had been moved to the front room so Emilio Reyes could see the street, and he watched it now the way he had once watched job sites — appraising, proprietary, as if the passing cars were bids he'd already won. He was thinner than the house remembered him. His hands, which had built half the marinas on this coast, lay folded on the blanket like tools put away for winter.

"They found the records," Daniel said.

No preamble. He had promised himself no more preambles, no more circling, no more of the long diplomatic approaches he'd learned at his father's knee. He stood in the doorway holding the folder against his ribs like a shield held backward.

His father didn't flinch. That was the terrible thing — he didn't flinch. He only turned the television off with the remote he still kept under his pillow, and looked at his son with eyes gone pale as sea glass.

"The mechanic's son kept everything," Emilio said. "I always wondered about that boy. Quiet. Watched me too closely when I paid him." A breath that cost him something. "Your mother's friend, too. The Chinese woman. Ruth."

"Calloway."

"I know her name, Daniel. I've known her name for twenty years." He said it without shame, and Daniel felt the floor tilt slightly, some load-bearing wall of his childhood giving way. "What do you want from me? A confession? I'm dying. The doctors gave me till spring, and spring here is a rumor."

"I want to know what you want *me* to do."

And there it was — the question underneath every question of Daniel's life. What did his father want him to do. Which school. Which firm. Which lie, delivered at fourteen from a witness stand in a voice that cracked on the word *saw*, coached line by line across the kitchen table while his mother made dinner one room away and pretended not to hear.

Emilio Reyes was quiet for a long time. Outside, a gull screamed once and quit.

"Let the dead stay dead," he said finally. "That's what I want. You take that folder, you burn it, and you live your life. The Zhou woman is dead. Nothing you do brings her back. But if it comes out—" his voice caught, not with grief, Daniel thought, but with the effort of calculation even now, even here— "it comes out on my name. Mine alone. Your mother didn't know. You were a child. There's no reason it has to touch either of you."

"There's a boy," Daniel said. "Wren. He lost his mother to your silence. He spent ten years hating the wrong person because I stood up in a courtroom and lied."

"You were fourteen."

"I was fourteen and you wrote my lines."

His father closed his eyes. For a moment — just a moment — Daniel saw something move behind them that was not calculation. Something older and more frightened, the face of a man who had driven away from a body on a wet road at night and spent twenty years driving.

"I know what I did," Emilio said softly. "I've known every day. You think the money bought me peace? It bought me a house full of rooms I couldn't sit in." He opened his eyes. "But peace isn't justice, mijo. Peace is what's left when justice would only break more things. Let it be. Please." And then, quieter, almost inaudible: "I'm asking you. As your father. One last thing."

Daniel stood there with the folder against his ribs and understood that this was the real auction. Not Lot 47, not the house on the bluff — this. His father bidding everything he had left, which was almost nothing, which was only the word *please* spoken by a dying man who had never once asked anyone for anything.

"I have to think," Daniel said, and left before his father could see what thinking was costing him.

* * *

Twenty-two days became nineteen.

Wren counted them the way he had once counted measures of rest in a concerto — silently, precisely, feeling each one pass through him and close behind. He walked the coast road at dusk now, out past the auctioned house, past the bluff where the gulls hung motionless in the wind, and tried to imagine both futures as if they were keys he could test with one finger.

In one future, the story ran. Ada Kwan's byline, the invoices reproduced in gray newsprint, the postcards quoted in sequence like a psalm. His mother's name cleared in the only court that still had jurisdiction — public memory. And Daniel's father dying with the truth read aloud over his bed, and Daniel's mother — Elena, who had waved to Wren from gardens all his childhood, who had once given him lemonade on a hot July afternoon with hands that smelled of basil — Elena learning, at sixty-one, that her whole marriage had been built on a drowned child's worth of silence. And the town, the small wet town that had whispered *Zhou boy, Zhou family, that family* for a decade, learning it had convicted the wrong ghost.

In the other future, the folder burned. His father's name stayed clean. Daniel carried it, whatever *it* was, for the rest of his life — and Wren carried the knowing, and Ruth carried the knowing, and the dead woman in the ground outside town carried it best of all, silent forever, having paid in full for a debt no one ever billed her for.

Mercy, Wren thought, walking. They keep telling me mercy is the gentle option. Nobody tells you mercy is also a sentence. Somebody serves it. It's just usually not the one who grants it.

His phone buzzed. Daniel.

*"Can you come tomorrow. He's asking for you."*

Wren stood very still on the road. Below, the tide was going out, peeling the water off the sand in long silver sheets, exposing everything the sea had hidden all day — stones, kelp, the white curved bones of something that had once been alive.

"Why?" he typed. Then deleted it. Then typed again: *"Why does he want me?"*

The reply came fast, as if Daniel had been holding it: *"He won't say. He's lucid today. These days are running out — the doctors say weeks, maybe days. He wants to talk to you before—"*

Before. Everyone spoke in befores now.

Wren looked down at the exposed beach, at all that revealed and glistening wreckage, and felt the nineteen days compress around him like a closing fist.

"Yes," he sent. Then, after a moment: *"Daniel. Whatever happens in that room — the story runs or it doesn't — I need you to know I don't hate you anymore. I haven't for a while. I just needed to say it where you could read it."*

Three dots appeared. Vanished. Appeared again.

*"Come at four,"* Daniel wrote. *"He's sharpest at four."*

* * *

Daniel met him at the gate.

They had gotten good at this — the meetings, the silences, the careful loading and unloading of words between them. Standing side by side at the low gate below the house, they might have been old friends, or brothers, or the two halves of one ruined summer finally introduced to each other. The wind came off the water smelling of salt and cold iron.

"He rehearsed it," Daniel said quietly. "Whatever he's going to say to you. I heard him practicing last night, talking to himself when he thought I'd left. Same phrase over and over." He hesitated. "I couldn't make out the words."

"Does he know about the story?"

"He knows the clock's running. Ada called the house yesterday — I gave her the number, I'm done hiding it. He took the call himself." Daniel's jaw worked. "Twenty minutes. When he hung up he asked for a glass of water and then he asked for you, and those are the only two requests he's made all week that weren't about painkillers."

Inside, the house smelled of Wren's childhood — woodsmoke and eucalyptus and, faintly, impossibly, the lemon polish Aunt Mei had used on the banister he now passed with his hand trailing along it. Time in this house did not move forward. It pooled.

His father-in-everything-but-law — no. Not that. Never that. The man in the bed was smaller than Wren remembered, and the remembering was itself strange, because Wren had spent ten years refusing to remember him at all, and now here was the whole man, shrunk to a husk in hospice linen, propped against pillows, watching the door with eyes that were waiting for something.

"Wren Zhou," Emilio Reyes said. His voice was gravel poured slow. "You got tall."

"You got honest," Wren said. "Or so I'm told. Briefly. On Tuesdays."

Something flickered across the dying face — surprise, then, unbelievably, amusement. "Ruth talks to you straight, doesn't she. She always talked straight. That's why I never let your mother visit her without an excuse prepared." He gestured at the chair beside the bed. "Sit. I don't have the wind for standing and neither do you, by the look of you. You sleep?"

"No."

"Good. Honest men don't." He drew a breath, and Wren saw him gather himself the way a man gathers a heavy tool, testing its weight. "Twenty-two days. Nineteen now. I want to make you an offer before the newspaper makes it for me."

"I don't want your money."

"Everybody wants my money. It's the only language I ever taught this town to speak in." The pale eyes held him. "But that's not the offer. Here's the offer. I'll sign a statement. Full confession, dated, witnessed, admissible. Everything — the road, the car, the girl on the bicycle, the years after. I'll sign it and your journalist can print it the hour I'm dead. You get the truth. The record gets corrected. My name gets dragged exactly as far as it deserves." A pause, thin as paper. "And my wife and my son get to bury me first. They get one funeral where the ground hasn't been salted. That's the trade, boy. Truth on delay. Justice with a burial attached."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere downstairs a clock Wren had grown up under counted its steady dry seconds, indifferent as surf.

"And if I say no?" Wren asked. "If the story runs Thursday?"

Emilio Reyes smiled. It was not a cruel smile. That was the worst part — it was tired, and human, and almost kind. "Then you get your justice while I'm alive to choke on it, and Daniel stands at my bedside reading the headlines, and Elena learns what she married, and you learn what it costs to be right." He lifted one ruined hand, let it fall. "I did the math twenty years ago, Wren. Your mother did it better. Ask yourself why she chose the way she chose. She wasn't protecting me. She was doing exactly what I'm asking you to do — deciding how much truth the living can carry, and putting the rest of the weight on herself."

Wren stood. His legs felt borrowed.

"I'll give you my answer in nineteen days," he said. "Same as everyone else."

At the door he paused. Behind him the dying man watched the street again, and Wren understood suddenly, with a clarity that felt like cold water, what phrase Emilio Reyes had been rehearsing alone in the dark — because he had seen the man's lips move as he turned away, shaping it one last time, soundless, practiced, ready:

*I'm sorry.*

Whether it was meant for Wren, or for the girl on the bicycle, or for the son on the stairs — that, the old man would take with him, unless someone made him spend it while he still had breath to spend.

Outside, Daniel was waiting by the gate, and the light was failing, and somewhere up the coast road Ada Kwan's cursor blinked in an empty document titled ZHOU — FINAL, nineteen days from the door that was already beginning to swing shut.
