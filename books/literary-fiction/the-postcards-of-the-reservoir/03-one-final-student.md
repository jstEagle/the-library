## 3. One Final Student

The reservoir road was the kind of road that existed to be regretted. It switchbacked down from the estate in a series of apologies—gravel, then dirt, then something geologists would call "surface" and everyone else would call "a rumor of a road"—until the trees opened up and there it was: the water my family had made, lying flat and silver under a sky that couldn't decide whether to rain.

I had dressed for an interview. Blazer, sensible shoes, notebook with three prepared questions written out in my most professional handwriting, which is to say handwriting pretending it hadn't been taught by nuns. I was Lena Brightwater, or Brightwater-Lockridge on the good stationery, and I was going to conduct an oral history with the last speaker of a dying language, and I would be respectful and thorough and home by four.

That was the plan. The plan survived approximately eleven seconds past the cottage gate.

* * *

The cottage sat at the reservoir's eastern edge, low-roofed and stone, with a garden that had clearly stopped taking orders from anyone decades ago. Rosemary gone feral. A fig tree leaning like a drunk against the wall. And everywhere, everywhere, water in view—the whole gray-green sheet of it, held back by the dam at the far end, my great-grandfather's concrete testament to the idea that nothing wild should be allowed to simply *be* where profit could stand instead.

Ivo Marchetti was outside, splitting kindling.

He was smaller than I'd expected, which sounds condescending but isn't meant to be. Photographs of him existed—there was one in the company's centennial brochure, taken thirty years ago, a lean man in a canvas jacket standing at the spillway looking like he personally resented every drop that passed through it. The man in front of me now was seventy-some years folded into a cardigan, hands like tree roots, white hair cut short as if long hair were a luxury he'd voted against. He split wood the way some people pray: rhythmically, without hurry, without any indication that I existed.

"Mr. Marchetti," I said. "My name is Lena Brightwater. I'm Sera Brightwater's daughter."

The axe came down. The kindling parted. He set the next piece upright with maddening calm.

"I know who you are," he said. His English was accented but precise, each word placed like a stone in a wall. "You have her chin. And her way of standing on gravel like it insulted you."

I looked down. My feet were, in fact, planted in a stance of mild grievance. "I was hoping to ask you some questions. About Kessari. About—" I gestured vaguely at the water, which covered a valley, which had covered his village, and felt the gesture die halfway through like everything else I'd tried to say since the funeral.

"No," he said.

"No?"

"You want to interview me." He said the word the way you'd say *mildew*. "You will write it down. Put it in a book maybe. A paper. People will read it at breakfast and say, how sad, and turn the page to the weather." He stood the axe against the chopping block and finally looked at me. His eyes were brown and unimpressed. "Your mother did not come here for interviews. Sit down anyway. There is coffee, if you drink it like a person and not like an American."

"I'm not American."

"Worse. You're a Brightwater." But he said it almost gently, the way you'd note a scar someone was born with. "Come."

* * *

The inside of the cottage smelled of woodsmoke, coffee, and paper—old paper, the sweet dusty smell of a life kept in documents. Every wall had shelves. Not books, mostly. Boxes. Folders. Postcards rubber-banded into bundles like sheaves of wheat. I recognized the shape of the collection before I understood it, because I'd spent ten days cataloguing forty years of the same thing in my mother's locked desk.

"You've seen them already," Ivo said, watching my face. "Her postcards. The ones she never answered."

"How do you know about those?"

"Because half of them are mine." He poured coffee into two cups that didn't match, which somehow felt more honest than matching cups would have. "She sent me one every year on the fourth of March. Forty-one of them. I answered none." He handed me a cup. "So we are even, your mother and I. Two people who talked to each other for forty years without ever speaking."

I sat across from him at a table scarred with rings and knife-marks and what might have been a burn in the shape of Italy. "Why didn't you answer?"

"Why didn't she?" He smiled, and it was not a warm smile; it was the smile of a man holding a door shut against something. "You first. You came all this way down a bad road in shoes that cost more than my roof. Why?"

The prepared questions sat in my bag like dead birds. I left them there. "Because after she died I found a desk full of postcards she never answered, and I realized I didn't know anything about her. Actual anything. I knew her schedule. I knew her opinions about other people's gardens. I didn't know—" My voice did something embarrassing. I drank coffee to cover it. It was terrible and perfect.

"You didn't know her language," Ivo said quietly.

"That's not literal. She spoke English."

"Everything is literal eventually." He got up, went to a shelf, and pulled down a single postcard—not from a bundle, but separate, kept somewhere particular. He held it a moment before turning it around, and I saw his hand wasn't quite steady, and I understood I was being shown something he had decided, over many years, never to show anyone.

It was a photograph of this same cottage, younger, paint intact. The message side was covered in handwriting I recognized instantly—my mother's small, slanted script, the one I'd watched sign birthday cards and sympathy notes and checks for forty years. Except it wasn't English. The letters were unfamiliar, looping and angular at once, dense as birdtracks, and among them ran accents and marks I'd never seen any alphabet wear.

"She wrote to you," I said. "In Kessari."

"One sentence. Once. After twenty years of silence, one sentence." He set it down between us like a card played face-up. "Read it."

"I can't read it."

"No," he agreed. "No dictionary can read it. I have checked. I own every dictionary of Kessari that exists, which is to say three, two of them bad. This sentence is not in them." He tapped the card once, lightly, as if it might bruise. "There is a word here that exists only because she used it. She made it. Or she remembered it from somewhere no book went. Do you understand what that means? A language dies when its last word is spoken by no one. Your mother invented a word, alone, in a house full of people who loved her wrongly, and mailed it to the only man alive who could have understood it—and then, I think, hoped he wouldn't."

"What does it say?"

He looked at me for a long time. Outside, wind moved across the reservoir and the light on the water shifted like something breathing.

"If I tell you, you will know it and go home and write your article and it will be a fact you own. Facts are cheap. I am old, Miss Brightwater. I am the last one, and when I die Kessari becomes a sound in recordings and a squiggle on gravestones. I do not have time to give away what I know to people who want to *have* it." He leaned forward. "But I have less time than that, if the truth is told, and so I will make you an offer I should not make."

"Which is?"

"I will teach you. Not an interview. Lessons. The language, properly, from the ground up—water words first, because Kessari begins with water the way your family's money does." Something flickered across his face, too fast to name. "And when you can read this sentence yourself—with your own eyes, word by word, understanding why no dictionary holds it—then you will know what it says. Not before."

"That could take years."

"Yes."

"I don't have years. I have an estate full of lawyers and an uncle who calls weekly, and I live six hundred miles away, and I came here to write an article."

"Then write your article." He spread his hands, magnanimous, infuriating. "Tell the world a dying man knows a dying language. Everyone will nod. The page will turn to the weather."

I looked at the postcard. My mother's handwriting, in a tongue I couldn't touch, sent to a man she'd apparently written to every year for four decades without once expecting—or wanting—an answer. Forty-one postcards. I had found perhaps sixty in her desk, from a dozen different hands, all unanswered. She had been having entire silent correspondences. Whole relationships conducted entirely in the negative space of the mail. What kind of woman builds a life out of things she refuses to say?

The kind I'd been grieving without recognizing, I thought. The kind whose silence I'd inherited like the china.

"Why does it matter that I learn it myself?" I asked. "Really. If you just want the sentence known—"

"Because of *how* it will be known." For the first time his voice lost its dryness and showed the bedrock under it. "A sentence like this one can be carried two ways. As a stone you throw, or as a stone you build with. Thrown, it breaks whatever it hits, including the thrower. Built with—it has to be understood from inside, load-bearing, every part of it. You cannot throw what you have labored over. Labor changes the hand." He sat back. "Your mother chose to throw nothing and build nothing. She chose silence, forty years of it, and look what it grew. I will not finish my life teaching her daughter to do the same thing in a prettier font."

The wind pushed at the windows. Somewhere out on the water, a loon made its one insane syllable and quit.

"And if I say yes?" I asked. "What does 'fully' mean, exactly? Because I notice you haven't actually asked me to commit to anything yet. You've just been very dramatic about furniture."

Something moved in his face that on a younger man I'd have called delight. On him it looked more like pain remembering itself as appetite.

"It means you come every week. Rain, grief, lawyers, uncle—all of it. It means homework, and I am a hard grader, and there is no grade. It means when the lessons get to the words for leaving—and they will, early, because Kessari is obsessed with leaving, a language built by people who lived above a flood line—you do not skip class." He picked up the postcard and turned it face-down, and I felt the loss of it like a door closing. "And it means you do not show me to your uncle. Aldous Brightwater called here in the spring, wanting to buy my cooperation for the centennial. I told him the only thing I own is a language, and it is not for sale, and also that his tie was ugly. In Kessari, incidentally. He did not know. That was the best day I had all year."

Despite everything—a dead mother, a ruined plan, coffee like punishment—I laughed. It came out cracked from disuse, but it came out.

"One condition," I said. "Mine."

He raised an eyebrow, an entire conversation in a single muscle.

"When I can finally read it—whatever it says—you let me decide what to do with it. Not you. Me. It's her handwriting. She was my mother before she was your—" I stopped, because I didn't know what to put there, and he watched me stop, and something passed between us that neither of us named: two people standing on either side of the same locked door, each convinced the other had the key.

"Agreed," he said softly. "Now. First lesson. Stand up."

"We're starting now?"

"The water doesn't wait for your calendar, and neither do I." He was already moving to the window, gesturing me toward the gray expanse of the reservoir, his village's grave, my family's fortune, gleaming and still. "Kessari has eleven words for still water. Your dictionaries list four. We begin with the seven they forgot—and with the reason they were forgotten, which is a reason your family should hear with the lights on."

I took out my notebook, crossed out my three dead questions, and turned to a clean page.
