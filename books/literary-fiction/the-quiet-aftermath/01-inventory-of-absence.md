## 1. Inventory of Absence

The house smelled of my mother the way a bell smells of its last ringing—faintly, and only if you stood very still and believed in it. Bergamot and dust, the lemon oil she rubbed into the banister every Sunday for forty years, and beneath all of it the mineral cold of a place that had stopped being lived in and started being kept. I stood in the front hall with my coat still on, holding the estate folder against my chest like something I might be robbed of, and listened to the house not answering me.

Outside, Halloway Bend went on with its afternoon. A truck downshifted on River Road. Someone's dog gave three ceremonial barks and quit. The mill itself I could see from the porch if I leaned far enough—its long brick flank gone the color of an old bruise, windows boarded in a checkerboard pattern, the water tower still standing over everything like a headstone nobody had gotten around to carving. My mother had looked at that mill every day of her life. She had looked at it, I realized now, the way you look at a person you're keeping a secret from.

I had come to settle an estate that consisted, according to the county's appraisal, of a house, a 2004 Corolla, a savings account, and "miscellaneous household contents." The appraisal was not wrong. It was simply incomplete, the way a coroner's report is incomplete. What my mother actually left me was a silence with a floor plan.

So I did what I do. I am, by trade, a keeper of lists—my publisher calls it an editorial temperament; my last boyfriend called it a pathology—and I sat down at her kitchen table with a legal pad and began an inventory. Not of the estate. The estate would take care of itself. I inventoried the questions.

*Item one,* I wrote. *Why did she keep every card I ever sent her, rubber-banded by year, and not one photograph of the two of us together after 1998?*

The kitchen offered no comment. A fly tested the window glass and gave up. I kept writing.

*Item two. Why did she drive to Millbrook every month on the second Tuesday, cash withdrawn in exact increments, when she claimed she never went anywhere?*

I knew this because of the bank statements, which I had collected from the shoebox in her closet with the reverence of an archaeologist and the growing nausea of a daughter. Forty-one years of statements. Forty-one years of small, regular withdrawals, always in cash, always on the second Tuesday, always the same amount, adjusted upward over the decades as if following some private cost-of-living index. Payments, I had thought at first. Debts. But my mother owed no one. She paid her bills the day they arrived. She had told me once, laughing, that credit was for people who expected to be forgiven.

*Item three,* I wrote, and my pen slowed. *What was she afraid of?*

Because that was the question underneath the others, and I had known it since the funeral, since the moment I watched the townspeople of Halloway Bend file past her casket and say, each of them, some version of the same sentence. *She was a good woman. She never said a word against anyone.* They said it with such uniformity, such rehearsed warmth, that it stopped sounding like praise and started sounding like testimony. A eulogy given by forty people in unison is not a eulogy. It is a cover.

* * *

I spent three days in the house before I found the tin.

The first two days I worked the way grief wants you to work: mechanically, room by room, opening drawers and closing them, bagging the obvious trash, setting aside the obvious treasures. Her handwriting appeared everywhere, small and upright and patient—labels on jars, notes in book margins, a grocery list from some abandoned week still pinned under a magnet. *Flour. Yeast. Ask about Tomas.* I read that last line four times before I noticed I'd read it at all. *Ask about Tomas.* No surname. No context. Just the imperative, sitting between flour and yeast like it belonged there, like it was a thing she'd meant to do the way she'd meant to buy flour, and then the week ended, and then the years ended, and she never asked about Tomas, or she asked and never told me, or—

I wrote it down. *Item four. Who is Tomas?*

The house had three bedrooms. Hers, mine—kept made up with a quilt I outgrew at twelve, a museum of a girl who left—and the sewing room, which had never contained a sewing machine in my lifetime. On the third day I emptied the sewing room. Behind the boxes of fabric was the closet, and behind the coats in the closet was a shelf, and on the shelf, wrapped in a pillowcase gone soft as skin, was a tin.

It was an old biscuit tin, the kind that once held shortbread, the kind every household in this valley kept screws and buttons and death certificates in. This one was locked. A small brass padlock, green with age, hung through a hasp that someone had soldered on with more care than skill. I turned the tin over in my hands. It was heavy. It shifted with a dry, papery weight—paper, folded, a great deal of it. I shook it gently, like a gift I was afraid of, and heard the soft collapse of documents settling against documents.

No key. I searched for an hour. I went through her jewelry box, her desk, the flour canister, the pocket of every coat she'd owned since the Nixon administration. I found a 1967 penny, a dental bridge in a matchbox, and a receipt from a motel in Millbrook dated the week after I was born. No key. The key, I understood, had been disposed of with intention. Whatever was in the tin was meant to stay in the tin until it couldn't, and the person who could have told me the difference had been in the ground at Fernhill Cemetery for nine days.

I set the tin on the kitchen table and sat across from it and wrote: *Item five. What did she lock away, and why did she keep the lock where I would find it?*

Because that was the thing I kept circling. The tin was not hidden. It was *placed*. Behind coats, yes—but in a house this small, behind coats is a location, and my mother knew every inch of this house the way a nun knows her cell. She had put it where a daughter emptying a sewing room would find it, and she had locked it so that finding it would not be enough. She had wanted me to hold the question. She had not wanted to answer it. Even from the grave she was choosing her words, and the word she chose was *no*.

* * *

The photograph came out of the tin's vicinity by accident, which is to say I went back to the closet in a state of what I would generously call method and found it slipped inside the pillowcase's other half, flat as a held breath.

It was a photograph of the mill. The east entrance, the one the women used, and I knew that because Edna Pruitt's history of the county—the one in the library, the one I'd been assigned to read in eleventh grade and had skimmed—had a caption about the separate doors, the men's entrance facing town and the women's facing the river, as if the river were the lesser shame. Eleven women stood on the loading steps in the photograph, in aprons and headscarves, squinting into a sun that had set sixty years ago. The image was sepia going to gray, soft at the edges, the kind of photograph that is less a record than a rumor.

And every face had been scratched out.

Not blurred. Not faded. *Scratched*—deliberately, furiously, with something sharp dragged across the emulsion until each face was a white ruin, a static burst where a woman had been. Eleven women. Eleven wounds. The scratches were uneven in their violence: some faces had been worked over until the paper thinned, one had only a single line across the eyes, and one—fourth from the left, a small woman with her hand resting on the shoulder of the girl beside her—had been scratched with such restraint, such terrible precision, that I understood the scratcher had known her, had not been able to bring herself to fully erase her, and had done the minimum required to make her anonymous anyway.

I sat on the floor of the closet with the photograph in my lap for a long time. The light through the window went from afternoon to evening without my noticing. Somewhere in the walls the house ticked and settled, the sounds of a structure keeping its own counsel.

Here is what I knew, and what I wrote, my hand not entirely steady: *Item six. Eleven women at the mill. Eleven faces destroyed. Someone in this house could not bear to look at them, and could not bear to let anyone else look at them either.*

And beneath it: *Item seven. Which one was she?*

Because she had to be one of them. That was the arithmetic I couldn't escape. The photograph had lived in the pillowcase with the tin's wrapping, had been kept, had been *kept*—my mother threw nothing away, not a rubber band, not a birthday card—and yet the faces were gone. You do not scratch out the faces of strangers. You scratch out the faces of the people you knew, the people who might be recognized, the people whose eyes, looking out of a photograph across sixty years, might ask you something you had agreed never to answer.

The mill had killed a man once. I remembered this from Edna Pruitt's book, from the single dry paragraph the collapse received: a wall section, a night shift, one worker dead—Tomas Reyes, 1963, ruled accidental, the town moved on. *Ask about Tomas.* The name on my grocery list and the name in the county history, converging on my kitchen table like two lines of a proof.

I put the photograph face-down on the table. I could not look at the scratches and think at the same time; they were mutually exclusive activities. I made tea I didn't drink. I sat across from the locked tin and the face-down photograph and the legal pad with its seven items, and I understood that I had come to Halloway Bend to close my mother's accounts and had instead opened one.

The house darkened around me. Down the hill, the mill stood in the last light with its boarded eyes, and I thought: *she looked at you every day for forty years, and you never once told me why.*

*Item eight,* I wrote, in the dark, by the streetlight coming through the window. *Find the key.*

And then, because the pen was honest even when I wasn't: *Item nine. Find out why there is no key to find.*

The library opened at ten. I had seen, driving through, a display in the front window—LOCAL HISTORY: HONORING OUR MILL HERITAGE—and beneath the laminated photographs, a small handwritten card offering corrections, *inquire within.* I had not thought about it at the time. I thought about it now, the way you think about a door you passed that you suddenly need to be standing in front of. Someone in this town kept a card in a window asking to be corrected. Someone had been waiting a long time for the record to be wrong out loud.

I gathered the tin, the photograph, the pad. At the door I paused with my hand on the light switch and looked back at the kitchen—my mother's kitchen, where I had eaten ten thousand meals and learned nothing—and said, out loud, to no one, in the tone of a woman beginning a sentence she will spend a book trying to finish:

"Who was Tomas?"

The house kept its silence. But it was, I noticed for the first time, a listening silence.
