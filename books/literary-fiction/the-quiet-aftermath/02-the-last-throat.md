## 2. The Last Throat

The Halloway Bend Public Library occupied the old Carnegie building on Corliss Street, its sandstone face gone the color of wet ash, and I went there on my fourth morning in town because the estate lawyer had told me, in the flat tone of a man reciting a grocery list, that probate would take months and I might as well get comfortable. Comfort was not a word this town understood. The library smelled of cold radiators and newsprint and something older underneath—paper decaying at its own pace, unbothered by anyone's schedule. A card catalog still stood against the east wall, emptied of cards, used now to hold pencils and a dying fern. Nobody had updated anything since roughly the year my mother left.

I had come for the county histories. If I was going to inventory a house full of silence, I wanted at least the official version of things—the minutes, the milestones, the photographs of men in narrow ties standing in front of buildings that no longer stood.

The local history display lived in a glass case near the reference desk, and it was being corrected when I arrived.

The woman doing the correcting must have been past ninety. She was small in the way that very old people become small—not stooped so much as condensed, as if the years had pressed her down into her most essential self—and she wore a man's cardigan over a floral dress and orthopedic shoes the color of oatmeal. She had a pair of reading glasses pushed up into white hair and another pair low on her nose, and she was using a ballpoint pen to write directly on an index card inside the case, her hand steady, her expression one of profound and patient disapproval.

"Ma'am," said the librarian behind the desk, a young man with a beard he hadn't fully grown into, "you can't—"

"I've been correcting this display since before you were born," the woman said, without looking up. "In 1978 they got the date of the flood wrong. In 1984 they spelled Keshwa as *Keshua*, which is not a spelling, it is a wound. Last month they put up a photograph labeled 'Mill Workers, 1962' that was actually taken in 1959. You can tell by the third window on the loading dock. It was bricked over in 1961." She finished writing, capped the pen, and only then turned her head toward me. Her eyes were pale gray, nearly colorless, the eyes of someone who has looked at everything twice. "You're standing where the light is bad for reading. Move two feet to your left."

I moved. I don't know why. There was something about her voice—it didn't request; it assumed compliance the way gravity assumes falling.

"You're the Zhou girl," she said.

It wasn't a question, which unsettled me more than the statement would have. "I'm Mara Zhou."

"Your mother was Lian." She said it like a fact she'd been keeping in a drawer. "She came in here every Thursday for eleven years. Sat at that table"—she pointed with the capped pen at a scarred oak table under the north window—"and read the same three books about estate law, because she was trying to understand what she'd inherited from her own mother, and she never once checked them out. She read them here and put them back, week after week, like penance." The old woman studied me with those colorless eyes. "You have her way of standing. Like you're waiting for a door to open that you're not sure you want to go through."

"I'm settling her estate," I said. "She passed last month."

"I know when she passed. This town knows when anyone passes. It's the only newspaper we have left." She turned back to the display case and tapped the glass with one knuckle, once, precisely. "Look at this photograph. Bottom row, second from the left."

I leaned in. Behind the glass was a black-and-white image of women outside a mill entrance—I recognized the building from the photograph in my mother's tin, though this one was intact, unscribbled, the faces whole. Rows of women in headscarves and canvas aprons, squinting into sun. The caption read: *Textile Workers, Halloway Bend Mill, c. 1960.*

"That's my mother's handwriting on the correction," Edith said. "No. That's mine. But look at the faces."

I looked. And then I saw what I had spent three days telling myself I hadn't seen in my mother's copy: several of the faces were scratched out. Not faded, not damaged—*scratched*, deliberately, with something sharp, long ago, the emulsion gouged away to the paper beneath. Here the wounds were older, softer, but unmistakable. Four faces. Five. I counted twice.

"Why would anyone—"

"Because a photograph is a testimony," the woman said, "and some testimonies get edited." She straightened up with the care of someone negotiating daily with her own skeleton. "Edith Vann. You'll hear my name around town, usually attached to the word 'difficult.'"

"Mara," I said again, uselessly, and then, because the silence demanded filling: "Are you from here? Originally?"

A sound came out of her that might have been a laugh in a younger throat. "I'm from everywhere the mill recruited. My father came over in 1921 with nothing but a trade and a language nobody within four hundred miles spoke. Keshwa." She pronounced it carefully, two syllables, the second one rising slightly, like a question that had decided to be an answer. "There were two hundred speakers in this valley once. Two hundred throats carrying it. Now there is one." She touched her own collarbone lightly, almost formally, the way you'd touch a grave marker. "Mine."

The radiator clanked. Somewhere in the stacks a book fell—or was dropped—and neither of us moved toward the sound.

"The language will die with you," I said. It came out blunter than I intended, the way things did lately; grief had stripped some courtesy layer off me and I kept saying true things in bare rooms.

"Yes," Edith Vann said. "That is the plan everyone else has made for it. I haven't agreed to it yet." She gathered her pen, her spare glasses, a canvas tote bag heavy with books, moving with the economy of someone who wasted nothing, not motion, not words, not time. Then she stopped and looked at me for a long moment, and whatever she saw made her nod slowly, as if confirming a figure in a ledger.

"Your mother owed me a word," she said.

The sentence landed strangely, the way a key lands in a lock you didn't know your house had. "Owed you—what do you mean, a word?"

"A word," she repeated. "One word. In one language. For sixty years she carried the debt and for sixty years she paid everything except that, because paying that would have cost more than money." She shouldered her bag. "You're going to ask me what word. Everyone asks. Your mother asked, at the end, and I told her the same thing I'll tell you: come and learn enough to recognize it, and then you'll know."

"I don't speak—Keshwa. I've never even heard it."

"No," she agreed. "You've heard English your whole life, which is a language very good at hiding things. Keshwa doesn't hide. That's its flaw and its genius. It makes you say who did a thing, who let it happen, and who profited, all in the same breath. There is no passive voice worth the name. There is no 'mistakes were made.' There is no—" she paused, choosing—"there is no word for *accident*. Do you understand what I'm telling you? A language without accidents. Everything that happens, happens because someone acted or someone allowed. Sixty years ago this town needed a word for accident very badly, and it didn't exist, so they borrowed silence instead."

My hands had gone cold around the strap of my bag. The scratched faces swam behind my eyes, superimposed over the ones in the case before me. *Bottom row, second from the left.* One of the erased women had been wearing a scarf patterned in small diamonds—the same pattern, I realized with a lurch, as the fabric remnant I'd found lining my mother's locked tin.

"I'm not staying," I heard myself say. "I'm here for the estate. Weeks, maybe. Then I'm gone."

"Of course," Edith said, entirely unbothered. "Everyone is here temporarily until they aren't. Your mother said the same thing in 1963 and stayed thirty years." She started toward the door, then stopped, and delivered the rest without turning around, to the room rather than to me, as if the offer were too large to be aimed at a single person:

"I am ninety-one years old. I teach no one anymore because there is no one left worth teaching and no time left to waste. But I will teach you. Sounds first, then grammar, then the sentence. Mornings, my kitchen, starting whenever you find the courage. I won't explain why you. When you can hear the word your mother owed me, you'll understand why yourself, and you'll wish you didn't."

The door opened onto Corliss Street and the gray light of the valley, and she went out into it, small and upright, a single throat walking away carrying two hundred voices.

The librarian cleared his throat behind me. "She does that," he said apologetically. "Comes in, fixes things, leaves. Nobody hires her. Nobody stops her either." He hesitated. "You okay? You look like you've seen—"

"A photograph," I said. "Just a photograph."

But I stood there a long while after he went back to his desk, looking through the glass at the women with their edited faces, at the caption with its comfortable lie of a date, and I thought about a language with no word for accident, and a mother who read estate law like penance and paid a debt in anonymous installments, and a locked tin in a dead woman's kitchen waiting for a key that was apparently a word.

I should have walked out. I had a lawyer to call, a house to empty, a life in another city folded up in a suitcase. Every reasonable instinct I owned said: finish the paperwork, ship the furniture, leave this town its silences—they were here before you, they'll be here after.

Instead I wrote in the margin of my inventory notebook, under the entry for the locked tin, a single line:

*Ask the old woman what word.*

And under that, after a moment, in smaller handwriting, almost as if someone else were holding the pen:

*Mornings. Her kitchen.*

* * *

I told myself I'd sleep on it. I told myself a great many things that night, lying in my childhood bed under a ceiling I'd once mapped by glow-star constellations, listening to the house tick and settle around its own absences. The tin sat on the dresser where I'd left it, dull and patient, keeping its counsel.

At six the next morning I gave up on sleep, dressed in the dark, and dug through my mother's desk until I found what I was somehow certain would be there: a page in her address book, in her small vertical handwriting, an address on Weaver Street with no name attached, just a number and the letters *E.V.* and, beside it, in ink slightly fresher than the rest, a note that stopped my breath:

*Thursdays.*

The same day, every week, for eleven years, my mother had sat in a library reading law books she refused to check out. And somewhere in that same ledger of habit, in handwriting she'd thought no one would ever audit, was this address, and this day of the week, and no explanation at all.

The house on Weaver Street was narrow and blue-painted, with a garden gone gloriously feral and a wood stove pipe breathing thin smoke into the cold air. Edith Vann opened the door before I knocked, as if she'd felt my hesitation coming up the walk.

"You took less time than your mother," she said. "Eleven years, and she never crossed this threshold. Come in. We start with sounds."

And that was how, on the fifth morning of a visit meant to last weeks, I sat down at a stranger's kitchen table to learn a dying language—one word at a time, one debt at a time—with a locked tin waiting back at the house and a photograph of five erased women burning behind my eyes.

Edith set a battered notebook in front of me, its pages soft as cloth, filled edge to edge with her handwriting in two scripts. She opened it to the first blank page, uncapped her pen, and drew a single line down the middle.

"On the left," she said, "we write what it looks like. On the right, we write what it means. Most days, they will not match."
