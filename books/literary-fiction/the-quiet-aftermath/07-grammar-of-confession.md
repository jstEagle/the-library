## 7. Grammar of Confession

Edith Vann had taught children once, in the years when the mill still ran and the company school allowed Keshwa only in the last ten minutes of Friday, a reward for good behavior, as if a language were a sweet to be rationed. She had forgotten how it felt to watch a mouth learn. Now, watching Mara Zhou's mouth struggle around the vowel that lived between *ah* and *uh*—the one Edith's own mother had said was the sound of a door opening onto weather—she remembered, and the remembering hurt in the old way, like pressing on a bruise to prove it was still hers.

"Again," she said.

Mara tried again. The vowel came out flattened, Americanized, apologetic.

"No. You are sorry for making the sound. Don't be sorry. The sound doesn't care if you're sorry."

"That's not a thing you can say about a sound."

"In Keshwa you can." Edith set down her tea. The kitchen smelled of cardamom and the dust of old paper; she had been sorting her papers again, though there was no longer any point, since everything worth keeping would soon belong to this woman across the table. "In Keshwa, sounds have obligations. That vowel is obligated to open. If you close it, you've broken a promise to whoever is listening."

Mara wrote something in her notebook. She had filled two notebooks already, her handwriting small and upright, a notary's daughter's handwriting even if she didn't know it yet. Edith watched the pen move and felt the sentence stir inside her chest where it had lived for sixty years, coiled like something hibernating, waking now because winter was ending whether she wanted it to or not.

* * *

They had been at the final sentence for three days.

Not learning it—Edith refused to give it whole until Mara could hold its pieces—but circling it, the way you circle a wound before cleaning it. The sentence was eleven words long. Eleven words, and Edith had carried them up out of the wreckage of 1963 and kept them alive through six decades of English, through funerals and grant applications and the slow erosion of everyone else who had ever spoken them aloud. Eleven words. Some nights she recited them to the ceiling just to feel her throat do the work, the way a swimmer treads water in the dark to remember which way is up.

Today she began with the grammar, because the grammar was the confession.

"You know by now we don't mark time the way English does," she said. "No past tense sitting on top of the verb like a hat. We mark obligation instead. Who was bound to act. Who was bound to allow. Who received the benefit of what was done or not done."

"I know. *-sha*, the actor bound by duty. *-ven*, the one whose permission was required. *-tai*—" Mara hesitated.

"Say it."

"*-tai*. The one who benefited."

"And every verb must carry all three, or the sentence is considered unfinished. A lie of omission. There's no word in Keshwa for leaving someone out politely." Edith folded her hands on the table. Her knuckles were enormous now, the knuckles of a woman who had wrung sixty years of washwater and grief through the same joints. "English lets you say *the mill collapsed*. Beautiful little sentence. Nobody in it. Nobody acted, nobody allowed, nobody benefited. In Keshwa that sentence cannot exist. It would be like a door with no house around it."

Mara looked down at her notebook. "The town says accident."

"The town says many things in unison. You've noticed."

"I've noticed." Mara's pen hovered. "Edith. Why does the language work this way? Most languages don't."

"Because we were weavers." Edith rose, went to the window where the light came through thin curtains the color of weak tea. Outside, October was taking the leaves off the maple in her yard one argument at a time. "A cloth shows every hand that touched it. Pull one thread wrong and the whole weave records it—not the thread's fault, mind you, but the record doesn't care about fault, only about what happened and who did what. Our grandmothers wove that into the grammar. Every action is a cloth. Someone made it. Someone let it be made. Someone wore it warm while another went cold." She turned back. "You cannot speak honestly in Keshwa without distributing responsibility. That is why they let the language die, Mara. Not because it was useless. Because it was accurate."

The clock in the hall ticked the way old clocks do, reluctantly, like a witness being persuaded.

"Now," Edith said, and sat again, and felt her heart begin its familiar uneven drumming, "I will teach you the frame of the sentence. Not the words yet. The bones."

She spoke slowly, syllable by syllable, and Mara transcribed the sounds without understanding them, which was correct, which was how Edith herself had learned scripture from her mother: first the body memorizes, then the mind is permitted to follow.

"There. Eleven words. Three verbs. Each verb carries all three marks of responsibility." She tapped the page. "Read me the first verb's endings."

Mara sounded them out. "*-sha-ven-tai.*"

"Actor, permitter, beneficiary. Good. Now the second."

"*-sha-ven-tai.*"

"The same. And the third?"

Mara looked up. For the first time since the lessons began, Edith saw fear move across the girl's face like weather over water.

"They're the same," Mara said. "All three verbs. Same actor, same permitter, same beneficiary."

"Yes."

"But that means—" Mara stopped. Her pen had gone still. "One person did it. One person allowed it. One person profited. And it's the same person in all three places."

"It is a strange sentence," Edith agreed mildly. "Most confessions spread the weight. This one gathers it."

* * *

That night Edith did not sleep well, which was normal now, sleep having become a country she could visit but not live in. She lay listening to the house settle and thought about Lena Zhou, dead three weeks, and about the tin in Mara's mother's closet that Edith had never seen but knew existed the way you know a scar is under someone's sleeve.

She had met Lena Zhou exactly twice. Once in 1964, in the county clerk's back office, where the girl—young, careful, newly hired—had slid the false testimony across the desk for filing and would not meet Edith's eyes. Edith had translated the workers' words into that harmless English herself; that was her sin, the original one, the load-bearing wall of all her guilt. But the girl had taken the pages, stamped them, filed them, and said nothing. Sixty years of silence, notarized.

And once more, thirty years later, outside the pharmacy, when Lena Zhou had pressed an envelope into Edith's hands—money for the Reyes family, the eleventh such envelope—and whispered, in Keshwa so badly pronounced it broke Edith's heart precisely because of its badness: *"I am paying. I will always be paying."*

Using the obligation-marked form. *-sha.* The actor bound by duty. The girl had learned enough Keshwa from the mill families to know that in this language you could not pay anonymously, grammatically. Whoever spoke, confessed. So she had confessed, once, badly, in a parking lot, and then spent the rest of her life confessing in money instead of words.

Edith had never told Mara any of this. She had only said, that first day at the library: *Your mother owed me a word.*

Now the word was coming due, and it was going to land on the daughter like an inheritance, which was exactly what it was.

* * *

On the fourth day, Mara arrived early. Edith heard her car—the careful speed, the engine that needed a valve job—and had the tea already poured, because she had known, the way teachers know, that the question had been forming in the girl all night like frost on glass.

"Who is it?" Mara asked, standing in the doorway, not sitting. She held her notebook against her chest like a shield. "The sentence. I worked the morphology all night. One actor, one permitter, one beneficiary, constant across all three verbs. And I checked the noun cases you gave us in September—the animate human class, the ones used for people who are named. The actor marker agrees with a named person. Someone specific. Someone you know." Her voice thinned. "Someone I might know."

Edith poured the second cup anyway. Steam rose between them.

"Sit down, child."

"Tell me who the sentence is about."

"The sentence is about Tomas Reyes," Edith said. "It has always been about Tomas Reyes. It says what happened in the east stairwell on the ninth of March, 1963, and it says it in a language that refuses to let anyone hide. It names the man who ordered the supports removed to save the morning's quota. It names the man above him who signed the order knowing. It names the men below who stayed silent for their wages. It names the town that chose deafness. It names—" and here her voice did what voices do at ninety-one, caught on a splinter of itself, "—it names the clerk who filed the lie and made it legal."

The kitchen was very quiet. Somewhere outside a crow argued with October.

"My mother," Mara said. It was not a question. It was a stone dropped into a well, and they both listened for the bottom.

"She was nineteen," Edith said. "She was a clerk. She did what her employer told her, and then she spent forty years doing the opposite, secretly, in envelopes, in a language she barely had. I am not telling you she was innocent. In Keshwa there is no innocent. I am telling you she was *-sha-ven-tai*: she acted, she permitted, she benefited—her benefit being the smallest and strangest of the three, which was the right to keep working in a town that would have destroyed her otherwise. And then she paid. Oh, how she paid." Edith pushed the cup toward the girl. "Drink your tea. Your hands are shaking."

Mara sat. She did not drink. She opened her notebook to the page with the eleven words transcribed in phonetics, sounds she could make but not yet mean, and stared at them as if the ink might rearrange itself into mercy.

"If I translate it," she said finally, "fully, the way you're teaching me—with all three names, all three burdens—then translating it indicts her. There's no version where it doesn't. The grammar won't allow it. You built the grammar so it wouldn't allow it."

"I didn't build the grammar," Edith said. "I just refused to lie in it. There's a difference, and the difference is my whole life."

"And if I don't translate it—if I let it die with you—"

"Then Tomas Reyes stays a footnote in a display case at the library, corrected by an old woman nobody asked to fix anything. And your mother stays a kind woman who sent anonymous checks. Both true. Neither whole." Edith reached across the table and closed the girl's notebook gently, under her own hand. "Love and indictment wear the same coat in this language, Mara. You put on one, you're wearing both. That is the last thing I can teach you, and I can't teach it. You have to catch it, like a fever."

Mara looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious and grieving all at once, and Edith thought: *yes, this one, this is the throat, this is where the language goes next.*

"When do you teach me the actual words?" Mara asked quietly.

Edith looked out the window, at the maple nearly bare, at the light going the color of old brass.

"Soon," she said. "When I'm sure you'll carry it the rest of your life. However long either of us has."

But that night, alone, she took out the small tin of her own—different from Lena Zhou's, older, dented—and held it in her lap without opening it, and her heart stuttered twice and steadied, and she understood that soon was a promise she might not have time to keep.
