## 17. Auction Day, Morning

I did not sleep. I want to say that honestly, at the start, because everything that happened afterward happened on top of that sleeplessness the way a house is built on bad soil—everything leaning, everything slightly wrong, and yet standing.

The night before the auction I sat in my apartment with the ledger open on the kitchen table and added the numbers again, as if arithmetic were a prayer that might be answered differently on the eleventh recitation. Fourteen thousand two hundred and sixty dollars. Fifteen years of night shifts, of lunches eaten standing over the sink, of shoes worn until the soles went thin as paper. It was not nothing. It was also not enough. The opening bid alone was listed at eighteen thousand, and Corliss Vance's people had been seen walking Marrow Lane with clipboards for two weeks, measuring the lots like men sizing up a body for a coffin.

At some point past three—the hour I knew best, the blue hour, the hour that had been mine alone for fifteen years—I stopped adding and just sat there with my hands flat on the table, listening to the building's pipes tick as they cooled. My mother used to sit like that, I realized. Hands flat on a table, staring at nothing, when she thought no one was watching. I had inherited her posture the way other people inherit chin shapes or bad knees.

I showered. I shaved with the care of a man preparing for his own sentencing. I put on the only jacket I owned that could be called respectable, the gray wool one Odile had once said made me look like "a substitute teacher who has given up," and then I took it off and put it back on twice more before leaving.

The courthouse was on Calloway Street, eleven blocks from the Halcyon Branch, and I walked because I could not bear the idea of sitting still in a bus while the city carried me toward the thing I was afraid of. Walking at least let me pretend I was choosing. That is what I have done all my life, I think—chosen small movements so I would never have to admit how much of my life was flight.

* * *

The crowd gathered on the courthouse steps the way weather gathers: without malice, without intention, simply because conditions were right. By nine-thirty there must have been eighty people, maybe a hundred, clustered in the pale October light. Investors in quilted vests. A woman in a red coat who talked too loudly about cap rates. Two young couples clutching pre-approval letters like boarding passes. Men with the particular tan of people who golf with clients. And scattered among them, quieter, the desperate ones—you could always tell the desperate ones, because we stood slightly apart from our own bodies, the way you do at funerals, watching ourselves from a few feet back.

A clerk in a green cardigan set up a folding table at the top of the steps and arranged a shoebox of numbered paddles beside a coffee urn that steamed like something wounded. Number 47. That was mine. I had registered the week before, in a fit of resolve that now felt like it belonged to a different man, a braver man, a man who had not spent the intervening days rehearsing his own retreat.

My hands shook holding the paddle. Not a fine tremor—a real shake, the kind you cannot talk your way out of. I put it under my arm and crossed my arms over it, so that to anyone looking I was merely cold.

Vance's agents moved through the crowd like weather fronts. You could feel them coming before you saw them—a shift in the air, conversations dipping, heads turning. There were four of them, dressed in variations of the same charcoal suit, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who already knew the outcome and were present only for the paperwork. One of them, a broad-shouldered man with a voice like gravel poured slowly, stood near the front and worked the crowd in a low murmur. I caught fragments as he drifted past where I stood on the periphery.

"Vance Development's got the whole east side of the block already... only makes sense to go together... individual buyers get eaten alive in these things..."

He wasn't lying, exactly. That was the craft of it. Every sentence was technically true and entirely poisonous, the way my father's assurances had been. *This is for us, Ves. This is so we'll be secure.* Words shaped like shelter that were actually walls.

The agent reached the woman in the red coat and she laughed at something he said, a bright carrying laugh, and I understood that he had already won her, or she had already decided to lose, and the auction hadn't even begun.

I looked down the length of Calloway Street toward the river and calculated, with the cold precision available only to the terrified, exactly how long it would take me to walk home. Twenty minutes. Maybe fifteen if I abandoned dignity. No one would know. No one was watching.

That was the thought that undid me—not the money, not Vance, not the paddle trembling under my arm like a broken wing—but the word *no one*. Because it was the word I had built my whole life inside. No one knows where I go at night. No one has my number memorized. No one would notice if Vesper Vane simply folded himself back into the closed stacks and disappeared the way he'd been disappearing for fifteen years. The retreat wouldn't cost me anything, because I had already paid everything; I was already nobody, already unread, already shelved in the dark where no hand would reach for me.

I turned to go.

* * *

"Forty-seven."

Odile was standing beside me. She had not been standing beside me a moment ago—or perhaps she had, and I had been too far inside my own skull to notice, which was more likely and more damning. She wore her good coat, the black wool one with the missing button she'd never replaced, and she held two coffees from the cart on the corner, and she handed me one as though we had arranged this, as though it were the most ordinary thing in the world for her to appear on the courthouse steps at nine-forty on a Tuesday morning.

"How did you—" I started.

"You told me the date. Three weeks ago. You told me the date and then you pretended you hadn't." She blew across the lid of her cup. "You tell yourself you're very hard to read, Vesper. You're not. You're the easiest read in the building. You alphabetize your grief."

"I'm not doing this," I said. "It's not—it was never realistic. The numbers don't work. They were never going to work."

"No," she agreed, which startled me. She didn't argue. She looked out at the crowd, at the agents in their charcoal weather, at the clerk arranging his paddles, and she sipped her coffee with the patience of someone who had all morning and intended to use it. "The numbers don't work. You're going to lose this auction. Probably in the first five minutes. Vance will open high and close it fast and by lunchtime they'll have a sign on the lawn with a rendering of six identical townhouses where your mother's garden used to be."

Every word landed like a stone dropped into still water. I waited for her to take it back. She didn't.

"So why are you here?" I asked.

"Because losing here is different from losing at home." She turned and looked at me then, fully, the way she looked at patrons who came to the reference desk with questions they were ashamed of—without pity, without flinching, with a kind of fierce professional attention that said *your question deserves an answer, whatever it is.* "You've lost this house a hundred times, Vesper. In your head, every night, for fifteen years. Alone, in the dark, where nobody sees. If you walk away today, it's the same loss, the hundred-and-first, and it costs you nothing and changes nothing and you go back to the stacks tonight and shelve somebody else's returned books and pretend the blue hour belongs to you." She paused. "But if you stand here—if you stand on these steps with a number in your hand and let them see you try—then losing becomes a thing that happened to you instead of a thing you are."

The clerk tested the microphone. Feedback squealed down Calloway Street and a pigeon exploded off the courthouse cornice.

"My hands are shaking," I said. It sounded childish. I meant it as confession.

"I can see that."

"I nearly walked away just now."

"I know. I watched you turn." She said it without accusation, which somehow made it worse and better at once. "I stood behind you the whole time. I figured you'd either leave or you wouldn't, and either way I wanted you to know someone saw which one it was."

I looked at her. Small, sharp Odile, who catalogued the world's damage with dry precision and kept her own locked in a drawer she never opened, standing on the cold steps of a courthouse at ten in the morning for no reason that served her at all.

"Why?" I asked.

She considered the question seriously, the way she considered everything, turning it over like a book whose condition she was assessing.

"Because when my mother died," she said, "I stood outside her building for two hours and couldn't make myself go up. And there was a neighbor, an old man with a dog, who didn't say anything helpful. He just stood there too. And after a while I went up." She shrugged. "I've been trying to return the favor ever since. Mostly to strangers. You're the first one who asked."

The clerk raised his hand. The crowd began to press forward up the steps, funneling through the doors, and the agents converged at the front like iron filings finding their magnet, and somewhere beneath my ribs my heart was going so fast I could feel it in my teeth.

"The numbers don't work," I said again, but it came out differently this time. Less like surrender. More like a man checking the weather before sailing anyway.

"No," Odile said. "So stop doing math and start doing the other thing."

"What other thing?"

"The thing you came here to do." She nodded at the paddle under my arm. "Hold it up."

* * *

Inside, the courtroom smelled of dust and floor wax and old decisions. The folding chairs filled row by row, and I took a seat near the back because the back is where I have always lived, but Odile steered me sideways, into the aisle seat of the third row, saying only, "You'll want to see the auctioneer's face. Everything's in the face."

The judge's bench sat empty; this was no court, merely a room borrowed for the disposition of debts. At the front, the auctioneer—a compact man with a voice like a well-tuned engine—reviewed his papers while the clerk circulated with the final notices. On the wall behind the podium hung the county seal, its Latin motto half-eroded by a century of boiler steam, and beneath it, propped on an easel, a photograph of the property.

14 Marrow Lane.

I had prepared myself for many things, but not for that. Not for seeing it here, enlarged and glossy, rendered official: the sagging porch, the boarded windows, the scarred back wall visible even from this angle like a burn scar on a face turned away. Someone had photographed it in late summer light, and the light was kind, and the kindness was unbearable. It looked like what it was. A house waiting to find out who it belonged to.

"That's yours," Odile said quietly. Not a question. Not quite a statement either. Something between—an anchor dropped.

"It was," I said. "Once."

"It still is. That's the whole problem with houses." She folded her hands in her lap. "They keep belonging to you whether you claim them or not. Might as well claim them."

The auctioneer stepped to the podium. The room settled into that hush that precedes all irreversible things. He explained the terms in a bored, practiced cadence—cash or certified funds, thirty days to close, sold as-is where-is, no warranties expressed or implied—and each phrase fell on the room like a gavel tap. Sold as-is. Where-is. As I was. Where I was.

"Opening bid," the auctioneer said, "eighteen thousand dollars. Do I hear eighteen?"

And the room, which had been a room full of separate private terrors, became suddenly a single animal. Paddles rose along the front rows like a field of pale flowers opening at once. The woman in the red coat. The quilted vests. The gravel-voiced agent, bidding with lazy authority on behalf of a principal who was not required to show his face.

"Nineteen. Nineteen-five. Twenty. Twenty-two..."

The numbers climbed with terrible smoothness. I sat with paddle 47 face-down on my knee and my heart slamming against the paddle's edge, and I listened to the price of my childhood rise beyond my reach dollar by dollar, and part of me—the old part, the shelved part, the part that had survived my father by making itself invisible—whispered *see, see, it was never possible, you can sit back down now, you can go home.*

Beside me, Odile did not whisper anything. She simply placed her hand flat on the armrest between our chairs, palm up, an offer without words, and left it there.

"Twenty-eight thousand," the auctioneer called. "Twenty-eight. Do I hear twenty-eight-five?"

The gravel-voiced agent bid. The red coat hesitated, calculating, then dropped out with a small defeated motion, folding her paddle like a fan. The field thinned. The animal grew quiet. What remained was Vance's money and two investors who were clearly in over their heads and me, in the third row, with fourteen thousand dollars and a shaking hand and a witness.

"Twenty-nine thousand. Going once—"

I raised the paddle.

The motion traveled up my arm from somewhere deeper than muscle—from the attic, from the photographs, from the boy in the garden holding his father's laughing face against his shoulder. The paddle rose and the auctioneer's eyes found it and something flickered across his professional face, the brief surprise of a man noticing an unexpected bidder in the third row.

"Twenty-nine thousand, number forty-seven. Thank you, sir. We're at twenty-nine. Do I hear twenty-nine-five?"

The gravel-voiced agent turned around. He looked at me—really looked, for the first time—and I watched him do the arithmetic the way men like him do it: quickly, dismissively, arriving at *no threat.* He smiled a small courteous smile, the smile of a man humoring a child at a wedding, and raised his card.

"Thirty thousand. Thirty, thank you. Do I hear thirty-five? Thirty-five?"

Odile's hand was still on the armrest. I had not taken it. But it was there, palm up, steady, and I found that my own hand had stopped shaking—not entirely, not all the way, but enough. Enough to hold the paddle level. Enough to be seen.

Because that was the thing I finally understood, sitting in the borrowed courtroom with my whole history hanging on an easel behind a stranger's microphone: the terror had never really been about losing the house. I had lost it years ago, in fire and silence and fifteen years of deliberate forgetting. The terror was about being witnessed in the wanting. About standing in a bright room full of strangers and letting them see that Vesper Vane, night librarian, unread man, wanted something. Wanting things was what had destroyed my father—wanting until ownership curdled into control, control into cruelty, cruelty into ash. I had sworn off wanting the way amputees swear off ladders.

But Odile was beside me. Mrs. Okonkwo's casserole dish was washed and waiting in my cupboard. Somewhere out in the crowd, I didn't know where, were people from Marrow Lane who remembered my mother's roses, and I had not yet learned what they intended, and neither had I.

"Going once at thirty thousand," the auctioneer sang. "Number twelve. Going once—"

I raised the paddle again.

The agent's smile thinned by a degree. The auctioneer's eyebrows lifted. And in the third row of a county courtroom, in the plain gray jacket of a substitute teacher who had given up, Vesper Vane sat upright with his number showing, thirty-one thousand dollars committed against fourteen thousand in the bank, and no plan whatsoever except to remain visible.

Odile leaned toward me, and for the first time all morning there was something warm underneath the dryness of her voice.

"There he is," she said. "Now hold on. It's going to get worse."
