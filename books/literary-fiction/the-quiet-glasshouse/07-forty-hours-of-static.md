## 7. Forty Hours of Static

Wren found me at eleven o'clock that first night, standing in the kitchen doorway in her mother's cardigan, holding the shoebox like it was something she'd dug up out of a grave and immediately regretted.

"You're still awake," she said.

"I'm always awake. Tuners are nocturnal. We come out after the pianos have stopped pretending to be asleep."

She didn't laugh. She set the box on the kitchen table between us — the table where I'd eaten four thousand breakfasts as a boy, where Marguerite had once taught me to fold a napkin into the shape of a swan because Aldous liked swans, though he never once said so — and she peeled back the flaps with the care of someone defusing something.

Forty-one cassettes. Hand-labeled in Marguerite's upright, schoolmistress hand: dates, years, a few single words. *Kitchen. Study. Bad night.* The last one simply said *again*.

"The solicitor said they were probably nothing," Wren said. "Dementia patients ramble. She'd sit with the recorder going for hours." She turned a cassette over in her fingers, reading the label like tea leaves. "But she never rambled. Even when she couldn't find the word for *spoon*, she was precise about everything else. It was the words for things that went. Never the things themselves."

"And you want me to listen with you."

"I want," she said carefully, "to not be alone in the room when I hear whatever's on these."

There it was. Not an invitation — a requisition. Twenty years ago I'd have read it as proof I was still staff. Tonight, exhausted, grief-struck, radiator-drunk on the house's hum, I heard something else underneath it: fear. Wren Voss, who could gut a man with a well-placed remark about his shoes, was afraid of her mother's voice coming out of a machine.

"Get the good lamp," I said. "And something to drink. This is going to be like listening to a piano with forty broken strings. Nobody should do that sober."

* * *

We set up in the study, because it seemed wrong to do it anywhere else. The tape deck was Aldous's — a silver-and-black monster from the seventies that still worked the way old machines work, reluctantly and then perfectly, like a mule deciding to cooperate. Wren fed in the earliest cassette. The counter clicked. The reels began to turn.

First came the hiss. That oceanic static, the sound of blankness waiting to be filled. Then a chair creaking. Then Marguerite.

"*Testing. Is this — yes. All right.*"

I had heard her voice every day for twenty years and then not at all for six days, and hearing it now was like pressing a bruise to check if it still hurt. It did.

"*The eleventh of March. I'm told I should talk. Dr. Ferro says the memories need somewhere to go or they'll pool.*"

A pause. In the pause you could hear the room: the tick of the mantel clock, a radiator settling, wind against glass. The house, present even on tape, keeping its own minutes.

"*Pool,*" Marguerite repeated, tasting the word. "*Like water in a cellar. He thinks I don't know what's down there.*"

Then nothing. Ninety seconds of breathing and clock-tick. Then, quietly, almost tenderly:

"*The glass kept what the glass kept.*"

The sentence ended and the tape kept running — another minute, two — before Wren reached out and stopped it. Her finger hovered over the button a moment longer than it needed to.

"That's it," she said. "That's the one. She said it for twenty years. At dinner, to the mirror, to me on the phone when she no longer knew my name but knew she should say something. *The glass kept what the glass kept.* I used to think it was just noise. A phrase that got stuck."

"Phrases don't get stuck," I said. "Pianos get stuck. There's always a reason — a bent pin, a swollen bridge. Something physical holding the note down."

"You're saying there's something holding *her* down."

"I'm saying we should listen to all of them."

Wren looked at the shoebox the way you look at a staircase you know goes down into a cellar you've never entered. Then she squared her shoulders, poured two fingers of the good whisky she'd found in Aldous's old cabinet, handed me mine without asking whether I drank — she knew; she'd always known — and fed in cassette number two.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about listening to forty hours of a dead woman's voice: it stops being a recording and becomes a residence. By the third night — because one night became three, the way grief does arithmetic on your calendar — I knew the geography of those tapes better than I knew the geography of the house above us. The kitchen tapes smelled, somehow, of bread. The study tapes had a draft in them. And the late ones, the ones labeled in a shakier hand, had a quality I can only call *underwater*, as though Marguerite were speaking from beneath the surface of her own life, and the static was the water between us.

Mostly she talked about nothing. The price of coal. A hat she'd seen in town in 1963. Complaints about the cook, apologies to the cook, recipes recited like rosaries. Her mind moved through its rooms turning over furniture, checking drawers, finding some of them empty and standing in front of the emptiness with terrible dignity.

"*I can't remember if Aldous liked pears,*" she said on reel nine. "*He must have. Everyone likes pears. Why would I marry a man who didn't like pears?*"

"She did love him," Wren said softly. It was the third night; we'd stopped pretending this was a chore and started admitting it was a vigil. We sat on the floor now, backs against the desk, legs stretched out, like teenagers. Like the children we'd never quite been allowed to be. "People assume it was practical. It wasn't. She adored him. She just — held it at arm's length. Everything at arm's length. You could live in this house your whole life and never be closer than arm's length to her."

"I know," I said.

Wren glanced at me. "Yes," she said. "You would."

It wasn't an apology. Wren doesn't apologize; she acknowledges, which is harder and worth more. I let it sit between us, warm as the whisky.

On reel fourteen, the sentence changed.

Not the words — the words were identical, *the glass kept what the glass kept*, delivered in that flat, careful voice she used when she was holding herself together with both hands. What changed was what came after. For the first time, instead of silence, there was a second sentence, spoken so low the microphone barely caught it:

"*He shouldn't have signed.*"

Wren sat up so fast she spilled whisky on the rug her mother had bought in Lisbon and never liked.

"Play it again."

I rewound. The hiss. The breath. "*The glass kept what the glass kept.*" And under it, like a stone under moss: "*He shouldn't have signed.*"

"He," Wren said. "Not *they*. Not *it*. He."

"He," I agreed.

"Do you know how many times I've replayed that sentence in my head? Thousands. Tens of thousands. And it never occurred to me it might be half of something. That it might have a *second line*." She laughed, a short unhappy sound. "God. She spent twenty years saying a sentence with the ending bitten off, and we all just — nodded. Like it was a proverb. Like it was *decoration*."

"It's not decoration," I said. "Nothing in this house is decoration. That's the whole problem with this house."

* * *

Reel nineteen was labeled *bad night* and it earned the label.

It opened mid-sentence, which was unusual — most tapes began with her testing the machine, establishing herself, arranging the room. This one began as if we'd walked in on her.

"*— I know what you're going to say. I know. But you weren't there. You didn't see his face when they — *"

A long pause. When she resumed, her voice had dropped into a register I'd never heard from her, not once in twenty years: young. Frightened. A girl's voice wearing an old woman's throat.

"*Tomas. Tomas Verhoeven. If anyone ever asks — no. No one will ask. That's the whole point of it, isn't it. That's what the money is FOR.*"

The name landed in the study like a chandelier falling.

Wren grabbed my wrist. Not gently. Her nails went in and I let them, because I needed the pain to confirm I hadn't invented what I'd just heard.

"*Verhoeven,*" she whispered. "Like the man at the funeral. Brandt. He said — he toasted the *Verhoeven family*, and everyone looked at him like he'd sworn in church."

"I remember."

"Who is Tomas?"

"I don't know," I said, which was true, and then, because lying to Wren in the dark felt suddenly impossible: "But I think I've been tuning around his absence for twenty years."

She looked at me. In the lamplight her face was stripped of everything she usually wore — the wit, the armor, the Voss chin. What was left was just a person, orphaned twice over, holding a dead woman's wrist-watch of a voice in her hands.

"Keep playing," she said.

The tape ran on. Marguerite wept — briefly, furiously, the way she must have done all her life, in locked rooms, on schedule, budgeted like everything else she did. Then the crying stopped as if switched off, and the flat voice returned, and the sentence came again, *the glass kept what the glass kept*, and this time it sounded less like a confession than like a spell being renewed. A ward. Something you say nightly to keep a door shut.

And then, near the end of the reel, three sounds in quick succession that I will hear until I die:

Glass breaking. A sharp intake of breath — hers. And a man's voice, distant, distorted by distance or by decades, saying something we couldn't make out, except for its last word, which was "*— remember.*"

The tape hissed itself empty. Neither of us moved to stop it. We let the static run, forty seconds, a minute, the sound of a machine faithfully recording nothing, which is, when you think about it, the most honest thing a machine ever does.

"Was that—" Wren started.

"On the tape? No." I pressed stop. The reels wound down with a soft mechanical sigh. "Recordings don't work like that. Whatever that was, it happened in the room while she was recording. Or..." I hesitated.

"Or it happened somewhere else entirely," Wren finished, "and she carried it with her, and it leaked."

Outside, the house ticked and settled. I realized I was doing what I always did — translating the building, reading its sounds for meaning — and that for once the building had nothing to add. Or nothing it was willing to say. Even the radiators had gone quiet, as if they too were listening.

* * *

We listened to nine more reels that night. By four in the morning we had a ritual: Wren worked the deck, I took notes in the margin of a tuning ledger I'd repurposed, and between us we assembled Marguerite's drowned sentence piece by piece, like raising a piano from a lake and laying its parts out on the floor.

Fragments surfaced. A date — the fourth of November, year unstated, but she mentioned frost on the glasshouse, which narrowed it. A phrase — "*the signature, the stupid brave signature*" — repeated twice on different reels, years apart, identical intonation both times, the way a stuck note repeats. A reference to "*the east wing before it was sealed*" that made Wren frown, because she'd grown up believing the east wing had always been sealed, the way you believe mountains have always been mountains.

And once — reel twenty-three, 2:14 a.m., both of us grey-faced and hoarse from whispering — Marguerite said, clear as a bell, in the voice she'd used to call us in to dinner:

"*Coral would understand. Coral hears everything.*"

Wren paused the tape. We looked at each other across the shoebox, across forty hours of a dead woman's voice, across twenty years of a living one's silence.

"Well," Wren said finally. "She wasn't wrong."

"No," I said. "She wasn't. That was rather the difficulty."

Because here is the thing I haven't said yet, the thing I packed alongside my tuning forks when I came back to this house: I *had* heard everything. All those years, tuning my way through the estate, I had registered the east wing's wrongness the way you register a false note — instantly, involuntarily, professionally. The sealed wing didn't hum like the rest of the house. It didn't hum at all. Every other room in the Voss estate had a voice, a decade, a temperature of memory in its pipes. The east wing was a rest in the music. A held breath. And I had marked it down, all those years ago, as *not my business*, and tuned around it, and called the tuning good.

Marguerite had known that about me. Had counted on it, maybe. *Coral hears everything* — and says nothing. Perhaps that was why she'd kept me. Not despite the silence. Because of it.

I didn't say any of this to Wren. Not yet. Some confessions need daylight, and it was four in the morning, and the whisky was gone, and her eyes were red-rimmed and fierce and more like her mother's than either of them would have tolerated hearing.

"We should sleep," I said.

"In a minute." She held up the last cassette we'd played, turning it in the light. "Look at the label again."

I looked. Beneath the date, in that shakier late-period hand, almost invisible unless the light caught it sideways, was a single word I'd taken for a smudge:

*Tomas.*

Underlined. Twice.

"Wren," I said slowly, "where does your family keep its old records? Ledgers, deeds, correspondence. The boring paper."

"The cellar archive. Why?"

"Because tomorrow," I said, "while you sleep off forty hours of your mother, I'm going to go read the house's paperwork the way I read its pipes. And I'm going to find out who Tomas Verhoeven was, and why the only place his name survives in this entire estate is on the back of a cassette his sister hid in a shoebox."

Wren studied me for a long moment. Then she did something she hadn't done since I was twelve years old and fixed her music box without being asked: she put her hand briefly, awkwardly, on my shoulder.

"Don't break anything," she said.

"I never break anything," I said. "I tune it. Whether it wants to be tuned or not."
