## 6. The Sentence

The attic smelled the way attics are supposed to smell, which was a small mercy in a week that had offered very few: dust and cedar and old paper, the particular sweetness of things that have been left alone on purpose. Ada stood at the top of the ladder with a flashlight in one hand and Tomas's list of instructions in her head — *boxes marked L are yours, boxes marked T are mine, don't step between the joists unless you want to meet the kitchen ceiling* — and felt, for the first time since arriving, something adjacent to calm.

Attics were honest rooms. They didn't pretend to be anything but what they were: warehouses of other people's decisions about what to keep.

Her father had been a keeper. Her mother had been a thrower-away, or perhaps simply an earlier leaver, and after she went, her father's keeping had thickened into something like devotion. He'd kept receipts from 1987. He'd kept three broken umbrellas, as though rain might apologize. He'd kept every school report Ada had ever brought home, including the one from age nine in which Mrs. Calloway wrote, *Ada has a gift for languages and a talent for hiding behind them,* which Ada read now by flashlight and decided was either the kindest or the most damning sentence anyone had ever written about her.

She worked through the boxes methodically, because method was how she loved things. Tax records. Christmas decorations, including the angel whose face had melted in some forgotten summer and whom they'd named, cruelly and forever, The Incident. A shoebox of Ruth's things that Ada opened and closed again within four seconds, like touching a stove.

*Later,* she told herself. *You're here for papers. Legal ones. Ones with clauses.*

The clause was why she'd climbed up here at all. Mr. Ferro, the lawyer, had explained it twice in his office that smelled of lemon polish and other people's misfortunes: if a document written in the family dialect could be authenticated and translated before the sale, the court was obliged to honor whatever it said. Six weeks. He'd said it the way doctors say *we caught it early* — hopeful, and hedged.

Ada had spent thirty years translating other people's sentences. Contracts, novels, a memoir by a retired footballer that had required more diplomacy than any treaty. She knew how words behaved when you leaned on them. What she did not know — what she had been carefully not knowing for fifteen years — was whether her father's dialect still answered when she called.

It had been answering less and less for decades. That was the thing nobody warned you about losing a language: it didn't leave all at once, like a guest storming out. It left the way tide leaves a beach. You turned around and something you were sure of yesterday was simply gone, no note, no forwarding address. She'd lost the word for *gull* first, absurdly, standing in a city park watching pigeons, reaching for it and finding only the borrowed word where the true one used to live. Then the word for *dusk over water.* Then, God help her, last spring, mid-translation of someone else's novel about a woman returning to her childhood home — the universe had a sense of humor, just not a kind one — the word for *home itself.*

She'd substituted the common term without thinking. Her editor hadn't noticed. Nobody would ever notice. That was the horror of it: erasure that left no mark, a theft where the stolen goods were replaced so smoothly even the victim signed off on the inventory.

So she climbed into the attic with a flashlight and a legal deadline and the private, unspoken hope that the language was still in there somewhere, folded up in her father's handwriting like a shirt that still smells of the person who wore it.

* * *

The box she found him in was marked, in his square, patient hand: **WORDS**.

Not *papers*. Not *documents*. Not *estate*. **WORDS**, underlined once, the underline slightly crooked, as if he'd drawn it while thinking about something else.

Ada sat down on a joist — carefully, Tomas — and lifted out the contents. Notebooks, mostly. School exercise books with marbled covers, the cheap kind that fell apart if you looked at them sternly. And inside them, page after page, her father's hand: lists. Word lists. Columns of them, dated across twenty years, like a man taking inventory of a stockroom he could feel emptying.

*Sea-glass — the word for it, the old one —* followed by a phonetic scrawl. *The sound ice makes on the pond in March.* *What you call a wave that comes in smaller than the one before, the polite wave, the apologetic one.* There was no English equivalent written beside most of them. He wasn't translating. He was recording. Preserving specimens, pinning wings to cork.

Ada laughed once, a short surprised sound that echoed off the rafters, because of course. Of course her father, a quiet man who'd fixed boats and said maybe four hundred words a day, half of them *pass the* — of course he'd been doing this. Sitting up here alone through the long grey years after everyone left, writing down the language as it drowned, one word per breath, like a man bailing out a boat with a teaspoon and full knowledge of the sea.

She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand and kept reading, because crying could wait; it was patient, it always got its turn.

Near the bottom of the box, beneath the notebooks, was a single loose sheet. Better paper than the rest — heavier, cream-colored, the kind of page you take down deliberately, the kind that says *this is not a note, this is a document.* It had been folded in thirds and unfolded many times; the creases were soft as cloth.

One sentence.

That was all. One sentence in the dialect, written larger than his usual hand, with the care of a man writing something he expected to matter. No date. No salutation. No name at the top, none at the bottom. Addressed to no one, which is to say, possibly, to everyone, which is to say — Ada's stomach performed a slow, cold roll — possibly to whoever found it.

She read it.

And this was the thing she would try to explain later to Tomas, who nodded in the wrong places, and to Marisol, who didn't nod at all, and finally to no one, walking the beach at midnight talking to gulls whose proper name she no longer knew: reading it was like hearing your own voice on a recording. Familiar and wrong. Recognizable and foreign. Half the words rose up to meet her, warm, immediate, hers — and half stood at a distance, wearing familiar coats she couldn't swear she'd seen them in before.

She could read the beginning clearly. *This house—* no. Not quite. The first word was the old word for house, the deep one, the one that meant not the building but the staying-in-it, the word that had *hearth* somewhere in its bones the way a stone has a river in its history. *This staying-house belongs—*

Belongs. Or *was given.* The verb was one of those her language had that English didn't bother with, a verb that made ownership sound temporary, custodial, a coat you wore and passed on. Her father had never believed in owning things outright; he'd owned a boat he called *the bank's* until the day he paid it off, and then called it *mine, temporarily.*

Then came the middle of the sentence, and the middle was where the strangeness lived. Three words she could not parse at all — she sounded them aloud in the dusty attic and they tasted right and meant nothing, like hearing a song from childhood in a dream. One word she recognized and wished she didn't:

*Ruth.*

Just — Ruth. Sitting in the middle of the sentence like a stone in a loaf of bread. Not *my daughter Ruth*, not *Ruth who*, just the name, bare, the way you say the name of someone who doesn't need introduction because they've been in the room the whole time.

Ada set the page on her knee. Her hands were not entirely steady, and she noted this the way she noted everything, professionally, as data.

There was more she could almost reach. Near the end, a phrase built on the root for *forgive* — or was it *forget*? In the dialect the two words were siblings, near-twins, differing by a single breath at the end, and she had always suspected this was not accident but theology. Forgive-forget. Forget-forgive. Which one had her father written? She leaned over the page until her nose nearly touched the paper, as if proximity could restore what distance had taken.

And the very end — the last few words — she could read perfectly, effortlessly, the way you can see clearly the far shore of a lake while the water beneath the boat stays dark:

*—to whoever stays.*

Ada sat in the attic for a long time after that. The flashlight dimmed; she didn't notice. Somewhere below, Tomas moved through the house making the small domestic sounds he'd been making alone for years, and the sounds came up through the floorboards like warmth through a grate.

One sentence. Unfinished-feeling, addressed to no one, mentioning the house and Ruth and forgiveness-or-forgetting and whoever stays. A legal document, potentially, if Mr. Ferro was right. A message, certainly, if her heart was. And she could not read it. Not whole. Not honestly. She could translate contracts in four languages, she could make a retired footballer sound like a philosopher, and she could not read one sentence her own father had written in the tongue she'd been born into, because the tongue had gone out like a tide while she was busy being fluent everywhere else.

The fear arrived quietly, the way weather does. Not fear of the sentence — well, yes, fear of the sentence, fear of what *Ruth* was doing in the middle of it, fear of what a man writes in secret on good paper and folds and unfolds enough times to wear the creases soft. But underneath that, older and colder: the fear that the sentence was a door, and doors, once opened, do not care what you were doing before.

She thought about leaving the page in the box. Putting the lid back on. Telling Ferro she'd searched and found nothing, letting the auction run its course, letting the developer pour his foundations over the garden where a radio was buried — she remembered the radio suddenly, absurdly, its silver dial, Marisol's solemn face, Ruth's laugh — and going back to the city to her rented life, where all the words were borrowed and none of them were hers.

She thought about it for approximately eleven seconds.

Then she folded the page along its soft old creases, put it in her jacket pocket — against her chest, which felt dramatic and also correct — and climbed down the ladder to find out whether Ivo Petraglia was still alive, because if anyone else on this coast still carried the old words, it was him, and because a sentence addressed to no one is still waiting for somebody.

From the kitchen, Tomas called up, "Tea's on," and added, after a pause in which he clearly debated saying anything at all, "You've got attic dust on your face. Left cheek."

"Family heirloom," Ada called back. "I'm keeping it."

In her pocket, against her heartbeat, the folded page waited — patient as her father had been, patient as paper, holding its one unfinished sentence like a held breath.

Outside, the light was going gold over the water, and Ada thought, involuntarily, in the borrowed word: *sunset.* And then, from somewhere deep and nearly gone, the true word surfaced for half a second — bright, exact, hers — before sinking back down.

She was going to need help. That was the truth of it, and the beginning of everything else.
