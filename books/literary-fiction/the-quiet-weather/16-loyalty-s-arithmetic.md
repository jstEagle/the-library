## 16. Loyalty's Arithmetic

Tomas had a system for the house. He'd never called it that out loud — systems were for men with clipboards and hard hats — but the truth was he ran the place like a lighthouse keeper runs his light: not because anyone asked him to, but because if he stopped, something would go dark and no one would know until it was too late.

Mondays he checked the gutters. Wednesdays he walked the perimeter with a flashlight, looking for rot or raccoons or the slow coastal rot of salt air in the window frames. Fridays he paid whatever bill needed paying, usually by standing at the post office counter while Mrs. Okonkwo counted out his crumpled bills and said nothing about how long it took him to gather them. She was kind that way. The whole village was kind that way, which is another word for quiet, which is another word for complicit, though Tomas didn't have those words. He had simpler ones: *you keep what you tend.*

He had tended this house for fifteen years without owning a plank of it.

The evening he decided to talk to Ada, he'd found her light on in the attic window past eleven. That alone worried him. Ada Lockridge had been in town three weeks and she still slept like a woman guarding a border crossing — lights off at nine, up before the gulls. A light burning at eleven meant she wasn't sleeping. It meant the sentence.

He put the kettle on first. This was not avoidance; it was strategy. You did not walk into a difficult conversation with cold hands. His mother — his foster mother, Ada's mother, the only mother he'd ever claimed — used to say that tea was how you told someone *I am about to say something you won't like, but I brought you something warm anyway.* The woman had negotiated every hard moment of their childhood over tea. Tomas had learned the ritual at her elbow, standing on a chair to reach the cups.

By the time he climbed the attic stairs with two mugs, the light under the door had gone soft, the way lamplight does when someone finally sits down instead of pacing.

He knocked with his elbow, because his hands were full, and heard her say, "It's open," in the flat voice she used when she'd been reading old paper all day. Paper voice. He knew it well. He'd heard it from her father, once, sorting receipts at this same table.

* * *

The attic smelled the way it always had: cedar shavings, dust, and the ghost of pipe tobacco that had soaked into the beams sometime around the flood and never left. Ada sat cross-legged on the floor with pages fanned around her like a card game she was losing. Her reading glasses were pushed up into her hair, which meant she'd given up on them, which meant the words were fighting back.

"Tea," Tomas said, setting a mug down where a page wasn't.

"You're a saint."

"I'm a man with a kettle." He lowered himself onto the old trunk by the window — the one they'd used as a pirate ship, a stagecoach, a submarine, depending on the season — and wrapped both hands around his own mug. "You've been up here since supper."

"I've been up here since supper," she agreed, not looking up.

He watched her work. Her lips moved sometimes, shaping syllables in the dialect, testing them against the page and finding them wanting. Three weeks ago she hadn't been able to remember the word for *home*. Now she sat in the middle of a sentence her dead father had written, trying to pull it whole out of a language that was going out like a tide.

Tomas loved her. He wanted that stated plainly, even if only inside his own head, because what he'd come to do would look, from the outside, like the opposite of love.

"Ivo came by today," he said.

That got her attention. She looked up over the rim of her glasses. "Ivo? I went to see Ivo."

"He came here. To the house. While you were at Marisol's." Tomas turned his mug a quarter turn. "He wouldn't come in. Stood on the porch like a man waiting for a bus that scared him. He said—" and here Tomas made himself look at her directly, because a lie delivered sideways was still a lie, "—he said you'd stopped asking questions at the right time, and he hoped you'd have the sense to stop again."

Ada took her glasses out of her hair and put them on, which was what she did when she was deciding whether to be angry. "Did he now."

"He also said the word he stopped at. The one in your father's sentence. He wrote it down." Tomas reached into his shirt pocket and produced a folded scrap of paper, soft as cloth from handling. "He thought you should have it, even if he couldn't say why out loud. Old men are cowards with good aim."

She took the paper. Unfolded it. Whatever was written there, she read it three times, and Tomas watched her face do the thing faces do when a door opens onto a room you already suspected was there.

"The boathouse," she said quietly.

"There was a fire," Tomas said. "Winter of '89. Ruth was in it, or near it, or—nobody ever said exactly. Your parents sent her away inside a month. Told everyone she'd gone to an aunt's. And everyone in this village nodded along like sheep at a fence line, because your father bought their silence with groceries and your mother bought it with shame, and shame spends better than money in a small town."

Ada set the paper down very carefully, as if it might bruise. "You knew."

"I knew there was a fire. I knew Ruth left in a hurry and nobody cried at the dock, which is how you know somebody didn't die but also didn't leave happy. I didn't know details until this week." He drank his tea. It had gone lukewarm; he drank it anyway. "Marisol knew more. Ask her sometime, if you can get her to stop doing arithmetic long enough."

"Tomas." Ada's voice had gone careful, translator-careful, the voice she used when a sentence could break in her hands. "Why are you telling me this?"

And there it was. The door he'd built the whole conversation to arrive at, standing open in front of him, and beyond it the thing he actually came to say.

"Because I want you to stop," he said.

* * *

She stared at him. He let her. He'd rehearsed this on the walk over — not the words, exactly, but the weather of them. Calm. No thunder. Thunder would make her dig in, and Ada Lockridge dug in like a fence post.

"Stop," she repeated.

"Let the house go." He said it fast, the way you pull a splinter. "Auction happens, Gerald gets his tower or the cousin gets his title or whoever, and we all go on living. You go back to the city. Marisol keeps her shop. I keep—" he gestured at the beams, the dust, the dark windows below them, "—whatever this has been. And your father's sentence stays a private thing between him and God and whoever reads it after we're gone."

"It determines who owns the house."

"It determines what happened to Ruth," Tomas said. "That's what it determines. The house is just the excuse. You read that sentence out loud at an auction, in front of the whole village, in front of a court stenographer, and you're not translating anymore, Ada. You're testifying. About your parents. About what they did to their own daughter because they couldn't stand the neighbors knowing. About Marisol, who was twelve years old and paid in canned peaches to keep her mouth shut, and who has kept it shut for thirty-five years so well she probably doesn't remember where the lock is anymore."

"She deserves to have it opened."

"Maybe. Maybe she does. But that's her call, not yours, and not mine, and not a room full of strangers holding numbered paddles." He set his mug down on the trunk lid, on the exact spot where the paint had worn through from years of their sitting. "You want to know what I've learned keeping this house? Nothing stays standing because it's right. It stays standing because somebody shows up every Monday and checks the gutters. Silence is a roof, Ada. Ugly word for it, but that's the trade. You keep the rain off people whether they deserve it or not, and one day you look up and the thing's still standing and everybody underneath it is dry."

"And Ruth?" Ada's voice cracked down the middle. "Was she dry?"

Tomas looked at his hands. They were fifty-eight years old and spotted like old fruit, and they had fixed this roof twice and rebuilt the porch rail and replaced every pane of glass in the kitchen window, and none of it, not one hour of it, had done anything for Ruth Lockridge, wherever she was.

"No," he said. "Ruth got rained on. That's the truth and I won't dress it up. But hauling the whole roof off thirty-five years later doesn't un-rain her. It just gets everybody else wet. Including her, if she's alive, because I promise you she built herself a life on top of that silence, same as the rest of us, and you don't get to renovate a person's foundation without asking."

Ada stood up. Walked to the little round window at the gable end — the one they'd pretended was a ship's porthole — and looked out at the dark garden, where a radio had been in the ground for longer than either of them had been adults.

"You love this house," she said.

"I love the people in it." He said it simply, because it was simple. "The house is just where I keep them. And right now the people are you, and Marisol, and a memory of Ruth, and two dead parents who did a cowardly thing for cowardly reasons, and Mrs. Vane, who's been carrying a stone in her chest since 1989 and will drop dead of relief or grief the day it comes out, possibly both. You read that sentence aloud and every one of them gets dragged into the light at once. Is that translation? Or is it demolition?"

"Translation is demolition," Ada said, turning to face him. "You know that, don't you? Every honest translation tears the original apart to find out what it's made of. My whole career. Thirty years. I take sentences apart gently and put them back together in a new language and something always breaks in transit. Always. The question is never whether something breaks. It's whether what survives is worth the breaking."

Tomas felt the argument tilt under him, the way ice tilts.

"So tell me," she said, coming back toward him, "what survives if I stay quiet? Tell me what's left standing in ten years. Ivo dies. Mrs. Vane dies. Marisol's ledger goes in a drawer and then a skip. The dialect goes, the sentence goes, the reason any of us were ever a family goes. And the house stands empty with new owners who nail a placard over my height marks, and we all stay dry, Tomas. We stay dry as bone. Is that your arithmetic?"

He didn't answer right away. Outside, the sea did its patient work against the rocks, the sound this village had been falling asleep to for four hundred years and would fall asleep to for four hundred more, dialect or no dialect.

"My arithmetic says," he said slowly, "that I watched this family break once already, and I was the one who stayed behind to sweep up. I was eleven years old when they took Ruth away and I swept up. I was fifteen when you left for university and I swept up. I have been sweeping this floor for forty years, Ada, and I am asking you — I am asking you, who I love, who I carried on my shoulders down to the water when you were six — not to make me sweep up again."

Ada sat down beside him on the trunk. Not touching. Close enough that he could feel the warmth of her, which was its own kind of argument.

"If I let it go," she said, "and the sentence dies untranslated, what do I tell Ruth? If she's alive. If I find her. What do I say — *our parents erased you and I helped keep the erasure tidy*?"

"You tell her the truth quietly," Tomas said. "In a kitchen. Over tea. The way families do it."

"Families don't do it that way. Families do it the way ours did it. Badly, in secret, with groceries."

Despite himself — despite everything — Tomas laughed. It came out rough, half cough, but it was a laugh, and Ada almost smiled, and for a moment they were just two kids on a pirate ship in an attic that smelled like cedar and pipe smoke, and the world outside the porthole window was small enough to hold.

Then she picked up her father's page from the floor, and smoothed it on her knee, and the moment passed the way moments do.

"I need to sleep on it," she said.

"That's fair."

"It's not fair. It's a stalling tactic. But it's what I've got." She looked at him sidelong. "You'd really let Gerald have it? The developer? You'd watch them knock it down?"

"I'd hate it," Tomas said honestly. "I'd hate it every day for the rest of my life. But I'd rather lose the house than lose Marisol's heart or Ruth's grave-quiet. Houses come back. People don't."

Ada folded the page closed, and for one second — he'd swear this later, swear it on the roof he'd repaired twice — for one second she looked like she might agree with him.

Then the attic light flickered, and somewhere below them, in the dark of the hall, the front door opened and closed, and they both heard footsteps on the stairs — quick ones, businesslike ones, the footsteps of a woman who had made a decision and was arriving to enforce it.

Marisol appeared in the doorway with her ledger under her arm and her coat still buttoned wrong, and she looked from Tomas to Ada and back again, and said:

"Before either of you decides anything — there's something buried in that garden, and I think it's time we dug it up."
