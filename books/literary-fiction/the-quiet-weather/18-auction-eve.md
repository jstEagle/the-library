## 18. Auction Eve

The kitchen table had become a country, and Ada its reluctant cartographer.

She had been at it since dawn — the good lamp pulled close, her father's page propped against the sugar tin, and around it a drift of paper like tidewrack: index cards in her handwriting, Ivo's phonetic scrawls on the backs of envelopes, Mrs. Vane's careful schoolteacher's script, and one sheet torn from Marisol's shop ledger because Marisol had remembered, mid-sentence over eggs, that her grandmother used to say *kellum* for the eaves and not *kellem*, and Ada had needed to write it down before it evaporated.

*Kellum.* The word had come back to her the way a name comes back when you see a face — not summoned, but recognized. She'd spent thirty years saying *eaves* in three languages and had forgotten that her childhood house had worn them like a hat.

The sentence lay before her, whole now, or nearly. Her father's hand was small and slanted, pressed hard into the paper as though he'd been afraid the words might blow away if he didn't anchor them:

*Va ruthen solla im kethra. Va solla va miren. Domath kellum — domath va stannat.*

Fourteen words. She had carried fourteen words through six weeks of dictionaries that didn't exist, of old men's quarreling memories, of a woman who had watched her family drive away at dawn and said nothing for thirty-five years. Fourteen words, and every one of them had cost something.

*Va ruthen solla im kethra.* Ruth is alive and named. Ivo had fought her on *ruthen* — insisted it meant simply "the girl," that her father could not have meant his daughter, that naming was too heavy a thing to do in a dying language. But Mrs. Vane, when Ada read it to her on the porch two days ago, had gone very still and then very quiet and then said, "He wrote her name into it. In our tongue you don't say someone's name unless you're calling them home." And that was that. Ivo had grumbled, but Ivo grumbled the way weather grumbles — out of habit, not conviction.

*Va solla va miren.* She is forgiven, and the silence with her. That middle clause had nearly undone Ada in the reading room of the county library, surrounded by strangers and microfilm. *Miren* — forgive, but literally, *un-close the door*. Her father hadn't written *I pardon*; he'd written *the door stands open*. The dialect didn't have forgiveness as an abstraction. It only had doors.

And then the last clause, the one the lawyer had underlined twice in red, the one that would decide everything by noon tomorrow: *Domath kellum — domath va stannat.* The house with its eaves — the house to whoever stays.

Not to Tomas, who had kept the roof on and the taxes paid and the lights burning in the window like a lighthouse keeper who'd never been relieved of duty. Not to Gerald with his surveyors and his smile like a handshake that checked your pockets. Not to the cousin with his paper claim and his paper life. To whoever stays. Her father, dying in a hospital bed in a city that never learned his name, had reached back through thirty years and written a riddle whose answer was not a person but a posture.

Ada set down her pen and pressed the heels of her hands into her eyes until the dark bloomed orange.

"You'll ruin your sight," said Mrs. Vane from the doorway, "reading by lamplight. My mother said so, and her mother said so, and neither of them ever went blind, which tells you what that wisdom is worth."

"I'm almost done."

"Almost done is the most dangerous phrase in any language." Mrs. Vane came in carrying two cups of tea and the particular dignity of a woman who had decided, somewhere in the past week, to stop being a stone and start being a person again, and who was still practicing. She set the cups down. "Read it to me once more. All of it. Slowly."

"In the old tongue?"

"Both. Like you'll do tomorrow." She lowered herself into the chair across the table, arranging her cardigan. "If I'm going to sit in that hall and watch you say it, I want to know exactly what I'm vouching for. Every word. I've kept quiet long enough; I won't be caught keeping quiet about something wrong."

So Ada read it. First in the dialect, her voice finding the shapes of it — the vowels wide as the harbor mouth, the consonants soft as shingle underfoot — and she felt, as she always did now when she spoke it aloud, the strange double sensation of speaking fluently in a language that was dying inside her own mouth. Then in English, word by word, checking each against the cards.

"Ruth is alive, and I name her. She is forgiven, and the silence with her — the door stands open. This house, with its eaves, belongs to whoever stays."

Mrs. Vane listened with her eyes closed, the way she did when she was weighing something against a memory. When Ada finished, the old woman was silent for a long moment.

"He got it right," she said finally. "Every word. Your father's mother taught him proper — the old harbor dialect, before they smoothed it all out for the radio." A pause. "You know, the winter they left, your father stood right where you're standing and told me it was better this way. Better for everyone. He couldn't even look at the boathouse when he said it." She opened her eyes. "Thirty-five years I thought he was a coward. Turns out he was writing you a letter the whole time. Just a slow one."

Ada laughed — a small, cracked sound, surprised out of her. "A letter that took six weeks to open."

"Some letters take a lifetime. My husband proposed by accident. Took him four years to work up to doing it on purpose." Mrs. Vane sipped her tea. "Now. *Stannat*. Say it again."

"*Stannat.*"

"And *domath*?"

"*Domath.* House. Home-house, not building-house. Ivo wanted me to use *havnath*, the word for any structure, but—" She stopped. Looked at the card. Looked at her father's page. "*Domath* is right. It has to be. It's the word I lost, in the city. The one I couldn't find."

Mrs. Vane nodded slowly, as if Ada had passed some examination she hadn't known she was sitting. "Then you've got it. Go to bed."

"It's seven o'clock."

"Go to bed anyway. Tomorrow will want you rested, and lying awake rehearsing will want you tired. Those are the only two things certain about tomorrow." She gathered her cup, paused at the door. "Ada. Whatever happens in that hall — whoever bids, whatever the judge says — you said the words right. That part's finished. The rest is just weather."

She left, and Ada sat alone with the sentence and the tea going cold and the lamp making its small gold circle on the table.

*Just weather.* She turned the phrase over. Tomorrow, in the auction hall, there would be Gerald with his lawyers, and the cousin with his deed, and a room full of villagers who had spent thirty-five years not asking questions, and somewhere in it — she still half-believed it was a rumor Tomas had started out of hope — possibly Ruth. None of it was in her control. All of it was weather. What was hers was the fourteen words, and they were finished, and they were right.

She should go to bed. Instead she took out a fresh card and, at the bottom, beneath the translation, wrote the dialect word for *home* in large letters: *DOMATH*. She looked at it for a long time. Thirty years of borrowed words, of renting her own mouth, and here it was — the first word she'd ever owned, come home ahead of her.

Footsteps on the porch stairs. Not Mrs. Vane's careful tread — heavier, slower, apologetic even in its walking. Tomas came in without knocking, the way he'd done since he was nine years old, holding his cap in his hands.

"Couldn't sleep either?" Ada asked.

"Didn't try." He looked at the table, the cards, the page. "Is it done? Truly done?"

"Truly. Checked against Ivo, against Mrs. Vane, against my father's own hand. There's nothing left to check."

Tomas nodded. He stood there working his cap in his hands, and Ada saw that he was wrestling with something, and she waited, because you didn't rush Tomas the way you didn't rush a tide.

"The lights are on," he said at last. "Front window. Same as every night for eleven years. Tomorrow, after the auction — if it goes bad, if the developer gets it — those lights go off for the last time tonight, and I'd like..." His jaw worked. "I'd like not to be the one to turn them off alone. If it comes to that."

"It won't come to that."

"You can't know that."

"No," Ada admitted. "But I can know the words are true, and true words have a way of landing where they're aimed." She stood, stiff from hours at the table, and put her hand briefly on his arm. "Leave the lights on, Tomas. One more night. Let them burn all the way through till morning, just this once."

He looked at her, and something in his face eased — the look of a man setting down a weight he'd forgotten he was allowed to put down. "All night," he agreed. "Like waiting for someone to come home."

"Exactly like that," said Ada, and did not say the rest: *because someone might.*

Tomas left. Ada banked the lamp, climbed the stairs past the height marks on the doorframe — hers, Ruth's, the pencil ghosts of two girls growing up in a house that was about to learn its future — and lay down in her childhood bed under a quilt that smelled of cedar and Tomas's careful laundering.

Through the window she could see the front window below, glowing steady yellow against the dark, and beyond it the black line of the harbor, and she thought: *somewhere out there, two provinces away or ten miles down the coast, Ruth is also awake on this same night,* and whether or not she came, the door stood open. *Miren.* Un-closed. Their father had built it into the very grammar.

She slept, eventually, deeply, dreamlessly — the sleep of a translator whose hardest job is done.

She woke to gray light and the smell of coffee and, downstairs, voices — Marisol's brisk alto, Mrs. Vane's dry one, Tomas's low rumble, all three of them in her kitchen at seven in the morning as though it were any ordinary day and not the day the house would be sold or saved. On the table, beside the coffee pot, someone had laid out her father's page, squared carefully with the table's edge, ready to travel.

And pinned to the door beside it, where the notice of auction had hung for six weeks like a nail in the family's chest, was a new piece of paper in Marisol's handwriting: *GONE TO THE HALL. BACK BY NOON. THE KETTLE STAYS ON.*

Ada picked up the page, folded it into her coat over her heart, and followed the voices out into the cold bright morning — toward the hall, toward the bidding, toward whatever the weather intended.

They were halfway down the lane when Marisol stopped walking.

Down at the end of the road, where it bent toward the village and the auction hall beyond, a figure stood at the bus stop with a single suitcase, looking up at the house — at its lit window, its mended eaves, its door standing open.

Ruth.
