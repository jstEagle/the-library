## 21. New Words

*Marisol*

The shop had been drowning for two years before Marisol admitted it, and even then she only admitted it to the tide chart pinned behind the counter, which kept her secrets better than any priest.

"Third quarter down again," she told it one morning in March, tapping the ledger with a pencil worn to a stub. The tide chart said nothing, being paper, but it had the decency not to look surprised.

The village had fewer mouths every year. The summer people bought their jam at the farm stand on the highway now, with its cheerful hand-painted sign and its card reader that didn't judge you for taking forty seconds to find your glasses. Marisol's shelves held things nobody under seventy needed: lamp wicks, darning mushrooms, tinned herring in a brand so old the label had gone sepia like everything else she refused to replace.

So when Ada stood in her doorway in April — actually stood in the doorway, not climbed through the window like a criminal the way they used to — and said, "Move the back room into the house," Marisol's first instinct was to say no on principle.

Her second instinct was to say no out of pride.

Her third instinct was to look at the ledger, which had begun keeping a new kind of account lately, entries she didn't know how to balance: *Ada came back. Tomas fixed my step without asking. Ruth sent postcards now, actual postcards, with actual stamps.*

"You'd want rent," Marisol said, because saying something practical was easier than saying thank you.

"I want company," Ada said. "Rent is what you'd pay me to stop pretending this is charity."

"It *is* charity."

"Then we're both liars and we'll get along fine."

That was the trouble with translators. They heard what you meant underneath what you said, and they had no respect whatsoever for the arrangement.

*Tomas*

Tomas had slept in the back of the shop for eleven years. Not slept *at* the shop — people assumed he had an apartment somewhere, some flat above the hardware store with a kettle and a view of the harbor. He let them assume it. It was easier than explaining that after his mother died he had simply never left, that he'd moved his bed into the stockroom between the flour sacks and the winter coats, and that he woke each morning to the smell of Marisol's coffee through the floorboards and considered himself, by any honest measure, a lucky man.

But a man of fifty-three sleeping behind a curtain made of feed sacks is a man who flinches when someone opens a door too fast.

So when the house became theirs — not anyone's, *theirs*, the court papers using a phrase the lawyer had to read twice, "held in common by agreement of the parties" — Tomas did the thing he always did with good news. He got out his tools.

The roof first. He'd been patching it for years in secret, shingle by shingle, the way you feed a fire you're not supposed to be keeping. Now he could stand in the yard in broad daylight with a whole bundle of cedar shakes and no reason to hide, and he found the feeling so unfamiliar he had to sit down on the porch steps for a minute.

"You all right?" Ada called from the window.

"Roof's heavier than I remembered," he said.

"The roof, or owning it?"

He didn't answer. She smiled and shut the window, having gotten what she wanted, which was the whole trouble with sisters, foster or otherwise.

That night he carried his bed up the stairs. His actual bed, the narrow iron one, disassembled into four pieces and reassembled in the room that had been his when he was nine, when the Lockridges took him in for what was supposed to be six weeks. Thirty-eight years ago. The mattress was new; the view was the same: the garden, the fence line, the crooked birch that had been crooked his whole life and showed no signs of improving.

He lay there listening to the house settle around him — the small sounds of a building that had been empty so long it had forgotten how to be full — and he thought: *so this is what everyone else gets.*

Then he thought: *took you long enough.*

* * *

*Marisol*

Moving the shop's back room into the house took three days, mostly because Marisol insisted on cataloguing every item before it crossed the threshold, and Tomas insisted on carrying three items at a time whether they were catalogued or not, and Ada sat on the porch steps with a legal pad making up categories that helped no one.

"Sentimental-fragile," Ada read aloud. "What's in that box?"

"Candles," Marisol said.

"What makes candles sentimental-fragile?"

"They were Mama's."

"They're from 1996 and half of them are melted into each other."

"That's what makes them sentimental," Marisol snapped, and took the box inside herself.

The truth — which she would admit to no one, least of all the tide chart — was that the back room had become unbearable to her. For thirty-five years it had held the groceries the Lockridge parents paid her family with, in exchange for silence about the winter of '89. Even after the truth came out, even after Mrs. Vane confessed and Ruth wrote back and the whole rotten arrangement was dragged into daylight at the auction, the shelves themselves remained. Every tin and jar sat there looking innocent, and every time Marisol stocked them she felt the old arithmetic running under her skin: *you were trusted, and trusting you cost them their daughter, and cost Ruth her childhood, and cost you—*

Cost her what? She still didn't know. That was the entry she could never close.

In the house, though, the same goods sat on different shelves, in different light, next to Ada's books and Tomas's tools and the radio they'd dug out of the garden, which now lived on the mantelpiece like a retired soldier. In the house, the tins were just tins. Marisol noticed this on the fourth evening, standing in the kitchen with a jar of pickled onions in her hands, unable to remember why she'd been bracing herself.

"You're doing the face," Ada said from the table, not looking up from her translation work.

"What face?"

"The one where you're auditing your own feelings."

"I don't have a face."

"You have several. I've been translating them since we were eight."

Marisol put the jar down harder than necessary and went to make tea, which was how she said things she couldn't say.

* * *

*Tomas*

The table was the problem.

Not the table itself — the table was fine, oak, older than all of them, scarred with rings from cups and one deep gouge Tomas had made at age eleven attempting to whittle. The problem was that a table meant for five had spent thirty years set for none, and now that it was set for four it seemed to hum with a frequency Tomas could feel in his teeth.

Dinner was Marisol's idea. "We're eating together," she announced one Tuesday, in the tone she used for announcing price changes, as if resistance were not an option. "It's wasteful cooking separately. There are economies of scale."

"There are," Ada agreed solemnly. "Economies of scale."

"I'm not doing it because I'm lonely."

"Of course not."

"It's *arithmetic.*"

"Naturally," said Ada, who had once read a sentence aloud in a dying language and changed the course of a courtroom, and who therefore believed words could do anything.

So they ate together. Tuesdays and Fridays at first, then — without anyone proposing it, without a vote, the way weather changes — every night. Marisol cooked the way she ran the shop: briskly, generously, with strong opinions about salt. Ada washed dishes and narrated her translation troubles, which had shifted from crisis to hobby now that the great sentence was done; she was working on reviving the dialect properly, compiling word lists, pestering Mrs. Vane with notebooks. Tomas fixed things. If nothing needed fixing he fixed it anyway, and if truly nothing needed fixing he sat with his hands around his cup and listened, which was its own kind of repair.

One Friday in May, Ruth called. The phone sat on the wall by the pantry, and Ada answered, and the rest of them pretended not to listen, which is to say they listened completely.

"She says the plum tree in her yard finally flowered," Ada reported, hand over the receiver. "First time in four years. She wants to know if ours survived the frost."

"Tell her the north branch died but the rest held," Tomas said.

Ada relayed it. A pause. Then she laughed — a short, startled sound, like a hiccup of joy — and said something into the phone in the dialect, three or four words, quick and shy.

"What was that?" Marisol asked.

"She taught me a word. For a tree that blooms late." Ada shrugged, pleased and embarrassed at once. "There isn't an English one. She says our grandmother used it about the plum tree every spring, and every spring acted surprised."

Tomas looked at the table. At the gouge he'd made at eleven. At the ring stains from cups belonging to people dead and scattered and returned. He thought about the six weeks that had become thirty-eight years, and about how he had spent most of them believing he was the one who stayed behind, the one who missed whatever was happening elsewhere.

He had it backwards, he realized. Everyone else had been waiting for the house to be a home again. He'd just been the only one patient enough to keep the lights on until they arrived.

"More potatoes?" Marisol asked him.

"Please," he said.

* * *

*Marisol*

In June, Mrs. Vane began coming to Sunday dinners, ferried up the hill by Tomas in the truck, gripping the door handle the entire way as though the truck might attempt something adventurous. She brought contributions from her garden with the gravity of a woman delivering evidence: rhubarb, then peas, then — in July — a basket of new potatoes so heavy Marisol suspected sabotage.

"You'll take some home," Mrs. Vane commanded.

"I grew these."

"All the more reason to share them. Hoarding vegetables is how grudges start." Mrs. Vane settled into her chair, the one nearest the window, and surveyed the kitchen with the expression of a general reviewing troops she had personally trained. "Well. Look at this. Four plates."

"Three plates and a guest," Marisol said.

"Mm." Mrs. Vane unfolded her napkin. "I've been a guest in this village for sixty years. It's remarkable how long you can be a guest in a place where you've buried two husbands and most of your opinions." She looked at Ada. "How many words today?"

"Forty-one," Ada said. "Including one Ivo got wrong and refuses to concede."

"He's stubborn."

"He's *wrong*, and he knows it, and he's going to pretend until he dies out of spite."

"Good," said Mrs. Vane. "A language needs spite in it. English has far too little. That's why it spreads — nothing holding it together but politeness."

Marisol watched them go at it, the two old women and the translator, sparring over vowels while Tomas quietly gave Mrs. Vane the seat with the cushion and Ada wrote *spite* in her notebook as though it were a technical term, and she felt something loosen in her chest that she recognized, distantly, from before 1989. Before the ledger. Before she learned that trust was a debt you could be ruined by.

She had spent her whole life keeping accounts. Debts, favors, apologies owed. And here was the strange discovery of her fifty-eighth year: that some accounts closed not by payment but by abandonment. You stopped tallying. You put the pencil down. You passed the potatoes instead.

"Tomas," she said, "your elbow's in the butter."

"So it is," said Tomas, and moved it, and the evening went on.

* * *

*Tomas*

On the last night of July, Tomas woke at three in the morning to the sound of someone in the kitchen.

Eleven years of sleeping behind a feed-sack curtain had given him certain reflexes, and his body went from asleep to armed in half a second before his mind caught up and reminded him: *you live in a house now. Houses have people in them. That's the entire point.*

He padded downstairs. Ada sat at the table in lamplight, surrounded by index cards, writing.

"Can't sleep?" he asked.

"Words won't stay put." She held up a card. "Look at this. *Kellum.* Ivo says it means the sheltered side of a hill. Mrs. Vane says it means the quiet hour before dawn. They're both right. They're both *certain.*"

"Maybe it means both."

"Maybe it means the language was never one thing. Maybe it was always just — whoever was speaking it, speaking it together." She set the card down. "Go back to bed, Tomas."

"In a minute." He looked out the dark window at the garden, at the spot where the radio had come out of the ground, grass grown back over it now as if the earth had finished its sentence. "You know what I keep thinking? That winter I moved in. Nine years old. Your mother asked if I wanted the light left on in the hall and I said no, because I was trying to seem tough."

"And?"

"And I've been leaving lights on ever since. Whole house, every night, for years. Told myself it was to keep the pipes from freezing or burglars away or some such." He turned the cup in his hands. "Turns out I was just waiting for somebody to come home to."

Ada was quiet a moment. Then she picked up a fresh card and wrote something on it and slid it across the table.

*What does it say?*

"*Kellum,*" she said. "The quiet hour before dawn. When the ones who watch the house can finally rest."

Tomas read it twice. Then he got up, turned off the kitchen light — the first time in thirty-eight years the house had gone fully dark while he was in it — and went upstairs to his own bed, in his own room, under his own repaired roof, and slept until the birds woke him, which in this house had always counted as sleeping in.

* * *

*Marisol*

August brought the first Sunday session.

Ada had posted a notice in the shop window — Marisol charged her for the space, one pickle, symbolic — and Marisol had expected three people, maybe four: Mrs. Vane, Ivo if he could be lured across his own grudge, possibly the Henderson boy who liked anything with a notebook in it.

Eleven people came. Eleven, in a village of maybe ninety, crowding into the kitchen with folding chairs and thermoses, and old Petra Okafor who hadn't spoken the dialect aloud since her husband died sitting in the corner saying nothing for an hour and then, near the end, correcting Ada's pronunciation of a single word — gently, almost inaudibly — and then crying, and being handed tea, and staying.

Marisol served refreshments because that was what she knew how to do with a room full of feeling: feed it. She moved along the walls with a tray, refilling cups, and caught fragments — *kellum, vasha, the word for the smell of rain on hot stone, the word for a door left open on purpose* — and thought about ledgers again, briefly, and then didn't.

Afterward, when the chairs were folded and the last of the visitors had gone, Ada stood at the doorframe of the kitchen — the old doorframe, the one with the height marks, hers and Ruth's and Tomas's, penciled ghosts climbing the wood — and wrote something new beside them with a carpenter's pencil.

Marisol came to look over her shoulder.

It was a single word, in the dialect, in careful letters. Below it, smaller, in English: *home.*

"You spelled it right," Marisol said. "I think. Don't check with Ivo."

"I'm not going to check it with anyone." Ada capped the pencil. "It's not a dictionary entry. It's a height mark. It's us, this year, at this size."

From the garden came the sound of Tomas arguing cheerfully with the plum tree, which had dropped a branch in the storm and was being forgiven for it in installments. From the road below, faintly, the sea did what the sea did. And on the counter inside, face-down where she'd left it that morning, Marisol's ledger lay closed — not balanced, not forgotten, just closed, the way you close a book you've finished and loved and don't need to reread, because you're living in the sequel now, and the sequel has better lighting.

"Sunday dinner's at six," she said. "Don't be late. Either of you."

"We live here," Tomas called from the garden.

"Exactly," said Marisol. "No excuses."
