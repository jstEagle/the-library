## 2. The Inventory of Unsaid Things

The house on Kessler Street had a way of announcing you when you came home. Not the door — the floor. The third board from the entrance, a strip of pine worn pale as driftwood, sang a single creaking note that Sebastian had heard every day of his childhood and had not heard once in thirty years. He stood on it now, suitcase in hand, and let it sing.

*Hello,* said the floor.

*Hello,* said Sebastian, to no one.

The realtor had left the keys in a lockbox with a code that was, of all things, his father's birthday, which felt less like convenience and more like a joke the old man had arranged posthumously. Sebastian let himself in at four in the afternoon on a Tuesday in October, and the house received him the way it had always received everyone: coolly, with the smell of dust and lemon polish and, underneath it, something faintly metallic, like a coin held too long in the mouth.

He had taken two weeks off work. Bridges did not inspect themselves, but they could wait; steel was patient in a way that fathers were not, and in a way that sons, apparently, also were not, because his father had died with a list of things unsaid between them long enough to require its own load rating.

Sebastian was, by trade and temperament, a cataloguer. He inspected bridges for the county — the same county, he sometimes noted with a private irony, in which his father had spent his final decades being a stranger to him at a range of eleven miles. His job was to find what was wrong with a structure before the structure found out for itself. You looked for hairline fractures, for corrosion blooming at the joints, for the places where water got in and quietly did its work. You wrote it all down. The writing down was the point. A crack that lived in a report was a crack that could be fixed. A crack that lived only in the steel was a crack that got to make its own decisions.

He decided, standing in the front hall with his coat still on, that he would treat the house the same way. Room by room. Defect by defect. It was, he told himself, the only sane approach to a house that had to be ready for market in six weeks, and it had the additional benefit of requiring him to look closely at everything except the actual problem, which was that his father was dead and had died the way he had lived — without leaving instructions.

Margot wanted everything boxed by Sunday.

He could hear her voice already, the way he could hear it across a phone line or a decade: *Sebastian, it's a house, not an archaeological dig. Box it, label it, donate it, done.* Margot, who had driven out every other weekend for the last four years of their father's life while Sebastian had sent cards and, twice, himself. Margot, who had earned the right to want it fast. Sebastian understood this the way he understood load paths — intellectually, completely, and without it changing what he felt, which was a slow, guilty need to go slowly.

He unpacked in the front bedroom, the one with the water stain on the ceiling shaped, if you squinted, like Portugal. Then he made a list. The list was titled, in his neat inspector's block capitals, KESSLER STREET — CONDITION SURVEY, and it began:

*1. Front hall floorboard (third from door) — creaks. Cosmetic. No action required.*

He wrote *no action required* and then looked at it for a long moment, because that was not true of the floorboard, which had creaked since 1987 and would creak until the house was torn down, and it was not true of anything else in the house either, but a survey was a survey and not a confession, and he was here to survey.

* * *

The first full day, he did the kitchen.

The kitchen was where the radiators lived — or rather, where the radiators asserted themselves, because the cast-iron monsters under the windows had opinions and expressed them in knocks and gurgles whenever the temperature dropped, a percussion section that had scored every breakfast of his childhood. The boiler in the basement was older than Sebastian, which was not an exaggeration; he had once found the installation date stamped on the plate and done the arithmetic and felt briefly like a man reading his own birth certificate.

The kitchen also contained the table where the family had eaten, the chairs where they had sat, and the particular silence where their father's explanations should have been. Sebastian ran his hand along the table's edge — a nick there, from a fork, from some dinner he could not place — and started his list.

*2. Kitchen — radiator valves seized. One drips. Recommend replacement prior to sale.*

*3. Window over sink — painted shut. Recommend forcing open, then regretting it.*

*4. Table — solid oak. Buyers will want it gone. Margot will want it gone. No action required.*

He was on item five when he opened the drawer under the phone — the wall phone, cream-colored, still plugged in, still with a working dial tone as far as anyone knew — and found the drawer's contents: a rubber band, a dead pen, a takeout menu from a Chinese restaurant that had closed during the first Bush administration, and a photograph.

The photograph was not of Sebastian. It was not of Margot. It was not of their mother, who had left when Sebastian was four and whose absence had been explained to him, in his father's single sentence on the subject, as *she went her own way*, which was the sort of explanation that teaches a child that explanations are not forthcoming in this house.

The photograph was of a woman he did not know, holding a child he did not know, in front of a house he did not know. The woman was laughing. The child was maybe six. On the back, in his father's handwriting — Sebastian knew the handwriting the way you know a voice, the narrow slanted capitals, the R that looked like a broken branch — was written: *Ada and —* and then the name had been scratched out. Not crossed out neatly. Scratched, with the pen pressed so hard it had torn the paper.

Sebastian stood in the kitchen holding the photograph and felt the particular vertigo of a man who has inspected a structure his whole life and just now noticed the crack.

His father had been Gerald Pemberton. Had been, Sebastian would have said, until this photograph. Gerald Pemberton had no past. Gerald Pemberton had come fully formed to Kessler Street sometime before Sebastian's birth, the way furniture arrives, the way weather arrives, and had never once — not at dinner, not at holidays, not in the hospital in his final weeks when Sebastian had sat with him through four hours of silence broken only by the beep of the machines — referred to a life before. Sebastian had asked, exactly once, at seventeen, *Dad, where did you grow up?* and had received the answer *around* and had understood, in the way children understand, that the question would not be asked twice.

And now here was Ada, laughing, and here was a child with a torn-out name, and here was Sebastian, forty-one years old, standing in his dead father's kitchen holding proof that the silence had not been emptiness. It had been a wall. And walls, in his professional experience, were load-bearing for a reason. You did not knock one out without knowing what it held up.

He put the photograph in his breast pocket, which was where he put things he intended to think about later and did not intend to think about now. Then he wrote:

*5. Drawer under wall phone — miscellaneous. Contents to be sorted with Margot.*

And under that, in smaller letters, almost against his will:

*6. Father — condition unknown. Further inspection required.*

He looked at item six for a while. Then he closed the notebook, because Margot was calling.

* * *

"Box it, label it, donate it," Margot said, on speakerphone, from three hundred miles away, where she lived with a husband and a career and a version of their father that Sebastian suspected was no more complete than his own. "Sebastian, I can hear you writing. You're making a *list*."

"I'm making an inventory. It's different."

"It's a list with a cover page. What's on it?"

"Radiators. Windows. The hall floor."

"Uh-huh. And what's under 'further inspection required'?"

Sebastian said nothing. Margot had always been able to hear his silences, which was a family trait that had done the family no good whatsoever.

"Nothing," he said.

"Right." A pause. He could picture her at her kitchen counter, the phone wedged on her shoulder, a pen in her hand because Margot also made lists, though hers ended in accomplished things. "The estate agent wants photos by the fifteenth. The good dishes go to me, the tools go to you, and everything else gets a dumpster. Agreed?"

"The tools are junk."

"The tools are yours because you're the one who'll pretend to use them. Agreed?"

"Agreed."

"And Sebastian — box the photographs without looking at them. Trust me. There's nothing in them you need."

He thought of Ada, laughing, in his pocket. He thought of the torn name. He thought about how his sister, who had sat at more of their father's deathbed hours than he had, might know exactly what was in those photographs, and had chosen, for years, not to look — and how that choice had a sound, when you listened to it, exactly like the sound of the third floorboard. *No action required.* The most expensive four words in any inspection.

"Agreed," he said again, and hung up, and did not agree at all.

* * *

That evening the temperature dropped, and the house did what the house had always done: it woke up. He heard it from the front bedroom — a knock in the walls, a gurgle, the long metallic sigh of pipes carrying water they had carried for fifty years. The boiler, that illegal antique, was running. He had not turned it on. Margot had not turned it on. The realtor certainly had not.

He went down to the basement with a flashlight, telling himself it was for the survey — *7. Basement boiler: verify condition* — and found the old beast ticking away, warm to the touch, its pilot light burning a small blue flag of insistence. Someone had lit it. Someone had been in the house. Or — and this thought arrived with a strange, unwelcome tenderness — the pilot had simply never gone out. Fifty years of winters and the little flame had held on through all of them, through the empty years too, through the decade when Sebastian had called at Christmas and no other time, burning in the dark for a family that had stopped coming down to feel it.

He stood in the basement a long time, listening to the radiators knock their slow code through the floor above. *Anyone home. Anyone home.*

Back in the kitchen, he took out the photograph again. Ada. The child. The scratched-out name. He got the phone book — his father had still kept one, of course, a phone book, an actual paper phone book, because Gerald Pemberton did not update anything, he simply outlasted things — and he looked up *Aldous*, because the scratched-out name, when he let himself see it, had started with an A, and because the woman at the funeral, the stranger who had touched the casket and said *I'm so sorry for the Aldouses*, had said it with the certainty of someone who had been told the name to her face.

There was one listing. R. Aldous, on a street across town.

He did not call. He wrote, instead, in the survey notebook, under item six, a single line:

*7. Structure contains undocumented rooms. Recommend locating original drawings before demolition.*

Then he turned off the kitchen light and sat in the dark with the radiators knocking, a man with a list of everything broken in the house and no entry at all yet for what was broken in himself, waiting for morning, when — he was almost sure — he would finally make the call.
