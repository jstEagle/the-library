## 5. Margot Counts the Cost

Margot had a system, and the system was this: one box per room, labeled in her own handwriting, which was legible, unlike everyone else's in this family. Kitchen: *utensils — keep*. Hall closet: *coats — donate*. Her father's study: *papers — shred, except tax*.

The system was holding. The system was the only thing holding.

She stood in the study on the third morning of the great Kessler Street excavation, a roll of packing tape in one hand like a weapon she hadn't decided to use, and listened to her brother and the stranger laugh downstairs. Actually laugh. Sebastian, who had spoken to their father maybe four hundred words a year for three decades, all of them variations on *fine* and *you too*, was laughing with a woman who'd known the old man for eight months and apparently gotten more out of him than Margot had gotten in fifty-one years.

She taped a box shut with more force than the cardboard deserved.

The truth — and Margot was honest with herself the way other people were honest with their accountants, reluctantly, annually, usually after something had already gone wrong — the truth was that she had driven here every Sunday for six years. Every Sunday. She'd brought the low-sodium soup he pretended not to hate. She'd refilled the pill organizer with its seven little compartments, Monday through Sunday, a week you could hold in your hand. She'd sat through his stories about bridges — load ratings, expansion joints, the goddamn gusset plates — because when your father talks about steel instead of feelings, you learn to take notes on steel.

And where was Sebastian? In another state, inspecting other bridges, calling at Christmas, sending birthday cards that arrived on time because he set reminders. Margot didn't even resent the cards. What she resented was that they worked. He got to be a good son by mail.

Downstairs, the laughter again. Wren's laugh was low and surprised, like she kept being delighted against her will. Sebastian's laugh she barely recognized; it had rusted from disuse sometime around 1998.

Margot pulled open the desk drawer and found, as expected, rubber bands, dead pens, and a receipt for a radiator valve dated eleven years ago. Eleven years. He'd been maintaining the boiler the whole time she'd been begging him to let it die. *It's illegal, Dad. It's a bomb.* And he'd just look at her with that mild face and say, *It's warm,* like warmth was an argument that ended discussions.

She dropped the receipt into the box marked *shred* and then took it back out. She didn't know why. She put it in her cardigan pocket instead, which was becoming a habit with her lately — rescuing small paper things from her own efficiency.

* * *

Wren appeared in the study doorway at noon, holding two mugs of tea like a peace offering from a country Margot had never invaded.

"I wasn't sure how you take it," Wren said. "So there's milk on the side."

"Black." Margot took the mug anyway, because refusing tea felt like a bigger statement than she wanted to make before lunch. "Thank you."

"Sebastian's measuring the radiators," Wren said, with the tone of someone reporting charming news. "He says they're cast iron, pre-war, worth saving."

"Wonderful. We can sell the house with its illegal boiler included. A fixer-upper with felony charm."

Wren smiled like she hadn't registered the sarcasm, or had registered it and decided to walk past it politely. "Can I ask you something? You don't have to answer."

"People say that and then they ask anyway."

"When did you find out? About the name."

Margot set the mug down on a stack of tax returns from 2009. "What name?"

"Aldous."

There it was. The word landed in the room the way it had landed at the funeral, foreign and cold, a coin from a country nobody in the family claimed. Margot looked at Wren — mid-forties, sensible cardigan, eyes that did the hospice thing of waiting without pressure — and felt the old anger rise up her throat like heat off the boiler grate.

"Fourteen years ago," Margot said. "I found a Christmas card in his coat pocket. From someone named Ada Aldous. It said, *Thinking of you at Christmas, whoever you are now.* Whoever you are now. Like a riddle. I asked him about it and he said it was nothing, a person from work, and I believed him because believing him was easier than the alternative, which was that my father had an entire self I'd never been introduced to."

Wren sat down slowly on the edge of a box labeled *misc — Margot*. "You never told Sebastian?"

"What was I going to tell him? 'Dad might have a secret past, please drive nine hours to discuss it'? He doesn't discuss. He inspects. He'd have made a list of possible explanations and filed them under *insufficient data*." Margot heard her own voice sharpening and couldn't stop it, didn't especially want to. "I carried it alone for fourteen years. So forgive me if I'm not thrilled that some stranger shows up at the funeral knowing more than I do. Knowing *anything*. Eight months of hospice visits and he told you everything, and his own daughter gets *it was a person from work*."

"He didn't tell me everything," Wren said quietly. "He told me about you, though. Both of you. Constantly. 'The children on Kessler Street.' That's what he called you. He talked about Margot who comes every Sunday, Margot who knows where everything is, Margot who fixed the porch step without being asked—"

"The porch step was rotting through. Anyone would have—"

"—and he said, and I wrote this down because I write things down, he said, *'Margot does the arithmetic of love and thinks nobody notices.'*" Wren let that sit in the dusty light of the study. "I don't know what it means exactly. But it sounded like noticing."

Margot stared at the window, at the dead fly on the sill that she'd been meaning to sweep for three days. Her eyes were doing something treacherous. She blinked it back down to wherever such things went in this family — a basement, probably, next to the boiler.

"Well," she said. "He had a way with words when the words cost him nothing."

"That's not fair."

"No," Margot agreed. "It isn't. Fairness is not the currency of this house. Nobody in this house has ever paid full price for anything emotional. We get it secondhand, dented, marked *as is*." She picked the mug back up and drank the tea black and bitter and exactly right. "Do you know what I'm angry about? Not the name. Not even Ada, whoever she was. I'm angry that he taught us the silence so well we thought it was a language. I married a man who doesn't talk about anything, and I stayed twenty-two years, because quiet felt like home. That's what he gave me. Inherited loneliness, passed down like the good china nobody uses."

Wren was silent for a moment. Then: "Sebastian said something similar last night. About measuring instead of speaking."

"Of course he did. To you. The stranger. Eight months versus fifty-one years, and somehow you're the confessor." Margot laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "I'm sorry. You brought me tea. You're very kind. You're kind in a way this family doesn't know how to be, and I resent it, which I recognize is insane."

"It's not insane," Wren said. "Grief with nowhere to go turns into anger. I've watched it happen in a lot of living rooms." She stood, smoothed her cardigan. "For what it's worth — he kept a list too. Your names, your birthdays, what you liked to eat. In his nightstand. Under a photo I've never seen anyone in."

Margot's chest did something structural. "A photo of whom?"

"I assumed it was your mother. But it isn't. It's a woman and a boy. Maybe ten years old." Wren paused at the door. "And Margot — there's a tin box. Downstairs, in the radiator cabinet in the basement. Sebastian found it yesterday. He wants to wait for probate. I think—" She stopped herself, the hospice worker's discipline reasserting itself. "I think some things shouldn't wait for paperwork."

She left. Margot stood alone in the study among her beautiful labeled boxes, fourteen years of chosen silence sitting in her stomach like a swallowed stone.

Slowly, she reached into her cardigan pocket and took out the receipt for the radiator valve. Eleven years ago. He'd been tending the heat the whole time. Tending the house. Tending whatever memory lived in a photograph of a woman and a boy, keeping the whole place warm for people who never came.

Margot wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, hard, once, businesslike.

Then she went downstairs to find her brother, and heard herself say, in a voice she didn't recognize as her own:

"Open the box."
