## 10. Bleeding the Radiators

The morning the condemnation notice died—formally, legally, with a stamp that Rosa later described as "the most beautiful piece of garbage I have ever seen"—the radiators of the fourth-floor walk-up began to sing.

Not sing, exactly. Gargle. A low, wet knocking in the pipes, like an old man clearing his throat before telling a story he's told a hundred times and will tell a hundred more. Ulyana stood in the basement with his ear against the riser, listening the way he used to listen to bridges, and diagnosed it in under a minute: air. Trapped air, all through the system, pockets of nothing where the heat wanted to be.

"Air," he told Dmitri, who had come down the stairs holding two cups of coffee and no pretense of doing anything else.

"Air," Dmitri agreed. "The building's been holding its breath all winter."

"Then I guess we let it exhale."

They started on the fourth floor, because Marisol's old apartment was where the sound was loudest, and because neither of them said so out loud, but both of them knew.

* * *

Bleeding a radiator is not complicated work. You find the valve, you fit the key or the flathead screwdriver, and you turn it a quarter turn until the trapped air hisses out—a thin, high whistle, like something small escaping—and then water comes, first in sputters, then in a steady thread, and you close the valve and move on. The whole operation takes four minutes if the radiator cooperates and forty if it doesn't. What takes longer is what happens while you kneel there waiting for the water to run clear.

Ulyana had spent seven years becoming an expert at almost-saying things. He could measure the distance between two words the way he measured the distance between expansion joints: precisely, obsessively, and without ever crossing it. But something had happened to him in March, standing in front of the tenants with Okafor's legal findings folded in his jacket pocket, when he had opened his mouth intending to say *I'll do whatever's needed* and instead said everything—the debt, the men who had threatened his mother, the money Marisol had borrowed against this building to make them go away, the years Dmitri had spent quietly covering payments while pretending, badly, to be cold. He had said it all in one breath, mid-sentence, the way a man steps off a curb into traffic, and then discovered that the street on the other side was not traffic at all but a room full of people who simply nodded, as if they'd known, as if they'd been waiting for him to catch up.

Rosa had cried. Mrs. Ferreira from 2B had cried harder. Dmitri had stood by the window with his arms crossed and his jaw working, and afterward, in the hallway, he had put one hand on the back of Ulyana's neck—not a hug, never a hug, they were still themselves—and squeezed once, and said, "You always did talk too much when you finally talked at all."

It was the closest thing to absolution either of them would ever offer, and it was enough.

Now it was April, and the debt was settled—Okafor had negotiated it down to a figure that made Ulyana wince and Dmitri shrug, and between Ulyana's savings and the small inheritance of half a building, it was done—and the developer had withdrawn, muttering about structural deficiencies that the city engineer, after a very thorough inspection conducted by a very thorough bridge inspector, had found to be overstated. ("Tolerances are within range," Ulyana had written in his report, and then, unable to help himself, added a second page.) And the radiators were singing, and it was time to bleed them.

* * *

Marisol's apartment smelled the way it always had: coffee, wool, and the faint mineral ghost of steam heat. Nobody had moved anything. They hadn't been able to. The afghan was still folded over the arm of her chair. Her reading glasses sat on the windowsill beside a cup she'd never finish, now home to a spider who paid no rent and whom nobody had the heart to evict.

"She'd hate this," Dmitri said, kneeling at the radiator by the window. "Us in here, touching her stuff."

"She'd hate it more if we froze."

"She'd hate it most if we did it wrong." He held out his hand. "Key."

Ulyana passed him the bleed key, the little brass square-toothed thing that had lived in the kitchen drawer since before either of them could reach the drawer. Dmitri fit it to the valve and turned, and the radiator began to hiss—that thin whistle, air leaving a body it had been trapped inside for months.

"You know," Dmitri said, watching the valve the way their mother—no, not their mother; he corrected himself silently, and then, these days, didn't correct himself at all—the way Marisol used to watch a pot she didn't trust, "she bled these herself every fall. Wouldn't wait for the super. Said a woman who owned half a building ought to know how it breathed."

"I remember. She made me hold the coffee can."

"You were eight. You mostly drank the coffee can."

"It was mostly milk."

"It was mostly your job." But he was smiling, the small crooked smile that Ulyana had spent seven years trying not to miss and had missed anyway, constantly, the way you miss a tooth.

The hiss thinned, stuttered, and then came the sputter—a brown spurt, then clear water running in a steady thread into the coffee can (they had bought new coffee cans for the purpose; some traditions deserve equipment). Dmitri closed the valve. The radiator ticked, settled, and began, quietly, to warm.

"That's one," Ulyana said.

"There's eleven more in this building."

"Then we'd better get started."

* * *

They worked floor by floor, top to bottom, the way you drain a system properly. In 3A, Mrs. Ferreira supervised from her armchair and offered commentary on both their techniques, none of it useful, all of it welcome. In 2C, Rosa's grandson, Marcus, age nine, appointed himself assistant and carried the coffee can with the gravity of a surgeon's nurse, asking questions—"Why's the air in there? How'd it get in? Is the air scared?"—that Ulyana answered with more seriousness than the questions strictly deserved, because Marcus was the only person in the building who asked about radiators the way Ulyana thought about them, which is to say as though they were alive and deserved consideration.

"The air isn't scared," Ulyana told him. "But it doesn't belong in there. Heat moves better without it. Everything works better when you let out what's trapped."

Marcus considered this. "Like my mom when she talks to her sister."

"Exactly like that," said Rosa, from the kitchen, and everyone pretended not to hear her.

By late afternoon they reached the second floor, and then the first, and finally the basement, where the boiler itself sat squat and iron-hearted, the building's actual chest. Ulyana checked the pressure gauge, opened the auto-vent, listened to the whole system settle into a hum that was less a sound than a temperature. Warmth moving through old metal. Water finding its circuit. Nothing trapped anywhere.

Dmitri leaned against the boiler and looked at him across the dim basement, in the light of one bare bulb, and said, "You're staying, then. Really staying. Not the penance version."

"Not the penance version." Ulyana turned the wrench in his hands. He had learned, this year, that some sentences you have to say twice—once for the other person and once for yourself. "I used to think staying would mean I owed too much. Like living here was a bill I couldn't pay. But that's not how buildings work. That's not how people work. You don't pay a place back. You just—you keep it running. You bleed the radiators. You fix what breaks. It's not debt. It's just... being part of it."

Dmitri nodded slowly. "She would've said it shorter."

"She would've said it while already doing it."

"Yeah." Dmitri pushed off the boiler. "Come on. 1A's radiator sticks. We'll need the big key."

* * *

That night, alone in what was now officially his apartment—the third-floor unit, the one with the good light and the radiator that clanked like a living thing, the one Marisol had apparently specified in her will with the note *"for Yanya, because he always liked the light"*—Ulyana took down the shoebox.

The letters were all read now, every one. Seven years of letters, one per year, each beginning *Dear Yanya* and ending *Come home, it doesn't matter why you left.* He kept them in order, oldest to newest, the way you keep anything that matters. But the box wasn't full anymore. There was room at the bottom. There had been room for weeks.

He took out a pen—he wrote by hand now, another thing the year had taught him—and a sheet of paper, and he sat at the kitchen table under the good light, and he wrote:

*Dear Marisol.*

*You were right about everything, which I know will be unbearable for you to hear, wherever you are, so I'll try to spread the credit around. Dmitri was right to protect me, even though he did it badly, in the family style. Rosa was right that the building keeps its own records. Even the boiler was right to burst when it did, though I maintain it could have picked a warmer night.*

*I'm staying. Not because I owe it. Because I choose it. I want you to know the difference, because I think you always knew it, and waited seven years for me to learn it, and never once said "I told you so," which for you must have been a heroic act of restraint.*

*The radiators are quiet. I bleed them myself now. I use your key.*

*It doesn't matter why I left. I'm home.*

He signed it *Yanya*—the name only she had ever used, the name he was keeping now, wearing it like a coat that finally fit—and folded the letter, and slid it into the shoebox beneath hers, oldest to newest, last of all.

For whoever comes next, he thought. Because someone always comes next. Someone always climbs the stairs of an old building carrying a box or a grief or a name nobody uses, and finds a room with good light, and a radiator that needs bleeding, and a drawer with a brass key in it, waiting.

Outside, April moved through the street in long slants of gold. Inside, the pipes hummed—warm water making its patient rounds through the old iron bones of the house, up through the floors, past every door, into every room, asking nothing, warming everything.

Ulyana put the lid on the shoebox, set it on the shelf where she would have kept it, and went to check the pressure gauge one more time, just to watch it hold steady.

It held.
