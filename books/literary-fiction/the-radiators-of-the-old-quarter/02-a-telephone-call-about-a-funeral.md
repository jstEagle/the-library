## 2. A Telephone Call About a Funeral

The call came at 6:47 in the evening, which in London meant the light had already begun its long surrender, and Cyrus Sandoval was standing at his kitchen counter photographing a bowl of soup he had not made.

It was a good photograph. He had learned to make good photographs the way a blind man learns to arrange a room — by memory, by geometry, by the testimony of others. The broth sat in the shallow ceramic bowl he'd bought in Lisbon three years ago, back when he still believed objects could hold luck. A slick of red oil caught the window's last grey light. Scattered on top: coriander, sliced chilli, a curl of lime zest he'd zested with the concentration of a surgeon. Beneath it all, a soup he had tasted only in theory.

He had eaten at Suya & Smoke that afternoon, a new place in Peckham run by two brothers from Enugu, and the review was due Friday. The brothers had come out to greet him after the meal, wiping their hands on their aprons, asking what he thought of the pepper base, whether it reminded him of home. He had said yes. He had said *home* as though home were a flavour he carried in his mouth instead of an address he hadn't returned to in eleven years. He had described the broth as "a slow argument between smoke and sweetness, resolved in favour of both," and the younger brother had laughed and repeated it to the kitchen, and someone in the back had whooped, and Cyrus had stood there in the warm wash of their joy feeling like a man who had accepted applause for a language he no longer spoke.

He could smell the soup. That was the cruelty of it — the nose had survived the catastrophe intact. He could smell the scorched depth of the stock, the green bite of the coriander, the lime like a struck match. His mouth watered. His mouth did everything right. It was only somewhere past the palate, in whatever dark antechamber where flavour becomes meaning, that the signal died.

Three years now. Three years since the fever in Marrakech — or the antibiotics, or the stress, or whatever the fourth specialist had shrugged about while charging him four hundred pounds — since taste left him the way a lover leaves: without a note, without a scene, without giving him anything to argue with. One morning his coffee tasted of warm nothing, and by evening the whole world had gone quiet on his tongue.

He had told no one. Not his editor. Not the women he occasionally let stay over and never asked to stay again. Not Adaeze, though God knew there were years when telling Adaeze anything had been as natural as breathing, before the breathing itself became a thing they had to schedule around each other.

So he photographed the soup, and cropped it, and adjusted the exposure until the oil gleamed like something worth wanting, and he was typing the caption into his notes app — *the broth arrives like an apology you didn't know you needed* — when his phone lit up on the counter beside the bowl, and the screen said:

**ADAEZE**

Cyrus looked at the name for a long time. Long enough that the phone rang twice more before he picked it up, and even then he answered it the way you answer a door you already know is bad news — too casually, one shoulder against the frame.

"Ada."

"Cyrus." Her voice came through flattened and strange, compressed by eight thousand kilometres and whatever she was doing to herself to keep it level. He heard, behind her, the particular layered noise of Lagos at evening — generators, horns, a church loudspeaker somewhere insisting on joy. "Are you sitting down?"

"I'm standing."

"Sit down."

"Adaeze." He set down the phone briefly to turn off the hob under the untouched soup, then picked it up again. "Just say it."

A pause. In the pause he heard her decide something — he could always hear Adaeze deciding things; she breathed differently before decisions, a small gathering-in, like a woman picking up a heavy pot off a low fire.

"Mummy is dead."

The words arrived and did not land anywhere. They hung in the kitchen, in the steam-fogged air above the bowl, among the hanging copper pans he'd bought for a life of cooking he no longer did. Cyrus became aware of small things with terrible clarity: the tick of the extractor fan cooling; a car alarm starting up and giving up on the street below; his own reflection ghosted faintly in the dark window, holding a phone, wearing the expression of a man listening to weather.

"When?" he said.

"Tuesday. Two days ago."

"Two days—" He gripped the edge of the counter. "Adaeze, why am I hearing this on Thursday?"

"Because I have been arranging a funeral alone for two days, Cyrus, and I called you the moment I could breathe." No tears in it. That was the frightening thing. Adaeze crying was a flood he knew how to survive; Adaeze not crying was a drought, and droughts killed things quietly. "I'm sorry. I didn't mean — I'm tired. I've been tired since 2014, if we're being honest."

He almost smiled. Almost. It was the kind of thing they used to say to each other across the dinner table in Yaba, passing rice, complaining about their mother with the easy cruelty of children who believed they would all live forever.

"I'm sorry I wasn't here," he said.

"You're never here, Cyrus. That's not news. That's just the arrangement." She exhaled, and he heard the sound of her sitting down somewhere hard — a plastic chair, probably, the kind that lived in every Lagos parlour, that groaned under any adult weight. "The wake is Saturday. Burial Sunday morning, at the church, then reception at the house."

"The house?"

"Where else? The compound. Half the quarter will come. They'll want to see the children of Funmilayo." A beat. "They'll want to see you. The famous one."

"I'm not—"

"Don't be modest, it doesn't suit either of us." Papers rustled on her end; he pictured her at the dining table, the one with the burn mark shaped like Brazil, her reading glasses pushed up into her hair, lists everywhere. Lists were how Adaeze prayed. "I need to know your dates. When are you flying?"

And here it was — the moment the whole call had been building toward, the moment Cyrus had been dreading since he saw her name on the screen, and he found, to his own quiet disgust, that the part of his mind that rose to meet it was not the grieving son but the professional. The scheduler. The man who managed his life the way he managed a tasting menu: courses arriving in order, nothing lingering past its allotted time.

"I can do five days," he said.

Silence.

"Five days," Adaeze repeated.

"Land Friday morning, leave Tuesday night. Wake, burial, reception, Monday to settle whatever needs settling with you, fly out Tuesday." He heard himself laying it out and hated the sound of it — the itinerary voice, the voice he used with publicists. He tried to soften it. "There's the column due Friday week, and the awards thing the following weekend, and—"

"The awards thing." Flat. Utterly flat. Somewhere in Lagos a dog began barking and did not stop. "Our mother is dead, Cyrus, and you are negotiating a five-day booking."

"That's not—"

"It's exactly what it is. You're treating her funeral like a reservation. Table for one, window seat, out before dessert." She laughed then, one dry note with no humour in it, the laugh of a woman who has stopped expecting anything and finds the absence almost funny. "You know what Mummy would say? She'd say, *at least he gave us notice.*"

"Adaeze—"

"No. Fine. Five days. I'll take five days, because five days is more than zero and I have learned, Cyrus, I have *learned*, to budget my expectations the way you budget your calendar." He heard her breathing, deliberately slowing it, the way she did before saying the true thing. "Do you want to know what she said about you? At the end?"

The kitchen went very still. Even the extractor fan seemed to hold its breath.

"She talked about you," Adaeze went on, not waiting for permission, because grief had burned through her patience months ago and left only the raw wood. "In the hospital, near the end, when she still had her mind most days. She'd ask me to read your reviews to her. Out loud. Every week. She couldn't get the paper version here so I'd print them at the business centre and read them over the phone, and she'd close her eyes and say, *my son writes food like other people write love letters.*" A pause. "She kept them, Cyrus. In a folder. In the drawer with the Christmas cards. Eleven years of your columns, and you couldn't give her five days without itemising the deductions."

Cyrus put his hand flat on the counter to steady himself against something that was not physical. The soup sat beside him going cold, smelling of everything, meaning nothing, and he understood suddenly that he was looking at his own portrait: beautiful, composed, hollow at the centre, photographed but untasted.

"I'll come," he said. His voice came out lower than he intended. "Friday. And I'll stay as long as — I'll work it out when I'm there. I can't promise from here. I don't trust myself to promise from here."

It was the closest thing to honesty he'd offered anyone in years, and perhaps Adaeze heard it, because the line went quiet in a different way — not the silence of anger but the silence of a woman recalculating.

"There's something else," she said finally. "About the house."

"What about it?"

"Nothing. Nothing yet. There are papers, and there are things I need to tell you face to face, and I'm not doing it over this connection." He heard her glance at something — a door, a window, the street. "Come Friday. Come and stand in her parlour and see what eleven years looks like from the inside. Then we'll talk."

"Ada—"

"Wake is Saturday, six o'clock. Wear something black that isn't a jacket, it's thirty-two degrees, you'll die before the eulogy." And then, softer, almost against her will, the sister underneath the administrator surfacing for one breath: "Cyrus. She waited for you to call at Christmas. She sat by that phone the whole day. I watched her do it. So whatever you're carrying — whatever it is that makes you count your days with us like coins — put it down for five days. Just five. For her."

The line held. Eight thousand kilometres of ocean and fibre and switching stations, holding two siblings in the same silence for the first time in longer than either would admit.

"I'll be there," he said.

"Bring nothing," Adaeze said. "Just bring yourself. Actually—" the smallest hitch, quickly smoothed, "—bring an appetite. The quarter will feed you whether you want it or not. You remember how they are."

He remembered. He remembered women pressing bowls into his hands before he'd finished greeting them, remembered pepper soup appearing at funerals and weddings and ordinary Tuesdays, remembered a street where cooking was a language everyone spoke fluently and he had once been considered gifted.

"I remember," he said, which was true, and also the beginning of a lie, because what he remembered was a tongue he no longer owned.

After the call ended he stood in his kitchen for a long time, the city humming below, the soup congealing gently in its photogenic bowl. Eventually he picked up his phone and opened the booking site, and his thumb hovered over the return date field — Tuesday, he'd promised himself, Tuesday night, five days, manageable, contained — and hovered, and hovered, and then, unable to explain it even to himself, he selected the flexible fare. The one that let you change your mind.

Then he ate the cold soup standing up, tasting nothing, and washed the bowl, and packed nothing, and lay awake until three in the morning listening to London pretend to be quiet, thinking about a drawer full of printed reviews, and a woman by a telephone, and a word that had surfaced unbidden in the middle of the night from some flooded basement of memory — a word he hadn't heard spoken aloud since he was a boy, a word that belonged to a shop and a street and a family that had once, impossibly, been whole:

*Radiators.*

He did not know why the word came. He only knew that when it did, he could almost — almost — taste rain on hot iron, and dust, and his grandfather's hands, and something sweet and lost that no specialist in London would ever be able to name.

* * *

Two days later, descending through brown harmattan haze toward Murtala Muhammed, Cyrus pressed his forehead to the window and watched Lagos resolve out of the smog — the water tanks, the zinc roofs, the impossible green insistence of trees between concrete — and felt the city reach up and take hold of him by the throat the way it always did, and thought, with the first honest feeling he'd had in weeks:

*Five days. God help me, I already know it won't be five days.*
