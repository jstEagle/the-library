## 3. The Old Quarter Remembers

The plane descended into Lagos the way a stone descends into water — with resistance, with noise, with the sense of being swallowed by something that had been waiting a long time to receive it. Cyrus Sandoval watched the lagoon spread beneath him, brown-green and glittering, and felt nothing he could name. That was the trouble now. Everything arrived unnamed. For three years his interior life had been a room full of furniture draped in white sheets: shapes present, surfaces covered.

He had not been home in eleven years. He knew this because Adaeze had said it on the phone, twice, in the tone she reserved for facts she intended to weaponize later. *Eleven years, Cyrus. You count them, I'll wait.* He had not corrected her. There was no correcting Adaeze; there was only surviving her.

At Murtala Muhammed Airport the heat met him like a relative — familiar, insistent, holding on too long. It pressed through his linen shirt within seconds and settled into the back of his neck, and Cyrus stood in the arrivals hall among trolleys and shouting drivers and a woman selling phone cards from a tray strapped to her chest, and he breathed in, out of habit rather than hope.

And the smell came.

That was the cruel arithmetic of his condition, the part no doctor had ever quite explained to his satisfaction: he had lost taste but not smell, or not entirely, or not cleanly — some muddled borderland where the two senses had once held hands and now stood apart, refusing to speak. The air of Lagos entered him whole. Diesel and dust and frying oil and the green-rot sweetness of the drainage channels and, underneath it all, something floral and warm he could not place until it placed itself: *scent leaf*, someone's kitchen already at work somewhere beyond the terminal walls, and his body responded — mouth flooding, stomach lifting — while his tongue sat in his head like a hired man who had stopped doing the job but refused to resign.

He could smell the food. He simply could not know it.

A driver named Emeka held a placard with SANDOVAL spelled in marker so confident it had begun to run, and Cyrus followed him out into the light, which was not light exactly but a kind of golden pressure, and gave himself over to the road.

* * *

Lagos traffic was the one honest thing left in his life, he decided somewhere between the airport and the mainland. It did not pretend. It did not flatter. It simply was — an ocean of brake lights and yellow danfo buses with scripture painted across their tails, GOD'S TIME IS THE BEST, NO CONDITION IS PERMANENT. He read the slogans the way other men read horoscopes. *No condition is permanent.* He wanted to believe it applied to tongues as well as fortunes.

Emeka talked the entire way. About the roads, about the government, about his second wife, about the price of cement. Cyrus answered in the intervals where answers seemed expected, and discovered that his London voice — the measured, faintly amused baritone that had made his column famous, the voice editors described as *wry* and *elegant* — sounded absurd here, like wearing a dinner jacket to a market. So he let it go. By the third hour he was saying *abeg* without irony and laughing at jokes he only half understood, and something in his chest loosened by a single degree.

They crossed into the old quarter in the late afternoon, when the sun had gone low enough to turn every wall the color of strong tea.

Cyrus saw it before Emeka said anything — the way the man slowed, almost respectfully, as though entering a church whose congregation had mostly died. The street narrowed. The buildings leaned toward each other overhead like old women sharing gossip across a compound wall. And there, on the left, where the road bent around a stubborn patch of shade, stood the shop.

Or what remained of it.

The radiator shop had been a long, low building once, corrugated iron over concrete block, its wide front opening rolled up each morning so that the street itself seemed to walk inside. His grandfather's kingdom. Now the roller door was a skeleton of rust, half-frozen mid-rise, and behind it sat darkness and the pale geometry of abandoned machinery — a vice still bolted to a workbench, a drum of something long since evaporated, the great quenching tank where radiators had been dunked and hissed like complaining animals. Someone had hung laundry across the doorway. A child's bicycle lay on its side in the weeds growing up through the cracked apron of concrete.

Cyrus asked Emeka to stop. He got out. He stood in the middle of the street like a man waiting for a photograph to develop.

The smell hit him first, and it undid him more thoroughly than the sight. Rust, yes — but rust is a smell of blood and time, and under it the ghost of solder flux, of engine oil baked into concrete for forty years, of hot metal quenched in water. His grandfather's shop had smelled like this. His childhood had smelled like this. The odor rose off the ground as if the street itself were exhaling after holding its breath since 1989, and Cyrus stood in the middle of it with his eyes closed and his useless tongue pressed to the roof of his mouth, and he thought: *I can remember how this tasted. I can remember standing here at seven years old with a strip of dried fish, tasting rust and smoke and salt all at once.*

Memory, he was learning, was the cruelest form of taste. It gave you everything except the thing itself.

"You grew here?" Emeka asked from the car window, gently, for a talkative man.

"Next street over. The house." Cyrus pointed without looking. "The shop was my grandfather's."

"Ah." Emeka nodded slowly, the nod of a man receiving news he already suspected. "This whole street, everybody knew Radiator. My own father carry our own one come here from Surulere. Before the trouble."

Cyrus turned. "What trouble?"

But Emeka had already become fascinated by his side mirror, adjusting it with great concentration, and would not be drawn further. Cyrus filed the word away — *the trouble*, definite article, capital T in the voice if not on the page — and got back into the car.

* * *

The family house stood three doors down from the shop, set back behind a low wall that had once been white and was now the color of old teeth. It was a solid 1960s building, flat-roofed, deep-eaved, built in the era when people built houses the way they built marriages — for duration, without much decoration, expecting weather. The burglar-proof bars on the windows had bloomed orange. A rain gutter hung loose on the right side like a broken arm. The blue paint of the front door — his mother's blue, chosen the year Cyrus was born, repainted every harmattan by her own hand until her hands couldn't — had faded to a bruised grey-violet, peeling in long curls that lay on the step like fallen petals.

Adaeze was waiting on the veranda, and she looked older than the house.

She wore black, of course, though the funeral was days away; Adaeze had been in mourning colors since the phone call, possibly since before it, as though she'd had the grief pre-ordered. Her hair was wrapped plainly. She had their mother's broad face and their mother's habit of standing with her arms folded not defensively but judicially, as if the world were an argument she was prepared to hear out.

"Cyrus."

"Ada."

They embraced. It lasted four seconds, which for the Sandoval siblings constituted a record. Over her shoulder he saw the state of the veranda — the wooden louvres of the front windows gone grey with rot, two panes missing entirely, a plastic chair with a torn seat angled toward the street as though someone had been keeping watch.

"You're thinner," Adaeze said, holding him at arm's length and inspecting him the way she inspected everything, for defects.

"You're the same."

"I'm older. Look properly." But there was the ghost of a smile, quickly suppressed, and then she was turning, leading him inside, talking logistics — the wake would be Thursday, the service Saturday, Aunt Bisi arriving tomorrow with half of Abeokuta in tow, had he eaten, of course he hadn't, there was soup on the stove.

Inside, the house smelled of camphor and disuse and, faintly, of yesterday's stew — and Cyrus stood in the dim hallway while his sister's voice went on about arrangements, and let the smell of the place walk through him.

Every house has a smell that is really a biography. This one began with kerosene and floor polish, moved through mothballs and Bible-paper, detoured into the sharp medicinal era of his mother's last illness, and ended here, in this thinned-out present tense, where the rooms had begun to forget themselves. The parlor curtains were drawn against the heat. In the half-dark he could see the furniture shrouded in the white lace antimacassars his mother had crocheted in another century, the framed photographs on the wall — his grandfather stern above the shop, his parents' wedding, himself at graduation looking unbearably pleased with himself — all of it filmed with the fine grey dust that is the signature of absence.

"The squatters are gone?" he asked.

"For now." Adaeze's voice flattened. "Two families were living here when Mama went into the hospital. I paid them to leave. One went. The other—" she gestured upward, vaguely, toward the back of the house, "—the woman, Mrs. Eze, says she'll go after the burial. She cooked for Mama, near the end. When I couldn't be here every day." A pause, loaded. "Somebody had to be here, Cyrus."

"I know."

"Do you?" Not cruel. Just level, the way she'd learned to say things during the eleven years he'd given her plenty of practice.

He didn't answer. He walked into the kitchen instead, because the kitchen was the room he feared and therefore the room he needed to see first.

It was small and dark-tiled and perfect in its ruin. The mortar between the tiles had gone black. A pot sat on the cold stove under a woven lid. On the wall beside the window, where sunlight used to fall in the mornings, the paint had peeled back in a map-shaped wound, and beneath it — he stepped closer, heart performing a slow, stupid stumble — there were marks. Faint, in ballpoint, in his mother's slanted hand, half-legible through decades of steam and scrubbing:

*pepper soup — more uziza than you think. she knows.*

*she knows*. As if the recipe itself were a person being addressed. As if his mother had written letters to the future on the walls of her kitchen and trusted the future to keep them.

Cyrus put his fingers against the writing. The wall was cool. Behind him Adaeze had stopped talking, and when he glanced back she was watching him with an expression he couldn't read — or could read too well, and chose not to.

"She wrote on the walls," he said. "All these years, and I never—"

"She wrote everywhere." Adaeze came to stand beside him, arms still folded. "You weren't here to see it."

The sentence landed without force, which somehow made it worse. Force he could have deflected. This just sank.

From the back of the house, through the corridor, came a sound: the scrape of a pot, the soft pad of bare feet. Then a woman's voice, calling out mildly, in the tone of someone who has lived in a house long enough to feel entitled to announce herself —

"Adaeze? Your brother has come? Tell him there is pepper soup. It will make him cry, but it is good pepper soup."

Adaeze's jaw tightened almost imperceptibly. "Mrs. Eze," she said. "I told you about her."

Cyrus stared down the dark corridor, toward the voice, toward the simmering pot and the woman who had cooked for his dying mother, and felt the house shift around him — the way old houses do at dusk, settling, remembering, making room.

Pepper soup. Of all things.

"Bring it," he heard himself say, before caution could reach him. "I'll take it in here."

Adaeze looked at him for a long moment, and outside, far down the street, a generator coughed awake, and somewhere a radio found a highlife song it had been looking for all day, and the old quarter went on remembering things its children preferred to forget.
