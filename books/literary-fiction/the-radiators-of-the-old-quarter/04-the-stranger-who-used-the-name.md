## 4. The Stranger Who Used the Name

The wake began the way Lagos wakes always began: with too many plastic chairs and not enough shade, with a generator coughing somewhere behind the house like an old man clearing his throat, and with women arriving in waves of ankara — indigo, rust, gold — each print announcing a family, a church, a grievance. Cyrus stood at the edge of the compound with a bottle of warm Fanta he had no intention of drinking and watched his mother's life assemble itself into an audience.

Three years in London had taught him to read rooms the way other men read menus. He could tell you which of these women had cooked since dawn (the ones whose wrappers were tied high and loose, hands still smelling of stockfish), which had come only to be seen weeping (the ones whose head-ties were immaculate, whose eyes stayed dry until they caught someone watching), and which had come for Adaeze (the cluster of middle-aged women near the gate who kept glancing at his sister with that particular pity that is also assessment). He catalogued all of it automatically, professionally, the way he once catalogued a dining room in Mayfair — and then hated himself for it, because this was not a service. This was his mother lying in a borrowed coffin under a canopy strung with fairy lights that flickered when the generator stuttered.

Funmilayo Sandoval had wanted, according to Adaeze's phone call, to be buried quickly and without fuss. She got neither. The old quarter did not permit its matriarchs to leave without ceremony; ceremony was the rent the living paid on their own eventual deaths. So there would be three days of sitting, of hymns sung slightly off-key by a choir of aunties, of visitors who ate rice and stew from coolers and spoke about Funmilayo in the past tense as though testing whether the words held weight.

Cyrus had been in the house two days and had not yet gone inside beyond the front room. The house itself was a rebuke. Its walls wore their age openly now — paint gone the grey-green of old copper, a crack running up the eastern corner like a vein. When he was a boy it had seemed enormous, a palace of corridors and secrets. Now it looked shrunken, embarrassed, a woman who had once dressed well and could no longer afford the dry cleaner. He kept finding himself standing in doorways, unable to remember why he'd entered.

"You will greet people," Aunt Bisi said, materializing beside him with the suddenness she'd always possessed, a heavy woman in navy lace who moved like weather. "You cannot stand here holding that Fanta like a microphone you're afraid to speak into."

"I'm greeting people."

"You are observing people. There is a difference. Your mother's friends want to see your face. They remember you as a boy who ate everything."

*Who ate everything.* The phrase landed in him like a stone dropped down a well. He waited for the splash. It didn't come.

"Bisi," he said, "I'm tired."

"Tired." She took the Fanta from him and drank from it herself, a small act of confiscation that was also forgiveness. "Your sister has not slept. Your uncle Dele flew in from Abuja and has already argued with two people about parking. And you are tired. Fine. Be tired. But do it nearer the chairs."

He let her steer him into the compound proper, into the smell of the gathering — woodsmoke and fried plantain and the green bite of scent leaf drifting from the cooking pots at the back, smells that reached him fully, vividly, and stopped at some border just behind his teeth. That was the cruelty of his condition that no doctor had quite explained: nothing was numb. His nose worked. His memory worked. It was only the last inch of the journey, the arrival, that had gone dark. He lived in a country he could see perfectly but never enter.

* * *

The afternoon thickened toward evening. More chairs. More hymns. A man Cyrus vaguely recognized as a former neighbor gave a speech about Funmilayo's generosity that contained three anecdotes Cyrus knew to be invented, delivered with such conviction that Cyrus found himself almost believing them — a professional admiration he immediately suppressed. Adaeze sat at the front receiving condolences with her hands folded, her face composed into something between grief and bookkeeping. She had flown back from Enugu a week before the death, when it became clear the end was coming, and she had done everything — the hospital, the undertaker, the church, the food — while Cyrus negotiated his five days from a kitchen island in London. He could feel her awareness of this the way you feel sun through a window: not touching him, but warming everything she turned away from.

It was nearly six o'clock, the light going amber and long across the compound, when the stranger arrived.

She came through the gate alone, unhurried, in a plain dress the color of unsweetened cocoa, carrying nothing but a small handbag and herself. Young — younger than Cyrus, maybe thirty, maybe less — with close-cropped hair and a way of walking that suggested she had already looked at everything in the compound once and was now merely confirming it. She did not go first to the condolence register. She did not join the queue of women waiting to embrace Adaeze. She stood for a moment just inside the gate, scanning the chairs, the canopy, the house itself, with an attention so deliberate it was almost tactile, and Cyrus — who had spent fifteen years noticing how people entered rooms — felt the hair rise on his forearms.

She was reading the house. Not looking at it. Reading it.

Then she moved, threading between the chairs with an ease that said she knew where every one of them would be before she arrived, and went straight to Adaeze.

Cyrus watched it happen from twenty feet away. He saw the young woman take both of Adaeze's hands. He saw Adaeze's polite condolence-face begin its practiced softening — the face she put on for strangers — and then falter, because the stranger was speaking before Adaeze could arrange anything, leaning in, and whatever she said made Adaeze's eyebrows move.

And then the stranger embraced her. Not the sideways, air-kiss embrace of acquaintances at funerals. A full embrace, arms around the shoulders, cheek to cheek, held a beat too long, the embrace of family — and over Adaeze's shoulder her eyes found Cyrus across the compound, found him already watching, and held him.

Then she released Adaeze, stepped back, and said — clearly, warmly, in a voice pitched to carry exactly as far as the nearest dozen chairs and no farther:

"Please, forgive me for coming late. I couldn't miss mourning Mama Funmi. I grew up on her stories." She pressed her hand briefly to her chest. "The Radiators have always been good to my family."

The word went through the compound like current through wet rope.

Cyrus heard it before he understood it — heard the silence arrive half a second after the sound, the particular silence of forty people stopping at once, spoons pausing in bowls, a hymn dying mid-verse somewhere near the canopy. Not the hush of respect. The hush of a trapdoor.

*The Radiators.*

Aunt Bisi's hand closed on Cyrus's forearm hard enough to leave marks.

Nobody moved. The stranger stood in the middle of the compound as though she had announced the time of day. A fly walked across the rim of an untouched glass of malt. Somewhere behind the house the generator stuttered, recovered, stuttered again.

Adaeze recovered first, because Adaeze always recovered first. "I'm sorry," Cyrus heard her say, her voice doing something complicated — courtesy stretched over confusion stretched over something harder. "I don't think — have we met? I'm Adaeze. This is our mother's wake."

"I know whose wake it is." The stranger smiled, and it was a real smile, unarmored, which somehow made it worse. "I'm Ifeoma. Ifeoma Adeyemi." She let the surname sit in the air for exactly long enough. "My father used to talk about this street the way other men talk about the women they loved. I feel I've known all of you my whole life."

"Your father?" said Uncle Dele, appearing from wherever uncles appear from, his voice already carrying the flatness of a man preparing to end a conversation.

"He's dead," Ifeoma said simply. "Eleven years now. Which is partly why I'm here. Family should be told."

*Told.* The word had edges. Bisi's grip on Cyrus's arm tightened further, and he realized she had gone very still in a way that had nothing to do with grief.

"We should sit you down," Adaeze said, in her hostess voice, her crisis voice, steering toward the register and the rice and the safe machinery of hospitality. "You'll sign the book. You'll eat something."

"I'll eat something," Ifeoma agreed pleasantly, and followed her to the table, and the compound exhaled, and the hymn started up again uncertainly, like a conversation resumed after a stranger passes.

But the word stayed in the air. Cyrus could feel it hanging over the chairs, over the canopy, over the peeling house itself — *the Radiators* — spoken aloud, casually, affectionately, by a woman young enough that she could not possibly remember the thing the word named. He turned to ask Bisi what it meant, and found her already looking at him, and her face stopped the question in his throat.

Because Bisi was frightened. Not startled. Not confused. Frightened — the specific, disciplined fright of a woman watching a locked door open from the inside.

* * *

She pulled him aside ten minutes later, through the side passage where the water tanks stood and the smell of the cooking pots was strongest, away from the chairs and the choir and the register. Her hand never left his arm. In the amber light her face had lost its funeral composure entirely.

"Where did you find her?" Cyrus asked. "Is she a friend of Mummy's? Some church—"

"She is nobody's friend." Bisi said it fast, low. "Listen to me. That name. Where did you hear it last?"

Cyrus searched. The word sat in his childhood like furniture he'd stopped seeing: *the Radiators*. It had been a shop — his grandfather's shop, the radiator repair place at the top of the street, the one in the black-and-white photograph that hung in the corridor of the London flat, the one nobody ever talked about except as scenery. Grandpa's shop. Before. He'd assumed the name died with the shop, or with the quarter's interest in it, or simply with time, the way names do.

"I don't know," he said. "Years. Decades. Nobody says it?"

"Nobody has said that word since 1989." Bisi's voice dropped further. "Not in this house, not on this street, not at any wedding or burial or naming ceremony I have attended in thirty-five years. Do you understand what I'm telling you? We are a family that can say *cancer* and *debt* and *your father left us* without flinching. But that word—" She shook her head. "That word we buried deeper than your mother. And this girl walks in off the street and says it like a greeting."

"Maybe she heard it somewhere. Old neighbors talk. Maybe it means nothing—"

"Cyrus." Bisi took his chin in her hand, the way she had when he was nine and lying about a broken plate. "She said *the Radiators have always been good to my family.* You heard it. Good *to* her family. As in owed. As in a debt being acknowledged." She released him. "And then she said her father is dead and family should be told. Whose family? Told what?"

Through the gap between the water tanks Cyrus could see the compound again: the chairs, the canopy, Adaeze seated rigidly beside the register while Ifeoma signed her name in it — he watched her write, unhurried, the pen moving longer than a signature requires, an address perhaps, or a message — and then return the pen and smile at Adaeze, who smiled back with the exact expression of a woman holding a door shut against wind.

"She knows something," Cyrus said.

"Of course she knows something. People who know nothing don't choose words like that. People who know nothing say *sorry for your loss* and eat their rice." Bisi smoothed her lace front with both hands, a gesture Cyrus had seen her perform before every difficult thing she'd ever done. "The question is what she knows, and who sent her, and why now — your mother is not yet in the ground, Cyrus. Not yet in the ground, and somebody has come to collect."

"That's a terrible thing to say at a wake."

"It's a true thing to say at a wake. Your mother taught me the difference." For a moment something moved behind Bisi's eyes — grief, actual grief, surfacing through the alarm — and then it submerged again. "Stay away from that girl. Let Adaeze handle her. Adaeze handles things."

"Adaeze handles things because I never come home," Cyrus said, and was surprised to hear it leave his mouth.

Bisi looked at him for a long moment. Whatever she saw in his face made her sigh, a big theatrical sigh that was also completely sincere. "Eat something," she said finally. "There is pepper soup at the back. Mama Ngozi made it. Your mother's recipe." She patted his cheek once, hard, the way she used to. "And Cyrus — if that girl speaks to you, if she uses that word to you — you come to me before you answer her. Promise me."

He promised, because promising was easier than asking the question underneath, which was: *why does a word need guarding? What is a family protecting when it protects a silence?*

He didn't go for the pepper soup. He stood in the passage a while longer, listening to the hymn and the generator and the small sounds of a community feeding itself around a coffin, and he thought about the stranger's eyes finding his across the compound — not Adaeze's, not Dele's, not the eldest son's, though he was the celebrated one, the one whose name appeared in print. Hers had found *his*, and held, and in them he had seen something he recognized from his own mirror: the look of a person performing calm while something enormous stands behind them, waiting to be said.

When he finally returned to the compound, the light had gone. The fairy lights buzzed overhead. Ifeoma's chair by the register was empty, her line in the book drying in the heat.

Cyrus crossed to it when no one was watching and looked down.

Below her name, below the address in Surulere, she had written a single sentence in a small, level hand:

*I am sorry I never met her. My father loved her very much.*

He stood there until the ink blurred — from the heat, he told himself, from the heat — and somewhere behind him, faintly, the choir began another hymn, and the word *Radiators* hung in the dark above the whole compound like smoke that would not rise.
