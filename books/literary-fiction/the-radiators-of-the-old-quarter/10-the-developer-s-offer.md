## 10. The Developer's Offer

The letter arrived on a Thursday, which meant nothing to Cyrus except that it was the day Mama Ngozi taught him to pound yam and his shoulder still ached from it in a way he had come to treasure, the way a man treasures any proof that his body is being used for something honest. It was hand-delivered, which was itself a message. Nobody in Lagos hand-delivers a letter unless they want you to know they could have found you anywhere.

He was sitting on the veranda of the family house, in the chair that had been his father's, with a notebook open on his knee and nothing written in it. The morning was doing what Lagos mornings did — pressing its wet heat against his skin like a palm, filling the compound with the smell of dust waking up, of the gutter breathing, of someone two houses down frying plantain in oil that had been used too many times. He could smell all of it. He could describe all of it. He simply could not taste any of it, and this was the arithmetic of his life now, the sum he carried everywhere: perception without possession.

The envelope was cream-colored, heavy stock, embossed with a logo he didn't recognize — a stylized sun rising over what might have been a skyline or might have been a row of teeth. Inside, on paper almost as heavy as the envelope, was a letter from a company called Meridian Horizon Development Partners, and it began, *Dear Esteemed Heir*, which told Cyrus everything before the second sentence told him more.

*We are pleased to submit an initial offer for the acquisition of the parcel of land and improvements situated at and around [the family house], Old Quarter, Lagos Island, as part of our flagship regeneration project...*

The figure was on the third line. Cyrus read it twice, then a third time, because the first two times his eyes had slid off it the way eyes slide off anything too large to be believed. It was not a fortune in the way novels say *fortune*, all silks and steamships. It was a fortune in the modern way: a number with enough zeroes to make a man's entire London life look like a rounding error. Enough to buy the flat in Islington outright and another one to rent out. Enough to never write another word for money again, if he wanted — though of course he would keep writing, because the writing was the only part of him that had never lied about being hungry.

*This offer is contingent upon the execution of sale agreements by all legal heirs of the estate of the late Funmilayo Adeyemi Sandoval,* the letter continued, and here Cyrus felt something shift in his chest, a small cold settling, like a stone dropped into a well whose depth you had always assumed you knew. *All legal heirs.* He read the phrase again. He thought of Ifeoma, who was at that moment, he knew, in the kitchen — she had taken to spending her mornings there, learning the stove, learning the pots, learning the house the way you learn a language by living inside it rather than studying it. He thought of what she had shown him in the tin under the floorboards, the letters, the deed fragment with two names where the family's memory held only one.

*All legal heirs.* The developers did not know what they were asking. Or perhaps they knew exactly, and did not care; a clause was a clause, and ambiguity was a lawyer's problem, not theirs.

He set the letter on his knee and looked out at the street. A danfo ground past, conductor's arm swinging. Two children chased a deflated football across the compound gate. The old quarter went about its business, indifferent to the fact that its death had been priced, notarized, and slipped through a gate in a cream envelope.

He should have felt tempted. That was the strange part. He sat there waiting for temptation the way you wait for a bus you have already decided not to board, and what came instead was something quieter and more troubling: relief. The pure, shameful relief of a man being handed an exit. Sell the house, sign the paper, fly back to London, and never have to stand in this parlor again while a stranger's eyes asked him a question the whole family had spent thirty-five years arranging itself around. Money, he understood now, was not primarily a temptation toward anything. It was a temptation *away* — away from the tin under the floor, away from Tunde's name, away from the slow, humiliating apprenticeship of a man relearning what his tongue had forgotten. Escape, dressed in a suit, holding a pen.

He heard her footsteps before he saw her — Ifeoma moved through the house with a particular gait, unhurried, deliberate, the walk of someone who had decided the house would learn to recognize her. She came out onto the veranda with two cups of tea, set one beside him without asking, and sat on the step below his chair, the way younger relatives did, the way, he supposed, her father might once have sat.

"You've read it," she said. It was not a question. She had seen the envelope in his hand from the kitchen window, or she had simply known; Cyrus was beginning to suspect she knew things by a mechanism that had nothing to do with seeing.

"They want everyone to sign," he said. "All legal heirs."

"Yes." She blew on her tea. "I received mine yesterday. Hand-delivered. A very polite man on a motorbike who seemed surprised I was the one who opened the gate." She smiled without much humor in it. "They did their research. They know exactly how many of us there are. That's the first thing these people do — count the heirs. It's how they know the price."

"And are you one? Legally?" He asked it carefully, the way you test ice. "The deed fragment—"

"The deed fragment is a fragment." She said it steadily, but he heard the iron under it. "A promise written down is not the same as a promise witnessed. My father learned that difference at the cost of everything. But the lawyers will enjoy arguing about it. And the developers would rather pay me off than fight me in court for three years. So yes. I am one. I am, at the moment, the most expensive one."

Cyrus looked at her — this woman who had walked into a wake and spoken a dead word into a silent parlor, who had stayed when every polite instinct said leave, who carried her father's letters in a tin like a heart in a box — and he understood that she was not here for money. She had had a month to ask for money. She had asked for nothing but the name.

"They'll call a family meeting," he said.

"They've already called it. Adaeze told me this morning." A pause. "Adaeze is very happy about the letter, Cyrus. Have you noticed? She's been humming."

* * *

The family convened on Saturday, in the parlor, under the ceiling fan that turned with the weary authority of a thing that had outlived everyone who installed it. Cyrus counted them the way the developers had counted them: Adaeze, upright in the front chair as though she had been rehearsing; Aunt Bisi fanning herself with the letter itself, which she had apparently decided was best used as a weapon; two cousins from Surulere he barely knew, already on their phones; Uncle Segun, who appeared at every family crisis like a vulture that kept a calendar; and Ifeoma, seated slightly apart, on a wooden chair brought in from the kitchen, because no one had offered her a place on the sofa and no one had asked her to leave, and she had made her peace with both.

The man from Meridian Horizon was named Mr. Balogun. He was younger than his suit, polished and patient, with the particular warmth of a man who has learned that warmth closes deals faster than pressure. He spread documents on the center table like a man laying out a feast.

"What we are proposing," he said, "is not simply a purchase. It is a *restoration* of this district's dignity." He had a slideshow on a tablet he turned toward them at intervals: renderings of glass and pale wood, a promenade, fountains. The old quarter airbrushed into a place where the old quarter had never existed. "Boutique residences. A cultural pavilion — we are very committed to heritage, you understand. There may even be a plaque." He said *plaque* the way other men say *monument*. "The parcel cannot proceed without full consensus. Every heir. That is our commitment to fairness — no one is left behind. And the number—" he let the number sit in the air among them, "—is divided per stirpes. Equitably. Everyone walks away whole."

Cyrus watched what the money did to the room, and it was like watching water find every crack in a wall at once. The cousins stopped looking at their phones. Uncle Segun leaned forward and began, immediately, to speak of *the family's interest* in a tone that made clear the family's interest and Segun's interest had long ago merged. Aunt Bisi's fan went still. And Adaeze — Adaeze sat with her hands folded and her spine straight, and Cyrus saw on her face something he had never seen there in all his life: ease. Thirty years of staying, of sacrificing, of being the one who did not leave, of being the one who paid — and here, at last, was the invoice. She was not greedy. She was *owed*, and the developer had read that off her in the first five minutes the way Cyrus read a menu.

"The house is falling down," Adaeze said, and her voice was calm, almost gentle. "The roof leaks. The wiring would burn us in our sleep. Mama is gone. What exactly are we keeping? A building? Or are we keeping a wound?"

Nobody answered, because nobody had an answer that wasn't the wound itself.

It was Uncle Segun who raised the problem, and he raised it with a lawyer's delicacy, tilting his head toward Ifeoma as though she were a piece of furniture that had learned to talk. "There is, of course, the question of the... additional party. The young lady's claim is — untested. Sentimental. A fragment of paper and some letters. The company's lawyers will want certainty. Perhaps the family could come to an internal arrangement first. A smaller allocation, in exchange for a waiver. A graceful settlement."

*Graceful.* Cyrus heard the word do its work — the old word, the family word, the word that had been used in 1989 to make a man's erasure sound like a kindness. He watched Ifeoma receive it. She did not flinch. She folded her hands in her lap and looked at Segun with an expression of such level, unhurried attention that he eventually looked away first.

"I have read the letters," she said quietly, to the room, to no one, to Cyrus. "All of them. My father wrote to this family for nineteen years. Do you know what he asked for, in nineteen years of letters? Not the house. Not once. He asked them to tell his children they existed." She let that stand in the parlor with the fan turning above it. "So no. I will not be waiving anything. And I will not be settled *gracefully*. I will be settled truthfully, or not at all."

The room went the way rooms go when someone says a true thing in a place built on an untrue thing — a silence with a pressure behind it, like the moment before a storm makes up its mind. It was Aunt Bisi, of all people, who broke it, her voice thin: "The girl should not speak of matters that were settled before she was born."

"Settled," Ifeoma repeated, tasting the word. "Yes. That is the word my family would use too."

* * *

Afterward, Cyrus found Adaeze in the kitchen, standing at the window with her back to him, and for a moment he simply watched his sister — the sister who had stayed, whose hands had done everything his hands had escaped — and saw that she was holding the developer's letter folded small in her fist, tight, the way you hold a ticket.

"You're going to sign," he said.

"I'm going to *live*," she corrected, without turning. "There's a difference, and you of all people should learn it. You've been living on descriptions your whole life, Cyrus. Mama's house, Mama's stories, Mama's Lagos — you ate them secondhand and called it a life. I did the actual eating. I stayed. I cooked. I buried what needed burying, including—" her voice caught, barely, and smoothed itself over, "—including certain things. And now the world is offering to pay that bill. With interest. And you want to stand in the doorway of the kitchen and talk to me about signing."

"I haven't said anything."

"You don't have to. You have the same face you had at the wake. The face of a man delaying." She turned then, and her eyes were wet and furious and older than her face. "Delay is a decision, Cyrus. It just hasn't admitted it yet. Sign the paper. Take the money. Go back to London and write about food you can't taste — at least be honest with yourself that that's what you're choosing."

She left him standing in the kitchen that smelled of yesterday's pepper soup, and Cyrus stood a long time at the window she had abandoned, looking out at the yard, at the wall where — he knew now, Mama Ngozi had told him, in her slow confessional way, over a mortar and pestle — his mother had once taught a disowned brother's daughter's recipes to nobody, cooking for a son she could not acknowledge, writing her loyalty into the plaster where only the house could read it.

He went to his room and took out the cream envelope and the pen, and laid the contract on the desk, and read the figure one more time — escape, in a suit, holding out a pen — and found that his hand would not move. Not from principle. He was not at all sure it was principle. It was something smaller and more physical: the same thing that had happened with the pepper soup on Mama Ngozi's dare, the flicker, the heat without meaning. A feeling in him that the paper was asking him to swallow something, and some buried, reawakening part of him — the part that had wept into a bowl — refused to swallow.

He did not sign. He put the pen down, and the not-signing sat in the room with him like a second person.

It was nearly dark when the knock came — soft, twice, at the front door, and when he opened it there was a boy on the step, one of the compound children, holding a note in a fist gone soft with sweat. "Aunty said to give you this," the boy said, and was gone before Cyrus could ask which aunty.

The note was in Mama Ngozi's hand, lopsided and sure, in the handwriting of a woman who had never needed anyone's permission to write what she wrote:

*Come tomorrow. Early. Before the family wakes. There is something about the house your mother told me the night before she died, and I am too old to keep it, and too old to say it twice.*

Cyrus stood in the doorway holding the note, the unsigned contract behind him on the desk, and felt the old quarter shift beneath him like a floorboard lifting.
