## 12. Tunde, Named

Ifeoma had learned the house the way you learn a person — by its silences first.

There was the silence of the back room where the roof leaked and nobody repaired it, because repairing it would mean admitting the house was still worth keeping. There was the silence of the corridor outside Funmilayo's bedroom, which everyone walked through quickly, as though grief were a landlord who charged by the hour. And there was the silence that lived in Cyrus Sandoval himself, a silence she had been circling for weeks now the way water circles a drain, patient, certain of the center.

She found him on the veranda on an evening in late October, when the harmattan was still only a rumor and the air held both dust and rain in suspension. He sat in his mother's old rattan chair with a notebook open on his knee, not writing. The pen rested in the gutter of the notebook like something abandoned mid-surgery. Beyond the low wall, the quarter was doing what it always did at dusk: frying onions, arguing over football, sending up its smoke and its music and its children's voices in equal measure. Ifeoma stood in the doorway for a long moment before he noticed her, and she used that moment to study him — this man whose face she knew from magazine photographs, from her father's desk drawer, from the one grainy newspaper clipping Tunde Radiator had kept in a plastic sleeve as if it were a relic of a saint rather than a review of a restaurant in London.

He looked thinner than the photographs. He looked like a man carrying a question he had not yet permitted himself to ask.

"You're not writing," she said.

"I'm resting my hand." He closed the notebook without embarrassment. There was a courtesy to Cyrus that she had come to recognize as armor — the politeness of a man who had learned early that charm was cheaper than truth and lasted longer at dinner parties.

"May I sit?"

"It's your veranda," he said, and then, hearing it, flinched slightly. "I mean—"

"I know what you mean." She took the wooden stool by the wall, the low one, the one that put her looking up at him. She had chosen it deliberately. What she had to say would be easier for him to hear from below, from someone smaller, someone who could not be mistaken for an accuser. "Cyrus. I need to tell you something, and I've been waiting three weeks to say it because I wanted to say it properly, and because I was afraid."

The evening sounds filled the pause. Somewhere down the street Mama Ngozi's radio was playing King Sunny Adé, the guitar lines spilling out the window like water finding every crack.

"Afraid of what?" he asked.

"Afraid you'd stop seeing me the way you see me now." She folded her hands. "Right now you see me as a stranger who said a strange word at a funeral. That's a small thing. It can be explained away. Aunt Bisi has already half-explained it away — shock, grief, a girl who heard the name somewhere. But what I have to tell you is not small, and once I tell it, you will either let me into this family or you will never look at me again, and I have grown fond of being looked at."

Cyrus set the notebook on the floor beside the chair leg, carefully, the way men handle things when they want their hands to have nothing else to do. "Say it," he said.

* * *

She had rehearsed this speech perhaps two hundred times. In the shower at Adaeze's guest room. On the walk back from the market, weaving through okada fumes and hawking children. Lying awake under the mosquito net while the geckos ticked in the ceiling. Every version had been eloquent. Every version had begun with her grandfather's shop, the radiators, the street that had named itself after the heat that came off them in the dry season — and every version had collapsed somewhere around 1989, the year the family's story developed its hole.

So she abandoned the speech entirely and began where the hole began.

"My father's name was Tunde Sandoval," she said. "Before he was anything else, before he was my father, before he was poor — that was his name. Tunde Sandoval. Son of Ezekiel Sandoval, who owned the radiator shop on this street, who fixed the cars of half of Lagos Island and fed the other half from the pot his wife kept going behind the workshop. Tunde was the second son. The first was your father, Kunle. And there was a daughter between them — Funmilayo."

Cyrus did not move. But something moved in him; she saw it pass across his face like the shadow of a bird crossing a wall.

"Tunde grew up in this house," Ifeoma continued. "He slept in the back room — the one with the leaking roof. He learned engines at his father's elbow. When Ezekiel's hands started shaking, it was Tunde who kept the shop running, four years, five years, while Kunle was in England making money and Funmilayo was learning to cook from the women of the quarter. The shop survived on Tunde. This house survived on Tunde. The zinc sheets on the roof in those years were bought with Tunde's sweat. I'm not telling you this to make a claim yet. I'm telling you so you understand that everything you're sitting inside of passed through my father's hands on its way to yours."

"The name," Cyrus said quietly. "At the wake. 'The Radiators.' You called us—"

"Because that's what we were. All of us. The street called the whole household the Radiators — Ezekiel, his wife, the children, even the apprentices who slept in the shop. It wasn't a surname. It was a belonging. You didn't have to be born into it. You had to be warmed by it." Her voice caught, and she let it catch; she had decided that hiding her feeling would be its own kind of lie. "My father loved that name. He told me about it when I was small. He'd say, *Ifeoma, we were the Radiators, we were the people the street came to when they had no one else.* And then he'd go quiet, because the name was also the wound. The last time anyone said it aloud in this family's presence was the day my father was erased from it."

"Erased how?" Cyrus asked. But she thought — she was almost sure — that he already knew. That some part of him had known since the wake, since Aunt Bisi pulled him aside with her face drained of color, since Adaeze began telling the story of the schism with a hole in the middle of it and watched her brother across the table to see if he'd notice.

"He married my mother," Ifeoma said. "Ngozi Adeyemi. A Yoruba Muslim woman from a family of weavers in Iseyin. Not Christian, not Igbo, not approved, not educated past secondary school, not dowry-able in the eyes of Ezekiel's wife — your grandmother — who had plans for Tunde involving a banker's daughter and a plot in Lekki. Tunde chose my mother anyway. And the family—" She stopped. Breathed. "The family did not choose him back. Ezekiel was dead by then; it was your grandmother who ruled the house, and she ruled it absolutely. She stood in that parlor — that same parlor where we sat shiva for your mother three weeks ago — and she told my father that if he walked out the door with that woman, he should not walk back through it. And he walked out. And she made the rest of the family make it true. No letters answered. No visits. His name removed from the family ledger. Adaeze won't remember it — she was a baby. But your mother remembered. Your mother was eleven years old when her brother walked out of this house, and she ran after him to the corner, and he carried her back on his shoulders, and that was the last time they touched until—"

"Until when?" Cyrus's voice had gone thin.

"Until she started cooking for him in secret. Twenty-two years later. But I'm ahead of myself." Ifeoma reached into the cloth bag at her feet and brought out the bundle she had carried across Lagos and across years: oilcloth tied with twine, soft at the edges from handling. "Do you know how my father died, Cyrus?"

"No," he said. "Adaeze said—" He stopped. "Adaeze didn't say."

"Adaeze doesn't know. Or Adaeze knows and calls it something else. My father died in 2011, in a rented room in Mushin, of a sickness that ten thousand naira of proper medicine would have slowed down. He was fifty-eight. He had spent the last fifteen years fixing generators in a one-man workshop too small to swing a cat in, because no bank would lend to a man with no family name behind him and no family would lend to a man with no family at all. He died with my mother holding one hand and me holding the other, and do you know what he said? Not *forgive them.* Not *they wronged me.* He said, *Tell Funmi the pepper soup needed more uziza.*" 

Ifeoma heard herself laugh, one broken note, and pressed her knuckles to her mouth.

"That was his joke. Their joke. When they were young, before everything, Funmilayo used to cook for the workshop boys, and Tunde used to stand over her shoulder saying it needed more uziza, and she used to chase him with the spoon. It was the password between them, all those silent years. *Tell Funmi the pepper soup needed more uziza.* Meaning: I remember you. Meaning: I never stopped being your brother. Meaning: I am dying, and the last thing I want to be is the boy in your kitchen teasing you about seasoning."

Cyrus had gone very still. In the failing light his face looked carved, a mask of a man, and Ifeoma understood suddenly that stillness was his native language — that here was a man who had spent three years unable to taste and God knew how many years unable to feel, and who had built an entire celebrated life on describing flavors he could not experience, and who was now sitting on his dead mother's veranda hearing that his dead mother had led a secret life of loyalty beneath the floorboards of his inheritance.

"She cooked for him," Cyrus said at last. "You're saying my mother cooked for him. For years."

"For nineteen years. From 1992 until his death. Once or twice a month, a girl — a cousin's daughter, a go-between — would carry a covered pot from this kitchen to Mushin. Pepper soup. Egusi on his birthday. My mother swore the egusi arrived hot every single time, which means the girl ran, which means your mother timed it, which means your mother *cared about the temperature*, Cyrus, do you understand? That is not duty. Duty sends a card. That is love, conducted through a courier, in defiance of a matriarch, for two decades, and never once admitted."

"And you know this how?"

"Because she wrote to him." Ifeoma untied the twine. Her fingers were steady; she had practiced this too, the unwrapping, so that it would not shake. "Not often. She couldn't risk much. But there are letters. Eleven letters over nineteen years. And there are others — his, unanswered, from the early years, when he still tried. And there is this."

From the bottom of the oilcloth she drew a paper, yellowed and creased into eighths, and unfolded it with the care of a priest handling a relic. "This is a copy of a deed fragment. My father kept the original; this is his hand-copy, made in 1998 when he finally accepted the original was falling apart. When Ezekiel died, the property papers were disputed for a time — you'd know this history better than I, but the short version is that the house and the shop were formally registered in Ezekiel's name alone, but there was an earlier agreement, witnessed, that the property belonged to *the working sons*. Plural. Kunle sent money from England; Tunde worked the shop. Two sons, one promise. Your grandmother burned the original agreement — my father believed she did; he never proved it — but the lawyer who witnessed it, a Papa Balogun, kept his own file, and in 1998, near the end of his life, he gave my father a certified extract. It says what it says. I've had a lawyer in Abuja look at it. He says it's not a deed. He says it's a ghost. He says courts don't honor promises made over radiator steam. But ghosts don't need courts, Cyrus. Ghosts just need someone willing to say their name."

She held the papers out to him.

For a long moment Cyrus did not take them. He looked at the bundle the way a man looks at water when he has been walking in the desert and cannot quite believe it is real — wanting it and fearing it in the same breath. Then he reached out and lifted the top letter, and Ifeoma watched him read his mother's handwriting for the first time in whatever span of years had passed since her death, and watched his lips move faintly around the opening words — *Dear Tunde, forgive the shortness of this letter, Ngozi the girl is watching the gate* — and watched the composure, the famous composure, the composure that had reviewed three hundred restaurants without once admitting it felt nothing, begin to fail.

"She signed it *Funmi*," he said. His voice came out sanded down to the grain. "Nobody called her Funmi. We called her Mama. Even Adaeze. Even the aunts. Who calls their mother Funmi?"

"A brother does," Ifeoma said gently. "A brother who used to steal from her pot and get chased with a spoon. Read the third letter. The one from 2003. She writes about you."

He turned pages with hands that were not quite steady. The lamp in the parlor behind them clicked on automatically, and its light fell through the doorway and lay across the veranda floor in a long gold bar, and in that light Ifeoma watched Cyrus Sandoval read:

*"Tunde, I saw your namesake on television — well, the television in Mama Ngozi's parlor, you know Baba Chike charges us to watch. The boy reviews food in London. He described a soup and I thought, he writes like you talk, all flourish and no shame. Is he happy, do you think? He looks thin. Feed him if he ever comes home. Feed him even if he doesn't deserve it. That is the family disease, feeding people whether or not they deserve it, and I caught it from you."*

Cyrus lowered the letter. He did not wipe his face, and the tears went down it plainly, and he let them, and Ifeoma thought: *there it is, the man underneath the critic, the son underneath the reviewer.* 

"She knew," he said. "About me. About—" He gestured vaguely at his own mouth, at the great unnameable absence. "No. She couldn't have. This was 2003. I lost the taste in—" He stopped. Recalculated. "She wrote *is he happy*. She wrote it like a question she already knew the answer to."

"Mothers write questions they already know the answers to," Ifeoma said. "It's how they give you room to lie."

* * *

They sat with the letters between them for a long time while the dusk deepened into full dark and the quarter's noises changed register — the frying sounds giving way to generators, the children called indoors one household at a time. Cyrus read every letter. Some he read twice. At the fourth one he laughed, sudden and startled, a real laugh — his mother describing a goat that had gotten into the compound during a funeral and eaten the funeral rice, and the widow's fury, and how the family had agreed never to speak of it and how she was therefore writing it down immediately. At the ninth he went quiet for so long that Ifeoma checked whether he was still awake. He was reading the last letter, dated six weeks before Tunde died, the one that ended: *"Tell him the pepper soup needs more uziza. Tell him I know. Tell him I have always known, and that knowing has not been enough, and that I am sorry in the way that changes nothing, which is the only sorry cowards have."*

"She called herself a coward," Cyrus said.

"She was a coward," Ifeoma said, "and she was brave, and both things are true, and I have decided I am allowed to hold both because I am tired of families that keep only one." She leaned forward. "Cyrus. I didn't come here to litigate. I came to the wake because my mother was dying and she made me swear — she made me *swear* — that I would stand in front of the Sandovals and say the word Radiator out loud, once, before I buried her. She wanted to die knowing the name had been said in that house again. That's all the wake was. That's all I intended. But then I stayed, because I saw this house dying, and I saw your sister drowning in it, and I saw you—" She hesitated.

"Saw me what?"

"Starving," she said. "Sitting in a house full of food, starving. And I recognized it, because my father starved the same way. Not for food. For the table. There's a difference, and it's the whole difference, and I think you know it, because I've watched you eat Mama Ngozi's cooking like a man reading a letter he can't answer."

Cyrus looked at her for a long moment. The lamplight made one side of his face gold and left the other in shadow, and Ifeoma thought he looked exactly like the photographs of Tunde she kept in her own drawer — the same architecture of jaw and brow, the same careful eyes, the resemblance that had struck her the first night at the wake and had frightened her more than any rejection could.

"There's a developer," Cyrus said finally. "Coming Friday. Adaeze wants to sign. The money would be—" He shook his head. "It would change everything. For all of us. And now you're telling me the house stands on a promise to a line of the family we erased, and I'm supposed to—what? What is it you actually want, Ifeoma? Say it plainly. You've earned plainness."

What she wanted. She had turned the question over for months, and the honest answer embarrassed her with its size. She took a breath.

"I want the question this family arranged itself around to be asked out loud, once, by one of you, in daylight. The question is: *Was Tunde our brother, or was he not?* Because you cannot have it both ways forever. You cannot erase a man and keep his labor. You cannot burn the agreement and keep the house it built. Either the Radiators were a family, in which case my father belongs in its story and his daughter belongs at its table — or the Radiators were a business, in which case sell it, take the developer's money, and stop pretending there was ever anything here but zinc and profit. Choose. That's all. That's the whole of what I want. Someone in this family must choose, out loud, where my father stands."

"And if we choose wrong?"

"Then I take my letters and my ghost of a deed and I go home to my mother, and I will have lost nothing but three weeks of hope, which is cheap." She stood, and gathered the oilcloth, and paused with it half-wrapped. "But you won't choose wrong, Cyrus. I've watched you for three weeks. You're the only one in that parlor who flinches. Flinching is the soul refusing to sign."

She was at the door when he spoke her name — her actual name, *Ifeoma*, which he had never used before, always *Miss Adeyemi* or nothing at all.

"The pepper soup," he said. "That he asked about. At the end. Did anyone ever tell her?"

Ifeoma smiled, and it hurt, the way true things hurt.

"I'm telling her son," she said. "It's taken me twenty-six years to find someone in this family worth telling."

Inside the parlor, the telephone began to ring — the old landline, the heavy black one that had hung on that wall since before any of them were born, ringing with the flat urgent insistence of news that would not wait for morning. Cyrus looked at it. Ifeoma looked at Cyrus.

Neither of them moved to answer it.
