## 13. The Family Council

The rain began at noon, as if the sky had been waiting for an excuse, and by two o'clock it had driven every argument indoors. Adaeze stood in the parlor of the family house and watched the water sheet down the windows that her mother had once hung with lace curtains, now gone grey as cobwebs, and she thought: this is what we have come to. A room full of Sandovals, a lawyer's letter on the table, and a stranger's daughter sitting in the corner like a question nobody wanted to answer.

The developer's man — Mr. Okonkwo, polished shoes, a voice like a bank advert — had come and gone that morning. He left behind figures so large they seemed to belong to another country: sums with commas in them, commas like little handles you could grab to pull yourself out of a life. All heirs must sign within eleven days. Eleven days. Adaeze had counted them twice on her fingers, then once more, because numbers that large made her distrust her own arithmetic.

She looked around the parlor. Aunt Bisi sat nearest the window, fanning herself though the room was cool, her wrapper pulled tight as if the house itself might try to take something from her. Her sons had not come; they had sent their regrets from Abuja and their proxies instead, which was to say they had sent nothing but permission. Cyrus sat across from her, his long body folded into Mama's old armchair as though he were trying to make himself smaller than the furniture remembered him being. And Ifeoma — Ifeoma sat apart, near the door, a notebook closed on her lap, her hands still, her face arranged into the particular patience of someone who has learned that rooms close over her like water.

Adaeze felt the old fatigue settle into her shoulders, the one she had carried since she was nineteen years old and gave up her university place in Ibadan because somebody had to stay. Somebody always had to stay. That was the whole architecture of the family: some built lives abroad and some held the walls up, and the ones who held the walls were expected to be grateful for the privilege.

"We are here," she said, "to discuss the offer."

"We are here," said Aunt Bisi, "to discuss *her*." The fan stopped. "Every time this girl opens her mouth, something falls out of it that should have stayed buried. Tunde this. Tunde that. My sister is three weeks dead, may God rest her, and this child wants us to dig her up and interrogate her."

"She has letters," Cyrus said quietly.

"Everybody has letters." Bisi snapped the fan open again. "I have letters. Letters prove nothing except that people write things down when they are angry or in love, and both conditions are temporary."

Adaeze heard her own voice before she had decided to use it, flat and hard, the voice of the woman who had paid the school fees and buried the mother and scrubbed the parlor floor for thirty years. "Bisi, stop. We will do this properly or not at all." She turned to Ifeoma. "You asked for this meeting. Speak."

Ifeoma unfolded from her chair like someone rising from prayer. She did not open the notebook. She stood in the middle of the parlor carpet, on the worn roses of it, and Adaeze saw that her hands were trembling and that she was holding them still anyway, which took a kind of courage Adaeze recognized and resented.

"My father's name was Tunde Sandoval," Ifeoma said. "He was born in this house. He repaired radiators in that shop until the day your father told him never to come back. He married my mother against their wishes, and for that he lost everything — the name, the shop, the street. He wrote to your mother for nineteen years. Nineteen years of letters. She kept them under the floorboards of the kitchen, every one, unanswered — or answered only in the way she could answer, which was food. Mama Ngozi has told me. Your mother cooked for my father secretly, all those years. His favorite dishes. The recipes are written on the walls of that kitchen in her hand. This house stands on his labor and her guilt, and I am asking you — I am *asking* you — to acknowledge that before you sell it out from under both our dead."

The silence afterward had texture. It had weight and grain, like harmattan dust. Rain filled it, drumming on the zinc awning outside, running in ropes off the eaves.

Aunt Bisi broke it first, and Adaeze could hear the waver in her, the crack running through the porcelain. "Your mother," Bisi said slowly, to Ifeoma, "was a good woman. Whatever else—" She stopped. Started again. "Funmi cooked for him? All those years?"

"Mama Ngozi carried the plates," Ifeoma said. "Back gate. After dark."

Bisi put the fan down. Picked it up. Put it down again, and Adaeze understood that something in the old woman had just shifted on its foundations, quietly, the way houses shift before anyone admits the walls are moving.

But Adaeze had spent her whole life being the one who said the unsayable thing so that no one else had to bleed saying it, and she said it now.

"You want acknowledgment," she said. "Fine. Here is acknowledgment. Do you know why Tunde was erased? Not because of who he married. That was the story we were given, the polite story. I learned the rest when I was twenty-two, going through Mama's papers for the land registry. Tunde didn't just marry out. When Papa died, Tunde signed away his half of the shop — sold it, cheap, to a man who gutted it and turned it into a beer parlor, and then came back two years later wanting his half back, wanting the *house*, wanting to be a Sandoval again when it suited him. Mama bought back the shop with money she didn't have. She worked fifteen years to pay that debt. So yes — she erased him. Yes, she turned her face away. Because survival sometimes looks like cruelty from the outside, and none of you were here to watch her choose between sentiment and solvency. You were all in London or Abuja or wherever ambition had taken you. *I* was here. I watched her count coins at that table." She pointed at the table, and her finger shook, and she hated that it shook. "So do not stand in my mother's parlor and tell me she owed your father softness. She owed him nothing. She fed him anyway. That was more grace than most of us get."

It came out of her like blood from a cut she'd been keeping pressed shut for decades, and when it was done she was breathing hard, and Cyrus was looking at her with an expression she couldn't read — not pity, worse than pity, something like recognition — and Ifeoma had not moved.

"I didn't know that," Ifeoma said finally, and her voice was smaller now. "My father never said. In the letters he only ever wrote that he was sorry."

"He was sorry," Adaeze repeated. "Sorry is a word. The debt was real."

"And so the answer is to erase his children too?" Ifeoma's voice found itself again, rising. "To let a developer flatten the shop and the kitchen and the wall where your mother wrote her recipes, where she wrote *his* recipes, the ones she cooked for him in secret — to bury it all under concrete and a car park, and call that settling accounts?"

"The money," Adaeze said, "would settle everything. For everyone. Including you. Okonkwo's offer includes compensation for any claimant—"

"I don't want compensation." Ifeoma said it fast, before the word could be taken from her. "I want the name. I want the wall. I want one room of this house to remember my father existed. Is that so much more expensive than money?"

And there it was, Adaeze thought. There was the whole war in one sentence. Money, which could be divided, distributed, forgotten. Or memory, which could not be divided at all, only shared or destroyed.

Cyrus spoke then, and his voice was strange, hoarse, as if he'd been silent so long the words had rusted. "Mama kept the letters under the floor. Nineteen years. If she truly wanted him erased, why keep them?" He looked at Adaeze. "You knew about the sale of the shop. Did you know about the letters?"

Adaeze hesitated one beat too long, and Cyrus saw it, and she watched him understand, and she hated him for it, hated him for arriving late to everything and still managing to be the one who saw clearly.

"I knew she kept *something*," Adaeze said. "I didn't read it. Some things you don't read, Cyrus. Some doors you leave shut because the whole house is built around them being shut. You want to know how a family survives? It survives by agreeing together on what it will not look at. That is not a lie. That is a *foundation*."

"A foundation of what?"

"Of peace!" It tore out of her louder than she intended. "Thirty years of peace! Christmases without shouting. Funerals without scandal. You think that's cheap? You think peace grows wild? Somebody pays for it, always, and in this family it was me and Mama paying, year after year, while the rest of you sent photographs from your better lives!"

The rain chose that moment to intensify, hammering the roof, and under its cover Aunt Bisi began, very softly, to cry. Nobody moved toward her. It was that kind of crying, the private kind, the kind that asks to be witnessed and not consoled.

Ifeoma stepped forward. "Auntie," she said gently, to Bisi, "your sister loved him. Whatever happened with the shop — she loved him enough to cook for him for nineteen years. You don't have to defend the erasure. You only have to admit it happened."

"*Don't*—" Bisi's head came up, wet-eyed, furious. "Don't call me auntie like you belong here. Don't stand in my sister's parlor and rearrange my memories. I was a child when Tunde left. I remember the day. I remember the rain—" She stopped, hearing herself, hearing the rain on the roof right now, and her face did something complicated and terrible, grief and recognition arriving in the same instant, and she pressed the fan against her mouth.

"What do you remember?" Cyrus asked, very quietly.

Bisi lowered the fan. "I remember Mama standing in that doorway," she said, "in the rain, watching him go down the street. And I remember she stood there a very long time. Longer than anger stands. I used to think she was angry." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "I don't think I thought that, even then."

The parlor held its breath. Adaeze felt the foundation she had described — the agreed-upon silence, the shut door, the whole load-bearing lie — feel it groan like a floorboard under a weight it was never rated for.

"This is unproductive," she said, reaching for the chairwoman's tone she had used in a hundred meetings of the neighborhood association. "We have ten days. The offer expires. Whatever Tunde was or wasn't, the practical question is whether we sign. Bisi, your sons have already instructed—"

"My sons," Bisi said, "have not stood in this house in six years." She looked at the rain. "I haven't signed anything yet."

"Bisi—"

"And I won't be rushed. Not in my sister's house. Not about my sister's secrets." She rose, gathered her wrapper with dignity, and paused beside Ifeoma on her way to the door. For a moment the old woman's hand hovered — Adaeze saw it hover — near Ifeoma's shoulder, an unfinished gesture, a blessing interrupted mid-air. Then Bisi walked out into the rain without it, and the screen door clapped shut behind her like a full stop.

The parlor was three people now, and the silence among them was different from the silence before — thinner, broken open, no longer load-bearing.

Ifeoma turned to Cyrus. "Tell them," she said. "Not for me. Tell them what Mama Ngozi told you. Tell them what's written on the kitchen walls. They'll believe you. You're the famous one. Your words have weight in this family — everyone defers to the one who left."

"They don't," Cyrus said. "Believe me. Words are the one thing I've never been able to make true."

"Then make this one true."

"Cyrus." Adaeze's voice had gone cold and careful, the negotiating voice. "Whatever you're thinking of doing — whatever speech you're drafting behind those eyes — remember that if we don't sign, the offer dies, and the house continues to rot on our hands, and *you* will be the one who cost this family its future. You've been here two weeks. You don't carry what carrying costs. I do. I always have."

"I know," Cyrus said, and it was the gentleness in it that frightened her. "That's exactly why I can't sign yet, Adaeze. Everything you sacrificed was for this family. But this family was built on top of a man it pretended wasn't underneath. I've spent three years writing about food I can't taste. I know what pretending costs. I'm the expert." He stood, unfolding from the armchair, tall again, unbearable again. "I need two more days."

"Two days." Adaeze laughed, and there was nothing warm in it. "Two more days of what? Of her?" She gestured at Ifeoma, and saw the girl flinch, and hated herself, and kept going anyway because the momentum of thirty years does not stop on a sixpence. "She sits in this house cooking and cleaning and writing in that notebook, cataloguing our shame, and you both call it truth. Well, truth has a price too, and neither of you is paying it. I am. As usual."

"Adaeze—"

"Get out," she said to Ifeoma, and the words came out steadier than anything she'd said all afternoon, which was how she knew, even as she said them, that they were the wrongest words of her life. "Get out of this parlor. This is a family matter."

Ifeoma looked at her for a long moment. Then at Cyrus. Then she picked up her notebook, and tucked it under her arm, and said, in a voice that did not shake at all:

"When you're ready to talk about the kitchen wall, you know where I sleep. Third room on the left. The one your mother kept locked for nineteen years."

She went. The screen door clapped. The rain went on falling, indifferent, ancient, and Adaeze stood alone in the parlor with her brother, beside the table where the lawyer's letter lay, and outside, faintly, through the drumming, she could hear the kitchen — someone had left a pot simmering on Mama Ngozi's stove, and the smell of pepper soup came through the walls like a voice through a dream, patient, insistent, refusing to be silenced.

Cyrus was looking toward the kitchen doorway with an expression she had never seen on his face before.

"Cyrus," she said warningly. "What are you thinking?"

He didn't answer. Down the hall, the pot simmered. And somewhere above the stove, on the smoke-darkened plaster, in their mother's handwriting, a dead woman's recipes waited for whoever would finally come to read them aloud.

* * *

In the third room on the left, Ifeoma sat on the edge of the bed that had been her grandmother's, and opened her father's letters, and read the last one again by the grey light of the rainy afternoon.

*I have stopped expecting an answer,* Tunde had written, in a hand going shaky at the edges. *I write now the way a man talks to a river. But I hear you send food, and I eat it, and I say thank you into the dark, and that is enough. Tell my sisters the shop was never mine to sell. Tell them I only ever wanted to come home.*

Below it, in different ink, newer, shakier, her mother's dying hand: *He got the food. He knew.*

Ifeoma closed the tin. Outside, the rain was easing, and through the thin wall she could hear the family arguing on in the parlor, voices rising and falling like weather, and she pressed her palm flat against the plaster — the cool, old, stubborn plaster of the house that had been promised — and waited, the way her father had waited, the way the soup simmered, for the heat to do its slow work.
