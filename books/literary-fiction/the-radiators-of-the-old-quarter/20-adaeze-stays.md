## 20. Adaeze Stays

The morning after the feast, Adaeze woke in her old room with the ceiling fan turning above her like something that had forgotten its purpose, and for a long time she lay still, listening to the house.

It was a different house now. She could hear it in the way sound moved through the rooms — the kitchen already alive below her, water running, the clatter of a pot lid, Ifeoma's voice low and unhurried, giving instructions to someone. Mama Ngozi's granddaughter, perhaps. Or one of the quarter's children who had begun drifting in the way they did, drawn to the smell of stock and the promise of being useful. The house had been quiet for so many years that Adaeze had come to think of silence as its native language, the way you might think of a limp as part of a body. Now the silence had been evicted, and she lay in her childhood bed listening to the noise of a household that was no longer hers and did not yet know what to do with her.

She had not signed. That was the fact she kept turning over, the way her tongue — no, that was Cyrus's affliction, not hers; her tongue worked perfectly, had always worked perfectly, had tasted every meal she had ever cooked for other people and swallowed her own portion standing at the sink — the way her mind kept returning to a stone in the shoe. She had not signed, and the developer's men had come at noon the previous day, before the feast, and stood in the parlor with their folder and their pens, and Cyrus had stood up in front of the whole quarter with a letter in his hand and given away the house.

Given it away. As if a house were a thing you could hand over like a plate at a table. As if thirty-one years of scrubbing its floors and arguing with its plumbing and sleeping in it alone after everyone else had gone could be transferred with a sentence and a handshake.

She had clapped. She remembered that with a strange, detached clarity — her own palms coming together, the sound sharp in the compound air, her face arranged into something the neighbors would read as joy. What else did she know how to do with her face? She had spent a lifetime arranging it. When their father died, she had arranged it. When Funmilayo began to fail, she had arranged it. When Cyrus flew in from London with his five-day itinerary and his expensive shoes and his grief managed like a hotel booking, she had arranged it, and she had cooked for the wake, and she had washed the dishes, and she had not said one word of the forty thousand words sitting in her chest.

Now the sale was dead. The money was dead. The future she had built in her head — the small flat in Yaba, the shop, the rest, the *rest*, that word she had whispered to herself for three decades like a prayer — all of it had died on the altar of Cyrus's redemption, and the whole quarter had watched it happen, and the whole quarter had applauded.

She got up. Her knees announced themselves the way they always did now, a creaking she had inherited from her mother along with the cooking pots and the temper. She dressed in the wrapper she wore for working mornings, tied her hair back, and looked at herself in the mirror that had hung on this wall since before she was born. The mirror was spotted with age, her face arriving through it slightly blurred, slightly doubled, a woman and her ghost occupying the same frame.

"Adaeze," she said to the reflection, testing the name the way you test a floorboard. "Adaeze, who stayed."

It had been her identity for so long that she did not know what was underneath it. That was the truth she could not escape now that the house had been taken out of her hands. She had stayed — that was the story, the family story, the story Aunt Bisi told at gatherings with a hand on Adaeze's shoulder, *our Adaeze, she stayed, she took care of everything* — and the staying had been real, had cost real things, had eaten real years. But somewhere along the way the staying had stopped being something she did and become something she was, and a person who is only what she has sacrificed is a person with nothing left to lose and nothing left to want, and that person wakes up one morning at fifty-four years old in a house that has just been given away and discovers she has no idea what she is for.

Downstairs, the kitchen smelled of ginger and smoke and something frying.

* * *

She found Ifeoma at the stove with her sleeves pushed up, stirring a pot of what smelled like pepper soup base, and the sight of her produced in Adaeze a tangle of feelings she could not separate — resentment and grief and a grudging, unwelcome respect, all of it braided so tight she could not find the ends.

"You're up early," Ifeoma said, without turning. "There's tea. The kettle's on the small burner."

"I know where the kettle is," Adaeze said. "It's my kitchen."

Ifeoma turned then, and Adaeze watched her decide something — watched the younger woman weigh her words the way she seemed to weigh everything, carefully, with that patience that had unnerved the whole family for weeks, that patience that had turned out to be a siege engine. "It is," Ifeoma said. "It was your kitchen. I'm not going to pretend otherwise."

"Then what are you going to pretend?"

"Nothing." Ifeoma set down the spoon. "I'm going to stand here and tell you what I've been thinking about since yesterday, and you can tell me I'm mad, and I'll still say it, because I've learned in this family that if you wait for permission you wait forever."

Adaeze took the tea because her hands wanted something to hold. The mug was hers — had been hers since 1998, chipped at the rim, the faded print of a long-defunct bank — and there was something almost obscene about the ordinary comfort of it, the heat soaking into her palms, in the middle of the ruin of her plans.

"Say it, then."

"The kitchen stays open. That was my condition, and Cyrus agreed, and the quarter expects it. Mama Ngozi has already told four people. By Friday the whole street will believe it's happening." Ifeoma wiped her hands on her apron — Funmilayo's apron, Adaeze noticed, the one with the burnt corner, the one their mother had refused to replace for twenty years. "But I can't run a kitchen. I can cook — my father made sure of that, he said a person should always be able to feed themselves and at least three others — but I can't run one. I don't know the suppliers. I don't know the quarter's debts and grudges. I don't know which neighbor pays late and which one you feed free and which one you feed double because his pride can't accept charity. You know all of that. You've been doing it your whole life, quietly, for a family that didn't thank you."

"Careful," Adaeze said.

"I'm not finished." Ifeoma's voice stayed level, but her chin came up, and in that moment she looked so much like the photographs of Tunde that Adaeze's chest tightened. "I'm asking you to co-run it. Not to work for me. Not to make peace. To co-run it. Half of everything — the decisions, the profits, the failures. I'm asking because you're the only person alive who knows how this kitchen actually works, and because—" she paused, and Adaeze watched her choose the honest word over the diplomatic one, "—because it was your mother's kitchen too, and she cooked for my father in secret for fifteen years, and I think if she were standing here she would want both of us in it."

The silence that followed was long enough for the soup to come to a boil, for the lid to begin its small rattling complaint, for Adaeze to drink half her tea without tasting it.

"You think," Adaeze said finally, "because she cooked for him in secret. You make it sound like love. It was guilt."

"Maybe it was both."

"It was guilt," Adaeze said, and heard her own voice crack on the word, and hated it. "She turned him away from that door in 1989 and she never forgave herself, and the cooking was her penance, and the letters in that tin were her shame, and now you've dug all of it up and the whole world thinks Funmilayo Sandoval was a saint with a secret heart, and I—" She stopped. The words were coming too fast now, the forty thousand of them, pressing against the gate. "I was the one who found her crying over those pots. Do you know that? Year after year. She'd be stirring pepper soup at midnight and weeping into it, and she'd say it was the onions, and I'd say yes, Mama, the onions, because what else do you say? I carried that house's secrets too. Nobody gave me a tin."

Ifeoma did not flinch. That was the thing about her that Adaeze could neither forgive nor dismiss — she did not flinch. She stood in the steam and the morning light and let the anger land on her like rain on a roof.

"I'm not asking you to forgive her," Ifeoma said. "Or me. Or Cyrus. I'm asking you to run a kitchen."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I'll do it badly, and the quarter will be kind to me because I'm Tunde's daughter, and it will fail within a year, and the house will go to whoever can afford the repairs, and the name will be a story old people tell." She picked the spoon back up. "But you won't say no."

"And why is that?"

"Because you said yes to everything else this family ever asked of you, and this is the first thing anyone has ever asked you to do for yourself."

* * *

Adaeze walked out of the kitchen and down the corridor and out into the compound, where the morning had fully arrived, the light lying thick on the courtyard stones, the flame tree at the gate dropping its red flowers onto the ground her mother had swept every day for fifty years. She walked past the spot where the radiator shop had stood — now a shuttered kiosk, then a barbershop, then nothing — past the wall where she and Cyrus had chalked hopscotch grids while their grandfather's customers watched and laughed, and she stood at the gate and looked down the street that had been the whole world once.

The quarter was already moving. Women with basins on their heads. A okada swerving around a pothole with the confidence of long acquaintance. Two men arguing cheerfully outside the provisions store about football, or debt, or both. And everywhere, it seemed to her, people were looking at the house. Not rudely. The way you look at a place where something has happened — the way, she supposed, people had looked at it in 1989, when Tunde stood in the rain at that door, when the street learned what the Radiators were willing to do to their own.

She had been eleven years old. She remembered the rain. She remembered her uncle's voice through the door, and her father's silence, and the shape of Tunde's shoulders under his soaked shirt as he turned away, and she remembered — this was the memory she had spent her life not having — that she had been proud. Proud that her family was strong enough to close a door. That was what the erasure had taught the children: that strength and cruelty were the same thing, and that the person who stayed and said nothing was the loyal one.

She had built a life on that lesson. She had stayed and said nothing, and the staying had been rewarded with a chipped mug and a peeling house and a brother who arrived once a year and a mother who wept into her soup, and when the developer's money had finally come — the one payment the universe seemed prepared to make on thirty years of debt — it had been taken from her at the last moment and given, in front of everyone, to the daughter of the man at the door in the rain.

And yet.

And yet she had clapped. She kept coming back to that. She had clapped because some part of her — the part that was not the ledger, not the sacrifice, not the arranged face — had recognized the truth when it was spoken aloud. The house was built on Tunde's labor. The recipes were Funmilayo's penance. The money was a bribe the family was about to accept to keep from ever saying his name. She had clapped because Cyrus, of all people — Cyrus, the one who left, the fraud with the dead tongue — had done the thing she had never had the courage to do, which was to stand up in front of the quarter and say the true thing.

She was not a generous enough woman, this morning, to feel glad about it. She suspected she might never be. But she could feel, underneath the anger, like a pulse under a bandage, the thing Ifeoma had named: the first question anyone had ever asked her that was not *will you stay*.

* * *

She came back into the kitchen at noon. Ifeoma was at the table with a notebook, writing lists in a small careful hand, and Mama Ngozi's granddaughter was washing rice at the sink, and the room was full of the ordinary industry of feeding people, and Adaeze stood in the doorway and felt the old machinery of herself — the arranging, the enduring, the staying — grind to a halt and sit idle, waiting for instructions that did not come.

"I thought about what you said," Adaeze said.

Ifeoma looked up.

"I'm not doing this for you," Adaeze said. "I want that understood. I'm not doing it for Cyrus, and I'm not doing it for Mama's ghost, and I'm not doing it because the quarter expects it. I'm doing it because I have cooked for this family for thirty-one years and been paid in thanks, and I am done being paid in thanks. If this kitchen makes money, I take half. If it fails, it fails on both of us. And the menu is not changing. Your father's recipes can go on the wall if you want, but the pepper soup is made my way, because I learned it from her, and I learned it right."

Ifeoma's face did something Adaeze had not seen before — a loosening, a crack in the patience, something young and startled and glad. "Half of everything," Ifeoma said. "My way on Mondays. The market accounts are yours. And I want you to teach me the ones she never wrote down."

"Those aren't recipes. Those are habits."

"Then teach me the habits."

Adaeze set down her mug — her chipped, ridiculous, irreplaceable mug — on the table between them, and heard herself say, before the part of her that calculated consequences could intervene, before regret could arrive and begin its familiar work:

"Yes. All right. Yes."

It was done in three syllables, the way the biggest things are. She said yes before she could weigh it, before she could arrange her face around it, before the ledger in her head could open and begin its accounting — and she felt, in the moment after the word left her, something she had not felt since she was a girl chalk-drawing on this courtyard's stones: not peace, nothing like peace, she was far too angry for peace and expected to be for a long time — but motion. The sensation of a door opening onto a room she had never entered, in a house she had thought she knew down to its bones.

Ifeoma put out her hand. Adaeze looked at it for a moment — the hand of the stranger, the intruder, the daughter of the door in the rain — and then she took it, and the grip was firm, and neither woman said anything about forgiveness, because it was not forgiveness, and both of them knew it, and both of them let it be enough.

"First order of business," Adaeze said, turning to the sink, tying on her mother's apron, "is that you're browning the onions wrong. Everyone browns them wrong. Sit down and watch."

And that was how it began — not with a ceremony, not with the quarter watching, but with onions, and heat, and two women at one stove who did not love each other yet, learning the habits.

It was Mama Ngozi's granddaughter, drying plates, who looked up at the window and said, "Auntie Adaeze — there's a man at the gate. He says he's from the newspaper. He says he's looking for the family of the critic who gave away the house."

Adaeze's hands went still in the water.

Outside, at the gate, the man from the newspaper waited, and behind him, at the end of the street, someone else was standing — someone she had not seen in thirty-one years, someone with Tunde's shoulders, watching the kitchen window with the rain of that old day still, it seemed, not quite dry on him.
