## 7. The Man Who Objects

He came back on the third day, when the frost had begun to give and the reservoir was breathing its cold breath across the valley, and Dominic saw him before Dominic saw the truck—because the truck announced itself, a rusted Ford with a toolbox in the bed and a bumper sticker gone gray with sun, and because some part of Dominic had been waiting for him since the wake, the way you wait for a tooth to start aching again.

Dominic was on the porch of his mother's house with a cup of coffee he had no intention of drinking. The house still smelled of lilies and ham and the particular sweetness of too many covered dishes. Inside, his mother was sleeping in the chair by the window, her hands folded like a woman posing for a portrait she hadn't agreed to sit for. The funeral was tomorrow. Ten o'clock. Everything was arranged, printed, paid for. Everything was a lie with a program folded beside it.

The man got out of the truck slowly, the way men do when they've decided to be deliberate. He was smaller than Dominic remembered from the wake—grief and years had compressed him—but his hands were enormous, a laborer's hands, knuckles like river stones. He wore a canvas jacket and a cap that said VALLEY HARDWARE in faded red. He stopped at the bottom of the porch steps and looked up, and his eyes were Marisol's eyes, the same dark amber, the same way of holding light without giving anything back.

"You're the son," he said. Not a question.

"I'm Dominic."

"Tomás Reyes." He didn't offer a hand. He looked past Dominic, at the door, at the windows, at the whole house, as if appraising it for demolition. "Your mother in there?"

"She's resting. You can talk to me."

"I came to talk to you." He climbed one step, then stopped, and Dominic understood that the single step was a concession, a border he was willing to stand at but not cross. "You came to see my sister."

It wasn't a question either. The Reyes siblings, Dominic was learning, didn't ask questions. They stated the world as they believed it to be and dared you to amend it.

"I did," Dominic said.

"She fed you."

"She did."

Tomás nodded slowly, and something moved behind his eyes that might have been pain or might have been its opposite. "She cooked. What did she cook?"

"Ropa vieja. And rice with—" Dominic stopped. He hadn't told anyone. He hadn't told Marisol herself how the taste had come back to him at her table, faint and trembling, like a bird with a broken wing trying to lift off a windowsill. "Rice with peas. And fried plantains."

"Rafael's plate," Tomás said. The name came out of him like a stone dropped down a well; Dominic listened for it to hit bottom and heard nothing. "Forty years, and she still cooks Rafael's plate. You know what she did the week the valley flooded? You know what she did when your father drove out of it with another woman's ring on his truck's rearview mirror?"

"I don't."

"Neither do I," Tomás said. "That's the point. She never told me. She never told anybody. She went into that house on the hill and she closed the door and she stayed closed for forty years, and now you come along with your dead father and your nice shoes and she opens the door and cooks his plate, and you sit there and eat it, and you don't even know what you're eating." He took a breath, and it shook a little at the end of it, and that shake was the truest thing he'd said. "You're eating her whole life, son. That's what you're eating."

The coffee had gone cold in Dominic's hands. He set it down on the railing because his fingers had begun to ache around the cup, and he was grateful for the ache, the way a man whose leg has fallen asleep is grateful for the pins and needles.

"You were at the wake," Dominic said. "You called us the Radiators."

"I did."

"What does it mean?"

Tomás looked at him for a long time. The reservoir glittered behind him, flat and silver, holding its drowned town the way it had held it for forty years—without comment, without apology, without giving anything back. Somewhere down there was the street where Rafael Vasquez had been born. Somewhere down there was the barbershop and the church and the dance hall where a young laborer had held a girl named Marisol while the water rose behind them.

"You really don't know," Tomás said at last, and there was something almost like pity in it. "He didn't even leave you that."

"He left a word in a tackle box. That's all."

"The Radiators." Tomás said it the way you'd say the name of a dead friend. "That's what the dam crew called themselves. The men who worked the furnaces and the boilers, the night shifts, the ones who kept the machinery hot so the concrete would cure in the cold. Twelve men. Your father was the youngest. They'd sit on the shore after shift and drink and sing and talk about what was going to be underwater by spring, and they called themselves the Radiators because they were the ones who brought the heat." He paused. "It was a joke. It stopped being a joke. Eleven of them are dead now. Your father was the last."

"And Marisol."

"Marisol was there before the name was. Marisol was there before the dam was." Tomás's jaw worked. "My sister gave that man four years. Four years of Sundays. And then the water came, and the money came, and the respectable name came, and he chose. He chose the family with the house in town over the girl from the labor camp, and he chose it so clean that nobody ever even knew there'd been a choice. He got to be a whole new man. She got to be a secret."

The wind came off the water and pushed through Dominic's jacket, and he thought of his mother in the chair by the window, her hands folded, her silence folded around forty years like a coat she'd learned to wear so well it had become her skin.

"What do you want, Mr. Reyes?"

"I want what's in the ground to stay in the ground." Tomás came up one more step, uninvited, and now they were close enough that Dominic could smell engine oil and coffee and something older, something like the inside of the bait shop, the smell of the valley itself. "Tomorrow, ten o'clock. There's going to be a lot of people there. Old crew families. People who knew Rafael before he was Vasquez. People who've spent forty years not knowing, and who can spend forty more. And I'm going to be standing there, and I'm going to be watching you, and if you get up in front of all those people and start telling stories—" he let it hang, let Dominic fill in the rest "—then I'll tell my stories. All of them. The letters. The Sundays. The things my sister never said out loud that I'm not going to say out loud either unless somebody makes me."

"You'd hurt her," Dominic said. "To protect her."

"I'd hurt everybody a little," Tomás said, "to keep from hurting her a lot. You think the truth is a gift. You're young. You think because you found it, you have to hand it out. But some truths are like the town under that water. You can go down and look at it if you want. Divers do it. Kids on a dare. But you don't raise it. You don't drag it up onto the shore and make everybody look at it in the sun. It belongs where it is."

"And my father belongs where he is?"

"Your father," Tomás said, "belongs wherever your mother says he belongs. That's the woman you should be asking. Not my sister. Not me. The woman who kept his house and his name and his secret for forty years, and who is going to be standing at that graveside tomorrow, and who is going to hear whatever you decide to say." He stepped back down, one step, then the other, until he stood on the gravel again, a smaller man once more, an old man in a hardware cap with his sister's eyes. "I'm not threatening you, son. I'm telling you the price of things. Out here we know the price of things. Your father knew it. That was his whole life, knowing the price and paying it and never once asking for change."

He turned toward the truck, and Dominic, without knowing why, said: "Does she know you came? Marisol?"

Tomás stopped. For a moment his shoulders were very still, and Dominic understood that he had touched the one place where the brother's certainty didn't reach.

"No," Tomás said. "And she won't. Because there's nothing to know. Nothing happened here. Nothing is going to happen tomorrow." He opened the truck door. "Say the safe words, son. Say the words everybody expects. It's the kindest thing you'll ever do for two women who spent their whole lives being kind in the dark."

The truck pulled away in a long curve of gravel dust, and Dominic stood on the porch until the sound of it faded into the sound of the water, which was no sound at all, which was the sound the valley had been making for forty years.

* * *

Inside, his mother was awake. She was standing at the kitchen counter with her back to him, doing nothing—her hands resting on the edge of the counter, her head slightly bowed, the posture of a woman listening to a room. On the stove, a pot of water was coming to a boil for no reason, because his mother always put a pot on when she didn't know what to do with herself, and the steam rose and fogged the window over the sink, and through the fogged glass the reservoir was a white blur, a held breath.

"Tomás Reyes," she said, without turning around.

"You know him."

"I knew him when he was fourteen and used to steal plums off Mr. Aldana's tree with your father." She turned. Her face was dry and composed, but her eyes had the look of water that has been held back by a wall for a very long time. "He was at the wake. I saw him. I was waiting for him to come here."

"Mom." Dominic crossed the kitchen. "What he said—about tomorrow. About what he'd do."

"I heard the truck." She almost smiled. "That truck has been announcing itself in this valley since before you were born."

"He says if I tell the truth tomorrow, he'll tell everything. All of it. The letters, the—" Dominic stopped. "You knew. About Marisol. You've always known."

His mother looked at him for a long moment, and then she did something he had never seen her do. She sat down. Not in the chair by the window, where she had sat for forty years of evenings, but at the kitchen table, in the plain light, like a woman at the end of a long shift.

"I found the first letter in 1983," she said. "In his boot. I was looking for a receipt. I read it standing in the hallway, and then I put it back, and I never took another one, and I never said a word." She smoothed the tablecloth with one hand, an old gesture, a gesture from a house that no longer existed. "You want to know why. Everybody wants to know why. Your friend Tomás wants to know why. Even your father, I think, spent his whole life waiting for me to ask why."

"Why, Mom?"

"Because he came home." She said it simply, terribly. "Every night, for forty years, he came home. He ate what I cooked. He fixed what I asked him to fix. He sat with me when my mother was dying, and he held my hand in the hospital, and he never once made me feel like a choice that had been made instead of a person who was loved. Whatever he'd left in the valley, he left it. And I thought—" her voice caught, and she let it, "—I thought, if I make him say it out loud, I make it true twice. Once in the letters, and once in the air. And the air is where we lived. So I let the letters stay under the floor."

The pot on the stove had begun to boil over, a thin hiss of water hitting the burner, and neither of them moved to lift it. Outside, the light was going long and gold, the last day of the official story, and the reservoir held its shine like a coin at the bottom of a well.

"Tomás is wrong about one thing," his mother said. "He thinks the truth is the town under the water. That it belongs down there." She looked up at Dominic, and in her eyes, for the first time, he saw not the wall but the woman who had built it, brick by brick, out of love and silence, and who was now, at the end, tired of holding. "But I've been under that water a long time, mijo. And I'm telling you—some of us would like to come up."

She reached across the table and took his hand, and her grip was stronger than he remembered, and it did not let go.
