## 8. Iris's Claim

I had rehearsed the drive up to Coldharbour House a dozen times, and every rehearsal ended with me turning back at the gate.

The lane climbed through beech woods the color of old coins, and where the trees broke open there was the water — flat, pewter-grey, enormous, lying over the valley like a lid. I had seen photographs of what it covered. I had grown up with them spread across my grandmother's kitchen table while she traced the drowned streets with one cracked fingernail: *there was the bakery, there was the school, there was where your great-grandmother is buried.* A town reduced to an atlas of the dead. And above it all, on its green shoulder of hill, this house — this long, low, grey-stoned house with its chimneys standing like fingers pressed to lips.

I parked where the gravel widened, beside a Range Rover that cost more than my car, my flat, and three years of my rent combined. My hands were shaking. Not from fear. From the specific fury of a person who has spent eleven years collecting scraps while other people lived comfortably on top of the whole cloth.

The door opened before I knocked. August Coldharbour stood in the frame with his mother's funeral still on him like weather — dark suit, unshaved, eyes rimmed red. He looked, I thought, exactly like a man who had been cataloging objects instead of feelings. I recognized the type. I'd spent years being it myself.

"You came back," he said.

"You left the gate unlocked."

"That wasn't an invitation."

"It wasn't a lock either." I held up the folder under my arm. "I have something to show you. It'll take twenty minutes. After that, if you want me gone, I'll go, and you'll never see me again, and you can spend the rest of your life being polite about your mother the way everyone in your family is polite about everything."

Something moved behind his eyes — not anger. Recognition. He stepped aside.

* * *

He led me to the study, because of course he did; the whole house seemed arranged around that room the way a wound arranges itself around the thing that caused it. Shelves of leather spines nobody read. A desk with brass handles worn bright. On the blotter, an inventory list in his handwriting — *inkwell, silver; letter tray, mahogany; globe, terrestrial, damaged* — each item numbered, weighed, valued. Grief converted into probate. I knew that trick intimately. When my father died I had inventoried his toolbox down to the individual screws rather than open the letter I found taped inside the lid.

"Your solicitor said not to let me in," I said.

"My solicitor says a lot of things."

I sat across the desk from him and laid the folder down but didn't open it yet. This part mattered. This part I had practiced.

"My name isn't really Vane," I said. "It's Vane by marriage, two marriages back. The name I started with is Fenwick. Thomas Fenwick was my grandfather."

August went very still. Stillness, I would learn, was his native language — the family dialect of silence, spoken fluently even by the ones who hadn't chosen it yet.

"He was born in Mereswater in 1931," I went on. "Baptized in St. Aldhelm's, the church that's under your reservoir. His father kept the ironmonger's shop on Fore Street. In 1962 the water authority bought the valley under compulsory purchase, and the Fenwicks were moved to a council estate in Ashcombe with four hundred other people who were told it was progress. Thomas was thirty-one. He never got over it. Neither did anyone else, but Thomas least of all, because of what — or who — he lost along with the town."

"Don't," August said quietly.

"I haven't said anything yet."

"You're going to say my mother's name."

I opened the folder then, and took out the photograph — the one I had carried for six years, first in a shoebox, then in a fireproof box, then against my body in an inside pocket through two house moves and a divorce. A young woman on a stone bridge over the Meres, laughing, hair half-coming-down, caught mid-turn as though someone had called her name. On the back, in pencil faded almost to nothing: *Edith — June 1959.*

I turned it around and set it in front of him.

He looked at it for a long time. Long enough that I watched the man in front of me come apart and reassemble twice. His hand rose toward the photograph and stopped an inch away, hovering, as if it might be hot.

"That's my mother," he said.

"That's Edith Fenwick. Born in Mereswater, 1936, daughter of the schoolmistress. She was nineteen when that picture was taken. She was engaged to my grandfather for two years before your family happened to her."

"She was Edith Coldharbour her entire life. She was *my mother* her entire life—"

"And who told you that? Who decides what a person's entire life is?" I heard my voice climb and made myself come back down. Anger was fuel but it burned fast; I had learned to ration it. "Mr. Coldharbour. August. I'm not here to take your mother from you. I'm here because someone took mine — took my grandfather's whole history — and yours is the only house where the record might still exist."

"What record?"

"The real one." I began laying out the folder's contents on the blotter, next to his neat inventory, so that my evidence sat among his valuations. "Eleven years ago my grandmother died and I inherited a biscuit tin. In the tin: forty-one letters, most of them fragments, sent to Thomas between 1957 and 1961 and then returned unopened after the wedding. Newspaper clippings about the reservoir construction. A map of Mereswater drawn from memory by my great-aunt. And a single sentence in my grandfather's handwriting, written the year he died, which I have read perhaps ten thousand times." I put the card down last, face up. *They didn't just drown the town. They drowned what the town knew.*

August read it. Read it again. "What did the town know?"

"That," I said, "is the question I have spent eleven years and every pound I've ever earned trying to answer. I've interviewed thirty-two survivors of the clearance. I've filed forty-seven Freedom of Information requests. I've sat in county archives until the archivists knew my coffee order. And here's what I know so far. The construction schedule for the dam ran eighteen months late in 1977 and 1978. The delay was blamed on weather. But the site diaries from those months are missing from the water authority archive — not misfiled, *missing*, with replacement pages inserted that don't match the paper stock of the rest of the volume. Somebody paid to make pages disappear. And the only entity with both the money and the motive was the Coldharbour Trust, whose fortune — whose *entire* fortune, everything this house sits on, everything your uncle Hal defends like a border — comes from the construction contracts your grandfather won when the reservoir was approved."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below us a clock ticked with the patience of furniture.

"You think we buried something," August said. "Literally."

"I think in February 1978, a survey was commissioned regarding the old burial ground at St. Aldhelm's. I think the survey found more graves than the relocation contract accounted for. And I think somebody decided that moving them properly would cost too much and delay the flooding past the point where the financing collapsed — so they flooded anyway." I met his eyes. "I don't have proof. I have a shape where proof should be. But shapes have a way of filling in, Mr. Coldharbour, when people stop protecting them."

* * *

He didn't throw me out. He also didn't believe me — not fully, not yet; belief is a load-bearing wall and no one knocks theirs out in an afternoon. Instead he did something I hadn't predicted. He talked.

Not much. Fragments. But sitting across that desk, with the photograph of his young mother lying between us like a third person, he told me about locked drawers in the desk I was sitting beside. About a ledger with pages razored out. About a woman at the wake — me — saying a word that made his uncle Hal go the color of candle wax.

"Hal offered me money yesterday," he said. "To hand over papers I haven't even found yet. Unopened. Think about that. An offer for a thing that officially doesn't exist."

"I've thought about little else for eleven years."

"Why?" he asked suddenly, and it wasn't hostile — it was genuinely bewildered, the bewilderment of a man who had never needed to ask why truth mattered because he'd never lacked for comfort. "Why does it matter this much? Everyone involved is dead. The town's been underwater sixty years. What do you actually get?"

I should have had a polished answer. I'd given versions of it to journalists, to archivists, to my own reflection. But looking at him — at the inventory list, at the red-rimmed eyes, at the photograph he still hadn't touched — I gave him the true one instead.

"When I was nine," I said, "my grandmother took me to the edge of the reservoir and pointed at the water and said, *everything you are is under there, and they made it illegal to look.* That's what a drowned village does to the people who came from it. It doesn't kill them. It makes them illegible. My grandfather worked as a caretaker for thirty years and never once said where he was from. When the census taker asked his birthplace he said Ashcombe. He erased himself before anyone else could. He died without ever once saying the word Mereswater aloud in his own house." My voice cracked and I let it. "You want to know what I get? I get a grandfather who gets to have been from somewhere. I get a grave — if it exists — that gets to have names on it. I get the record corrected. Not compensation. Not apology, though God knows I'd take one. *Correction.* There's a difference. Money makes things quiet. Correction makes things true."

August looked down at the photograph again. And this time he touched it — one finger, at the corner, careful as a man touching a bird.

"She laughed like that," he said. "I didn't know she'd ever laughed like that. The woman I knew was—" He stopped. Started again. "Careful. Careful about everything. Words especially. She could go an entire Christmas dinner without finishing a sentence."

"Practiced," I said.

"What?"

"It's what happens to people who spend their lives not saying things. They get good at it. It looks like elegance from the outside." I closed the folder. "Here's what I'm asking, and then I'll go. Not money. Not silence, either — I won't pretend to offer that, we both know I can't. I'm asking you to open the drawers. Read whatever's in them. Your mother wrote to my grandfather for years — I have her side of it, or pieces of it, and somewhere in this house is probably his side, or hers, or the truth about 1978. You're the executor. Legally, morally, whatever word you need — it falls to you. You can burn it all and I'll fight you in the press and probably lose, and your family will stay rich and comfortable and I'll die with my biscuit tin. Or you can look. That's all. Just look."

* * *

He walked me out through the hall, past portraits of Coldharbours who had all, apparently, been born middle-aged and satisfied. At the door he paused.

"The letters you have. Her letters to Thomas. Do they say anything about—" He couldn't finish it. The art of almost saying it, already visible in him like a vein under skin.

"They say she loved him," I said. "They stop in 1979, mid-sentence, and never resume. Whatever happened, happened in 1979, and she carried it for forty years, and three days ago she died with it. You're the only person alive who can ask her now. You'll have to do it in writing, same as she did."

For a moment neither of us moved. Two griefs facing each other across a threshold — mine eleven years old and honed, his three days old and raw — and I understood, standing there, that we were going to end up on the same side of this whether either of us liked it or not. Righteousness alone wins nothing; I'd proven that for a decade. Guilt alone wins nothing either; it just curdles. But the two together, his and mine, braided — that was something the Coldharbour machinery had no procedure for.

"Iris," he said, as I reached the gravel. "The survey report. 1978. If it exists — where would it be?"

"In the gap," I said. "In the pages someone cut out of your mother's ledger. People who hide documents always hide them in the wound they made."

I drove down through the beech woods with the reservoir sliding grey beside me, and in the mirror the house shrank behind its trees, and I thought: *he'll open the drawers tonight.* I was wrong about that. It would take him another day, and a solicitor's warning ignored, and a key that had been waiting in a drawer of its own. But I was right about the essential thing.

That night, for the first time in eleven years, I slept without the biscuit tin within reach of the bed. I didn't need to guard it anymore. The record had begun correcting itself — and the hand turning the pages belonged, at last, to a Coldharbour.
