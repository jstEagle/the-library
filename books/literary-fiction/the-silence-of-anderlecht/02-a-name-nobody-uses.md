## 2. A Name Nobody Uses

The rain came down on Anderlecht like a verdict, soft and total, and Edmund stood beneath the green awning of the crematorium listening to it drum the plastic sheeting over the flower arrangements. Margriet Vandenberg-Reyes had chosen this place years ago — he'd found the receipt in her desk drawer while sorting papers he was never meant to sort, a deposit paid in 1998, the year his grandfather died. She had planned everything, as she planned everything: the order of service printed in heavy cream stock, the photograph of herself at forty, severe and beautiful at the keyboard of a Bösendorfer that no longer existed, the hymn cut to two verses because, as she once said of everything, *the third verse is where people start to weep, and I won't have it.*

There were perhaps thirty people under the awning and inside the small chapel. Edmund counted them the way he counted everything now — mop strokes, floor tiles, the seconds between the 7:14 and 7:31 trams. He knew most of the faces distantly: conservatory administrators in funeral black that fit them better than their concert dress ever had; an ancient man who might have been one of Margriet's students from the fifties, holding himself upright by will alone; two women from the church committee his grandmother had terrorized into efficiency decades ago. No musicians of note. That was the thing about dynasties, Edmund thought. They outlive their own music.

His mother stood at the front receiving condolences with her spine like a metronome bar. Anouk Reyes wore the same coat she had worn to his father's funeral eleven years ago, and to his debut fifteen years ago — though that night it had been new, and she had gripped his shoulders backstage and told him that tonight the name Reyes would mean something again. He watched her accept a widow's handshake from the old student and thought: *she has been rehearsing grief for so long she no longer knows which parts are real.*

Edmund had not spoken to his mother in four months. They had not argued about it; there had simply been nothing left to say that either of them could survive saying. He had come to the funeral because someone Reyes had to carry the urn, and because — though he would never have admitted it even to himself — he wanted to see if anyone else came. If anyone came for *her*, the woman, rather than for the name.

* * *

The chapel smelled of lilies and cold stone. The service was exactly what Margriet had designed: precise, beautiful, and airless. The organist — a young woman Edmund vaguely recognized from the conservatory's evening classes — played a Franck chorale with careful, bloodless competence, and Edmund sat in the second row with his hands flat on his knees and listened to the way the acoustics swallowed the low register whole. He knew this room. He had played here once, at fourteen, a memorial recital for some patron whose name was engraved on a brass plate near the door. He remembered the same swallowing silence after the final chord, the sense that sound itself came here to be interred.

The eulogy was delivered by a notary, because Margriet had written it herself and no one else could be trusted to read it without improving it. It was all dynasty and discipline. *She believed music was not a gift but a debt.* Edmund stared at the back of the pew in front of him and felt the old familiar numbness settle over him like the dust sheets he pulled off pianos every morning at work.

That was the other thing nobody at the conservatory knew. That the man who mopped the rehearsal rooms, who tuned nothing but knew when a piano was a quarter-tone flat by the ache of it in his teeth, who stacked chairs and unclogged sinks and was called *meneer* only when someone wanted something moved — that man had stood on the Henry Le Boeuf stage at nineteen and made three thousand people forget to breathe. There was a framed poster of his debut still hanging in the corridor outside Hall C, behind a fire extinguisher, half-faded. He cleaned around it every week. Neither of them acknowledged the other.

He had walked off that stage mid-concerto. He had never explained why, and no one had ever asked in a way that deserved an answer. Fifteen years of silence, and the world had done what the world does with prodigies who break: it had filed him away and moved on to the next bright, unbroken child.

* * *

Afterward, in the colonnade outside, the rain had thinned to a grey mist that beaded on the bare plane trees. People clustered in knots, murmuring, nibbling catered quiche they pretended not to notice. Edmund stood apart with his collar up, watching the mist gather in the gutters, waiting for the moment when it would be socially acceptable to leave his own grandmother's funeral.

"You're the grandson."

He turned. A woman stood a meter away, slightly too close, the way people stand when they've rehearsed an approach and then abandoned the choreography. She was perhaps thirty-five, tall, with dark hair pinned back carelessly and the kind of face that looked like it had decided long ago not to ask anything of anyone. She wore a black coat that was wrong in every particular — too short in the sleeves, too wide in the shoulder, a cheap wool that had gone shiny at the elbows. A mourner's costume bought or borrowed in haste, by someone who did not own mourning clothes.

"I'm sorry," Edmund said. "Are you—"

"Vandenberg," the woman said.

The word went through him like cold water through a crack in a wall.

Not *Reyes*. Not *meneer*. Not *you're Margriet's grandson, aren't you, I saw you play once*. Not any of the safe formulations. She said *Vandenberg* the way you'd say a password into a house everyone believed had burned down — quietly, precisely, watching his face for the door to open.

"I'm sorry," he said again, and heard how his voice had gone flat, janitor-flat, the voice he used for deflecting. "You must be thinking of someone else."

"No." She didn't move. Rain misted on her shoulders and she didn't seem to feel it. "I'm not."

Behind her, across the colonnade, his mother was looking at them. Anouk had gone very still in the way she went still before difficult movements — Edmund had seen it a thousand times across a thousand practice-room thresholds, the stillness before the hard passage, the breath held at the top of the phrase. She was watching the woman in the ill-fitting coat the way you watch a hand moving toward something fragile.

"Who are you?" Edmund asked.

"My name is Ilse." She hesitated, and for the first time something flickered under the certainty — not doubt exactly, but the effort of holding doubt down. "Ilse Vandenberg."

The mist drifted between them. Somewhere down the drive a tram sighed past on wet rails, and neither of them turned toward the sound.

"That's not possible," Edmund said. "There are no Vandenbergs. There haven't been since—" He stopped. Since his grandfather died. Since the name had been folded away like winter clothes, put in a drawer, replaced by *Reyes*, his father's plain, blameless, anonymous name. His mother had done it when he was four years old. *We're the Reyeses now,* she'd said, and he had accepted it the way children accept everything, as weather.

"There's me," Ilse said.

"How?"

She looked at him then — really looked, taking inventory, and he understood suddenly that she had studied photographs of him, that she knew his face from somewhere other than the corridor poster. "Tomas Vandenberg was my father," she said.

The name landed in a part of him that had no language. Tomas. The uncle who wasn't spoken of. The gap in the family album between 1971 and 1980, pages that existed but held nothing — he had noticed it as a boy, run his fingers over the empty squares where photos should have been, and been told *water damage*. The uncle whose name, if it was ever said at all, was said the way you say the name of a disease you've recovered from.

"Tomas died before I was born," Edmund said slowly. "Before I—" He couldn't remember what he'd been told. That was the thing. He couldn't remember, because he had never been told anything at all. There was no story of Tomas. There was only the absence shaped like a story.

"He died in Liège in 1989," Ilse said. "Alone. In a rented room above a bakery. I was six." Her voice stayed level, but her hands, jammed in the pockets of the wrong coat, had closed into fists. "My mother was Belgian. They weren't married. When he got sick she took me to Rotterdam and he—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "He wrote to me. Letters. I have letters, Mr. Reyes. Letters about a house on the Rue de Fiennes. About a piano. About a family that erased him so thoroughly that his own nephew doesn't know he existed."

Across the colonnade, Anouk had begun to move toward them, unhurried, terrible, a woman crossing a stage she had walked before.

"I don't know what you want," Edmund said.

"Neither do I, entirely." And there it was at last — the crack in her, the thing that made her human instead of accusation made flesh. "A share of a house I've never seen. Answers. Someone who answers letters." She almost smiled. "You answered mine. You're the only Reyes who ever has."

"I'm not a Reyes," Edmund heard himself say, before he could stop it, before he understood what he was admitting.

Ilse's eyes changed. Something passed between them in the rain-smell and the lily-rot and the murmur of mourners — recognition, unwelcome and absolute, two strangers discovering they were both orphans of the same silence.

Anouk arrived. Edmund felt her before he saw her, the way you feel a change in pressure before a storm. "Edmund," she said. "We're leaving." Then, to Ilse, with a courtesy so polished it was a blade: "I don't believe we've met."

"No," Ilse said. "You wouldn't have."

"We really must go." Anouk's hand closed on Edmund's arm — light, proprietary, the grip that had steered him through every stage door of his childhood. "It's been a long day."

But Edmund was looking at Ilse, and Ilse was looking at him, and somewhere beneath the numbness of fifteen years something had shifted, some plate deep in the earth, and he could hear — faintly, absurdly, in the middle of his grandmother's funeral — the sound of a locked door at the end of an upstairs hallway, and someone knocking on the other side of it.

"The house on the Rue de Fiennes," he said. "Number twelve?"

Ilse blinked. "Yes."

"I know where the key used to hang," Edmund said, and felt his mother's grip tighten on his arm like a tourniquet.
