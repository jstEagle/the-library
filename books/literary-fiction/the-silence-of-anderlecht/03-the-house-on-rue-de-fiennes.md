## 3. The House on Rue de Fiennes

My mother told me I was made of weather. She said it the way other mothers say *you have your father's eyes* — a small cruelty dressed as tenderness, delivered over the sink while she scrubbed pots that never came clean. *You're made of weather, Ilse. You blow in, you blow out. Nobody builds for you.*

She was wrong about the building part. Somebody had built for me. It had simply taken me thirty-one years to find the address.

I had grown up in Rotterdam, in a flat above a fishmonger's where the smell of brine worked itself into the plaster and never left, so that even our bread tasted faintly of the sea. My mother — Tante Ria, though she was no one's aunt — had come to Brussels once, when I was nine, and stood outside a house on a street called Rue de Fiennes for eleven minutes by the clock in the pharmacy across the road. I counted because counting was how I kept myself from crying. Eleven minutes, and then she took my hand and said, *That's enough,* and we went back to the station, and back to the flat, and back to the silence that had a shape, the way all silences do if you live inside one long enough.

The shape of ours was a man named Tomas.

She never said his name outright. She said *your father* the way you'd say *the war*, or *the fire* — an event, not a person. But there were letters. Fourteen of them, tied with butcher's twine in a biscuit tin at the bottom of her wardrobe, beneath a false bottom she thought I didn't know about. I had known since I was twelve. I read them all before I was fifteen, lying on my stomach on her bed with the letters spread around me like a deck of cards, learning my father's handwriting the way other children learned their catechism.

He wrote beautifully. That was the first thing. The second thing was that he wrote like a man who expected to be interrupted.

*Little one* — he called me that, though he had never seen me — *if you are reading this, then your mother has finally let the past into the house, and I hope it behaved better with you than it did with me.*

There were photographs too, or parts of them. A man at a piano, his face torn away where someone had cut him out — but the hands remained, resting on the keys as if mid-phrase, and I used to hold those hands up to the light and try to imagine what face belonged to them. A woman with a jaw like a hatchet, unsmiling, wearing pearls as if they were armor. And on the back of that one, in pencil, faded almost to nothing: *Margriet, 1974.*

My grandmother, then. Or half of one. Families, I would learn, are like houses: some rooms you inherit, and some rooms stay locked, and nobody tells you which is which until you've already signed something.

* * *

Tomas Vandenberg died in March, in a hospital in Ghent, alone except for a nurse who later told me he'd spent his last coherent hour asking whether anyone had opened the piano. Not *was anyone with him*. Whether anyone had *opened the piano*. I have thought about that question more than any other sentence ever spoken to me. There are people who die asking after money, people who die asking after forgiveness. He died asking after an instrument, as if inside it — under its lid, among its strings — something of his might still be waiting to be let out.

The solicitor's letter reached me in April. I read it standing in the fishmonger's doorway with the gulls screaming overhead, and I remember thinking: so this is what it feels like to be claimed. Not claiming — being claimed. All my life I had been the one doing the reaching: writing to registries, to churches, to a father who answered twice and then went quiet, to a family that existed for me only as a hatchet-jawed woman in a photograph. Now a stranger in a grey suit informed me that I was heir to a share of a house on Rue de Fiennes, and that there were conditions, and that the family — such as it survived — had been notified.

The letter named them. Anouk Reyes. Edmund Reyes.

I looked up the second name that night and found him everywhere and nowhere. A poster from fifteen years ago, a boy of nineteen in white tie, eyes already old. Reviews written in the past tense, as if he had died, though the dates suggested otherwise. *A talent that arrives once a generation,* one critic had written, and then, three years later, the same critic, shorter this time: *Whatever became of Edmund Reyes?*

I knew something about whatever-became-of. It had been my whole biography.

* * *

I wrote to him first. It seemed correct to approach the living before the dead, and of the two names, his was the one attached to an address. I said who I was — plainly, without ornament, because I had learned that secrets lose their power the moment you stop whispering them — and I asked for nothing except a meeting. I expected what I always expected: nothing, or worse than nothing, a lawyer's letter telling me the family would contest, that my mother's claims were baseless, that Vandenberg was a name that did not extend its branches to weather like me.

His reply came in nine days. Handwritten. He answered everything, apparently. He was, I would learn, the only Reyes who answered anything.

We met at a café near the Gare du Midi, under a awning streaked with pigeon droppings, and I recognized him before he sat down — not from the poster but from the photograph, from the hands. He had his uncle's hands. I nearly said so and didn't; there are things you save, like coins, for moments that can bear their weight.

"You have her jaw," he said instead, sitting down. "Margriet's."

"I have a photograph of Margriet," I said. "It's the closest I've come to inheriting anything."

He flinched. I watched it move through him like weather through wheat — there and gone, smoothed over by a lifetime's practice at being unreadable. We ordered coffee neither of us drank. Between us on the table I laid the biscuit-tin letters, and I watched a man look at his own history in another person's handwriting.

"He wrote to me," I said. "Twice. When I was nineteen and again at twenty-five. Then nothing. Did he—"

"He stopped writing to everyone." Edmund turned a letter over, studying the postmark as if the date might explain something the words could not. "After— there was a period. Things went quiet in that house. I was a child. I thought quiet was just what houses did."

"And now?"

"Now I think quiet is usually maintained." He said it carefully, the way a man tests ice. "By someone. For a reason."

I had brought the photograph — the one with the cut-out face and the surviving hands — and I put it on the table between us. He looked at it for a long time. Around us the café went on with its ordinary noises, cups and gossip and the hiss of the espresso machine, and none of it touched the little climate we had made at that table, which was the climate of two people discovering they had been kept apart on purpose.

"Will you help me?" I asked. "See the house. That's all. I'm not asking you to give me anything or take sides. I just want to stand in a room my father stood in. I've been claiming him in absentia my whole life. I'd like to claim him in person, once, before the lawyers turn him into paperwork."

Edmund looked at the photograph again — at the hands on the keys — and something crossed his face that I couldn't name yet, though I would learn its name later, in a hallway full of dust, standing before a door with no key.

"When?" he said.

* * *

We went on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays felt less like trespassing than Sundays would have. The house on Rue de Fiennes stood behind a wall of ivy gone feral, its windows clouded with the particular grime of years, its front door painted a green that had once been proud and was now the color of old pond water. A house doesn't have to fall down to be abandoned. It only has to be unclaimed. This one had the look of a thing holding its breath.

Edmund had a key that stuck. He worked it with the patience of a man accustomed to doors resisting him, and when it gave, the smell came out to meet us: dust and cold ash and paper, and underneath it all, faint as a pulse, beeswax — someone had loved this wood once, polished it, and the polish remembered.

The hall ran long and dim. A staircase rose into shadow. On the wallpaper, faded roses climbed toward a ceiling lost in gloom, and I stood in the doorway a moment longer than I meant to, because my legs had decided this was a threshold and thresholds deserve ceremony.

"Which room was his?" I asked.

Edmund didn't answer at once. He was looking up the stairs with an expression I recognized from my own mirror — the look of a person returning to a place that had returned to them first.

"Upstairs," he said finally. "At the end of the hall. But—" He stopped. Started again. "There's a room up there that was always locked. Even when I was a child. Even when we lived here."

"Locked how?"

"Properly locked. Not a bedroom lock. A door that means *no*."

We climbed. The stairs complained under us in voices, each step a different pitch, and I thought absurdly: *even the house is an instrument.* At the top, the corridor stretched away into brown light, doors on either side standing ajar like mouths mid-sentence, and at the far end, exactly as promised, a door that was different. Darker wood. No handle-wear, no scuffed paint, none of the honest damage a door earns from being used. This one had been spared use the way a relic is spared touch.

Edmund tried the handle anyway. Of course he did. It didn't turn, and the sound he made — small, involuntary, almost a note — told me he had tried this door many times in his life, and been refused every time.

"There's no key," he said. "There was never a key. Not in the bowls, not in the drawers. I looked for years when I was a boy. I thought—" He pressed his palm flat against the wood, the way you'd check a patient for fever. "I thought maybe I'd imagined the room. That it was just a wall that had learned to look like a door."

I put my hand beside his. The wood was cold, colder than the corridor justified, as if the room behind it were keeping its own season.

"My father died asking whether anyone had opened the piano," I said quietly.

Edmund went very still.

"What piano?" he said.

And from somewhere below us — down the stairs, through the hall, in a room we hadn't entered yet — the house settled on its foundations with a long wooden sigh, the sound a building makes when it has waited a very long time to exhale.
