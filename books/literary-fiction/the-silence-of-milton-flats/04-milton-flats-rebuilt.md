## 4. Milton Flats, Rebuilt

The bus took four hours and I spent all of them rehearsing.

Not what I'd say—I'd already said everything worth saying to Ada Voss, who had a talent for listening the way some people have a talent for lying, completely and without visible effort. No, what I rehearsed was my face. The face I would wear when I stood in front of whatever remained of my brother's life. Grief was the obvious choice. Grief was expected, appropriate, and safe. But grief was also a lie of its own kind, because you cannot grieve a man you never met, and that was the plain fact sitting beside me in the window seat: Daniel Whitlock died eleven years ago in a fire I'd sent postcards to. Whoever this Marchetti person was, I was about to meet him for the first time, and I resented him for it.

I got off at a stop Ada had described as "the one with the pharmacy that's always closing," which turned out to be accurate in a way that told me she'd lived here a long time. The block itself announced nothing. That was the first surprise. I had carried Milton Flats in my head for over a decade as a wound—a burned thing, an absence—and here it was, rebuilt, ordinary, a row of narrow brick buildings with awnings and window boxes and a bakery on the corner called Ferro's that smelled so aggressively of cardamom I nearly forgave it for existing.

Because that was the address. Above the bakery. Third floor. Ada had written it on the back of a funeral card, which felt like the kind of joke Daniel himself might have made, if I'd known him well enough to know his jokes.

I stood across the street for twenty minutes. A woman walked a dog. A man argued cheerfully into his phone about a fantasy football trade. The world declined to recognize the significance of the moment, which is the world's way.

* * *

Ada met me at the door with keys she apparently still had, because someone—Marisol, she said, or maybe Theo—hadn't gotten around to changing the locks, and no one had said out loud why. The apartment smelled like him before it looked like anything. Not cologne; I wouldn't have known his cologne. It smelled like coffee gone cold in a pot and pencil shavings and something faintly chemical, like darkroom fluid or model glue, and I understood immediately that smell is the most dishonest sense we have, because it made me mourn a stranger.

"You can take your time," Ada said. "I'll be downstairs. Theo's working the counter today."

She left me alone with him, which was either kindness or strategy. With Ada I suspected both were always in play.

The apartment was small and ruthlessly organized, the kind of order that isn't tidiness but authorship. Books shelved by height rather than subject, which offended me until I saw the marginalia—dense, furious, funny annotations in a hand I recognized, because I'd spent years studying our father's handwriting on ledgers and contracts, and Daniel's hand was our father's hand loosened by half a degree, like a song played slightly off tempo. He'd underlined passages and argued with them. One book of essays had a note in the margin that read simply: *Ruth would hate this. Good.*

I sat down on a chair that wasn't offered to me and laughed until it turned into something else.

* * *

The photographs started in the hallway and multiplied into the living room like a slow infestation.

There he was, obviously—Daniel, but older than the brother in my head, grayer at the temples, squinting in most shots the way people do when they're laughing and don't want to admit it. There was Marisol, whom I recognized from nothing and knew instantly, the way you know a person from how they stand next to your sibling. There was Theo in every stage from toddler to the tall, wounded teenager currently selling bread downstairs, gap-toothed on a tricycle, solemn at a science fair, mid-shriek on someone's shoulders at what appeared to be a parade.

And then, between a picture of a birthday cake shaped badly like a rocket and a framed ticket stub from some concert, there was me.

I want to be precise about this, because precision was all I had left. It was me at the lake—the summer before the fire, the last summer anyone pretended we were a family that functioned. I was standing at the end of the dock in Paul's ridiculous fishing hat, holding up a fish I hadn't caught, and I was laughing. I remembered the moment. I remembered hating the hat and loving the afternoon and having no idea anyone was watching.

He'd been there. On the shore, or behind me, or somewhere in the green blur of that day, my brother—who by then must have already been planning his disappearance, or at least circling it—had stopped and taken a photograph of me without my knowledge, kept it for eleven years, hung it in his hallway above a bakery in a city where nobody knew my name, and looked at it often enough that the frame's corner had faded where the sun hit it.

I did not know whether to be moved or violated. The two feelings arrived together, holding hands, and refused to separate. Being watched by love is still being watched. I stood in his hallway with my own laughing face staring back at me and understood that I had been, all these years, part of the furniture of another man's life—a sister-shaped absence he'd furnished with a single stolen image.

There was a note taped to the back of the frame. I took it down. Of course I took it down. You would have too.

It said, in that almost-familiar handwriting: *E., at the lake. She doesn't know I'm leaving yet. She'll write anyway. She always writes.*

*Always writes.* Present tense. Eleven years of postcards to a burned building, and he'd known—not guessed, known—that they'd keep coming. Which meant every card I sent, every banal weather report and non-confession, may have been received, read, kept. Somewhere in this apartment, possibly, was a shoebox containing the other half of my correspondence, the half I'd spent a decade pretending didn't exist because the alternative was unbearable.

I found it in the bedroom closet. Under sweaters. Unlabeled, because he was the kind of man who labeled nothing he loved.

* * *

I didn't open it. I want credit for that.

I held it on my lap for the better part of an hour, sitting on a dead man's bed in a dead man's room while the bakery below sent up smells of cinnamon and the light moved across the floor in the unhurried way light does when it has nowhere to be. My thumb worked under the lid's edge twice. Both times I stopped, and I'd like to tell you it was out of respect, but the truth is uglier: I stopped because once I read them, I would know exactly how much he'd known about us—about me—and there was a version of that knowledge I couldn't survive before I'd finished deciding what to do with everything else.

Eleven years of one-sided letters, and it turned out the correspondence might have been two-sided all along. I had built a whole moral architecture on the assumption that I was talking to ash.

Downstairs, the bell above the bakery door rang. Voices. Then a boy's voice, cracking in the middle the way fifteen-year-old voices do, saying something that made the whole room laugh—including, unmistakably, a laugh I recognized, because it was our mother's laugh, or close enough that grief reached up and grabbed me by the throat.

Theo. His son. Who had laughed like her.

I put the shoebox back exactly where I found it, aligned to the millimeter, because order was the only inheritance I had to offer anyone. Then I went downstairs to meet my nephew, carrying my face—the rehearsed one, the grieving one—and hoping to God it would hold.

He looked up from behind the counter, flour on his forearms, and went very still.

"You're her," he said. "You're the one who writes."
