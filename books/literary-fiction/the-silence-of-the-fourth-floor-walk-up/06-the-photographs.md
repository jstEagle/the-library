## 6. The Photographs

The box had been living under my bed for eleven years, which is longer than I'd lived in the walk-up and roughly the length of one presidential administration. It was a shoebox once—some dead brand called Keds that my grandmother must have bought in a decade when shoes came with consequences—and it had been promoted over the years to a hatbox, then to a storage bin, and finally to its current position: the cardboard box from a microwave I'd thrown away in 2014. The box had outlived the appliance. There was probably a lesson in that, but I've never been good at lessons. I'm good at filing.

I took it out on a Sunday because Sundays are for rituals, and because the radiator in my apartment had started making a sound like a man clearing his throat before saying something he'd regret, and I needed to be near the window where the light came in honest and slanted and made even my floorboards look like they had somewhere to be.

The truth is I hadn't opened it since I moved in. That's not neglect. That's curation. There is a difference between not looking at a thing and deciding, daily, not to look at it. I had made an art of the second kind.

My grandmother's name was Tala Villalobos, though the box didn't say so. Nothing in the box said anything, at first. It just sat there on my kitchen table radiating the particular patience of objects that have waited long enough to stop caring whether you open them.

I made tea first. This is important. You do not approach a box like this empty-handed. You bring an offering or you don't come at all.

* * *

Inside, in order of excavation:

One wool shawl, moth-bitten in a way that looked almost intentional, like lace someone had designed by letting insects do the needlework.

A rosary whose beads were some dark seed I couldn't name, strung on wire gone green at the knots.

A recipe card, handwritten, for something called *pan de salu*, which is either bread or a blessing or both; the ink had faded to the color of weak tea, and half the ingredients were given as "enough."

And underneath all of it—the sediment layer, the part the box had been protecting—photographs.

I laid them out on the table the way I lay out documents at work, left to right, chronological where possible, and I did what I always do with other people's histories: I catalogued.

Photograph one: a woman on a stoop, squinting, holding a baby who may or may not have been my mother. The woman's face was my mother's face with forty extra years of weather on it, which meant it was also, distantly, mine. I put that one face-down. Not ready. Some documents you file closed.

Photograph two: two girls on a fire escape, maybe sixteen, maybe seventeen, laughing at whoever held the camera. One was my grandmother—younger than I'd ever seen her, all collarbones and defiance, hair pinned up in a style that suggested she'd done it in four seconds and dared the world to comment. The other girl—

The other girl I knew.

Not from memory. From the fourth floor. From the hallway, from the sound of slow feet and a door that opens at exactly seven each morning. Marisol Achebe, sixty years younger, arms linked through my grandmother's arm, mid-laugh, caught in the exact moment when laughing turns into saying something true.

I sat down. My tea went cold and I let it, which for me is the equivalent of screaming.

Here is what I want to be clear about: I am not a woman who is surprised easily. Surprise requires expectation, and I have spent a decade dismantling my expectations the way you'd dismantle a bomb, wire by careful wire, until nothing in me could go off. I file records for people with names like mine—long names, names that spill off forms, names that clerks shorten without asking—and I have learned that the safest thing a name can be is expected. Nothing about this photograph was expected.

Two girls on a fire escape. My grandmother and the woman upstairs. Linked at the elbow like the city itself had welded them there.

I turned the photograph over. On the back, in pencil, in handwriting I recognized the way you recognize a voice through a wall—faintly, structurally, without being able to swear to it—one word: *antes*. Before.

Before what? Before the box. Before the silence. Before whatever happened between the girl on the fire escape and the woman who died owing me an explanation I'd stopped billing her for.

* * *

There were more photographs. Seven total. In three of them, Marisol appeared—Marisol and Tala at what looked like a church picnic, Marisol and Tala and a crowd of women whose faces all shared some family resemblance to each other but not to me, Marisol alone in a doorway holding a notebook the way my grandmother's box had been held: patiently, protectively, like it might get away.

In the others, strangers. A man in a butcher's apron giving the camera a thumbs-up with a hand that had clearly seen things thumbs should not see. Children on a sidewalk chalk-drawing something that time had erased. A wedding party spilling down church steps, everyone mid-shout.

I studied them the way I study everything: for errors, for omissions, for the small telltale gaps where a story has been edited. Because here is the thing nobody tells you about growing up with silence instead of stories—you become an auditor. You learn to read absence the way other people read print.

My mother never talked about Tala. Not *wouldn't*—*didn't*, the way you don't step on a certain crack, the way you don't say a certain name indoors. When I was small I thought my grandmother was dead, because that was the only category my mother offered for people who existed nowhere in conversation. It wasn't until I was nineteen, filling out a form that asked for emergency contacts and family medical history, that I learned Tala Villalobos had been alive the whole time—alive, and ninety minutes away by train, and unreachable, because the number my mother gave me was disconnected, and when I asked her about it she said, in the flattest voice I have ever heard come out of a human mouth, "There's nothing there anymore."

She wasn't talking about the phone line.

Tala died when I was twenty-three. I found out from a county records search I ran at work, on a lunch break, using skills I will neither confirm nor deny. There was no funeral I was told about. There was a box, shipped to my mother, forwarded to me, unopened, because my mother wrote on the forwarding note—in actual ink, in her actual hand—"Give this to the girl. She'll know what to do with it."

I did not know what to do with it. I put it under my bed and built a life on top of it, which is, if you think about it, what foundations are.

* * *

Under the photographs, folded into eighths and gone soft as cloth at the creases, was a letter.

I want to tell you I hesitated. I want to tell you I respected the privacy of the dead, that I set it aside, that I slept on it. But I have told you already that I am an auditor of silences, and a letter in a box is a document addressed, however obliquely, to whoever finally opens the box. My mother said I'd know what to do with it. Knowing what to do with it, apparently, was this.

The handwriting was my grandmother's—I knew it now, having spent an hour with the recipe card, calibrating. The letter was dated fourteen years ago. It was short. Letters written in anger usually are; anger doesn't believe in postscripts.

It was addressed to no one, or to everyone, or possibly to me. It began in the middle, the way arguments do when they've been going on so long the beginning has worn away:

*"You call her a traitor. Fine. Write it in your book of words, then, since you count everything. But ask yourself what your counting saved. I hid what had to be hidden so it would still be here to count. You kept it in the open and lost it anyway. Traitor. Look in the mirror, Marisol, and count what you see."*

I read it three times.

Then I did something I'm not proud of, which is nothing. I sat very still in my kitchen while the radiator cleared its throat and the Sunday light moved across the table like a slow hand, and I felt the specific vertigo of a person who has inherited a debt and only just discovered the ledger.

Because here is what I had, in full, of the feud between Tala Villalobos and Marisol Achebe: one letter, calling one of them a traitor, written by the other. And here is what I knew about why: nothing. Less than nothing, because the letter assumed knowledge, the way family letters always do—it argued with a history I'd never been given, citing grievances I'd never been taught, in a language I could only half feel my way through.

*Treason.* My whole childhood, the word had hung around my grandmother like smoke around a coat. Not spoken—my mother didn't speak—but present, structural, load-bearing. When I asked once, at nine, why we didn't visit Grandma Tala, my mother had looked at me with an expression I now recognize as terror wearing a mask of boredom, and said, "Because she chose wrong." Chose wrong *what*? A side? A man? A word?

I had grown up on inherited anger the way some kids grow up on inherited money—spending it freely, never questioning where it came from, never once reading the fine print of the trust. I had disliked a woman I'd never met, on behalf of a mother who'd never explained, over a betrayal no one had ever named. That is not conviction. That is heirloom resentment, polished yearly, displayed proudly, worth nothing.

And now the woman from the photographs—the traitor's accuser, the keeper of the word-count, the slow footsteps on the fourth floor—was living thirty feet above my head, singing in a language I almost recognized, opening her door at seven every morning like punctuation.

I looked at the photograph again. Two girls on a fire escape. *Antes.* Before.

Before the letter. Before the accusation. Before whatever war had been fought in whispers and hymns and notebooks while I was being born into the armistice, raised on the treaty terms, taught to keep my voice down in a language I didn't even know I was keeping quiet.

My mother burned the address. I've known that a long time—not as fact, at first, but as the shape a fact leaves when it's removed, the way you know a tooth is missing. Postcards to a place that isn't there aren't letters; they're prayers with stamps. And I had sent them every Sunday for ten years, curating my life into small reportable surfaces—*the job is fine, the apartment is fine, the weather is fine*—while the real inventory, the ledger of unsaid things, grew fat and unread in a shoebox under my bed.

I got up. I got my coat. I don't fully remember deciding to; I remember the coat, and the photograph in my inside pocket, face-in, like contraband, like evidence, like a summons.

The fourth floor is forty-eight steps from mine. I have counted them for years, the way I count everything, without ever once climbing them with intent.

Tonight I climbed them with intent, and stood in front of the door with the brass knocker worn smooth as a river stone, and raised my hand—

and heard, from inside, faintly, the same song from the stairwell weeks ago, the lullaby-shaped thing, the language I almost knew—

and knocked.
