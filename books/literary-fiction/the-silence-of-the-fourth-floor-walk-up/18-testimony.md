## 18. Testimony

The municipal building smelled like floor wax and old paper, the way institutions smell when they want you to believe they have been here longer than you and will be here after. Jude stood in the corridor outside Hearing Room 2C with a folder pressed to her chest like a shield she'd forgotten how to lower.

She had dressed carefully, which was itself a confession. For ten years she had dressed to be overlooked—greys, browns, a coat the color of wet pavement. This morning she had put on her grandmother's scarf, the one with the frayed red edge that Tala wore in frame three of the photographs, and she had caught herself in the hallway mirror looking like someone who intended to be remembered.

*Bad plan*, said the old voice in her head, the inventory voice, the ledger-keeper. *You have spent a decade perfecting the art of not being seen. Today is not the day to fail at it.*

But another voice had been growing all winter, low and patient, with Marisol's cadence. That voice said: *You did not learn two hundred words to keep them in a drawer.*

The corridor was filling. Mrs. Okonkwo from 3B had brought her church hat, the enormous blue one with the silk peonies, which meant she was prepared for either a wedding or a war. The Delgado twins from the second floor stood on either side of their father like bookends, holding signs they'd made from poster board: SAVE OUR BLOCK, though the S had been drawn backwards and no one had the heart to fix it. Mr. Petrov, who Jude knew only as the sound of slow footsteps and opera through the floor, had shaved for the occasion and looked startled by his own face.

And there were the others—the ones who wouldn't speak. The woman from 1A who kept her curtains drawn even in summer. The man who worked nights and slept through the landlord's notices taped to the lobby door. They had come anyway. They stood along the walls of the corridor with their hands in their pockets, witnesses without testimony, present the way furniture is present.

"You're pale," said Mrs. Okonkwo, patting Jude's arm. "Did you eat?"

"I ate."

"Liar." She pressed a foil-wrapped something into Jude's hand. "Eat it in there if you have to. Fainting is dramatic but it doesn't stop bulldozers."

Jude almost smiled. Almost.

Her phone buzzed. A text from the hospital, from the nurse Marisol had bribed with cookies to send updates: *She's awake. Watching the clock. She says tell you: three words, then breathe.*

Three words, then breathe. It was the drill. Every evening for two weeks, over the hospital phone, Marisol had made Jude rehearse—not the English statement, which was easy, which was safe—but the Kavaya'tl. *Say it until your mouth forgets it's foreign. Say it until it's just your mouth.*

Jude had frozen every time the rehearsal got past three listeners. At Marisol's bedside with one nurse in the room, fine. In the hospital chapel with Mrs. Okonkwo and the Delgados watching, her throat closed like a fist. She had stood there, mouth open, words dissolving, and heard—in the terrible echo chamber of memory—a classroom thirty years ago, a teacher's bright mocking voice: *Say it again, sweetie, let's all laugh at how her mother talks.* Her mother standing in the doorway of the parent conference, accent thick as river mud, shrinking by inches.

That was where the silence started. Not with Jude. With a woman trying to make herself smaller so her daughter could be safe. And the daughter learning the lesson too well, building a whole life out of smallness, out of postcards to ash, out of names filed alphabetically for other people while her own went unpronounced.

"Ms. Villalobos?" A clerk with a clipboard. "Are you testifying? I need you on the list."

"I—" The folder felt suddenly heavy. "Yes."

"Name?"

Jude opened her mouth. Closed it. Somewhere in her chest, Marisol's voice: *Your name first. Always your name first. A language lives or dies in what people call themselves.*

"Jude Villalobos," she said. And then, before she could stop herself, before the clerk could move on: "It's pronounced Vee-yah-LOH-bos. Not Villa-luh-boss. If anyone reads it aloud."

The clerk blinked, wrote something in the margin, and moved on. Mrs. Okonkwo made a small approving sound, like a kettle deciding to be kind.

* * *

Hearing Room 2C was smaller than television had promised and colder. The zoning board sat behind a raised bench of blond wood, five of them, four men and a woman, with nameplates and water pitchers and the particular posture of people who have already made up their minds and are merely waiting for the paperwork to justify them. The developer's team occupied the front rows: suits, tablets, an architect's rendering of the block transformed into glass and steel, the fourth-floor walk-up erased and replaced with something called THE MERIDIAN, which sounded less like a building than a dental plan.

The tenants' side was fuller, louder, more nervous. Their lawyer was a tired young woman named Priya who had taken the case mostly free and apologized twice for not being better. Beside her sat the translator—a hired interpreter, court-certified, who would render the Spanish-speaking tenants' statements into English for the record.

The hearing began. There were procedures. There were stipulations. The developer's lawyer spoke for twenty minutes about blight and opportunity and the economic vitality of the corridor, using the word "underutilized" eleven times, and Jude counted each one because counting was what she did instead of panicking.

Then the tenants testified.

Mrs. Okonkwo spoke about the block for nine minutes without notes, her church hat nodding like a ship's figurehead, and even the board leaned in. Mr. Delgado testified through the interpreter, his hands doing half the talking. The night worker from the third floor spoke so quietly the board asked him twice to repeat himself, and he did, and Jude watched him do it and thought: *that is braver than anything I have ever done, including this.*

Two hours in, the interpreter broke.

It wasn't dramatic. He simply reached the end of what his certification covered. The next witness was old Ana Reyes from 1C, ninety-one years old, who had lived on the block since before it had a name, and she testified in a Spanish so old and regional that the interpreter's face went smooth with panic. He tried. He caught maybe one sentence in three. Ana kept going, faster, her voice rising, and the board's expressions shifted from patience to polite blankness, and Priya whispered urgently into her phone, and the stenographer's fingers hovered over dead keys.

"She's saying," the interpreter offered weakly, "something about... the garden? And her husband? I'm sorry, her dialect is—I can't—"

"This is highly irregular," said the chairman, glancing at his watch. "If the witness cannot provide testimony the board can understand—"

Ana Reyes stopped. She looked at the bench, at the rendering of THE MERIDIAN, at the interpreter who couldn't carry her words across the room. And Jude saw her begin, slowly, to fold herself smaller. Saw the shoulders come down. Saw the mouth close around whatever else she'd come to say.

*There it is*, Jude thought. *The oldest trick in the world. Make them unable to be understood, and they'll make themselves quiet. You don't need to evict a language. You just need to make the room where it's spoken too small.*

Her heart was going like a fist on a door.

*Three words, then breathe.*

She stood up.

The sound of her chair scraping back was very loud in the cold room. Five faces turned toward her. Priya's eyes went wide. Mrs. Okonkwo's hand flew to her hat brim.

"Madam Chair," Jude said, and her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "Point of order. The witness's testimony is being lost. I'd like to offer a translation—and then a statement of my own."

"Are you qualified to translate?"

"No," Jude said honestly. "I'm qualified to do something else."

She walked forward. The folder stayed on the table; she didn't need it. What she needed was in her body now, two hundred words deep, drilled over hospital phones and kitchen tables, sung badly and corrected patiently, carried up four floors of stairs in a body that had finally stopped pretending it was from nowhere.

She knelt slightly beside Ana Reyes's chair. "May I?" And Ana, after a moment of searching her face for something—family, maybe, or just permission—nodded.

Jude listened. And to her own astonishment, the old woman's Spanish unlocked something adjacent, something neighboring, the way a key near the right one sometimes turns a stubborn lock. Kavaya'tl had borrowed from everyone who ever passed through its valley; it was a language built out of other languages' leftovers, out of survival. She caught the shape of it: the garden, yes. The husband, forty years dead, buried under the tree he planted. The fig tree. *They cannot dig up the fig tree,* Ana was saying, over and over, *they cannot dig up the fig tree, my husband is in the roots.*

Jude translated it into plain English, sentence by sentence, and the stenographer's keys woke up, and the board wrote things down, and Ana Reyes finished her testimony at full volume, upright, her hands open on her knees.

When it was done, the chairman cleared his throat. "Thank you. Ms.—Villalobos, is it? Do you wish to testify as well? We're approaching the hour."

*Villalobos*, mispronounced, flat, wrong.

Jude stood at the front of the room. She did not look at the developer's lawyers. She looked at the wall behind the bench, at the seal of the city, and beyond it, in her mind, at a fourth-floor kitchen where an old woman had once told her: *A language doesn't die when its last speaker dies. It dies a little earlier, when its speakers decide no one is listening. Don't you dare decide that today.*

"I'm going to say my statement twice," Jude said. "First in Kavaya'tl. Then in English. Kavaya'tl is the language of my grandmother, Tala Villalobos, who taught it in secret in a church basement when the schools banned it, hiding it inside hymns so it would survive long enough to reach me. There is one fluent speaker left. She is in St. Vincent's Hospital right now, listening to this on a radio we smuggled in against regulations, and she made me promise to say my name correctly, so I'm going to start with that."

She breathed. Three words, then breathe.

"My name is Jude Villalobos. My grandmother's name was Tala. My teacher's name is Marisol Achebe. And the building you are voting to erase is called by everyone who lives in it simply *the block*, because it never needed another name."

And then she spoke Kavaya'tl.

It came out slower than rehearsal, and her accent bent in places Marisol would have pinched her for, and somewhere in the middle her voice cracked on the word for *home*—*kavaya* itself, the root, the house the language was named for—but it came out. All of it. Into a municipal hearing room that had never heard it before, into the official record, into the mouths of strangers. She spoke of the building with no elevator where neighbors knew each other by footsteps. She spoke of the dead: Tala, silenced and secret; the husbands and mothers buried in gardens and memories up and down the block; the words themselves, the ones that had died waiting for someone to use them. She spoke of grief-as-weather and apology-as-return, words with no English home, and said that a neighborhood was also a word that didn't translate—that what the developers called *underutilized* was, in her grandmother's language, a single word meaning *full of what cannot be counted*.

The room had gone completely still. Even the developer's lawyers had stopped typing.

Then she gave it again in English, sentence for sentence, and ended:

"You have a rendering of what you'll build here. Glass, steel, a name that sounds like a dental plan. But you cannot render what you're demolishing, because it isn't on the lot. It's in a notebook on a hospital tray table, two hundred and six words long, held by one throat. I am asking you—not as a tenant, not as a plaintiff, but as the second speaker of a language you've never heard—to give us time. Because last week a stranger emailed me asking to learn. Last week, the language grew. Things that grow should not be paved."

Silence. Then Mrs. Okonkwo's hat nodded once, solemnly, like a verdict of its own.

"The board will recess for deliberation," said the chairman, and his voice, Jude noticed, had lost some of its varnish.

* * *

In the corridor afterward, the tenants gathered around her the way people gather around a fire. Ana Reyes took both of Jude's hands in hers and said something long and soft, and though Jude understood perhaps a tenth of it, she understood all of it.

"What did I say?" she asked the interpreter, who had followed them out, still shaken.

He smiled tiredly. "She said your grandmother would be proud. And she said—" he paused, listening to his own translation, "—she said the fig tree sends its regards."

Jude's phone rang. The hospital.

She answered with her heart in her teeth, and the nurse's voice came through, tinny and strange, holding the phone up to the radio static and to something underneath it—an old woman's voice, thin but unmistakable, singing.

Not a hymn. Not quite. Something older, in Kavaya'tl, the melody winding through the hospital ward like smoke finding a window.

"She's been singing since the recess ended," the nurse said. "We can't get her to stop. Is—is everything all right down there?"

Jude pressed the phone to her ear and listened to the language, loose and alive, pouring out of the one throat that had held it, spilling over at last.

"Yes," Jude said, and found she was crying in a public corridor without minding at all. "Everything is exactly all right. Tell her the vote comes tomorrow."

"And tell her—" the nurse paused, covering the receiver, then returned. "She says she has something for you. A word. She says it's the last one in the notebook, and she's saving it."

"For what?"

"For dawn," the nurse said. "She says to come at dawn."
