## 22. First Speaker, Second Speaker

The radiator in apartment 4B still knocked twice before it settled, and I had come to think of it as applause.

On the first Saturday of April, seven people climbed the four flights to Marisol's old kitchen—no elevator, never an elevator—and arranged themselves around a table that had been built for four and had spent its whole life being disappointed. There was Devi from the second floor, who brought her daughter Priya because, she said, the girl hummed everything and might as well hum something that mattered. There was Mr. Okonkwo from the corner store, who claimed he was only there to supervise his wife but took notes faster than anyone. There was the twins from 2C, Elena and Rosa, who had grown up hearing their grandfather speak Kavaya'tl to his tomatoes and wanted to know what he'd been saying all those years. ("Mostly complaints," I told them. "He was telling the tomatoes they were ungrateful." They wrote it down anyway.) And there was Sam, twenty-three, a nursing student who had found the recording of my testimony online and emailed me eleven times before I answered once.

Seven students. One cassette player, silver-faced, its counter stuck at 047 no matter how far the tape ran. And me, standing at the head of the table with Marisol's notebook open to the first page, feeling like a woman holding someone else's umbrella over a crowd in the rain.

"Before we start," I said, "you should know I'm not a teacher."

"Neither was she," said Mrs. Okonkwo, nodding at the cassette player as if Marisol were sitting inside it, which, in every way that counted, she was.

"That's true," I admitted. "She used to say the language teaches itself, and we're just the furniture it leans on."

I turned the notebook toward them. The first page, in Marisol's small, stubborn handwriting: *Kavaya'tl is not dying. It is waiting.* Below it, the word she'd taught me first—the word for *stay*—written three times, each time more certain than the last.

"We begin where she began," I said. "With staying."

* * *

I want to tell you about the sound of a language coming back.

It doesn't arrive all at once, like a train pulling into a station. It arrives the way light comes into a room in the morning—one window at a time, one face lit up when a syllable lands right, one person laughing because they've just said something out loud that their mouth has never made before and their body somehow remembers. Rosa got the word for *rain-on-warm-stone* on her fourth try and pressed both hands flat against the table like she could feel it. Mr. Okonkwo, who had sworn he had no ear for languages, discovered he had an excellent ear for the word meaning *more-than-enough*, which he pronounced with such relish that his wife accused him of practicing it at dinner.

Devi's daughter Priya didn't speak at all for the first hour. She just listened, chin on her fists, the way I used to listen through Marisol's door before I ever knocked on it. Then, near the end, while the others fumbled through a call-and-response, she sang. Just a few notes—the opening phrase of Tala's hymn, the one hidden inside church music, the melody that had drifted up a stairwell two years ago and found me humming it in my own kitchen without knowing why.

The room went quiet. The cassette player's little red light glowed.

"That's the hymn," I said, when I could.

"I know it," Priya said, puzzled. "I've always known it. My grandmother sings it when she thinks nobody's home."

Of course she did. That was how the language had survived every attempt to bury it—not in schools, not in books, but in kitchens and stairwells and gardens, sung to tomatoes and babies, disguised as nothing, carried by people who thought they were only humming.

I wrote in the margin of Marisol's notebook, in pencil, so it could be erased if I was wrong: *Speaker number nine.*

Then I crossed out the pencil and went over it in ink. I was not wrong. I had stopped allowing myself the luxury of maybe.

* * *

After class, while the others clattered down the stairs arguing about whether the word for *bread* also meant *luck* (it does; this is the kind of thing that makes a language worth saving), I sat at Marisol's table with a postcard and a pen.

You know the ritual by now. Ten years of Sunday postcards to an address that had burned to its foundations before I was old enough to understand what fire takes and what it leaves. A decade of writing to ash, of curating a life out of omissions, of signing myself with a name shortened to almost nothing—*Jude*, a name like a door left ajar.

But the address on this card was real now. Found at last, in the way all true things are found: slowly, then suddenly, then with a terrible ordinary finality. My mother lived in a ground-floor apartment in a city four hours south, near a laundromat and a bus stop, and she had lived there for six years, and she had not moved, and she was not hiding. She had simply been waiting, the way the language waited, the way Marisol waited, for someone to knock.

The card itself was a photograph of the building. Our building. The developer's lawyers had lost the rezoning vote pending review, and pending review had become, through the slow alchemy of tenacious tenants and a very patient city councilwoman, *not happening*. The block stood. The fourth floor stood. Someone had taken a picture of the facade on a bright morning, laundry strung between windows like flags of small nations, and I'd bought a stack of fifty from the shop on the corner.

I wrote:

*Mama —*

*Spring came early here. The tomatoes on the roof are already showing off. I've started teaching — you'll laugh, but it's true — a class in the language your mother kept and your grandmother hid and I spent thirty years pretending I didn't hear. Seven students. Eight, counting the girl who already knows the hymn without knowing she knows it.*

*I'm not writing to ask anything. I learned that from you, or rather I learned the wrong version of it for a long time, and now I'm learning the right one. I'm writing because the address is real and so am I, and because Marisol — the woman on the fourth floor, the one I told you about, the last one who spoke it alone — used to say that a word kept too long stops being a word and becomes a stone. So here. This is me setting some stones down.*

*The building stands. We stood for it. You would have liked the woman who taught me to stand. She counted words the way other people count money, and she left me all of hers.*

*I'll write again next Sunday. And the Sunday after that. Not to an absence anymore. To you.*

*With love, and finally with the whole of it —*

And then I signed it. Both names. The name the city gave me and the name my grandmother's mother gave our family, the one I had practiced saying alone in Marisol's kitchen until it stopped tasting like a stranger's coat and started fitting my shoulders. I wrote it in English letters, and beneath it, carefully, in Kavaya'tl — the spelling Marisol had drilled into me, each mark a small act of return.

Two names. Two languages. One hand.

I looked at the signature for a long time. Then I put the card in my coat pocket, because there was one more stop to make before the mailbox.

* * *

Tala's grave sits on a hill in the cemetery across the river, under a maple that will be spectacular in another month and is currently just ambitious. The stone is modest. The dates are worn soft. Someone—Tala's daughter, I suspect, though she has never admitted to it—leaves fresh rosemary every few weeks, and I have started leaving it when she doesn't, so that between the two of us the grave has never once gone bare.

I visited on Sundays at first, then whenever the notebook grew heavy, which was often. Because here is the thing nobody tells you about inheritance: it isn't property. It's homework.

Marisol died at dawn on a Tuesday in February, and her last word to me was the Kavaya'tl word for *begin*, and I have thought about that every day since. Not *goodbye*. Not even *stay*, though she loved that word best and taught it first. *Begin.* She handed me the language the way you hand someone a shovel at the start of a long dig—not as a burden, exactly, but with the honest acknowledgment that the work is the gift.

So I read to Tala. I read the notebook aloud, page by page, word by word, because Marisol never managed to say her apology to the woman she'd hated for surviving by disguise, and because I had promised. Some Sundays I read the apology itself, the one I'd written into the back pages in both our hands' worth of ink: *You hid it to save it. She hid it to save it. I hid myself to save nothing at all. Forgive us the arithmetic of fear.* Other Sundays I just read vocabulary. *Rain-on-warm-stone. More-than-enough. Stay. Begin.* The dead, I have decided, are not picky audiences. They are patient ones.

Today I read her the roll call of the class. All seven names, plus Priya, plus the three strangers who had emailed after hearing the testimony recording—a man in Ohio whose grandfather had spoken Kavaya'tl in a factory town that no longer exists, a linguistics student who wrote in careful, reverent sentences, a woman who simply said *my mother hummed this and I never knew what it was and I cried on the bus*.

"Nine," I told Tala. "Nine speakers. Maybe ten by summer. The language isn't dying. It's doing what you did—it's hiding in plain sight, in kitchens, in hymns, in a fourth-floor walk-up with no elevator." I set the rosemary down. "You would have hated the stairs. Everyone hates the stairs. They come anyway."

The maple creaked its agreement, or possibly the wind. At Tala's grave, I have learned, you take agreement where you find it.

Then I walked back across the river, past the corner store where Mr. Okonkwo had taped a handwritten sign in Kavaya'tl above the register (*MORE-THAN-ENOUGH, NOW IN TWO LANGUAGES*), up the four flights, past the second floor where the twins' grandfather's tomatoes would soon be getting an earful in a tongue they hadn't heard in forty years, to apartment 4B, where the radiator knocked twice—applause—and I dropped the postcard for my mother into my bag to mail tomorrow, and opened Marisol's notebook to the first empty page.

Because there was one more thing. There is always one more thing; that is what a living language means.

At the top of the page I wrote the date. Beneath it, the new word—the one the class had needed today and hadn't had, the one I'd lain awake constructing the way Marisol had taught me, from roots and rhythm and the old logic of the tongue. It meant *the-sound-of-a-room-filling-with-voices-that-were-told-to-be-quiet*. It was, admittedly, a long word. Marisol would have complained about my vowels and then written it down.

Below the word, I added a line of my own, the way she'd added lines to hers, the ledger growing in both directions now—backward into everything saved, forward into everything not yet said:

*First speaker: Tala, who sang it into hiding.*
*Second speaker: Marisol, who kept count alone.*
*Third speaker: me, who took ten years to say my own name.*
*Fourth through tenth: the Tuesday table, the girl who knew the hymn, the strangers on the bus.*

*To be continued.*

I closed the notebook. Outside, spring was making its case to the courtyard, and somewhere below, Priya was singing to somebody's tomatoes, and the words were rising up the stairwell the way they always had—quietly, stubbornly, in a language that refused, that waited, that began.

The mailbox was on the corner. I had a postcard in my bag with a real address on it and a whole name signed at the bottom, in two languages, neither of them borrowed.

Sunday was tomorrow. I had fifty cards left.

I picked up my keys, and I went down to meet the week.
