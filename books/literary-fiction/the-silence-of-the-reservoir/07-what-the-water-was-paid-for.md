## 7. What the Water Was Paid For

The ledger did not leave the shop that night. I want to say it was caution that kept me there, but the truth is simpler: my legs would not carry me up the stairs to bed. I sat in the back room with the lamp turned low and the ledger open on my knees, and outside the window the reservoir lay flat and black as a held breath.

I had taken the ledger home from St. Brigid's in my tuning case, wedged between felt mutes and rubber wedges where no one looks. Father Ambrose had signed the auction paperwork without glancing at me. *Decommissioned, sold as-is.* The church was closing; the diocese wanted the piano gone by Sunday. Nobody had opened that bench in years, maybe decades. The hinges had screamed like something being born when I lifted them.

Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, was a book bound in gray cardboard—the kind stationers used to sell for keeping accounts of feed and seed. My father's handwriting ran down the first page in columns so neat they looked printed.

*E. Tullis — $400 — signed.*
*M. Okafor — $350 — refused first offer, accepted second.*
*R. Vann — house + $500 — departed quietly.*

And beneath each name, smaller, in the private notation he used for musical shorthand—intervals written as fractions, syllables reduced to their consonant skeletons—a second column I could not read. Not then.

I had spent the evening telling myself this was an account book. A record of payments. Dam companies kept records; valleys were bought the way timber was bought, and someone had to write the numbers down. It was only when I reached the middle pages that the word *paid* began to change its meaning in my hands.

*J. Pruitt — injured at the trestle — settled through counsel — will not speak again.*
*A. Ferro — fell from the bluff road — no inquiry requested.*
*D. Yarrow —*

I stopped reading aloud even inside my own head. My father's own name, in his own hand, dated three days before the water took him. The entry after it was not a payment. It was a single line in the coded notation, longer than any other, running down the margin and continuing on the next page and the next, as if once begun it could not be stopped—as if he had known he was writing against a deadline.

I set the ledger on the workbench and pressed both palms flat against the wood until the trembling stopped. The shop around me ticked and settled. Forty pianos stood in the dark like a congregation waiting to be told what they already suspected.

* * *

Here is what every child in Marrow's Reach learns without ever being taught it: you do not say the valley's name out loud. You say *up the river*, or *the old farms*, or nothing at all. When the state put up the historical marker by the overlook—*INUNDATED 1963 FOR FLOOD CONTROL*—someone paid to have the word *inundated* used instead of *drowned*, though I have never learned who, and I have never needed to learn, because the answer was always the same shape: a tall house on Corvus Hill with its windows facing away from the water.

My mother never spoke of my father's death. She spoke of him constantly—his hands, his ear, the way he could hear a cracked soundboard through a closed door—but never of the last night, never of the water. Once, when I was nine, I asked her why he'd been at the dam site at all, since the company had its own men and the piano tuners of the world had no business on a construction grade at midnight. She looked at me for a long moment with an expression I now recognize, because I have worn it myself for thirty years in the houses of the rich: the practiced smile that ends conversations.

*He went to deliver something,* she said. And then, softer, almost to herself: *Something that couldn't be posted.*

She died still smiling that smile. I inherited it along with her tools.

So I sat in my shop at two in the morning holding her husband's handwriting and understood, finally, what she had known and carried and refused to set down anywhere, even in grief, even in sleep. He hadn't fallen. The word *fell* appears four times in the ledger, and each time it appears, a payment follows it or precedes it, and each time the payment is larger than the one for the families who merely left.

That is the arithmetic of the thing. That is what the water was paid for. Not the land—the land was cheap, stripped farms and stubborn orchards, bought low from people with no leverage. What cost money was the silence afterward. The Harrows didn't buy a valley. They bought the town's consent to forget it, and they got it wholesale, house by house, signature by signature, and where signatures failed—

I turned the page.

* * *

The coded column defeated me until nearly dawn, and then it yielded the way old locks yield—not all at once but in stages, each tumbler falling with a small internal click I felt in my teeth.

His system, I came to see, was built from the same material as his music. He wrote intervals instead of letters: a rising fourth for one class of sounds, a falling minor third for another. Syllables became their opening consonants. Numbers stayed numbers but were written as note-values—a quarter note for ten, a half for twenty—and the whole of it moved left to right like a melody, with rests where he wanted emphasis. He had invented it as a boy, my mother told me once, to pass notes in church. He had grown old enough—or believed himself grown old enough—to use it for this.

The first phrase I decoded took me forty minutes and read:

*They knew the grade would fail.*

Four words. I read them perhaps fifty times. They knew the grade would fail. The cofferdam, the haul road, the trestle where J. Pruitt lost his arm—they knew. Which meant the accidents were not accidents. Which meant the ledger's gentle euphemisms—*settled through counsel, no inquiry requested*—were invoices for things done deliberately, priced and initialed, and the initials at the bottom of each page were not clerks'.

They were H's. Three different H's, in three different hands, but all H's. One of them I recognized without wanting to: the heavy, confident loop of Aldous Harrow, the patriarch whose funeral I had tuned through last month, whose mansion's radiators had sung to me of a grief I had mistaken for ordinary mourning.

I thought of that house. I thought of the way the whole town still bent toward it, gently, like grass toward a fence line. Marcus Harrow was coming home for the estate settlement—I had heard it at the feed store, at the pharmacy, everywhere news travels in a town too small for secrets and too polite for speech. The estate would pass to him entire: the hill, the orchard land above the waterline, the timber rights, the silent majority interest in the Marrow's Reach Savings & Loan where half the town's mortgages lived.

The Harrows still owned the town. They owned it more thoroughly than they owned the drowned valley beneath it, because a valley stays bought only as long as no one asks the price, and here in my hands was the price, itemized, in my father's hand.

Fury is a strange thing in a man who has practiced invisibility for thirty years. It has nowhere to go. It fills the body like floodwater filling a basement, rising over the steps one by one—the furnace, the shelves, the photographs—until there is nothing left above the surface but the roof, and the roof is a smile.

I made tea I did not drink. I walked the length of the shop twice. Outside, the sky went from black to the color of wet slate, and the reservoir appeared at the edge of the lot, flat, patient, holding everything it had been given.

* * *

Toward morning I went back to the long marginal sentence—the one that began under my father's own name and ran on for pages, growing more compressed as it went, as if the hand writing it had been hurrying, or shaking, or both.

The opening decoded slowly, phrase by phrase, and each phrase cost me something.

*If I am drowned it was Aldous Harrow's order.*

I sat back. The lamp hummed. Somewhere in the walls of the shop a pipe knocked twice and went quiet, the way pipes do, the way houses do when they have said all they intend to say.

*If I am drowned it was Aldous Harrow's order, given through his brother Emmett, who pays the sheriff.*

Emmett. Not Aldous alone. A brother I had heard of the way you hear of weather in another county—dead these thirty years, buried on Corvus Hill beside the parents, his stone as clean and honored as any saint's. The brother who handled the valley purchases, the town said, back when the town said anything at all. The brother everyone remembered as the reasonable one, the soft-spoken one, the one who shook hands at the grange hall.

*Who pays the sheriff.* Sheriff Dann Vann—R. Vann's cousin, if the ledger's earlier entries were honest, and I no longer had any reason to doubt them. Vann had signed the drowning report. *Misadventure. No evidence of foul play.* I had been fourteen. I had stood in his office while he explained the water and the dark and the slippery grade, and he had put his hand on my shoulder, and my mother had thanked him.

We had thanked him.

I copied the decoded phrases onto a separate sheet in my own plain hand, because some part of me already understood that the ledger might not stay mine forever, and then I stopped, pen hovering, at the next cluster of symbols. This one resisted. The intervals were denser, the shorthand compressed past sense, and near its end the notation changed character entirely—it stopped behaving like words and started behaving like music. Actual pitches. A melodic figure I almost recognized, transposed, inverted, threaded through the cipher like wire through cloth.

I played it out silently on the workbench with my fingers, the way my father taught me to read scores before I could read sentences. Four notes rising. A rest. Then the figure again, a third lower.

It was the song. Or the bones of it—the melody the radio had played to me on the drive home from Corvus Hill, the melody no one had ever written down, the melody my father composed the night before the water closed over him. He had hidden it here, inside the accusation, the way he hid truth inside jokes at supper, the way this whole town hid its history inside its manners.

He had written his confession into a song and his song into a code, and he had done it in the hours between signing his last entry and walking down to the dam site to deliver something that couldn't be posted.

* * *

Daylight came gray and thin. I washed my face, put on my good coat, and drove the reservoir road with the ledger locked in the trunk and the radio off, because I could not bear anything in the air just then but the sound of my own tires.

At the overlook I stopped. Below, the water lay over the valley the way a sheet lies over furniture in a closed house—shaped to everything beneath it, concealing nothing, revealing nothing. Somewhere under that surface was the church where my father had played, the school, the Tullis farm, the road he had walked at midnight with whatever he was carrying. The state would tell you it was ninety feet deep at the center. The state would tell you many things.

A truck pulled in behind me. A man got out, coffee thermos in hand, and stood at the rail looking at the water the way men do—without seeing it. He nodded to me. I nodded back. We stood there a moment, two citizens of Marrow's Reach, practicing our smiles at the view.

Then he said, conversationally, to the water rather than to me:

"They're saying drawdown inspection come spring. Lower the pool six feet, maybe more." He drank his coffee. "First time since '63. Be strange, seeing the rooftops again."

He got back in his truck and left, and I stood at the rail with my heart going hard, understanding that the clock I had not wound had started anyway. Six feet of drawn-down water. Old foundations surfacing. Boats where cornfields used to be. And somewhere in the Harrow estate offices, I would have bet the shop, paper was already moving toward shredders and burn barrels, because families like that do not wait for inspections to tidy their history.

I drove back into town past the savings & loan, past the diner where the waitress was refilling cups and not looking at anyone, past the courthouse where Sheriff Vann's report still lived in a filing cabinet like a seed in frozen ground. At the corner of Main and Church, a boy was delivering papers, and he was humming.

I rolled down the window to listen.

He was humming my father's song. Badly, half-remembered, the way children hum things they've caught from the air—from a radio, from a parent, from somewhere. It was loose and wrong and it went through me like a blade through felt.

Someone was teaching it to them. Someone had put it on a tape and mailed it into the dark, and it was spreading through the town the way water spreads through dry ground, finding every crack.

I gripped the wheel and drove home to finish the translation, because whatever was coming—drawdown, heir, reckoning—I intended to know, before it arrived, exactly what my father had died carrying, and I intended to say it out loud in a voice no one would mistake for a smile.
