## 1. The Smile That Ends Conversations

The mop bucket had a squeak in its left wheel, and Emrys Blackwood had decided by the second day that she preferred it that way. A silent bucket would have been an invitation to thought, and thought was a country she was trying not to visit this week. The squeak gave her something small and mechanical to hate, and hatred of a wheel was so much easier than hatred of anything larger.

She pushed it down the east corridor of the Meridian Conservatory at six in the morning, when the building belonged to the cleaners and the ghosts. Light came through the tall windows in long amber panels, catching the dust she hadn't reached yet, illuminating her failures in real time. Somewhere below, the boilers ticked. Somewhere above, a practice room door opened and closed — some student who couldn't sleep, couldn't stop, couldn't do anything but play. She recognized the compulsion the way you recognize your own handwriting.

Her name tag said EMBRY. She had chosen it herself from the temp agency's list of available shifts, a name close enough to hers that she wouldn't fail to answer when called and far enough that no one would say it and flinch. Embry. It sounded like a person who had never stood on a stage in Vienna. It sounded like someone whose hands were for bleach and microfiber cloths, and that was true now, and she was trying to make it true all the way down.

Twenty years ago she had walked these corridors the way royalty walks — slowly, so people could look. Now she walked them quickly, eyes down, so they couldn't. She had learned the geometry of invisibility the way she'd once learned Chopin: with discipline, with repetition, until it lived in the fingers.

* * *

The first one to almost-recognize her was Marcus Lehn, second chair cello, who had been a first-year violinist back when she was the conservatory's bright particular star. He came around the corner near the faculty lounge at seven-fifteen, coffee in hand, and his eyes did the thing she had come to think of as the flicker — the half-second where recognition arrives and is then, with visible effort, escorted out of the building.

"Morning," he said, to her shoulder.

"Morning," she said, to his coffee.

He passed. She counted under her breath — one Mississippi, two Mississippi — and heard him stop ten feet away, hear the hesitation in his footsteps like a skipped beat, and then continue on without looking back. She smiled at the floor tiles. It was the smile she'd perfected over two decades, the one that ended conversations before they began, the one that said *you are mistaken, and I will not confirm what you saw*. Not cold, exactly. Cold invited challenge. This smile was more like frosted glass: you could see there was a person behind it, but not clearly enough to be sure, and no one knocked on frosted glass.

There had been a time — she let herself remember exactly one minute of it while she wrung out the mop, because total abstinence only made the wanting worse — when Professor Anselm Voss had introduced her to a visiting delegation from the Berlin Philharmonic's youth program. *This*, he had said, hand warm on her shoulder, *is the finest pianist this conservatory has produced in fifty years.* She had been seventeen. Her hair had been pinned up with her mother's old pearl combs, and her hands had smelled faintly of the lemon oil her father insisted she rub into them every night, as if talent were a piece of furniture that needed maintaining.

*Play something for them, Emrys.*

And she had. Of course she had. She always played when asked; that was the whole architecture of her childhood. She had played the opening of the Blackwood Sonata No. 2 — *her* sonata, everyone said, though even then the word had sat strangely in her mouth, like a coin she suspected was counterfeit but spent anyway because spending it kept the lights on — and the delegates had leaned forward in their chairs, and Voss had glowed like a lamp, and her father had stood at the back of the room with his arms folded, wearing the expression he wore instead of pride, which looked almost identical to disappointment but paid better.

She rinsed the mop. The memory went back into its drawer. She was getting faster at closing it; that was the only technique twenty years had improved.

* * *

The Meridian Conservatory occupied a converted textile mill on the river side of Halloway Street, all brick and iron and windows built for people watching looms, repurposed for people watching teachers. Emrys had been assigned the east wing: rehearsal halls, the small recital room, the corridor of practice rooms with their famous doors — each one triple-layered for sound, each one bearing a brass number and, beneath the numbers, the ghost-scars of tape where students had once stuck up photos, quotes, threats to themselves (*PRACTICE OR PERISH*, someone had written at fifteen, and meant it).

She cleaned Room 7 on Wednesday morning and stood in its doorway longer than the job required.

Room 7 had been hers. Not officially — rooms weren't assigned by seniority until graduate school — but de facto, the way certain pews become certain families' pews. She had practiced here four hours a day from fourteen to nineteen. She knew the exact spot on the floor where the boards sang if you rocked your weight wrong during a pedal change. She knew which radiator valve hissed in January. She knew that if you pressed your ear to the wall between 7 and 8, you could hear the other pianist's left hand, muffled, like a heartbeat through a coat.

The piano inside was new, of course. A Steinway B, well-maintained, its fallboard closed. Someone's sticker was peeling off the music desk — a cartoon frog, the kind conservatory students affix to everything as if to prove they are still, despite all evidence, having fun.

Emrys set down her supplies, took out her cloth, and wiped the piano's lid with the thoroughness of ritual. Under the dust her cloth found the wood grain, and under the wood grain — nothing. Pianos don't remember. That was the mercy of them and the cruelty. Every note she had ever played in this room had gone into the air and died there, and the instrument held no record, and neither did the room, and the only archive that existed was inside her body, in the hands, in the reflexes, and those were hers alone, unsellable, unseizable, useless.

She caught herself flexing her fingers above the keys. Not touching. Hovering. The distance between her fingertips and the ivory was maybe two inches, and it might as well have been the distance between the living and the dead.

She pulled her hand back, wiped the sticker residue off the music desk, and moved on.

Invisibility, she had learned, was not the absence of being seen. Anyone could hide. Invisibility was a craft, and like all crafts it had fundamentals. Rule one: never move quickly. Quick movement draws the eye. Rule two: carry something. A person carrying a mop is furniture; a person carrying nothing is a question. Rule three — and this was the hard one, the one that took years — never defend yourself. The moment you explain, you exist. The moment you exist, you can be discussed. Let them think what they'll think. Let the flicker cross their faces and pass. Let them go to lunch and say, *you'll never guess who I think I saw pushing a mop*, and let the other person say, *no, that can't be right, she teaches in Zurich now, doesn't she? Or Prague,* and let the uncertainty do your work for you. Rumor needs confirmation the way fire needs oxygen. Starve it.

She had gotten very good at starving things.

* * *

Thursday brought the students back in force, and with them the hardest part of the week: the ones who had been children when she fell, and the ones who hadn't been born.

A cluster of them passed her in the corridor outside Hall C — four kids with instrument cases slung like weapons, arguing about a conductor — and one of them, a cellist with a red scarf, glanced at her name tag and then at her face and frowned slightly, the frown of someone assembling a puzzle from a box missing most of its pieces.

"You look familiar," the girl said.

"I have one of those faces," Emrys said, and smiled the smile.

It worked. It always worked. The girl shrugged and moved on, already forgetting, because seventeen-year-olds are merciful without knowing it: they cannot hold onto other people's histories; their own take up all the room.

But Emrys stood in the corridor a moment after they'd gone, listening to the building fill with sound. Scales ascending somewhere above her, endless and patient. A clarinet murdering the same phrase over and over with the dogged optimism of the untalented-but-persistent. A voice teacher's distant *support, support, sing from the floor*. The conservatory made noise the way a body makes noise when it's alive — constantly, redundantly, without shame.

She had loved this building once with the whole stupid violence of the young. She had loved it the way you love anything that tells you who you are. And when it turned on her — when the story broke, when the photographs surfaced, when the word *fraud* began appearing in print attached to her name with the casual permanence of a middle initial — she had learned that buildings love you back only conditionally, and the condition is usefulness, and the moment you stop being useful you become a problem, and problems get carried out.

They had carried her out. Not physically. Physically she had walked, spine straight, past the cameras on the front steps, wearing the gray coat her sister Maren had bought her for the occasion because Maren understood that ruin is a costume drama and you must dress for the role. Dorian had driven. Nobody had spoken in the car. Her father had not come to the car at all; he had sent a message through Maren, three words long, and Emrys had never repeated those words aloud to anyone, not in twenty years, not to therapists, not to the night, not to the mirror. Some sentences you bury so deep that eventually you can almost believe you never heard them, and the almost is the only mercy available.

The scandal itself — the newspapers had needed only a week to dismantle what her father had taken fifteen years to build. There had been questions about the sonatas. About provenance. About a name nobody in the family would discuss, a name that vanished from programs and letters and conversation as thoroughly as if it had been erased with solvent. Emrys had been twenty-four. She had known less than the journalists did, which was the cruelest discovery of that season: that she had been the instrument, and instruments are never told what they're playing.

She scrubbed a scuff mark off the baseboard with a patience that surprised her. The scuff surrendered. Most things surrender to patience. It was the things that didn't — the locked doors, the sealed files, the silence of her siblings — that had taught her the limits of the virtue.

* * *

Friday evening, the shift supervisor — a broad, unhurried man named Rufus Okafor with gray in his beard and forty years of the building in his knees — walked the east wing with her at the end of her shift, checking her work with a flashlight beam rather than criticism.

"You missed the sills in Hall C," he said, not unkindly.

"I'll get them."

"Mm." He swept the light along the windowsill, where dust lay in a fine gray tide line. "You work quiet. Most of the new ones chatter. Nervous, mostly. You're not nervous."

"No."

He considered her in the dark, the flashlight lowered now, his face half-lit and unreadable. He had the particular stillness of men who have watched institutions for decades and learned that everything interesting happens in the margins. "Been in this building thirty-one years," he said. "I've seen everybody come through. Students, maestros, trustees, thieves. Faces change. Buildings don't." He clicked the flashlight off. "Sills tomorrow, then."

"Yes, sir."

"And Embry." He said the false name without irony, without emphasis, handing it back to her like a coat check ticket. "Break room microwave's on the fritz. Use the one in the boiler room. It's mine, but it works."

That was all. He shuffled off toward the loading dock, and Emrys stood in the darkened hall with the strange sensation of having been seen completely and assessed as harmless — or perhaps not harmless, but *herself*, whatever that was worth now. It was the first time in a week, possibly in a year, possibly in two decades, that being seen hadn't felt like standing trial.

* * *

Saturday she stayed late. Not from diligence — the sills were done by nine — but because the boiler room microwave required a walk through the basement corridors, and the basement corridors at night were the closest thing the conservatory had to honesty. Down here the marble gave way to concrete, the portraits gave way to pipes, and the institution's beautiful lie about itself — all that gilded dedication to Art with a capital letter — thinned out until you could see the machinery underneath. Steam, voltage, water pressure. Things that actually kept the building alive, none of which had ever been photographed for a brochure.

Rufus was there, and so were two others: Tomás Reyes from security, doing paperwork at a card table, and a thin, quick-eyed woman named Priya who worked in the archives and ate her dinner at eight-thirty every night with the regularity of a tide. They made room for her without ceremony. Tomás slid the hot sauce across the table before she'd asked for it, reading some need off her face. Priya asked her exactly one question — "East wing?" — nodded at the answer as if it explained everything, and returned to her book.

Nobody asked where she'd worked before. Nobody asked about the flicker, the smile, the name tag. They talked about a leaking roof, a trustee's terrible taste in wine, the centennial gala coming in the spring and the mountain of extra work it would dump on all of them. Ordinary talk. Load-bearing talk. Emrys sat among them with her plastic container of rice and beans and felt something in her chest unlock by a single degree, like a door that had rusted shut beginning, very slowly, to give.

This, she thought, walking home at midnight through streets glossy with rain. This is the whole of it now. A bucket with a squeaky wheel. A microwave in a boiler room. Hands that clean instead of play.

It was enough. She had made it enough, the way you make a meal from scraps, and she had even taught herself to taste it.

Her phone buzzed as she unlocked her apartment door. Maren. Emrys stared at the screen for a long moment — Maren called twice a year, Christmas and crisis, and it was October — before answering.

"It's Dad," Maren said, without hello. Her voice had the flat polish she used for delivering verdicts. "He died last night. The funeral's Thursday. Dorian's handling the arrangements." A pause, calibrated. "You should come, Embry. Family should be there."

Emrys stood in her dark kitchen with the phone against her ear and the rain ticking at the window, and heard, underneath her sister's composure, the thing Maren could never quite conceal: fear.

"I'll be there," Emrys said.

She hung up and did not sleep. Outside, the city went on making its noise, and somewhere across town her father lay in the stillness he had finally earned, and Emrys sat at her kitchen table with her hands open in her lap — the hands, the ruined, faithful, extraordinary hands — waiting for Thursday the way you wait for a storm you can feel in your teeth.

She didn't know yet that someone would be waiting at the funeral too. Someone with another family's name in her mouth, spoken like a key.

But her teeth ached all the same.
