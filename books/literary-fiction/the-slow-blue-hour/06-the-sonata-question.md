## 6. The Sonata Question

The manuscript came home with her the way stolen things do — folded against her body, warm from the heat of her own skin, as if it had already become part of her.

Emrys hadn't planned to take it. That was what she told herself, mopping the corridor outside Room 114 at two in the morning three nights later, that she hadn't *planned* anything, that the pages had simply been there when the lock gave, and a person could hardly be blamed for what a broken lock put in her hands. But she knew the shape of a lie when she held one. She had spent twenty years polishing them for other people. The truth was simpler and worse: she had taken the manuscript because her hands had wanted it, and her hands had been wanting things all week that her mind refused to name.

She lived now in a rented room above a laundromat on Delancey Street, a space barely wider than the upright piano the previous tenant had abandoned and she had refused to sell. The piano was out of tune in six places and perfect in one — the middle register, where most of life's honest music happened anyway. She set the manuscript on the music stand at four in the morning, after her shift, after the shower that never quite removed the smell of industrial lemon, and she turned on the single lamp with its crooked shade, and she looked.

The paper was old. Not ancient — not the vellum-and-foxing antiquity of museum pieces — but old the way a person is old at forty: still strong, still itself, but carrying weather. Hand-ruled staves, slightly uneven, the work of someone who ruled his own paper rather than buying it pre-printed. The ink had gone brown at the edges of every stroke, the way iron-gall ink does, drinking oxygen for decades. And the hand —

She knew handwriting the way other people knew faces. Twenty years of editing her father's correspondence, of filing his sketches, of being handed pages with the expectation that she would play them sight-unseen and make them beautiful. Aldous Blackwood wrote music the way he did everything else: with pressure, with certainty, each note-head stabbed rather than placed, beams like fence rails, dynamics marked in a slanting fist that seemed to lean over the page and dare you to disagree. *Con forza*, always. Her father had never once written *dolce* without immediately qualifying it.

This was not that hand.

This was smaller. More patient. The note-heads were round, almost tender, and the slurs curved like something drawn in a single breath. Where Aldous's dynamics shouted, these *asked*. There was a marking above the opening phrase of the second movement that Emrys read three times before she understood why it made her chest hurt: *come una voce che ricorda* — like a voice remembering. Her father would sooner have died than write *remembering*. Remembering implied something behind you. Something lost.

She played the first eight bars.

Her fingers found the notes before her mind finished reading them, and that was the first wrongness, the first hairline crack: the passage felt *familiar*. Not familiar the way a piece you've studied is familiar. Familiar the way a room you dreamed about is familiar — you know where the door is, you know the floor will be cold, and you cannot say how. Her left hand crossed over her right at a place no sane editor would mark a crossing, and it felt like coming home through a window instead of a door.

She stopped. Sat very still. The laundromat below had gone quiet; even the dryers had finished their long tumbling complaints. In the silence she could hear the building's pipes ticking, and under that, fainter, the sound of her own pulse, and under *that* — she would have sworn to this until the day she died — the memory of an applause that had happened twenty-five years ago in a hall she had cleaned three nights running without once looking up at the stage.

She turned back to the first page and began, deliberately, coldly, to compare.

---

The Blackwood Sonatas. Three of them, published between 1998 and 2001, collected in a single volume by Halvorsen & Rhee with a gold-stamped spine and an introduction by a musicologist who had since won a chair at Oxford on the strength of his analysis of their "startling interior coherence." Emrys had grown up inside those sonatas the way a snail grows inside its shell — they had been shaped around her, or she around them, and neither of them could be separated without breaking something. Sonata No. 1 in E minor had been her debut piece at fifteen. Sonata No. 2 had carried her through Vienna, through Salzburg, through the season the critics started calling her "the Blackwood voice" as if a person could be a vehicle for a style rather than a girl with hands. Sonata No. 3 had never been performed publicly. It had been withdrawn — her father's decision, announced at breakfast, unexplained — six weeks before the scandal.

She owned one copy of the collected volume. She had kept it through everything: the bankruptcy of her reputation, the years of hotel pianos and cruise ships and teaching positions that evaporated, the slow slide from concert halls to churches to community centers to, finally, this room above a laundromat. She had told herself she kept it because a musician does not burn books. But some part of her had known, even then, that you keep the thing that ruined you close, the way you keep a scar covered but touched.

She fetched it now from the bottom drawer of the dresser, from under the sweaters, wrapped in a plastic bag against damp. Set it beside the manuscript. Opened both to their first movements.

And then she did what she had been trained to do since she was six years old, the only discipline her father had ever given her that was pure: she looked. Really looked. Note against note, bar against bar, the way a jeweler looks at a stone against a known forgery.

The melody of the practice-room manuscript's opening movement was not identical to Blackwood Sonata No. 1. That was the terrible elegance of it. It was *related* — the same harmonic skeleton, the same rising fourth that opened No. 1's first theme, but here the fourth rose and then fell back, hesitated, asked its question twice before answering. Where the published sonata strode, this wandered. Where the published sonata resolved its second phrase with a bright Picardy third — the flourish every critic had praised as "characteristically assertive" — this version sat unresolved for two full bars, hovering, and then slipped sideways into a key the published version never visited at all.

It was, she thought, her hands shaking so badly she had to press them flat against her knees, it was the same house. But this was the architect's drawing, and the published sonata was the house after someone else had renovated it — knocked out walls, painted over the windows, added a façade.

Or — and the thought arrived with the gentleness of a knife — this was the house, and the published sonata was the photograph of the house that the family put on its Christmas card.

She worked until dawn. She was trained for this too, though she'd never thought of it as training: a lifetime of practicing past exhaustion teaches you to keep working when your body wants to stop, and a lifetime of secrets teaches you to keep working when your soul does. Phrase by phrase, movement by movement, she laid the manuscript against the published score and found the relationship everywhere. Not plagiarism in the crude sense — not note-for-note theft, which any conservatory sophomore could prove in an afternoon. Something subtler and more damning. The published sonatas were *arrangements*. Compressions. Someone had taken a larger, stranger, more grieving work and edited it into brilliance — cut the hesitations, sharpened the resolutions, sanded down every place where the original hand had written *dolce* and meant it.

Someone had taken a voice that remembered and taught it to declare.

* * *

It was nearly seven when she found the fingering marks.

She had catalogued them without thinking, the way you catalogue wallpaper — the small penciled numbers above certain passages, 1s and 2s and 4s, a thumb-mark here, a substitution there. Editorial fingerings. Every published edition has them; every working copy accumulates them. She had assumed they were the manuscript's own, the composer's suggestions to some future player.

Then she reached the passage in the second movement — the one marked *come una voce che ricorda* — bars 34 through 41, the descending sequence where the right hand must decide, as every pianist eventually must, whether to take the turn with the third finger and risk smudging the connection, or cross under with the fourth and preserve the legato at the cost of the phrase's natural weight.

Above the fourth bar of that sequence, in pencil, faded almost to nothing, was a tiny numeral: **4**. And beneath it, smaller, pressed so lightly the pencil had barely kissed the paper: **3–4**, with a small circle around the whole figure.

Emrys stared at it.

She knew that circle. She had drawn that circle. At fourteen years old, preparing for her debut, working through the second movement of the Blackwood First Sonata with her father standing over her like weather, she had struggled with exactly this passage — because it was *the same passage*, transposed up a minor third, revoiced, but the same bones, the same descending sequence, the same impossible choice of fingers. And fourteen-year-old Emrys, who was afraid of her father but not yet afraid of the music, had worked out her own solution: cross under with the fourth, take the turn with a 3–4 substitution, and mark it — she had marked everything in those days, little circles around her decisions, little flags planted in conquered territory — with a small circle around the fingering, so that her father would see she had decided, that this was hers, that some part of the music belonged to her even if none of the rest of her did.

He had slapped the pencil out of her hand when he saw it. *You don't annotate a master's work,* he said. *You serve it.* And she had never circled a fingering again.

But she had circled this one. On this page. In pencil that had faded for twenty-five years.

On pages she had never seen.

The room tilted. Not metaphorically — she actually gripped the edge of the piano, actually waited for the vertigo to pass the way you wait out a wave, because the alternative was to fall off the bench in a rented room above a laundromat at seven in the morning with a stolen manuscript in her lap. The lamp light seemed to come from very far away. Somewhere below, the laundromat's first dryer of the day shuddered into motion, and the vibration came up through the floorboards like a heartbeat that belonged to someone else.

Because here was the arithmetic, and it admitted no mercy: the fingering marks on the manuscript matched, note for note, substitution for substitution, circle for circle, the fingerings in her own working copy of the published sonatas — the copy she had used at fourteen, the copy still sitting open on her piano bench, annotated in a teenager's careful hand. Whoever had prepared the published edition had transferred *her* fingerings onto this manuscript — or had taken the manuscript's fingerings and put them into her edition. Either way, the two documents had been in the same room. Had passed through the same hands. Had been *worked on together*, by someone who played the passages the way she played them, who chose the fourth-finger crossing she chose, who circled things the way she had circled things for exactly one year of her life before her father forbade it.

Her own fingerings. Her own fourteen-year-old solutions, preserved in pencil on a manuscript she had never seen, in a hand that was not her father's, for a piece the world knew as her father's work.

Which meant the order of events was not the order she had believed. Which meant the sonata had existed — fully, completely, in this patient round-handed script — before her debut. Before her fourteenth year. Before, possibly, she had ever touched it. And someone had sat with this manuscript and her and the published proofs and had copied *her* markings backward into the source, or forward into the edition, stitching the two together so thoroughly that no one — not the Oxford musicologist, not the critics, not the girl herself — had ever noticed the seam.

Except the seam was her own handwriting. Her own child's hand, fossilized in someone else's document.

She thought: *I am the proof.* Not letters, not testimony, not witnesses. Her. Her fingerings. The scandal had taken her career, her name, her place on every stage in the Western world — and all along, folded into the music itself, was the evidence that the music had never been her father's, that it had passed through her fourteen-year-old hands on its way to becoming famous, wearing her fingerprints like a thief wearing a stolen coat.

She laughed. It came out wrong, a single dry syllable with nothing behind it, and she pressed her knuckles to her mouth.

*Come una voce che ricorda.* Like a voice remembering. She played it again — she had to, her hands insisted — and this time she heard what the marking meant. The movement wasn't remembering a theme. It was remembering a *person*. The lodger. Teodor Ilić, the gentle man in the garden flat who had tuned the piano sometimes, who had smelled of pipe smoke and cheap tobacco and had once, when she was eleven, stood in the doorway listening to her practice and said, *You play like someone apologizing. One day you will play like someone arriving.* The only adult besides her mother who had ever spoken to her about music as if music were a place she might go rather than a debt she owed.

He had written this. He had written all of it — the hesitations, the unresolved bars, the voice that remembers — and her father had taken it and sharpened it and signed it, and then the world had handed it to *her* to perform, so that the daughter's fame became the forgery's alibi. Who would question the source when the source produced such a player? She had been, all along, the living certificate of authenticity. The Blackwood voice. She had carried the stolen goods across every border in Europe, and the customs officers had applauded.

The light through the curtainless window had gone from grey to white to the flat gold of morning. She should sleep. She had a shift at nine; Rufus would notice if she came in hollow-eyed, and Rufus noticed everything, which she was beginning to understand was a form of kindness.

But she sat at the piano a while longer, the manuscript open on the stand, her working copy open beside it, two documents lying together like estranged sisters forced into the same photograph. And slowly, with the particular dread of a person testing ice, she let herself think the thought underneath all the other thoughts:

*If the First Sonata was his — what about the Second? What about the Third?*

The Third. Withdrawn six weeks before the scandal. Never performed. The sonata her father had locked away and refused, to the end of his life, to publish or destroy — the one work in the Blackwood catalogue that existed, as far as she knew, only in the family archive Dorian kept sealed in the house on Wexford Terrace.

The Third Sonata had always frightened her. Even the rumor of it. Maren went silent when it was mentioned; Dorian changed the subject with the smoothness of long practice. And she had assumed — she had needed to assume — that it was simply unfinished, a failure, the reason her father's late period was a blank.

But she no longer trusted her assumptions. They had been built, all of them, on a foundation she had just watched crack.

She looked at the last page of the practice-room manuscript. Beneath the final double bar, in that small patient hand, was a date: *March 1999.* Two months before Teodor Ilić died. And beneath the date, half-erased, as if the writer had begun to sign and stopped himself:

*T. I—*

The dash trailed off the edge of the ink stroke like a road ending at a cliff.

Emrys closed the manuscript. Closed the published volume. Sat in the morning light with her hands flat on her knees, feeling the dryer-vibration come up through the floor, and understood that she could not put this back. Whatever she did next — return the pages, burn them, bury them under twenty more years of silence — she had already seen the seam. You cannot unknow your own handwriting.

Outside, Delancey Street was waking up: the bakery's shutter rattling up, a bus sighing at its stop, the ordinary machinery of a city that had no idea. She had a shift at nine. She had floors to mop, including the corridor outside Room 114, where a lock hung broken on a door that was supposed to be sealed, and someone — Vera Ilić, the woman with her father's eyes and her patience — was surely waiting to learn what Emrys had found.

Or perhaps waiting to learn whether Emrys would tell.

She dressed for work in the dark, and the whole time, her hands kept moving through the fourth-finger crossing, silently, over and over, the way they do when they are the only part of you that has already decided.
