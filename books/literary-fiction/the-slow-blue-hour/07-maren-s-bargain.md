## 7. Maren's Bargain

The boardroom of the Halloran Foundation smelled of lemon polish and old money, which was to say it smelled of Maren's own decisions, layered year over year like varnish. She had chosen the scent herself, eleven years ago, when she'd taken the chairmanship and replaced the previous director's potpourri with something cleaner. Lies, her mother used to say, should at least be tidy.

She stood at the long window overlooking Meridian Square while her assistant set out coffee — real cups, porcelain, not paper; the difference mattered to men like Gerald Voss — and watched October strip the plane trees along the square. Below, a woman in a gray uniform pushed a mop bucket across the flagstones toward the conservatory's service entrance. Maren watched her for longer than she meant to. The woman moved with a peculiar economy, no gesture wasted, as though invisibility were a discipline she'd studied. Emrys had once played Chopin that way, every note inevitable, nothing left over.

"Maren." Gerald's voice behind her, warm and damp as a handshake. "You look wonderful. Terrible business about your father."

"Thank you for coming on short notice."

"For you? Always." He took his coffee and settled into the chair at the head of the table — *her* chair, she noted, letting him have it. Men like Gerald needed to feel they were sitting somewhere important. It cost nothing to let them.

Her assistant withdrew. The door clicked shut, and the room became what private rooms become: a place where sentences could finally be finished.

"I'll be direct," Maren said, taking the seat beside him rather than across, angling herself into the posture of an ally. "The centennial gala is in six weeks. The foundation has committed two hundred thousand dollars, and the board of trustees has been told the tribute will honor our father's legacy as composer and benefactor."

"And?"

"And I've heard things, Gerald. Small things. A question asked by a researcher in the archives. An accession record pulled from 1999. Someone requested the original manuscripts under Teodor Ilić's name." She let the name sit in the air between them like a struck bell, watching his face do the small, controlled thing it did — the flicker at the corner of the eye, quickly ironed flat. He knew the name. Of course he knew the name. Everyone who mattered had always known the name; that was the whole architecture of the thing. The lie didn't work because people believed it. It worked because people agreed to stop looking at it.

"That's archive housekeeping," Gerald said. "Old records get audited before a centennial. It means nothing."

"Probably," Maren agreed pleasantly. "But my sister works nights there now."

Gerald's cup paused halfway to his mouth. He set it down with exaggerated care, the way men handle things when they're buying time. "Emrys. Yes. I heard something about that. Custodial staff?" His tone performed delicacy without achieving it.

"She wanted to come home," Maren said simply. It was even true, in the way most useful sentences are true. "Whatever else happened twenty years ago, this building was hers before it was anyone's. I don't pretend to understand all her reasons. But I know my sister, and I know what it looks like when she starts asking questions. She asked them once before, when she was fourteen, and our father locked the practice room and told her some music wasn't ready for her yet. She stopped asking then. She's not fourteen anymore."

Gerald turned his cup slowly on its saucer. Outside, a gust took half the yellow leaves off one plane tree in a single motion, a small bright detonation against the glass.

"What is it you want from me, Maren?"

"The same thing you want. Quietly." She opened the leather folder she'd brought — thin, deliberately thin; thick folders frightened men like Gerald into imagining conspiracies — and slid a single page across the table. "A revised program note for the gala. Shorter. Less biographical detail around the composition dates of the Blackwood Sonatas. 'Composed over several years in close collaboration with colleagues' — something like that. Vague enough to survive scrutiny, specific enough to sound scholarly. Your archivist signs off, your historian signs off, and nobody pulls threads because there's nothing loose to pull."

Gerald read it twice. She watched him weigh it, and she watched something else move underneath the weighing — relief, mostly, but also appetite. That was the part she'd learned to expect and still hadn't learned to stomach: how much powerful men enjoyed being handed their complicity in writing.

"And in exchange," he said slowly, "the foundation's pledge stands. And perhaps grows. The board has been discussing a named hall—"

"The Aldous Blackwood Recital Hall," Maren said. "Yes. Two hundred fifty thousand, announced at the gala. Provided the program notes go out as drafted and the archive access logs stay unremarked upon."

"You're asking me to help bury something."

"I'm asking you to help curate something." She kept her voice level, almost gentle. This was the sentence she'd rehearsed in the car, in the shower, in the sleepless geometry of three a.m., and it came out now smooth as poured cream. "Every institution curates, Gerald. You don't display every canvas in storage. You hang what serves the work. My father was difficult — cruel, if we're being honest, and I'm the only member of this family who'll say so aloud — but the sonatas are real. Whatever their provenance, they have taught four generations of pianists what the piano can do. Children study them in conservatories on three continents. Are you proposing we tell those children their fingers learned nothing true?"

It was her best argument, and it was also, she knew, a lie — or not a lie exactly, but a load-bearing wall painted to look like a window. She'd spent eleven years inside this particular house and she knew its structure intimately: which truths held up the roof, which ones you could knock through for light. The sonatas were magnificent. That was true. Their magnificence did not launder their origin. That was also true, and she kept it in the basement where the foundation kept everything else.

Gerald was quiet for a moment. When he spoke again, the transactional warmth had returned, the deal essentially closed, both of them pretending otherwise. "There's one complication you should know about. Since we're being candid."

Maren felt the room tilt a degree, the way rooms do just before bad news arrives. "Go on."

"A woman has been making inquiries. Not through channels — directly. Letters to the board, a visit last month asking about deceased composers' estates, accession records from the late nineties. She gave a name." He hesitated, savoring or dreading it. "Ilić. Vera Ilić."

The name went through Maren like cold water through cloth. She thought of the funeral — three weeks ago now, the rain, the too-small gathering — and the composed stranger with the foreign vowels who had greeted the family as *the Hallorans*, the old name, the buried name, spoken aloud over the grave dirt as casually as a comment on the weather. Dorian had gone the color of candle wax. Maren herself had ushered the woman out with the flawless hospitality of someone drowning a guest politely. She had assumed grief had sent her a crank, a conspiracy-minded stranger who'd read something online. She had not slept well for a week afterward anyway, and had told herself it was the funeral.

"She claims to be his daughter," Gerald said. "Teodor's. There's no estate record, no next of kin listed anywhere. As far as the law is concerned, the man was nobody. But she has letters, she says. Family correspondence. And she's talking about the centennial. About attending."

Maren looked down at her hands. They were her mother's hands, broad-knuckled, capable; they had signed ten thousand checks and folded themselves calmly in lapels during every crisis of the family's public life. They were perfectly steady now, and she hated them a little for it.

"What does she want?"

"Recognition, presumably. Money, eventually. They always want money eventually." Gerald said it with the breezy cynicism of a man who had never been nobody. "I can have the firm look into her. Quietly. Establish whether she's credible, whether there's any documentation that could surface badly. Contain it."

"No." The word left Maren faster than she intended, sharper. She softened it immediately, the way you catch a door before it slams. "No lawyers. Lawyers make things real. If she's ignored, she stays a crank. If we send counsel, we've confirmed there's something to counsel about." She heard her father in her own voice and made herself stop, breathe, redirect. "Leave her to me. Family matters stay in the family. That's how we've survived everything else."

Gerald studied her with new interest, the interest of a man revising an estimate upward. "You've handled this kind of thing before."

"Our whole life has been this kind of thing," Maren said, and smiled — the family smile, the one that ended conversations, the one she'd inherited fair and square along with the foundation and the guilt. "Draft the program note. Announce the hall. Leave the rest to me."

* * *

He left. She stayed.

This was her ritual, after hard meetings: the empty boardroom, the city going amber outside, the porcelain cups cooling on the table like small abandoned lives. Her assistant would clear them later and never wonder why the chairman lingered. Maren had built a life out of lingering in rooms after the truth had left them.

She took out her phone and looked at the photograph she kept there — not in the favorites, never in the favorites, buried three folders deep where only she would find it. Four children on the back steps of the garden flat, squinting into summer sun. Dorian at twelve, already stooped over a book. Herself at fifteen, arm slung protectively around the smallest shoulders. And Teodor Ilić crouched in front of them all, laughing, his dark hair going gray at the temples, holding up a burnt tray of the almond cookies he always burned because he could never stop composing long enough to watch the oven. The lodger. The gentle man in the garden flat who had taught Emrys fingering games disguised as clapping songs, who had called Maren *malena* — little one — and treated her, at fifteen, as though her opinions about Stravinsky deserved serious rebuttal.

Their father had carried those boxes out of the flat himself. She had seen him from her bedroom window, past midnight, two weeks after the ambulance came. She had been seventeen and she had known, the way children of difficult men always know, and she had said nothing, and the saying-nothing had become a career, a marriage, a foundation, a life. Some lies are load-bearing. Her therapist — the third one, the good one — had suggested the phrase was itself a symptom. Maren had paid her generously and changed the subject.

She dialed Dorian. He answered on the second ring, because Dorian always answered, because Dorian had been answering ever since their father taught them that unanswered questions were how families died.

"It's me. We have a problem." She watched the leaves come down. "The daughter is real, Dorian. Vera Ilić. She has letters. And Emrys is working nights in the same building as the archive."

Silence on the line, long enough that she checked the call hadn't dropped. When Dorian spoke, his voice had the careful flatness he used for cataloguing damaged items. "What do you want to do?"

What did she want to do. What she wanted was to burn the letters, buy the woman off, redraft the program note, and fly to Lisbon where nobody knew any of their names — either set of names. What she wanted was to be fifteen again on the back steps with almond cookies and a man who listened, before everything calcified. What she wanted, underneath all of it, in the basement where the foundation kept everything else, was for someone else to carry this for once.

"We protect them," she said instead. "Both of them. Emrys doesn't know what she's walking into, and this woman — whatever she wants, she's grieving too. Nobody gets hurt. We find out what she actually has, and we decide what's survivable, together, like always."

"Together," Dorian repeated, without inflection.

"Dorian." She pressed two fingers to the bridge of her nose. "I'm not Father. I'm not going to lock doors and burn things and call it love. I'm asking you to trust that I can fix this the way I've fixed everything since he died. Quietly. Without anyone bleeding."

Another silence. Then, very quietly, in the voice of the twelve-year-old with the book: "Maren. There's a folder in his papers I haven't shown you. From 1999. I think—" A breath. "I think you should see it before you promise anything to anyone."

The line went soft with static and held breath. Outside, the last of the leaves were coming down, and somewhere below, in the amber light of the square, a woman in gray was pushing her mop bucket through the conservatory's service entrance, carrying questions Maren had spent twenty years refusing to ask.

"Tomorrow," Maren said. "Bring it tomorrow."

She hung up and stood alone in the boardroom a while longer, among the cooling cups and the tidy lies, telling herself — as she would tell herself again at midnight, and again at dawn — that everything she was about to do, she was doing for love.
