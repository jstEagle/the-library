## 10. The Year Everything Burned

The year everything burned began, as most conflagrations do, with warmth.

Emrys was fourteen in the spring of 1999, and she believed — the way only children and tyrants believe — that the world had arranged itself for her benefit. The Meridian Conservatory had accepted her into its pre-professional program on full scholarship. Her father had bought a new suit for the occasion, charcoal wool, and worn it to the acceptance meeting as though it were armor he intended to pass down. And in the garden flat behind their house on Vellacott Street, Teodor Ilić had begun teaching her how a phrase could breathe.

She remembered, decades later, mopping the conservatory's marble floors at two in the morning, that it had started with breathing. Not scales. Not theory. Breathing.

*Play it like you're afraid to wake someone,* Teodor would say, standing at the window of their parlor with his back half-turned, his hands clasped behind him as though holding something fragile he refused to set down. *Music is a house where someone is sleeping. You may walk through it. You may not run.*

Her father hated when Teodor taught this way. Aldous Blackwood believed in discipline the way other men believed in God — totally, and with an appetite for sacrifice. He kept a ledger of Emrys's practice hours in a green notebook, and every Sunday he reviewed it aloud at dinner while Maren, eleven and watchful, moved food around her plate, and Dorian, sixteen and already careful, asked questions designed to redirect their father's attention like small controlled fires.

But Teodor — Teodor lived in the garden flat and paid his rent partly in money and partly in music, and Aldous tolerated him because Teodor was useful, and because Teodor was ill in ways that made him easy to underestimate.

That was the thing Emrys understood only now, twenty years later, pushing a mop through corridors that still smelled of floor wax and ambition: everyone had underestimated Teodor Ilić. Including her. Especially her. She had loved him the way children love gentle adults — completely, and without once asking what it cost him to be gentle.

* * *

The debut was in October. She played the Blackwood Sonatas — her father's sonatas, the program said, composed by Aldous Blackwood over fifteen years of private labor — and the hall rose for her at the end like a single animal getting to its feet.

She could still feel that applause in her sternum. It had a weight, a physical pressure, like being embraced by something enormous that did not know its own strength. She was fourteen years old and five feet one, and for nine seconds she was the most important person in the city.

Afterward, backstage, her father held her face in both hands and said, *You see? This is what we built.* We. As though she had been a partner and not a piano. As though the sonatas had sprung from his hands alone, fully formed, like Athena from the skull.

And Teodor stood at the edge of the green room, holding a bouquet of carnations he'd clearly bought himself because no one else had thought to, and he was smiling, and Emrys — even at fourteen, even drunk on applause — noticed that the smile didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. It sat on his face the way a hat sits on a hook.

*You were extraordinary,* he told her, and kissed her forehead, and his lips were cold.

She remembered thinking: *he smells like medicine.*

She remembered thinking nothing else about it at all. That was the mercy of being fourteen. You could stand inside a burning house and notice only that the light was pretty.

* * *

The autumn tightened around them like a fist closing.

Aldous's control, which had always been strict, became architectural. He rearranged Emrys's lessons without consulting her teacher. He answered interview questions on her behalf, standing slightly in front of her so that photographers caught his shoulder more often than her face. He began saying *we* about everything — *we have decided*, *we are considering*, *we feel* — until Emrys privately wondered if there was still an *I* anywhere inside herself, or if her father had annexed it along with everything else.

Maren learned to disappear into the kitchen during these months. Dorian learned to be indispensable — running errands, managing correspondence, becoming, at sixteen, the kind of boy who knew where the important papers were kept. Emrys sometimes thought her siblings had each found a different way to survive the same weather: Maren went underground, Dorian went to work, and Emrys went into the music, which was the only room in the house her father couldn't follow her into.

Except that it wasn't hers either. She just didn't know that yet.

Teodor grew thinner through November. He stopped coming up for Sunday dinners, pleading fatigue, and Aldous stopped pretending to want him there. But twice a week, still, Teodor came to the parlor for her lessons, and those hours were the only ones all week when Emrys felt like a person rather than a project.

He never touched the piano during her lessons. That was the strange thing she'd forgotten until now, until the practice room with the unlocked door, until the manuscript in the unfamiliar hand. Teodor taught entirely away from the keyboard — pacing, humming, drawing phrases in the air with one long finger, telling her stories about Belgrade, about his mother who had sung, about the war years he described only obliquely, in images: *a winter with no birds*, *a street that learned to be quiet*. And then he would say, *Now show me what I mean,* and she would play, and he would close his eyes, and for an hour the house belonged to the two of them.

Once — only once — she came early, and heard him playing alone in the garden flat. It was the third sonata. The one the program credited to her father. Except what she heard through the glass of the garden door was not the version she'd been coached to perform; it was wilder, sadder, with a middle section that unfolded like something being confessed rather than announced. When she knocked, the playing stopped mid-phrase, and Teodor opened the door looking not embarrassed but frightened — actually frightened, a man caught at something — and he said, *Ah, little one. I was just... improvising. Old habits.*

She had believed him. Of course she had believed him. Fourteen-year-olds believe the people they love, because disbelief requires a kind of power children don't yet have.

Now, twenty years later, with the manuscript pages spread across a janitor's cart in her memory, she understood: he hadn't been improvising. He had been mourning. Playing his own music the way you visit a grave — privately, before dawn, knowing the headstone bears another man's name.

* * *

December came, and with it the coughing.

It started as a tickle he waved away. By mid-month it had become a sound that traveled through the garden flat's thin walls at night — a wet, rhythmic tearing, like paper being slowly ripped. Maren, who slept in the room above the flat, complained about it once at breakfast, and their father said, *The man won't see a doctor. Some people prefer their own counsel.* And that should have been the sentence Emrys remembered. That should have been the alarm bell.

But she was rehearsing. There was a competition in February, a radio broadcast in March, a manager in London who wrote letters her father read aloud at dinner like scripture. There was no room in the schedule for a lodger's cough.

The last time she saw Teodor alive was the nineteenth of December. She was certain of the date because it was the day her father brought home the review — the first major review, the one that called her *a vessel of rare refinement* and called the sonatas *a major statement by a composer of unusual maturity* — and pinned it to the refrigerator with a magnet shaped like a strawberry, and made them all toast with ginger ale.

Teodor came up that evening, unsteady on the stairs, wrapped in a coat indoors. He stood in the kitchen doorway watching them celebrate, and Emrys saw him look at the review, and look at her father's hand pressed flat and proprietary against her shoulder, and something moved across his face that she had no vocabulary for then. Grief, maybe. Or surrender. Or the particular exhaustion of a man deciding, finally, to stop fighting for something.

*Little one,* he said to her. *Come here.*

She went. He crouched — it cost him visibly — so their eyes were level, and he took both her hands in his, and his fingers were cold as cellar stone.

*Whatever happens with the music,* he said, *you remember this. The hands are yours. No one gave them to you. Whatever anyone tells you later — whoever claims anything — the hands are yours. Do you hear me?*

She said yes. She didn't understand. She was fourteen and there was ginger ale and a review on the refrigerator and her father was watching from across the kitchen with an expression she would spend twenty years decoding — not anger, not fear, but a kind of grim accounting, a man checking a ledger and finding the balance acceptable.

Teodor kissed her hands, both of them, formally, like a courtier. Then he stood, wished them all a happy Christmas in his careful, accented English, and went down the stairs into the dark.

Three days after Christmas, Maren found him.

* * *

They told her it was his heart. They told her quickly, in the living room, with the good curtains drawn, and her father did the telling while Dorian stood at the window and Maren cried in a way that seemed, even to eight-years-younger Emrys, slightly ahead of itself — crying the way you cry when you've known news before it's spoken.

There was a funeral, small and cold, at a church Teodor had apparently attended. Emrys wore black wool that scratched. Almost no one came. A woman from some embassy, an old man who wept continuously and whom no one introduced, a priest who pronounced the name *Ilić* carefully, as though handling something breakable. Her father spoke briefly about *a talented man, a private man, taken too soon*, and Emrys noticed that he never once said the word *friend*.

The garden flat was emptied within the week. Emrys came home from rehearsal one afternoon and the windows were dark and stripped of curtains, and there was a skip bin on the grass, and Rufus — though she didn't know his name then, just a broad-shouldered man from somewhere, carrying boxes — was loading her father's car with things wrapped in newspaper. She remembered thinking it was strange that her father was doing it himself, personally, on a Tuesday night, in the rain, instead of hiring it done. She remembered thinking the boxes looked heavy for containing only a dead man's sheets and dishes.

Then January came, and February, and the competition, and the broadcast, and the name *Blackwood* began to travel, and there was no more time for thinking about the garden flat, which was let to a young couple by spring, and the strawberry magnet came down off the refrigerator eventually, and the review yellowed under whatever replaced it, and the year everything burned closed over like water.

* * *

Now she stood in the recital hall at three in the morning, mop dripping into its bucket, and the whole of 1999 reassembled itself around her with terrible clarity — the way a negative develops, slowly, all at once.

The fright on Teodor's face when she caught him playing. The review pinned beside the ginger ale. *The hands are yours.* The boxes carried out in the rain by her own father, two weeks — she could calculate it now, precisely, from the accession dates Priya had shown her — two weeks after the funeral. The name change that followed within the year, Halloran to Blackwood, a family rebaptized in the same season the sonatas received their dedication: *To A.B., without whom none of this exists.*

Without whom none of this exists.

She gripped the mop handle and the hall breathed around her, vast and indifferent, the same hall where she had debuted at fifteen — no, fourteen, she had lied about her age even to herself all these years, rounding up toward the legend — and she understood at last what the child in the scratchy black wool had witnessed without comprehending: not a death, but a transaction. A gentle man had died owing something, or owning something, or simply possessing the misfortune of genius without appetite, and her father had walked out into the rain and collected.

And Teodor — dying, coughing, cold-handed Teodor — had known. Had spent his last weeks not fighting for the music but kneeling on a kitchen floor kissing a child's hands, planting the truth in the only soil he trusted to keep it: *the hands are yours*. Not the name. Not the credit. The hands. Because he had known, perhaps, that the girl would need the truth someday, and that she would only be able to receive it through her own fingers, at her own hour, in her own time.

The night shift ended at six. In the east windows the sky went the color of dishwater, then pearl. Emrys wheeled her cart past the sealed practice rooms, past the locked archive, past the plaque in the lobby reading ALDOUS BLACKWOOD HALL, and stopped at the staff entrance, and stood very still.

Tomorrow — today — was Sunday. Dorian reviewed the family papers on Sundays. Maren called at ten. And somewhere in the city, a woman named Vera Ilić was waiting for answers Emrys now possessed in fragments, sharp-edged and bleeding.

She pushed through the door into the gray morning, and the word she had been unable to say for twenty years finally formed itself, small and complete, in the cold air of her breath:

*Dad.*

Not as a question anymore.
