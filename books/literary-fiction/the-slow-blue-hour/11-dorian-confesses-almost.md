## 11. Dorian Confesses, Almost

The archive room at Halloran House had no windows, which Dorian had always considered its chief virtue. Light faded paper. Light faded reputations. Light, in his experience, was the enemy of everything his father had spent forty years building, and so Dorian had built his life in the windowless places — the climate-controlled rooms, the acid-free boxes, the careful dark where the past could be kept at a constant fifty-five percent humidity and never, ever asked to explain itself.

He was cataloguing correspondence when he heard her key in the outer door.

He knew it was Emrys before he heard her voice. She was the only one of his siblings who still used the front entrance like a person with nothing to hide. Maren came through the garden, always, the way their mother had. Dorian himself used the service door off the mews, though the service door had been his father's preference and Dorian disliked acknowledging how many of his father's preferences he had absorbed like damp into plaster.

"Your lights are on," Emrys said from the doorway. "I could see them from the street. You never leave the lights on."

"I was working."

"At eleven at night." She came in. She was still wearing her work clothes — the grey uniform of the Meridian Conservatory's night crew, the name tag she never wore, the lanyard she did. She smelled faintly of floor wax and cold air, and something in Dorian's chest contracted at the sight of her, because she had their mother's way of standing in a doorway as though the doorway were a question she was prepared to wait out.

"You could have called," he said.

"You don't answer."

"I answer Maren."

"You answer Maren because Maren asks questions you've already rehearsed the answers to." She pulled a chair from the wall — one of the two visitor chairs, positioned, he noticed with a small private shame, at a deferential distance from his desk — and sat down in it facing him. "I found the fingering marks, Dorian."

He did not move. He had learned stillness from their father, too. Stillness was the first thing Aldous Blackwood had taught all three of them, though he had called it composure and had taught it with the back of his hand and the flat of his voice and, once, memorably, with a metronome thrown across the music room.

"The sonata," Emrys said. "The one in the sealed practice room. The one in your precious archive under accession number — I copied it, Dorian, I have it written on my hand — 1999.114. The fingering is mine. I wrote it when I was fourteen, in the garden flat, on music I was told was an exercise. Teodor's exercises, he said. My teacher's little studies. I marked up those pages because Teodor asked me to. He said a composer needs to know what a young hand wants to do before the young hand knows it." Her voice did not rise. That was the worst of it. She spoke the way she now mopped floors: steadily, thoroughly, leaving nothing behind her but the shine of what had been exposed. "So you tell me how my fourteen-year-old fingering got into the manuscript of the Blackwood First Sonata. Published under our name. Performed by me, at fifteen, to a standing ovation, in the hall where I now empty the trash."

The archive room's climate system hummed. Dorian watched the needle on the hygrometer and thought, absurdly, that the humidity had crept up half a point, and that his father would have had something to say about that.

"Sit down," he said, though she was already sitting.

"I am sitting."

"Then let me tell you a story," he said, "and you can decide at the end whether it's the one you wanted."

* * *

He began, as he always began, with the winter of 1998 — the year before everything — because the theft only made sense if you understood the debts, and the debts only made sense if you understood Teodor Ilić, and Teodor Ilić only made sense if you had watched him, as Dorian had, at seventeen, through the kitchen window, standing in the garden flat's small yard in his overcoat in February, conducting nothing. Conducting the dark. His hands moving through the cold air with total seriousness, no audience, no music on a stand, just the man and the tempo he alone could hear.

"He was ill," Dorian said. "Not just his heart, at the end. Before that. He owed people, Emrys. Serious people, not the bank. He'd come here from — well, the story was Zagreb, but the papers said otherwise, and the papers were right. He owed money to people who had helped him leave, and they had begun to help themselves. There were letters. There were visits. You were twelve; you wouldn't have been shown. Maren was away at school. I was the one who answered the door."

Emrys said nothing. She had their mother's patience too, and Dorian hated her a little for it, because it meant he could not rush.

"The sonatas existed before Papa ever touched them. I want you to hear me say that plainly, because I know what you found, and I'm not going to insult you by pretending the archive is wrong. The archive is never wrong. That's the whole point of the archive." He laced his fingers on the desk, and saw that his hands, at fifty-one, had begun to look like his father's, and made himself continue anyway. "Teodor wrote them in that flat. He played them at night with the window open because the radiator clanked and he said the clanking was in B-flat and he'd learned to compose around it. Papa heard them. Papa — " and here Dorian paused, because this was the part he had never said aloud to anyone, the part that lived in the burned folder's ashes and in the part of the folder he had not burned because he could not — "Papa heard them and knew they were better than anything he had written in thirty years of trying. And Teodor knew he knew. And Teodor was dying. And the people he owed were circling. And there was a child in the house who could *play* them."

"You," Emrys said.

"Me first. I was adequate. You were — " He stopped. He had spent twenty years not finishing that sentence, and found he still could not finish it. "You were the instrument it needed. Papa saw it before anyone. Perhaps before you did."

"So he took the music."

"He *bought* it," Dorian said, and heard how the word cracked down the middle like a plate. "There are papers. There was an agreement — Teodor signed it. Assignment of rights, in exchange for the settlement of his debts and a provision — " his voice did something he did not authorize it to do — "a provision for you. For your education. For the conservatory fees. Teodor insisted on that clause, Emrys. He negotiated for it. I read it. I have read it a hundred times. He would not sign over the sonatas unless the money went to your training. Papa wanted to structure it as a loan against the house. Teodor refused. He said — I have the letter — he said, *'The girl will carry this music further than I ever could, and I will not have her carry my debts with it. Let the debts die with me. Let her have clean hands.'*"

The climate system hummed. Somewhere in the house a pipe knocked twice and went quiet, and Dorian thought of the radiator in B-flat.

"So he signed," Emrys said slowly. "He signed away his own sonatas. While he was dying. And then he died. Two weeks later they were accessioned under Papa's name."

"Two weeks after the funeral," Dorian said. "The conservatory wanted them. Papa gave them the fair copies and kept the sketches. The sketches are in the vault. I've never shown them to anyone."

"Why keep them?"

"Because they're the proof," Dorian said, "of whatever story you decide is true."

* * *

She stood up then. She walked the length of the archive room, past the boxes labeled in his own careful hand — B-HALLORAN, 1961–1998; CORRESPONDENCE, A. BLACKWOOD; PRESS, 1999–2001 — and stopped at the shelf where the burned folder's neighbors sat, their spines unbroken, their secrets intact, and he understood that she had already seen the gap. Of course she had. She had been coming here for weeks, he realized now, since the funeral, since the woman named Vera, since the practice room. She had been reading his archive the way she used to read scores: silently, completely, at a speed that made him feel slow.

"The name," she said, with her back to him. "When did we become Halloran?"

"March 2000." He said it the way he said accession numbers. "After the second scandal letter. After the journalist from the — it doesn't matter which paper. Papa hired a firm. Mother's maiden name was Halloran; her people were from Cork; there was no Halloran left alive to object. It cost him eleven thousand pounds and a friendship with his solicitor, who thought it was cowardice. Papa said it was armor. He said a name is the cheapest thing a man can change and the most expensive thing to defend."

"And mine? My name was on every program in Europe by then. Emrys Blackwood. Did the firm change that too?"

"You were seventeen," Dorian said. "The career was already — " He chose the word with the care of a man defusing something. "Over. The conservatory revoked the fellowship. The competitions withdrew the invitations. Papa said the name change was to protect Maren and me, and that was true, and it was also a lie, and I have never been able to separate the two halves of it, and I have tried, Emrys. I have spent twenty years trying."

She turned around. In the archive's flat light her face looked the way it had at fifteen, on the stage, before the first note — that terrible openness, that readiness — and then it closed, and he watched it close, and he understood that he had just lost something and could not yet say what.

"You're telling me he was protecting us," she said. "That's your story. The debts, the dying man, the signature, the clause about my education. A tidy little testament. The father as shield." She came back toward the desk but did not sit. "Here's what doesn't fit, Dorian. I've had three weeks and a mop bucket and nothing to do but think. Teodor stayed silent. He signed, he stayed silent, he died — fine. But Papa didn't stay silent. Papa put his own name on the title page. He put *Blackwood* on it in letters an inch tall. If it was protection, it was protection that required a theft. If Teodor sold them, why seal the practice room? Why vanish him from every program? Why did no one at the conservatory, in twenty years, ever say his name to me — not once, not when I played the sonata, not when they gave me the prize *for* the sonata? You don't erase a man you bought fairly. You erase a man you stole from."

"The debts — "

"The debts died with him. You said so. He said so. *Let her have clean hands.*" She said the words back to him gently, and they cut deeper for the gentleness. "So whose hands were dirty, Dorian? Because mine have been dirty for twenty years and nobody would tell me what with."

He looked at his hands on the desk. His father's hands. He thought about the folder he had burned three weeks ago, the night after the funeral, in the yard, with the garden hose coiled and ready — the folder with the second agreement, the one dated after Teodor's death, the one with the clause that had never appeared in any archive because Dorian had accessioned it under a false year and then, when Vera Ilić's name appeared in the guest book of his father's funeral like a knife laid on a coffin, had carried it outside and fed it to the flames page by page, telling himself it was duty, telling himself it was mercy, telling himself, in the end, nothing at all, just watching the paper curl.

"There's something I haven't told you," he said.

"I know," Emrys said. "There's something you haven't told me. I can smell it. You've had it in this room for twenty years and now it's the only thing in the room."

"The agreement Teodor signed — the one with your clause — it wasn't the only one." His voice had gone very quiet, the way it went quiet in this room, the way everything in this room was quiet. "There was a second document. Dated eleven days after he died. Papa's hand. It reclassified the first agreement. It — Emrys, it said the sonatas had been *commissioned*. By Papa. That Teodor had been paid as a work-for-hire. That the authorship — the legal authorship, the *moral* authorship — had been Papa's from the start. It turned a dying man's bargain into an employee's receipt. It turned Teodor into a ghost writer in his own garden flat. And it was dated after his death, which means he never saw it, which means he never signed it, which means — "

"Which means it was forged," Emrys said.

"Which means I don't know," Dorian said, and heard, at last, the truth under twenty years of cataloguing: *I have never wanted to know.* "Papa told me the first agreement was lost. He told me the second was the true record. He was crying when he told me — you never saw Papa cry, but I did, once, in this room, and he said, *'I did it for the children, Dorian. I did it so the debts couldn't touch her.'* And I was twenty-three, and I wanted to believe him, because the alternative was that my father was a thief and my whole life was built on his theft and my sister's hands were not clean at all, they were the dirtiest hands in the building." He made himself look at her. "So I believed him. I accessioned the second agreement under a false date. I kept the first one in the folder with the letters from the people Teodor owed. And when you started asking questions — when *she* started asking questions — I burned it. All of it. The debts, the threats, the first agreement. I burned the only document that proved Teodor ever agreed to anything."

The silence that followed was the longest of Dorian's life, and he had sat through his father's funeral in it.

Emrys picked up her lanyard from where she'd set it on the visitor chair. She looped it over her head with the unhurried motion of a woman clocking out.

"The woman at the funeral," she said. "Vera. She has letters, Dorian. She told me she has letters of Papa's. If the first agreement is ash, then her letters are the only version of Teodor's side left in the world — and his side is the one we erased." She paused at the door, and for one moment her face was fifteen again, on the stage, and he could not tell if she was about to forgive him or sentence him. "You gave me a story where Papa was a shield. I believe you believe it. But you burned the evidence, brother. You burned it, and now the only truth left is whatever Vera's paper says — and whatever I decide to do with it."

"Emrys — "

"The centennial," she said. "Three weeks. Priya showed me the gala program draft. *A tribute to Aldous Blackwood, Composer.*" Her hand rested on the door frame, and he saw that it was steady. "I'm going to ask you one more time, and I want the whole truth this time, not the archive version. What did Papa carry out of the garden flat the night Teodor died?"

Dorian opened his mouth.

And found, to his horror, that he could not remember whether what he knew came from his father's confession or from the thing he had seen himself, at seventeen, through the kitchen window at two in the morning: his father in the yard, in the rain, carrying a wooden crate toward the house, walking quickly, the way men walk when they hope the dark is not watching — and behind him, in the garden flat's lit window, a shape that did not move, that did not move at all.

"I don't know," Dorian said.

Emrys looked at him for a long moment — the look she must have learned on the night shift, the look that sees everything and reports nothing — and then she pulled the door shut behind her, and Dorian sat alone in the windowless room with his father's hands and his father's archive and the growing certainty that he had just confessed to everything except the one thing that mattered.
