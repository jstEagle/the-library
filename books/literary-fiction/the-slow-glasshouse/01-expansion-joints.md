## 1. Expansion Joints

The crack at Pier 3 measured 0.4 millimeters, and Ulyana Halloran wrote it down in her field book the way she wrote everything down: without opinion.

She knelt on the inspection platform under the Halsted viaduct, forty feet above the river, and ran her thumb along the fracture. Concrete doesn't lie. It fails in a language of hairlines and spalls and rust-bleed, and if you learn to read it, it tells you exactly how long it has left. Steel expands in summer heat and contracts in winter cold, and every bridge is built with joints to absorb that movement—gaps that let the structure breathe without tearing itself apart. The expansion joint at the north abutment had been installed in 1961 and was now packed with grit, road salt, and forty years of deferred decisions.

"Anything?" Ray called from the bucket truck, where he was photographing the underside of Deck Span B.

"Transverse cracking at Pier 3. Point-four mils." She stood, brushed concrete dust off her knee. "Nothing structural yet."

"Yet," Ray said, and laughed. He'd been doing this thirty years and laughed at everything, which Ulyana had decided was either wisdom or a symptom.

She climbed back up into the light. From the deck, the city arranged itself in its usual argument—cranes over the east lots, the old neighborhoods going gray and empty one block at a time, the river carrying whatever the rain gave it. Ulyana had spent nineteen years reading structures for a living, and she trusted them more than people for a simple reason: a structure's failure was always legible in advance. You could point to the exact millimeter where things went wrong. People failed everywhere at once, invisibly, and then acted surprised.

Her phone buzzed against her hip. A text from Tom: *Milk. Also your mom's landlord called again — wants the flat cleared by end of month. I can do boxes Saturday if you want.*

She read it twice, typed nothing, put the phone away.

That was the thing about Tom. He was reliable the way a load rating was reliable. He would show up Saturday with boxes and tape and he would carry more than his share and he would not complain, and when she said thank you he would say *anytime* and mean it, and this was their marriage: two competent people maintaining a structure that had never once been tested to failure because neither of them had ever applied an unusual load. She loved him. She was fairly sure she loved him. But she loved him the way she loved the Halsted viaduct—with respect, with attention, with a schedule of inspections—and some mornings, standing at the bathroom mirror, she caught herself thinking about her marriage in exactly the vocabulary she used at work. Wear. Fatigue. Serviceable condition. Rating: adequate.

Adequate bridges got replaced eventually. Nobody said that out loud about marriages.

* * *

Her mother's flat was on the third floor of a building that smelled like other people's cooking, and it had taken Ulyana three weekends to get through the kitchen and the living room and most of the bedroom. Her mother had been a woman of ferocious order—labels on the spice jars, shoes in pairs, receipts kept for seven years in case anyone ever audited her life—and so the clearing had been less grief than inventory. Dishes: donate. Linens: donate. The good coat: Ulyana took it home and hung it in her own closet where it watched her get dressed every morning like a supervisor.

Tom came Saturday, as promised, with boxes and tape and a thermos of coffee he'd made too strong on purpose because she liked it that way. They worked well together. That was the problem, maybe—they worked well together, like a crew, like colleagues, and somewhere in eleven years they had stopped being anything else and nobody had filed the paperwork.

"The wardrobe's next," Tom said, standing in the bedroom doorway with his sleeves rolled up. "You want me to take it apart? It won't fit through the door whole."

"It came apart when they moved in. There'll be bolts."

They tipped the wardrobe forward onto its face, an old oak thing heavier than sin, and Tom unscrewed the backing panel while Ulyana held the frame steady. The panel came away in sections. Behind it, wedged into the gap between the wardrobe's back and the wall—a space no wider than a hand, a space her mother had filled deliberately, knowing that whoever found it would have to dismantle the furniture to do it—was a shoebox.

Not a shoebox. A stationer's box, once white, now the color of weak tea. Heavy. Tied with string in a knot Ulyana recognized because her mother had tied every package of her life in that same double loop.

"What is it?" Tom asked.

"I don't know."

He reached for it and something in her—something fast and involuntary, a reflex she didn't know she owned—said, "I've got it."

Tom looked at her for half a second longer than usual. Then he nodded and went to tape up the linens, because Tom respected boundaries the way he respected everything: quietly, completely, without ever asking what they were protecting.

Ulyana sat on the bare mattress—her mother's mattress, still bearing the shallow fossil of her sleeping body—and untied the knot.

Letters. Forty-one of them, she counted later. Envelopes, sealed, stamped, addressed in her mother's small upright hand—the handwriting Ulyana knew from grocery lists and birthday cards and the labels on the spice jars—to:

*Emil Kowalczyk*
*The Glasshouse, Wren Street*
*Old Halsted*

Every single one of them addressed to the same man. Every single one sealed. None of them sent. The stamps were decades old; one of them was a denomination that hadn't existed since Ulyana was in grade school. Postmarks: none. Dates on the letters themselves, visible through the thin paper or written on the backs of the envelopes in pencil: 1979. 1981. 1984. 1987. And then nothing. The correspondence just stopped, mid-life, the way a sentence stops when the speaker walks out of the room.

Ulyana sat on the mattress holding the box in her lap and felt the numbness she had been wearing since the funeral—the efficient, professional numbness, the numbness of a woman who had planned the service in four days and cried exactly twice and told everyone she was fine, which was true in the way that a bridge rated for light traffic only is fine—felt that numbness develop its first crack.

It was a small crack. Point-four millimeters, maybe. Hairline. Nothing structural yet.

But she knew what cracks did. She had spent nineteen years watching them propagate. A crack was not damage; a crack was information. A crack was the structure telling you where the load actually was.

*Emil Kowalczyk.* She turned the name over. She had grown up four blocks from Old Halsted and she did not know the name. Her father had died when she was nine—she remembered the hospital, the smell of it, her mother's hand clamped around hers in the corridor—and after that it had been the two of them, a closed system, her mother running the household like a maintenance schedule: school, church, groceries, bills, bed. Her mother had never mentioned a glassblower. Her mother had never mentioned wanting anything she didn't already have.

And yet here were forty-one letters, sealed for longer than Ulyana had been alive, hidden behind a wardrobe by a woman who labeled her spice jars, addressed to a man on Wren Street with the word *Glasshouse* where a street number should have been.

Wren Street. Ulyana knew Wren Street. Everyone who worked the east side knew Wren Street—it was in the demolition survey zone, the whole neighborhood scheduled to come down, and she'd heard the councilmen talk about it the way you talk about a condemned structure: obsolete, blighted, better for everyone once it's gone.

She should put the box down. She should re-tie the knot, re-seat the panel, screw the wardrobe back together, and carry it to her own house unopened, because these were her mother's private words and privacy was a kind of structural integrity and she of all people believed in not loading a structure past its rating.

She picked up the first envelope anyway. Held it. The paper had gone soft at the corners, the way paper does when a hand has held it many times before sealing it. Her mother had held this letter, Ulyana thought. Had read it over. Had possibly wept on it, or laughed, or both. Had sealed it, stamped it, walked as far as the mailbox—and turned around. Forty-one times. Forty-one trips to the mailbox and forty-one turns back.

Or maybe she'd never even gotten that far. Maybe the mailbox was the fantasy and the wardrobe was the truth.

Ulyana slid her finger under the flap. The seal gave with a sound like a small bone breaking.

Inside, one page, both sides, dated June 1979. It began:

*Emil — You will be at the corner of Wren and Marsh on Thursday at six, and I will not be there, and you will hate me, and you should. I have thought about how to say this for nine days and there is no honest way to say it except straight out...*

Ulyana stopped breathing.

Down the hall, tape shrieked off a roll. Tom's footsteps crossed the kitchen, steady, unhurried, a man maintaining his own structure. The river outside carried its cargo. Somewhere across town, on Wren Street, in a building called the Glasshouse, a man named Emil Kowalczyk might still be alive, waiting since 1979 for a letter that was never coming—or might be dead, or gone, or scattered with the rest of the neighborhood that the city had already drawn its lines around.

Ulyana read the first line again. Then she folded the letter along its original creases, put it back in its envelope, and set the box on her knees, and understood—with the particular clarity of someone who has just found the crack that runs through everything—that she was going to have to open all of them.

And that she was going to have to find him first.
