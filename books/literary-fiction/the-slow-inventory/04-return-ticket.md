## 4. Return Ticket

The bus to Marlow Bend left from a station Edmund had never noticed before, though she had lived in the city eleven years and had walked past its entrance—narrow, tiled, smelling of diesel and old rain—hundreds of times. That was the thing about doors you didn't intend to go through. They became architecture. They became wallpaper. She could have passed that doorway every day for the rest of her life without once seeing it, the way she had passed her own reflection in shop windows, in the dark glass of the laundromat, in the faces of men who asked her name at parties and forgot it before she finished giving it.

She bought a single ticket with cash. The clerk, a young man with earbuds in, didn't look up. Perfect, she thought. This is how I like to be seen: not at all.

The certified letter sat in her bag like a stone wrapped in paper. She had read it four times since yesterday, each time hoping the words would rearrange themselves into something less final. *In re: Estate of Margaret Ashgrove, deceased — Fenwick Lane property to be offered at public auction.* Six weeks. The handwriting on the envelope was not Thomas's—it belonged to his solicitor, a man named Pell whose letters had once come to their father, back when their father was alive to owe people money—but it might as well have been Thomas's hand. Everything Thomas did now came through intermediaries. He had learned, as she had, that distance was easier to maintain through third parties.

She hadn't told him she was coming. She hadn't told anyone. There was no one, strictly speaking, to tell. Her landlady would notice the rent arriving late rather than notice Edmund leaving; her clients knew her only as a voice on the phone and a signature that wasn't hers. She had spent the morning ghosting a eulogy for a woman's brother—a good one, she thought, tender and specific, full of details about fishing and a bad knee and a joke about hospital Jell-O—and she had written it in forty minutes flat, because grief was the one language she spoke fluently in other people's names.

Now she sat by the window as the city thinned into suburbs, suburbs into fields, fields into the long green corridor of the river valley, and watched herself travel backward through geography the way other people traveled through time.

The bus smelled of vinyl and lemon cleaner and something faintly burnt, which made her stomach clench until she realized it was only the engine. Fifteen years, and the smell of smoke still reached for her first, before anything else. She pressed her forehead to the cool glass and let the fields blur.

*You would laugh at me,* she wrote mentally, composing a postcard out of habit, addressing it to an address that was a charred foundation now, a lot with weeds growing through it. *You would say, Edmund, you always did run toward fires. And I would say, Mother, I have never once run toward anything in my life.*

* * *

Marlow Bend announced itself the way it always had: too late. One moment there were hills, and then the road dropped into the valley and the town was simply there, folded along the river like a letter someone had started writing and abandoned. The water tower. The grain elevator. St. Brigid's steeple, leaning two degrees east, leaning a little more every year, so that the town's whole skyline seemed to be slowly bowing.

Edmund got off with her single bag and stood on the pavement outside the gas station that used to be Duffy's, listening to the bus pull away behind her. The sound of it receding was the sound of her last exit closing. She felt a familiar panic rise—the old instinct to turn around, to flag the bus down, to return to the city where no one knew her face or her history or the shape of what she'd done—and she breathed through it the way she'd taught herself to breathe through everything: slowly, invisibly, like a woman pretending to admire the weather.

But this was the weather here: low clouds the color of dishwater, air that tasted of river silt and cut grass, the particular damp chill of October in a valley town where autumn arrived early and stayed late, like a guest who had nowhere better to be.

She walked. There was no reason not to. The town was small enough that walking was the native mode of transport, and she wanted—God help her, she wanted—to arrive at Fenwick Lane on foot, the way she'd arrived everywhere as a child, letting the streets deliver her home.

The town had changed and hadn't changed, which was somehow worse than either alone. The bakery on Corbett Street was a vape shop now. The hardware store survived, improbably, its window still displaying a single snow shovel in October, an act of faith or stubbornness she found almost moving. A dog she didn't recognize barked at her from behind a fence she did. The elementary school's playground equipment had been replaced with plastic things in primary colors, but the oak tree was still there, the big one, the one under which she'd taught Thomas to skip stones in the drainage ditch, and seeing it stopped her for a full thirty seconds on the sidewalk while a woman across the street pretended not to stare.

*They'll remember you,* said a voice in her head that sounded like dread wearing her mother's tone. *Of course they'll remember you. You're the girl who ran.*

Because that was the story, wasn't it? That was the version that had traveled those fifteen years ahead of her, arriving in Marlow Bend long before she did: Edmund Ashgrove, seventeen years old, who had been unreachable the night her mother's apartment burned. Who hadn't answered the phone. Who hadn't come. Who had let everyone believe whatever they believed about why.

She had never corrected the record. Correction required presence, and presence was the one currency she'd sworn never to spend again.

* * *

Fenwick Lane was shorter than she remembered. Everything was shorter than she remembered—that was the trick of childhood, inflating distances, making the walk to school an expedition, making the house on the corner a castle. But the house itself had not shrunk. If anything it had grown larger in its decay, the way dying things do, spreading outward into their own ruin. The porch roof sagged over boards gone silver-gray with weather. Plywood covered the downstairs windows, and someone—Thomas, presumably, or the solicitor's people—had nailed it with a thoroughness that looked like anger. Weeds stood waist-high in what had been her mother's garden, and one of them, absurdly, defiantly, was a sunflower, blooming yellow and stupid against all that gray.

Edmund stood at the gate and could not make herself open it.

Her hand was on the latch. The latch was cold. And between her palm and the metal there seemed to exist some membrane, some surface tension of the past, that fifteen years of absence had stretched across the gateway like a skin over milk. She thought: *I will stand here for exactly one minute, and then I will turn around, and I will take the next bus back, and I will write to my mother tonight about the sunflower, and none of this will have happened.*

"Edmund Ashgrove."

Not a question. Not a shout. Just her name, spoken in a warm contralto with the faint music of another country underneath it, spoken the way you say a word you've kept safe for a long time.

She turned.

A woman was coming up the sidewalk carrying a canvas shopping bag, gray hair braided and pinned in a crown, dressed in a quilted coat the color of eggplant. She moved with the unhurried authority of someone who owned her own pace. Edmund knew the face from a decade and a half ago—knew it younger, rounder, attached to a different life—but recognition arrived whole, unbidden, terrible in its ease.

"Mrs. Okonkwo," Edmund said. Her voice came out rusty. "Hello."

"You got tall," Mrs. Okonkwo said, "or I got short, and I know which one it is." She set down her bag on the pavement with the deliberateness of a woman who intended to stay a while. "Look at you. Standing at that gate like it's going to bite."

"It might."

"Mm." Mrs. Okonkwo studied her—not rudely, but thoroughly, the way you study a photograph for changes. "You look like your mother. Around the eyes. She had that look, like she was reading something over your shoulder that nobody else could see."

Edmund's throat tightened. "I'm just—" She gestured at the house, at nothing. "The auction. I saw the notice. I wanted to see it."

"I know why you're here." Mrs. Okonkwo picked up her bag again, but didn't move. "Six weeks is not very long. You should eat something. You've got that look people get when they've been traveling on buses and eating vending machine food." She said it as fact, not question, and Edmund realized with a small vertigo that the woman had already assembled the entire narrative—the certified letter, the bus, the empty stomach—from nothing but the sight of her standing at a gate.

"I'm fine," Edmund said automatically. It was her professional phrase, the one she typed into other people's condolences. *He is at peace. She knows how much you loved him. I am fine.*

"Mm," Mrs. Okonkwo said again, in the tone of a woman filing *fine* under *not fine*. Then, without transition: "Your mother grew the best tomatoes on this street. Everybody knew it. Even the Hendersons, and the Hendersons would rather die than admit anybody beat them at anything." She nodded at the weeds, at the lone sunflower. "That one came up on its own. Volunteer. From seeds in the soil, I expect. Things do that. Come back when you think they're gone."

Edmund looked at the sunflower. It swayed slightly in the wind off the river, gaudy and alive among the ruins of the garden, and she could not speak.

"The house has been empty a long time," Mrs. Okonkwo went on, gentler now. "Empty houses are like empty chairs at a table. Everybody sees them. Nobody says anything. But we all—" she paused, choosing the word with care, "—*waited*. For somebody to come stand at that gate. And here you are."

Here I am, Edmund thought. Saying nothing. Having said nothing for fifteen years.

"I should go," she managed. "I haven't booked anywhere to stay, I—"

"Number fourteen has a room. The Okafor place. Tell them Adaeze sent you, they won't charge you the first night." Mrs. Okonkho adjusted the strap of her bag and finally turned to go, then paused, and looked back, and what was in her face was so plainly, unbearably kind that Edmund had to look away at the sunflower again. "It's good to see you, Edmund Ashgrove. It's been a long time coming."

And she walked on up the street, unhurried, leaving Edmund alone at the gate with her name still hanging in the air—her actual name, spoken aloud, in the town where she'd buried it, spoken without a bill attached, without a request, without any transaction at all. Just given. Like tomatoes. Like a volunteer flower.

Edmund stood there a long time. The wind moved through the weeds. Somewhere down the street a screen door banged, and a child laughed, and the ordinary machinery of a town that had never stopped running went on running without her, as it had for fifteen years, waiting.

Then she opened the gate. The hinge cried out—one long, rusted note—and the sound went through her like a hand through water.

Inside the yard, the grass was hip-high, and the front door wore a padlock, and beyond the plywood windows the house was keeping whatever it was keeping. Edmund stood on the porch her father had built, under the roof that sagged, and raised her fist to knock on a door of a house that belonged to no one anymore, and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that she had not come to look at the house at all.

She had come because somewhere inside it was the answer to a question she had spent fifteen years refusing to ask.

And tomorrow, whether she was ready or not, she was going to have to face the man who held the key.
