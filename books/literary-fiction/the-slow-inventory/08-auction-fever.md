## 8. Auction Fever

Thomas counted the house the way other men prayed.

He did it every morning now, since the notice went out and the boards came off the windows and the auctioneer's man walked through with his clipboard and his little laser wand that blinked red against the wallpaper like a pulse. He counted the rooms—eleven, if you counted the pantry, which their mother always had, because a pantry was a room where things were kept safe, she said, and Thomas had never forgotten that. He counted the doors: nineteen, four of them swollen shut with decades of damp. He counted the stair treads on his way down each morning: fourteen, the fifteenth still loose at the front edge, still singing its small wooden note under his heel, the same note it had sung for forty years.

Fourteen treads. He made himself say it. Fourteen, and not fifteen, and not thirteen, and nothing else about the staircase at all.

The catalogue was nearly finished. He'd spent six weeks on it, photographing everything the auctioneer might want—the Georgian sideboard with its water-stained top, the harp-back chairs missing two of their number, the grandfather clock that hadn't run since before Edmund left—and writing descriptions in the flat, honest language of sale. *Walnut, Victorian, some veneer loss.* *Brass fender, good condition.* He was good at this. He had discovered, over these weeks, that he was very good at this, and he understood what it meant: that a man who could describe a thing precisely enough did not have to feel it. The sideboard was walnut, Victorian, some veneer loss. It was not the table where their mother had laid out the good plates twice a year and once, memorably, for no reason at all, a Tuesday in April, because the light was nice and her children were home. None of that went in the catalogue. The catalogue was mercy.

His own debts, by contrast, resisted counting. Not because they were large—they were, but largeness he could handle; largeness had a shape—but because they moved. They shifted like silt under current. The second mortgage on the shop in Ashford, the loan against the van, the credit line he'd opened to cover the winter when the heating contract fell through and old Mr. Prewitt died owing him four hundred pounds that his estate would never pay. He kept the numbers in a notebook in the inside pocket of his coat, and some mornings he took the notebook out in the van in the car park behind the Fenwick Lane property and added the column again, hoping the arithmetic would come out different, knowing it wouldn't. Forty-one thousand, give or take. A number with no bottom to it. A well you dropped stones into and heard nothing.

And then Callum Reeve came to the house on a Thursday, in a grey coat too fine for Marlow Bend, and offered to make the whole question of numbers go away.

* * *

Thomas found him standing in the front garden, hands in his pockets, looking up at the facade with the expression of a man appraising a horse—not the animal itself but what it would fetch, though in Reeve's case the appraisal seemed also to include something like affection, which was worse. Affection from a buyer was a tool. Thomas had sold things all his life; he knew how charm worked at close range.

"You must be the brother," Reeve said, extending a hand. "Callum Reeve. Reeve Development. I sent a card round."

"I got the card." Thomas had gotten the card and put it in the drawer with the batteries and the dead torches, the drawer of deferred things. "Auction's open bidding. You'll have your chance like everyone else."

"Oh, I'll be there." Reeve smiled. He had the kind of face built for smiling, broad and even, teeth like a picket fence, and the smile reached all the way up as far as it needed to and no further. "But I thought I'd save us both the theater. Can I buy you a coffee? Or—" he glanced past Thomas's shoulder into the hall, at the dust-sheeted furniture looming like a congregation under shrouds, "—I could come in. If the house is receiving visitors."

The house is always receiving visitors, Thomas almost said. That was the trouble with it. But he stepped aside and let the man in, and hated himself a little for the courtesy, and more for the leap of hope underneath the courtesy, the animal part of him already doing arithmetic: *well above reserve, well above reserve.*

Reeve walked the ground floor slowly, touching nothing, which Thomas noted and appreciated. Other men touched. Reeve looked, and asked questions that sounded like interest and were actually measurement. Ceiling heights. Damp course. Whether the outbuildings went with the title. He stood a long time in the kitchen, in the exact spot where the morning light came through the window over the sink and lay across the floorboards in a long pale parallelogram, and Thomas watched him stand there and understood that Reeve was seeing the kitchen not as it was but as it would be—gutted, opened into the back parlor, bi-fold doors onto a patio where the coal shed stood. Thomas had seen developers do this trick before, standing inside the present tense while living comfortably in the future one. It took a certain kind of imagination. He almost admired it.

"Your sister," Reeve said, without turning around. "She's been here."

It wasn't a question. "You've seen her?"

"At the viewing last week. She was in the blue bedroom upstairs." Reeve turned then, and his face was pleasant and open and entirely unreadable, a door painted on a wall. "She didn't seem to know who I was. I don't think she knew I was there at all, actually. She was—" he searched for the word, found it, delivered it gently, "—elsewhere."

"She's helping sort," Thomas said. "For the sale."

"Mm." Reeve let the syllable sit in the air between them, unbothered, unhurried, a man who had learned that silence did half his negotiating for him. Then: "Mr. Ashgrove. May I make you an offer? A private one. Before the auction."

* * *

They sat in the back parlor because it was the only room with chairs that weren't sheeted, and even there Thomas had to pull the dust off the window seat so Reeve wouldn't sit in forty years of settled grey. Reeve produced a folder from inside his coat—of course he carried the folder, of course he'd come prepared, this whole visit had been staged down to the sympathetic pause in the kitchen—and laid out a single sheet, printed, with a figure at the bottom that Thomas read three times because the first two times his eyes slid off it like water off wax.

Eighteen percent above reserve. Cash. Completion in twenty-one days. No chain, no conditions beyond vacant possession, and a clause—Reeve tapped it with one finger—that waived any survey contingency. He wanted the house as it stood, ghosts included, sight unseen and survey unsurveyed.

"The planning permission's already drafted," Reeve said. "I won't pretend otherwise. Four units off the existing footprint, keeping the street elevation, restoring rather than demolishing—I know how these towns feel about demolition, I've read the parish council minutes, all eleven riveting pages of them. I'm not the villain of this piece, Mr. Ashgrove. I'm the ending. The auction will go higher than my offer, possibly, if two fools fall in love with the place in the same afternoon. But auctions are weather. This is climate." He slid the pen across the window seat. "Twenty-one days from signature, the money's yours. Both halves, held in escrow pending your sister's agreement—or bought outright from you if she'll sign, which I understand may take some persuading."

"And if she doesn't sign?"

"Then we meet again in three weeks and I bid like everyone else, and you take your chances with the weather." Reeve smiled his picket-fence smile. "I'd rather not. I like certainty. I suspect"—and here his voice dropped into a register of such reasonable warmth that Thomas felt it land somewhere below his ribs—"you like certainty too."

Thomas looked at the pen. It was a good pen, heavy, brass fittings, the kind that cost more than the paper it signed was worth. He thought about the notebook in his coat pocket. Forty-one thousand, give or take, and the give-or-take always gave. He thought about the shop in Ashford and the letter from the bank he hadn't opened yet because opening letters was a skill he'd lost somewhere along the way, or maybe never had—he'd let their mother open everything, after their father died, even his own post, because her hands were steadier, and then she was gone too and the unopened mail had simply accumulated, a sediment of deferral, strata of brown envelopes.

Twenty-one days. Vacant possession. Which meant Edmund out, and everything in the house out, catalogued and crated and dispersed to strangers, and the blue room—

The blue room. He had not gone up there. Six weeks of cataloguing and he had photographed every room in the house except that one, had written *bedroom, contents TBC* in the inventory and left the door shut, and every morning when he counted the stair treads he counted fourteen and did not look up at the landing where the blue door stood, because the door was blue the way the sea is blue, a color that means depth, and Thomas had spent fifteen years learning to walk on surfaces.

Vacant possession in twenty-one days meant he would never have to open it. The buyers' men would open it. Strangers with gloves and crates would carry out whatever was in there, and it would become someone else's problem, someone else's inventory, someone else's grief to misfile. He could sell the room without ever entering it. He could sell the whole past the way you sell a house with a body in the walls—as long as nobody asks, as long as the survey's waived, as long as the paperwork moves fast enough, the thing stays buried and everyone gets paid.

That was the thought that frightened him: not that Reeve was offering money, but that Reeve was offering speed. Speed was the real product. Twenty-one days was not a timeline; it was an anesthetic. Sell fast and you never have to feel the selling. Bury quick and the grave doesn't get a headstone, doesn't get flowers, doesn't get a sister standing over it asking questions in her quiet terrible way, the way she'd been asking all week with her hands, lifting objects out of drawers and holding them up to the light and saying *this one matters, Thomas, this one mattered to her*—as if the house were full of testimony instead of furniture.

Because that was what Edmund was doing here. He'd watched her for a week now, moving through the rooms with that careful, haunted attention, and he knew sorting when he saw it, and this wasn't sorting. She was reading the house. She'd always been the one who read things—their mother's silences, their father's moods, the weather coming in off the marsh two hours before it arrived. And now she was reading his inventory, his beautiful flat merciful inventory, and finding the feeling underneath it, and sooner or later she would find the blue door, and he could not bear—

"Mr. Ashgrove?" Reeve said. "You've gone somewhere."

"Nowhere," Thomas said. "Nowhere at all."

* * *

Reeve left the folder on the window seat when he went. Of course he did. The pen too. Thomas stood in the back parlor after the front door closed and listened to the car start and fade down Fenwick Lane, and then the house resumed its sound, the sound it had made all his life, which was less a sound than a settling, timbers easing against each other like an old man shifting in a chair, and beneath it the tick of nothing—the grandfather clock dead on the landing, stopped at 4:17, stopped since the week after the funeral, since their mother had reached up with a key and stilled it and said *we'll wind it when there's news worth marking,* and there had never been news worth marking, not in thirty years, and the clock stood in the dark of the hallway holding 4:17 forever like a fly in amber.

Thomas picked up the folder. The figure at the bottom was still there. Eighteen percent above reserve, cash, twenty-one days. Enough to clear the lot—the Ashford mortgage, the van, the credit line, Prewitt's ghost debt—with eleven thousand left over. Eleven thousand of clean air. He tried to imagine it, the specific physical sensation of owing nothing, and found he couldn't quite; the closest he could come was the memory of the moment after a fever breaks, lying in bed as a boy, the sweat cooling, the weight lifting off the chest. That. That was what the number felt like. Health. He wanted it the way a drowning man wants a rope, without dignity, without negotiation.

He put the folder in the drawer with the batteries and the dead torches, the drawer of deferred things, and told himself he wasn't deciding, he was only storing it, and heard his own voice use their mother's cadence to lie to himself, and sat down heavily on the window seat in the dust he'd shaken off for Reeve and put his head in his hands.

Through the ceiling came a faint sound. Floorboards, upstairs. Edmund, moving from room to room, her tread light and deliberate, a person going through a house the way you go through a book you're afraid to finish. He listened to her cross the landing—past the clock, past the blue door—and stop, and the stopping lasted a long time, longer than a pause, shorter than a decision, and then the tread moved on toward the back bedrooms, and Thomas realized his heart was going hard and stupid against his ribs.

She'd stopped at the door. She'd stood at the door the way he stood at it every morning, and then she'd moved on—but she'd remember. She was building a map of this house in her head, room by room, object by object, and the blue room was the one blank space on it, and Edmund had never in her life left a blank space alone.

He took out the notebook and added the column again. Forty-one thousand, give or take. Then he wrote beneath it, in his small ruled hand, the figure from the folder, and stared at the difference between the two numbers, which was not really a difference between numbers at all. It was the distance between drowning and dry land, measured in days. Twenty-one days, and the blue room sold unseen. Twenty-one days, and the stairs counted by strangers who would never know why the fifteenth tread sang.

From upstairs, faintly, he heard Edmund's voice—low, talking to herself, or to the house, or to their mother, who knew. And then a sound he couldn't place until he did: paper. A drawer sliding. Paper, many sheets of it, being lifted out and turned over, one after another, patient as rain.

Thomas closed the notebook and went upstairs to see what she'd found.
