## 13. Appraisal

The appraiser arrived on a Tuesday that smelled of rain that never quite fell, a gray pressure sitting on the town like a lid. His name was Wendell Pryce, and he came up the walk of Fenwick Lane with a leather folio under one arm and a jeweler's loupe hanging from his neck on a black cord, so that from a distance he looked like a doctor making a house call to a patient already dead.

Edmund watched him from the front window of what had once been the morning room. She had been awake since five, lying in her childhood bed in the blue room with the unsent letters stacked beside her like a pulse she could feel through the mattress. She had read them all now—every one—and the reading had changed the house for her. It was no longer only a museum of their silence. It was an archive. And this morning a stranger was coming to put prices on it.

Thomas answered the door. She heard his voice, clipped and formal, doing the thing he did with strangers: becoming the competent brother, the one who had stayed, the one who could be trusted with a folio and a pen.

"Mr. Pryce. Thomas Ashgrove. My sister is here as well."

"I'd hoped so," Pryce said, and Edmund heard something in it—not surprise, but satisfaction, as though the house itself had been incomplete until both its children stood inside it.

She came into the hall. Pryce was a small man, sixty or thereabouts, with reading glasses pushed up into thinning white hair and hands that moved constantly, flexing, opening, as if he were already handling everything he saw. He looked at Edmund the way he would later look at the furniture: thoroughly, without flattery, and with a kind of respect.

"You'll want to be present," he said. "Both of you. People think appraisal is about value. It's not. It's about attention. I'm going to look at every object in this house and tell you what the market thinks of it. The market is a fool, but it's a fool with money, and you're entitled to know its opinion." He opened his folio. "Where shall we begin?"

"The cellar," Thomas said immediately.

* * *

Edmund followed them down the stairs into the cold, where the light came in through ground-level windows furred with moss, green-gray, underwater light. Her father's workbench still ran along the far wall, and on it, undisturbed, lay the archaeology of his last ordinary days: a jar of screws sorted by size, a hand plane with a split handle, a coffee tin full of pencils sharpened down to stubs. The smell hit her before anything else—oil and sawdust and the ghost of pipe tobacco, though he had been dead fifteen years and the tobacco longer than that.

Pryce moved along the bench like a man reading scripture aloud in a language he didn't believe in.

"Bench, pine, pre-war, two hundred, maybe less—the top's warped. Hand plane, damaged, ten pounds if someone wants it for parts. Tools, assorted—" he gestured at the pegboard with its empty hooks and shadow-outlines of tools long gone, "—negligible. The jar of screws I won't insult either of us by pricing."

Thomas wrote nothing down. Edmund noticed that. He had brought a notebook—he always brought a notebook—but his pen stayed capped, and his eyes kept going to the bench's end, to the drawer there, the shallow one, the one their father had kept locked when they were children and which had been open ever since the night he died, its contents spilled across the floor and gathered up afterward by whoever gathered such things.

Pryce opened it now without ceremony. Inside: sandpaper gone soft as cloth, a fuse or two, a rubber band that had melted into a black crescent, and a watch.

"There we are," Pryce said, and lifted it out.

* * *

It was nothing. That was the first thing. A man's wristwatch, gold-plated once, the plating worn through at the lugs to the base metal beneath, the crystal cracked in a starburst from some fall it had taken years before it stopped. The strap was replaced with a cheap brown one, the kind sold at chemists' shops, the kind their mother used to buy two at a time because he went through them. The second hand was missing entirely. The minute hand stood at twenty past four forever, which meant nothing, which meant it had simply stopped whenever it had stopped, and no one had wound it again.

Pryce turned it over. He held it to the window light. He took the loupe off its cord and screwed it to his eye and looked, and looked, and the cellar was very quiet, and Edmund became aware of her own breathing, and of Thomas beside her, absolutely still, the way he had been still his whole life whenever feeling got too close—as if stillness were a wall he could hide behind.

"Case back's inscribed," Pryce said at last. He read it aloud, flatly, professionally: "'To R., from M. All my time.'" He lowered the loupe. "Sentimental engraving. Reduces resale value, ironically. Buyers don't want another man's love on the inside of a case."

"How much?" Thomas said. His voice came out strange, stripped.

Pryce weighed the watch in his palm. "As scrap? As an object? Five pounds. Perhaps eight to a collector of mid-century pieces, if the movement could be restored, which it can't—the mainspring's seized, the balance staff is likely broken. It's a dead watch, Mr. Ashgrove. It's worth almost nothing." He set it down on the bench, gently, next to the jar of screws. "And yet you're both looking at it as though I've just appraised a child."

Neither of them answered. The green light came through the little windows. Somewhere above them a pipe ticked as the house adjusted to the weather.

"It's my father's watch," Edmund said finally, and heard how inadequate it was, how the sentence contained none of it—none of the weight of the wrist it had circled, none of the sound it must have made ticking against a steering wheel, against a banister, against the chest of a man shouting, none of the fact that it had stopped at twenty past four on a night in November and no one in this family had ever said out loud whether that was before or after.

"Yes," Pryce said. "I know whose it is. I've done probate work in this county for thirty years, Miss Ashgrove. I knew your father slightly. He bought his paint from my brother-in-law." He closed the drawer with two fingers. "Here is what I will tell you, and then I will say no more about it, because it isn't my business and I've made it my business too often in my life. In thirty years I have learned that houses are full of objects worth five pounds that are worth everything, and objects worth everything that are worth five pounds, and the whole profession consists of pretending I can tell the difference. I can't. Nobody can. The market will take this house apart in six weeks and price every stick of it, and the sticks will go to strangers, and the strangers will never know what any of it weighed." He picked up his folio. "Shall we do the upstairs?"

But as they climbed the stairs, Edmund glanced back, and saw that the drawer had not quite shut—or that Thomas, behind them both, had opened it again.

* * *

Thomas catalogued the rest of the morning in his head rather than in the notebook, because writing things down had begun to feel like a lie. Numbers had been his scaffolding for fifteen years. Every object in this house had a figure attached to it in his mind, and the figures had held him upright the way rebar holds concrete: invisible, load-bearing, cold. Dining table, walnut, Victorian, three hundred. Mother's sewing machine, forty. The good china, service for twelve, one hundred and eighty if the pattern was complete, and it wasn't, because Edmund had broken six plates the summer she was fourteen and their mother had laughed about it, actually laughed, and Thomas had carried the arithmetic of those six plates for three decades without knowing why until this week.

Now the numbers were failing him. Pryce had priced the watch at five pounds and Thomas had felt something crack in the load-bearing wall.

He had come downstairs that morning intending to get through the appraisal efficiently, to keep Edmund at a professional distance, to protect himself from whatever she had found in the blue room—because he knew she had found something; he had seen the letters' absence from the box where his mother had hidden them, and he had said nothing, and the saying-nothing sat in him like a swallowed stone. He had intended to be the competent brother for four hours and then see Pryce out and pour a drink and count something, anything, spoons or stairs or the seconds between lightning and thunder.

Instead he was standing in his parents' bedroom while a stranger lifted the vanity mirror and said, "Beveled glass, original, lovely piece, hundred and fifty," and Thomas was thinking: *five pounds*. Five pounds for the watch that had been on his father's wrist the night—

He made himself not finish the thought. He had gotten very good, over fifteen years, at not finishing thoughts. It was a skill, like the counting. You approached the edge of the thing and you turned back and you tallied the china instead.

But the watch was in the cellar, in the drawer, and the drawer did not lock anymore, and Pryce had left it lying on the bench among the screws like a coin dropped from a pocket, and all through the upstairs rooms—all through the water stains on the ceiling he priced at "deduction from lot" and the wardrobe he called "good solid pine, two hundred, easily"—Thomas kept seeing it there. Twenty past four. All his time.

His mother's handwriting, engraved inside the case. *All my time.* She had given him her time and he had spent it drinking, and she had kept giving it, buying him watch straps two at a time at the chemist's, and Thomas had stood in that shop once, age eleven, holding the cellophane packet while she paid, and the shopkeeper had said, "Husband hard on them, is he?" and his mother had smiled and said, "You have no idea," in a voice so pleasant and so bottomless that Thomas had understood, even then, even at eleven, that there were entire countries inside a person that no one visited.

"Mr. Ashgrove?"

He surfaced. Pryce was looking at him over the folio. Edmund was looking at him too, and her look was different—sharper, more afraid.

"The attic," Pryce said. "Or shall we call it a day? I have enough for a preliminary schedule."

"The attic," Thomas said.

* * *

The attic was hot and low and smelled of insulation and old paper, and it held the things no one had wanted in any room: boxes of schoolwork, a christening gown gone ivory with age, a bicycle with a rusted chain, Christmas decorations in a box marked XMAS in their mother's block capitals. Pryce worked quickly here, assigning the world its prices. "Textiles, nominal. Bicycle, salvage. Decorations—" he paused over the box, over a glass bauble the color of deep water that Edmund remembered hanging on the tree every year of her childhood, that she had hung herself at nine, at twelve, at sixteen, standing on the rung of a chair while her father steadied the chair with one hand and held his drink with the other, "—nominal."

Nominal. Edmund turned the word over. It came from *name*, she thought. From naming. As if to name a thing were to make it small.

By early afternoon they had walked the whole house, and Pryce stood in the hall with his folio full of figures and summed it up the way a priest sums up a life at a funeral: kindly, briefly, inaccurately. "Altogether, contents plus fixtures, you might realize four thousand pounds at auction, perhaps five if the furniture draws the right crowd. The house itself is the real asset, of course, and the house is land, and the land is worth what the developer will pay for it, and what the developer will pay has nothing to do with any of this." He snapped the folio shut. "That's the truth of it. Forgive me."

He shook Thomas's hand. He shook Edmund's. At the door he paused and said, quietly, not quite to either of them: "The watch. If it means what I think it means, don't let it go into Lot 47 with the miscellany. Miscellany goes in boxes. Boxes go to car boot sales. Car boots go anywhere." Then he was down the walk and into his car and gone, and the rain that had threatened all day finally began, fine and silver, ticking softly on the porch roof like a second hand that hadn't stopped after all.

* * *

They ate lunch without discussing it—bread and cheese at the kitchen table, the table Pryce had valued at ninety pounds—and the rain came properly now, drumming, washing the windows clean in slow sheets. Edmund washed up. Thomas dried. They had not done this together since they were children, and neither remarked on it, and the not-remarked-on-ness of it filled the kitchen like a third person.

It was Thomas who finally spoke, staring at the towel in his hands.

"Pryce said five pounds."

"He did."

"For a thing that—" Thomas stopped. Started again, lower. "Mum wore his watch after he died. Did you know that? For about a year. She'd wind it every morning at the stove. I used to watch her do it. She'd hold it up to her ear first, like she was listening for something, and then she'd wind it, and put it in her apron pocket, and that was that. She never wore it. Just wound it." He set the towel down with great precision, aligning its edges with the counter's. "Then one year she stopped winding it. I asked her why. She said, 'Some things you have to let stop, Tommy.' And I thought she meant the watch."

Edmund stood with her hands wet in the sink, the rain loud on the glass, and heard underneath his words the shape of everything he wasn't saying—the same shape she had spent a week learning from the letters, the shape of that November night, the shape of a secret with two custodians. Her mother had known. Her mother had chosen. And Thomas—

"Thomas," she said carefully, "the night Dad died. You were home."

The towel's edges were aligned perfectly. He did not look at her. Outside, the rain fell on Fenwick Lane, on the garden where the apple tree still stood, on the whole drowned gray world, and somewhere down the road a car slowed—she heard it, distantly, an engine easing past the house and stopping, though she paid it no attention then—and Thomas said, in a voice like a man stepping off a ledge:

"Edmund, there's something I have to tell you, and you're not going to—"

But the doorbell rang, insistent, cheerful, twice, cutting the moment cleanly in two, and when Thomas went to answer it, the man on the step was smiling, umbrellaed, and holding out a card that read, in confident gold lettering: **CALLUM REEVE — REEVE DEVELOPMENTS — "BUILDING TOMORROW'S MARLOW BEND."**
