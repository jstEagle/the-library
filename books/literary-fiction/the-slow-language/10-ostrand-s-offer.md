## 10. Ostrand's Offer

The trouble with old neighborhoods, Ostrand had learned in thirty years of buying them, was that they were full of people who thought a house was a person. They wept over floorboards. They talked to trees. They stood in doorways with their arms crossed as if their grief were a legal instrument. It was never a legal instrument. What was a legal instrument was a signature, a notary stamp, a filing date—and Ostrand had a filing date, and it was eleven weeks out, and everything between now and then was sentiment to be managed.

He managed sentiment the way he managed everything: with excellent coffee, a beautiful office, and the appearance of infinite patience.

The office was on the fourteenth floor, glass on two sides, and he had chosen the hour deliberately—late afternoon, when the light came in low and gold and made even the demolition renderings on the far wall look like paintings. The renderings were good work. The tower rose out of the block like a held breath, all glass and pale stone, and at its base, in the artist's merciful imagination, the old Ishikawa house had been erased so cleanly that no one looking at the image would ever know something had stood there. Ostrand had approved that version over two others. The other renderings had kept a fragment of the house—a chimney, a gable—out of some designer's notion of memory. Memory, in Ostrand's experience, was a negotiating position, and you did not concede it before the first offer.

Yara Ishikawa arrived eleven minutes late, which he noted with approval rather than irritation. Late people were unsettled people, and unsettled people signed faster. She came in with rain still beaded on her shoulders and a canvas toolbag over one arm, and she looked around the office the way he'd seen her type look around rooms—not at the art, not at the view, but at the baseboards. He'd done his research. The piano tuner. The lodger. The woman who, according to Ren Ishikawa, had never once in her adult life made a decision larger than which tuning fork to carry.

"Mr. Ostrand." Her handshake was brief and dry. Tuner's hands. He'd expected that.

"Ms. Ishikawa. Thank you for coming. Can I get you anything? The espresso machine is the one indulgence I permit myself."

"Water is fine."

He poured it himself, which he always did for the difficult meetings—there was a kind of surrender in a rich man carrying a glass of water, and surrender disarmed people. She took it and did not sit until he did, and he filed that away too. Careful. Observant. The uncle had undersold her, which meant the uncle was frightened, which meant the uncle was useful.

"I'll be direct," Ostrand said, "because I suspect you prefer it. My firm is acquiring the parcel. Your uncle has already agreed in principle. The house comes down in the spring, and the tower—" he gestured at the rendering, letting the light do its sentimental work on the glass— "goes up by the following winter. What that means for you is a question of timing and dignity, and timing and dignity are exactly what I'm here to offer."

"Dignity," she repeated. Not hostile. Testing the word the way she might test a note, pressing it to hear if it rang true.

"A settlement well above market. A two-bedroom in the Meridian—corner unit, west light, the building everyone in this city pretends they don't want to live in. Moving costs covered. And my personal assurance that the process will be quiet. No agents tramping through your grandmother's rooms with clipboards. No open house. No strangers measuring her kitchen." He let that sit. He had learned that the fastest way to a holdout's signature was to describe the alternative so precisely that refusing felt like inviting the cruelty personally. "You could be out before the frost. Or you could wait for the appraisers, the surveyors, the title people, and the notices stapled to the door. Your choice. But it's the same ending either way, and one of the paths is kind."

She drank her water. She looked at the rendering for a long moment, and he watched her do the thing he'd been told she did—listening, somehow, to something. He had a brief, irrational image of her hearing the building behind her, forty stories down and half a mile away, the way a doctor hears a heart through a stethoscope.

"The frost is six weeks off," she said.

"Seven, if we're lucky." He smiled. "Time moves faster in contracts."

* * *

He had prepared the folder the way he prepared everything—beautifully. Heavy paper, real photographs of the Meridian unit rather than renders, the offer letter on the first page with the number large enough to read from across a desk. He slid it across the glass and watched her open it, and watched the number land. It landed. He saw it in the small stillness of her hands, the way she pressed her thumbnail against the folder's edge. Good. The number was generous because generosity was cheap. The land under the house was worth forty times the offer once the parcel was assembled, and the parcel was assembled except for one signature.

"There's a condition," she said.

"One condition." He leaned back, open-palmed, the posture of a man with nothing to hide. "We close quickly. Thirty days. Before the new year." Before, he did not say, any title search. He let the silence do that work, and the silence was expert.

"Why thirty days?"

"Financing tranches. Construction windows. The bank's calendar, not mine." All true, in the sense that all his sentences were true the way a well-tuned piano is in tune—technically, note by note, with the whole designed to deceive. "Ms. Ishikawa, I've built in this city for thirty years. Every delay I've ever eaten came out of some family's patience, not mine. I'd rather pay you well and move fast than pay you the same and make you live through a winter of surveys."

She turned a page. He watched her read the way she presumably read everything—thoroughly, slowly, her finger tracking the margin. He had time. He poured himself an espresso he didn't want, to give his hands something elegant to do.

"You knew my grandmother," she said, not looking up.

It was not a question, which was itself an answer to a question he hadn't asked her, and Ostrand felt the first genuine flicker of interest he'd felt in weeks. "I knew of her," he said carefully. "Everyone in this district knew of the Ishikawa house. It's been the last holdout on that block since before I was born."

"That's not what I asked."

He set down the cup. There it was. Not the grief, not the money—the question underneath, the one the uncle hadn't mentioned because the uncle didn't know she'd ask it, or didn't know she could ask it. He considered his answers the way he considered renderings: three versions, and he chose the one with the chimney removed.

"My firm did a preliminary survey of the parcel four years ago," he said. "Standard practice. We look at every property we might someday want. Deeds, easements, encumbrances." He paused, and let the pause be just long enough. "Your grandmother's file was thick."

"Thick."

"Old. There were documents in it from the forties. Handwritten amendments. A deed that had been—revised." He watched her face with the attention of a man who had spent his career watching faces at the moment numbers were read aloud. She didn't flinch. She went still, the way water goes still before it freezes, and he understood suddenly that he was not negotiating with a lodger. He was negotiating with someone standing in a drafty house listening to its pipes.

"Revised," she said.

"Re-recorded. Amended. The language doesn't matter. What matters is that a file like that takes time to work through, and time is the one thing my schedule doesn't have." He spread his hands, the picture of candor. "I'm telling you this because I'd rather you hear it from me than from a lawyer you're paying two hundred dollars an hour to tell you less. The house has a complicated past. Complicated pasts make for slow closings. My offer is designed to make all of it—every page of that file, every question anyone could ever ask about that land—irrelevant. You sign, you're paid, the parcel is clean, and nobody has to dig through 1946 ever again."

He heard it as he said it—how it sounded, laid out in the gold light like that. He had meant it to sound like mercy. It sounded, for one unguarded sentence, like what it was. He recovered with the smile, the open hands, the tilt of the head. "I'm a developer, Ms. Ishikawa. I'm not a historian. I buy land. Whatever happened on it before I owned it is a story, and stories don't hold up towers."

* * *

She didn't sign. He had known she wouldn't—not today, not in his office, not with the number still ringing in her ears. The uncle would have signed in the elevator. The niece took the folder, thanked him in a voice with no temperature in it at all, and stood, and at the door she stopped.

"You said revised," she said. "Whose signature was on the revision?"

And there it was, the question he had been managing since the survey came across his desk four years ago in a folder with a coffee ring on the cover, the question he had hoped the uncle's urgency would bury before it was ever asked. He could have lied. He was good at lying; it was the least of his skills. But he had learned—late, expensively, in a lawsuit in his forties that had cost him a year and a marriage—that a lie told to someone who was going to find out anyway was not a shield but a debt, and Ostrand did not carry debts. He carried leverage.

"Her own," he said. "Your grandmother's."

The door closed behind her with the soft, expensive click of good hardware, and Ostrand stood alone in the gold light with the untouched espresso and felt the peculiar satisfaction of a man who has just shown someone the cliff edge and watched them look down. Fear made people sign. But so did grief, and so did guilt, and the niece had walked out of his office carrying all three like a toolbag over her shoulder.

He picked up his phone and dialed Ren Ishikawa, who answered on the first ring, because Ren always answered on the first ring now, the way men do when they owe money to people who do not make friendly calls.

"She didn't sign," Ostrand said. "But she asked about the deed. The forty-six deed, by name." He listened. He watched the rendering across the room, the tower rising out of nothing, the block beneath it already erased in the artist's ink. "No. Don't go at her with family. She's past family—you people made sure of that years ago." He listened again, longer, and something in the voice on the other end made him stop pacing.

"Wait," he said. "What do you mean, she has the letters?"
