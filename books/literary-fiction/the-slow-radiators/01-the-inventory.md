## 1. The Inventory

The contract was in Dutch, which meant Jonah Dunmore could translate it in his sleep, and some mornings it felt as though he had. Clause 14.3: *the carrier shall not be liable for spoilage of perishables resulting from delays attributable to weather.* He typed it out in clean, bloodless English — *weather* — and thought about how many languages he owned the word for rain in, and how few of them still lived in his mouth.

Thirty years a translator. Contracts mostly, lately; once upon a time, poetry, when poetry paid anything at all, which was never. The port city had been full of tongues when he was young — the fish market alone ran six languages before dawn — but the ships got bigger and the crews got smaller and the tongues thinned out like the herring. Now his work arrived by email from Rotterdam or Antwerp, and his mother's language arrived nowhere at all.

He finished clause 14.4, saved the file, and did what he did every day at four o'clock, which was to open the bottom drawer of his desk and take out the notebook.

It was nothing fancy. A school exercise book, the kind with the marbled cover, bought in bulk sometime in the nineties. On the first page, in his own handwriting, younger and more certain than he felt now, he had written: *Things I Never Said to Her.*

The list ran eleven years deep. Some entries were whole paragraphs. Some were single words. Entry 1: *I'm home.* Because she died on a Tuesday while he was at the airport picking up Maren from a semester abroad, and by the time he got to the hospital there was nothing left in the room to say things to, only machines saying things to each other in their own language, which was beeping.

Entry 2: *Thank you for the coat.* She'd made him a wool coat when he was nineteen, enormous and gray, the color of weather itself, and he'd complained about it in front of his friends and worn it secretly for twenty years after she stopped asking whether he liked it.

Entry 9: *Sing it again.*

That one he'd written late at night, and he could not now remember which song he'd meant, which was its own small grief — that even his regrets were losing their specificity, going soft at the edges like fruit left too long.

Jonah read down the list the way other men checked the racing results. Not to win anything. Just to see the numbers.

*Entry 31: I stayed because of you.*
*Entry 32: I left because of you.*
*Entry 33: Both of those are true and I never sorted them out.*
*Entry 44: Your language is dying in me and I let it.*

He had written that last one three years ago, after a dream in which his mother stood in their old kitchen on Kessler Street asking him to pass the salt, and in the dream he'd answered her in English, and she'd looked at him the way you look at a stranger wearing your son's face. He woke up with his mouth full of a word he couldn't hold onto — something round, something warm, the word for the particular light through a kitchen window in late afternoon — and by breakfast it was gone, and he understood that he would spend the rest of his life losing words at exactly this rate, one per dream, until there was nothing left but English, which was a fine language, a useful language, a language in which he could say *clause 14.4* without any trouble at all.

The doorbell rang at five.

Maren had a key but never used it, a fact he found both touching and mildly insulting, as though his house were a country she respected the borders of. He went down the hall opening the drawer on the way — the notebook back in its place, under the spare printer cartridges, where no one would ever look, least of all him, except daily.

"You look terrible," Maren said, kissing his cheek. She was thirty-four and had her grandmother's stubborn chin and her father's habit of arriving unannounced and calling it a visit.

"Thank you. I've been cultivating it."

"New contract?"

"Dutch. Frozen herring liability." He held up the printout like a fishmonger displaying the catch. "You have no idea how much money changes hands over fish that goes slightly wrong."

"I have some idea. You tell me every time."

"It's a good number this week. You'll want to sit down."

She sat down. She always took the chair by the window, the one her grandmother had reupholstered twice and Jonah had refused to touch since, so that the fabric wore through in the shape of one specific person's sitting, which is a strange kind of portrait but a true one. Maren settled into it and the chair seemed, briefly, satisfied.

"I brought soup," she said, producing a container from her bag. "Lentil. Don't make the face."

"I wasn't making a face."

"You were making the face you make when someone brings you food, which is the face of a man being told he's loved against his will."

"That is not a real expression."

"It's your most common one."

She went to the kitchen to decant the soup, and Jonah stood in the middle of his living room feeling the peculiar ache of being known. Above the stove — her stove, he'd brought it to two apartments and finally here, though the tiles around it were new — there was a gap where a clock used to hang. He'd taken the clock down eleven years ago and never found a replacement, and now the pale rectangle on the wall marked the hours better than any clock: it said *before*, and everything else in the room said *after*.

They ate the soup. It was good. He told her so without making the face, and she acknowledged this with a nod, like a referee.

Then she put down her spoon and asked the question.

"Why didn't you ever teach me?"

Jonah knew immediately which question it was, because there was only one question in the world shaped like that — quiet, careful, set down between them like a cup that might spill.

"Teach you what?" he said, buying time, though he knew, and she knew he knew.

"The language. Grandma's language. You speak it — spoke it. I heard you on the phone with her once, when I was little. You sounded like a different person. Like the words fit better." She turned her spoon over. "And then one day I realized I didn't know a single word of it. Not one. You taught me French, for God's sake. Useless French. I can order a croissant in Paris and that's it."

"An excellent croissant, though."

"Dad."

"A flawless croissant. A croissant with a future."

"Dad." But she was smiling despite herself, which was the trouble with Maren — she inherited his deflections and recognized them mid-flight. "Why?"

He looked at his soup. There were answers available, the real ones, the ones in the notebook in the desk drawer upstairs. *Because every word I gave you would have been a word I couldn't protect you with. Because the language was tied to a neighborhood that was already coming apart, and a father we don't discuss, and a version of me that didn't survive learning English well enough. Because I thought if I handed you nothing, nothing could be taken from you.* Because silence had seemed, for thirty years, like a roof over her head rather than a wall between them.

Instead he said: "Because you'd have corrected my accent."

Maren waited. She was good at waiting; she'd learned it from him, ironically, from all those years of watching him decline to say things.

"That's the joke," she said finally. "That's the joke you're offering me instead of an answer."

"It's a very good joke. I've been workshopping it for decades."

"Dad." Softer now. "Do you still... do you still have it? In you? Any of it?"

And here was the thing he could not explain, sitting across from his daughter in the kitchen where the clock used to be: that the language was still in him the way water is still in a stone, invisibly, structurally, having shaped everything about him including the shape of its own absence. That he dreamed in it sometimes and woke grieving. That when he read the shipping contracts, certain numbers came to him first in her voice, because she'd taught him to count before anyone taught him anything in English, and counting was the last room of the house he still lived in.

"Some of it," he said. "Enough to embarrass myself."

"Embarrass yourself, then. Say something. Anything."

He opened his mouth. And this was the moment — he would think about it later, that night, standing at the drawer — this was the exact moment when the word he wanted rose up in him, round and warm, the word for the light through a kitchen window, the word from the dream, and he reached for it the way you reach for a handrail in the dark —

— and found English waiting there instead, punctual, fluent, useless.

"Hello," he said. "Hello, that's what I've got. Thirty years of professional translation and I can offer you hello."

Maren laughed, because he'd made it funny, because making it funny was the family trade, older than translation, older than the port. But she watched him a second longer than laughter required, and he saw in her face — her grandmother's chin, her own mind working behind it — that she had filed the moment away somewhere, the way he filed clauses, the way he filed unsaid things.

She left at eight, taking the empty soup container and refusing a ride to the station. At the door she hugged him, hard, unexpectedly, the way she'd done when she was small and afraid of thunder.

"Think about it," she said into his shoulder. "Not for me. For you."

Then the door closed, and the apartment did what it always did after she left, which was to become larger and quieter, a harbor at low tide.

Jonah climbed the stairs to the study. He did not turn on the lamp. He opened the bottom drawer, took out the marbled notebook, and wrote, by the light of the streetlamp through the window:

*Entry 112: Why I never taught her. (Answer pending. Eleven years and counting.)*

He looked at the entry for a long time. Then, beneath it, without deciding to, his hand added:

*Entry 113: Sing it again.*

He stared at that. He hadn't written that one since the beginning. His pen hovered, wanting to cross it out, wanting to ask it questions. Which song? When? Who would sing it, now, with her eleven years gone and the kitchen tiles sold off with the house?

He put the notebook away, under the printer cartridges, and went to bed.

At 11:47 that night, half-asleep in front of the radio he kept on for company, Jonah Dunmore heard a piano begin a phrase he knew the way he knew his own heartbeat — and then a voice, low and unhurried, singing a song composed in a kitchen on Kessler Street by a woman who had sworn, laughing, hand flat on the table, that no one would ever record it.

He sat up in the dark.

His mouth, before his mind could stop it, filled with the next line — in her language, whole, intact, alive — and he sat there trembling, listening to a dead woman's song sung by a stranger's voice, while the announcer, in easy English, prepared to tell him who she was.
