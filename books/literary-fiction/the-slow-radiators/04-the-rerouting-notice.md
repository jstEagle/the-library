## 4. The Rerouting Notice

The notice was laminated, which struck Jonah as the cruelest part of it. Lamination is what you do to things you intend to keep — birth certificates, recipes, the small card of a saint tucked into a wallet. The transit authority had laminated its announcement of erasure, as if to say: *this decision is permanent and we are proud of it.*

He found it taped inside the shelter at the Kessler Street terminus on a Tuesday morning in late October, when he had gone there for no reason he could defend to himself except that his feet had made the argument without consulting him. The 14 bus. He hadn't ridden it in years — he drove now, or walked, or arranged his life so carefully around the old neighborhood that he never once had to pass through it. But the route itself still existed. That had always been a comfort, the way knowing a door is unlocked is a comfort even when you don't open it.

*Effective November 14th,* the notice read, in a font chosen by someone who hated both fonts and people, *Route 14 will be permanently rerouted via the Harborline Express corridor. Service to Kessler Street, Aldous Avenue, and adjacent stops will be discontinued.*

Discontinued. As if the street were a product line. As if eleven years of not going somewhere could be converted, administratively, into no longer being able to.

Jonah stood in the shelter reading the notice three times, because the first two times he'd been reading something else underneath it — his mother's voice, saying *Kessler Street* the way she said it, with the stress on the second syllable, the word rolling slightly downhill. Kes-SLER. In English it came out flat, KEH-sler, a word with no topography. He had spent thirty years flattening words for a living. It seemed suddenly monstrous that the city was doing it too, and doing it to streets.

A woman with a wheeled shopping cart stopped beside him and read over his shoulder.

"About time," she said. "That bus takes forever."

"It takes exactly as long as it needs to," Jonah said, before he could stop himself.

The woman looked at him the way one looks at a man who has just defended a bus. She rattled off down the ramp toward whatever the Harborline corridor would offer her instead, and Jonah stayed, hands in his coat pockets, watching the empty road where the 14 would arrive in — he checked the schedule bolted to the pole — nine minutes.

Nine minutes. He decided to wait. There was no reason to wait. He told himself this clearly, the way he told himself many clear things that changed nothing.

* * *

The 14 arrived at 7:42, groaning, half-empty, smelling of diesel and the particular industrial pine scent the transit authority used to suggest cleanliness without achieving it. The driver was a broad-shouldered young man with a beard that suggested he was growing it against his will, under contract.

"You getting on?" the driver asked.

"I'm deciding," Jonah said.

"Decide fast. Schedule's a schedule."

And there it was again — the theme of the season, apparently. Schedules. Deadlines. The city had begun moving like a translation project with a due date, everything being converted from one thing into another whether it consented or not. Three weeks from tomorrow, this route would simply stop existing, and the streets it served would become places you could only reach by car, by foot, by deliberate pilgrimage — which was to say, places most people would quietly stop reaching at all.

Jonah got on. He paid his fare in exact change, coins counted out of a jar he kept for parking meters, and took the third seat from the back on the left side, because that had been his seat when he was nine years old and the seat still knew it. Buses remember. Not officially — there's nothing in the maintenance manual about memory — but the vinyl had a particular give at that spot, worn into the shape of decades of other people's waiting, and Jonah fit into it like a hand into a glove left out in the sun.

The route unspooled. Harbor Road, where the fish market had become a wine bar with Edison bulbs. The viaduct, where the pigeons had not changed at all, pigeons being the last honest citizens of any city. Then the turn onto Aldous Avenue, and Jonah felt it happen in his chest — that slow drop, like an elevator starting — as the windows began showing him things he had spent eleven years not looking at.

There was the corner store where Mr. Havel had sold him penny candy and corrected his grammar in two languages simultaneously, a feat of bilingual pedagogy that should have been studied. There was the laundromat with the broken clock frozen at 4:15, frozen, Jonah realized with a small shock, since at least 1989. There was the hill where his mother had taught him the word for *wind*, standing with her coat open, arms out, saying the word twice — once soft, once thrown into the air like something she wanted the wind itself to catch.

He did not say the word now. He noticed that he did not say it, and he noticed how quickly the noticing passed, and how smoothly the silence closed over the place where the word should have gone, the way water closes over a stone.

At Kessler Street the bus stopped. Nobody got on or off. The driver looked at him in the mirror.

"This is your stop?"

"No," Jonah said. "Yes. I don't know."

"That's usually a yes," the driver said, with the patience of a man who had heard every possible answer to every possible question and had distilled them all into a kind of weary philosophy. "Door's right there whenever you're ready."

Jonah didn't get off. He rode the loop back. He watched the whole route run in reverse, all the way to the depot, and then sat there while the driver looked at him again in the mirror, and then he got off, and stood on the curb, and understood that he had just done something strange: he had ridden to the edge of his own past and then turned around at the door of it, politely, the way you decline a party you're already standing inside.

He started walking home. Halfway up Meridian he stopped at Dov's shop — the record shop, defunct in every official sense but still open four afternoons a week, because Dov had decided long ago that bankruptcy was a technicality and music was not. Dov was behind the counter alphabetizing records nobody would buy, which was his form of prayer.

"You look terrible," Dov said warmly. This was affection, in Dov's dialect.

"I rode the 14," Jonah said.

Dov set down a record he'd been holding — something Czech, judging by the cover art of a sad accordion — and gave Jonah his full attention, which was a physical event, like a lamp coming on. "All of it?"

"All of it. Both directions."

"And?"

"And they're rerouting it. Three weeks. There's a laminated notice."

Dov said a word in the old language that Jonah understood perfectly and would not have been able to translate under oath. That was the strange arithmetic of it now: comprehension intact, production failing. He could understand everything and say almost nothing, like a man locked outside his own house with all the lights on inside.

"They laminated it," Jonah added.

"Of course they laminated it." Dov came around the counter. "Listen. When my mother died, they tore down her building within the year. Not because it was unsafe. Because it was *available*. The city has a nose for grief, Jonah. It can smell where people aren't looking anymore, and it moves in." He put a hand on Jonah's shoulder. "You're looking now, though. Aren't you? Since the radio."

Since the radio. Since Vera Olem's voice coming through the speaker at two in the morning singing a song that existed nowhere on paper, nowhere on tape, nowhere but in a kitchen on Kessler Street thirty years ago — and in the throat of a woman Jonah had never met, who had somehow been entrusted with it. He had not yet answered her message. *Come hear the rest of the song if you can name what's missing.* He had played it eleven times. He had written *what's missing* in his notebook and stared at it until the words lost meaning, which, as a translator, he recognized as a professional hazard and a personal symptom.

"I'm looking," Jonah said.

"Then ride the bus," Dov said. "Every day, if you have to. Until the last one. Some doors you have to walk through while they're still hanging on their hinges. After that it's just an opening in a wall, and wind comes through it, and it isn't a door anymore."

* * *

So he began visiting the terminus daily.

It became a ritual with the accidental architecture of ritual: same bench, same time, seven-thirty in the morning, coffee in a thermos that leaked if held at the wrong angle, so that he learned to hold it at precisely the right angle, which felt like a metaphor he declined to examine. He watched the 14 come in at 7:42 and leave at 7:44. Two minutes at the end of the line. The driver — bearded, philosophical — began to recognize him and eventually stopped asking if he was getting on, which Jonah appreciated more than he could say. There is a great mercy in being allowed to wait somewhere without having to explain the waiting.

What was he watching for? He tried to articulate it to Maren on the phone Wednesday night, and got as far as "it's like watching a tide go out" before realizing the tide metaphor was doing more work than he was.

"A tide," Maren repeated. She had her mother's gift for repeating a sentence in a way that revealed it as nonsense. "You're watching a bus like a tide."

"The route goes out a little further every day," Jonah said. "First they'll cut the early runs. Then the Sunday service. I read the phasing schedule. They do it gradually so nobody riots. You wake up one morning and the thing that carried you everywhere is just — gone, and everyone acts like it was always temporary."

Maren was quiet for a moment. Then, gently, in the tone she used when she was about to be kinder than he deserved: "Dad. Is this about the bus?"

"No," Jonah said.

"Is it about Grandma's song?"

"...Partially."

"Is it about the fact that you haven't called this Vera woman back?"

Jonah held the thermos at its correct angle and watched steam rise from the cup lid. Across the street, the 14 pulled in, right on time, faithful as a dog.

"She asked me to name what's missing," he said. "In the song. And Mare — here's the thing. I've been keeping a notebook for years. Everything I never said to your grandmother. It's a long list. And I thought, surely, somewhere in thirty years of unsaid things, there's a verse. There has to be. But every time I get close to it, the words—" He stopped. Started again. "They keep turning into English. Mid-sentence. Like they're embarrassed."

Maren was quiet again. When she spoke, her voice had shifted into a register he associated with her childhood fevers, when she'd been brave and small at once.

"Dad. Come to dinner Sunday. Bring the notebook."

"The notebook is private."

"I know. Bring it anyway. You can hold it closed the whole time if you want. That's very on-brand for you."

He laughed despite himself — a real laugh, surprised out of him, the kind that makes a man on a bench look briefly like someone else. Down the block, the 14 sighed shut its doors and pulled away toward the harbor, carrying strangers past his mother's street, and Jonah raised his thermos to it slightly, a toast between old acquaintances.

Three weeks. Nineteen days, now. He counted them the way you count anything you're losing: precisely, and against your will.

That night he took out the notebook — the inventory, the ledger of unsaid things — and turned to the last page, where he had copied out Vera's message word for word. *If you can name what's missing.* Below it, in fresh ink, he wrote a single line:

*Day one. The tide is going out. Learn to swim in the other direction.*

Then he crossed it out, because it was terrible, and beneath it wrote the only honest sentence he'd managed in weeks:

*I know what's missing. I've known all along. I just can't say it yet — and in nineteen days, neither will anyone else.*

He closed the notebook. Outside, far off, he could hear the 14 on its final loop, the engine note thinning into the dark like a voice going away down a long street — and he made himself a promise he wasn't sure he could keep: that tomorrow, he wouldn't just watch it come in.

Tomorrow, he'd find out where it went after the last stop.
