## 9. Renn Harrow Comes In From the Rain

The rain came in off the harbor around eleven, the kind that didn't fall so much as arrive sideways, and the library filled with the smell of wet wool and radiator dust. Ansel liked rain nights. Rain nights thinned the regulars to the true believers—Dev off shift, boots steaming by the biography stacks; the man from periodicals, whose name she still didn't know and had stopped needing to, asleep with his cheek on a 1997 issue of *The Atlantic*; a graduate student who had been trying to understand Hegel for so long that Ansel suspected he'd begun to enjoy the suffering.

It was the kind of night where nothing happened, which was Ansel's favorite kind. Nothing was a discipline. Nothing was a craft she'd been perfecting for seven years.

At 11:42, the front doors sighed open and let in a man with an umbrella he hadn't bothered to close properly, shaking it with the cheerful violence of someone who had never once been cold in his life.

"Christ," he said, delighted, to the room in general. "It's biblical out there."

Ansel looked up from the returns cart and the floor did something strange. It didn't move. It *tilted*—the way a boat tilts, the way a room tilts in the half-second before you decide whether you're going to be sick. She put one hand flat on the cart's metal edge and waited for the world to level out.

It was him. Older. Softer around the jaw, the way money softens men, silver coming in at the temples like he'd ordered it. But the same walk—loose-hipped, unhurried, the walk of a person who had never once entered a room wondering if he was welcome in it. Renn Harrow. The landlord's son. The man who had knelt in a stairwell with a screwdriver and a pair of wire cutters while a building full of sleeping people trusted him, and who had looked up—she would swear to her grave he had looked up—and seen her at the window across the alley.

He didn't recognize her. Of course he didn't. She had been a shape in a window, a woman in a doorway, a sister in a funeral pew. She had spent seven years being no one at all, and the work had held.

"Evening," he said, arriving at the desk, dripping. "You're open late. I love that. There's something so civilized about a place that stays open past a reasonable hour." He said it like he was giving the building a compliment it should be grateful for.

"Until two," Ansel said. Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it. Somewhere behind her sternum her heart was performing a drum solo, but the voice—seven years of practice, the voice of a woman who had unlearned finishing sentences—held.

"Great. Great." He was looking past her at the shelves, at the reading room, at the radiator hissing under the window where Mirela usually sat, though Mirela wasn't in tonight—Tuesdays she stayed home, she'd said, for her legs, which was a lie Ansel had let stand because everyone was entitled to their small mercies. "Actually, I'm not really here for a book. I'm here to look at the building. We're doing—" he made a vague, expansive gesture that seemed to encompass the roof, the foundation, and the concept of architecture itself, "—an assessment. Harrow Properties. You may have seen the notices."

"I've seen the notices."

"Right, right." He leaned an elbow on the desk, and Ansel registered, with a strange clinical detachment, that he smelled like expensive cologne and rain and, faintly, underneath it, like nothing at all. Like a man with no history. "You know what's funny? Everybody acts like we're the villains. Demolish the old library, boo, hiss. But this block is dying. You can see it. Half these storefronts are empty. We're going to put up something the neighborhood actually *needs*." He smiled, and it was a good smile, it had clearly been to a professional about it. "Mixed-use. Ground-floor retail, some affordable units—we do the affordable thing, it's a whole program. And a community space. Maybe even a library. A *real* one. No offense."

"None taken," said the part of Ansel that had spent seven years practicing almost-saying. The other part of her, the part that had once been a girl who finished sentences, who laughed too loud at her brother's terrible jokes, who had stood at a window and watched and done nothing—that part was screaming in a language she didn't speak anymore.

"Can I ask you something?" Renn said. "You work nights. You must know this building better than anyone. Does it—leak? Structurally, I mean. People say old buildings are solid, but honestly they're held together with prayer and asbestos."

"It leaks," Ansel said. "The roof over periodicals. The east stairwell. The basement floods in spring."

"East stairwell," he repeated, and something flickered across his face—not guilt, nothing so honest as guilt, more like a man hearing his own address read out at a meeting. "Huh. Yeah. Old wiring in those stairwells too, I'd bet. Fire code nightmare, buildings like this. You know what the fire marshal told us? Half these places, the alarms haven't been tested since the nineties. You could pull one and get nothing but dust." He laughed. "Scary, right? People sleep through their own emergencies."

Ansel's hand was still flat on the returns cart. She noticed that her knuckles had gone the color of the radiator.

"There was a fire," she said. "On Marsh Street. Seven years ago."

"Was there?" He said it lightly, the way you'd say *was there a game last night?* But he'd gone still, the way a cat goes still, and she understood that he was not a man who forgot fires. He was a man who kept a ledger of them. "Anyone hurt?"

"Three people died."

"God." He shook his head, and the shake looked right, it looked like what a decent man would do, and that was the most frightening thing about him, Ansel thought. He knew exactly what decency looked like from the outside. He'd studied it. "That's terrible. That's—yeah. Terrible." He glanced at the rain on the windows as if the dead might be out there in it. "Old buildings," he said finally, softly, like a eulogy, like a defense exhibit. "They kill people, and everyone acts surprised."

*You cut the wires,* Ansel did not say. *You knelt in the stairwell of 44 Marsh Street on the fourteenth of October and you cut the alarm wires and you looked up and you saw me and you walked away and three people died and one of them was my brother and one of them was four years old.*

What she said was: "The returns bin is on your left if you'd like to browse."

* * *

He browsed. That was the unbearable thing—he browsed. He wandered the stacks with his hands in the pockets of a coat that probably cost more than Ansel's yearly salary, pulling out books at random, reading first pages, putting them back in the wrong places with the serene confidence of a man who had never had to reshelve anything in his life. Dev drifted over to the desk, following the smell of money the way moths follow porch lights.

"Friend of yours?" Dev murmured.

"He's here about the demolition."

"Ah." Dev looked over at Renn, who was now examining the local history section with the expression of a man appraising a horse he intended to buy at a discount. "He looks like a yacht that learned to walk."

"Dev."

"Sorry. He looks like a *nice* yacht that learned to walk."

Ansel almost smiled. That was the thing about Dev—she could always tell when Ansel's silence was the comfortable kind and when it was the kind with teeth in it, and tonight she'd clocked it in under a minute and stationed herself at the desk like a large, kind piece of furniture with opinions.

Renn came back at midnight with a book. Of course it was a book about the neighborhood—*The Harbor District: A Photographic History*, 1987, water-stained, worth about four dollars.

"This is great," he said. "Look at this. Same block. Look how sad it was even then." He turned the book around to show her a photograph of a street she knew better than her own face. Her brother's building was in it, third from the corner, its brick still the color of brick instead of the color of ash. "We're going to fix all this. You watch."

"Will you want a card?" Ansel asked. "For the book."

"Sure. Yeah. What do you need?"

"Photo ID and proof of address."

He produced a driver's license without hesitation, and she took it, and her hands did not shake, and she read the name she had spent seven years not saying aloud: Rennick A. Harrow. Date of birth that made him thirty-eight. Address in the hills above the city, where the houses had names instead of numbers.

She typed him into the system. She made him a library card. She watched the machine print his name on the card and thought: *I could keep this. I could lose it. I could drop it in the returns bin and let it sit for a decade in the lost-and-found box, a small paper ghost of you filed under H, between a hairbrush and a hymnal.* The fantasy was so vivid and so petty that it steadied her.

"Here you are," she said. "Three weeks, renewable twice."

"See, this is great," Renn said, taking the card, and he meant it, which was the worst part. He was having a lovely time. He was a man collecting small pleasant experiences the way other people collected stamps. "I haven't had a library card since I was a kid. My mom used to bring us. She was a big reader." He said it warmly, wistfully, a man with a mother, a childhood, a heart, apparently. "Anyway. Thanks. Keep the lights on, huh? Not for much longer, but—keep them on." He winked, and the wink was probably meant to be kind.

He left at 12:20. The doors sighed shut behind him and the rain took him and the room exhaled.

* * *

Ansel stood at the desk for a long moment with her hands flat on the wood, doing the breathing Odile had once taught her without knowing she was teaching it—four counts in, six counts out, the judge's trick for staying impartial while someone lied to you in open court.

"You okay?" Dev asked.

"He doesn't know who I am."

"Who's he, then? Besides a yacht."

Ansel looked at the returns cart, at the book he'd left—no, he'd checked it out, he'd *checked it out*, Renn Harrow now possessed a library card with her library on it—and at the rain running down the dark windows.

"He's why I don't finish sentences," she said, and then heard herself, and was appalled, and covered it fast: "Metaphorically. It's a metaphor. Forget it."

Dev, to her eternal credit, did not forget it. Dev just nodded slowly, the way you nod at a dog that's finally shown you the thing it's been barking at, and said, "Okay. Well. He checked out a book about the neighborhood. That's a thing a guilty man does, right? Like, in movies. The guy goes back to the scene and buys a postcard."

"It's a thing a *developer* does," Ansel said. "He's pricing the grief."

But that wasn't what kept her up. What kept her up, after Dev left, after the periodicals man snored himself into a new position, after the Hegel student finally surrendered at one-fifteen with the expression of a man released from prison—what kept her up was the flicker. The half-second, when she'd said *Marsh Street*, when Renn Harrow's face had gone still and careful, the flicker of a man checking his own ledger. He didn't know who she was. But he knew what she'd said. And men like Renn Harrow kept files in their heads—she knew that better than anyone—and somewhere in his file there was an entry, seven years old, about a woman in a window, and tonight a night librarian had said *Marsh Street* to him in a voice that didn't shake, and he would go home, and he would sleep well, but somewhere in the back of that well-rested head a small clerk would pull the file and look at it.

* * *

At 1:40, the doors sighed again, and Ansel's heart did the tilt-thing all over before she saw it was only Mirela, hooded, dripping, moving slowly, one hand pressed to the small of her back.

"Tuesdays you stay home," Ansel said. "Your legs."

"My legs decided they wanted a walk." Mirela lowered herself into the chair across the desk, breathing carefully, and looked at Ansel for a long moment with those eyes that had never once believed anything Ansel said. "You're pale."

"It's the fluorescents."

"Mm." Mirela unwound her scarf with the deliberation of a woman unpacking for a long stay. "A man came in tonight. Expensive coat. He was here when I walked past—I saw him through the glass, standing at your desk, laughing at something. He had a laugh like a door closing on money." She paused. "Harrow."

Ansel said nothing. In Vessari, Mirela had told her, there was a word for the silence that answers a question—*sevvet*, the pause that is itself an admission—and Ansel's silence now was so fluent, so native, that it might as well have been the whole language.

"He didn't recognize you," Mirela said. It wasn't a question either.

"No."

"And how did that feel?"

Ansel opened her mouth to say *fine*, which was the word she'd been using for seven years the way other people used *hello*. What came out instead, in the small hours, in the warm hiss of the radiators, to a dying woman who had never once accepted her silences at face value, was the truth, or the edge of it:

"Like a ghost serving coffee to the man who burned the house."

Mirela nodded slowly, unsurprised, as if she'd been waiting seven years—or however long she'd been waiting—for exactly that sentence, and reached into her bag, and set a slip of paper on the desk between them. On it, in her small precise hand, was a word Ansel didn't know yet, and beneath it, in English, the translation:

*vel-ashen — to stand in the presence of the one who harmed you, and be unrecognized, and carry it alone.*

"Tonight's word," Mirela said gently. "We'll start there."
