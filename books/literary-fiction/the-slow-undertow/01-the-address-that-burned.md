## 1. The Address That Burned

The postcards came in packs of twenty from the stationery shop on Harbor Street, and Xanthe Montrose bought them the way other men bought cigarettes—without ceremony, without pleasure, without any real intention of stopping. The shopkeeper had stopped asking him what he used them for years ago. She had learned that his answer—a small shrug, a smile that arrived precisely on time and left nothing behind it—was not an invitation to conversation but a polite wall built in the shape of one.

He wrote them at night, at the kitchen table of his rented house on the bluff road, under a single lamp with a green glass shade. The house was small and white and unremarkable, chosen for exactly those qualities. From its window you could see the water go dark at dusk, and then the lights of the crab boats, and then nothing. He liked nothing. Nothing was reliable.

Tonight's postcard was a photograph of the harbor at low tide, all mud and silver, the boats sitting tilted on their keels like tired animals. He turned it over. The back was ruled with faint lines, meant for a message, though he almost never wrote messages. The address took up most of the card anyway.

*14 Alder Row.*

His handwriting was beautiful. It had been beautiful for thirty years, since the days when he'd been paid by the page to make words legible, to render speech into record, to turn the mess of human testimony into something a court could hold. His hand did not tremble now. It never trembled. That was the trouble with him, he sometimes thought—that everything in him had been trained into stillness, and stillness, practiced long enough, becomes indistinguishable from death.

He wrote the address slowly, each letter placed like a stone in a wall. Then, beneath it, as he always did, a single line.

*Tonight the tide was out so far I could see the bones of the old pier. I thought you would have wanted to know.*

No name. No signature. There was no one to sign it to.

14 Alder Row had burned in March, eleven years ago. He knew the date because he had read about it in a newspaper he'd gone out of his way to find, driving forty miles to a town with a bigger library, because the local paper here didn't cover the city anymore and he could not bring himself to subscribe to anything with the city's name in the masthead. The fire had started in the kitchen, the article said. Electrical, most likely. The house had been empty at the time. The lot had since been cleared. There was no 14 Alder Row. There was no Alder Row at all, not anymore—the street having been renamed, in some municipal act of forgetting, after a developer who had never lived there.

But the postal service was a patient bureaucracy, and undeliverable mail did not vanish; it went to a dead letter office, where someone opened it looking for clues, found none, and filed it away. Somewhere in a warehouse outside the city there was a bin, or a shelf, or a box, holding eleven years of postcards addressed to a house that no longer existed. Xanthe found a strange comfort in this. The words were not destroyed. They were simply held, unread, in the dark—which was more than they deserved, and less than he did.

He finished the line. He did not stamp the card tonight. Stamping could wait until morning; the ritual had its order, and order was the only absolution he had ever been offered, and it was no absolution at all. Penance without forgiveness. A debt paid into an account that had been closed.

He set the card face-down beside the others—there were four finished ones waiting, which meant tomorrow's walk to the post office would be a heavy one—and looked out the window at the black water.

* * *

In the mornings he walked to town along the cliff path, because the road felt too exposed, too much like being seen from above. The path smelled of salt and gorse and, in autumn, of rotting kelp piled at the tideline like something the sea had coughed up and disowned. He walked at a pace that discouraged company—not fast, which invited curiosity, but steady, metronomic, the pace of a man with somewhere uninteresting to be.

The post office opened at eight. He arrived at ten past, when the first rush of fishermen mailing checks home had thinned and before the retirees arrived to gossip at the counter. Mrs. Calloway ran the window, a broad woman with reading glasses on a chain and a memory for faces that Xanthe had tested, carefully, over three years, and found to be shallow. She knew him as the quiet man who mailed postcards. She did not know his name. He had made sure of this by never giving it, letting her read it off the return address he'd printed once, early, on a sheet of labels: *X. Montrose.* A name reduced to an initial was a name half-buried.

"Four today," she said, taking the cards from him. "You do love your postcards."

"I do," he said, and smiled.

The smile was his finest instrument. He had spent years perfecting it, the way he had once spent years perfecting shorthand, drilling it in the evenings until the symbols flowed off his pen like water. This smile sat exactly between warmth and dismissal. It said *I am friendly* and *this conversation is over* in the same instant, and it worked on nearly everyone. Mrs. Calloway smiled back, stamped the cards, and turned to her sorting. Conversation ended. The world resumed its turning.

It had not always worked. Once—a year ago, maybe two—a woman at the counter, a summer visitor with sunburned shoulders, had asked him why anyone would send postcards with no message on them. She'd seen the backs as he laid them out. Blank except for the address. She'd been curious in the open, unguarded way of people on holiday, and his smile had slid off her like rain off glass.

"They're practice," he'd said. "I'm practicing my handwriting."

"For what?"

And he had stood there with the smile dying on his face, feeling the question go through him like a hook through soft flesh, and finally said, "For nothing," and left without buying stamps, and had not returned for two weeks, until he was sure she'd be gone.

For nothing. He thought about that often. Thirty years ago he had written down everything a man said in a courtroom, faithfully, completely, because fidelity was the whole of his job and he had believed in it the way priests believe in transubstantiation—mysteriously, totally, without needing proof. And then one afternoon he had written down something that was not said, and omitted something that was, and the difference between those two acts had fit inside a single transcript page, and a man named Tomas Reyes had gone to prison for nineteen years because of it, and Xanthe Montrose had driven north until the ocean stopped him and had begun writing letters to a dead woman's burned house.

Because the witness who could have freed Reyes—Marisol Vega, her name was, had been, would be until he died—had lived at 14 Alder Row. She had come to his office twice, before the trial, with what she'd seen. He had taken her statement. He had typed it up himself. And then Judge Harrow had summoned him into chambers, and the transcript had gone into a drawer, and Marisol Vega had recanted—or been persuaded to recant, the distinction mattered to no one who mattered—and six months later she was dead, and the house was sold, and eventually the house burned, and Xanthe kept writing to it as if paper could reach the dead, as if the dead letter office were a kind of purgatory where his words might accumulate until they weighed enough to open some door.

They did not weigh anything. He knew that. Eleven years of postcards weighed nothing at all.

* * *

That afternoon the light came in low and gold across the harbor, and Xanthe sat in the public library because his house had become too quiet even for him. The library was the one public place where silence was not suspicious. A man alone with a book drew no eyes. He had a table by the maritime section, near the window, where the dust moved in the light like something alive.

He was not reading. He was watching the old man.

Emeric Vasseur, though Xanthe didn't yet know his name—he thought of him as the fisherman, the one who came in Tuesdays and Thursdays and sat at the far carrel with stacks of books that predated the library's own cataloging system. Today the old man had three dictionaries open at once, two of them in languages Xanthe couldn't identify, and he was muttering. Not the aimless muttering of the elderly or the mad, but something rhythmic, deliberate, a man testing words against his teeth like coins bitten for gold. Occasionally he wrote in the margins of the oldest dictionary—in pencil, thank God, though the librarians would still have wept—and occasionally he laughed, short and private, at something no one else could hear.

There was a word for what the old man was doing, Xanthe thought. He just didn't know it yet.

He watched him for an hour. The old man's hands were ruined in the way of hands that had hauled rope for fifty years, knuckles swollen, fingers bent like gull wings, and yet they moved over the pages with a tenderness that made Xanthe look away, suddenly, as if he'd interrupted something intimate. When he looked back, the old man was staring at him.

Not glancing. Staring. With the flat, unhurried attention of a man who has spent a lifetime reading water and cloud and knows exactly how long a thing takes to change.

Xanthe gave him the smile. The good one. The wall in the shape of a welcome.

The old man did not smile back. He looked at Xanthe the way you look at a knot you already know how to untie, and then he closed his dictionaries, one by one, deliberately, and gathered them against his chest, and crossed the room, and stood at Xanthe's table looking down at him with eyes the color of weather.

"You," he said, in a voice roughened by salt and decades, "write things down."

It was not a question. Xanthe felt the floor tilt slightly, the way a boat tilts when something shifts below decks.

"Everyone writes things down," Xanthe said.

"No." The old man shook his head slowly. "Everyone writes *some* things. You—" he tapped the table with one ruined finger, once, like a judge's gavel, or a stenographer's key, "—you are a man who has written down something he should not have. I can see it sitting in you. It sits in you like ballast."

The library went very quiet around them. Out the window, the tide was coming in, covering the bones of the old pier inch by inch, the way water covers everything, given time.

"My name is Emeric Vasseur," the old man said. "I am the last one who speaks it, and I need a student before I die. I have decided it will be you."

Xanthe opened his mouth to refuse—to deploy the smile, the shrug, the whole polished arsenal—and found, to his considerable alarm, that nothing came out.
