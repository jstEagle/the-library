## 13. Rio, Answered

Rui found her the way he seemed to find everything: sideways, through someone who knew someone, with a phone call at an hour that suggested he had forgotten time zones existed.

"Paloma. Are you sitting down? No — don't sit. Walk around. It's better for the blood." He was already talking before she'd said hello. "I spoke to Marta Lencastre — you remember, she taught history at the liceu before it closed — and she put me on to the Sociedade de Genealogia e Heráldica in Lisbon. They have a man there, Alberto, who does nothing but trace emigrants. Nothing else. He's like a bloodhound with a filing cabinet."

"And?"

"And I gave him what we had. Duarte Fonseca, born 1943 in Vila do Conde, printer by trade, arrested March 1973 under Decree-Law whatever-it-was, released — we think — sometime after the twenty-fifth of April, no local record afterward. Alberto said that pattern was common enough. Men came out of prison into a country that didn't want to look at them. Half of them left within a year. The question was where."

Paloma stood at the kitchen window of her flat, watching a fishing boat come in against the grey morning water. She had learned, over these weeks, to hold herself very still when Rui called, the way you hold still when a bird lands near you.

"He found him," she said.

"He found *her*," Rui said, and let the correction sit there for a moment, letting her feel its shape. "Duarte Fonseca died in Rio de Janeiro on the fourteenth of September, 1998. Heart failure, according to the civil registry. But he had a daughter. Beatrice Fonseca Lima. Born 1969 — which means she was four years old when they took him. She's alive, Paloma. She's sixty-one years old and she lives in Niterói, across the bay from the city, and Alberto has an address."

The boat reached the harbour wall and began, slowly, to turn. Paloma realized she had not breathed since *she's alive*.

"You could write to her," Rui said, gentler now. "Or you could fly there. Or you could do neither, and I would understand that too. But I thought you should know the door is open. Whether you walk through it is your business."

After he hung up she stood at the window a long time, until the boat had finished turning and gone about some other business than hers.

* * *

She wrote the letter eleven times.

Not drafted — wrote. By hand, because it felt obscene to type it, because a letter about letters should be made the slow way, ink pressed into paper, mistakes visible and crossed out rather than deleted as if they had never happened. Eleven sheets went into the drawer where she kept things she couldn't finish: the legal translations she'd flagged for review, a birthday card for Tomás bought in October and never sent, a postcard from Porto she'd written to an old friend three years ago and never posted. The drawer was becoming an archive of unsent things, and she recognized, with a small cold clarity, that she was her grandmother's granddaughter in more ways than blood.

The problem was never the facts. The facts were easy; she was a translator, facts were her daily bread. *My name is Paloma Almeida. My grandmother was Inês Almeida, née Inês Barros. In 1971 and 1972 she wrote forty-one letters to your father. He received none of them. They were found behind a wardrobe in her house this spring, after her death.*

The problem was everything around the facts. How did you tell a stranger that her father had been loved? That he had been erased so thoroughly that even his death record had fled the town where he'd been born? That the woman who loved him had married another man — Paloma's grandfather, the informant, though Paloma could not yet bring herself to write those words to anyone — and had spent fifty years keeping his letters sealed behind wood?

On the eleventh attempt she stopped trying to explain and simply told the truth, plainly, the way Amara's affidavit had taught her: that testimony does not need ornament, only accuracy, and that accuracy is a kind of respect.

*Mrs. Fonseca Lima,* she wrote. *I don't know what you know about your father's life before Brazil. I know almost nothing myself, and most of what I know comes from letters he was never allowed to read. I am writing because I believe you have a right to them, or to their story, and because I have questions that only you might answer. If you read this letter and wish never to hear from me again, I will honour that completely. But if any part of you has wondered about Portugal — about the years before you — then I am here, and I have things to show you.*

She signed it. She addressed it. She stamped it.

At the post office in the praça, the clerk — a young woman with chipped blue nail polish who had known Paloma's grandmother — weighed the envelope and looked up. "Rio de Janeiro? Who do you know in Rio?"

"Someone I'm about to," Paloma said, and heard, with surprise, that her voice did not smile the sentence shut. It stayed open. It invited the question and then walked away from it, out the door, down the steps, into the street where the light had turned gold and ordinary.

It cost her one euro fifteen. It was the hardest thing she had ever mailed.

* * *

Weeks are strange units of time. A week can pass in an afternoon if you fill it with work, and Paloma filled hers ruthlessly: the legal contracts that paid the rent, a museum catalogue, a slow patient project translating a book of Azorean poetry that her editor had wanted for two years. She checked the letterbox every day at first, then every other day, then trained herself to check it only when she happened to pass, which was a lie she told herself while taking deliberate detours past it.

Rui called twice. Once to say nothing had come yet, which she hadn't asked him to report and which somehow made it worse — the silence now had a witness. Once to say that he'd been thinking about the printing shop, and that he remembered the smell of the ink, and that his own father used to buy pamphlets there before the war, real ones, dangerous ones, and had he ever told her that? He hadn't. He was telling her now. Everyone, she was learning, was waiting for permission to speak.

Tomás did not call at all. Since the night he'd found the letters and demanded they be burned — since *you weren't here, you don't know what it cost us to be quiet* — they had exchanged exactly four messages, all logistical, all about the house, all signed off with a coldness that had once, decades ago, been warmth between children sharing a bedroom during summer visits. The demolition notice was coming; the developer's lawyers had promised it in writing. Thirty days, they'd said. Tomás had signed preliminary papers. Every day the house stood was a day borrowed against a debt someone else had decided to collect.

She worked on the final letter again, at night, the shorthand spreading across the page like weather. Three words still resisted her. She had begun to suspect they always would — that some part of Inês had built them to resist, had chosen sounds that blurred, meanings that folded back on themselves, so that whoever decoded the sentence would have to stand inside the uncertainty the way Inês herself had stood inside it for fifty years. Not a locked door. A door that only opened onto fog.

She was reading that fog for the hundredth time when the email came.

Not the letterbox — an email, forwarded from the genealogy society, sent by a Beatrice Fonseca Lima to Alberto, forwarded to Rui, forwarded to her, subject line empty except for a single word: *Inês.*

Paloma did not open it immediately. She made tea she did not drink. She sat down. She opened it.

*Dear Mr. Alberto, please forward this to the granddaughter.*

*I received your letter six weeks ago and I have read it perhaps two hundred times. I carried it in my handbag for a month. My husband thought I was losing my mind.*

*I will tell you what I know, which is very little, because my father was a man who had made silence into a craft. He was a printer in Brazil too — did you know that? He had a small shop in Santa Teresa, wedding invitations and restaurant menus, nothing political, never anything political again. But he kept one drawer locked his whole life and we were never allowed to touch it. When he was dying, in '98, he asked my mother to bring him the contents of that drawer, and he held them, and he cried — I had never seen him cry, not once, not at his mother's funeral — and he asked me a question I have carried for twenty-seven years without knowing whom to ask it of.*

*But first. Enclosed with this email you will find photographs of two documents. The first is a letter. It is the only letter my father ever received from Portugal. It arrived in 1975, after the revolution, forwarded twice, water-damaged. He kept it for fifty years. It is signed Inês. I believe it is your grandmother's handwriting, since you will know better than I.*

*The second photograph is of a note my father wrote himself, in the hospital, two days before he died. His hands were bad by then. You will forgive the writing.*

*He asked me: find out if she ever forgave herself.*

*I am asking you, Paloma Almeida, because you are the one who found the letters, and because you are the one who wrote to me, and because I am seventy-one years old next month and I cannot carry this question into another decade alone.*

*Did she ever forgive herself?*

*With hope and fear in equal measure,*
*Beatrice*

The attachments loaded slowly, the way important things always seem to. The first photograph showed a sheet of paper gone soft and brown at the folds, water-stained along one edge, the ink faded but legible — and yes, unmistakably, it was Inês's hand, the careful schoolroom script of her later years, the script Paloma knew from shopping lists and birthday cards and the labels on jam jars in the pantry of the house that was scheduled to die in thirty days.

The letter was short. Paloma read it standing up, then sitting down, then standing again, because her body could not decide what posture grief required.

*Duarte. I don't know if this will reach you and I don't know what I will do if it does. They tell me you are alive. For twenty months I did not let myself believe it, because believing it meant admitting what I had done, and I could not admit what I had done and continue to feed my child and sleep beside my husband and walk through my town with my face arranged correctly. Do you understand? I chose my safety over yours. I told myself it was for the baby. Some nights that was even true.*

*I am not asking forgiveness. I am telling you that I know what I did, and that I have kept every word I ever wrote you, and that if you want them burned I will burn them, and if you want them I will send them, and if you want nothing I will send nothing and live with it, which is what I have been doing anyway.*

*Your daughter has your eyes. Someone showed me a photograph. I look at her sometimes at the market and I cannot breathe.*

*Inês*

Paloma set the phone down on the table with exaggerated care, the way you set down something that might go off.

So there had been one letter. One that slipped through — mailed in a moment of courage or collapse in 1975, forwarded through whatever channels exiles used, arriving sodden and late in a city where a former printer was setting type for wedding invitations and pretending the twentieth century had never touched him. One letter out of forty-two. And Inês had kept the other forty-one behind a wardrobe for half a century, and had instructed — Paloma thought of the note taped under the glasshouse bench, *so that someone after me may choose* — had instructed that they never be sent, and yet had sent this one, this single breach in her own wall, and Paloma understood suddenly that the instruction and the letter were the same act viewed from different angles: a woman who could not choose, choosing to leave the choice behind her like an inheritance.

And the second attachment. The note in the dying man's hand, the letters large and wobbling:

*Beatriz — pergunta a ela se algum dia se perdoou.*

*Ask her if she ever forgave herself.*

Paloma read it until the words stopped being words. Then she got up and walked out of the flat and down to the water, because the walls were too close, because the question had entered her chest and needed a horizon to expand against.

Did she ever forgive herself.

The answer was in the letters. The answer was in all forty-one of them, in the eight-month gap, in the resumed correspondence addressed to a changed address, in the grief without explanation, in the final letter's untranslatable sentence, in the sealed envelope marked *não enviar*. Inês had answered the question every day of her life for fifty years, in the only language she trusted — the private one, the hidden one, the one written to a man who would never read it. And the answer, Paloma knew now, was no. It was no, and it was also something larger than no: it was a refusal to make the guilt smaller, a refusal to translate it into comfort. Some sentences, her grandmother had apparently decided, were only survivable untranslated.

But that wasn't what Beatrice was asking. Beatrice was asking whether there had been peace at the end, or at least a truce. And that Paloma could not answer from the letters alone — or could she? She thought of the last letter, dated 1974, the sentence that named what her grandfather did and asked forgiveness of no one. Asked forgiveness of no one. Not: forgave no one. Asked forgiveness of no one — refused to beg, refused to grovel, and in refusing had perhaps, in some oblique Inês-way, begun the only forgiveness available to her, which was the forgiveness that requires no permission from the person harmed because the person harmed is dead or gone or unreachable, and the only one left to do the forgiving is yourself, and you decline, and you live, and you keep the letters.

Paloma stood at the sea wall until the cold came through her coat. Gulls argued over something in the shallows. A child ran the length of the beach screaming with joy about nothing.

Then she went home and began to write the reply, and discovered that her hands were shaking too hard to hold a pen, and laughed — actually laughed, alone in her kitchen at nine at night — because of course. Of course the answer to a fifty-year-old question would arrive in the form of a question. Of course the door Rui had opened led not to an ending but to a room with more doors.

She wrote: *Dear Beatrice. I have your letter and the photographs. I have read your father's note a dozen times and I will read it a dozen more. Before I answer your question — and I will answer it, as honestly as translation allows — I need to send you something first. There is a letter of your father's that no one has ever seen, written in a language he and my grandmother invented together, and I am close to understanding it. Give me a little time. What I owe you is not a guess. It is the truth, rendered faithfully.*

She hesitated over the closing. Then she wrote: *Your father was loved here. Whatever else happened — and much of it was terrible, and I will not soften it for you — he was loved, and he was mourned in secret for fifty years by a woman who kept his name alive in a language of two people. That is also true. I wanted you to have that sentence in your handbag alongside the others.*

She signed it, attached scans of three of the early letters — the warm ones, the 1971 ones, the ones where Inês's handwriting ran confident and laughing across the page — and sent it into the dark over the Atlantic.

Then she pulled the final letter toward her across the table, spread her notes around it like a surgeon's instruments, and looked at the three words that had resisted her for weeks.

She had a deadline now. Not the developer's thirty days — something older and heavier than that. A dying man's question, crossing an ocean twice, waiting in a handbag in Niterói.

*Find out if she ever forgave herself.*

Paloma picked up her pen.

Outside, far down the coast, the lights of her grandmother's house — Tomás's house, the developer's house — burned in the windows of an empty bedroom, and behind the wardrobe the false panel gaped open on nothing, because everything it had guarded was now on her kitchen table, asking to be finished.
