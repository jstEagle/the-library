## 5. The Tenant's Almanac

The week after the viewing, Quentin kept a list.

He had started it years ago, in the back pages of his tuning ledger, though he had never given it a name until now. *The Tenant's Almanac*, Marisol would have called it, if she'd ever seen it, and she would have laughed at him for it — her brother, the man who rented rooms in other people's houses and took notes on their plumbing like a naturalist cataloguing birds. But the list was real to him, more real than most of what he owned. Every house had a voice. Every radiator had an opinion. And after forty years of listening, Quentin Ishikawa had learned which houses told the truth and which ones lied.

The piano on Delancey Street hummed honestly. He wrote that down Tuesday morning, kneeling under the window of a third-floor walk-up while its owner, a cellist with paint-stained hands, made tea in a kitchen the size of a drawer. The building was 1911, steam heat, original risers, and when the boiler kicked on at seven each morning the whole pipe run sang a low, unembarrassed C — slightly flat, slightly rusty, but true. The radiators here had nothing to hide. They clanked and gurgled and knocked like old men playing dominoes, complaining about the weather, announcing every arrival at the front door. A house like this, Quentin thought, could never keep a secret even if it wanted to. You could hear the arguments through the walls not because the walls were thin but because the pipes carried everything — the slammed doors, the crying in the bathroom, the long silences that were worse than either. And the people who lived here seemed to know it. They lived loudly. They forgave out loud too.

*Honest,* he wrote. *Flat C. Forgives itself nightly.*

* * *

The house on Wexford Avenue was another matter entirely.

It was one of those renovated brownstones where the new owners had spent a fortune making everything look old — the wide-plank floors, the apron sink, the Edison bulbs hanging from cloth-wrapped cord — and a second fortune hiding everything that actually was. The radiators had been swapped for slim European panels, painted the same white as the walls, as if heating were something to be ashamed of. When Quentin pressed his palm against one during a break between tuning passes, he felt almost nothing. No history. No knock of a previous life. Just a smooth, mute warmth, like a handshake from someone who has already decided not to remember your name.

The piano itself was fine — a Bechstein, recently restored, holding its tune well enough. It was the house that was out of tune. Quentin worked through the afternoon in a silence so managed it felt rehearsed, and by the time he packed his tools he had a headache he couldn't attribute to anything but the rooms themselves. In the ledger, under the client's address, he wrote: *Lies through its pipes. Beautiful liar. Nothing in the basement.*

He thought about Daniel all the way home on the bus.

That was the trouble with the almanac. It had started as a way of listening to houses, and it had become a way of avoiding one. Every honest radiator he catalogued was a small act of devotion to a craft, and every lying one was a mirror. Because he knew, now, what kind of house his childhood home was. He had heard it at the viewing — the exact afternoon, replayed through the iron like a recording pressed into metal sixty years ago and still spinning. His father's voice going up an octave. Daniel's voice staying level, which was always how you knew Daniel meant business. The door. The door slamming hard enough that the shock had traveled down through the pipes and into the basement and settled there, in the cold iron, waiting for a man with a tuning hammer and no good sense to come along and put his ear against it.

Quentin had stood in that empty bedroom with his hand on the radiator cover and let the whole thing happen again. And then he had walked out, shaken Mrs. Okonkwo's papery hand, promised to think about the letters, and gone home to his rented room above the dry cleaner's, where the radiator knocked twice at night like a polite ghost and asked for nothing.

* * *

Wednesday he tuned a spinet for a widow named Ferreira who talked the entire time, bless her, about her late husband's habit of falling asleep mid-sentence, and the piano needed almost nothing, and Quentin found himself grateful for the work the way other men are grateful for anesthesia. Thursday was a school in the east end, thirty uprights in a hall that smelled of floor wax and children's lunches, and the radiators there banged so cheerfully, so democratically, that he stayed an hour past his invoice just to listen. Friday he canceled two appointments and told himself it was fatigue.

What he did instead was sit at the kitchen table of his rented room with a sheet of good paper and begin a letter to his brother.

*Daniel,* he wrote. *I heard the house.*

He stopped. Looked at it. Heard it in his own head in his own voice, and then heard Daniel's answer before Daniel ever gave it — *heard what, Quent? Houses don't talk. That's your little superstition, isn't it, the tuner's magic trick* — and Quentin understood that he could not write this letter honestly, because honesty required him to say things he had spent forty years declining to say. That he had been in the hallway that afternoon. That he had heard the door slam and had gone back to his room and done his homework. That he was fourteen years old and afraid, yes, but fourteen-year-olds grow up, and grown men do not get to keep using their fourteen-year-old selves as an alibi.

*I should have asked you,* he tried instead. Crossed it out. *You have to tell me what Mother signed.* Crossed that out too, harder, the pen nearly going through the paper, because *have to* was a word that belonged to Marisol, and Marisol's certainty had begun to feel like a hand between his shoulder blades, pushing him toward an auction room, toward a bidding paddle, toward becoming a man who fought over a deed.

He sat with the ruined page for a long time. Downstairs the dry cleaner's press hissed; above it, his radiator ticked its two o'clock knock. Then he folded the letter, went down to the alley, and burned it in the coffee can he kept for such purposes — because there had been others, over the years, unsent letters to his father, to Ada, once even to the house itself, addressed simply *To Whom It May Concern at 14 Alder Row* — and watched the ink curl and go black. The smoke smelled briefly of iron. He told himself the calm he felt afterward was peace.

* * *

Marisol called Sunday evening, and he knew before he answered, because she called on Sundays the way weather arrives — inevitably, and with pressure behind it.

"They opened bidding online," she said, without hello. "Pre-auction. Did you see?"

"I don't look."

"Of course you don't look. Quentin, there are already four bids. Four. If we wait for the day itself—"

"You'll bid," he said. "You should bid. It's your right."

"It's our *house*." Her voice did the thing it always did, rising into that register that had governed their childhood, the eldest-sister frequency that could stop traffic. "It's the house they took from Mom. From all of us. And you're going to stand there with your little notebook and your little hammers and tell me you're not even going to—"

"I'm not going to bid, Mari."

The silence on the line had texture. He could hear her breathing, and beneath it, faintly, the sound of her own radiator — hers was an honest one, he knew, a big cast-iron monster in a rented duplex that knocked like a heartbeat — and he thought, absurdly, *we are both tenants listening to pipes.*

"Why?" she said finally, and it wasn't angry anymore. It was worse than angry. It was the voice she used at hospital bedsides. "Why won't you fight for anything, Quent? You didn't fight when Dad died. You didn't fight when Mom signed whatever she signed. You didn't fight when Daniel—" She stopped herself. Even Marisol had a wall she wouldn't say past on the phone.

"I listen," Quentin said. "That's not the same as fighting. I know that. But it's what I have."

"And what does the house say? Since you're the expert." Bitterness now, but also, underneath, a real question. She had never once mocked the gift, not seriously. That was the strange mercy of Marisol: she thought he was being fanciful, but she respected the fancy.

Quentin looked at the letter ash in the coffee can by the door. "It says the same thing it said the day Dad's voice went up an octave," he said. "It says somebody knows something. And it says I already know who."

"Then *go*," she whispered. "Before the auction. Before some stranger buys our whole childhood at a markup."

"I'll go," he said, because it was easier than explaining that he had already drafted the going, and burned it, and that the burning had felt like relief, and that the relief frightened him more than Daniel ever had.

After she hung up, the room was very quiet. The radiator knocked its two knocks. Quentin sat with the almanac open on his knee and read back through the week's entries — the honest flat C on Delancey, the beautiful liar on Wexford, the democratic banging of the school — and at the bottom of the page, in the margin, he found he had written something earlier in the day without remembering writing it, the way you find a bruise:

*Alder Row. Still holding its note. Sixty years and nobody has released the pedal.*

He closed the book. Outside, the last bus of the evening sighed past below his window, and he saw, pinned to the pole at the corner, a small white notice with a transit seal on it, fluttering in the wind like something trying to get his attention.
