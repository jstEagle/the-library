## 8. Six Weeks, One Room

Daniel had fixed a thousand things in his life—carburetors, grandfather clocks, the jaw of a man who'd swung at him outside a pool hall in Norrköping—and he had learned one law above all the others: you cannot repair what you refuse to open up. You can polish the casing. You can make it look whole from across the room. But until you take it apart and look at what's broken inside, every fix is a lie with screws in it.

He thought about that law a lot these days. He thought about it while he waited for Sebastian Okonkwo to finish her sentence.

She had been trying to finish it for three days.

* * *

The house on Vintergatan had no heat anymore, so they gathered in the parlor with their coats on, breath ghosting in the afternoon light that came through windows Miriam had already paid to have washed. That was Miriam all over—buy the window before you've decided what the house is for. She sat on the good armchair like she owned it, which technically she would in six weeks, her ankles crossed, her coat cashmere, her face carrying the particular patience of a woman who has never once been told to wait her turn.

Tomas leaned against the mantel. He was fifty-one now and still built like the dockworker he'd become, hands like shovels, and he wanted money—he'd said so plainly at the first meeting, said it without shame, said the house owed them and Miriam could afford the debt. Ruth sat by the window with her knitting because Ruth knitted when the world got loud, and the world had gotten very loud since Sebastian played them half of a cassette tape and said, in a voice like someone reading a weather report for an approaching storm: *I think we should hold our own hearing.*

No lawyers. No press. Not yet. Just the parlor, the survivors, the tape, and whatever truth could survive being spoken aloud in a room where it happened.

It had been Sebastian's idea. She'd stood in the middle of the parlor with her janitor's lanyard still around her neck—she hadn't taken it off; maybe it was armor, maybe penance—and laid it out like a work order. Testimony. The tape. Witnesses. Everyone who came would speak or stay silent, but no one would leave without hearing everything.

And then, three days ago, she'd started rehearsing her own statement, and Daniel had discovered something he had not expected to discover about the great Sebastian Okonkwo, the girl whose hands had made grown critics weep:

She couldn't do it.

* * *

The first day, she stood where the piano used to be—there was a pale rectangle on the floorboards where it had lived for thirty years, and she stood inside that rectangle as if it were a chalk outline—and she got four sentences in.

*"My name is Sebastian Okonkwo. In the winter of 1994 I was fifteen years old. On the night of December the ninth, Henrik Voss came to this house—"*

And stopped. Stood there with her mouth open like a door with nothing behind it. Then she said, "Tomorrow. I'll have it tomorrow," and walked out to her cart, and Daniel heard her cleaning a practice room at the conservatory until nearly midnight, though the conservatory was closed.

The second day she got further. She'd written it out longhand on lined paper, and she read it in a flat voice, and it was terrible—not wrong, but terrible the way a police report is terrible, all dates and distances, Voss's car, Voss's cologne, the study door, the hallway, the choice she made in the hallway when she was fifteen years old and terrified and ambitious and had been told since she was nine years old that men like Henrik Voss were the reason the roof didn't leak. She read about how she'd let Daniel take the blame. How she'd said nothing at fifteen, and then said less at eighteen when the conservatory's lawyers asked her gently if she remembered anything useful, and she'd said no, and they'd smiled, and her career had opened like a flower.

She got to the part where she named what she'd done—*I traded him*—and her voice did something Daniel had never heard it do. It went small. Not quiet. Small. The way a sound goes when it's ashamed of itself.

"I can't," she said. "Not the rest. Not out loud."

Ruth's needles clicked once and stopped. Tomas looked at the ceiling. Miriam said, kindly, terribly, "Take your time, we have six weeks."

And Daniel, who had driven forty minutes unannounced to sit in a cold parlor and watch this, understood suddenly that kindness was exactly the wrong instrument here, and that if anyone handed this woman an exit she would take it, not because she was a coward—he'd watched her walk back into Halden Conservatory pushing a mop cart, and that took a spine—but because she had spent thirty years perfecting the art of leaving rooms before the hard part.

So he said, "You'll finish it."

She turned and looked at him for the first time that day.

"You'll finish it," he said again, "because the version you just read isn't testimony. It's a summary. A summary is what lawyers make. We're not lawyers."

"Daniel—" Miriam began.

"No." He kept his eyes on Sebastian. "I'm sorry, Miriam. But no."

* * *

Here is what Daniel had never told any of them, not in thirty years, not even Ruth, not even in the letter he wrote Sebastian that said only *You were there*: he had gone back. Twice. Once at nineteen, standing outside the conservatory gates in the rain, watching through the fence as students crossed the courtyard below the portrait of Henrik Voss—the portrait, the benefactor, the man whose name was carved over the entrance in letters deep enough to hold rainwater—and he had stood there wanting to scream and instead had gotten in his van and driven home and eaten dinner and slept. And once at thirty-four, when a journalist doing a retrospective on Voss's donations called him, and he'd said, politely, "I wouldn't be a credible source," and hung up, and then sat in his shop for two hours unable to remember what he'd been fixing.

He knew what silence cost from the inside. That was why he couldn't let her buy her way out of it with effort. Effort was cheap. Everyone admired effort. The whole world ran on people trying hard at things that didn't matter.

* * *

On the third day, the others stayed away. Miriam had a gala. Tomas had a shift. Ruth had sent word through Sebastian that she thought everyone needed air. So it was just the two of them in the parlor at four in the afternoon, the light going amber through the clean glass, and Sebastian standing in the piano-shaped absence with her papers, and Daniel sitting where the sofa used to be, on a folding chair he'd brought himself because he refused to sit on the floor of that house like a child again.

"From the top," he said.

"You don't have to—"

"From the top."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she read it. All of it, the flat terrible summary, and when she reached the place where she always broke—*I traded him*—she broke again, right on schedule, her voice collapsing into that smallness, and she lowered the pages and said, "I'm sorry. God, Daniel, I'm sorry, I don't know why I can't—"

"Yes, you do."

"It was thirty years ago. It shouldn't—"

"You're not failing because it was thirty years ago." He stood up. He hadn't meant to stand up. His knees decided before he did. "You're failing because the paper is doing the talking. As long as you're reading, it's a document. Documents can be filed. You need it to come out of your actual mouth, in front of us, and then in front of whoever else needs to hear it, and you know that, which is why you keep stopping at the exact sentence where it stops being a document and becomes you."

Her jaw tightened. He watched her reach for the smile—the famous one, the one she'd worn like a uniform, the one that ended conversations. He saw it start to assemble itself, muscle by muscle, and he watched her catch it, mid-assembly, and put it down. That cost her something. He could see it costing her.

"I don't know how," she said finally. Quietly. Honestly, for the first time.

"Yes, you do. You did it when you were eleven." He nodded toward the wall, toward the memory of the piano. "First recital. You threw up in the coat closet and Mama found you and you told her you couldn't go out there, you'd die, literally die, and she said—" he had to stop, because his throat closed on the words, because he hadn't said Mama's words aloud in decades and they came up hot— "she said, *the music doesn't need you to be brave, Sebastian. It just needs you to not stop.*"

Silence. The radiator ticked. Somewhere upstairs a shutter knocked against its frame in the wind, patient as a metronome.

"And I don't need your apology," Daniel said. "I want to be clear about that, because I think you've built this whole hearing in your head as a machine for manufacturing forgiveness, and I can't give you that, and neither can anyone else in this room. What was taken doesn't come back. My twenties don't come back. The record doesn't unseal itself because you feel bad."

Sebastian flinched. He made himself keep going.

"What I need is for it to be *said*. Out loud. Where it counts. Not in this parlor to five people who already believe you—that's rehearsal, that's tuning up. Where it counts is wherever Voss's name still buys silence. The conservatory. The record. The world that decided I was the boy who did it and you were the girl who survived him." He picked up his folding chair and set it down again, needing his hands to do something. "You were the only one who could have stopped it, and you didn't, and then you spent your life being brilliant enough that nobody asked why. So the payment isn't apology. The payment is the truth, delivered, in full, on the record, even if it costs you the last thing you have left—which is the story everyone tells about you."

"That's not mercy," she said. Her voice shook.

"No," Daniel agreed. "It isn't."

But he crossed the room anyway—all twelve feet of parlor floor, past the pale rectangle where the piano had been—and he did the thing he had not done in thirty years, which was to stand close enough to touch her, and he didn't touch her, he held his hand out flat between them like a man offering a tool.

"Saturday," he said. "The whole statement. Your voice, no paper. And after that, we talk about the journalist Miriam knows. Because the parlor is step one, Sebastian. It's not the destination."

She looked at his hand. Then at his face. And something moved through her expression that he recognized from the old tapes, from the recordings Mama used to make of household evenings—the concentration before the first note, terror and readiness in the same face.

"Saturday," she said.

She took his hand. Her grip was stronger than he expected. Pianist's hands, janitor's hands now, and steady, finally, for the first time all week.

Outside, the wind picked up along Vintergatan, and upstairs the shutter knocked and knocked, and Daniel thought: three days left of rehearsal, six weeks left of house, and somewhere in a drawer at Halden Conservatory, he was certain of it now, someone had already heard rumors about the tape.

Because that morning, a black sedan had parked across the street for forty minutes, engine running, watching the windows.
