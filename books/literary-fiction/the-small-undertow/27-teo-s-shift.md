## 27. Teo's Shift

Teo had been covering Silas's desk for eleven minutes before he understood that something was wrong, and the thing that told him wasn't a sound but a silence—the absence of the small percussion of returns being stamped, the cart wheels, the pencil Silas always tapped twice against the wood before writing anything down. The night library had its own weather, and Teo had learned to read it the way Odile read fog off the water: by what it took away.

He looked up from his flashcards. Renal physiology. Third pass through the same card, which meant he hadn't been reading it either.

Silas was still at the desk. That was the strange part. She was sitting exactly where she always sat, spine straight, hands flat on the blotter, lamp angled over the returns log—and she was not moving. Not the way a person rests between tasks, with the loose readiness of someone about to reach for the next book. She was still the way furniture is still.

"Silas?" he said, keeping his voice at library volume, which was barely a voice at all.

Nothing.

He came around the end of the circulation desk slowly, the way you'd approach a bird that had hit a window and was sitting up but not yet flying. Up close he could see what the lamp had hidden: her lips were gray. Her chest was doing something wrong—rising in quick shallow sips, like she was drinking air instead of breathing it. Her eyes were open and fixed on the middle distance of the returns log, on nothing, on some interior country Teo couldn't see the border of.

"Hey," he said. "Hey. I'm going to touch your shoulder, okay?"

She didn't say okay. Her hand came up—not pushing him away, more like a drawbridge half-raised—and he stopped, crouched down so they were level, and put both his own hands flat on the desk where she could see them. Nursing school had drilled this into him until it lived in his hands: show your empty palms. Slow everything down. You cannot talk someone out of a body; you can only sit beside the body while it finishes whatever it's doing.

"Silas. It's Teo. You're having a panic attack. Your body thinks something is happening that isn't happening right now, and it's lying to you, and the lie will run out of fuel in a few minutes. I'm going to stay right here."

Her mouth opened. What came out was not a word. It was a sound like a hinge, dry and small, and then her breath went faster, and her hands pressed down against the blotter as if the desk were tilting, as if the whole building were listing toward the river.

*The river,* Teo thought, and didn't know why, and filed it away.

---

Here is what Teo knew about Silas Nystrom, after eight months of nights:

That she reshelved with a precision that bordered on devotion, every spine flush, every call number squared to the shelf edge, as if the books were sleeping and deserved quiet. That she kept a pencil behind her ear and lost it constantly and had seventeen pencils because Mr. Bao bought her a new one every time he saw one go missing, and she accepted each one with the same grave nod she gave everything, as if receiving tribute. That she drank her coffee black but kept a tin of honey behind the desk for Teo's tea, and had never once acknowledged doing this, and would deny it if asked. That she knew every regular's name, their holds, their sections, their moods—and that no one, as far as Teo could tell, knew hers beyond *Silas, night desk.*

That she worked nights. That when he'd asked her why—once, weeks ago, in the soft hour around four when the library felt like the inside of a held breath—she'd said, *Daylight is for people who want to be seen,* and smiled, and the smile had been so practiced it might have come with instructions.

He'd thought about that answer a lot since. He was twenty-six, three semesters from an RN, running on vending machine coffee and the particular loneliness of a person whose family lived four thousand miles away and called only with news that required him to be brave. He knew what it looked like when someone built a life shaped like a hiding place. He'd done it himself for two years after his mother died—the overnight shifts, the empty hours, the theory that if you were useful enough at 3 a.m. you wouldn't have to be a person at any other time.

So when Silas's breath went wrong at 2:47 on a Tuesday, Teo did not do the thing most people would have done, which was to make it bigger by making it visible. He did not call out for Odile. Not yet.

"Can you look at me?" he said. "You don't have to talk. Just my face."

Her eyes dragged over, caught, held. Good. That was good. Contact was a rope; you threw it and held your end.

"There you are," he said. "Now here's what we're going to do. Square breathing. In for four." He breathed in, exaggerated, theatrical, so she could copy the shape of it. "Hold four. Out for six—longer out than in, that's the trick, it tells the nervous system the emergency is over. I'll count. You just steal my rhythm. Steal it, I don't need it."

She got maybe two cycles in before the wave took her again—her shoulders climbing, her hand flying to her sternum, pressing there like she was holding a door shut against something on the other side. And then, horribly, quietly, she started to cry. No sound. Tears straight down, jaw locked, the crying of someone who had learned somewhere, long ago, that grief was contraband.

"Okay," Teo said softly. "Okay. That too. That's allowed here. Nobody's even awake, Silas. Nobody but us and Mr. Bao, and he's reading obituaries, he's seen everything there is to see."

At the sound of Mr. Bao's name her head turned—a fraction, involuntary—toward the reading room, and Teo understood two things at once. One: she didn't want Odile to see this. Two: she trusted Mr. Bao's discretion without knowing she trusted it, the way you trust gravity.

"I'll get water," he said. "I'll be gone ten seconds. Watch the desk. Guard it with your life."

It was a stupid joke and it worked, or partly worked—her mouth twitched, a muscle remembering another job—and when he came back with the paper cup she had both hands wrapped around the returns log like a raft.

---

Odile appeared at 3:05, and Teo never learned how she knew. Later he suspected she had simply been watching all along, from her chair in the periodicals, the way retired ferry pilots watch water—casually, continuously, with a lifetime's respect for how fast conditions change.

She didn't rush over. She came the long way, past the biography stacks, unhurried, and arrived at the desk carrying her coat over her arm as though she'd just decided to leave, and stood on Silas's blind side, and said, in her low river-gravel voice:

"Nystrom. I'm putting my coat on the back of your chair. That's all. Coat goes here. I go back to my magazine."

And she did. She draped the coat—an absurd gesture, a claim of territory, a way of saying *I am staying and I don't require anything from you*—and went back to her seat, and turned a page she almost certainly didn't read.

Teo watched Silas's breathing change. Not fixed. But the panic had somewhere to land now besides the floor of her own skull. There were witnesses. Quiet ones. Ones who asked nothing.

Mr. Bao came by at 3:20 with a fresh pencil, set it beside her elbow without comment, and paused just long enough to say, "In my village we had a saying. The well does not apologize for being drawn from." Then he shuffled back to his obituaries, leaving Silas staring at the pencil as if it were written in a language she used to speak.

By 3:40 the wave had passed. Not gone—Teo could see it in the tremor of her hands, the way she flinched at the pneumatic tube's soft thump—but passed, the way weather passes, leaving everything soaked and standing.

"Talk or don't," Teo said, settling onto the stool at the side of the desk, giving her the choice of looking at him or not. "I'm studying renal anyway. You'd be doing me a favor, honestly. The loop of Henle is a nightmare."

A long silence. The clock over the returns bin ticked. Somewhere in the stacks a radiator knocked its old complaint.

"Why are you like this?" she said finally. Her voice was wrecked, sandpapered, and she seemed surprised by it, as if she hadn't spoken in longer than an evening.

"Like what?"

"This." A small motion of her ruined voice taking in him, the desk, the coat on her chair, the pencil. "Nobody taught you to do this. Or somebody did, and it cost you."

Teo considered lying, or deflecting, and found he didn't want to. Four a.m. made honest men of them all; it was practically library policy.

"My mom died when I was twenty-three," he said. "Pancreatic. Fast. I did the whole thing—you know, the hospital vigils, the insurance calls, the funeral where everyone told me how strong I was, which is a thing people say when they mean *I'm glad it's you and not me.* And afterward I picked up night shifts everywhere I could find them. CNA work, mostly. Because at night, nobody needs you to be okay. They just need you to be there. It's the only job where showing up is the entire qualification." He turned a flashcard over without reading it. "Took me about two years and one very blunt preceptor to figure out I wasn't working nights. I was hiding in them. There's a difference, and it shows eventually. Usually at 2:47 on a Tuesday."

Silas looked at him then—really looked, the first time in eight months—and Teo watched her decide whether to be angry and watched the anger lose to something else.

"You counted the time," she said.

"Habit. We chart everything."

Another silence, but a different species of silence. A inhabited one.

"It was the letters," she said, so quietly he almost missed it. "There's a box. From my aunt. Unsent letters, thirty years old, addressed to me. I've had it for—" her hand made a vague shape in the air, "—a while. I haven't broken the tape. Tonight I almost did. And my body apparently has opinions about that."

Teo nodded slowly, the way you nod at a patient describing pain you can't reach but believe completely.

"For what it's worth," he said, "you don't have to earn the opening. Of the box, or—or anything else. People think healing's a payment plan. Do the work, get the relief. Mostly it's just—" he searched for it, "—letting yourself be carried for a minute. By whoever showed up. Which tonight was me, and a coat, and a pencil."

Silas looked down at the pencil. Mr. Bao's eighteenth pencil, yellow, sharpened, ordinary. She picked it up and rolled it once across the blotter, and something in her face moved—some tectonic adjustment far below the surface, the kind you feel rather than see.

"The well doesn't apologize," she repeated.

"Nope. Apparently that's a whole proverb."

* * *

They closed at five, as always, and the ritual unfolded around Silas like water around a stone: Odile returning for her coat with a nod that contained an entire conversation; Mr. Bao folding his newspaper into precise eighths; Teo stacking chairs. Silas stood at the door while they set the alarm, and when they stepped out into the blue-black cold of the earliest hour, Odile fell into step on her left and Teo on her right, unasked, unremarked, a formation so natural that none of them commented on it, and that was the point of it.

They walked her to Vell Street. Nobody suggested it; it simply happened, three sets of footsteps falling into rhythm, breath steaming under the streetlights. At her corner Odile stopped.

"Same time tomorrow," she said. It was not a question and it was not about the shift schedule, and Silas heard it correctly, because she nodded once, gravely, the way she received tribute.

Teo walked the extra block with her, to her actual door, because he could tell she wanted company and would never ask, and because the city at this hour belonged to them—to the night people, the ones who kept the lights on in the lighthouse while everyone else slept.

At her steps she paused. Her keys were in her hand. She didn't put them in the lock.

"Teo."

"Yeah."

"That box. It's in my apartment. Under the bed." She was looking at the door, not at him. "I've been telling myself I'm waiting for the right time. I think I've been waiting for someone to sit next to me while I do it. Which is—" her jaw worked, "—not something I know how to ask for."

"You just did," Teo said. "That was asking. Textbook. I should give you a card."

Something escaped her that was ninety percent laugh. "Tomorrow night? After close? Here, I guess. I can't—I don't think I can do it in the apartment. Too many walls that already know."

"Here," he agreed. "After close. I'll bring the honey."

She let herself in. Through the frosted glass of the door he watched her silhouette stand unmoving in the entryway for a long moment, and then climb the stairs, and he stood on the sidewalk in the cold feeling the specific fullness that comes from being allowed to help—until he remembered, with a lurch, the box itself. The unsent letters. One of which, Ruthie had mentioned once, over tea Silas hadn't known about, was the last letter of a father who had left, and whose final page no one had ever read.

Tomorrow night, then. Tape broken. Whatever was inside it, finally speaking.

Teo turned up his collar and walked home through the waking edges of the city, already rehearsing how to be quiet enough to listen.
