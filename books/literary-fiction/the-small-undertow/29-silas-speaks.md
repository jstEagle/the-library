## 29. Silas Speaks

The letter was still on the desk when the library emptied.

Teo had gone at two, pressing his palm flat against the counter on his way out—their new gesture, wordless, meaning *I'm here if you need me*—and Mr. Bao had folded his newspaper into quarters and left it squared at the edge of the table like a man setting a place for someone who wasn't coming back. Odile had lingered longest, standing at the biography shelves without pretending to browse, until finally she'd said, "Go home when you're ready. Not before," and let herself out into the blue hour before dawn.

Now it was four in the morning and the building hummed around Silas the way it always did, that low electrical drone she'd spent twenty years mistaking for silence. The letter lay where she'd left it, her father's handwriting marching up the page in lines that leaned slightly right, as if even his sentences were trying to get somewhere. *I couldn't stay and watch her do that to you,* he'd written. *I told myself I was leaving for other reasons. There were no other reasons.* And then, at the bottom, in a hand that pressed so hard the pen had nearly torn through: *Silas. Speak.*

She had read it eleven times. She knew because she'd counted, the way she counted everything—returns shelved, minutes elapsed, increments of a bid she couldn't afford. Counting was how she'd survived. Counting was also, she understood now, how she'd hidden. A number could not be blamed. A number did not drown.

She picked up the phone.

Marion's number sat in her contacts under a single M, no name attached, because naming things gave them weight and Silas had spent three decades keeping her mother light enough to carry. The time difference meant it would be one o'clock in Arizona. Marion slept badly—she'd said so on every call for twenty years, weather and deflection, the choreography of avoidance performed so smoothly it had almost passed for conversation—and so there was a decent chance she'd be awake. And if she wasn't, well. Silas could leave a message. She could leave thirty years of messages. The voicemail would hold what the family never had.

Her thumb hovered over the call button.

*What happened at the water?* That was the question Odile had asked her to say aloud, the question everyone had arranged their silences around, and Silas had been circling it for weeks now like a moth against glass—able to see the shape of the answer, unable to pass through. But sitting here with her father's letter open beside her, she realized the question had changed. It wasn't *what happened at the water* anymore. Ruthie had given her that: a slip, a current, seconds, a nine-year-old girl who reached and missed and reached and missed while the river took her brother by the ankles. What happened at the water was an accident. What happened after the water was the wound that had never closed.

What happened after the water was a mother who turned from the riverbank and looked at her surviving child and said, in a voice Silas could still feel along her spine, *Where were you?*

And a nine-year-old girl who had answered the only way she could. By disappearing. By going mute for a year, then quiet for thirty. By becoming a person who cataloged other people's returns at three in the morning because returns were safe—books came back, books always came back, no book had ever been pulled under by an undertow and kept.

She pressed the button.

It rang once. Twice. On the third ring there was a rustle, the sound of a body turning over in sheets, and then Marion's voice, thick with sleep but sharpening fast: "Silas? What's wrong?"

Nothing was wrong. Everything was wrong. That was the old arrangement too—nothing wrong, nothing ever named, the whole family standing in a burning house complimenting one another on the warmth.

"Mom." The word came out strange. She hadn't called her Mom since—she tried to remember and couldn't, which was its own kind of evidence. For twenty years it had been Marion, the name her mother had chosen for herself out in Arizona, the name of a woman who wore linen and drank iced tea and had opinions about succulents, a woman assembled piece by piece to replace the one who had stood on a riverbank in August and broken in half. "I read Dad's letter."

A silence on the line. Not the empty kind. The loaded kind, the kind that has furniture in it.

"Ruthie gave you the box," Marion said finally. Her voice had gone careful, the way voices go when someone steps onto ice and is testing whether it will hold. "She had no right."

"She had every right. You just didn't want to be the one."

"Silas—"

"I'm not finished." Her own voice surprised her. It didn't shake. It was steady in the way her hands had learned to be steady over the legal letters and the auction notices and the reshelving cart—practiced steadiness, steadiness built plank by plank over twenty years of nights. But underneath it, for the first time, there was something moving. Something alive. "I've been not-finishing this conversation my whole life. So I'm going to finish it now, and you're going to listen, because you owe me thirty years of listening and we both know it."

The line held. Somewhere in Arizona a ceiling fan ticked; she could hear it, faintly, the way she could hear everything tonight, every frequency in the world suddenly tuned to her.

"I got your letter," Silas said. "The first one. From the week after the funeral. You wrote that you were sorry. You wrote that you'd said something terrible and that you'd take it back if you could, that you'd spend your life taking it back." She looked down at the page, though she didn't need to; the words had burned themselves into her. *I don't know why I said it. I know it isn't true. Please, baby, please don't believe it.* "And then you never sent it. And then you never said it. And instead you moved four thousand miles away and changed your name and called me twice a year to talk about rainfall."

"You were a child," Marion whispered. "You were a baby. I thought—I thought if I said it out loud it would make it real, that I'd really done it, that I'd really—" A breath, ragged. "That I'd really done that to you."

"It was already real." Silas heard her voice drop, heard it go young. "Do you understand that? It was real the moment you said it. *Where were you?* I was right there, Mom. I was six feet away. I reached for him. I want you to know that, I need you to know that—I reached for him and my fingers touched his sleeve and the water took him anyway. I have replayed those four seconds every day for thirty years. Every day. Do you know what that does to a person? Do you know what it does to a nine-year-old to be handed a death like it's a job?"

"I know." Marion was crying now; Silas could hear it, the wet catch in the breath, and some distant part of her registered that she had never once heard her mother cry on the phone, not in twenty years of calls about weather. "I know, sweetheart, I—"

"No." The word came out harder than she intended, and she let it stand. "Don't call me sweetheart yet. Don't soften it yet. Because I did the math, Mom. I've been doing the math all month—you'd appreciate that, you always said I got the numbers from you. Thirty years. Thirty years of not speaking. Of working nights so I wouldn't have to see people. Of not owning anything, not signing anything, not putting my name on a lease or a plant or a life. Of being a tenant everywhere, including inside my own head. And all of it—every year of it—grew out of one sentence you said on a riverbank because your grief needed somewhere to go and I was the nearest body."

The fan ticked. The library hummed. Silas realized she was standing now, though she didn't remember rising, one hand braced on the returns cart, its metal cold under her palm.

"And I forgave you," she said. "That's the part that breaks my heart. I forgave you years ago, probably, quietly, without telling anyone, because that's what I do—I absorb things, I shelve them, I put them where nobody sees them. I forgave you the way you forgive weather. But forgiveness isn't the same thing as silence, and I confused them, and you let me confuse them, because the silence worked for both of us. It kept you from having to say sorry and it kept me from having to hear it. We built a whole family out of that gap. Dad left because of it. Did you know that? He says so. In writing. He couldn't watch what you were doing to me, so he left, and then it was just the two of us performing a mother and daughter for strangers, and neither of us ever once said his name in front of the other."

"Anders," Marion said. Just the name. It cracked down the middle as she said it, split open like river ice in spring.

"Anders," Silas agreed, and her eyes were burning now, and she let them. "I miss him."

Three words. She'd rehearsed them in the shower, on the ferry stairs, walking Vell Street's cracked sidewalk past the house with its boarded windows. She'd assumed they would come out broken. Instead they came out whole, and enormous, filling the phone line the way water fills a hull.

"I miss him every day. I miss his stupid tin of rocks. I miss him humming when he ate. I miss the way he'd wait for me at the top of the hill even though waiting was torture for him, even though he was the most impatient kid alive, he waited, every single time, because I was his little sister and that was the law. And I have never once said that out loud in thirty years because saying it meant admitting he was gone, and admitting he was gone meant admitting the day it happened, and the day it happened was the day you looked at me like I was the reason the sky fell." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand. "So here it is. All of it. Are you listening?"

"I'm listening."

"The river took him. Not me. A current took him, and a slip of mud, and four seconds that no nine-year-old on earth could have beaten. It was an accident. It was an accident, and it wasn't my fault."

The words went out of her like a tide going out—there was no pulling them back, and she didn't want to, and she felt the strangest sensation as they left, as if a room in her chest that had been sealed since childhood had suddenly opened a window and the air coming through smelled like cut grass and sunscreen and the copper-and-mud smell of the river in late summer, Anders's smell, the last ordinary morning's smell.

"It wasn't my fault," she said again, quieter, testing whether it would hold. It held. "And I miss him. Both things are true at the same time. Nobody taught me they could both be true at the same time."

On the other end of the line, Marion made a sound Silas had never heard her make—not a word, not quite a sob, something older than either, the sound of a woman setting down something she'd carried so long her spine had grown around it. The fan ticked. A car passed somewhere on Marion's street, tires hissing on hot pavement, and Silas thought absurdly: *it's still summer there. It's always summer there. That's why she chose it. She's been trying to live in a season that never ends so she never has to reach August.*

"Say something," Silas said. Her voice was wrecked and she didn't care. "Please. Anything true."

The line breathed. Once. Twice. And Silas stood in the empty library at four in the morning with her father's letter open on the desk and her brother's name in her mouth for the first time in decades, and she waited—waited the way she had never let herself wait for anything, without counting, without a fallback plan, without the exit mapped—waited for her mother to become, in whatever small imperfect way a person can become it after thirty years, someone who answered.

"Silas," Marion began, and stopped, and started again, and Silas gripped the cart and listened.
