## 1. Borrowed Words

The minister said *beloved grandmother* the way a man sets down something heavy, and Marguerite translated it in her head before she could stop herself. *Geliefde grootmoeder.* No—not quite. The dialect her grandmother spoke had no word for beloved that sat comfortably in the mouth; it had a word that meant *carried-in-the-heart*, two syllables folded around a soft consonant she could feel in her chest but not produce. She had been translating professionally for twenty-six years. She could move between nine languages with the ease of a woman changing coats. And she could not find that word.

She sat in the front pew of St. Aldric's with her hands folded in her lap like an exhibit, and she listened to the eulogy the way she listened to everything now: as source text.

*Margaret Sterling was a woman of quiet faith.* — Her grandmother had not been quiet. Her grandmother had sung while she kneaded bread, songs with no names, songs in a language that belonged to no nation, that had come down through mill towns and border villages and the mouths of women who'd been told to stop speaking it. Marguerite had learned those songs before she learned English. They were the first words she ever owned.

*A devoted mother and matriarch.* — Matriarch. The word made her think of the kitchen table, the way her grandmother's hands would go still over the flour when anyone from outside came to the door, as if the language itself were something to be covered with a cloth.

The minister moved on to the Twenty-Third Psalm, and Marguerite gave up on him and looked instead at the coffin, at the spray of white chrysanthemums her mother had chosen because they were dignified, and she tried—she genuinely tried—to remember the lullaby. The one about the river taking the moon's reflection but never the moon itself. She could hear the melody perfectly. She could hum it, if she dared, right there in the pew. But the words had gone the way water goes from a held hand: not all at once, but steadily, so that one day you looked down and your palm was only wet, and then not even that.

When had it happened? She tried to reconstruct it the way she reconstructed any translation problem: by working backward from what remained. She still had *goodnight*. She still had the word for *bread*, though she suspected she was pronouncing it wrong now, anglicized, flattened. She had lost the word for *river*, which seemed like a cruelty—the song was about a river—and she had lost the word for *stay*, which was worse, because that was the word her grandmother had used most, holding her at the door of the kitchen on winter evenings. *Stay. Stay a little.*

She did not have it anymore. She checked, the way you check a pocket you know is empty, and it was empty.

Beside her, her brother Theo sat with his jaw set in the shape of competence. He had organized this funeral the way he organized everything—florists confirmed, obituary placed in two papers, the reception catered by the same firm that had done Uncle Edmund's service. He wore grief the way he wore his suit: correctly, expensively, without a single wrinkle out of place. Their mother sat between them, upright as a fence post, her gloved hands resting on her black handbag, her face arranged into an expression Marguerite thought of privately as *the family weather*: composed, gray, permitting nothing through.

The church was full. That surprised her a little—her grandmother had outlived nearly everyone—but the pews held neighbors from the town, old customers of the shop, people whose faces Marguerite half-recognized from childhood summers. Behind them, near the back wall where the light through the stained glass went thin and cold, stood a woman Marguerite did not recognize at all.

She was perhaps sixty, tall, wearing a grey wool coat that had been good once and was now simply honest—mended at the cuff, buttoned to the throat despite the warmth of the room. She did not sit. She stood against the wall with the stillness of someone who has attended many funerals and learned how to occupy one without disturbing it. Her hair was iron-colored, cut short, practical. She watched the coffin with an attention that felt less like mourning than like accounting.

Marguerite looked away, then back. The woman hadn't moved.

It was during the final hymn that it happened. The congregation rose, and the organ swelled into something safe and familiar, and Marguerite stood with her family, mouthing words she'd known since childhood, and across the heads of the mourners the woman in the grey coat turned her gaze from the coffin—from the coffin, finally—and found Marguerite's face and held it.

And mouthed a name.

Not *Sterling*. Marguerite saw the shape of it clearly, three syllables, the middle one open and round, the lips doing something English didn't ask them to do. It was not a word she knew. It was not a name she knew. But the woman said it silently with such precision, such deliberate clarity, that it landed on Marguerite like a stone dropped down a well—she heard it all the way to the bottom of herself, in some place that answered before her mind could object.

Then the hymn ended, and the woman turned back to the coffin, and the moment closed over like water.

* * *

Afterward there was the graveside, wind coming off the hills, the gravediggers waiting with the patient rudeness of men who had another service at two. Theo shook hands. Her mother received condolences like tribute. Marguerite stood at the lip of the grave and watched the coffin go down and thought: *I should say something. In her language. Whatever is left of it.*

She opened her mouth.

What came out was silence, and then, quietly, in English: "Goodbye." A word her grandmother had pronounced with a foreign weight all her life, *good-bye* stretched into two full notes, because it wasn't really her word either. Everything her grandmother had said in English was borrowed. Everything Marguerite had left of hers was borrowed too. They had met, at the end, in a language that belonged to neither of them, and Marguerite had called that intimacy, and maybe it was, and maybe it was also a slow theft she hadn't noticed until the thief was dead.

She became aware that the woman in the grey coat was standing four feet away, at the edge of the gathered family, hatless in the wind, not pretending to grieve and not apologizing for it.

"You're not family," Theo said, without turning fully, in the tone he used for redirecting salesmen.

"No," the woman said. Her voice was low and even, with an accent Marguerite couldn't place—something eastern, something worn smooth by decades of elsewhere. "I'm here for her, though. I've been waiting a long time to be able to say that."

Her mother's head turned a degree, no more. "I don't believe we've met."

"No," the woman agreed. "You haven't." She looked at the grave, at the raw earth stacked beside it on the canvas, and something crossed her face that was not simple. Grief, yes, but under it something older and harder, the way bedrock sits under soil. "She was kind to my family once. When kindness cost something."

"That's very nice," Theo said, in the voice that meant *this conversation is over*, and extended his hand in the gesture that meant it even more firmly. "Thank you for coming."

The woman ignored the hand. She was looking at Marguerite again, and there was something in the look that Marguerite recognized from her own work—a reader who knows a text better than its author does. "You have her ears," the woman said. "Did you know that? The same ears."

"I'm sorry," Marguerite began, "who are you?"

For a moment the woman seemed about to answer. Then her eyes went past Marguerite's shoulder—to the house visible beyond the cemetery wall, grey slate roof among the elms, the house Marguerite had grown up visiting, the house that smelled of beeswax and cardamom and, underneath everything, of locked doors—and whatever she saw there changed her mind about something.

"Another time," she said. "There will be time now."

She drew a card from her coat pocket—cream stock, plain type—and held it out. Marguerite took it before she decided to. On it was a name, *I. Voss*, and an address in the old mill district, the part of town the city kept promising to redevelop and never did.

"Voss," Marguerite read aloud, and the name sat strangely in her mouth, and the woman watched her say it with an expression Marguerite would spend weeks trying to name. It was not satisfaction. It was closer to hunger finally allowed to eat.

"Say it again," the woman said softly.

Behind her, her mother said, sharply, "Marguerite."

And Marguerite—who had spent her whole life being fluent in other people's silences, who could read a room in six languages—heard in that single word every instruction she had ever been given: *don't ask, don't repeat, don't remember.* She heard it, and she understood it completely, and she heard herself say it anyway.

"Voss," she said again.

The woman nodded once, as if a debt had been acknowledged, and walked away across the wet grass toward the road, her grey coat darkening at the hem with damp, not looking back.

"What an odd woman," her mother said, in the tone reserved for things that would be dealt with later, thoroughly, and never spoken of again.

Theo laughed, short and dismissive. "Cemetery loonies. Every funeral has one."

But Marguerite stood looking at the card in her glove, at the address in the mill district, at the name that had sounded in her mouth like a key turning in a lock she hadn't known her house had—and behind her the gravediggers began their work, and the first spadeful of earth hit the coffin with a sound like a door closing, or like a door opening, she couldn't yet tell which.
