## 2. The Grey Coat Stranger

The reception after the funeral was held in the house, because my mother believed grief should be served on good china.

I stood in the kitchen doorway with a glass of wine I wasn't drinking and watched the room perform itself. Neighbors in dark wool. Theo's colleagues from the firm, their ties loosened exactly one inch. My mother moving through it all with the serene efficiency of a woman who had rehearsed this day for years—directing caterers, refilling plates, accepting condolences with a nod calibrated to suggest both devastation and composure. She had polished the banisters that morning. I'd heard her at six a.m., rag in hand, working the wood until it shone like something that had never held a handprint.

*Your grandmother would have hated this,* I thought, and then tried to think it in her language, the way I used to think everything in her language when I was small, and found nothing there. Not silence exactly. Silence implies an edge, a shape where sound used to be. This was more like reaching into a pocket you've carried your whole life and finding the lining torn through, the coin long gone, only the faint worn path where it used to sit against your hip.

I had spent the eulogy translating. That's what I do; it's the only skill I've ever trusted. The minister spoke in careful, generic English—*a life of service, a woman of quiet faith*—and in my head I rendered him into French, then German, then back again, because if I kept the words moving between languages they couldn't land on me. But somewhere around *she will be sorely missed*, I caught myself trying to translate into the other tongue, the one that belonged to her, the one she sang to me in over the sink when I was four years old with flour on my hands—and my mind came up empty. Not one word. Not the word for bread. Not the word for *stay*.

She had died speaking it, probably. Alone in that upstairs room, calling out to someone in a language no one left alive could answer her in.

I set down the untouched wine.

That was when I noticed the grey coat.

It hung at the back of the parlor, near the bookshelf nobody used, on a woman who did not move like a mourner. Mourners lean. They hold their elbows in, make themselves smaller, apologetic about taking up space among the bereaved. This woman stood square, weight even on both feet, hands folded in front of her like a person waiting for a train that is definitely coming. She was perhaps sixty, grey-haired under a dark scarf, with a face that might once have been handsome and had decided long ago to stop spending effort on the question. Her eyes were doing what mine had been doing all afternoon: reading the room. Translating it.

She caught me looking. She didn't look away.

There is a moment—I know it from conferences and embassy dinners and every awkward cocktail hour of my professional life—when a stranger across a room decides whether you are approachable. Most people decide no. This woman decided yes with the speed of someone who had already done the deciding before she arrived.

She crossed the room unhurried. People parted around her without knowing why.

"You're the granddaughter," she said. "The translator."

"Marguerite Sterling." I offered my hand because my mother raised me to, and the woman took it briefly, her grip dry and cool and brief as a stamp.

"Wren," she said—not my name, but close enough to startle me—"you were watching the door all through the service. And during the eulogy your lips moved. Counting? Or translating?"

"Translating," I admitted. "Habit."

"What language?"

"Italian, mostly. At the end." I hesitated. "And one I don't speak anymore."

Something moved behind her eyes—not sympathy, nothing so soft. Recognition, maybe. The look of a woman confirming a fact she'd written down years ago.

"She sang to you in it," the woman said. "When you were small. In this house."

The floor seemed to tilt a degree, the way a boat does when someone shifts their weight. "How could you possibly know that?"

Instead of answering, she turned her head slightly, toward the hall—the long dim corridor with its framed photographs of Sterlings being respectable in successive decades—and said, in a low clear voice, a single word.

I didn't understand it. That was the terrible thing. It went through me like a bell struck in another room, familiar in its shape, in its music, in the exact way my grandmother's voice used to rise at the end of a sentence—and I could not tell you what it meant. It sat in my chest untranslated, a letter addressed to me in a hand I recognized from childhood bills and birthday cards.

"That's not our name," I said, before I knew I was going to say anything.

"No," the woman agreed. "It isn't."

"My grandmother's name was Sterling. This is the Sterling house."

"Yes," she said again, and now there was something almost gentle in it, which was worse. "So it says."

"Theo!" My brother's voice arrived ahead of him, bright and carrying, the voice he used with clients. He materialized at my elbow in his funeral suit, glass in hand, smile already deployed. "Marguerite, Mrs.—?" He extended his hand toward the stranger with the smooth entitlement of a man who assumed the world had been waiting to be introduced to him.

"Voss," she said. She did not take his hand.

"Voss," Theo repeated pleasantly. "Family friend of Mother's?"

"I'm here for Edith," the woman said. "For what she kept. And what she owed."

Theo laughed—a short, polite bark, the laugh he used when a client made a joke beneath responding to. "Well. She was much loved. If you'd like to leave your details, we're compiling—"

"I know what this family compiles." Her gaze moved past him, down the hall, and stopped on the wardrobe.

You have to understand: every house has furniture, but not every house has *furniture*. The hall wardrobe was ours in the second sense. Dark walnut, carved with vines, older than any photograph on the walls, standing at the turn of the corridor like a sentry. As children we were forbidden to open it—not locked, just forbidden, which in our house was stronger than any lock. My grandmother used to rest her hand flat on its door when she passed, the way some people touch a mezuzah or a saint's foot. I had asked her once, age seven, what was inside. She had looked at me for a long moment and said, in English, carefully: *Coats.* Then, softer, in the other language, something else—and I had laughed, because whatever she'd said sounded funny, and she'd laughed too, and kissed my hair, and I never learned what it meant.

The woman in the grey coat looked at the wardrobe the way you look at a grave you dug yourself.

Then she turned back to me, and from inside the coat she produced a card—heavy stock, plain, no logo—and pressed it into my palm with a precision that made it feel less like a gift than a summons.

"If you want to know what your grandmother was keeping," she said, "and what she was paying for. Tuesday. Any hour."

On the card, in small type: a name—*I. Voss*—and an address in the old mill district, the streets where the town used to keep its looms and its laborers and everyone it preferred not to see. Below the address, handwritten in faded ink, two words in that same language I couldn't read. The letters leaned like people walking into wind.

"This is inappropriate," Theo said, stepping between us, his pleasantness thinning to show the wire underneath. "This is my mother's home. Whatever you think you—"

"Think?" Ida Voss looked at him for the first time, fully, and I watched my brother—who had never once been looked *through* in his life—take half a step back. "I don't think, Mr. Sterling. I remember. There's a difference, and you'll learn it."

She nodded to me—just me—and let herself out. Through the front window I watched her go down the drive without hurrying, grey coat fading into grey afternoon, a woman leaving a house she had apparently been waiting her whole life to enter and hadn't needed to.

* * *

Theo waited until the last guest had gone, until the caterers' van pulled away and my mother climbed the stairs with a candle to check on nothing in particular. Then he closed the kitchen door and said, "Whatever she gave you, give it to me."

"A card." I put it on the table between us rather than in his hand. "An address. In the mill district."

He picked it up, read it, and performed dismissal the way he performed everything—thoroughly, with witnesses. "Some crank. Funerals attract them. Grief tourists, conspiracy aunties, people who knew the deceased 'better than anyone.' You get a strange one every time." He flipped the card over, saw the handwriting, frowned for half a second—only half—and set it face-down. "Mother will want it gone."

"Theo. She said a name. In the hallway. She said it looking at the wardrobe, and Gran used to touch that wardrobe every day of her life, and I never once saw what was inside it, and today a stranger calls us by another name and you're standing there telling me it's a *grief tourist*—"

"Margo." He used the childhood name deliberately, the way you'd use a hand on someone's shoulder to steer them. "Gran is three days dead. Mom hasn't slept. The estate papers come Thursday. What you're feeling right now is grief wearing a costume, and that woman handed it a business card." He slid the card across the table to me, face-down, gentle as a doctor. "Throw it away. Please. For Mom."

I took it. I told myself I was taking it to throw it away.

My mother was in the study when I passed, feeding the fire—not because the house was cold, but because burning things requires a witness, and she had always understood ceremony better than anyone I knew. She saw the card in my hand. She held out her palm, and I gave it to her, and she read it—both sides, the leaning words, the mill district address—with a stillness that lasted three full seconds too long.

Three seconds is nothing. Three seconds is also, in a translator's ear, an entire unspoken paragraph.

Then she dropped it into the fire and watched the corner catch, and the flame walked up the heavy stock, and the handwritten words curled black and rose, unreadable, ash by ash.

"Some people," my mother said, poking the coals flat, "collect other families' ghosts. Don't let her give you hers."

*Other families'* ghosts. I stood in the doorway and turned the phrase over, and it did not fit together the way she meant it to fit. Other families'. As if ghosts came assigned. As if ours were settled, accounted for, buried at proper depth.

Upstairs, the house ticked and settled around its new silence. Down the hall, at the turn of the corridor, the wardrobe stood shut, and I thought—for the first time not as a child but as a translator, a professional reader of what is withheld—that my grandmother had touched it every day of her life the way you touch a debt.

The card was ash. The address was not.

I had read it twice. Mill district, blue door, third floor. *Tuesday.*

Tomorrow.
