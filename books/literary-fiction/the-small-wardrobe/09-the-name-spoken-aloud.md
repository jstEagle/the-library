## 9. The Name Spoken Aloud

The probate court sat in a converted customs house on the river, all brass fittings and tall windows that turned the morning light the color of weak tea. Marguerite had dressed for it the way she dressed for funerals—dark wool, no jewelry—and then, standing before her grandmother's mirror at dawn, she had taken off the dark wool and put on instead the grey blouse Ida Voss had pressed into her hands two nights before. *Wear it when you speak,* Ida had said. *It was my great-grandmother's. It has been in rooms like this one before.*

The letters were in her satchel, wrapped in linen. Eleven of them, in her grandmother's hand, half in English and half in the dialect that had no name on any map—the language of the river villages, the singing language, the one Marguerite had spent thirty years translating everyone else's words because she could not keep hold of her own.

Her mother sat three rows back, spine straight as a banister, gloved hands folded. Theo sat beside his lawyer, a soft-bellied man named Corliss who smelled of pipe tobacco and discretion. Neither of them would look at her. That was fine. She had stopped needing to be looked at somewhere around four o'clock this morning, when she had read the last letter aloud to an empty kitchen and heard her own voice come out in a language she thought was dead.

"All rise."

She rose with the rest of them. The registrar, a woman near retirement with reading glasses on a chain, took her seat and opened the file without ceremony. "In the matter of the estate of Edith Sterling. We are here to determine disposition per the terms of the will, which leaves the whole of the estate—to whom do I have counsel?"

Corliss stood. "For Theodore Sterling, primary beneficiary and executor designate." He said *designate* the way a man lays a card face-down on a table.

"And the other claimant?"

Marguerite stood. Her knees were steady, which surprised her. "I'm not represented," she said. "But I'm not a claimant either. Not anymore."

A murmur moved through the small gallery like wind through wheat. The registrar looked over her glasses. "Then state your purpose, Ms.—"

"Sterling," Marguerite said, and let the word sit there, ugly and borrowed, while the room absorbed it. "That's the purpose."

* * *

Theo's lawyer had filed first thing: a motion to exclude certain documents from consideration as unauthenticated, possibly forged, prejudicial to an orderly administration of the estate. Corliss made his argument in eleven minutes. He used the word *sentimental* twice and the word *irrelevant* four times. He described the letters as "the product of grief and manipulation by parties with a documented grievance"—Ida, seated in the second row in her good coat, did not flinch—"and I'd remind the court that anyone can produce paper. What matters is provenance."

"Provenance," Marguerite repeated, when it was her turn. She stood at the little table they'd cleared for her, and she did not open her notes, because she had decided at dawn that she wouldn't need them. "Yes. Let's talk about provenance."

She unwrapped the linen. She laid the letters out one by one, and the registrar leaned forward despite herself, because even folded flat you could see the age of them, the foxing along the edges, the iron-gall ink gone brown as old blood.

"These are in my grandmother's hand. I grew up watching her write grocery lists in this hand. Birthday cards. Checks. I know her capital E the way I know my own face—it leans left, like it's trying to leave the page. If the court wants authentication, there are forty years of correspondence in the county archive to compare against. My brother knows where it is. He helped me find the boxes."

"That is not what these proceedings—"

"But provenance isn't only handwriting," Marguerite went on, and her voice did something she didn't authorize; it dropped lower, steadier, the voice she used at podiums when a poem needed to land. "Provenance is where a thing comes from. So. Where does the name Sterling come from?"

Silence. The tea-colored light lay across the floorboards between her and the bench.

"In 1943, a family named Voss lived at the mill end of Water Street. They held the deed to the house my family still occupies. When the roundups came through the district, my grandmother's neighbor—a man named Emil Voss—came to her door with his wife and his daughter and asked for shelter. And she gave it. I want that understood. Whatever else happened, she opened the door. She hid them in the hall wardrobe for eleven months, and the wardrobe was built for that purpose, deeper than any wall allows, bolted from the inside, ventilated through the chimney flue. I've stood inside it. There are pencil marks on the inner wall where a child measured herself against the winter."

Ida's breath caught audibly behind her. Marguerite kept her eyes forward.

"When it was over, Emil Voss was dying—he'd been injured in the last weeks, badly, hiding in the cold—and he made my grandmother a promise in exchange for the one thing he had left. His name. His legal identity, his papers, his deed. Take it, he told her, and keep the house safe until my daughter can return to claim it. Keep our name alive. And my grandmother wrote him a letter saying yes. This is that letter." She held it up. "And then she never gave the name back. Because a name, once worn long enough, starts to fit. Because the daughter married out of the district under another name to survive, and the house was comfortable, and the war receded, and every year it became harder to say *this was never ours.* Until the lie wasn't a lie anymore. Until it was just the family. Until it was just us."

"Your Honor," Corliss began, "this is theater—"

"It's testimony," Marguerite said. "And it isn't finished."

* * *

Here is what she had learned about translation, in thirty years of it: that you cannot translate a living language from memory alone. Memory smooths. Memory prettifies. You need the text in front of you, the actual marks on the actual page, or the words come out wearing your clothes instead of their own.

She had the texts. Eleven letters. And she had spent the night with them the way she'd once spent nights with other people's poems—pencil in hand, ear bent close, letting the dialect come up out of the paper and re-inhabit her mouth. It came slowly at first, like blood returning to a numb limb, painful and then warm. By the third letter she was weeping and reading at the same time. By the eleventh she was fluent again, or something adjacent to fluent, the language of her childhood rising in her throat like water finding its old channel.

Now, in the customs house, with the registrar watching and her mother's gloves white-knuckled in the third row, Marguerite picked up the first letter and read it aloud.

Not the English half. The other half.

"*Liewer Emel,*" she began, and the syllables came out of her whole and unbroken, the vowels broad as the river, the consonants soft as river mud. "*Du host mir din nam gegebn und ikh hab en drogen als en mantel—*" Dear Emil. You gave me your name and I wore it like a coat—

The room changed. She felt it happen. The registrar went very still. Somewhere behind her, someone whispered, and someone else shushed them. The dialect had not been spoken aloud in that building—in that whole city, perhaps—in sixty years, and it filled the tall windows and the brass rails and the tea-colored light like something returning to a house it remembered.

She read the confession entire. *I wore it like a coat and then forgot it was ever yours. I raised children in it. I buried a husband under it. Forgive me the comfort. Forgive me the years. The house holds a door that opens inward; tell your blood where the key sleeps.*

She read the second letter, and the third. Emil's answer, dictated to a nurse, thanking Edith for keeping the promise of shelter and asking nothing—not the house, not the name, only that the child's pencil marks be left on the wardrobe wall, so that someone, someday, might know a girl had lived there and grown.

By the fifth letter, Marguerite was not performing. She was speaking to her grandmother across the distance the way she had spoken to her across the kitchen table, and the language between them was no longer lost, had never been lost, had only been waiting in the letters like seeds in a drawer.

When she finished, the silence had a texture to it. The registrar removed her glasses and pressed the bridge of her nose.

"The court will want those documents examined," she said at last. "But I'll note for the record that the hand is consistent, the paper is consistent, and the witness has read them in a dialect that, frankly, counsel for Mr. Sterling could not fake if he tried." A dry pause. "Does counsel wish to respond?"

Corliss stood, consulted Theo—who gave a small, sharp shake of the head—and said, "We renew our objection and request a continuance."

"Denied. The will's condition is explicit: the estate passes to whoever keeps the family name." The registrar looked down at the letters, then up at Marguerite. "Ms. Sterling. Do you wish to keep it?"

Marguerite thought of the wardrobe standing open in the hall, empty now, its false back pried away, its inner wall bare except for the pencil marks. She thought of the name Sterling sitting on her like a coat that had never been hers, heavy at the shoulders, smelling of someone else's smoke.

"No," she said. "I surrender it. All of it. The house, the accounts, the name. Let the record show the estate belongs to the Voss family, and always did. Let the name go back where it came from."

* * *

Theo stood up so fast his chair skidded. "You're insane," he said, loud enough for the gallery, loud enough for the record. "You're throwing away everything she built—everything *we* built—for a story. For strangers." His face had gone the color of the light. "Fine. Enjoy being nobody."

He walked out. The doors swung twice behind him and settled.

Corliss gathered his files with the efficiency of a man who had seen worse days for better clients, murmured something about advising his client, and followed.

And then the courtroom was nearly empty, and Marguerite turned, expecting to see her mother gone too—expecting the pew to be vacant, the gloves withdrawn, the silence enforced from a distance as it had been enforced all her life.

Her mother was still there.

She had not moved. Her hands were still folded, but the knuckles had relaxed, and she was looking at the letters spread on the table with an expression Marguerite had never seen on her face—not grief exactly, and not shame, but the particular exhaustion of a woman setting down something very heavy after carrying it a very long way.

"You knew," Marguerite said quietly. "You knew, and you stayed."

Her mother rose. She crossed the floor slowly, and when she reached the table she touched the edge of the topmost letter with one gloved fingertip, as if testing whether it was real.

"She sang to you in that language," her mother said. Her voice was low, stripped of all its polish. "Did you know I understood every word? I understood, and I never said. That was my part in it. Everyone in that house had a part." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious and relieved all at once. "There's something else in the wardrobe, Marguerite. Under the floor of it. She told me once, the week before she died, and I chose—I *chose*—not to look. I couldn't carry two truths. But you apparently can."

Outside, the tea-colored light had shifted toward gold. In the second row, Ida Voss rose from her seat, holding her ledger against her chest like a book of psalms, and waited.
