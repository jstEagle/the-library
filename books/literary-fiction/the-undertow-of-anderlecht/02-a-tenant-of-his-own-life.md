## 2. A Tenant of His Own Life

The rent envelope had been sitting on the hall table since Tuesday, and by Friday morning it had acquired the weight of a small animal I was pretending not to notice.

I wrote the check at the kitchen table with the radio off, because certain transactions deserve silence. Three hundred and forty euros, made out to Dirk Vandevelde, for the house on Rue Wayez that my mother bought in 1962 with money nobody in the family ever explained and nobody in the family ever asked about. The check was the thirty-seventh of its kind, one per month since the winter she died, and in all that time Dirk had never once cashed one late and I had never once asked him the question that sat underneath the whole arrangement like a pipe beneath the floorboards: *why am I paying you rent for a house that should have been mine?*

We were both fluent in this language. It was the only language we shared.

The envelope went into my coat pocket, and the coat went with me into a morning so cold that the street itself seemed to be holding its breath. Anderlecht in January has a particular quality of light—low, gray, industrial, the color of dishwater that has been used too long. The trams ground past on their rails with a sound like a knife being drawn slowly along the underside of the world. I walked the twelve minutes to the market on the Place de la Vaillance the way I walked everywhere: at a pace designed to be forgettable.

This is a skill, forgettability. People think it's a failure of character, but I assure you it can be practiced and refined like any other. You learn to dress in the colors of the buildings. You learn to answer questions with questions, to give your name in a voice so flat that it slides off memory like water off oiled wood. You learn, above all, never to be the most interesting person in any room, street, or conversation. For thirty years I had been the most forgettable man in a neighborhood that prided itself on forgetting things—Anderlecht forgets Erasmus lived here, forgets the abbeys, forgets the slaughterhouses that gave the streets their names. I fit right in. I was a monument to the municipal talent.

At the market, of course, they all knew me. That was the joke of it, the exquisite irony I only admitted to on cold mornings like this one: a man can spend three decades making himself invisible and still be beloved. They knew *Casper*. They knew the piano tuner with the careful hands and the polite smile, the one who fixed Madame Peeters's upright every spring and never charged her full price because her husband had died and her pension was small. They knew I liked my herring pickled and my coffee black and that I always, always paid in cash.

What they did not know could have filled the house I lived in.

They did not know my mother's name, though two of them had lived on the street when she was still alive. They did not know she had been a composer, or that she had died in the front bedroom with the window open in November because she said the house needed to breathe. They did not know that I had found her sitting up in bed with her hands arranged on the blanket as if on keys, fingers curved, mid-phrase, as though the last thing she did in this world was play something she never let anyone hear.

They knew I tuned pianos. They did not know I listened to radiators.

* * *

The market in winter is a study in steam. Breath rises from the crowd in a low fog; the frites stand exhales its grease-sweet cloud; the fishmonger's crushed ice smokes faintly in the cold. I moved through it collecting greetings the way a man walking through cobwebs collects them on his sleeves—without effort, without wanting them, without quite being able to refuse.

"Casper! Good morning, Casper!"

Monsieur Delaunay from the cheese stall, waving a knife at me with the cheerfulness of a man who has been up since four and considers this a moral achievement. "The Gouda came in yesterday. Aged eighteen months. I put one aside for you."

"That's very kind, Delaunay."

"And how is the house? Still standing?" He laughed at his own joke, the same joke he had been making for thirty years. *Still standing.* As if the house were a ruin, as if I were a caretaker of something already dead.

"Still standing."

"And your mother's piano? Still in tune?" This from Madame Okonkwo, who sold flowers two stalls down and had the sharpest eyes of anyone on the square. She was wrapping bare-root rose bushes in newspaper, her hands moving with the certainty of long practice, and she looked at me the way she always looked at me—directly, warmly, and with something underneath the warmth that I had never been able to name and had learned not to examine.

"Nobody plays it," I said, which was true, and which was also the kind of answer that sounds like information and functions as a wall.

"A piano that nobody plays." She shook her head, half smiling. "That's a sad thing, Casper. That's like a letter nobody reads."

I bought the roses from her—red ones, though I had no one to give them to; I put them on my mother's grave because it was the only conversation we still had—and I let her talk about her grandson in Liège and the price of chrysanthemums and the cold coming in from the sea, and I answered in the correct places, and I left knowing nothing about her that she hadn't decided to tell me and having told her nothing about me at all. This was the arrangement. I had thought it was an arrangement we both understood.

I was carrying the roses and the cheese and the rent envelope toward the tram when I understood, with the slow cold certainty of water finding a crack, that something had changed.

It was nothing I could have pointed to. No one was following me—I would have known; I know streets the way other people know faces, and a stranger on the Place de la Vaillance stands out like a wrong note in a familiar chord. It was subtler than that. It was the quality of the attention. For thirty years the neighborhood's gaze had rested on me the way light rests on a wall: evenly, without interest, sliding off. This morning something in that gaze had *focused*. I felt the difference the way a tuner feels a string go from flat to true—not a change in the sound, but a change in the tension underneath it.

At the flower stall, Madame Okonkwo had asked about my mother's piano. She had never once asked about my mother's piano in thirty years. She knew my mother had existed—everyone of a certain age knew that—but the subject had been closed the way a door is closed after a funeral, and no one, in three decades, had so much as laid a hand on the handle.

And Delaunay had said, *still standing*, and laughed, and for half a second before he laughed his eyes had gone to my coat pocket, where the rent envelope was, and then away, too quickly.

I told myself it was nothing. I was very good at telling myself things were nothing. It was, after all, the family trade.

* * *

The bank on Rue Wayez has a machine that takes checks and issues receipts, and I fed the rent envelope into it the way I fed the machine every month, without ceremony, and stood for a moment in the blue fluorescent light listening to the building's heating system tick through its morning cycle. Every building talks. Most people have simply agreed not to hear it. The bank spoke in the flat bureaucratic dialect of boilers and cash drawers, and I understood it perfectly, and it asked me nothing, and I loved it a little for that.

I was halfway home when the feeling of being watched returned—not behind me, but beside me, or rather *around* me, the sense that the day had acquired an audience. I passed the friterie where I had eaten every Friday since 1989 and the woman at the counter said, "See you next week, Casper," and I said, "Next week," and the exchange was so exactly like every other exchange we had ever had that its very perfection suddenly seemed suspicious, like a recording played back too cleanly.

At home, I stood in the hall with the roses dripping melted snow onto the floorboards and did the thing I do when the world becomes illegible: I listened to the house.

The radiators were in their mid-morning register, the low conversational gurgle of a system that has been running since six and has nothing dramatic left to report. The radiator in the hall—the one I think of as 1971, because that was the year my mother stopped playing the song, though I didn't learn to associate the two until much later—was ticking as it cooled, a slow irregular pulse, like a heart deciding whether to keep going. The one in the front room, 1974 by my private calendar, was silent, because that radiator had a valve that stuck in cold weather and had stuck, I sometimes thought, the very week she left the conservatory, the week the letters stopped, the week the house itself seemed to decide to hold its breath.

I know how this sounds. A grown man assigning years to radiators. But when you live alone with a dead woman's silence for thirty years, you furnish the silence with whatever is at hand. The radiators were what was at hand. Each one had a season of her life folded into its iron, the way a piano's soundboard holds every note ever played on it—not audibly, not provably, but *structurally*, in the grain.

I put the roses in water. I did not take them to the cemetery. I stood at the kitchen window instead, looking out at the yard where the coal cellar hatch made its dark rectangle in the frost-killed grass, and I thought about Madame Okonkwo's face when she had said *a letter nobody reads*.

She had said it lightly. Everything on that square is said lightly; it is a neighborhood that communicates in the conditional mood. But there had been something under it. A test. The way you tap a wall to find out what's behind the plaster.

And I realized, standing there with the frost on the glass and the roses bleeding color into the water, that in thirty years of perfecting my invisibility I had never once considered the possibility that someone else in this neighborhood had spent those same thirty years *watching*. That while I was curating my anonymity—answering in questions, dressing in the colors of buildings, paying in cash—someone else had been keeping accounts. That the warmth of the market might not be ignorance at all. It might be patience.

The telephone rang.

It rang the way it always rang, an ugly mechanical jangle from the hall, and I let it ring three times, because I always let it ring three times, because a man who answers on the first ring is a man who is waiting, and I had built my whole life on never appearing to wait.

I picked it up.

"Casper." Dirk's voice, flat as a ledger. "We should talk. About the house."

"We always talk about the house, Dirk."

"Not like this." A pause, and in the pause I heard something I had never heard in my cousin's voice in thirty years of rent receipts: hesitation. "There are things about the estate. About Mother's—about the settlement. Things I should have told you a long time ago."

The radiator in the hall ticked. 1971, ticking. The frost crawled on the window. Somewhere down the street a tram sang its single long note, and I stood in my mother's kitchen holding a telephone that suddenly felt like the receiver of a bomb, and I heard myself say, in the flattest voice I owned:

"Sunday, then. Come for coffee."

"Sunday," Dirk said, and hung up, and the dial tone hummed into my ear like a struck string that would not stop ringing.
