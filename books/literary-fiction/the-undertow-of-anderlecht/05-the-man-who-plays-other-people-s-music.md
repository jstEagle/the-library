## 5. The Man Who Plays Other People's Music

The chapel at Sint-Lucas Hospital had been built for a congregation that no longer came. Twelve pews, four occupied on a good Sunday, and an upright piano in the corner—dark walnut, out of true, its middle register sagging like a tired shoulder. Elias Verhoeven had complained about it to the chaplain for eleven years, and the chaplain had smiled and done nothing, because the piano was a gift from a family whose daughter had died in this ward, and some instruments you did not replace. You simply learned their faults and played around them.

Elias knew how to play around faults. It was, he sometimes thought, the only thing he had ever truly known how to do.

He arrived at six in the morning, before the nurses changed shift, when the corridor lights were still dimmed to their night setting and the building made the sounds buildings make when they believe no one is listening: the tick of cooling pipes, the far-off sigh of a door, a trolley's wheel finding its rhythm somewhere below. He wore his coat over his pajamas because the chapel was cold and because his body no longer held heat the way it used to—it leaked warmth like a cracked kettle, and Sister Marthe had stopped scolding him about the coat months ago. She only brought him a blanket now, folded over her arm like a question she already knew the answer to.

He sat on the bench and lifted the fallboard with both hands, because his left could no longer be trusted alone. The arthritis had come for his knuckles first, then his wrists, and now there were mornings—the mornings getting more numerous—when his fingers felt like things attached to him rather than parts of him, strangers at the ends of his arms who had once been fluent in a language he was forgetting.

But not yet. Not this morning.

He began, as he always began, with scales—not for technique but for courage. C major, hands together, slow as a rosary. Then B minor, which he liked because it sounded like weather coming. He let the chapel hold the sound. The stone took the notes and gave them back softened, the way time gives everything back if you wait long enough.

Then he turned to the piece.

It had no name. Fifty years, and it had never had a name, though he had offered it several and she had refused them all with a shake of her head that he could still see, could still feel, the way you feel a stone in your shoe long after you've taken the shoe off. On the manuscript—in her hand, small and slanted and pressed so hard into the paper that the pen had nearly gone through—there was only a dedication line at the top, and even that was crossed out. He had spent one entire winter, decades ago, trying to read what lay beneath the crossing-out. He had held the page up to lamplight, angled it against the window, laid it flat and closed his eyes and traced the grooves with a fingertip until his touch learned what his eyes could not.

Two words. He would not say them aloud, even alone, even here. Saying them felt like opening someone else's mail.

He played it through once, all the way, without stopping, because that was the rule he had kept since 1974 and he did not break rules he had made for the dead—or for the living; he had never been sure which she was anymore. The piece was not long. Four minutes, perhaps five if you took the middle section slowly, and he always took the middle section slowly, because that was where she had written *poco rubato* above the staff and then, beneath it, in pencil later, fainter: *like remembering.*

Like remembering. As if she had known, in 1974, that someone would still be playing this in another century. As if she had written instructions for exactly the man he became.

The last chord faded into the chapel's stone throat. Elias sat with his hands in his lap and breathed, and the breath caught where it always caught now, halfway down, and he waited for it to finish its business before he went on.

* * *

Brussels, October 1974. Rain like nails.

He was twenty-four and already being called a prodigy, which is a word adults use on young men the way farmers use weights on growing things—to keep them from rising too fast. He had played the Brahms Second that night at the Palais des Beaux-Arts, and played it well, well enough that his teacher had gripped his shoulder backstage with both hands and said, *Now they will want pieces of you. Give them only the music.* And Elias, twenty-four and invincible, had nodded and understood nothing.

The stage door opened onto the rain and onto her.

She stood under the awning where the smokers huddled, though she was not smoking. She held a folder against her chest with both arms, the way you hold something you have decided to protect with your body if it comes to that. Young—younger than him, or the same age, it was hard to tell; fear either ages people or freezes them, and she looked frozen, preserved at some earlier moment of herself. Dark coat, dark hair plastered wet at the temples. Her eyes went past him constantly, checking the street, the door, the cars sliding by on their cushions of water.

She said his name. Not a question—an identification, like a customs officer. *You are Verhoeven.*

*I am,* he said, and waited, because famous young men learn to wait; people come to them with requests, scores to sign, marriages to bless.

*You play other people's music,* she said. *That's what you do. You take what someone wrote and you make it live in your hands and you give it to rooms full of strangers.*

He started to say something modest—he no longer remembered what—and she pushed the folder against his chest, hard, so that he had to take it or let it fall.

*Then do that,* she said. *For me. For this.*

Inside the folder: a manuscript, handwritten, eleven pages, the ink slightly blurred where the rain had found its edges. He glanced down at it—he couldn't help it—and the first bars rose off the page into his inner ear instantly, whole, the way a face rises from a photograph. He had that gift; everyone said so. He heard music the way other people heard voices calling their names.

*This is yours?* he asked. *You wrote this?*

Her face did something complicated. Grief and fury and a kind of terrible relief, all arriving at once like passengers pushing through a narrow door.

*It doesn't matter whose it is,* she said. *That's the point. That's the whole—* She stopped. Breathed. Started again, quieter, and the quietness frightened him more than the shouting would have. *If I keep it, it dies with me. Or worse—they take it and put their name on it and it lives, but it lives as a lie. I can't—* Her voice cracked and she hated it, he could see her hating it. *I can't fight anymore. I'm leaving. Tonight. And I need someone to keep it alive who isn't me. Someone whose name means something. Someone they won't dare.*

*Who is they?*

She looked at him then, directly, for the first time, and whatever she saw in his face made her decide against answering. Or maybe she saw that he already half-knew; conservatories are villages, villages are gossip, gossip travels faster than trains. There had been whispers for two years about a professor and his students' manuscripts, whispers that died whenever they approached anyone with the power to act on them.

*Promise me,* she said. *Not that you'll perform it. Not yet. Promise me you'll keep it alive. Learn it. Carry it. Wait until it can exist without destroying anything—without destroying anyone. Can you promise that? A stranger, in the rain, asking you for the only thing she has left?*

And Elias Verhoeven, twenty-four years old, holding eleven pages of the most beautiful music he had ever read, said yes.

She nodded once, briskly, business concluded, and stepped back into the rain. At the curb she paused. He thought she was going to give him her name. Instead she said:

*Don't try to find me. If you look for me, they'll follow you. Let it come to you someday, or let it never come. Either way, the music stays alive. That's all. That's everything.*

Then she was gone—a dark shape crossing a wet street, a door of a tram closing behind her, and then not even that. Just the rain, and the folder going soft and heavy in his arms, and the doorman asking if Monsieur wanted a taxi.

* * *

He told no one. This was the first part of the promise, the part she hadn't asked for but that he understood instinctively: possession of the score must be invisible, because visibility was exactly what she was fleeing. He hid the manuscript inside the cover of a volume of Czerny exercises, the most boring music ever printed, the last place any curious eye would look. When he moved apartments—and he moved often, in those years, the way young careers move—the Czerny traveled in his own suitcase, never in the shipped trunks. He carried her music the way you carry a secret through a crowded room: casually, with your heart hammering.

He learned it within a month. Of course he did. His hands were twenty-four and arrogant and they devoured it—but learning it was not the same as understanding it, and understanding came slowly, over years, the way a landscape comes to a traveler who walks instead of rides. In the fast passages he heard her anger. In the middle section—that suspended, wandering middle section, *like remembering*—he heard what the anger was protecting. Something tender. Something that had a name, the crossed-out name, the two words buried under her pen strokes.

He performed it for the first time in 1979, in a small hall in Antwerp, unannounced, slipped into a program between Schumann and Debussy as an untitled encore. A critic wrote the next day that the unknown work was "either the discovery of the decade or the delusion of one," and Elias cut the review out and burned it, because the phrase *delusion* stung in a place he didn't want examined. After that he played it rarely, carefully, always anonymously credited—"Composer unknown, manuscript in private collection"—and each time he watched the audience during the middle section, watching for a face. Hers. Or someone who knew hers. A sister, a friend, a lover, anyone who might sit up straight in the eleventh row and think, *I know that melody, I heard it through a practice-room wall in 1971.*

No one ever did. The music stayed alive, as promised, and utterly orphaned.

There were nights—he would admit this only to himself, and only in the chapel, only at dawn, when lies get too tired to stand—that he wondered whether keeping the promise had been devotion or cowardice. She had said *wait until it can exist without destroying anyone*, and he had waited, and waited, and waiting had become a career of its own, a comfortable vocation. He had become celebrated precisely for this: the man who played other people's music, who subsumed himself, who vanished into Chopin and emerged into Ravel, who gave interviews saying things like *I am only a doorway; the rooms belong to the composers.* Critics adored the humility. Audiences found it moving.

None of them knew it was a sentence he had passed on himself. None of them knew that somewhere in a locked drawer in his house in Ghent lay eleven pages by a woman whose name he did not know, whose face he could still draw from memory in the dark, and that every concert of his long, praised, decorated life had been built on top of a single broken appointment: the day he was supposed to stop hiding her music and start fighting for it.

He had told himself, for fifty years, that the conditions were never right. That the professor lived too long, that the legal machinery was too dangerous, that she had asked for patience and patience was what he gave. All true. All also, in the chapel light of six a.m., beside the piano with the sagging middle register, insufficient.

* * *

Sister Marthe found him there at seven-fifteen, as she always did, hands still on the keys, not playing now, just resting them on the wood like a man resting his hand on the shoulder of an old horse.

"You're pale," she said.

"I'm seventy-four."

"You're paler than seventy-four." She draped the blanket over his shoulders without asking permission; after eleven years, permission had been retired from their arrangement. "Dr. Lammens wants to move the consultation to Thursday. He says there are options we should discuss."

Options. The word had a hospital smell to it, antiseptic, faintly hopeful, entirely false. Elias nodded and let her talk about Thursday while he looked at his hands—these hands that had carried a stranger's music across half a century, that had given it to thousands of ears, that had never once given it back to her.

Because that was the failure underneath all the other failures, the one he circled at night like a moth against glass: he had kept the music alive, but he had never returned it. Never found her. Never set the eleven pages in front of her and said, *Here. It survived. It's yours. Take it home.* He had honored the letter of the promise and evaded its spirit for five decades, telling himself that *let it come to you* meant *do nothing*, when he had always, deep down, known it meant *be ready*. Be ready, and when the moment came, act. And the moment had never come, because he had never gone looking for it, because looking risked the thing he feared most, which was not the professor, not the scandal, not even breaking his word—

It was finding her and having nothing to say.

"Thursday," he repeated, to give her something. "Tell Dr. Lammens yes."

Marthe gathered her blanket-corner and paused. "The radio producer called again," she said. "About the charity broadcast. They want you for the second of December. They're saying it might be the last one." She said it gently, the way you set down something fragile. "I told them I'd ask."

Elias looked at the piano. The morning light had reached it now, lying along the fallboard, warming the old walnut to the color of bread crust.

"Tell them yes," he said.

After she left he sat a while longer, and his right hand, acting on its own, on fifty years of habit, found the first three notes of her song and played them very softly into the empty chapel—three notes, no more, a knock on a door.

Somewhere out there, he thought, in a city or a town or a house with bad radiators, she was alive or she was dead, and either way the debt stood. He was dying with a stranger's music in his keeping and a stranger's promise uncompleted, and the broadcast in December would be heard by everyone in Flanders except, probably, the one person it was meant for.

He was wrong about that. But he wouldn't know it for another week—until the morning a nurse brought his mail, and among the circulars and the card from the conservatory alumni association he found an envelope with an Anderlecht postmark, addressed in a careful, repressed, unmistakably inherited handwriting, and containing a single sheet that began:

*Monsieur Verhoeven — you played my mother's song on the radio.*
