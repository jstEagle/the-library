## 20. Last Measures

The morphine made time soft at the edges, and Elias Verhoeven had learned to be grateful for that. Days no longer arrived one after another like bills; they pooled. He woke when the light through the chapel-facing window turned the color of weak tea, and he lay still and listened to his own breathing the way he had once listened to a hall before walking out on stage—measuring the room, taking its temperature, deciding what it could hold.

What this room could hold was shrinking. He knew the numbers better than the nurses thought he did. The fluid in his lungs had a schedule of its own now, and it did not consult him. Some mornings he could manage eight bars before his hands went to rest in his lap like tired birds. Other mornings, only four.

But there were things to do yet, and he kept them ranked in order of difficulty, the way he had once ordered a recital program: opening with something gentle to win the audience over, saving the hardest piece for last.

The hardest piece was a sentence. He had been carrying it for fifty years, and it weighed more than any score.

*She asked me not to find you.*

He said it silently, testing it against the ceiling. Then against the window. Then, once, aloud, in Dutch, to the empty room, and heard how small it sounded, how inadequate, like a man bringing a single candle to a house fire.

The boy—the man, Casper, thirty years a piano tuner in Anderlecht, who had sat at the wheeled upright two days ago and played the song badly and completely and said his mother's name out loud as though setting it down on a table between them—was coming back today. Sister Agnes had confirmed it at dawn, her voice pitched low in the corridor, that particular hush nurses use when they have decided a patient is listening even with his eyes closed.

Elias had listened. He was doing very little else these days besides listening, and he found it was not so different from performing. You waited. You attended. You let the other voice lead.

* * *

Casper came at three in the afternoon, when the winter light was already giving up. He carried a canvas bag that clinked faintly—tools, Elias guessed, or perhaps the letters; he had seen the man's hands shake when he'd handed those over, fifty years of unsent mail delivered in a rubber band—and he stood in the doorway of the private room as if the doorway were a border checkpoint and he had forgotten his papers.

"You came back," Elias said.

"You asked if I would."

"I asked. I didn't expect." Elias gestured at the chair beside the bed, the one with the worn arms where visitors sat. "Sit. Please. Standing there, you look like a man waiting to tune something, and I warn you, my piano days are behind me."

"Your hands moved yesterday," Casper said. He sat, but on the edge of the chair, knees together, a man prepared to leave at any moment and knowing he wouldn't. "In your sleep. You were playing something fast."

"Bach. The Capriccio on the Departure of a Beloved Brother." Elias smiled at the ceiling. "My fingers have decided to give farewell concerts without consulting me. I wake up mid-trill." He turned his head, and the effort of it was considerable, and he let Casper see the effort because he had decided long ago, in the dark hours, that this visit would contain no performances. That was the whole point. That was the debt. "I have something to tell you, and I want to tell it while I can still choose the words. Tomorrow I may only have the true ones, and the true ones are crueler."

Casper's hands went still on his knees. Elias noticed the calluses, the flattened fingertips of a tuner, a man who had spent a lifetime pressing wires into agreement. His mother's hands, he thought. Her hands had been narrow too, quick, always half-curled as if around something she was protecting.

"In 1974," Elias began, "a young woman stood outside the Conservatoire Royal after a Tuesday evening concert, in the rain, holding a manuscript wrapped in a scarf. I have told you this part. What I have not told you—" and here the sentence he had rehearsed against the ceiling rose in him like water finding its level, "—is what she said after I promised to keep the music alive."

The radiator under the window ticked. Hospital radiators all spoke the same language, Elias had learned; they said *wait, wait, wait.*

"She said: *Promise me two things.* The first was that I would play it, someday, somewhere, so that it existed outside her body. She said a song that lives in only one person dies with that person, and she had already died once that year, in every way that mattered." He paused. His chest did its familiar tightening, and he rode through it, because stopping now would be worse than drowning. "The second promise was harder. She said: *I have a son. He is six years old. Do not look for him. Do not write to him. Do not let him find this music until he comes looking for it himself.*"

Casper did not move. Outside, a siren rose and fell along the Ghent ring road, someone else's emergency passing by their window like weather.

"She said," Elias continued, softer now, "that if she sent the music to him—if she forced it into his hands—he would spend his life wondering why she gave it away instead of keeping him. But if he ever came looking, then he would already know the answer. Because a man who goes searching for a song is a man who wants it. And she could not bear"—his voice caught, and he let it—"she could not bear to be wanted only out of confusion. She wanted to be sought."

The silence afterward was long. Elias watched Casper's face do several things at once: the flinch, the arithmetic, the slow terrible understanding arriving like furniture being carried up stairs, one piece at a time.

"She built the search into the song," Casper said finally. His voice was rough. "Fifty years. She made me come to you."

"She made you free to come," Elias corrected gently. "There's a difference, and I have had fifty years to argue about which one it was, and I will tell you honestly: some nights I believed one, some nights the other. Keeping that second promise was the hardest thing I have ever done, and I include learning the Rachmaninoff Third with a broken finger." He laughed, a dry sound with no air behind it. "Do you know what it is, to hold an address in your hand and burn it? Because I did. In 1979, when I finally worked out where she had gone—Anderlecht, of all places, ten kilometers from the hall where she handed me the score—I stood in my kitchen with the envelope, and I remembered her face, and I burned it in the sink. And then I sat on the floor of my kitchen and cried like a child, because I had kept a promise to a stranger at the cost of a friendship I never got to have."

"You could have written anyway."

"Yes." Elias closed his eyes. "And every year, on the anniversary, I composed the letter I would have written. Dear madam, the song is alive, I played it in Antwerp, an old woman in the third row wept and I pretended it was for Chopin. Dear madam, I am dying, forgive me, I am going to break the second promise because the first one is failing and I cannot let both go dark. Dear madam—" He opened his eyes. "You see? Fifty years of letters. Your mother and I were pen pals who never mailed anything. It appears to run in the company she kept."

That earned him something almost like a laugh from Casper, wet and short, and Elias counted it as a victory.

"The broadcast," Casper said. "The announcer said you called it 'the gift.' Was that the beacon? Were you calling me?"

"I was calling whoever was listening who had ears to hear it." Elias shifted against the pillows, and Sister Agnes's warning about exertion rang distantly in his memory like a bell in another building. "A dying man is permitted one immodest act. I chose mine. I put your mother's song on the national airwaves without permission, without attribution, knowing it might reach no one, knowing it might reach the wrong person, knowing it might simply die with me unclaimed. And then a letter arrived from a piano tuner in Anderlecht who wrote—" he closed his eyes, retrieving it, because he had read that letter forty times, "—*I believe this piece belonged to my mother. I have no proof. I only know that I have heard it before, in a house where no radio was playing.*"

"That's what I wrote?"

"Word for word. 'In a house where no radio was playing.' I read that line and I had to sit down, and I was already sitting down, so I had to lie down." He smiled. "It was the best review the piece has ever received."

* * *

They sat while the light failed. A nurse came, adjusted something, measured something, wrote something, and left again with the practiced invisibility of hospital staff, and Elias thought: there but for the grace of a different cowardice go I. Thirty years of tuning pianos and leaving no trace. He recognized the creed. He had practiced a version of it himself, dressed up as devotion.

"Casper." He said the name deliberately, tasting it. "May I ask you something impertinent?"

"You've earned it."

"When you played the other day. Badly, you said. Completely, I say." He held up a hand before Casper could protest. "I have taught conservatory students for forty years. I know the difference between a performance that fails and a performance that arrives late. Yours arrived late. It took thirty years to get from the door to the bench, and then it walked straight to the middle of the piece and stood there. My question is: what did it feel like? Not the playing. The saying of her name."

Casper looked at his hands for a long moment. "Like putting weight on a leg I'd been told was broken."

"And was it?"

"No." A pause. "It held."

"There," Elias said, "is your proof. Better than any manuscript page. Better than the archive in Brussels with the professor's name all over your mother's sketches. A leg that holds." He coughed, deep, and waited out the long shudder of it, and when he surfaced, Casper had risen halfway from the chair, and Elias waved him back down with two fingers. "Stay. I have one more thing, and then I am going to sleep for a week, or whatever the local equivalent is."

He nodded toward the corner of the room, where the hospital's wheeled upright stood under its dust cover—a serviceable instrument, tuned twice a year by a man from Brussels who did acceptable work and asked no questions.

"They tell me I have days," Elias said. "The word they use is 'transition,' as if I were changing careers. I have made my arrangements. There is a recording engineer named Wouter De Smet who owes me a favor from 1988 and has agreed to come tomorrow morning with portable equipment. And I would like—" and here his voice, which had held steady through fifty years of confession, wavered on the simple mechanical words, "—I would like us to record the song. Four hands. One take. Whatever take we get. If you stumble, we keep it. If I run out of breath in the final phrase, we keep that too. I don't want a performance. I want a document. Proof that the music passed from her hands to mine to yours, and that on one particular morning in Ghent, all three of us were in the same room."

Casper stared at him. "I'm a tuner. I fix pianos. I don't—"

"You kept thirty pianos in tune across Brussels for thirty years. You have put your hands inside more instruments than I have. You know where the sound lives." Elias leaned forward, and the motion cost him, and he paid it gladly. "And you played it once already, alone, at night, with no one watching. This is the same thing, except I will be watching, and a microphone, and God, presumably, if He has nothing better scheduled. Play it with me. One take. Let the song finish its journey with witnesses."

The radiator ticked. Somewhere down the corridor a trolley rattled over a seam in the floor, that small seismic event hospitals produce hourly, the world insisting on its ordinary business.

"Why one take?" Casper asked quietly.

"Because she never played it twice." Elias held his gaze. "I have honored that for fifty years—every public performance was the first and only version of itself, unrepeated, unrehearsed beyond necessity. It drove my agents mad. But it was the rule she gave me with the score, folded into the paper like a pressed flower: *never the same way twice, Elias. If it becomes habit, it becomes his.*" He did not need to say whose *his*. They both knew the professor's name now; it was in the archive, in the published scores, in the family machinery of erasure that Dirk had confessed to like a man emptying his pockets. "One take. We make it a habit-proof thing. A single event in the history of sound, like a snowflake, like a Tuesday in 1974 in the rain."

Casper was silent for a long time. Elias watched him wrestle with it and recognized the wrestling—it was the same match he had refereed inside himself for half a century, the opponent wearing the face of everyone who had ever told him to stay small, stay safe, stay unrecorded.

"All right," Casper said at last. "One take. But I want the letters in the room. Hers. While we play. On the piano lid."

Elias felt something loosen in his chest that had nothing to do with the fluid. "Yes," he said. "Yes. She should attend."

* * *

Wouter De Smet arrived at nine the next morning with two microphones in flight cases covered in stickers from festivals dead and gone, and he set them up with the economy of a man who had recorded deathbed sessions before and knew that the equipment must be invisible, must leave no trace, must simply open a window and get out of the way. He shook Casper's hand, said, "Meneer Underhill, I've heard your work—you tuned the Erard in the Flagey studio in 2011, C-sharp octave, gorgeous," and Casper blinked as though being recognized were a physical contact sport.

They placed the letters on the piano lid, weighted at the corners with two of the hospital's small paper cups, and Elias was wheeled to the bench, and Casper sat on his left, and for a moment neither of them touched the keys.

"Talk to me first," Elias said. "Tell me about the radiators."

Casper looked at him sideways. "The—"

"Your letter mentioned a house full of talking radiators. Each one a different year, you said. I have been thinking about it for weeks. I want to play this piece in a warm room, metaphorically speaking. Tell me about one radiator. Any one."

A pause. Then, slowly, like a man lowering himself into cold water: "The one in the front hallway. 1968. It knocked at night in a rhythm of three, and my mother said it was the house counting us—one for her, one for me, one for the song." His hands settled on the keys. "She said houses remember who lived in them. I thought she was being fanciful."

"She was being accurate," Elias said. "Houses are the most honest audiences I've ever had. They never applaud, but they never lie either." He flexed his fingers, felt the morning's ration of strength sitting in them like coins in a pocket, and glanced at Wouter, who raised one thumb above the recorder.

"One take," Elias said.

"One take," Casper answered.

They began.

Elias took the lower voice, the walking bass line that was the mother's part, steady as a heartbeat, steady as a radiator counting a family, and Casper took the melody above it—the song itself, the gift, the thing she could neither give away nor destroy until she found the one way to do both. Casper's playing was imperfect. His thirds wobbled in the second phrase; he rushed the turn into the middle section, caught himself, corrected. Elias's breath ran short in the seventh bar and he simplified the bass, thinning it to octaves, and Casper, without looking, without hesitating, absorbed the change and carried the harmony for both of them, the way you carry someone who stumbles on ice.

Somewhere in the middle of the piece, Elias stopped hearing flaws altogether. What he heard instead was 1974: rain on the conservatory steps, a scarf-wrapped manuscript thrust into his hands, a young woman's voice saying *keep it alive*, and beneath that, further down, the dawn practice sounds of a house in Anderlecht, a boy asleep upstairs while his mother played a song she would permit herself exactly once. The room filled. The microphones drank it. Wouter De Smet stood by the window with his eyes closed, and Sister Agnes appeared in the doorway and did not come in, and the radiator under the window ticked its patient triple rhythm, counting them—one for the dying, one for the living, one for the song.

In the final phrase, Elias's lungs gave what they had. He let the last bass note decay unwritten, unfinished, and Casper held the melody's final tone alone, sustained it past all decency, past all technique, a long pure thread of sound that bent slightly sharp at the very end because his arm was shaking, and then released it.

Silence. The kind that has a floor to stand on.

Wouter lifted his head. "That's it," he said softly. "That's the take. I'm not touching it. No edits, no cleanup. It goes out as it happened."

"It goes out attributed," Elias said, and his voice was nearly gone now, spent, and he was glad to spend the last of it here. "Full name. Composition by Margriet Underhill, née Vandenberghe, Anderlecht, circa 1968. Performed by Elias Verhoeven and Casper Underhill, her son. Four hands. One take." He turned his head on the pillow toward Casper, whose hands still rested on the silent keys as if reluctant to break the circuit. "She's out of the shadows, meneer Underhill. All three of us are. How does the leg feel?"

Casper looked down at his own hands a long moment. When he spoke, his voice was steady, and it was the steadiest thing in the room.

"It holds," he said. "It holds."

Outside, the January light was climbing the wall opposite the window, and in the corridor a trolley passed, and the radiator counted them once more—three, three, three—as if the house, or the hospital, or the world itself had finally learned their names.
