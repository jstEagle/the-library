## 22. Undertow

The house on Corliss Avenue had a voice, and in the first week it was all Ansel could do not to answer it.

It was a small house—a worker's cottage from the twenties, clapboard gone silver, a porch that sagged at the corner like an old man leaning on his cane—and it spoke constantly, the way old buildings did when no one had thought to hush them. The water main knocked twice before dawn, always twice, a habit it had learned decades ago and never unlearned. The furnace had a wheeze on its third cycle, a thin asthmatic note that meant a bearing would need replacing by spring. And upstairs, in the room Vera had claimed for herself—the one with the morning light and the view of the neighbor's pear tree—the floorboards sang a particular minor chord underfoot, three steps from the door to the window, that Ansel had come to think of as Vera's theme.

She was tuning a piano in the front parlor of a house on Delmore Street the first Tuesday of October when she realized she'd been listening to buildings her whole life but had only just learned what they were saying.

The Delmore piano was a young one, barely thirty years old, a mass-produced upright with laminate pretending to be walnut, and it had almost nothing to tell her. Its history was shallow: a family with two children who had banged out recital pieces and then abandoned it, a decade of silence, a couple who bought it secondhand and let it drift a quarter-tone flat without noticing. Ansel worked through it methodically—set the pins, true the unisons, ease the hammer felt where a moth had been at it—and felt the way she always felt in houses like this: polite, welcome, and slightly bored. New buildings were like new people. They hadn't suffered enough yet to be interesting.

But even this one had its small confessions. A scratch low on the case at child-height, long healed over with wax. A pencil mark inside the lid where someone had once written *DO NOT MOVE — HEAVY* in a hand that pressed so hard the graphite had nearly cut the wood. Someone had been afraid of losing this piano, or afraid of the labor of keeping it. Ansel touched the mark with her thumb and thought: somebody loved something here, clumsily.

That was the whole trade, really. That was what she'd learned in the last year, what Halden Street had taught her at the cost of everything else: every building was a confession waiting for someone patient enough to hear it. Most people walked through their lives inside these wooden and plaster witnesses and never asked them a single question. They lived inside testimony and called it shelter.

She finished the Delmore job, packed her tools into the canvas roll her grandfather's hands had worn smooth before hers ever touched it, and drove home across town—across the river, past the chain-link fence where Milton Flats used to be.

* * *

They had left the fence up longer than anyone expected. The demolition itself had taken only four days in March—a building that had stood for ninety years came down in less than a hundred hours, dust rising over the neighborhood like a held breath finally released—but the lot had sat empty since, weeds coming up through the rubble field, the fence hung with the developer's faded banner promising LUXURY RESIDENCES COMING SOON. Coming soon had become a kind of local joke. The financing had stalled, or the market had turned, or the alderman had grown suddenly cautious about anything with the Flats' name attached to it. Ansel didn't follow the details closely. What she knew was this: the ground was still open, still bare, still holding its shape like a bed after someone gets up.

Vera walked there sometimes. Ansel had gone with her twice, early on, and then understood it was something Vera needed to do alone, the way you visit a grave alone even when the person beside you loved him too. She would stand at the fence with her hands folded on the top rail—she was thinner now, the heart condition steady but undefeated, managed the way you manage weather rather than cure it—and look at the empty rectangle where the lobby had been, where the boiler room had been, where a man named Emilio Marchetti had once knelt in the dark beside a stranger's body and decided, in the space of a single breath, to become a liar for love of a frightened girl.

Ansel went there too, sometimes, though less often now. Grief, she had learned, was not a place you lived. It was an undertow—you could stand in water that looked calm and feel it pulling at your ankles, patient, indifferent to your plans, and the only defense was to know it was there. To respect it. To keep your feet.

Her grandmother's grave she visited monthly, on the first Saturday, out of a discipline she couldn't have explained to anyone. The stone read ALMEIDA, because that was the name her grandmother had chosen forty years ago when she chose the lie, and Ansel had decided the stone could keep it. But underneath, in smaller letters she'd paid to have added, it read also: *née Marchetti.* Let the record show both names. Let anyone standing there with a curious mind feel the seam in the story and pull at it. Her grandmother would have hated it. Her grandmother, Ansel believed, in whatever place she occupied now, had also—finally, grudgingly, the way she'd done everything—understood.

* * *

The archive was on the third floor of the county historical society, a room that smelled of cold paper and lemon polish, presided over by an archivist named Ms. Ferreira who wore white cotton gloves even in summer and treated every document like a bird that might startle.

Ansel had donated the letters in June. All of them: the correspondence between her grandfather and the woman signing herself *V.*, sewn into the soundboard of the upright piano for forty years; the account in Emilio Marchetti's own hand, recovered from the bricked alcove in the basement, describing the night of February 9th, 1980, in language so plain it read like a man setting down tools; the letters from her grandmother, found in her estate, revealing the architecture of the plea—the slumlord's threats, the fear that a scandal would give him the pretext he wanted to condemn the building and scatter its people, the terrible arithmetic by which one man's name seemed a fair price for sixty families' homes. And the statement Vera Okonkwo had made, sitting at the kitchen table on Corliss Avenue with her oxygen concentrator humming behind her, dictating while Ansel wrote, her voice level until the very end and then not.

Ms. Ferreira had catalogued them under a heading they had chosen together, the three of them, one evening in the parlor with the pear tree going gold outside the window: **The Marchetti–Okonkwo Papers, 1979–1981, with related materials, 2024.** Both names. Both families. One hyphen doing the work of forty years.

"They'll be available to the public," Ms. Ferreira had said, not asking, confirming, her gloved hands hovering over the boxes as if warming them. "Anyone who comes."

"Anyone who comes," Ansel had said. "That's the point."

Because that was the other thing the year had taught her. There was the legal record—the inquest transcript, sealed plea, the sentence served, all of it closed neatly as a drawer—and there was the truth, which lived in different custody entirely. The law had failed Emilio Marchetti in 1980 and could not un-fail him now; the statute had run out, the dead man's death remained officially what the paperwork said it was, and Harold Voss—the philanthropist, the library plaque, the beloved name on the hospital wing—remained untouched by any court on earth. But the community had rendered its verdict anyway, quietly, in the way communities do: the plaque removed on a Tuesday morning by two maintenance men who worked slowly and said nothing; the donations returned by letter, each one worded with a politeness more devastating than any accusation; the man himself still walking the same streets, seen at the same galas, growing visibly smaller inside his reputation, which is the only punishment time knows how to administer. He had not confessed. He never would. Ansel had stopped expecting him to somewhere around midsummer, and found that letting go of that expectation was not the same as forgiving him. Some debts you simply stop trying to collect and keep forever.

But the papers were public now. A graduate student had already come, in September—a young woman writing about housing and memory in post-industrial cities—who sat with the letters for six hours and cried once, briefly, at the page where Emilio wrote *I am not sorry for the lie, only that she will have to carry it, and I would carry it double if the carrying could be split.* The student had asked Ansel, shyly, if she was related. 

"I'm his granddaughter," Ansel had said. "And she's my family." She had nodded toward Vera, asleep in the reading room's good chair, her coat over her knees like a blanket. "Both things are true."

* * *

On the last Saturday of October, Ansel tuned a piano in the lobby of the new public library branch on Fenwick Street.

It was a decent instrument—a used grand, donated, its provenance murky in the way donated instruments always were—and she worked on it through the morning while librarians moved chairs around her and a custodian buffed the floors in slow overlapping arcs. The building was only eleven years old and had almost nothing to say, but the lobby had good acoustics, live and warm, and when she finished the tuning she sat down and played, because the room invited it and because she had promised herself she would never again leave a finished piano unplayed.

She played the piece she'd played in the lobby of Halden Street on the last night—the Chopin nocturne, the one in E-flat, the one her grandfather had loved—and she played it thinking about how sound was the most temporary thing humans made and somehow the thing that lasted longest. The building was gone. The radiators that had sung the building's whole diary—decades of tenants, arguments, births, the winter night a girl arrived with a secret and a suitcase—were scrap metal somewhere. But the sound of that final night existed still, in the ears of everyone who stood in that lobby, in the way Mrs. Duval hummed it without knowing, in the twins' account of it told to their grandchildren, in the memory of the pipes themselves vibrating sympathetically under the floor as if the house were singing along. Sound outlived its container. That was the whole mystery. That was why she'd chosen this trade without ever understanding she was choosing it.

A librarian drifted over while she played and stood listening with her arms crossed, and when the nocturne ended she said, "My grandmother lived on Halden Street. Fourth floor. Before they tore it down." And then, awkwardly, "I read about it. In the archive. The papers."

"Thank you for telling me," Ansel said, and meant it more than the librarian could know, because this was how it worked now—this was the mechanism, running exactly as designed. The truth moving hand to hand, ear to ear, the way music moved. Not preserved in amber. Alive, and passed along.

* * *

Home by late afternoon. The cottage on Corliss Avenue with its porch light already on, because Vera got cold in the evenings now and liked the light on before dark, the way some people liked a fire lit before they needed it.

Ansel came up the walk and heard, before she opened the door, the radio—Vera's opera station, playing something Italian and enormous—and beneath it, faintly, Vera herself singing along, half a beat behind, in the confident wrong key of someone who has decided enjoyment matters more than accuracy. Ansel stood on the porch a moment in the cold gold light and let the sound reach her through the door, and thought: this is what the house sounds like now. This is its history, starting.

Inside, the parlor smelled of tea and the lemon oil Ansel used on the old upright—the Halden Street piano, rescued, restored, the soundboard scarred where the letters had been sewn in and taken out, scars Ansel had decided not to hide. Vera sat in her chair with a blanket over her legs and a photograph album open on her lap, one of the albums they'd salvaged, full of pictures of people whose names they were reconstructing together like archaeologists working a small, beloved site.

"There's a woman here," Vera said, tapping a faded image of a tenant picnic, circa 1983, "and I cannot remember if she was the Okafor woman or the woman who married into the Okafors. It's driving me mad."

"We'll ask Mrs. Duval Sunday."

"Mrs. Duval will tell us a story instead of an answer, and the story will take an hour."

"Yes," Ansel said, hanging her coat, "she will," and heard her own voice come out fond.

She put the kettle on. She fed the cat—they had a cat now, a gray stray who had appeared on the porch in July and appointed himself to the household by decree—and she carried two cups into the parlor and sat in the chair opposite Vera's, the chair angled toward the window, and for a while neither of them said anything, which was the privilege of family, the luxury of shared silence, the thing Ansel had not known she was missing until she had it.

"You played today," Vera said. Not a question. She could hear it in Ansel's hands, she claimed—the difference between practice and performance, the slight loosening in the fingers after a room had received the music.

"The library on Fenwick."

"And? How was the room?"

"Kind," Ansel said. "Young. It hasn't been through anything yet." She wrapped her hands around the cup. "It'll get there."

Vera smiled and turned back to the album, and the opera swelled, and outside the pear tree dropped its leaves one by one onto the lawn with a patience that felt deliberate, seasonal, right.

Later—after dinner, after the dishes, after Vera had gone up the stairs playing her three-note minor chord in the floorboards—Ansel sat at the upright with the lamp on and opened the flat wooden box where she kept the things that were hers alone: her tuning tools, her grandmother's thimble, and a single sheet of paper she had copied out in her own hand, because some documents deserved to exist in more than one version, in more than one custody.

It was the last line of her grandfather's account, the line he'd written in the basement alcove in 1980 and bricked into the wall of the house that held him, the line she had read aloud in the lobby on the last night while the radiators sang:

*A house remembers what we ask it to. Ask it everything.*

Below it, in the box, lay a fresh sheet of paper and a pen, because Ansel had started something of her own—not an account, not yet, maybe not ever, but notes. Observations. The water main on Corliss knocking twice before dawn. The pencil mark inside a piano lid on Delmore Street. The way a young librarian's face changed when she said *my grandmother lived on Halden Street,* as if naming it gave her back a piece of ground the bulldozers hadn't reached. Someday, maybe, someone would want these. Someday a stranger with a curious mind would find the Marchetti–Okonkwo Papers and pull the thread, and then go looking further, the way Ansel herself had gone looking, following sound into story.

She wrote the date at the top of the page. She wrote the address of the house, because houses mattered, because houses witnessed. Then she set down the first line, in her own plain hand, in the tradition of the man whose tools she carried:

*This house is new to me, but it is already talking.*

Upstairs, the floorboards answered—three steps, a minor chord—and the furnace began its cycle, and the water main waited for dawn to knock its faithful twice, and the undertow pulled gently at everything, as it always would, and Ansel Almeida Marchetti, tuner of pianos, reader of rooms, keeper of two names, listened.
