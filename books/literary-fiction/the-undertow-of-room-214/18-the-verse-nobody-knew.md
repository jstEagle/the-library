## 18. The Verse Nobody Knew

The kitchen of the Wrenfield had never been designed for what we were about to do in it. It had been designed for porridge and arguments, for Odile's mother's enormous cast-iron pans, for the particular acoustics of a room where a radiator ticks like something counting. The table was scarred oak, older than any of us, and when I laid Tam's notebook down on it—wrapped now in a dish towel because the cover had begun to shed its cardboard skin like an old animal molting—the sound it made against the wood was small and final, a coin dropped into a well.

Marisol sat across from me with her hands flat on the table, the way she did when she was waiting out weather. Dev stood at the counter with his phone propped against the sugar tin, camera app open, red dot blinking like a pulse.

"You sure you want this recorded?" he asked. "First take and everything."

"Especially the first take," I said. "If we wait until it's good, we'll be waiting for a version that doesn't exist. Songs aren't good. They're *true*, or they're nothing."

He looked at me like I'd said something profound, which annoyed me, because I hadn't—I'd said something my brother used to say, and I only recognized it as it left my mouth.

The notebook opened to the last pages first, because water warps paper toward its ending. I turned back past them, past the final entry I still could not read past the first line of, past the songs I knew, to the middle section where his handwriting went loose and slanted, the way it did when he was writing fast, when the song was arriving faster than he could hold it.

*Salt Hollow's got a floor and the floor is the tide,* he'd written. *You can build anything you want on it but you can't build deep.*

I had spent thirty years translating other people's words into other people's words. I knew every category of difficulty: the idiom that has no anchor, the grammar that thinks time differently than English does, the word that means one thing at low tide and another at high. But this was the first time I had translated a song into the language it had been born refusing. Tam wrote his lyrics in English because our mother banned the old tongue from the house after the church stopped holding services in it, after the last fluent families scattered inland, after she decided—God help her, she decided—that a language was a door and doors let weather in. He wrote in English the way you write a letter to someone who has moved away: in their new address, but thinking in the old one.

And underneath the English, in the margins, in pencil gone silver with age, he had left himself notes. Fragments. Half-words. The bones of verses in our dialect, spelled the way we actually said it and not the way the mission school insisted it be written.

That was the part nobody knew. That was the verse nobody knew—not because it was hidden, but because there had been no one left alive who could hear it whole.

Until now, apparently, there was me.

* * *

Marisol had heard him sing it eleven times.

She counted for me while I worked, not looking up from the notebook, her voice going somewhere between confession and inventory. Eleven times, down at the breakwater, that summer before the end. She'd been mending nets then, forty years old and already widowed twice by the water, and Tam would come sit on the far piling with his harmonica and sing to nobody, or so she'd thought, until the day she realized he was singing to the sea itself, the way you talk to a difficult parent.

"I never learned the words right," she said. "It wasn't mine to learn. But the shape of it—" She hummed, low, and the kitchen changed temperature. Not literally. But the radiator stopped ticking, or I stopped hearing it, and Dev's phone light seemed suddenly too bright, and there it was: the melody, carried in a throat that had kept it warm for three decades without knowing why.

"That's the chorus," I said. My voice came out strange, flattened, like sound underwater. "That's—he never sang the chorus for me. He always started at the second verse. He said the chorus wasn't finished."

"It wasn't," Marisol said simply. "He told me. He said he couldn't find the last line, and he wasn't going to steal one."

*Wasn't going to steal one.* As if lines were fish, as if the sea might provide if you were honest about your need. I looked down at the notebook and there it was, in the margin beside the chorus, in pencil, in our language, four words and then a blank space where the fifth should be:

*An tae gillem vor—*

And nothing.

The tide keeps what we give it. Except the sentence didn't end there. In our dialect, that phrase was incomplete on purpose—it was the kind of construction old women used when they wanted you to finish the thought yourself, a grammatical hand held out, palm up. *The tide keeps what we give it—* and then whatever you gave. Whatever you named. The song had been waiting thirty years for someone to answer it.

Tam hadn't been unable to finish it. He'd been unwilling. It was a question shaped like a melody, and he'd left it open the way you leave a door open.

I put my head down on the notebook, carefully, cheek against the warped paper, and Marisol reached across the table and put her hand on the back of my neck, rough palm, gentle weight, and said nothing at all, which was the correct translation of everything.

* * *

We worked until the windows went from grey to black.

It was slow in the way that real work is slow—not laborious but careful, the way you move through a house where someone is sleeping. I would read a fragment aloud in the old tongue and Marisol would match it to the melody she carried, testing syllables against notes like fitting stones into a wall. Where the meter broke—and it broke often, because Tam had thought in English rhythms and written in our sounds—we bent the melody instead of the words. "The words are the bones," Marisol kept saying. "You don't break bones to fit a coat."

Dev sat cross-legged on the counter—he'd migrated there around hour two, phone abandoned, just listening—and occasionally he'd ask a question that cut straight through everything. Why did that word have two meanings. What did the tide *do* with what it kept. Whether the language had a word for the feeling of missing something you never had.

It does. Of course it does. We are a coastal people; we invented half our vocabulary for exactly that. I told him the word—*savaen*, literally *the empty berth*, the space in the harbor where a boat should be—and watched him write it on his wrist in pen, and had to turn back to the notebook before my face did something I'd regret.

By ten o'clock we had the chorus whole. By eleven, the bridge. The verses came easier than I expected, because Tam's marginal fragments were more complete than they'd first appeared—he'd written phonetically, messily, but he'd written *everything*, all the way down to the little rising tone on the second syllable of *vor* that marked it as possessive rather than descriptive, a distinction that changes the entire meaning of the line and that no outsider could ever have heard on a cassette recording.

Which meant whoever mailed that tape had recorded a song that was, technically, unfinished. A question with the answer scratched out. And the person who mailed it had known that too, I think—had known they were sending a riddle into the world and hoping it would land in front of someone who could solve it.

My mother. Room 214. The register with her name in Odile's careful hand.

I set that thought down gently, the way you set down something hot, and picked the notebook back up.

"One more thing," I said. "The last line. The blank space. I can't fill it alone."

Marisol leaned back. "Why not? You're the one who speaks it."

"Because it isn't a word that's missing." I tapped the margin, the four silver words, the held-out palm. "It's an offering. Whoever sings it has to name what they gave the tide. It's different for every voice. That's how he built it. That's why he wouldn't steal a line—because the line belongs to the singer."

The kitchen was quiet. The radiator resumed its counting.

"So when we sing it tomorrow," Dev said slowly, from the counter, "everyone singing it is singing a different song."

"No," I said. "Everyone's singing the same song. They're just each telling the truth inside it."

* * *

Marisol

She had not sung in company since Rafael died, and before that, not since the year the boats stopped coming in, and she found—to her enormous irritation—that the voice remembered everything the body had tried to forget. It came up from somewhere below the ribs, uninvited, steady, and it carried the harmony before she'd decided to give it.

Gideon had the melody. Of course she did; it lived in her the way the harbor lived in the water, and hearing it come out of that thin, careful woman's mouth was like watching someone open a window in a house you'd boarded up yourself. Marisol had spent thirty years believing the song belonged to grief. Sitting in this kitchen, hearing Gideon bend the third verse around a word Marisol had never gotten right—*vor*, rising, *mine*, not merely *the*—she understood she'd been wrong. The song belonged to the keeping. Grief was just the first keeper.

They ran it through whole for the fourth time, and this time Marisol came in under the melody on the chorus, a third below, the way the fisherwomen used to do it on the boats, two voices braided so neither could be pulled out clean. Gideon faltered—startled—and then found her, matched her, and for eight bars the kitchen held something Marisol had not heard since she was young enough to believe the sea took requests.

When they finished, nobody spoke. Dev's phone had been recording since the second run-through; they discovered this later, discovered that the boy had quietly turned it back on twenty minutes ago without asking, because he had grown up enough in six weeks to know that some things you capture first and apologize for after.

"Play it back," Marisol said.

He did. And there it was, small and tinny through the phone's cheap speaker—two voices and a silence where a harmonica should be, and the open line at the end, the held-out palm, sung by Gideon in a language with maybe a dozen living speakers, offered to a kitchen full of people who understood none of it and all of it.

"For the first time," Dev said, "it exists."

"Not exists," Marisol corrected him, because she was old and it was late and precision felt like a form of prayer. "*Lives.* There's a difference. Things that exist can be lost. Things that live, grow."

Gideon was looking at the notebook—at the blank space, at the four words, at the whole architecture of a question her brother had built and left standing for thirty years, patient as a breakwater. When she spoke, her voice had gone very quiet.

"He knew someone would finish it someday. He just didn't know who." She closed the notebook with both hands, the way you close a door on a sleeping child. "It was supposed to be me. From the beginning. He wrote it for the only person who could ever sing it whole, and he left the last line open because he knew—"

She stopped. Marisol watched her arrive somewhere she hadn't planned to go, watched the color leave and return to her face.

"Because he knew I'd need to choose it," Gideon finished. "Not inherit it. Choose it."

Outside, the wind had come around to the northeast, the direction that meant weather, and somewhere down the hill the condemned houses creaked in their orange paint like animals sensing the end. Six days now. Maybe seven, if the stay held.

Marisol began putting the kettle on, because that was what you did when a night arrived at its true center, and as she struck the match she saw, over the flame, that Gideon had opened the notebook again—to the last page, to the entry addressed to her by name, the one she had flinched from for a week.

Her finger rested on the first line. Below it, this time, her eyes did not stop.

"Gideon," Marisol said softly. "The kettle's on."

But Gideon didn't hear her. She was reading, and the reading had her whole face, and whatever Tam Brightwater had waited thirty years underwater to say, his sister was finally, at last, letting it reach her.
