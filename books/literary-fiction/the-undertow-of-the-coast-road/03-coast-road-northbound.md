## 3. Coast Road, Northbound

The bridge over the Alder River had a crack in its third pier that Jonah had been watching for six years, the way a man watches a mole on his own back—without alarm, but without forgetting either. On Friday he'd measured it again: 0.4 millimeters of new movement since spring. He logged it in his report in his small, square handwriting, and then he sat in his truck in the county maintenance yard and did not start the engine for eleven minutes.

He knew because he checked his watch. That was the kind of man he was. He measured things even when there was nothing to measure, especially when there was nothing to measure, because a number was easier to hold than whatever the thing actually was.

Dana had left for her sister's in Portland on Thursday morning with one suitcase and a sentence: "I just need to not be in this house for a while." She'd kissed his cheek at the door, which was worse than not kissing him at all. A cheek kiss is what you give an uncle. It is what you give someone whose grief you respect but do not share.

So when the letter came—the second letter, the one from the county clerk's office with the auction date printed in bold like a threat—he read it standing in the kitchen at eleven at night, and by seven the next morning he was driving north with a duffel bag, a flashlight, and no plan worth the name.

He told himself it was reconnaissance. Inspectors reconnoiter. You don't condemn or certify a structure until you've walked it, put your hands on it, listened to it under load. The house on the coast road was, as of last month, technically his problem: the estate had never been fully settled, the taxes had limped along on some arrangement his mother must have made before she died, and now the county wanted the whole mess liquidated so a seawall could go through. He was going up to look at the asset. That was all. Asset was a good word. Assets didn't have dust on them, or height marks penciled on doorframes, or a smell.

* * *

The coast road northbound was a liar, and Jonah loved it the way you love a person who can't help exaggerating. Every few miles the road climbed a headland and the sea appeared—enormous, silver, casual about its own beauty—and then the road dipped inland through stands of shore pine and the sea vanished entirely, as if it had never existed, as if the whole ocean were something the road had made up to keep drivers interested.

He counted the appearances. Fourteen between the county line and Calloway's corner. He couldn't help it. Dana used to tease him about it—*you count everything, Jonah, you counted the stairs at our wedding venue*—and he'd said, "Somebody has to know how many," and she'd laughed, and he'd thought at the time that this was the same as being understood.

There was a stretch near Mile 40 where the fog sat on the road in a low white shelf, and he drove through it with his windows down, smelling salt and diesel and the green rot of kelp, and something in his chest unclenched half a millimeter. He noticed the unclenching. He noted it with suspicion. Feelings that arrive unannounced are usually carrying something.

His mother had driven this road every other weekend for twenty years, he realized somewhere around the ninth appearance of the sea. Down to the city to pick up whichever child the state was delivering, back up with the kid buckled into the passenger seat, probably silent, probably furious, probably eating the sandwiches she wrapped in wax paper because she believed—against all evidence—that presentation mattered to children who had been handed between four homes in two years. He tried to remember being one of those kids in the back seat, watching a new one get delivered, and found he could only remember fragments: a girl named Marisol who taught him to whistle through a blade of grass; a boy called Duck who was gone again within a month; the particular sound of his mother's voice reading aloud at night, pitched low, carrying through the thin walls of the foster rooms like a lighthouse signal meant for whoever needed it.

He had not thought about Duck in thirty years. The name arrived whole, with its owner's chipped front tooth attached, and Jonah gripped the wheel a little tighter and drove on.

* * *

The house stood where it had always stood, at the elbow where the coast road bent away from the water, and it looked—Jonah made himself look at it properly, the way he'd look at any failing structure—worse than he'd braced for.

Plywood over the downstairs windows. A county notice zip-tied to the porch rail, flapping. Orange spray paint across the siding: a parcel number and the letters **DEM** with a date after them. The paint had dripped, so that the D trailed down the clapboard like a tear, which Jonah supposed was exactly the kind of sentimental reading his mother would have smiled at and Dana would have found exhausting.

But the bones were good. He could see that even from the road. The house had settled slightly to the northeast over the decades—a lean you could see if you sighted along the roofline—but the frame was true, the roofline straight, the porch posts plumb within tolerance. His mother's house had been built by somebody who cared, sometime around 1930, out of lumber from trees that had grown slow in salt wind and therefore grew dense. They didn't cut timber like that anymore. Everything now was fast-grown and hollow-hearted.

He got out of the truck and stood in the gravel and let the place look at him back.

It smelled the same. That was the first ambush: the smell. Salt, cedar, the ghost of woodsmoke baked so deep into the shingles that forty years of weather hadn't evicted it. His body reacted before his mind could file an objection—his shoulders dropped, his breath went long—and he stood there in the middle of the coast road like a fool, homesick for a place he was standing in.

"That's Ishikawa's boy."

The voice came from next door, from a garden that had clearly won several long wars against the coastal climate. A woman was crouched among bean poles, wearing a canvas jacket and rubber boots and an expression of total sovereignty over her half acre. She was maybe seventy, silver hair cut short and practical, hands brown as work gloves. She rose with the unhurried economy of someone whose knees had negotiated a firm settlement years ago.

"Jonah," she said, before he'd said anything. "You have his chin exactly. And her way of standing like the ground might be lying to you."

"I'm sorry—do I—"

"Ruth Calloway. I lived next door since before you were born. I'm the one who reported the loose board on your porch steps in '78 and your mother fixed it and then brought me a pie, which I always suspected was a rebuke." She wiped her hands on her jacket and came to the fence line. Up close her eyes were pale gray and missed nothing, which Jonah recognized as a professional hazard they shared. "You're here about the auction."

"The letter said demolition pending sale. I wanted to—" He heard himself producing inspector language, load-rated sentences, and stopped. "I wanted to see it."

"Mm." Ruth looked past him at the house for a long moment. Whatever she saw, she kept. "Well. It's still standing. That's more than the county expected. They had it condemned twice and your mother appealed twice, and the second time she showed up to the hearing with three of her foster kids in their Sunday clothes and sat there saying nothing until the committee folded. Best legal strategy I ever witnessed. Silence, deployed at scale."

Something moved in Jonah's chest that was almost laughter and almost not. "That sounds like her."

"Does it?" Ruth said, and there was a small sharpness under the words, a hook set gently. "You'd know better than me, I suppose."

He let that pass, because he wasn't sure he could pick it up. He walked the fence line instead, running his hand along the top rail the way he'd run it along guardrails, checking for rot, finding none, finding only the smooth worn places where a hundred small hands had hauled themselves up to look at the sea. He pointed at the orange paint. "They tagged it already."

"They're eager. Marren Coastal Group wants their seawall corridor cleared before the permit window closes, and the county owes them a favor I could name but won't, because the man involved has grandchildren and I'm not a monster." Ruth said it lightly, but her jaw was set. "There's talk of a community bid. Token, mostly. Ruthless arithmetic on their side, sentimental arithmetic on ours. Sentiment loses auctions, Mr. Inspector, but sometimes it makes the record, and the record is not nothing."

"You've done this before."

"Once. A cannery, in '94. We lost, but we got the facade preserved and a plaque, and now tourists take photographs of a plaque in front of a condo building, which is either tragedy or comedy depending on my mood." She studied him. "You've been away a long time."

"Fourteen years. Fifteen in the fall."

"And you came up alone."

"Dana's in Portland," he said, and heard how much he gave away with the tense of it, and saw that Ruth heard it too, and filed it, kindly, without comment.

A gull screamed somewhere overhead, outraged about something. The sea appeared beyond the headland to the north, flat and pewter-colored, then disappeared again behind the pines, keeping its appointments.

Ruth unlatched her gate and came around to his side of the fence, which he understood as a promotion. She looked at the house the way he imagined she looked at her beans: assessing, proprietary, fond.

"I'll tell you what I tell everyone who asks about that house, which lately is surveyors, county men, and one very polite young woman from Marren who I fed tea out of pure spite." She paused. Jonah had the sudden animal sense of a threshold approaching, the way you feel a bridge change tone under your tires before anything visible changes at all.

"Your mother kept more than children here," Ruth said.

The wind came off the water and moved the grass along the fence line, and the orange DEM tag flapped once against the porch rail, patient as a metronome.

"What does that mean?" Jonah asked. His voice came out level. He was proud of that and ashamed of it in equal measure.

"It means," Ruth said, "that I promised her I wouldn't be the one to explain it, and unlike the county committee, I keep my word under pressure." She put one brown hand briefly on his forearm—briefly, firmly, the touch of a woman who had bandaged a great many childhood knees. "But I'll say this much for free, because you drove all that way and you look like a man who hasn't slept in a week. Whatever you think you're going to find in that house—you should sit down first. There's a chair in the kitchen, third from the window, wobbly on the left leg. Your mother never fixed it. She said a house should keep one honest flaw so nobody trusts it too completely."

She turned back toward her garden, then stopped, half-turned.

"She also said, near the end, that if either of her boys ever came up that coast road, the other one would follow inside a year." Ruth's gray eyes held his. "I confess I never understood what she meant by *either*. You were an only child as far as this town ever knew."

The sea appeared. The sea disappeared. Jonah stood in the gravel with his keys cutting a crescent into his palm and understood that he had come four hundred miles to inspect a house and had instead begun inspecting a history, and that the history, like every structure he'd ever condemned, had been failing quietly for years in places no one had thought to look.

"Third chair from the window," he said finally. "Left leg."

"Wobbly," Ruth agreed. "Mind how you sit."

* * *

He didn't go in that day. He told himself it was the light—two hours of daylight left, and a boarded house full of dark rooms deserved better than a flashlight tour—or that he should eat first, or that entering a structure alone without notifying anyone violated his own professional protocols, which was true and which he clung to like driftwood.

Mostly, though, he sat in the truck across from the house as the light went bronze and then blue, and watched the plywood windows hold the last of the sun, and felt the dread and the homesickness braiding together in his chest until he couldn't tell which strand was which. Both of them, he noticed, pointed the same direction: inward, toward the kitchen, toward the third chair from the window.

At full dark he drove south to the motel in Kestrel Bay, and in the room with the humming heater he took out his phone and typed *Theo Ishikawa* into the search bar, because Ruth had said *either*, and either implies two, and Jonah Ishikawa had spent his whole life measuring things, and there had always been, in the ledger of his family, a column he'd never seen.

The search returned nothing. He lay on the motel bed in his clothes and stared at the ceiling and did not sleep, and outside, all night, the sea kept up its work against the shore—patient, methodical, removing millimeters no one would think to measure until the cliff was gone.
