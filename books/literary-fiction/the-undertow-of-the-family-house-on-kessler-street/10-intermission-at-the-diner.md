## 10. Intermission at the Diner

### Dominic

The confession had hollowed him out the way a wasp hollows an apple — everything still standing on the outside, nothing left within. He drove with both hands on the wheel because if he let go of one he suspected it would start shaking and never stop. Marisol sat in the passenger seat of his eleven-year-old Corolla, silent in a way that was not peaceful. It was the silence of a woman holding something heavy and deciding where to put it down.

He had told her. On the porch swing, in the blue hour, with moths ticking against the bulb above them, he had said it: *I was outside your house that night. I saw the smoke. I drove away.* And she had not screamed. She had not wept. She had looked at him for a long time with an expression he would be unpacking for years, and then she had said, "I need pie. I can't do this on an empty stomach."

So now they were driving. Past the shuttered hardware store, past the church whose steeple had been struck by lightning twice in Dominic's lifetime, out along Route 9 where the trees thinned and the sky opened up like a hand unclenching.

"The Blue Jay," Marisol said suddenly. "There. Pull in there."

The Blue Jay Diner sat alone in a gravel lot, its sign flickering the way old signs do — not broken, just tired, blinking as though it had been trying to say one word for forty years and kept losing the middle of it. A single truck idled by the pumps next door. The windows glowed amber against the early dark.

Dominic parked crooked and did not correct it.

Inside, the air smelled of coffee burnt down to its essential bitterness and grease that had achieved a kind of permanence, a geological layer. The booths were vinyl the color of old mustard, cracked and mended with tape gone translucent. A radio behind the counter played something with a pedal steel guitar in it, low enough to be weather rather than music.

A waitress named Dot — her name tag said DOT, and she had the look of a woman who had been called Dot since before Dominic was born — slid two menus onto their table without asking what they wanted to drink. She poured two coffees anyway. They arrived black as engine oil.

Marisol wrapped both hands around her mug like a woman warming herself at a small fire. Her knuckles were still stained faintly with the gray dust of the house. Dominic noticed he had the same stains. They matched, the way survivors match.

"Pie," she said to Dot, when Dot came back. "What's good?"

"Nothing," Dot said. "But the cherry's least bad."

"Two cherries."

Dot left. Dominic stared into his coffee. His reflection floated there, warped and pale, a man seen through water.

"You can talk now," Marisol said. Not cruelly. Almost gently. "Or you can keep doing that thing where you rehearse sentences and throw them away. I've watched you do it for three weeks. It's exhausting."

"I don't know what people say after," Dominic admitted. "After they say the worst thing about themselves."

"They say less," Marisol said. "Usually. But you're going to have to learn to fill the space eventually, because I am not going to carry every conversation in this car and this diner and whatever comes next." She blew across her coffee. "Besides. We're not talking about it tonight. Tonight we're talking about nothing. I've earned nothing. You've earned nothing. Nothing is the whole menu."

* * *

### Marisol

She hadn't planned the diner. It had simply risen up in her like hunger — the animal knowledge that grief burns calories, that the body keeps its own ledger no matter what the heart is doing. Twenty minutes after a man confessed to abandoning her sister to a fire, Marisol Vega had discovered she wanted pie with a ferocity that embarrassed her.

And yet here it was, when it came: a wedge of cherry pie, canned filling gone soft and over-sweet, crust from a freezer bag, whipped cream from a canister. It was perfect. It was the best thing she had ever eaten, or close enough that the difference didn't matter.

Dominic ate the way he wrote postcards, she was coming to understand — carefully, completely, as though the act itself were a form of prayer. He cut each bite into equal portions. He did not talk with his mouth full. When Dot refilled his coffee he thanked her in a voice so sincere she glanced back at him twice on her way to the register, as if checking whether he was mocking her.

"So," Marisol said, because the silence had lasted exactly as long as she could stand. "Tell me something useless about yourself."

He looked up. He had a face built for looking up out of — long, mournful, the face of a man perpetually awaiting bad news and receiving it politely. "Useless."

"Completely. Nothing about Elena. Nothing about the fire. Something that would go in the world's most boring encyclopedia."

Dominic considered this with the gravity other men reserved for mortgages. "I can name every state capital," he said finally. "All fifty. In alphabetical order by capital."

Marisol put down her fork. "That is the most useless thing anyone has ever said to me."

"I also know the year every president died. Not was elected or born. Died."

"Why only deaths?"

"It started as a bet in 1987 and then I couldn't stop." He said it without shame, which somehow made it worse and better at once. "Your turn."

She hadn't expected a turn. She took a bite of pie to buy time. Outside, the truck by the pumps rumbled off, headlights sweeping the window, and for a moment their reflections were erased and then returned.

"I can tell," she said, "what a person had for breakfast by how they fold laundry."

"That's not possible."

"Towels folded in thirds, tight — oatmeal person. Methodical, hates surprises. Rolled towels — eggs, probably, something quick, someone in a hurry. Balled-up towels—" she pointed her fork at him, "—cold cereal, eats standing over the sink, has eaten standing over the sink so long he's forgotten chairs exist."

Dominic looked down at his hands. "I ball my towels," he admitted, in the tone of a man confessing a second crime.

"There it is." And then it happened — the laugh came up out of her before she could stop it, sudden and cracked and loud enough that the two men at the counter turned around. It wasn't even about the towels. It was about twenty years of towels, twenty years of everything, and the sheer absurdity of sitting in a dying diner on Route 9 with the man her family had cursed at every holiday, eating terrible pie, learning his laundry habits.

Dominic watched her laugh the way a man watches rain arrive in a drought — cautiously, as if it might be a trick.

* * *

### Dominic

He had not heard a woman laugh at anything he'd said since — he actually tried to count, sitting there with his fork suspended — and gave up somewhere in the last decade. There had been women, briefly, in the years after the fire. There had been a book club he'd joined and fled. There had been a neighbor, Mrs. Okafor, who brought him soup when he had the flu and whom he still sent a card every Christmas. But laughter aimed at him, warm, unguarded, with pie on a fork — that belonged to another life, a life from before he became a man who mailed four hundred letters to ash.

"You should eat your pie before it gets cold," he said, which was a stupid thing to say about pie, and she laughed again, shorter this time, and something in his chest unlocked half a turn.

The rain started while they weren't paying attention — a soft drumming on the tin roof of the Blue Jay that made conversation feel private, enclosed, like the whole building had been lowered into a well. Dot turned the radio up a notch. The pedal steel guitar sighed through some song about a highway and a woman who waited too long, and Dominic thought: *there are a hundred songs like this and every one of them is about me.*

"Tell me about Theo," he said.

It came out before he could weigh it. He knew the name from the letters — Elena writing about her nephew, little Theo, six years old and afraid of the dark, eight years old and already building things, ten years old and furious at the world in a way that made Elena laugh and worry in the same breath. But he knew Marisol had a son the way everyone knows things about strangers' families, and he realized, watching her face change, that he had crossed a line drawn in ground he couldn't see.

"He doesn't speak to me," Marisol said. Flat. The door closing.

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be curious, if you want. Everyone else is too polite to ask and too busy gossiping to care." She turned her mug a quarter-turn on the table. "Theo wanted me to sell the house the month after the funeral. I wouldn't. He said I was living in a museum. I said he was living in a wallet. That was the last real conversation we had — three years ago. He sends a card on my birthday. I send one on his. We are very good at cards." Her mouth twisted. "You'd know all about that."

"Yes," Dominic said quietly. "I would."

The rain thickened. Somewhere in the kitchen a pan clattered. Marisol looked out the window at the dark lot, the dark road, the dark pines bending a little in the wind, and when she spoke again her voice had lost its armor.

"He was in that house more than I was, growing up. Aunt Elena's house — he called it that even though it was half mine, half my brother's, all of ours. He learned to swim off the pier behind it. He broke his arm falling out of the maple in the side yard and Elena carried him a quarter mile to the car, this tiny woman, this hundred-and-nothing pounds, and she never once put him down." Marisol's thumb moved along the rim of her mug. "And now he thinks I'm keeping her corpse in the walls. Maybe he's right. Maybe that's what I've been doing for three years, and maybe that's why I found your box, because the dead get tired of being kept and arrange their own escapes."

Dominic didn't answer right away. He understood the impulse better than she could know — the preservation of ruin as a form of loyalty. He had preserved his own ruin for twenty years, one postcard at a time, a museum of one.

"Elena used to say," he offered, careful, testing the weight of the name aloud in front of her sister, "that a house isn't a memory. It's just where memories lived until they got up and walked around."

"Did she say that?"

"She wrote it. Letter fourteen. Or thirty-four. I'll know when we read them together."

Marisol studied him for a long moment. The neon from the sign outside washed the window red, then dark, then red. "You loved her," she said. Not a question. Not an accusation either — something stranger, a fact being set down between them like a third plate.

"Since I was nineteen years old."

"And you never told her."

"I was going to. I had it planned. There was a night—" He stopped. The night had a shape; the night always had a shape. "There was a night I was going to tell her. And then the fire."

Marisol nodded slowly, and he saw her take that in, fit it against her own version of events, file it somewhere. She didn't push. Instead she signaled Dot with two fingers and said, "We need more coffee. This pot is a war crime."

"It's been on since Tuesday," Dot agreed, materializing with the pot. "I keep telling Earl to make fresh. Earl does not listen." She filled their cups, looked from one of them to the other with the frank appraisal of a woman who had watched forty years of strangers sit in this booth, and added, "You two look like you buried something today."

"We did," Marisol said.

"Well." Dot capped the pot. "Pie helps. Sometimes."

* * *

### Marisol

They stayed two hours. She knew it was two hours because when they finally stood to leave, the clock above the register said 9:40 and they had come in at half past seven, and she could not account for ninety percent of the intervening time. Weather. The correct way to sharpen a knife (he was opinionated; she was more opinionated). Whether the town's new traffic circle was engineering or punishment. Her son. His mother, dead nine years, who had ironed pillowcases and believed thunder was God moving furniture. The pie plate between them went down to bare ceramic glazed with cherry syrup, and neither of them remembered finishing it.

At some point — she couldn't have said when — the quality of the silence between them changed. All evening it had been the silence of two people circling a crater. Now it was something else. Companionable. The silence of people who had seen the same wreck from opposite sides of the road and were, for tonight, done describing it.

Outside, the rain had softened to mist. The gravel lot shone under the flickering sign, every stone doubled in reflection. Dominic held the door for her, and the bell above it gave its tired ring, and the cold air hit her face like a hand pressing her back into the world.

"Thank you," she said, at the car.

"For driving?"

"For the pie. For the capitals. For—" she waved a hand vaguely, taking in the whole impossible evening, "—for not making it worse than it already was."

Dominic unlocked the car. In the dome light his face looked older and younger at once, scoured clean, a man whose beard needed trimming and whose eyes had been rinsed. "Marisol," he said, and paused, and she braced for it — the almost-saying, the swerve she was already learning to recognize in him, the sentence begun and abandoned like a bird changing its mind mid-flight.

"What."

"Nothing. Seatbelt."

She almost smiled. Almost. She buckled the belt and looked out the window at the Blue Jay receding behind them, its sign still stuttering its unfinished word into the wet dark, and she thought about how strange it was that the worst day of her adult life had ended in the best conversation she'd had in three years.

Her phone buzzed in her coat pocket. She fished it out, expecting Theo's birthday-card-equivalent, a text of pure punctuation.

It was her brother Ray instead. She read it twice as the headlights swept the road.

**Ray:** Developer called AGAIN. Wants to move closing UP. Says he can have papers ready in TEN DAYS. Call me tomorrow. Marisol. TEN DAYS. Stop stalling.

She looked at the message for a long time. Ten days. Ten days and the last physical piece of Elena's life would belong to a man who wanted to pour a parking structure on it.

Beside her, Dominic drove with both hands on the wheel, humming something tuneless and content, unaware. Marisol slipped the phone back into her pocket and said nothing, and watched the pines slide past in the rain, and began — without deciding to, the way she had written to him without deciding to — to count the days.
