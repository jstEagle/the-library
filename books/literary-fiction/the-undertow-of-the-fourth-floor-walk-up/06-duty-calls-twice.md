## 6. Duty Calls Twice

The summons came on a Tuesday, folded into the mail slot of the first-floor vestibule like a knife slipped between ribs. OFFICIAL NOTICE OF PRE-DEMOLITION INSPECTION, the letterhead said, in a font chosen by someone who believed authority lived in boldface. All tenants must vacate units for the duration of the assessment. All building staff must be present.

Rosalind read it standing in the gray light of the entryway, one hand still on her mop bucket's handle, the water in it gone cold and faintly gray with the dust of a hundred hallways. *All building staff.* That was her. Twenty years of making herself small, of choosing work no one looked at, and now the city wanted her present, accounted for, named on a form.

She carried the letter upstairs with her, past the second-floor landing where the window glass had been replaced with plywood painted a municipal green, past the third floor where Mrs. Okonkwo's door wore its wreath of dried lavender even now, even with the end coming. On the fourth floor she stopped at her own room—the janitor's quarters, a converted storage closet with a cot, a hot plate, a window that looked out onto the airshaft—and pinned the notice to the wall above the sink, where the other notices lived: the water shutoff schedule, the asbestos abatement memo, the eviction timeline that counted down like a held breath.

Then she went back to work. There were still four days until the inspection, and the building deserved what it had always gotten from her: everything.

* * *

The inspector arrived Thursday morning, two hours early, which Rosalind would later understand was deliberate—a man who liked to catch buildings and their keepers unarranged.

He was younger than she expected, with a clipboard and a lanyard and the particular restlessness of someone who measured things for a living and had never once been surprised by them. He introduced himself as Mr. Ferro, shook her hand with a grip calibrated to seem friendly, and asked if the super was available.

"I'm the only staff," she said. "I do the maintenance."

His eyes did the arithmetic—her age, her hands, the mop leaning against the wall—and arrived somewhere dismissive. "Just you? For a building this size?"

"Just me."

He made a note. She watched him write it and understood that she had just become a line item: *single custodian, apparent capacity adequate, TBD.*

The inspection took most of the day. Rosalind followed him through the building like a shadow with keys, unlocking doors he needed unlocked, holding flashlights over crawlspaces he needed lit, answering questions in the flat voice she'd spent two decades perfecting. Load-bearing walls. Pipe corrosion behind the plaster. The boiler, ancient and faithful, ticking in the basement like a heart that didn't know it was scheduled to stop.

Ferro was thorough in the way of men who loved systems more than places. He tapped walls and listened. He photographed the same water stain three times from three angles. In apartment 4B—the one with the warped floorboards near the window—he crouched and pressed his palm flat against the wood and said, "You can feel the whole structure breathing in here," and for one strange moment Rosalind thought he might be human after all.

But mostly he was procedure. And procedure, she had learned, was just another word for the machinery that decided when things ended.

It was in the afternoon, on the fourth-floor landing, that the second call came.

Not for her—for him. His phone rang from his breast pocket, a tinny electronic chirp, and he excused himself and turned toward the window, and Rosalind, waiting with her ring of keys, heard half a conversation conducted in the clipped dialect of men who worked for larger men.

"No, the Vane thing is separate—that's the gala, not the demolition... I know the timeline... Well, they want the site cleared before the announcement, don't they? The conservatory expansion. It makes a better press photo without the building in it."

Rosalind's hand tightened on the keys until the brass teeth bit into her palm.

*The conservatory expansion.* She knew the phrase. It had been in the newspaper six months ago, buried on page eleven: the Ashford Conservatory of Music—her conservatory, the one she'd fled at nineteen with nothing but a coat and a name she'd stopped using—announcing a major capital campaign. New wings. A recording complex. A performance hall bearing, no doubt, some donor's name. The article had included an artist's rendering, all glass and light, superimposed over the neighborhood like a dream laid down over a body.

And this building—the fourth-floor walk-up, her building, the place where she had hidden for twenty years sweeping other people's floors—stood exactly where the new wing's entrance was meant to go.

She had known that, of course. She'd known since the notices started arriving. But knowing a thing abstractly and hearing it spoken aloud in a stairwell by a stranger with a clipboard were different animals entirely. One was weather reported from far away. The other was rain on your own skin.

Ferro finished his call and turned back, all business again. "Sorry about that. Where were we?"

"The fourth floor," Rosalind said. Her voice came out level. She was proud of that, later. "You wanted the plumbing access."

* * *

That night she couldn't sleep. The cot in the storage closet was narrow and the radiator clanked its old complaint, and beyond the airshaft window the city performed its usual insomniac opera—sirens, a distant argument, the freight line moaning through the valley of rooftops. But none of it was what kept her awake. What kept her awake was the word *Vane*, hanging in the stairwell air long after Ferro had left with his photographs and his forms.

Elias Vane.

She hadn't let herself think the name in years—not whole, not with both syllables. She kept it the way you keep a broken appliance in a closet: out of sight, too heavy to move, too expensive to fix. Her mentor. Her composer-in-residence at Ashford, the man who had taught her counterpoint and ambition in equal measure, who had told her at seventeen that she had hands like weather, that she could make a piano sound like rain starting. The man whose music she had loved before she understood she loved him, and whose betrayal had come so smoothly, so wrapped in praise and paternal warmth, that she hadn't recognized it as theft until the recording was already playing in every shop window in the city.

*Nightfall Variations.* His name on the spine. Her melody in every measure—the one she'd played for him once, alone, in Practice Room C, shy and unfinished, asking what he thought. *What do I have here, Mr. Vane? Is it anything?*

*It's extraordinary,* he'd said, and his eyes had done something she was twenty years too young to read.

Eight months later it was his. Polished, orchestrated, recorded, famous. And she was gone—vanished mid-semester, nineteen years old, leaving behind a locker full of sheet music and a rumor that followed her like a stray dog for a decade before it finally starved.

She lay on the cot and listened to the building settle around her, its bones shifting in the cold, and thought: *he is having a gala.* The conservatory was expanding, the expansion bore his legend, and the ground beneath her cot was being cleared to make room for the monument. The geometry of it was almost elegant. Almost funny, if you had the kind of humor that survived things.

Twice, then. Duty had called twice today. Once as a clipboard and a flashlight, cataloguing her building for death. And once as a name in a stairwell, calling her back across twenty years to a debt unpaid.

She got up, crossed the four steps to the window, and looked out at the airshaft—the brick face of the neighboring building, close enough to touch, streaked with soot and old rain. Somewhere below, in the boiler room, the pipes ticked. The building breathed. And she thought about what Ferro had said with his palm on the floorboards of 4B: *you can feel the whole structure breathing.*

She had felt it for twenty years. She had never let herself ask what it was breathing for.

* * *

Friday she cleaned the way she always cleaned, but her body was elsewhere, moving through the motions while her mind sat in Practice Room C with the door closed and the radiators hissing and Elias Vane's cologne—sandalwood and something sharper, pencil shavings maybe—filling the small hot space between the upright piano and the walls.

She had played him the melody because he was the only one she trusted with it. That was the wound's true shape, she'd come to understand—not the theft itself but the trust that preceded it, the way she'd opened her hands and shown him the only thing she'd ever made that felt like hers. Nineteen years old and she'd handed him her heart in twelve bars and he'd smiled and said *extraordinary* and begun, quietly, professionally, to make it his.

Her mop moved through the third-floor hallway. Mrs. Okonkwo's door was open a crack, the smell of cardamom tea drifting out, and the old woman's voice followed Rosalind down the corridor: "You look tired, child. You sleep bad?"

"Inspection week," Rosalind said.

"Mm." A pause, the sound of a cup set on saucer. "My daughter says they are putting a music school where we stood. Can you imagine. All those pianos."

Rosalind's hands went still on the mop handle. "Yes," she said. "All those pianos."

"They should put the school inside people," Mrs. Okonkwo said, with the terrible simplicity of the very old. "Then nobody could tear it down."

Rosalind finished the hallway. In the stairwell, on the landing between the third and fourth floors, she stopped and stood for a long moment in the light that fell through the wired-glass window, dusty and gold, the color of endings and beginnings and every hour of every day she had spent in this building learning how to be no one.

Duty had called twice. The first call she could answer with a mop and a signature and a quiet grief. The second—

The second required her to know, finally, whether Elias Vane was still alive, still celebrated, still giving interviews in which he described his creative process with that soft, modest gravity the magazines had loved. Whether the legend had grown fat and honored or thin and forgotten. Whether there was anything left in the man that resembled the teacher who had once leaned over her shoulder at the keyboard and whispered, *slower there—let it ache*—advice she had taken, God help her, for the rest of her life.

Saturday evening, unable to stand her own silence any longer, she walked six blocks to the corner store with the newsstand rack and bought the one magazine she had avoided for two decades: the classical music monthly, glossy and smug, its covers a rotating parade of conductors and divas and composers with serious hair.

This month's cover was a photograph of a concert hall, vast and golden. Beneath it, in letters tall enough to read from across the street:

**ELIAS VANE AT SEVENTY: THE LIVING LEGEND LOOKS BACK.**

Rosalind stood under the store's buzzing fluorescent light, the magazine heavy in her hands, and read the subheading twice:

*"In an exclusive interview, the composer of Nightfall Variations discusses the work that changed American music—and the student who inspired it."*
