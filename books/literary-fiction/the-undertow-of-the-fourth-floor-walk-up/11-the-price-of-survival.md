## 11. The Price of Survival

The letter had been sitting in her coat pocket for three days before she worked up the nerve to open it again.

Rosalind knew its contents by heart now—the way you know a scar, not by looking but by the weather it predicts. *Miss Ashgrove. The Board requests your presence regarding matters pertaining to the Elias Vane retrospective.* Twelve lines on cream stock, embossed seal, the conservatory's name curling across the top in type that hadn't changed since she was nineteen and terrified inside those same walls. They wanted her to come. They wanted her to talk. Someone—she suspected the archivist, a pale young man named Okafor who had tracked her down through the payroll records of a building scheduled to die—had learned what she once was, and the institution that had swallowed her whole now wished to chew on the memory.

She set the letter on the kitchen table of her apartment, beneath the bare bulb, beside the shoebox of photographs she still hadn't finished going through, and she did what she always did when the past knocked too loudly: she went to work early.

* * *

The walk-up received her at six in the morning with its usual breath—that mineral exhalation of cold plaster and old pipe water, the ghost of a hundred years of cooking oil soaked so deep into the stairwell walls that no amount of paint would ever cover it. Fourth floor. She climbed without thinking, her body performing the arithmetic of the stairs the way her hands had once performed the arithmetic of the keyboard, each riser a note in a scale she'd memorized through repetition rather than love.

But this morning the stairs felt like something else. This morning they felt like penance.

She had told herself, for twenty years, that leaving had been simple. That a girl could walk out of a practice room, out of a city, out of a life, the way you walk out of a movie you've decided isn't for you—no explanation owed, no ticket refunded. She had told herself the story so many times it had worn smooth as river stone: *I was young. I was frightened. I wanted a different life.* All true. None of it true enough.

The truth had edges, and this morning, standing in the fourth-floor corridor with her mop bucket dripping onto linoleum gone amber with age, she let herself feel them.

It had been March. She remembered that much with terrible precision—the particular gray of March light in the practice rooms, the radiators clanking their death rattle, the smell of rosin and floor wax and other people's ambition. She had been nineteen, and Elias Vane had been forty-three, and the song had been hers.

*Undertow.* She'd written it in the spring of her second year, hunched over the battered upright in Practice Room 9 because it was the only room with a window that faced the river, and she needed the river. The melody had come to her whole one night after a lesson in which Vane had played her own exercises back to her and said, in his soft, reasonable voice, *You have real feeling, Rosalind. The trouble is you don't yet have permission to have it.* She'd gone home—or back to the dormitory that served as home—and the music had arrived like water finding a crack. A descending figure, five notes falling away from the tonic like something being pulled under. She had written it out by hand, all eight pages, and she had played it for exactly one person.

She had played it for Elias Vane.

And he had said, leaning over the manuscript with his forefinger tracing the bass line, *This is the first honest thing you've written.*

She had believed, then, that praise from him was a form of love. That was the price list she hadn't known she was signing. Every lesson, every late evening when the other students had gone and he'd kept her past dinner playing four-hand reductions of pieces she didn't want to learn, every time he'd touched her shoulder to correct her posture and left his hand there a moment too long—each of these had been entered into a ledger she couldn't see, and the balance came due the night she found her song.

Not performed. Recorded. His name on the label, hers nowhere. *Undertow*, credited to E. Vane, released on a small imprint that no longer existed, played now—twenty years later—on a radio in a building about to be demolished, reaching her ears like a message from a drowned woman.

But the finding wasn't even the worst of it. The worst was what happened after, the part she had never told anyone, the part that lived in her chest like a swallowed stone.

She had confronted him. Of course she had. Nineteen-year-olds confront; it is their nature and their gift. She had come to his office with the manuscript pages trembling in her hand and she had said, *That's mine. You know it's mine. Tell them it's mine.*

And he had looked at her—not with guilt, which she could have survived, but with a kind of weary tenderness, the look of a man watching weather he'd predicted—and he had said, *Who will believe you?*

Three words. He hadn't raised his voice. He hadn't threatened. He had simply held up the mirror of the world as it was: a celebrated professor, a chair on the jury of the Vanbrugh Prize, thirty years of students and colleagues and patrons; and against him, a scholarship girl from a mill town, no family worth naming, a mother who had died the year before and a father who drank, no lawyer, no recordings, no witnesses to the nights she'd shown him the work in progress. Only her word against the institution itself, and the institution was made of him the way the sea is made of water.

*Who will believe you?*

And then—he had done the thing she had never forgiven, the thing that was worse than theft. He had softened. He had come around the desk and taken her hands, the way he did when correcting her wrist position, and he had said, *Stay. Fight me if you want, ruin yourself if you insist, but think first. Or go, and I'll say nothing, and in ten years no one will remember there was ever a question. You're talented, Rosalind. Don't spend your talent on a grievance. Spend it on the piano.*

He had offered her the future as a bribe, and the shape of the bribe had been: silence, and continued study, and his protection, and everyone understanding without saying that she belonged to him now, artistically and otherwise, and that the song was only the first thing he would take.

She had chosen the door instead. But here was the truth she had spent two decades not saying, even to herself, even in the dark of her narrow bed above the laundromat: she hadn't chosen the door because of the song. The song was the wound, but it wasn't the reason. The reason was the night before, when he had asked her to stay after her lesson and the light had gone amber in the office windows and he had stood too close and said her name in a voice she recognized from recordings of himself playing Chopin—reverent, proprietary—and she had understood, finally and completely, what the ledger was for, what the balance was meant to buy.

She had run. Not from the theft. From the wanting. Because some traitorous part of her—nineteen, starved for exactly that reverence, exactly that attention—had not wanted to leave the room. And she could not bear a life in which she stayed and became the woman who accepted that arrangement, so she became instead the woman who abandoned everything: the piano, the degree, the boy named Daniel Reyes who waited for her by the river gate with two coffees going cold in his hands, the entire architecture of the person she had been building toward since she was six years old and first put her fingers on her mother's upright in a house that smelled like flour and coal dust.

Survival had cost her everything except the chance to forgive. She just hadn't known, until recently, that the person most in need of her forgiveness was herself.

* * *

The mop moved through its circuits. Hallway A, then B, then the corner room with the water-stained ceiling where the plaster hung down like torn paper. Rosalind worked the way she did everything now—thoroughly, silently, without witnesses—and the rhythm of it settled over her like a coat.

She had learned, over twenty years, that manual labor was a kind of music. Nobody told you this. Nobody wrote odes to it. But there was tempo in scrubbing, dynamics in wringing a mop, a long sustained line in pushing a broom down a corridor whose acoustics you came to know better than any concert hall. She had spent the first furious year after her flight refusing to touch a piano at all, as if the instrument were complicit. Then the lean years, working dish shifts and warehouse lines, when she couldn't have touched one anyway. Then the strange middle decade, when she began, secretly, to play again—in church basements, in the dead hours of the community center where she cleaned, on the broken-keyed upright in the lobby of a residential hotel where the manager looked the other way because she fixed the boiler for free.

She had never played *Undertow*. Not once. Her hands knew it—they would always know it; it was written into them the way the stairs of this building were written into her legs—but she had kept it locked, and the keeping had become its own kind of practice, daily, devotional, exhausting.

Now the lock was rusting. She could feel it. Ever since the radio, ever since the shoebox, ever since the letter with the embossed seal, the melody surfaced in her without permission—while she rinsed brushes, while she rode the bus, while she lay awake at 3 a.m. listening to the radiator tick. Five notes falling away from the tonic. Something being pulled under.

She leaned the mop against the wall in the corner room and stood a moment, listening to the building. It groaned in the wind off the river. Pipes knocked somewhere below, a code she almost understood. Underneath everything, the faint seismic hum of the city, which she had decided long ago was simply the sound of too many lives pressed too close together, all of them insisting.

"You're talking to yourself again," she said aloud, to the building, and her voice sounded strange in the empty room, unpracticed, a voice that had given up performance.

There was a knock. Two knocks, actually—soft, apologetic, at the corridor door. She turned and saw Daniel Reyes framed in the doorway, older now, gray threading his temples, holding a flat cardboard box against his chest the way you hold something you're afraid might be taken from you.

"I buzzed downstairs," he said. "Nobody answered. The super let me up." He hesitated. "I can come back."

"No." She heard how quickly it left her. "No. Come in."

* * *

They sat in the corner room because it had the window, and the window had the river, and between them it had become understood—without ever being discussed—that they were both people who needed to see water when the conversation got heavy. Daniel set the box on the floor between their chairs. Rain had started outside, fine and diagonal, ticking against the glass.

"Marguerite's estate," he said. "Her niece finally cleared the house. Took a year. This came out of the attic." He nudged the box toward her with his foot, gently, the way you'd nudge a sleeping animal. "I haven't opened it. I thought—I thought it should be you."

Inside were cassette tapes. Dozens of them, in cracked plastic cases, labeled in ballpoint in a handwriting Rosalind recognized instantly because it had once covered the margins of her theory assignments: *R.A. — Oct. — Rm 9.* Dates spanning two academic years. Her initials. Room 9.

"He recorded the lessons," Daniel said quietly. "All of them. I found out after you left. He used to tell us it was for pedagogy—for teaching demonstrations. Most of us forgot about it. Marguerite never forgot anything." He watched her face. "Rosalind. If your song is on any of these—if the dates line up—"

"Then there's a witness," she finished. Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "Twenty years late. On magnetic tape that's probably degraded to soup."

"Tape lasts longer than people say. If it's stored cool and dry—" He stopped himself, hearing the hope in his own voice, and looked down at his hands. "I'm sorry. I'm getting ahead of it. I know you didn't ask for any of this."

Outside, the rain thickened. Somewhere below, a car alarm went off and gave up. Rosalind lifted one cassette from the box—*R.A., April, Rm 9*—and turned it over in her hands. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed more than the piano she'd abandoned, more than the letter on her kitchen table, more than the whole fourth floor.

April. She closed her eyes and did the arithmetic of that spring. April was when she'd finished *Undertow*. April was when she'd played it for him, alone, the door shut, the radiator clanking, his forefinger tracing her bass line. April was when he had said *the first honest thing you've written* in that voice like warm honey poured over a hook.

If he had recorded that session—if his little pedagogical machine had been running in the corner, as it seemed it always was—then somewhere on this ribbon of brown plastic was the sound of her nineteen-year-old hands playing her own music, dated, documented, undeniable. Proof. The thing she had lacked at nineteen, delivered to her at an age when she no longer knew what she would do with it.

"They want me to come to the conservatory," she said. It wasn't what she meant to say. "The board. About the Vane retrospective. Someone found out who I was."

Daniel went very still. She watched him decide, deliberately, not to reach for her hand.

"And?" he said.

"And I don't know." She laughed—a short, airless sound. "I've had twenty years to compose what I'd say to him. Twenty years. I've rehearsed it in grocery lines and bus depots. And now that there might actually be a stage for it, I find I don't know a single word."

"Maybe you don't need words," Daniel said. "Maybe you need the tape."

She looked at the cassette in her palm. Through the smoky plastic shell she could see the wound spool of tape, tight and brown and patient, holding whatever it held, waiting the way buildings wait, the way rivers wait, the way certain songs wait under the water with their arms open.

"The retrospective is in nine days," she said. "He'll be there. They give him a hall and an orchestra and a documentary crew, and they call it a legacy." Her thumb moved along the cassette's edge. "And I'm supposed to push a mop through the ruins of a building while the man who buried my name gets a parade."

"What are you going to do?"

The rain ran down the window in long silver threads. Far below, the river moved in its channel, dark and constant, carrying everything eventually toward the sea.

Rosalind slid the cassette into her coat pocket, next to the letter, next to the folded photograph of the girl at the river gate, and stood.

"First," she said, "I'm going to finish this floor."

* * *

She worked until noon. When she finally descended the four flights, bucket in hand, the rain had stopped and the stairwell light fell in dusty bars across the steps, and on the landing between the third and second floors she stopped—because someone had chalked something on the wall, low, near the baseboard, in a child's uneven hand, half erased already by decades of sweeping feet:

*a song lives here*

She stood looking at it for a long time. Then she took out her phone, photographed the letters, and dialed the number printed at the bottom of the conservatory's cream-stock letter.

"This is Rosalind Ashgrove," she said, when a secretary answered. "Tell the board I'll come. Tell them Friday."

She hung up before her courage could file an objection, and stood alone in the stairwell of the dying building, listening to her own heart count out the days.
