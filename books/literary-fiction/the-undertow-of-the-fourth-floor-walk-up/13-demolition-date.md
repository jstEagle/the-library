## 13. Demolition Date

The notice went up on a Tuesday, which felt like a cruelty of timing, since Tuesdays were the days Rosalind mopped the stairwell and could not avoid the front door, and it was on the front door—laminated, official, stamped with the municipal seal like a wax impression on a death certificate—that the date had been posted.

**DEMOLITION SCHEDULED: MARCH 14.**

She stood with the mop dripping onto the tile, reading it the way she had once read sheet music: left to right, top to bottom, the whole architecture of it at once. Sixty-one days. She counted without meaning to, the way she used to count measures. Sixty-one days until the building came down, and with it the radiator that clanked in thirds, the window in the stairwell that caught the four o'clock light and turned it to honey, the elevator that hadn't worked since the Nixon administration and therefore kept everyone honest about their own stamina. Sixty-one days until the fourth floor walk-up became a memory with a chain-link fence around it.

She had known it was coming. That was the thing nobody understood about endings—you always knew. The knowing didn't help. It was like knowing a piece by heart and still being undone by the final chord.

"Roz." Mr. Okonkwo's voice came up the stairwell, gravel and warmth. He appeared at the landing with a roll of tape in his hand and an expression she'd learned to read over three years of shared employment: the particular set of his mouth that meant he had news and was deciding how much of it to spend. "You seen it."

"I've seen it."

"Fourteenth of March." He peeled a strip of tape, smoothed the corner of the notice that had already begun to curl, though the wind inside the vestibule was barely a rumor. "They gave us sixty days' notice, which is the law, and the law is generous that way. Generous like a doctor who tells you the date."

"You're taking it well."

He laughed, a short bark with no humor in it, and looked up the stairwell—the four flights rising into shadow, the banisters worn smooth by a century of hands. "I been taking it since they bought the block. This is just them writing it down." He patted the wall, the way you'd pat a horse you'd ridden a long way. "You want to know the worst part? It's not that they're tearing it down. It's that they'll tear it down and in five years nobody will remember there was ever a building here at all. Just a tower with a lobby full of dead orchids and a doorman who doesn't know your name."

"I'll remember it," Rosalind said, and heard how it sounded—too much weight in four words—and busied herself with the mop.

Mr. Okonkwo looked at her a moment longer than was comfortable, the way he sometimes did, as if he were trying to place her in some catalogue of people he'd known. Then he went back down to his office, and she finished the stairwell, and the water in the bucket went gray the way water always did, carrying off a little more of the building each time, so that in a sense she had been demolishing it herself, one shift at a time, all along.

* * *

That night she couldn't sleep. The radiator performed its clanking thirds, and the wind did its work on the fire escape, and somewhere below a siren rose and fell and rose again like a phrase that couldn't find its resolution. She got up at two in the morning and took the shoebox down from the closet shelf.

She had been through it a dozen times since the radio, since the day the song found her—her song, the one she'd written in a practice room at nineteen with rain on the windows, the one Elias Vane had recorded and released under his own name and called *Undertow*, the one the whole world now hummed without knowing it was humming her. She had been through the photographs, the program from her final recital, the single pressed flower that had lost its color decades ago and was now the color of forgetting. But tonight she went looking for something specific, because the date on the door had shaken loose something else, and she wanted to look at it in the dark, alone, the way you look at a wound to check whether it's still bleeding.

The photograph was near the bottom. Theo.

He was leaning against the conservatory's east entrance in the photograph, which was to say he was leaning against the same building she now cleaned at one in the morning, though in the picture it had been summer and the light was doing that thing it did in June, gilding everything and forgiving everything. He was nineteen or twenty, with his violin case propped against his leg like a companion, and he was laughing at something outside the frame—she remembered what, or she remembered that she used to remember, which amounted to the same grief. His collar was open. His hair was doing what it always did, which was whatever it wanted.

She had loved him the way you love a piece you can't yet play: completely, and with the constant knowledge of your own inadequacy.

She had left without telling him. That was the sentence she'd been carrying for twenty years, and the date on the door had made it heavier, because buildings at least got notices. Sixty days, laminated, stamped. Theo had gotten nothing. She had walked out of the conservatory in the middle of a February afternoon with a suitcase and a silence, and she had let him believe whatever he believed—probably, in the end, that he hadn't mattered enough to be told goodbye, when the truth was so much worse: he had mattered so much that telling him would have kept her there, and staying would have destroyed her, or so she'd believed at nineteen, when Elias Vane's shadow fell across everything and made even love feel like a room she wasn't permitted to enter.

She put the photograph face-down on the kitchen table, which was a small cruelty she permitted herself, and made tea she wouldn't drink.

Sixty-one days. And the thought arrived, as it had been arriving for weeks now, with the patience of tide water: *you could find him. You could tell him. You could say it—the thing you never said, the sentence you've been rehearsing for twenty years in stairwells and mop closets and the long gray corridors of other people's buildings.*

And beneath it, the counter-thought, older and colder: *or you could let the building come down and let the sentence die with it, unspoken, which is what you've been doing all along, and look how well it's worked.*

* * *

The next morning she did something she hadn't done in three years of employment: she went up to the fourth floor before her shift, just to stand there.

The fourth floor was the heart of the building, or had been, once—four apartments, all of them empty now except the ghosts of their furnishings, the pale rectangles on the walls where pictures had hung, the circles on the floors where plants had stood and drunk the light. The demolition notice had accelerated the emptying. The last tenant, a cellist named Marguerite who played scales at odd hours through the floor, had moved out in January, and now the fourth floor was Rosalind's alone during her shifts, and she had come to think of it as a kind of chapel.

She walked the corridor. The light through the stairwell window did its honey trick even in February, even this early, and the dust moved in it like something alive, like notation floating off a page. She pressed her palm to the wall of what had been Marguerite's apartment and felt the cold of it, the plaster's patient indifference.

"You're all getting torn down," she told the room. Her voice sounded strange in the emptiness, too loud and too young. "In sixty days. I thought you'd want to know."

The room said nothing, which was the honest answer.

She had been playing, in her head, since the radio. Not the stolen song—or not only that—but everything: Chopin she hadn't touched in twenty years, the Bach she'd played for her audition at eleven years old with her hands shaking so badly her mother had gripped the back of her chair like a woman holding onto a railing in a storm. The music had come back the way blood comes back to a numb limb—painfully, in floods, with pins and needles, with the terrible rush of feeling returning to a place that had learned to live without it.

And with it, the thought that had no business arriving and arrived anyway: *there's a piano in the conservatory. There are a hundred pianos. And in sixty days this building will be dust, and what exactly are you waiting for?*

* * *

On Thursday, the demolition company's surveyors came through.

Rosalind was on the second floor when she heard them—boots, voices, the electronic chirp of a measuring device. She descended to meet them the way you'd descend to meet a diagnosis. There were three of them: two men in orange vests and a woman in a hard hat who was running her hand along the banister with an expression Rosalind recognized, because she wore it herself sometimes. The expression of someone touching something old and knowing it was condemned.

"Evening shift?" the woman asked, seeing the mop.

"Every shift," Rosalind said. "I'm the janitor."

"You'll need to be out by the fourteenth. Building's got to be empty, structurally cleared, all hazardous materials logged." The woman said it kindly, the way people say irreversible things when they've said them many times. "You've got asbestos in the basement insulation, by the way. Nineteen-twenties construction. It'll have to come out before the ball comes down."

Asbestos. Of course. Even the building's lungs were poisoned. Even its breath was dangerous.

"Has it been a good building?" the woman asked suddenly—off-script, off the checklist, and the two men in orange vests exchanged a glance that said *here she goes again*.

Rosalind considered the question with the seriousness it deserved. "It's been honest," she said at last. "It creaks when it's tired. It's cold in the corners. It never pretended to be anything it wasn't." She thought of the conservatory, all marble and myth, and did not say the rest.

The woman nodded as if this were a satisfactory structural assessment, and they moved on, their instruments chirping their small bright measurements, tallying the building's body for its execution.

* * *

Friday, she called the number.

She had written it down weeks ago from the conservatory's website, in the margin of her crossword, where it had sat ever since like a splinter too deep to work itself out. The conservatory's main office. She had told herself she'd call to ask about the archives—she'd read that the school kept records of its alumni performances, and she wanted, she'd told herself, to find the program from her final recital, the official record of the night she played her own composition and then vanished from the world.

She knew it was a lie. She'd known it the whole time. She wanted to ask one question, and the question had a name, and the name was Theo.

The phone rang four times. A receptionist answered with a voice like polished wood.

"Conservatory admissions and administration, how may I direct your call?"

Rosalind opened her mouth and found that twenty years had sealed it shut. "I—" she said. "I'm calling about—"

"Yes?"

"The archives," she managed. "I'm a former student. I wanted to know if—whether the school keeps records of student compositions. From the nineties."

A pause, the sound of keys. "We do have an archive of student recital programs and, in some cases, manuscript submissions. If you give me the year and your name—"

And there it was, the trap she'd walked into with her eyes open: *your name.* Her name, which she had buried. Her name, which, once spoken into that polished-wood ear, would travel through that building like a note through a soundboard, vibrating in every hidden chamber, and would it reach Elias Vane? Would it reach the practice rooms, the east entrance, the ghost of a boy with a violin case?

"Never mind," Rosalind said. "Wrong school. Thank you."

She hung up and sat in her kitchen with the radiator clanking its thirds and her heart going like a beginner's metronome—uneven, rushing, dragging—and she understood that she could not do this in pieces. She could not call and hang up, look at photographs face-down, stand in empty rooms talking to plaster. The date was on the door. Sixty days, laminated and stamped, and the building was not the only thing scheduled for demolition.

She got out the crossword and looked at the number in the margin, and then she turned the page over, and on the blank side, in the handwriting she'd used for chord charts once upon a time, she wrote:

*Theo Brandt. East entrance, June light, laughing at something outside the frame. I owe him a sentence twenty years long.*

She looked at it for a long time. Then, beneath it, before she could stop herself, before the colder self could intervene, she wrote the second line, the one that changed everything:

*And I owe myself the song.*

She sat back. The radiator knocked. Somewhere across the city, in a marble lobby full of dead orchids, Elias Vane was presumably being a living legend, and the thought of him no longer made her feel small; it made her feel *due*.

Sixty days. Enough time to find a man. Enough time to walk into the conservatory that had swallowed her whole and walk back out. Enough time, if she was brave enough, which she had never been, to play the unfinished song in the empty building before the ball came down—her building, her chapel of creaks and honest corners—and let the last chord fall where it fell.

She folded the paper and put it in her coat pocket, over her heart, which was a sentimental gesture and she knew it, and did it anyway.

On Monday, she would go to the conservatory.

On Monday, the undertow would finally pull her under—or bring her, at last, to shore.
