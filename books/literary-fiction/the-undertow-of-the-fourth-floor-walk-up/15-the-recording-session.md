## 15. The Recording Session

The microphone arrived on a Tuesday, in a box too large for its contents, packed with foam peanuts that clung to Rosalind's sleeves like burrs. She had not ordered it. The return address was a music shop on Delancey she hadn't set foot in since she was twenty-two, back when she still went by a different name in her own head—back when *Rosalind Ashgrove* was something printed on programs and spoken into microphones by men with lacquered voices.

Inside the packing material, cradled like an artifact: a condenser mic, a small interface no bigger than a paperback, a pair of headphones with a note taped to one earcup.

*I heard you play through the window last week. You were right about the third floor's acoustics. —D.*

Deborah. Of course it was Deborah. The woman who ran the corner hardware store and who had, over eleven months of small kindnesses—a thermos of coffee left on the fourth-floor landing, a spare key when Rosalind locked herself out—become the closest thing to a friend the building allowed. Deborah, who apparently also knew someone at the music shop, and who apparently listened at windows, and who apparently understood what Rosalind had been doing in the empty apartments after hours: playing.

Rosalind sat on the floor of apartment 4C—the one with the good light, the one where the radiator knocked twice at dusk like a polite visitor—and held the headphones in her lap for a long time.

She had told herself the playing was nothing. A way to test the silence. A way to keep her hands from forgetting themselves before they forgot entirely. But she had begun bringing the keyboard up from the basement three weeks ago, an old Yamaha with two dead keys and a sustain pedal that stuck, and she had begun playing the song. Her song. The one Vane had taken. The one the radio had played back to her in October like a voice from a drowned place, singing her own grief in someone else's arrangement.

And now there was a microphone in her hands, and the message underneath was unmistakable: *If you're going to do this, do it properly.*

* * *

She spent two days deciding, which was two days longer than any decision in her life had ever taken. She had always been decisive at the piano—that was the thing people said about her, the prodigy thing, the thing that made teachers lean forward in their chairs. *She doesn't hesitate. She arrives.* Twenty years of mopping floors and scrubbing grout had taught her a different kind of arrival: the slow one, the cautious one, the one where you check whether the floor is wet before you trust your weight to it.

But the building was coming down in nine days. The notice had gone up on the lobby door that morning, official letterhead, orange stickers on every unit like warning labels on medicine. Nine days, and then the fourth floor would be dust, and whatever lived in these walls—the acoustics Deborah had mentioned, the particular hush of rooms that had absorbed forty years of other people's living—would be gone with them.

So: Tuesday night, or never.

She carried the equipment up at eleven, after the last tenant on the floor—a man named Okafor who worked nights and slept days—had left for his shift. She opened every window a crack, then closed them again; October air carried traffic noise, and traffic noise would ruin everything. She turned off the hallway light so its hum wouldn't bleed into the take. She arranged the keyboard against the wall where the plaster had fallen away in a long pale stripe, exposing the lath beneath like ribs, because she had discovered—without meaning to discover anything—that the dead wall softened the sound in a way she liked, took the edge off the high notes, made them feel remembered rather than merely played.

She set the microphone up and put the headphones on and heard, immediately, the room breathing. That was the thing about sensitive equipment: it didn't lie. It caught the furnace ticking down in the basement, a pipe contracting somewhere above, the far-off complaint of a truck downshifting on the avenue. It caught her own breath, shallow, and the small wet sound of her swallowing.

She thought: *This is how Vane must have felt, all those years, sitting behind glass while I played.* Except he had never sat behind glass. He had stood in the room. He had insisted on standing in the room, close enough that she could smell his cologne, tobacco, the chalk-dust smell of the practice building. *I need to see your hands,* he'd say. *The face lies. The hands tell the truth.*

She had believed him. Nineteen years old and she had believed him, because believing him was easier than asking why a grown man needed to stand that close to hear hands.

Rosalind pressed record. The little red light came on, steady as a pilot flame.

* * *

The first attempt failed because her hands shook.

Not visibly—she could have held a teacup steady enough—but inside, in the tendons, a fine tremor that translated itself into the keys as hesitation, as a kind of stutter in the phrasing. She got four bars in and stopped. Sat with her hands in her lap. Listened to the room breathe around her.

The second attempt failed because she tried too hard to make it beautiful. This was the old disease, the conservatory disease, the need to be *good*, to be praised, to make the man in the room nod his head in that slow way that meant approval and meant, also, that you existed. She played the opening phrase the way Vane had always wanted it played—clean, controlled, the dynamics graded like a report card—and it sounded like a recital, and a recital was the last thing this song had ever been.

Because here was the truth she had been circling for weeks, the truth the photographs in the shoebox had finally forced her to look at: the song had not been written for him. Everyone had assumed it. He had let them assume it. In interviews, decades later, he still called it "a piece I wrote for a student of mine," letting the ambiguity do its work, letting listeners imagine devotion where there had been something closer to theft.

But she had written it for the girl in the photograph. For June. For the summer she and June Calloway had rented a room in Rockaway with a broken fan and a view of nothing but other people's fire escapes, and June had danced barefoot on the hot floorboards while Rosalind picked out the melody on a borrowed keyboard with missing legs, propped on phone books. June, who had said, *play it again, play it again,* until the sun went down and the neighbors banged on the wall. June, whose laugh is in the song if you know where to listen—in the little lift at the end of the second phrase, the one Vane had smoothed out in his arrangement, ironed flat, because he had never known what it meant.

He had stolen the notes. He couldn't steal the reason for them.

Rosalind took off the headphones. Walked to the window. Below, the street did its late-night work: a taxi idling, a couple arguing softly outside the bodega, a dog pulling its owner toward some urgent scent. Ordinary life, continuing without her, as it had for twenty years. She had gotten very good at being absent from things. It was practically a profession.

Then she went back to the keyboard and did the thing she had not done since Rockaway: she closed her eyes and played for June.

* * *

The third take began badly and became something else.

Her fingers found the opening phrase and it came out wrong—not wrong the way mistakes are wrong, but wrong the way weather is wrong, unexpected, alive. The tempo dragged. The left hand wandered somewhere below where the written harmony should have been, into a register that growled a little, and instead of correcting it she followed it, because following it felt like walking downhill toward water. The melody entered late, almost reluctantly, the way a person enters a conversation they've been dreading, and then—there. The lift at the end of the second phrase. She gave it time. She let it rise the way June used to rise onto her toes, arms out, ridiculous and certain, in the middle of that shabby room with the broken fan.

The middle section was the part Vane had never managed to understand, the part his arrangement treated as a bridge, a passage between more important places. But it wasn't a bridge. It was the argument. It was the night in August when June had said, *you have to choose, Roz, you can't keep both of us,* meaning herself and the mentor and the career and the whole glittering apparatus that was already closing around nineteen-year-old Rosalind like a fist—and Rosalind had said nothing, had said nothing for three days, had let the silence do the choosing, and June had left on a Thursday morning and taken the song's ending with her.

That was why it was unfinished. Not because she'd run out of ideas. Because the ending belonged to a conversation that had never happened.

In the dark of apartment 4C, with the red light burning and the dead wall listening, Rosalind played the argument. Her hands knew it better than her mind did—the dissonances stacking up, resolving too late, stacking again, the bass insisting on a key the melody refused to confirm. It was ugly in places. It was supposed to be ugly. Vane had sanded all the ugliness out of his version and what remained was furniture, polished and lifeless.

She reached the point where the song had always stopped—the cliff edge, the unfinished sentence—and her hands hovered there, trembling now in earnest, waiting.

And then, from somewhere she had no name for, the next chord arrived.

It was simple. Almost embarrassingly simple—one voice moving stepwise against another, the dissonance finally, quietly giving up its argument, settling into a key that had been implied since the first bar but never stated. It sounded like forgiveness. It sounded like a Thursday morning in August, twenty years ago, if she had run down the stairs after June instead of standing at the window watching her go. It sounded like the thing she would never get to say to June Calloway, wherever June was now—if she was anywhere, if she was still—

The chord resolved. The song ended. Rosalind lifted her hands and the last of the sound rose through the room and faded into the plaster, into the lath, into the bones of the building, and the red light burned on, and she realized she was crying, had probably been crying since the middle section, tears running freely down her face in the dark, and she did not wipe them away because wiping them away would require admitting they were happening, and some part of her had decided, sometime during the argument, that she was done pretending.

She stopped the recording. Saved the file. Sat for a long moment in the dark with her hands still resting on the silent keys, feeling the way you feel after surfacing from deep water—the pressure gone, the light strange, everything louder than it should be.

* * *

She played it back once, standing, unable to sit through her own confession. It was imperfect. There was a bench creak at 1:14, a faint rattle of the window at the climax, her breath audible in the quiet passages like a swimmer surfacing. None of it mattered. Or rather—all of it mattered, all of it was true, and truth was the one thing Vane's version had never contained.

She copied the file twice—to the laptop, to the small drive she kept in her coat pocket, the drive that already held the photographs scanned from the shoebox, the letters, the evidence of a life she had buried so deep she'd nearly lost the map to it.

And then, because the room was warm with what she'd done and she wasn't ready to leave it, she played the song again. Not recorded this time. Just for the walls. Just for the fourth floor, which had held her secret for eleven months without telling anyone, which had knocked its radiators politely at dusk and kept its drafts to itself and asked nothing of her except that she show up, mop in hand, and care for it the way no one had cared for her.

Halfway through the second performance, she heard footsteps on the stairs.

They stopped on the landing. She kept playing—there was no reason to stop, tenants had heard her before, had left sometimes a folded dollar bill by the door like tips for a street musician, though she'd always returned them. But these footsteps didn't move on. They stayed. And through the door's frosted pane of glass, a shadow settled itself against the light from the hallway, patient, unhurried, the posture of someone who intended to wait until the song was over.

She knew the silhouette before she knew the man. Broad shoulders going slightly stooped with age. The head tilted, listening, the way he used to tilt it in the practice room—*the face lies, the hands tell the truth*—and her fingers stumbled on the final chord, landing hard, graceless, a door slamming.

Elias Vane knocked once.

"Rosalind." His voice, through the door, older and rougher than the voice on the radio, than the voice in her memory, but unmistakable—the voice that had named her, shaped her, unmade her. "I saw the demolition notice. I need to talk to you before they take this building down."

She looked at the laptop, its screen glowing faintly in the dark, the file sitting there—her song, her ending, her proof. Then at the door, at the shadow that had followed her across twenty years and two hundred miles and had finally, tonight, run out of hallway.

She reached over and switched off the lamp, leaving only the red standby light blinking in the blackness, and said nothing at all.
