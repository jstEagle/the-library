## 17. Vane's Confession

The conservatory had not changed so much as it had settled, the way old men settle into their chairs, deeper and more crooked each year. Rosalind stood in the atrium beneath the glass roof that leaked winter light in pale panes across the marble floor, and she understood that she had been summoned here not by letter but by gravity—that everything since the radio, since the mop water and the shoebox, had been pulling her toward this room, this hour, this man.

Elias Vane was waiting by the fountain that no longer held water. It had been dry for years, she supposed; the tiles were stained with mineral ghosts, rings within rings like the record of some vanished tide. He looked smaller than the photographs. Smaller than the legend. His hair had gone from silver to a colorless white, and his hands—the hands that had been on every poster, every program cover, folded over piano keys like a benediction—rested now on the dry rim of the fountain as if they had forgotten what they were for.

"You came," he said.

"You knew I would."

"I hoped." He smiled, and it was the smile from the brochures, the one that had sold out halls on three continents, but time had eaten the machinery behind it, and what remained was only the surface, thin as foil. "I wasn't sure hope still worked. At my age you stop trusting it."

Rosalind did not sit. There were benches along the atrium wall, iron and wood, but she stayed standing, her coat still buttoned, as though she might need to leave quickly—as though part of her already knew she would want to.

"You released my song," she said. No preamble. She had rehearsed nothing on the walk over because she had promised herself she would not perform. Not here. Not for him. "You put it on an album with your name under it. *Undertow.* You called it yours."

Vane nodded slowly, the way a man nods when the blow he expected finally lands and there is almost relief in it. "I did."

"That's all? *I did?*"

"What would you have me say, Rosalind?" He rose from the fountain rim with effort, one hand pressing against the stone. "That I'm sorry? I am. That I've been sorry for twenty years? Also true. But sorry is such a small word for what happened between us. It doesn't cover the ground."

"Then find a bigger word."

He looked at her then—really looked—and something in his face shifted, the performer falling away like a coat slipping off a shoulder, and underneath him was just an old man in a beautiful building that smelled of dust and rosin and other people's ambitions.

"Sit down," he said gently. "Please. This will take longer than either of us wants it to."

* * *

They sat in a practice room on the third floor—not the grand hall, not the stage where she had played her audition at seventeen with her heart going like a bird against a window, but a small room with a window overlooking the loading dock, a room with scuffed baseboards and a radiator that ticked. An upright piano stood against the wall, its finish dulled, its bench worn pale in the middle where decades of students had worn their worry into the wood.

Vane closed the door behind them. The sound of the atrium fell away. In the smallness of the room, his presence shrank further, until he seemed almost ordinary—a retired teacher, a widower, anyone.

"Do you remember this room?" he asked.

"No."

"You should. You wrote the first eight bars of *Undertow* here. October of your second year. You thought I didn't know. You'd stay after your lesson and play it with the door shut, and you'd stop every time before the ninth bar, because you hadn't found it yet." He lowered himself onto the bench, wincing. "You never found it, did you?"

Rosalind's throat tightened. "No."

"The song is still unfinished. Twenty-two years, and it's still unfinished." He said it not as accusation but as wonder, as if this were the most remarkable fact in the world. "Do you know what that means? Do you understand what kind of music refuses to be finished for twenty-two years?"

"It means I stopped writing it."

"It means the song is waiting." He turned on the bench to face her fully. "It means it has been waiting this whole time, patient as stone, and neither of us could bury it deep enough."

"Tell me why," she said. Her voice came out low and even, which surprised her; she had expected it to shake. "Tell me how my song got onto your album. Tell me how my name got erased. Start at the beginning and don't leave anything out, because I've spent twenty years filling the gaps with guesses, and I'm tired of guessing."

Vane laid his hands flat on his knees. Outside the window, the loading dock lay empty, a rectangle of gray light.

* * *

"You left in March," he began. "Nineteen years old. You left without telling anyone—not me, not the registrar, not the boy who waited for you outside the practice rooms. You left a note on my office door that said only: *I can't do this anymore.* Four words. I kept that note for fifteen years. I don't know where it is now. I've looked."

"I remember writing it."

"Do you remember why?"

The radiator ticked. Somewhere below them, a door slammed, and footsteps crossed the floor like rain.

"I remember being afraid," Rosalind said slowly. "I remember that everything had become loud. The competitions, the judges, the schedule—you built my schedule, Elias, you built it like a prison and called it a career. And I remember that I couldn't sleep, and that my hands hurt, and that when I sat down at the piano I couldn't hear anything anymore except what it would cost me. I remember feeling like I was drowning in a very shallow pool. Everyone kept saying, stand up, just stand up, and I couldn't. I couldn't figure out how."

"And I pushed you anyway." He said it plainly, without flinching, and she realized he had rehearsed this too—not the words, but the bearing of them. "The Tchaikovsky competition was in June. I told you that skipping it would end you. I told you that girls who rest get replaced. I said—" he paused, and his jaw worked, "—I said things I have spent two decades trying to unsay. And then you disappeared, and I discovered that I had won. I had driven you out of the building entirely. My best student in thirty years, gone, and the silence she left behind was the loudest thing I'd ever heard."

"So you took her song."

"Not at first." He shook his head. "At first I searched for you. Six months, maybe more. I hired people. I wrote letters to every conservatory, every competition, every cousin I could trace. Nothing. You had done it perfectly—erased yourself. And somewhere in that sixth month, I received a package. No return address. Inside was a cassette." He looked at his hands. "Eight minutes of you playing *Undertow*. The first eight bars, and then improvisation—hours of it compressed, or so it sounded—reaching toward the ninth bar and never landing. Whoever recorded it had been in the room. Or outside the door."

Rosalind felt the floor tilt slightly beneath her. "That was mine. I made a recording—I don't remember giving it to anyone."

"You gave it to Daniel."

The name landed in her chest like a dropped weight. Daniel. The boy outside the practice rooms. The lover she never chose—or rather, the lover she chose not to choose, because choosing him meant choosing a life, and a life meant staying, and staying meant the drowning would go on forever.

"He mailed it to you," she whispered.

"He mailed it to me with a note. One line. *She isn't coming back. Don't let the music die with her.*" Vane's voice cracked on the last word, and he pressed his lips together until he had mastered himself. "I want you to understand what I did with that note, Rosalind. I want you to understand it completely, because I have turned it over ten thousand times and I still cannot decide whether I am a thief or a gravedigger or something worse than both."

He rose, went to the piano, and lifted the fallboard. The keys were yellowed ivory, cold-looking in the gray light.

"I transcribed your cassette," he said. "Bar by bar. Your voicings, your hesitations—I kept the hesitations, did you know that? The places where you breathed. I notated your breathing. And then I finished it."

Rosalind stood up so fast the bench scraped. "You finished it."

"I found the ninth bar." He said it with no pride at all; his voice was ash. "It took me three years. Three years of living inside your eight bars, hearing what you were reaching for, and then one night in Vienna I woke at four in the morning and it was simply there, complete, as if it had been waiting in the room for someone to turn on the light. And I wrote it down. And I recorded it. And I put my name on it, because your name was nowhere to be found, and because—" he stopped.

"Because," she prompted, and her voice was ice now, clean and sharp.

"Because if I put your name on it, I would have had to find you. And finding you meant explaining. And explaining meant admitting what I'd done to drive you away. And I was—" he laughed, a terrible small sound, "—I was famous, Rosalind. I was *Elias Vane*. I had buildings named after me. I could not bear to be the man who broke the prodigy. So I became the man who stole from her instead. It seemed, at the time, like the smaller crime."

The radiator ticked. Outside, the gray light had begun to fail, the afternoon folding itself away.

"Did it sell?" she asked. "My song. Under your name."

"It sold more copies than anything else I ever recorded." He closed the fallboard softly. "Critics called it the finest thing I'd written. They said it showed a tenderness I'd never displayed before. Of course it did. It wasn't mine. It was yours—it was nineteen years old and in love and terrified, and every one of those things is in the music, and none of them are in me."

* * *

"Why tell me now?" Rosalind asked. "Why not let me go on not knowing? I heard it on a radio in a hallway I was mopping. I could have spent the rest of my life thinking it was a coincidence, a ghost, a trick of memory. Why call me here and hand me the knife?"

Vane returned to the bench but did not sit. He stood beside the piano the way teachers stand, one hand resting on its shoulder, and for a moment he looked every year of his age and then some.

"Because I'm dying," he said. "Pancreatic. Four months, the doctors say, and they're being generous to keep me cooperative. And because last week a woman came to my masterclass—a journalist, doing a piece on the anniversary of the album—and she asked me about the ninth bar. Where it came from. What inspired it. And I opened my mouth to give the answer I've given for twenty years, the one about a river in Austria, and I found that I physically could not say it. The lie had finally gotten too heavy to lift." He met Rosalind's eyes. "And then my assistant told me a janitor had been asking questions about the fourth-floor walk-up on Halsey Street. Asking about a demolition date. Asking about a woman named Ashgrove."

"You had me investigated."

"I had you *found*," he corrected gently. "There's a difference. I needed to know if you were real. If the girl on the cassette was alive somewhere, mopping floors, and whether she knew. And when I learned what you'd survived—all of it, the years after you left, the hospital, the—" he faltered, "—the things you carried. I understood that whatever I owed you, I had better pay it while I still had breath to speak."

Rosalind wrapped her arms around herself. The room had grown cold, or she had.

"There's more," she said. It wasn't a question. She could see it in the way he wouldn't quite settle, the way his hand kept moving on the piano's lid like a man smoothing a wrinkle that wouldn't smooth.

"There's more," he admitted. "And it's the part I've dreaded most, because the theft you can hate me for cleanly. This next thing is worse. This next thing, you might have to forgive me for, and I have no right to ask that."

"Say it."

He took a long breath, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped, become almost private, the voice of confession booths and deathbeds.

"The night before you disappeared, you came to my office. You were going to tell me you were quitting—quitting the competition, quitting the conservatory, all of it. I know this because you told Daniel first, and Daniel told me. He came to me frightened. He said, *she's going to throw it all away, talk her out of it, you're the only one she'll listen to.*" Vane's hand stilled on the piano. "So when you came to my office, I didn't listen. I did what he asked. I talked you out of it—or I tried. I told you that you'd regret it forever. I told you that without the piano you were nothing. I told you that Daniel would grow tired of you, that love was a season and music was a life, and that if you walked out that door you'd spend the rest of your days as a footnote in someone else's story."

Rosalind's breath had gone shallow. "I remember that conversation."

"I know you do. Here's what you don't remember, because you weren't there for it." He turned to face her fully, and his eyes were wet and unashamed of it. "After you left my office—after you went back to your dormitory and packed your bag and wrote your four-word note—Daniel came to me a second time. He was furious. He said I'd pushed you too hard. And I said something to him. Something cruel. I told him that if he loved you, he'd let you go—that holding on to you would be the selfish act, that you needed to be free of both of us, the mentor and the boy, or you'd drown in us instead of the music." His voice broke and reassembled. "And he believed me, Rosalind. He was nineteen. He believed me. And when you left, he didn't come after you. Not that night, not that week, not ever. Because I told him that letting you go was love, and he was young enough to take the word of an old man over the screaming of his own heart."

The silence that followed was total. Even the radiator seemed to hold its ticking.

"He waited," Rosalind said at last, barely audible. "In the shoebox. There were letters—he wrote letters for years. He never sent them. I thought—" She stopped. Pressed her knuckles to her mouth. "I thought he forgot me."

"He never forgot you. He married someone else eventually, a fine woman, and he named his daughter—" Vane's voice caught. "He named his daughter Rosalind. Did you know that?"

She shook her head. The room swam.

"He died four years ago," Vane said quietly. "Heart, very sudden, forty-one years old. And six months before he died, he came to see me one last time. He brought the shoebox. All the letters. And he said, *When I'm gone, find her. Give her these. And tell her the truth about that night—that I didn't stop loving her, I stopped trusting myself, because a great man told me my love was a cage.*" Vane stepped away from the piano, and his shoulders, always so square on the concert posters, had curled inward like paper near flame. "I promised him. And then I sat on it for four years because I was a coward twice over—once with your song, once with his letters. The demolition notice was published last month. I read that the building where you live is coming down, and I thought: she is standing in the last place that remembers her, and it is about to be rubble, and I will die with both my debts unpaid."

He reached into his jacket and drew out an envelope, thick, soft-cornered, tied with a rubber band gone brittle. He held it out across the space between them.

"His letters," he said. "All of them. And the cassette, if you want it. And my confession, which you now have, free of charge, and which you may publish, broadcast, or carve into my headstone. I don't care anymore what happens to my name. I cared for twenty years, and it bought me a full concert calendar and an empty house."

Rosalind did not take the envelope. Not yet. Her arms hung at her sides, and her whole body felt like an instrument someone else was playing, every string sounding at once.

"The ninth bar," she said. "Play it for me."

Vane blinked. "Now?"

"Now. Before I touch those letters. Before I decide anything about you. Play me what you found in my song."

He sat. He arranged his hands over the yellowed keys. And in the small cold room above the loading dock, Elias Vane—living legend, dying man, thief, gravedigger—began to play her music back to her: eight bars she knew better than her own heartbeat, and then the ninth, the tenth, the turn she had reached for at nineteen and never caught, arriving now under another man's fingers, wrong and right at once, like a stranger wearing her coat.

She listened with her eyes closed. And somewhere in the middle of it, without deciding to, she began to weep—not for the theft, not yet, but for the girl on the cassette, reaching and reaching toward a bar that had existed all along, waiting in the dark like everything else she had left behind.

When the last chord faded, Vane lifted his hands from the keys and looked at her across the ruined distance of twenty-two years.

"Now you know everything," he said. "What will you do with it?"

Rosalind opened her eyes. Through the window, the sky over the city had gone the color of wet slate, and far to the east, past the rooftops, she could just make out the block where the fourth-floor walk-up stood—its windows dark, its days numbered, holding her photographs and her letters and the unfinished song like a fist that refused, even now, even condemned, to open.

"I'm going home," she said. "There's someone I have to say things to. Finally. Before the walls come down and take the words with them."
