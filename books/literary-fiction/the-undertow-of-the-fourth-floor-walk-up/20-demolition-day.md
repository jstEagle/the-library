## 20. Demolition Day

They came at dawn the way weather comes—first as a rumor, then as a pressure, then all at once.

Rosalind stood across the street with her hands in the pockets of a coat she hadn't worn in twenty years, watching the trucks assemble beneath the fourth-floor walk-up like animals gathering to feed. The sky was the color of dishwater, that particular gray of October mornings when the light can't decide whether it wants to arrive at all. A chain-link fence had gone up overnight around the building's perimeter, hung with orange mesh and signs she could read from here without trying: DANGER. DEMOLITION IN PROGRESS. KEEP OUT. The words had the cheerful authority of things printed by machines, things that had never loved anything.

The excavator sat at the curb like a patient yellow dinosaur, its arm folded against its chest. Men in hard hats moved around it, unhurried, drinking coffee out of paper cups, stamping their feet against the cold. One of them laughed at something. The sound carried across the empty street and struck Rosalind oddly, like a note played in a room where music had no business being.

She had not slept. She had sat in her apartment—the one on Delancey with the radiator that knocked like a polite ghost—all night with the shoebox open on her lap, going through the photographs one more time as though the paper might yield something new if she held it to the lamp at the right angle. It hadn't. Photographs are stubborn that way; they keep only what they kept the first time. But somewhere around three in the morning she had understood that she wasn't looking for new information. She was keeping vigil. You keep vigil for the dying, and the building was dying, and so, in a way she couldn't yet name, was the version of herself that had lived inside it.

Now she watched the dawn crews and felt the strangest thing: not grief, exactly. Something more like stage fright.

Because last night, in the empty building, she had played it. All of it. The unfinished song—*her* unfinished song, the one Elias Vane had taken and trimmed and released into the world like a bird with its wings clipped so it would stay in his cage—she had played it through, every bar, including the ending she'd never written, the ending she'd been afraid of for twenty-two years. And the building had listened. That was the only word for it. The radiators, the stairwell, the water-stained ceiling of the fourth floor hallway—they had taken the sound into themselves the way old buildings take everything: silently, completely, keeping it.

And this morning they were going to knock it down.

"Ma'am?" A man in a reflective vest had crossed the street toward her, coffee cup in hand. "You can't stand there. Safety perimeter starts at the fence."

"I know," Rosalind said. "I'm just looking."

"You a resident?"

"No." And then, because it seemed suddenly important, suddenly true in a way it hadn't been an hour ago: "I used to be."

The man nodded with the mild incuriosity of someone who had heard stranger things before seven a.m. "Well. They always come back to watch," he said. "Funny how that works." He touched the brim of his hard hat and went back across the street, and Rosalind remained, obediently behind some invisible line only he could see, watching the building that had held her life the way cupped hands hold water.

It looked smaller than she remembered. Buildings do that, once you stop living in them—they shrink down to their actual size, which is never the size of what happened inside them. Four floors of brick the color of dried blood, fire escapes zigzagging down the front like afterthoughts, windows dark except for one on the third floor where someone had left a curtain. The super's window, maybe. Or no one's. A curtain hanging in an empty window, still doing its job for nobody.

* * *

By eight o'clock there was a crowd.

Not large—a few dozen people scattered along the sidewalk and across the street, drawn by the same gravity that had pulled Rosalind from her bed. An old woman in a quilted coat who might have lived here once, or whose sister had, or who simply collected demolitions the way other people collected sunsets. A young man with a camera on a tripod, filming the excavator as though it might do something surprising. Two children on scooters, forbidden by their mother to cross the street, circling each other in wide, bored arcs. A man in coveralls whom Rosalind recognized distantly—Mr. Okafor?—who had lived on the second floor back when she did, older now, grayer, standing with his arms crossed and his face set in an expression she recognized because she was wearing it too: not sadness, not anger, but the specific attention of people witnessing the erasure of a place that had witnessed them.

Nobody spoke much. There was nothing to say that the building itself hadn't already said over forty years of pipes knocking and stairs groaning and rain finding its way through the roof to stain the ceilings in shapes like continents nobody had discovered.

At 8:15 a supervisor walked the perimeter, checking things on a clipboard. At 8:30 the excavator's engine turned over with a sound like a large animal clearing its throat. The crowd shifted. The children stopped scootering. Somewhere a pigeon exploded off a cornice in a clap of wings, and everyone looked up at it, grateful perhaps for something moving in any direction but down.

Rosalind's phone buzzed in her pocket. She didn't look at it. She knew who it was. He'd texted twice yesterday—*Are you going? What time? I'll meet you there*—and she had answered neither, because she didn't know the answer herself until now, standing here, alone, by choice or by cowardice or by some third thing that didn't have a name yet. Daniel had waited twenty years for her to choose. She had spent most of them making him wait without telling him he was waiting. This morning, at least, she could be honest about the shape of it.

But the truth was she needed to watch this part alone. Whatever came after—the conversation, the choice, whatever she was finally going to say out loud after twenty-two years of saying everything except the thing—that belonged to the living. This belonged to the dead.

The excavator raised its arm.

* * *

It began at the corner, as demolitions do—not with drama but with procedure. The great steel claw swung in a slow arc and touched the brick almost gently, almost tenderly, and a section of parapet crumbled inward with a sound like a held breath releasing. Dust bloomed, pale gold in the early light, and drifted, and settled. Someone in the crowd made a small sound. The children had gone silent.

Then the claw came again, harder, and the building began to die in earnest.

Brick by brick is how she'd imagined it, but it wasn't like that. Whole walls let go at once, folding down into the basement they'd been hiding all these years, exposing rooms to the sky—rooms she knew. When the east wall came away she could see straight into what had been the second-floor landing, the place where the mailboxes used to be, and beyond it, impossibly intimate, the interior of an apartment with wallpaper still on the walls, faded roses climbing toward a ceiling that no longer existed above them. A doorframe stood alone. Someone's coat hooks were still screwed into the studs, holding nothing.

"That was the Alvarez place," said the old woman in the quilted coat, to no one, to everyone. "Marta Alvarez. She grew tomatoes on the fire escape."

"Marta Alvarez," Mr. Okafor repeated, like an amen.

Rosalind watched the wall come down and thought: *I practiced in that kitchen.* Not the Alvarez kitchen—hers, the fourth floor, the narrow one where she'd set her metronome on the counter because the table wobbled. The piano had been in the front room, the upright her mother had helped her buy secondhand, the one with the ivory missing from middle C. She'd sold it when she left. She'd sold it for almost nothing, to a man who came with a truck and straps and asked no questions, and she had stood in the emptied front room afterward and felt the silence move into her body like a tenant taking possession.

The claw reached up and took the fourth floor.

There was a moment—she would remember this moment for the rest of her life, however long the rest of it turned out to be—when the roof peeled back and the fourth floor stood open to the morning light, its rooms exposed like organs, and she could see it, all of it, the whole geography of the year she had tried to erase. The window over the sink. The closet where the shoebox had lived under a pile of winter coats. The hallway with the warped board near the bathroom that everyone learned to step over, and past that—

Past that, the front room. Empty now, of course. No piano. But she could see, for one suspended second before the wall came down, the exact patch of floor where the piano had stood, outlined in the lighter wood where the sunlight had never reached, a shadow burned permanently into the boards. The shape of the music. Still there. Still legible, after twenty-two years, to anyone who knew how to read it.

The wall came down. The dust rolled across the street in a slow beige wave, and the crowd flinched and covered their mouths, and Rosalind stood inside the edge of it and let it land on her hair, her shoulders, her lips. It tasted the way she remembered: plaster and coal smoke and time.

* * *

By noon it was half gone.

The workers broke for lunch and the crowd thinned, the way crowds do when the spectacle becomes repetition. But Rosalind stayed. The old woman stayed. Mr. Okafor stayed. They had formed a loose triangle on the sidewalk, an audience of three, and none of them acknowledged the others, but none of them left either. There is a fellowship among mourners that doesn't require introductions.

She thought about Vane.

She had spent the night, between photographs, thinking about him too—the way you probe a tooth you know is bad. Twenty-two years ago he had sat across from her in his office at the conservatory, this legend with his silver hair and his voice like a cello, and he had told her that the piece wasn't finished, that she wasn't finished, that talent like hers needed protecting from itself. And then she had run—run from him, run from the conservatory, run from Daniel waiting on the steps—and somewhere in the running, the song had stayed behind in Vane's keeping. And fifteen years later it had surfaced, recorded, released, celebrated, his name on the spine of it and hers nowhere, not even in the liner notes, not even as *after a theme by*. He had trimmed the ending off and called it complete. He had published her wound and signed it himself.

For two weeks since the radio, since Static, she had been carrying the anger like a stone in her chest, and she had assumed the stone was permanent, part of her geology now. But watching the building come down, she noticed something: the stone was shifting. Not dissolving—shifting. Because she had played the song last night, all of it, her version, the true ending, and the playing had done something the anger couldn't undo. He could own the recording. He could own the name on it. But he could not own what had happened in that hallway at midnight, the sound rising through four floors of dead building, hers, unrecorded, given to no one, kept by no one but the walls—which were, even now, coming down.

Maybe that was why he'd never let the piece have an ending. Maybe he'd known, all those years ago, that the ending was the part that belonged to whoever finished it. And he had never been able to finish it because it was never his.

Or maybe he was simply a thief, and thieves steal what they cannot make, and there was nothing more to understand than that. She supposed she would find out. There was a letter in her bag—written at four a.m., in handwriting that shook only at the beginning—folded into an envelope with his name on it. Whether she mailed it or delivered it or burned it, she didn't yet know. But it existed now. It had weight. Things with weight want to fall.

* * *

At two o'clock they took the stairwell.

Rosalind felt it go before she saw it—a tremor through the sidewalk, through the soles of her shoes, up through her shins like a chord struck low on the keyboard—and then the fire escape tore free and the whole eastern face slid down in a slow avalanche of brick, and where the staircase had been there was nothing, just sky and dust and the memory of ten thousand trips up and down, her schoolbag banging her hip, her sheet music curling in the damp, Daniel's footsteps two stairs behind her, always two stairs behind, patient as tide.

"Oh," said the old woman softly. "Oh, there goes the stairs."

And Rosalind found, to her astonishment, that she was crying.

Not the way she'd cried at nineteen—privately, usefully, tears as currency paid to no one—or the way she'd cried two weeks ago in the boiler room, furious and frightened. This was different. This was plain. Tears like weather, like the dust, falling because there was nothing left to hold them up. She cried for the stairs and the tomatoes on the fire escape and Marta Alvarez and middle C with its chipped ivory and Daniel's patient footsteps and her mother's voice on the telephone saying *come home, Rosie, whatever it is, it isn't worth your hands*—and it had been worth her hands, that was the joke, it had cost her exactly her hands, she'd never played seriously again, not really, not until last night—and she cried because the building had kept everything, all these years, quietly, the way buildings do, and now it was giving it all back to the air at once, and someone had to receive it, and she was the one standing there.

Mr. Okafor put a hand on her shoulder. Just briefly. Then took it away. It was enough.

* * *

By late afternoon there was very little left. A stump of the northern wall, two stories high, stood alone against the darkening sky like the last tooth in an old mouth. The workers moved slower now, more careful, the way you move at the end of anything. The dust had gotten into everything—into the crowd's coats, into their hair, into the folds of the orange mesh fencing. Rosalind's coat, her mother's coat, was gray with it, and she found she didn't mind. Let it be buried in it. Let it carry some of this away.

The supervisor with the clipboard came around again, checking the light, checking his watch. Tomorrow they'd finish. Tonight the stump would stand alone in the dark, exposed to the street, its interior rooms visible to anyone passing—doorframes opening onto nothing, wallpapered walls holding up no ceiling, the shadow of a piano burned into bare boards.

Rosalind turned to go.

And that was when she saw them, standing at the far end of the block, half-hidden by the fence and the failing light: two figures. One tall, gray-haired, unmistakable even at this distance, wearing a long dark coat, motionless, watching the ruin with his hands clasped behind his back like a man at a graveside. Elias Vane. Come to see it with his own eyes—the building, or the demolition, or perhaps, though she doubted it, her.

And beside him, closer, having clearly arrived separately, jacket collar up against the cold, scanning the thinning crowd with an expression she knew better than her own reflection—Daniel. Looking for her. Having come anyway, unanswered texts and all, because he had always come anyway.

Vane had not seen her. Daniel had not yet. Between them the stump of the building stood black against the last of the light, and the dust was still settling, and Rosalind Ashgrove stood very still in the middle of the empty street, holding a letter in her pocket and twenty-two years in her chest, as both of them—her past and her future, her theft and her love—began, slowly, to walk toward her.
