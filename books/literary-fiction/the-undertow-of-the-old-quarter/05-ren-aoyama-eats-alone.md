## 5. Ren Aoyama Eats Alone

The car smelled of his father's cologne, which was to say it smelled of money trying to pass for restraint. Ren sat in the back seat with the site plans unrolled across his knees, though he had memorized them weeks ago and no longer needed to look. He looked anyway, because looking at documents gave him something to do with his face.

"Glass frontage on the north elevation," his father's project manager was saying from the passenger seat, half turned around so his voice could travel the distance between them like a man laying planks over a gap. "Double-height atrium where the lane narrows — we open up the sightline to the station. Foot traffic doubles. The consultants are very excited about the sightline."

"The consultants," Ren said, "have never walked down that lane."

The manager smiled the way men smile when they have decided a remark is not worth decoding. Outside the window, Tokyo thinned and softened as they crossed the river into the old quarter — the buildings lowering themselves toward the ground, the signage going hand-painted, the trees suddenly older than anything with an address. Ren watched a shuttered tofu shop slide past, its noren faded to the color of weak tea, and felt the particular nausea of a man being driven through a place he is about to help kill.

He had been to the site twice before. The first time he'd come alone, on a Sunday, wearing a plain jacket, and had walked the lanes without telling anyone. He had bought coffee from a vending machine because there was nowhere else to buy it, and had drunk it standing in front of the Ishikawa house, reading the auction notice pasted to its gate like a diagnosis. The second time had been with the surveyors, hard hats and laser measures, their voices pitched in the cheerful register of men discussing square meters. Both times he had left with the same sensation: that the quarter did not resent him yet, but would learn to.

Now the car pulled up at the corner where the lane began — too narrow for vehicles, which was precisely the problem, or the point, depending on which memo you read. The manager got out first, unfolding himself into the daylight and immediately consulting his phone. Ren followed more slowly.

The morning was bright in the way of early autumn, light falling at a long angle so that every surface declared itself: the worn stone of the lane, the moss in its seams, the rust bleeding down from a drainpipe like old blood. The Ishikawa house stood at the lane's bend, set back behind a wall whose tiles had slipped in places, revealing under the eaves the pale ghost-wood where paint had given up decades ago. A city notice was stapled to the gate. DEMOLITION SCHEDULED. Ninety days, minus the eleven that had already passed since the letter went out.

Ren had argued against this acquisition. Not loudly — loudly was not a weapon available to him — but in meetings, in the careful language of risk: *heritage objections, community friction, negative press potential.* His father had listened with the patience of a man hearing weather reported from another country, then said, "The land is underpriced and the seller is the city. There is no seller easier than the city." And that had been that. Ren understood the arithmetic. He even, in his worst moments, admired it. What he could not do was feel it as anything other than what it was: the conversion of a place where people had lived into a diagram where people would shop.

"Structural report came back," the manager said, tapping at his screen. "Roof trusses are shot. Honestly, it's held up by habit."

*Held up by habit.* Ren looked at the house. From outside you could see the layered lives of it — the main structure prewar by the roofline, an addition tacked on sometime in the fifties with cheaper timber, a lean-to porch sagging under a century of rain. Three generations of patching. It wore its history the way old clothes wear their mends: visibly, unapologetically, as proof of use rather than shame.

His father had grown up in a house like this one, in a town in Saitama that no longer existed except as a highway interchange. Ren knew the story by heart because it was told at every dinner party: the poverty, the rice balls wrapped in bamboo leaf, the boy who studied by kerosene lamp and became the man who built towers. What the story never explained was why the man who escaped poverty had spent fifty years demolishing the places poverty lived in, as if erasing the evidence were the same as paying the debt.

They walked the lane. The manager narrated: here the atrium, here the service corridor, here the loading bay where currently a cat slept on a stack of crates outside a shop that sold nothing visible anymore. An old woman watering plants in buckets along her step watched them pass with the flat assessment of someone who has watched many men walk this lane holding papers. She did not greet them. Ren found himself unable to meet her eyes, and hated himself for it — hated the flinch, the small cowardice of a man who benefits from a thing and cannot bear to be seen benefiting.

At the bend, near the Ishikawa gate, the manager stopped and made a show of checking his watch. "I've got the contractor call at eleven. You're fine here on your own? You know the numbers better than I do at this point."

"I'm fine."

"You could get lunch after. There was a good soba place—" He checked his phone again. "Was. Closed last year." He said it without irony, without noticing he had said anything at all, and walked back up the lane toward the car, his footsteps receding until the quarter swallowed them.

And then it was quiet.

Not silent — the quarter was never silent; it breathed with small sounds, wind chime, water in a gutter, a radio somewhere playing enka at the volume of a private grief — but quiet in the way that lets you hear yourself inside your own life. Ren stood at the gate of the house his company intended to erase and did not go in. He had keys; he had authority; he had, technically, the right. That was exactly why he wouldn't. Entering felt like something a thief does before the theft is even necessary.

Instead he walked the lane's length slowly, cataloguing it the way the surveyors had catalogued it, but with different instruments. Here, a shrine shelf built into a wall niche, its tiny fox statue sun-bleached to blindness, still holding a fresh camellia — someone still tended it. Here, a bathhouse chimney rising above the rooftops, thin white smoke, the last one, he knew from the plans, the last communal bath in the district, marked on his father's rendering as RETAIL UNIT C. Here, a door left open on a dark shop interior where an ancient man sat behind a counter stacked with thread spools, selling nothing to no one, keeping the hours out of loyalty to some agreement made with himself decades ago.

*We are not building a complex,* Ren thought. *We are performing an amputation and calling the wound retail.*

He heard the camera shutter before he saw her.

A soft click, twice, from the direction of the Ishikawa gate. He turned and saw a woman standing just inside the wall's shadow, photographing the house — not the way tourists photograph things, sweeping and greedy, but the way people photograph what they are losing: close, deliberate, frame by frame, as if assembling evidence for a court that no longer exists. She was slight, dark-haired, dressed in a way that was expensive but worn carelessly, the way clothes get worn by someone who forgot they were wearing them. She moved along the wall taking pictures of details — the slipped tiles, the scorch-darkened stones near the kitchen side, the gate hinge — and she touched nothing.

There was something in the way she stood that snagged at him. A stillness that wasn't calm. He recognized it because he practiced it daily: the discipline of appearing composed while something underneath is quietly coming apart.

She lowered the camera and he saw her face properly, and the recognition arrived sideways, delayed, the way train stations arrive when you've been reading: he knew her name before he placed her face. Coral Reyes — no, that wasn't— *Coral Ishikawa.* No. The byline. He had read her reviews for years. Everyone in his father's orbit read her; his father quoted her in negotiations the way generals quote scripture. *Coral Reyes.* The restaurant critic. The one whose single paragraph could fill or empty a dining room for a season. The one whose prose was famous for a quality reviewers kept calling *restraint*, as if restraint were a flavor.

She was photographing her own childhood home. He understood this the way you understand things about strangers: instantly, completely, and without permission.

He should have walked away. Every instinct of self-preservation said so — she was press, she was dangerous, she was the exact person who should never see him here seeing this. But there was also the other thing, the thing his father would never understand: that Ren had spent thirty-one years inside his inherited life feeling like a tenant who couldn't find the lease, and here was a woman photographing a house she clearly couldn't enter either, and the symmetry of it pulled at him like current.

He stayed on his side of the lane and let her finish. When she finally lowered the camera and turned, she saw him, and he watched her register three things in sequence: stranger, suit, threat.

"Ms. Reyes," he said, and bowed, shallow and correct. "I'm sorry — I didn't mean to interrupt."

Her eyes went to his suit, then to the rolled plans under his arm, then back to his face, and he watched the third category — *threat* — consolidate. "You're with the developer."

It wasn't a question. He considered lying, briefly, the way one considers a door and doesn't open it. "I'm Ren Aoyama. My father's firm owns the option on this parcel."

Something moved through her expression — not surprise; she had known, then, or suspected — something more like confirmation, the weary satisfaction of a hypothesis proved. "The glass complex," she said.

"The glass complex," he agreed. And then, because silence was worse and because he was apparently determined to make this worse: "I voted against it."

"Voted." She almost smiled. It was the least warm thing he had ever seen a mouth do. "How did that work out?"

"It didn't."

She looked past him, up the lane, at the bathhouse smoke, at the fox shrine with its camellia, at all of it — the whole perishable organism of the quarter — and when she spoke again her voice had gone somewhere flat and far away. "Do you know what's under the floor of that house? Because I do. And none of your surveys will find it."

"No," he said honestly. "I don't know what's under the floor."

For a moment neither of them moved. Down the lane the radio changed songs. Somewhere a kettle whistled and was silenced. The light shifted off the slipped tiles and the house seemed, for a second, to lean slightly closer, listening.

"My card," Ren said, and took it out, and held it out across the distance between them — the distance being not two meters of lane but everything else. "If you ever need access to the municipal records on this parcel. Sealed ones. Sale histories, occupancy files. Things families don't always have."

She didn't take it. She looked at the card the way she'd looked at the gate — as a detail to be photographed, catalogued, held at evidence-distance. Then she took it after all, with two fingers, without thanks, and slid it into her coat pocket, and turned back to the house, and raised the camera, and dismissed him entirely, the shutter clicking once, twice, like a small verdict.

Ren walked up the lane toward the waiting car, and the strangest part, the part he would turn over that night in his apartment high above the city with a convenience-store meal he ate standing at the counter because sitting felt too permanent — the strangest part was not that she hadn't thanked him.

It was that walking away from her, he had felt, for the first time in years, less alone in the wrongness. And that frightened him more than the demolition did.
