## 6. The Cedar Box

The box had been sitting on the kitchen table for two days before Coral worked up the courage to touch it again.

She had found it on the second morning of cataloguing, wedged into the back of the *tansu* chest in the room that had been her grandmother's — the room with the low ceiling and the smell of camphor and old silk that had somehow survived fifteen years of emptiness. She had been working methodically through the drawers, photographing each item for the estate inventory, assigning numbers in her neat critic's hand: *kimono, summer weight, moth damage, no. 14; lacquer tray, cracked, no. 15.* It was the kind of work she was good at, this turning of a life into a list. She had been turning her own life into a list for years.

The box was small, no bigger than a bread loaf, made of cedar gone dark with age, its corners dovetailed with a precision no one bothered with anymore. There was no key in the lock. There was no key anywhere in the house — she had checked the chipped teacup on the *genkan* shelf, the tin beneath the kitchen sink where her mother had kept batteries and string, the coat pockets of every coat still hanging in the hall closet like the shed skins of people who had once had somewhere to go.

She had set it on the kitchen table, in the square of afternoon light that came through the window facing the lane, and then she had gone upstairs to photograph her mother's sewing machine and had not come back down until dark.

Now, on the third morning, she sat across from it with her coffee — convenience-store coffee in a paper cup, because the house had no kettle that worked and she had not bought one, because buying one would mean admitting she intended to stay, and she still told herself she was here only to inventory and leave — and she looked at it the way she used to look at a plate she could not taste.

*No. 16,* she wrote in the margin of her inventory sheet. *Cedar box, locked. Contents unknown.*

The house made its sounds around her. It was a house that had learned to speak again now that someone was living in it — the settling groans of the floorboards, the tick of the old water heater she'd coaxed back to life, the wind finding the loose shingle over the kitchen and worrying it like a tongue against a missing tooth. In the first days the silence had been total, a held breath. Now the house had decided she was a tenant and had begun its complaints.

Her phone buzzed against the table. Ren Aoyama.

*Ms. Ishikawa — I hope the inventory is going well. I've made some inquiries about the municipal records. There are sealed files from the land registry going back to the occupation. If you're interested, I can arrange access. No obligation. — R*

She read it twice, did not answer, and turned the phone face-down. There was something in the offer she did not yet have a name for — generosity with a hook in it, a fisherman's kindness. She would think about it later. She was getting good at later. Later was the country she lived in.

The box sat in the light.

She thought about her grandmother's hands. This was the strange mercy of memory, that it saved the hands when it lost the face — she could not summon her grandmother's expression anymore, but the hands came to her whole: broad-palmed, the knuckles swollen from decades of hot water, a burn scar on the left wrist shaped like a comma, as if even her body had paused mid-sentence. Those hands had lifted her as a child over the threshold of the kitchen and said, in the old accent she'd been too young to know was dying, words she had never understood and never asked about.

The hands had also, she remembered now with a small cold shock, kept the box. She had a fragment of an image — herself at six or seven, coming into this same room unannounced, and her grandmother sliding something into the *tansu*'s lowest drawer with a speed that was not casual, that was practiced. And the look she'd been given afterward had not been anger. It had been something more like a door closing gently so the child wouldn't see the room behind it.

Coral set down the coffee. She went to the tool bag she'd bought at the hardware store in the next district — she had told herself it was for prying up loose floorboards to check for rot, and it had been a lie even as she bought it — and took out the flat pry bar.

The lock was brass, small, older than she was. She could have gone into town, found a locksmith, done this properly. But some animal part of her understood that this was not a thing to be done properly. Proper was for other people's houses.

She worked the flat edge of the bar into the seam between the lid and the body, just at the corner where the wood had already begun its long argument with the nails. The cedar creaked. She pressed. The whole box flexed, groaned, and for a moment she felt like a burglar in her own inheritance — then the nails gave, one and then the other, with a sound like small knuckles cracking, and the lid came up on a breath of air that had been sealed inside since before she was born.

It smelled like her grandmother.

Not metaphorically — or not only. Cedar and camphor, yes, but under that something specific and unrepeatable: dried fish smoke, charcoal, the faint sweet rot of rice bran, the ghost of chicken stock reduced to its bones. The smell of the stall. The smell that had lived in her grandmother's hair and apron and skin for forty years, that had been the smell of being held. Coral sat down hard on the kitchen stool and put her hand over her mouth and breathed it in like a drowning woman breathing air, and for one full minute she could not do anything at all.

When she could move again, she looked inside.

The top layer was paper. Ration stubs — dozens of them, bundled with string gone soft as thread, each stamped with dates from the late forties, the kanji for rice, for coal, for sugar, some marked with the red slash of *already claimed*, some not. Under them, a child's rubber sandals, impossibly small, one strap broken. Under those, a photograph.

Black-and-white, the corners rounded from handling. The stall itself — she knew it instantly, though she had only ever seen it in this same photograph's descendants: the low wooden counter, the hanging cloth with characters she couldn't read, the great iron pot with its lid ajar, steam rendered as a white blur against the dark. Two women stood behind the counter. The younger one was her grandmother, unmistakably, though she looked like a stranger — young, thin, her hair tied back with what might have been a strip of cloth torn from a kimono, her arms bare to the elbow in the cold. She was not smiling. She was looking directly into the camera with an expression Coral had never been given a word for — defiance and exhaustion and something fiercer than either, the look of a person who has decided, this morning, this once, to be photographed as what she actually is.

Beside her stood an older woman, heavier, wearing a man's coat. Coral had no idea who she was. There was no one left to ask.

She turned the photograph over. On the back, in pencil, in her grandmother's hand — the same hand she knew from the shopping lists of her childhood, the characters leaning right like people walking into wind — was a single line.

But it was not Japanese. Not the Japanese she knew, not the stiff old-fashioned brushwork she'd seen in her grandmother's letters. The characters were different, rounder, some of them half-invented, and between them ran a scattering of marks that looked less like writing than like weather — small strokes, curls, a system of notation that seemed to be trying to record something that ordinary characters couldn't hold. One sentence. Maybe two. It ran the full width of the card and stopped, deliberately, the way a person stops speaking when they hear someone coming.

Coral held it up to the light as if the paper might be more legible from behind. It wasn't. She photographed it front and back, then photographed it again with the flash, then sat looking at the image on her screen, zooming in until the pencil strokes became graphite canyons and the sentence dissolved into pure texture, meaning gone, only gesture left.

She knew what it was not. It was not a recipe. Recipes were written to be followed. This was written to be kept.

* * *

Mrs. Ono came at four, the way she had come every day since Coral arrived — with something in a covered bowl, set down on the *genkan* step with a knock and a retreat, a system the two of them had fallen into without ever discussing it. Today Coral was waiting at the door before the knock finished.

"Mrs. Ono. Please. I need you to look at something."

The old woman's eyebrows went up — Coral had never asked her for anything, had barely managed more than a bow and a thank-you for the food — but she stepped out of her shoes and followed Coral into the kitchen, moving through the dead house with the unselfconscious ease of someone for whom it had never stopped being alive.

Coral laid the photograph on the table, pencil-side up, next to the opened box.

Mrs. Ono did not touch it. She stood over it for a long time, her hands folded into her apron, and Coral watched her face do something complicated — the way a still pond does something complicated when a stone goes in, all of it underneath.

"Where," Mrs. Ono said at last, and her voice had changed, gone flatter, "did you find this."

"In the *tansu*. Bottom drawer. It was locked."

"I know it was locked." She said it the way you confirm a fact of geography. She pulled out the stool — the same stool Coral had sat on that morning — and lowered herself onto it, and only then did she pick up the photograph, holding it by its edges like something consecrated.

"That's her," Coral said. "My grandmother. And I don't know who the other woman is."

"Nor do I," Mrs. Ono said, "and I looked at this stall every day for thirty years." She turned it over. She read the line of writing. Coral watched her lips move slightly, not reading — trying. Then the lips stopped.

"This is Tsugaru," Mrs. Ono said. "The old speech. From the north. Aomori." She set the photograph down with great care, face-up again, the young grandmother looking at the ceiling of the kitchen where she had once cooked. "Your grandmother came from up there. I suppose you didn't know."

"No one ever said."

"No one ever says. That's the whole point of coming down here, isn't it." Mrs. Ono was quiet for a moment. "My mother spoke a little. Her mother more. It's not Japanese the way you mean Japanese — it's what the north spoke before the radios got to it. There's no one left who talks it. Maybe a few very old people up in the villages, if any of them are still breathing. They tried to record it, years ago. Some university. I remember it in the paper."

"Can you read it?"

"I can hear maybe one word in ten, and I wouldn't trust the nine." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious at once, the particular anger of the old at the young for arriving too late. "Your grandmother wrote this and locked it and kept it sixty years, Coral. People don't do that for shopping lists. She wrote it in a language that was already dead when she wrote it, so that almost no one could read it — and so that it couldn't be erased either. That's not a note. That's a testament."

The kitchen was very quiet. The loose shingle ticked in the wind.

"What do I do?" Coral asked, and heard how young her own voice sounded.

Mrs. Ono stood, and put the photograph down in front of her, and did the thing she had never done: she put her hand, briefly, on Coral's head, the way you bless a child.

"You find someone who can hear the nine," she said. "You go north."

She collected her empty bowl from the step and was gone into the lane, and Coral sat alone with the box, the stubs, the sandals, the photograph of two women who had fed a street out of a pot in the worst years of the century, and the sentence that had waited sixty years in a dead language for someone to come who could not read it and could not leave it alone.

Outside, the light was going. Somewhere north, past all the cities stacked between here and the snow country, there were — what? A linguist. A recording. A ninety-year-old in a village who might hold the last living piece of a tongue her grandmother had carried south in her mouth and locked in a box.

Coral took out her phone. Ren's message was still there, patient as a trap: *access to the sealed municipal records.* And beneath it, in her search bar, she typed the words she should have typed the moment Mrs. Ono walked out the door:

*Tsugaru dialect — last speakers — Aomori.*

The results loaded. There were not many.

She booked the train for Thursday.
