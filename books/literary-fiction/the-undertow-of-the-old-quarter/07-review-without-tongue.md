## 7. Review Without Tongue

The nomination arrived on a Tuesday, which was fitting, because Tuesdays were the days Coral Ishikawa most reliably felt like a fraud.

She learned of it the way she learned most things now — through her phone, in the fluorescent purgatory of the editorial office, where the air conditioning breathed its one cold note over rows of desks and someone's instant noodles perfumed the room with a pork-bone richness she could describe but not perceive. The email came from the Japan Food Writers' Association: *We are pleased to inform you that your review of Kaiseki Ryōri Mizuhana has been shortlisted for the Tsuruoka Prize.*

Below it, before she had finished reading, a second message bloomed from her editor, Ogawa-san, all caps and exclamation points: **TSURUOKA SHORTLIST!!! I TOLD THEM. I TOLD EVERYONE.**

Around her, the office began to assemble itself into celebration. A junior editor appeared at her elbow with two paper cups of convenience-store sparkling wine, the kind that tasted — she remembered; she still had the memory of tasting, filed away like pressed flowers — of sweetened chalk and ambition. People clapped. Someone put a printed screenshot of the review on her desk with a gold star drawn in marker, the way you might decorate a child's homework.

Coral held the cup and smiled the smile she had practiced for fifteen years, the one that reached her eyes because her eyes were good at performing what her mouth no longer meant.

*Restraint,* Ogawa had called it, when the Mizuhana piece ran. That was the word the industry had settled on for her, years ago, back when the silence in her mouth first hardened into style. *Ishikawa doesn't gush. Ishikawa doesn't perform ecstasy. She observes.* Her reviews read like weather reports written by someone standing outside in the rain without an umbrella — precise about temperature, wind, pressure, and utterly silent on the question of whether any of it was beautiful.

They gave her prizes for it.

She looked down at the printed review now, at her own sentences, and read them as if they belonged to a stranger whose methods she had studied closely. *The dashi arrives first, a clear amber broth that announces the kitchen's confidence...* True, probably. She had taken notes. She had watched the other diners at Mizuhana close their eyes at the first sip, watched a man in a gray suit actually shudder with pleasure, and she had transcribed his face into her prose the way a portraitist transcribes a face — faithfully, technically, from the outside.

*The charcoal-grated wagyu dissolves against the tongue like a rumor resolving into truth.*

A rumor resolving into truth. Ogawa had underlined that line twice and called it the best sentence she'd written in a decade. It had come to her fully formed while she sat in the restaurant's hushed private room, watching steam rise off meat she could not taste, feeling the heat of it on her lips and nothing behind it, and some exhausted, clever part of her brain had reached for language the way a drowning swimmer reaches for driftwood. The editors loved her driftwood. They built awards out of it.

What none of them knew — what no one alive knew, except perhaps the numbness itself — was that Coral Ishikawa had not tasted anything since the morning of her mother's funeral. Fifteen years of eating as an act of archaeology. Texture survived; temperature survived; salt announced itself as a kind of pressure, sweetness as a faint suggestion, like hearing music through a wall. But flavor — the actual country of flavor, with its cities and rivers and dialects — had closed its borders to her the day the incense smoke went up from the crematorium chimney, and she had been living in exile ever since, writing postcards home in fluent, hollow prose.

She drank the sparkling wine. It was cold and it hurt pleasantly going down and it tasted of nothing at all.

"To Coral," said the junior editor, raising her cup. "To the best palate in Tokyo."

Coral raised hers. "To the best palate in Tokyo," she repeated, and drank again, and felt the toast go through her like water through a broken pipe.

* * *

That evening she took the long way home, which was not really a choice so much as a habit her body had developed without consulting her. The long way passed through Yanaka, through lanes that smelled — people told her — of grilled fish and tatami and old wood, though what Coral received was only the geometry of those smells: the warmth of vents, the coolness of shade, the faint prickle of charcoal smoke in her nose. She walked past shuttered shopfronts and a cat asleep on a vending machine and a bathhouse whose chimney still stood like a finger pointed at nothing, and she thought about the house.

Ninety days. Eighty-six now. The certified letter sat on her kitchen table beneath a bowl of keys, and every morning she lifted the bowl, saw the city's crest embossed in blue, and put the bowl back down. *Notice of public auction. Structure located 2-chōme, [redacted] district. Demolition scheduled following sale.* Her grandmother's house. Her mother's house. The house where, at six years old, standing on a stool beside a woman who is now only a scorch mark on a kitchen floor, Coral had licked broth off a wooden spoon and tasted something so complete that every meal since had been, without her knowing it, a search for its equal.

It was the last place she ever tasted anything. She understood this the way you understand a debt: not all at once, but accruing.

Her phone buzzed against her palm.

An unknown number, then a message, then — because the sender was apparently incapable of waiting — a second message, and a third:

**Aoyama Ren. We met briefly at the site last week — you were photographing the house. I don't know if you remember.**

**I've read your work. All of it, actually. The Mizuhana review is extraordinary.**

**This will sound strange by text. Would you have dinner? There's something I'd like to propose, and it concerns the house. And the municipal records, which are sealed, and which I can open.**

Coral stopped walking. Above her, laundry lines crossed the lane like rigging on a grounded ship. Somewhere a television murmured through a window, a game show audience laughing on cue.

She remembered him, of course. She'd noticed him the way you notice weather — a tall man in a good coat standing at the edge of the vacant lot with the posture of someone attending a funeral he hadn't planned. He had watched her photographing the house with an expression she'd catalogued automatically, professionally, the way she catalogued everything now: *not hostility. Something adjacent. Grief wearing a suit.*

She typed: **Dinner where?**

His reply came fast enough to suggest he'd been holding the phone. **My father's flagship. Ginza. I know how that sounds. Let me prove it can be otherwise.**

*How that sounds.* She almost smiled. It sounded exactly like what it was: the son of the developer who was buying her childhood out from under the city, inviting her to dinner at the temple of his family's empire, dangling sealed records like fruit. She could see the shape of the transaction already, the way she could see the shape of a dish from across a room. And yet.

And yet the words *municipal records* had done something to her chest that food had not done in fifteen years.

**Thursday,** she typed. **But I choose what I write. Whatever this is.**

**Obviously,** he replied. Then, after a pause long enough to register as honesty: **I hope you'll believe that by the end of the night.**

* * *

Thursday came, and with it Ginza, which always struck Coral as a city pretending to be a showroom. Aoyama's flagship restaurant occupied the fourth floor of a tower the family did not own but might as well have, all pale stone and indirect light, where the maître d' greeted Ren by name and the name opened doors the way names do when they're attached to money three generations deep.

Ren was waiting at a corner table, and he stood when she approached — an old-fashioned gesture, slightly too formal, as if he'd rehearsed it and then worried he'd rehearsed it. Up close he was younger than his coat suggested and more tired than his youth allowed. He had his father's jaw and, she would learn, very little else of his father.

"You came," he said.

"You sounded sincere," Coral said, sitting. "Or desperate. The two are difficult to distinguish over text."

"Then let me make it worse in person." He poured her water himself, waving off the sommelier with a small apologetic motion, and Coral noted that too — a man managing his own restaurant the way you manage a hostage situation. "First: I'm not here to buy your byline."

"That's usually the second thing people say."

"I know." He almost smiled. It transformed his face briefly into something unguarded, boyish, and then the armor came back down. "I've read everything you've written for eleven years. Since university. My colleagues think I follow food journalism. I don't. I follow yours specifically, which is either flattering or alarming depending on how you look at it."

"It's alarming," Coral said. "Continue."

He laughed — a real laugh, quickly suppressed, as though laughter were also something he'd inherited and wasn't sure he was permitted. The first courses arrived and Coral performed her ritual: she watched them, described them silently to herself, observed Ren watching them. He ate the way she wrote — attentively, correctly, without joy. She recognized the symptom immediately. It was like meeting a speaker of your dead language.

"You don't taste it either," she said.

The chopsticks paused. For a moment she thought he'd deny it, and she watched him decide not to, watched the decision cost him something. "No," he said quietly. "Not since I was nineteen. Not since my father informed me over dinner at this exact table that I would be taking over the development division, and that my opinions on the matter were noted and irrelevant." He set the chopsticks down. "Everything since tastes like that conversation."

Coral said nothing. There was nothing useful to say, and both of them knew the particular mercy of not saying it.

"The proposal," Ren said, and straightened slightly, businesslike now, though his hands stayed loose around his glass. "The firm wants a feature. Prestige press. The redevelopment of the quarter — heritage, renewal, the future of the city, all of it. My father wants your name on it because your name means restraint, credibility, the sense that the piece wasn't bought even though—" he gestured faintly at the room, at the tower, at the entire arrangement of the world— "it was."

"And you?"

"I want," he said, "to give you access to the municipal file on plot 2-chōme. Sealed since 1953. Land transfers, occupation-era permits, the stall licenses, the complaints, the sale documents. Everything the city has on the Ishikawa property for seventy years." He leaned forward. "You've been photographing that house like it owes you an explanation. I can get you the explanation. Or at least the paperwork version of it."

The room hummed around them. Somewhere a fish was being filleted with ceremonial precision, and Coral registered its beauty the way she registered all beauty now — completely, and from behind glass.

"And in exchange," she said, "the profile flatters the project."

"In exchange, the profile exists," Ren said. "What it says is yours. I mean that. I'll give you the records, full access, my father's cooperation — and if you write that the whole development is a crime against memory, I'll deliver it to my father myself and stand there while he reads it." He held her gaze. "I can't promise the piece runs uncensored. I'm not the one with the name on the building. But I can promise that what happens after is on me, not you."

It was well said. It was, Coral thought, possibly even sincere. That was the trap's finest craftsmanship — that every part of it was true, and the shape it made was still a cage. She would take the records. She would walk into the archive with his key and come out with her grandmother's paper trail, and whatever she wrote afterward would be written in the shadow of that debt, and everyone — her editor, her readers, Mrs. Ono, the quarter itself — would know exactly which door she'd entered through.

Complicity, offered as opportunity. Served on pale stone, with excellent water she couldn't taste.

"When," she said.

"Monday. I'll meet you at the archives." He hesitated, then added, quieter: "There's a photograph in the file. I've seen it. I shouldn't tell you that, but you strike me as someone who'd rather be warned than protected."

"What kind of photograph?"

"Of the stall," Ren said. "Opening day, 1947. Your grandmother is in it. So are about forty neighbors." He turned his glass slowly on the tablecloth. "And half the plot they're standing on isn't hers yet. That's the part the file explains. Or fails to."

Afterward he walked her to the elevator, and they rode down together in a silence that felt less like awkwardness than like two people listening for the same distant sound. At the lobby he said, "Thank you for coming," and she said, "Don't thank me yet," and they both understood that the real negotiation had not happened at the table at all. It would happen in the archive, among the sealed papers, between her and whatever her grandmother had signed her name to in a year when signing anything was an act of survival.

Outside, Ginza glittered its indifferent glitter. Coral stood a moment on the pavement, breathing the exhaust-warm air, and found that her hands were trembling — not with dread, exactly, and not with hope, but with the specific vibration of a locked door about to open.

Monday, then. The paper trail. The photograph of forty neighbors standing on land that wasn't theirs yet.

She hailed a cab and gave the driver her address, and all the way home she thought about her grandmother's hands — hands she could still see, though she could no longer summon the taste of anything they'd ever made — and wondered what those hands had held besides ladles.
