## 5. Almost Saying It

The care home sat on a hill above another valley, as if the whole country were a series of compensations—one drowned place traded for one dry view. Tamsin had driven up from Ashcombe that morning with the tin box belted into the passenger seat like a child, and now stood in the corridor of Beechwood House breathing the smells of lavender disinfectant and overcooked cabbage, rehearsing sentences they would never say.

*Hello, Mum. I've found her letters. The ones you never opened. I've found what Grandfather did.*

The words arranged themselves in Tamsin's head with terrible fluency. That was the translator's curse, always: perfect sentences available for every occasion except the ones that mattered.

A nurse with kind eyes and a lanyard reading *DEBORAH* led them down the corridor. "She's having a good day," Deborah said, in the tone people used for weather reports about places prone to sudden storms. "She was asking after the water this morning. Kept saying there was too much of it. We thought she meant the bath."

Tamsin's chest tightened. "Did she say anything else?"

"Words we didn't know." Deborah smiled apologetically. "Pretty words, though. Like singing."

The room was small and faced east, so that Edith Marchetti—born Edith Halloran, daughter of the drowned village's schoolteacher—received the sun before anyone else in the building, every morning, whether she wanted it or not. She sat by the window in a chair the colour of weak tea, hands folded in her lap like two sleeping birds, and when Tamsin came through the door she looked up without surprise, as if visitors arrived out of the past all the time here.

"You've got your father's way of standing," she said.

"I don't have a father."

"No." She considered this. "You don't. You've got his way of standing anyway."

Tamsin pulled the second chair close to hers. Up close, their mother smelled of talcum powder and something underneath it, mineral and cold, like stones lifted from a riverbed. Her hair had gone the white of birch bark. Her eyes were still brown, still quick, but the quickness moved now like fish under ice—you saw it flash and then it was gone, and you couldn't say where it had been.

"I brought some things," Tamsin said. "From Gran's cottage."

"Whose cottage?"

"Your mother's."

Edith frowned at the window. Beyond the glass, the hills went down and down into fields, orderly, unwatered, unwaterlogged. "My mother lived near the water," she said. "Everything near the water. Everything was near the water, then."

"Then?"

"Before." She said it the way other people said *during the war*. A single syllable containing an entire vanished geography.

Tamsin took out the letters—not the box itself; they hadn't been able to make themselves carry it inside—and chose the topmost envelope, the one addressed in a hand Tamsin now knew better than their own: *Miss E. Halloran*, in ink gone the brown of dried blood. They held it out.

Edith looked at it and did not take it. Her hands stayed folded. "I know that writing," she said.

"Do you?"

"It's Tam's writing." She said the name softly, and Tamsin felt it land somewhere below the ribs—the postman's name, Thomas Penhale, called Tam by everyone in the village because there had already been a Thomas at the school and a Thomas at the mill, and so he had been reduced, or elevated, to three letters that happened also to be Tamsin's own. Their mother had named them for him. Tamsin had learned this only last week, from a letter written forty years ago by a woman in love with a girl who would grow up to forget her.

"Tam Penhale," Edith said. "He walked our road every day rain or shine. He knew which letters were bad news before he knocked. He'd hold them flat against his chest, like—" she made a small gesture against herself, "—like keeping them warm. Or keeping them from biting."

"Did he bring you letters? From—"

"From Edie." The name came out whole, unbroken, and Edith's face changed, the ice shifting, the fish visible suddenly and entirely. "Edie wrote to me. Every week. For years." Her voice thinned. "They never gave them to me. My father took them. Or the company took them. Someone took them, and I waited, and I thought—" She stopped. Her hands unfolded and refolded. "I thought she'd stopped loving me. Forty years I thought that."

"She didn't stop." Tamsin heard their own voice crack on the second word. "Mum. She never stopped. She wrote until—"

But Edith had turned back to the window, and the moment closed over like water over a dropped stone.

* * *

They sat together through the afternoon in the manner of the very old and the very guilty, not speaking much. Tamsin read aloud sometimes, from the letters, translating as they went—the dialect coming up out of them like groundwater, unreliable, rising in patches. Some sentences flowed complete; others they had to render into plain English, and each rendering felt like a small betrayal, a stone replaced with plaster.

Edith listened with her head tipped, and twice she corrected the translation.

"That's not *glimmering*," she said, when Tamsin rendered a description of the reservoir at dusk. "That word means how light lies on water that's going to be taken away from you. There isn't an English word. You have to keep ours."

Tamsin stared at her. "Do you remember the word?"

Edith's mouth shaped itself around sounds, lost them, tried again. The fish under the ice. Then, quietly, with the concentration of someone threading a needle by feel: "*Lúthwen.*"

The word went through Tamsin like cold water through cloth. Ten years in London, ten years of translating other people's ambitions into other people's languages, and they had not known this word either—had not known that their own language contained a word for the specific grief of light on doomed water. They wrote it on their hand in biro, pressing hard, as if ink could hold what memory could not.

Later, sorting through the smaller bundle they'd brought—a handful of letters Edith had kept, returned to sender, unopened, their edges soft as old cloth—Tamsin held one up and asked, casually, aiming for casual and hearing their own voice shake: "Mum, do you remember the surveyor? The man who measured the valley?"

Edith's face did something complicated. "Everyone remembers him."

"What do you remember?"

"He had good boots." She smiled faintly. "Village boots wore through in a season. His lasted. He'd walk the fields with his instruments and everyone watched him, because whatever he wrote down decided everything. Whether we stayed or went. What we were worth." She laughed, a small dry sound with no floor under it. "We trusted the numbers. Numbers seemed clean. Numbers weren't from here."

"And when the readings came—when the order came—what did people think?"

"They thought the water wanted us." Edith said it simply, without mysticism, the way you'd report a fact of local weather. "That's what my grandmother said. The water had always wanted the valley back, and the men with instruments were just the water's hands." She looked at Tamsin then, directly, and for a moment her eyes were not confused at all. "Was that your question? Was that what you drove all this way to almost ask me?"

Tamsin's mouth opened. Closed.

Because here was the question, the real one, the one they had carried up the motorway like ballast: *Grandfather's readings were false, Mum. Your father-in-law falsified the levels, condemned the valley on paper years before the water came, and the letters prove it, and his name is in them, and your name is in them, and mine is, and I don't know what we are now.*

And here was what they said instead:

"I brought you some shortbread. From the shop in Ashcombe. The new one, by the car park."

"The car park," Edith repeated, and something in her voice suggested she understood perfectly well what lay beneath car parks, and chose, in mercy or exhaustion, to let it lie.

* * *

Near four o'clock, Deborah brought tea, and Edith grew tired in the way of the very old—all at once, like a lamp failing. But as Tamsin gathered the letters, she reached out and caught their wrist. Her grip was stronger than it should have been.

"Read me the last one," she said. "Tam's last one. I want to hear it in his voice."

"I never heard his voice."

"You have it in you somewhere. Read it anyway."

So Tamsin opened the final letter—the postman's last, the one with the sentence at its heart that they had failed, for eleven days now, to translate—and read the parts around it. The plain parts. The greeting, the weather, the small news of a village already half-empty, houses with their windows taken out and carried away like eyes. And Edith listened with her eyes shut, and when Tamsin reached the edge of the untranslatable sentence and stopped, she said, without opening her eyes:

"Keep going."

"I can't. Not that part. Not yet."

"Then say it in ours. Don't translate it. Just say it."

And Tamsin, who had spent a decade letting the dialect slip away word by word, who had lost even the heron, who had driven three hours to ask a question and swallowed it with shortbread—Tamsin looked down at the sentence in the dead postman's hand, and could not do it. Could not speak it aloud in any language, because speaking it would make it real, would make the grandfather real in his guilt, would make the mother complicit in her knowing, would make the translator the instrument of a ruin that had already happened and could not be undone.

"Not today," Tamsin said.

Edith opened her eyes. She looked at her child for a long moment, and there was no confusion in her face at all, only a patience so vast it was indistinguishable from sorrow.

"You're just like your gran," she said. "She spent thirty years not saying things. This house is full of them, you know. Unsaid things. They sink." She tapped the arm of her chair, once, twice, like knocking on a door at the bottom of water. "And then one day the level changes, and up they come."

At the door, Tamsin turned. "Mum. One word. Just one. Do you remember the word for heron?"

Edith gazed past them, out the east window, at the dry and blameless hills. Her lips moved. The fish flashed under the ice—and were gone.

"Come again Thursday," she said. "Bring the rest of the letters. Bring all of them."

In the car park, sitting behind the wheel with the engine off, Tamsin looked down at the word inked on their hand—*lúthwen*, light on doomed water—and found that beside it, sometime during the afternoon, they had written another word without noticing, in the same urgent biro, pressed hard enough to emboss the skin:

*Ruth.*

The archivist's number. The woman who had said, over lukewarm tea in the county archive, *these need authenticating, and authentication means other people, and other people mean it stops being yours.* Tamsin had left her three messages and not called back, guarding the letters like a dragon guards nothing, since dragons at least sleep on their gold.

The letters could not stay hidden. Edith had said it without saying it: the level changes, and up they come. And if Tamsin did not choose when and how, then someone else would—someone with lawyers, someone whose family name appeared in the compensation ledgers, someone who had already sent one polite letter asking, in the gentlest possible terms, whether the documents might be *made available for review*.

Tamsin started the engine. On the dashboard, the phone lit once with a message from an unknown number, and the preview read: *Mr. Marchetti — regarding the Halloran correspondence, we should speak at your earliest convenience.*

The water, Tamsin thought, pulling out onto the road, had always wanted the valley back.

Apparently it wanted the letters too.
