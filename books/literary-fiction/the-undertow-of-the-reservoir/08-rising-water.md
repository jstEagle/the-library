## 8. Rising Water

The letter came on a Tuesday, on county council paper, and it was three sentences long. *Following a review of regional archive provision, the Ashcombe Local History Collection will be consolidated with the county repository in Exeter. Your position is accordingly redundant, effective the end of the month.*

Three sentences to end eleven years. Ruth read them standing at her desk among the map drawers and the boxes of parish registers, and she noticed that her first feeling was not anger but a strange professional embarrassment, as though she had been caught doing something improper — as though keeping a drowned village's papers dry were itself a kind of obscenity.

She had worked here long enough to know how these things moved. The review had been announced in November, quietly, buried in an agenda item about efficiency. A consultant had visited twice, a woman with a lanyard and a laptop who asked questions about footfall and cost-per-item and never once opened a box. Ruth had shown her the Ashcombe collection anyway — the tithe maps, the school logbook with its last entry dated the week before the water came up, the photographs of the valley in summer, hayfields running gold to the river. The consultant had looked at the photographs for perhaps four seconds.

"Very atmospheric," she said.

Now Ruth packed the collection herself, because no one else knew which boxes held what, and because she could not bear to let strangers' hands do it. She wrapped each item in acid-free tissue. She labelled everything twice. It was the only prayer she knew.

It was while re-boxing the parish council minutes that she found the note tucked into the 1981 volume — a slip of modern paper, typed, unsigned: *Withdrawn from public access pending legal review. Ref: HW/83.* She recognized the reference code. Halliwell Water. She had seen it embossed on the solicitor's card that Tamsin had shown her, that evening in the archive when they'd laid the letters out on the big table like patients on trolleys, forty years of dialect ink under the fluorescent lights.

Someone had been into the collection before her. Someone had known exactly where to look.

Ruth sat down on her packing stool and held the slip of paper and understood, with the slow cold certainty of water finding a crack, that the funding cut was not an accounting decision. Collections all over the county were being consolidated; that was true; that was the beauty of it. You didn't need to burn anything. You only needed to move it somewhere no one would ever ask for it again, into a repository with a nine-month waiting list and a search room open Tuesdays and Thursdays. The past didn't need suppressing anymore. It only needed shelving.

* * *

Tamsin called her on the Thursday, and Ruth knew before they spoke.

"They've dropped me," Tamsin said. "All of them. Within a week of each other. The heritage trust, the publisher, the firm I've translated annual reports for since — God, eight years. No reasons given. One of them sent a card." A pause. "A card, Ruth. With a picture of a canal boat on it."

"Have you had another visit?"

"No. That's the thing. Silence is cheaper than lawyers." Tamsin's voice had the flatness Ruth had learned to hear as fear wearing professionalism. "I can cover the cottage through the winter if I take anything that comes through. Machine translation post-editing. Instruction manuals. There's a company that wants their sex-toy catalogue localized into fourteen languages."

"Tamsin."

"I know."

"Do you?" Ruth looked out of the archive window at the car park, at the rain stitching silver holes in the puddles. "Because I don't think you do. I think you think this is about money. It isn't. They're not trying to starve you out. They're trying to make you feel unreasonable. There's a difference. Starving takes too long and makes martyrs. Making someone feel unreasonable costs nothing and works almost every time."

The line was quiet for a long moment. Rain on Ruth's window; rain, presumably, on Tamsin's.

"Edith thought my grandfather was a good man," Tamsin said finally. "In the letters. She says it over and over — he was kind to her, he taught her to fish, he wept when the readings changed. And then he signed them anyway. What does that make him? What does that make—" The sentence stopped. Ruth waited. It did not continue.

"Come to the cottage Saturday," Ruth said. "Bring the letters. I want to see them in the house they were written near. And bring the sex-toy catalogue if you must, but I'd rather you brought the postman."

* * *

She arrived mid-afternoon to find the cottage door unlocked and Tamsin not answering, and for one bad moment Ruth stood in the low doorway with the rain coming off her coat and imagined every version of what silence in a house like this might mean. Then she smelled woodsmoke — faint, wrong, a smell like a fire that had been lit and abandoned — and followed it through to the front room.

The hearth was laid. Kindling, split logs, screwed-up newspaper from 2019. And on top of the newspaper, in a neat stack, the letters — Edith's letters, the postman's last letter on top, its envelope soft as cloth from handling. Beside the hearth, a box of long matches, unopened. The seal still intact.

Tamsin sat on the sofa with their back to the door, and Ruth saw at once that they had been sitting there a long time. There was a teacup gone cold and skin-filmed on the sill. The light through the window was the grey-green light of water seen from underneath.

"I got as far as striking nothing," Tamsin said, without turning. "Isn't that pathetic. I couldn't even manage the match."

"It isn't pathetic."

"It's the most honest thing I've done in years." They turned then, and their face was wrecked in the particular way of someone who has fought themselves to a draw. "Do you know what I kept thinking? Not *they'll ruin me*. Not even *it won't change anything*, though God knows I thought that too. I kept thinking about the words. These are the last things anyone wrote in that dialect, Ruth. Maybe ever. My mother can't speak it anymore. Three people alive can hear it. If I translate them properly, the English becomes the original — the dialect dies and leaves behind a ghost of itself in standard prose, tidy and legible and *safe*. And if I burn them—"

They stopped. Pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes.

"—if I burn them, at least they stay what they are. Whole. Untranslated. Mine."

"And theirs," Ruth said.

Tamsin lowered their hands.

"The letters were never yours," Ruth said, and heard her own voice come out steadier than she felt, because she had rehearsed nothing and meant all of it. "That's the whole point of them. Edith wrote them to be read. Forty years in a tin behind a wardrobe — that's not privacy, Tamsin, that's a message that missed its delivery. You're a translator. You of all people should know the difference between a secret and something that hasn't arrived yet."

"You sound like the postman."

"Good. He seems to have been the only one saying anything sensible." Ruth crouched at the hearth and lifted the top letter — the postman's, the one with the sentence Tamsin couldn't render, the one they'd read aloud to her once in the archive and then gone silent, staring at the page as though it had spoken their name. She held it out. "Take it off the fire, then. Properly. Not because I'm asking. Because he is."

Tamsin took the letter. Held it the way you hold something that has been underwater a long time and might still be alive.

"I'm frightened," they said. Simply, the way a child says it. "Not of them. Of getting it wrong. Of standing up in some room and reading his words and having them come out of my mouth wrong — flattened, or sentimental, or worse, *eloquent*. He wasn't eloquent. He was a man who walked eleven miles a day and loved his route. If I make him beautiful I've killed him differently than Halliwell Water did."

Ruth sat down beside them on the sofa. Outside, the rain had thickened against the glass, and the reservoir — invisible from here, but always present, always below the level of thought, the way a dead relative is present — lay over the valley doing what it had done for forty years.

"Then don't make him beautiful," she said. "Read him exactly. That's the whole discipline, isn't it? Fidelity. You told me fidelity was the only vow translators keep." She turned to face them. "So keep it. Publish the letters, Tamsin. All of them, uncut, the dialect and your English side by side, including the sentence you can't translate — print it in the original and leave it untranslated and let readers sit inside their own failure the way you've sat inside yours. Don't publish an accusation. Publish the letters. Let them accuse themselves."

"They'll bury the book. They'll bury me with it."

"Probably," Ruth said. "They're very good at burying. It's practically their core competency — look what they did to a valley." She reached over and touched the envelope in Tamsin's hands, the postman's name inked in his own careful hand. "But here's what they've never understood, and what your grandfather never understood, and what the consultant with the lanyard doesn't understand either. Water doesn't stay where you put it. It finds the crack. It comes up forty years later behind a wardrobe in a tin box, and there is nothing — nothing — that stops it except giving it somewhere to go."

Tamsin looked down at the letter. When they spoke, their voice had changed — not stronger, exactly, but levelled, the way a flooded field levels, everything sunk in it made visible.

"He asks for the valley's name to be spoken aloud," they said. "That's all. In the whole letter, that's all he asks. Not punishment. Not restitution. Just the name, said out loud, by someone, in the language it belongs to."

"Then say it," Ruth said. "Somewhere they can't unsay it after."

Tamsin was quiet a long time. The fire lay unlit in the grate, patient as a threat, and then it was simply kindling again, just wood and old newsprint, nothing more. Tamsin set the postman's letter on the table, squared its edges with the others, and Ruth watched them do it and understood that a decision had been made — not announced, not dramatized, but made, the way water decides, without ceremony, to rise.

"There's a hearing," Tamsin said. "Next month. Exeter. Some procedural thing about whether the letters count as evidence or property — the firm applied for it, wanting a ruling before they'd even talk terms. My solicitor says I don't have to attend. Says I can send a statement."

"Will you send a statement?"

Tamsin picked up the postman's letter and folded it, carefully, along its forty-year creases, and put it in their inside pocket, over the heart, where you'd carry something you intended to give away.

"No," they said. "I think I'll read one instead."
