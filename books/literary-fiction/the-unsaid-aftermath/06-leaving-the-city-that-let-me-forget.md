## 6. Leaving the City That Let Me Forget

The contract was forty pages of indemnity clauses for a shipping conglomerate, and I had translated eleven of them when I understood that I was not going to finish it.

It wasn't a dramatic understanding. Nothing shattered. I simply looked at the phrase *force majeure* on my screen and felt the word dissolve in my mouth like a sugar tablet — sweet, then nothing — and behind it, faint as a watermark held up to light, another word surfaced. The old word for a storm that comes without warning. *Ifele.* My grandmother used to say it while pulling laundry off the line, her hands moving faster than the sky could darken: *ifele na-abịa*, the unannounced thing is coming. I hadn't thought of that word in years. I hadn't permitted myself to think of it. And now it sat in my chest like a swallowed stone, and the English clause beneath my cursor looked like what it had always been: a way of saying *we are not responsible for what the world does to you.*

Five years in this city and I had built myself into an instrument of exactly such clauses. I made other people's evasions legible. I took the language of men who wanted nothing named and gave it polish, gave it flow, gave it a signature-ready sheen. And every evening I went home to an apartment with no photographs and cooked food that required no words and slept without dreaming, or dreamed without remembering, which amounts to the same mercy.

I saved the document. I did not send it.

My hand hovered over the trackpad the way a hand hovers over a stove it has learned to fear. Then I closed the laptop, and the screen's reflection faded from the dark glass, and there I was — or the shape of me, anyway, the shape this city had agreed to recognize.

* * *

Packing took less time than I expected, because there was almost nothing to pack. That is the arithmetic of erasure: subtract a language, subtract a history, subtract the names of the dead properly spoken, and what remains fits in one duffel bag with room left over. Two shirts I would not miss. A toothbrush. The chipped enamel cup I drank from every morning, carried not out of sentiment but because I had never decided against it, and undecided things are the last things to go.

And then Marisol's notebook.

It lived on the top shelf of the closet, wrapped in a cotton cloth I had folded around it five years ago with the particular care of someone burying something they intend to dig up later but cannot admit it. I stood on the chair and brought it down and the dust came off it in a soft gray breath, and I sat on the floor of my apartment — the floor of a home I was about to stop having — and unwrapped it.

The cover was stiff cardboard gone soft at the corners, the color of dried river clay. Marisol had written her name across the front in ballpoint, letters tall and slanting, the *M* crowned with an unnecessary flourish because even her handwriting performed. Inside, the pages were ruled paper crowded past their margins with her small fierce script, verses crossed out and resurrected, chord notations in a shorthand only she could read, and — here my throat closed — little drawings in the gaps between stanzas: a bird, a cooking pot, a door left open.

I had watched her write some of these songs. That was the thing I kept circling, the fact my mind kept returning to like a tongue to a broken tooth. I had sat on the step outside her family's kitchen while the fireflies came up from the grass, and she had hummed fragments at me and demanded opinions, and I had given them, careless opinions, seventeen years old and rich in nothing but certainty. *That verse is too long. This one doesn't rhyme.* She never recorded any of it. She said the songs weren't ripe. She said a song picked too early goes bitter in the mouth of whoever hears it.

Then the night came that burned the valley's memory out of itself, and I testified, and I left, and the songs stayed unripe forever, or so I believed until three nights ago when a stranger's voice on the radio sang one of them back to me, note for note, verse for verse, in a language I had spent half a decade teaching my mouth to forget.

I turned the pages slowly. The handwriting swam. Not from tears — I want to be honest here, and honesty means admitting that the tears didn't come, that grief in me had learned to arrive dressed as something else, as efficiency, as resolve, as the brisk folding of a shirt — but from a kind of vertigo. Because I could feel the words in the notebook wanting to be read, and I could feel my own literacy draining away from me like heat from a stone at dusk. There was a time I could have read every line fluently. Now I caught single words the way you catch familiar faces in a crowd of strangers: *that one, yes, I know that one* — and then the sentence closed over them and they were gone.

One page near the middle stopped my hand. A song I didn't recognize from the radio broadcast, or maybe a draft of it, earlier, rougher. Beneath the verse, in English — Marisol code-switching, which she did when she wanted to be unmistakable — she had written:

*If anything happens to me, the rest is where the water kept its voice.*

I read it four times. The water kept its voice. I had no idea what she meant. I only knew that the sentence was addressed forward, through time, through fire, through five years of my silence, directly to whoever held this notebook — and that whoever held it was always going to be me.

I wrapped the notebook back in its cloth. I put it in the duffel bag, on top, where I could feel its weight against my back.

* * *

Quitting was easier than it should have been, which told me how long I had already been leaving.

I called Priya, my contact at the firm, at an hour I knew she'd still be at her desk. I told her the contract would need reassignment. She asked if everything was all right, and I said yes, and we both knew the word was doing no work at all, just standing there in the sentence like furniture in an empty house. She said she'd need a reason for the file. I said, *family matter,* and heard the old lie come out smooth and practiced — the same lie I'd told customs officers, landlords, colleagues, myself. Family matter. As if the family were a matter, a pending item, a thing with a file number. As if they were not ash and absence and a valley I had fled under a sky the color of a bruise.

But then, walking home from the courier office where I dropped the keys, I passed the corner where the radio shop stands — the same shop whose speakers had poured Marisol's impossible song into the street three nights ago — and the owner was playing music again, some synth-heavy anthem about hustle and skylines, and I felt nothing. That was the strange gift of the last few days: the city had finally stopped pretending to hold me. All those years of careful quiet, of professional neutrality, of words chosen like chess moves — and it turned out the whole architecture rested on one assumption, that there was nothing to go back to. One song on one radio station, and the assumption collapsed like wet plaster.

There was something to go back to. It was singing.

* * *

The night bus left from the terminal on the east side, a cathedral of diesel and sodium light where the departures board flickered like a faulty conscience. I bought my ticket in cash. The clerk didn't look up. Eleven hours to the junction town, then a shared taxi up the valley road, if the road was still a road, if the valley was still a valley, if names still meant what they meant when I left.

I found my seat by the window. The bus filled slowly around me: a woman with a sleeping child folded across her lap like a parcel; two men arguing amiably about football; a young man in a university hoodie who fell asleep before the engine started, his head knocking gently against the glass with each pothole, a rhythm he seemed to have made peace with long ago.

We pulled out at 11:40. The city began to slide past the window — the ring road, the billboards, the towers with their crowns of red aircraft-warning lights blinking in the dark like slow mechanical heartbeats — and I watched it go with the detached tenderness you feel toward a place that never asked your name. Five years. It had given me work and anonymity and a vocabulary of contracts, and it had taken, in exchange, the only inheritance I had. Fair trade, I might once have said. I did not say it now.

Somewhere past the toll gates, the city thinned and the dark thickened, and the bus became a small lit room moving through an ocean of black fields, and I began, quietly, so as not to wake anyone, to rehearse.

That's what I'd been doing since the crash, since the song: rehearsing. Standing at my kitchen counter saying words aloud to an empty room like a man practicing a speech for a trial. Which, in a sense, I was.

I started with easy ones. *Mmiri.* Water. The *m* humming in the nose, the syllables rolling downhill. That one still lived in my mouth; it came out whole. *Anwụ.* Sun — harder, that deep vowel in the middle like a held breath. I said it twice and got it right the second time and felt a ridiculous flare of pride, a child's pride, spelling-bee pride, and hated how good it felt and loved how good it felt.

Then harder ones. The word for testimony. The word for witness. I reached for them and found the shelves empty — not empty exactly, but dusty, cobwebbed, the objects underneath still present but fused together, unliftable. I knew the concepts in English with terrible precision. *Witness:* one who saw. *Testimony:* that which is sworn. But the old words carried more than definition. The old word for witness contained, folded inside it, the idea of standing beside someone — you could not witness, in our language, from a distance; the grammar itself required proximity. I had learned that as a child and forgotten it as an adult, and in forgetting it I had somehow believed I could testify from across an ocean, in a borrowed tongue, at arm's length, and that distance would keep me clean.

The bus hit a long stretch of bad road. The woman's child woke, whimpered, and she began to sing to it — low, half-asleep, a lullaby I knew. Not the melody alone. The words. The actual words, in the actual language, worn smooth by generations of mouths: *sleep, sleep, the moon is watching over you.* My eyes burned. I turned to the window and watched my own ghostly face float over the passing dark, and I moved my lips along with the third verse, arriving late, stumbling, getting maybe half the words, and the half I got tasted like salt and like coming home.

This is what I want to record accurately, because the truth of that night was not courage. People will want to make it courage later — the tribunal, the articles, whatever comes — and I want it on the record that what I felt on that bus was terror wearing resolve's coat. Every mile north, the fear grew. Fear of the valley and what waited in it. Fear of Renner, whose name I had spoken only once in five years, aloud, to no one, like checking whether a wound still hurt. Fear most of all of the language itself — of opening the door I had nailed shut and finding either that the house was empty or that it was full, full of voices, full of the dead asking why I took so long.

Grief does that. It disguises itself as decisiveness. You tell yourself you are choosing, acting, moving — look how fast I packed, look how cleanly I quit — and underneath, all the while, something in you is simply mourning, and mourning is a form of motion too, the body's oldest way of traveling toward what it lost.

Around three in the morning, unable to sleep, I took out the notebook and a penlight and read Marisol's English sentence again. *The rest is where the water kept its voice.* I traced the letters with my fingertip. Her ink had faded to brown, the brown of old blood, of river mud, of things that were once vivid and are now evidence.

Where the water kept its voice. A cistern, maybe. A well. The river gorge below the old meeting house, where the women beat their laundry and the boys dove for coins and the water talked all day in its gravelly dialect — I could hear it now, actually hear it, the specific sound of that specific water, and the hearing frightened me more than anything yet, because it meant the forgetting had never fully taken. It meant the language hadn't died in me. It had only gone underground, patient as seed, waiting for rain.

The child across the aisle had fallen back asleep. Its mother tucked the cloth tighter around it and looked over at me — a brief, tired, appraising look, the look of a woman taking inventory of a stranger — and then she said something to me. Softly. In the old language. Just a pleasantry, I think, something about the road being long, though I couldn't be sure, couldn't parse it fast enough, and by the time I assembled a reply she had turned away, and my unsaid answer sat in my mouth like a coin I'd failed to spend.

She hadn't known who I was. To her I was just a traveler with a city face. But she had heard something in the silence where my reply should have been — some accent of loss, some hesitation the body can't fake — and her glance, before she turned away, held a flicker of recognition. Not of me. Of the condition. She knew what it looked like when someone's first language was dying in them. Maybe she'd seen it before. Maybe she'd been it.

I pressed my forehead to the cool glass. Outside, the land was changing — I could feel it before I could see it, the bus leaning into longer climbs, the air through the window seams turning cooler, greener-smelling, carrying wet stone and woodsmoke and something floral I had no English name for and suddenly, achingly, an old name for, though it took me a full minute of reaching to find it, and when it came it arrived whole, unbidden, like a bird landing on a wire: *urọma.* The scent of the flowering vine that covered the valley walls each spring. My grandmother's favorite. She used to say the valley wore perfume so the ancestors could find their way back.

Dawn was still two hours off when the bus crested the ridge and began, at last, to descend.

I didn't see the valley yet. The dark held it. But I felt it the way you feel a staircase in the black — the change in the body's expectation, the ground tilting toward the known. Somewhere down there was the junction town, and beyond it the road, and beyond the road the place that had been my village and was now, according to the brochures I'd refused to open, a destination. Renner's kingdom. His resort rising where the meeting house stood, his name on signage, his version of events laminated and weatherproofed and posted at every gate.

And somewhere down there too — hidden, waiting, patient as water — the rest of Marisol's song.

I closed the notebook. I let my hand rest on its cover, feeling the soft cardboard, the ghost of her handwriting underneath my palm.

*Hold on,* I whispered — in English first, out of habit, and then, deliberately, clumsily, syllable by syllable like a man crossing stones in a flooded river, in the language I was raised in and nearly lost: *nọrọ. Ana m abịa.*

Wait. I am coming.

The bus descended into the dark, and the dark opened.
