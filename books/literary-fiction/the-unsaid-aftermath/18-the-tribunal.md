## 18. The Tribunal

The hearing room had once been a courthouse, and the building remembered it. The plaster ceilings still carried their plaster rosettes, the wood of the benches still shone with a century of nervous hands, and the light came down through tall windows in long amber columns that made everything — the lawyers' tables, the stenographer's machine, the faces of the three commissioners — look as though it were already being preserved, already becoming evidence.

Gideon sat in the second row with their hands folded, watching dust turn slowly in one of those columns of light, and tried to remember how to breathe like a person who had nothing to hide.

The valley outside was at its most beautiful, which felt like an accusation. Renner's campaign banners hung from the lampposts along the approach road — *A FUTURE BUILT ON WHAT WE LOVE* — and the resort's shuttle buses passed every few minutes, carrying tourists up to the ridge where the village had stood, where now there was an infinity pool and a restaurant called The Overlook that served the valley's own recipes on slate plates, deconstructed, at prices no one from the valley could pay. Gideon had eaten there once, two nights ago, out of some grim anthropological duty. The soup had been Marisol's mother's soup. It had tasted of almost.

Inside, the room filled the way water fills a basin: quietly, then all at once. Farmers Gideon half-remembered, older now, their hands heavy on their knees. Women in church clothes. A cluster of young people near the back — Emeka among them, sitting very straight, a small recorder in his jacket pocket that he kept touching like a talisman, though it held nothing yet. Ruth had come down from the city; she sat near the aisle with her archivist's posture, notebook open, pen uncapped, ready to catch whatever fell. And scattered through the rows, wearing lanyards and pleasant gray suits, the lawyers.

There were so many lawyers. They moved like herons, patient and elegant, standing in the aisles murmuring into phones, and every one of them, Gideon knew, was paid by the same man whose name was on the banners outside.

Renner himself did not appear. He sent instead his presence: a reserved front row left conspicuously empty except for a single aide, a woman with a tablet who watched the room the way a security camera watches — without blinking, without judgment, without ever quite being a person. Gideon recognized the strategy from the contracts they used to translate. Absence, deployed correctly, is more intimidating than presence. The empty chair said: *I am so certain of winning that I do not need to watch.*

The commissioners took their seats at ten past nine. The middle one, a woman named Commissioner Adeyemi with reading glasses on a chain and the unhurried voice of someone who had spent decades listening to men lie politely, opened the proceedings by reading the matter aloud: a petition for restitution of communal lands in the valley known on current maps as Solmar Ridge, formerly known by other names.

She read the old name carefully. She pronounced it correctly. Gideon felt the word go through the room like a stone dropped into still water — the farmers straightening, the old women touching their collars, Emeka's hand going still on his pocket. One word, spoken accurately, and twenty years of erasure trembled.

Then the lawyers began, and the trembling stopped.

* * *

They had prepared for this day the way engineers prepare for floods, and their first move was exactly what Gideon would have written for them if they'd been drafting the scene.

Counsel for the Renner interests — a silver-haired man named Voss whose voice had the warm grain of a radio announcer — rose to make his opening statement, and he did not defend the land deal. That was the brilliance of it. He barely mentioned it. Instead he spoke about progress, about jobs, about the four hundred people employed at the resort, about the scholarship fund, the clinic wing, the road improvements. He spoke about the valley's *complicated history* with a tenderness that made Gideon's skin crawl, because it was the tenderness of a man handling something valuable and dead.

"And we must also," Voss said, turning gently toward the commissioners, "acknowledge the fragility of memory. This petition rests substantially on testimony given decades ago — testimony that was, at the time, thorough, credible, and *uncontested*. We will hear today, I expect, from individuals whose recollections have... evolved." He smiled at the room, a smile full of sympathy. "Memory is not a villain. But neither is it a map. And when a witness tells us one story under oath and another story twenty years later, the tribunal is asked not simply to decide what happened — but whether any of us can know what happened at all."

He let the doubt settle over the benches like weather. Then he sat down, and Gideon understood that the trap was not coming. The trap had been built years ago, brick by brick, on a morning when Gideon had stood in a borrowed language and said words they did not fully understand, and the transcript of that morning had been sleeping in a filing cabinet ever since, waiting, patient as a seed, for exactly this season.

* * *

The petitioners' counsel called witnesses through the morning. An old farmer named Bassey who described, in a voice that cracked only twice, the boundaries his grandfather had walked. A woman who produced a cloth bundle of documents — deeds, tax receipts, a photograph of the meeting house with its roof still on. Each testimony was a small act of resurrection, and each was met by Voss's team with the same gentle scalpel: *And you were present at the original proceedings? No? So your knowledge is secondhand? You say the fire began at the eastern storehouse — the record says the wind shifted that night, does it not?*

By noon the room had learned its lesson: speak, and be sanded down.

Gideon ate nothing at the midday recess. They stood outside on the courthouse steps in air that smelled of cut grass and diesel, watching the shuttle buses climb toward the ridge, and rehearsed. Not the English — the English was fine, the English had never failed them, the English was the problem. They rehearsed the other thing. *Ile. Uzo. Anyị.* Home. Road. We. Words that used to live in their mouth the way water lives in a riverbed, and that now had to be carried up by hand, one at a time, like stones.

Emeka found them there. He didn't say anything at first; he just stood beside them, looking at the same buses, until finally he said, "Ruth got the broadcast license confirmed. Tomorrow, if you testify tomorrow, the station carries it live into the valley. Every market stall. Every taxi." He paused. "Marisol's song too. All seven verses. Ruth says the legal threats don't hold anymore — not once it's part of a public hearing."

"Seven verses," Gideon said. Their mouth stumbled on the number's weight. "I've decoded five."

"I know." Emeka looked at them, and there was no reproach in his face, only a terrible patience. "Verse six is yours. Verse seven is everyone's. That's how she wrote it. I figured that out before I even understood the words — you can hear it in the melody. The last verse is written for more than one voice. There's nowhere to hide in it."

The bell rang inside. The afternoon session was beginning, and Gideon's name was next on the list.

* * *

They swore Gideon in in English, and the oath tasted like the contract work they'd done for five years — precise, binding, hollow.

Voss's junior counsel handled the early questions, establishing the record: yes, Gideon Vasquez, formerly of the valley, currently residing in the coastal city, employed as a translator. Yes, they had testified in the original proceedings. Yes, under oath. Yes, the transcript was accurate — Gideon saw the document itself for the first time in twenty years, photocopied and tabbed, their own younger signature at the bottom like a wound that had healed into a scar. *I have reviewed the statement and confirm it is true.* The sentence sat there in the record, in their own hand, and the whole room could see it, and the whole room would remember it.

Then Voss rose, and the temperature of the room changed.

"Mr. Vasquez." He pronounced the name beautifully, respectfully, a man who respected names the way he respected all tools. "Your testimony twenty years ago was instrumental. I want to be clear that no one disputes your sincerity — then or now." He folded his hands. "In that statement, you told the tribunal that the fire which destroyed the village began accidentally, in the eastern storehouse, during a storm. Is that correct?"

"It is what I said."

"It is what you *said*, yes." A soft ripple of appreciation from the gray suits. "And today you intend to tell this tribunal something different."

Gideon's mouth was dry. The column of amber light had moved; the dust in it turned faster now, or seemed to. "Today I intend to tell you what actually happened."

"*What actually happened.*" Voss repeated it thoughtfully, tasting it. "You understand the difficulty. Twenty years ago, you were young, frightened, recently displaced — and you gave your evidence in a language you had mastered but did not dream in. A language chosen, I'd note, by the previous administration's procedures, not by you." He turned a page with great gentleness. "Now you return, after two decades away, aligned with petitioners who stand to recover land worth a very great deal, and you tell us the first story was wrong. Tell me, Mr. Vasquez — what changed? Your memory? Or your interest?"

The question landed exactly where it was designed to land: not on the facts, but on the space between the two testimonies, the gap where a witness drowns. Gideon heard the old transcript rustling in every bench, heard the commissioners' pens pause, heard Emeka behind them go utterly still.

"What changed," Gideon said slowly, "is that I spent twenty years translating other people's words for money, and I learned something about languages that I didn't know when I was nineteen." Their voice came out steadier than they expected. "Every language has words it protects and words it buries. The language I testified in buried the truth that night. Not because the language is dishonest — because I was led, question by question, to a version of events that fit in it. The truth doesn't fit in that language. It never did. It fits in mine."

"Mm." Voss smiled his radio-announcer smile. "The truth fits in your language. That is a poetic claim, Mr. Vasquez. Tribunals, unfortunately, run on records. And your record—" he touched the tabbed photocopy almost fondly— "says otherwise. Unless, of course, you're telling this tribunal that you committed perjury twenty years ago?"

The trap closed. Gideon felt its teeth — say yes, and destroy your own credibility; say no, and your new testimony collapses into contradiction. The whole room leaned into the silence. Somewhere a ceiling fan ticked. Outside, faintly, a shuttle bus changed gears on the hill.

Commissioner Adeyemi removed her glasses.

"Counselor," she said, in her unhurried voice, "the witness hasn't finished answering your previous question. Let him finish." She put her glasses back on and looked at Gideon over their rims, and her eyes were not unkind. "Take your time. Some answers take twenty years. We have until five o'clock."

Gideon breathed. In the second row, Ruth's pen hovered. In the back, Emeka's hand rested flat against his pocket, against the recorder, against the song, against the tape hidden in a lawyer's safe across town — the master tape, the physical truth, waiting for its moment the way a verse waits for its voice.

And Gideon opened their mouth to answer — knowing that whatever came out next, in whichever language, would either bury them or begin to dig.
