## 22. The Song, Sung Together

The meeting house smelled of lime and cut timber.

They had stripped the gift shop down to its bones in the months after the tribunal — the racks of miniature Renner Valley keychains carted off, the laminated menus of the café that had occupied the east wing, the tasteful signage that had renamed our place of gathering "The Heritage Pavilion." Beneath it all the old walls stood waiting, patient as grandparents, and when the plaster came away and the archive wall emerged — Marisol's wall, the one I had found by candlelight while a security guard's flashlight swept the windows — the villagers did not seal it behind glass. They left it open to the air, the way a mouth is open for singing.

I arrived before dusk on the evening of the first full gathering, carrying nothing but Marisol's notebook and a folding chair I would never unfold. The notebook had become something between a relic and a working document; its spine was soft as cloth now from handling, its pages annotated in three hands — Marisol's sure slant, Emeka's careful block letters, my own uncertain script filling the gaps where I had guessed at words I should have known since before I could walk. We had finished the transcription in the spring. Every verse accounted for. Every name named.

The valley in late light did its old trick, the one I had forgotten and then relearned and now could not unlearn: the ridgeline went copper, then violet, then a blue so deep it seemed less like color than like memory itself, holding everything without showing anything. The terraces below the meeting house were overgrown still — restitution moved at the speed of paperwork, which is to say glacially, which is to say that Aldous Renner's lawyers were paid by the hour to make sure of it — but someone had cleared a path up from the road, and along the path someone had planted marigolds. Marigolds had not grown here in eleven years. I stopped and looked at them for longer than a stranger would have, and a woman passing with a basket of yams touched my elbow without stopping and said, "You're early," in our language, and the sentence went through me like warm water through cold stone.

*You're early.* Two words. A year ago I could not have parsed them. Now they simply arrived, meaning what they meant, and I answered, "The dead are earlier still," which was something my grandmother used to say about anyone who arrived before food was ready, and the woman laughed — laughed! — and kept walking, and I stood among the marigolds feeling the strange vertigo of having been, without noticing the exact moment, absorbed back into the grammar of a place.

Not fluent. Let me be honest about that, because honesty is the only offering I have ever had worth giving. I am not fluent. My verbs still stumble. There are whole registers of the language — the language of mourning, the language of negotiation, the elaborate courtesies of disagreement — that live in me now only as outlines, shapes I can trace but not fill. When I dream, I dream half in the borrowed tongue of the city, and sometimes mid-dream I feel myself translating, and wake tired as if from labor. Five years of erasure does not reverse in eighteen months of return. Some words I lost will not come home. I have made my peace with the losses the way you make peace with a scar: not by pretending the skin is whole, but by refusing to call the scar the whole of the skin.

But abandoned — no. That word no longer applies to me, and tonight it would not apply to any of us.

* * *

By full dark the meeting house was full.

Full in the old way, which meant overflowing, which meant children on laps and elders on benches carried in from three houses over and young men standing at the doors because there was nowhere else to stand and no one minded standing. I counted faces and failed — there were more than two hundred, maybe many more — survivors of the fire and their children and their children's children, and people from the neighboring valleys who had heard the broadcast and come out of solidarity or curiosity or the simple human appetite for being present where history happens. Ruth had driven up from the coastal city with the station's portable equipment and a look of exhausted triumph she wore like a shawl. Pastor Obi sat near the front. He had asked to sit near the front. No one had objected, though I saw more than one person watch him settle onto the bench with an expression I recognized from my own mirror: the arithmetic of forgiveness, still being calculated, columns not yet summed.

He had testified voluntarily in the winter, after the song went public, after the tape's audio — Marisol's voice, steady and damning, describing the men with the kerosene and the order signed in advance — had gone out over the community frequency and then, through Ruth's contacts, into the wider current of the national news. He had stood in a courtroom and said, in his own voice, unprompted: *I coached a frightened child to say what a powerful man needed said, and I have lived inside that sentence for eleven years.* He had wept. I had watched him weep and felt, to my own surprise, not satisfaction but a kind of kinship so unwelcome I had to leave the room. We had both survived by surrendering the truth. He had simply surrendered it faster, and been paid better, and hated himself more efficiently. In the months since, he had become the meeting house's most tireless restorer — hauling timber, mixing lime, learning again to speak to the village he had served as its betrayer. Whether that was penance or just another form of hiding, I did not ask him. Some questions you let a man answer with his hands.

Emeka appeared beside me as the benches filled, carrying a microphone cable coiled over his shoulder like a fisherman's net. He was taller than when I'd met him — they grow when you aren't watching, the young — and he had taken to wearing a small brass pin shaped like a cassette tape, which Ruth had given him and which he treated with the gravity of a medal.

"You're doing the last verse," he said. Not a question.

"I'm going to try."

"You're going to do it." He had Emeka's certainty, the certainty of someone who had learned the language from a tape at sixteen, syllable by syllable, alone in a storage room, and who therefore believed — rightly, infuriatingly — that no obstacle to speech was real. "You sang it in your room last night. I heard you through the wall at the guesthouse."

"That was practice."

"That was almost it." He grinned. "Tonight's the difference."

Ruth tested the microphone, and a feedback whine ran through the crowd like a bird startled upward, and then the room hushed in that particular way our gatherings had always hushed — not silence imposed but silence offered, a bowl held out to be filled.

Marisol's mother sat in the place of honor. She was small and straight-backed and she had insisted, against all the gentle objections of her family, on lighting the lamp herself, the way she had done at every gathering before the fire, before the resort, before the renaming. She struck the match. The flame caught. And in the trembling circle of that light I saw reflected — literally, in her eyes — the whole reason any of us were here: a woman who had lost a daughter and gained instead a song that refused to die, passed hand to hand like water in a drought, until it reached the sea of all of us.

She nodded once. Ruth leaned to the microphone. And the song began.

* * *

It began the way it always began now, with the first verse, the verse of naming — *the valley of our mothers, the valley of our names* — and the voices rose from the benches in a wave that seemed to come from under the floorboards, from the earth itself, as if the ground had been waiting eleven years to be sung to and remembered all the words. The children knew it best. That was the thing that undid me every time: the children, who had never known the village intact, who had been born into the renamed streets and the tourist brochures, sang without hesitation, without translation, their voices bright and unbroken above the older ones. Emeka had taught them in the school he'd started in the restored building, three afternoons a week, using the tape and the notebook and his own ferocious patience. They were the first generation in a decade to grow up with the language as a living thing rather than a wound.

Verse by verse the song walked us through it. The second verse, the coming of the surveyors with their instruments and their smiles. The third — the fire verse, the true verse, the one that had cost Marisol her life's quiet and given us back our history — and here the singing did not soften but grew harder, denser, the way grief does when it stops being private. People did not weep so much as burn quietly, each face lit from within. Obi sang this verse. I watched his mouth form the words about the men and the kerosene and watched him not flinch, and understood that for him this too was testimony, repeated weekly, a debt paid in installments of breath.

The fourth verse named the witnesses. It named me.

I had heard it sung a dozen times since the tribunal, and each time my own name arrived in Marisol's melody I felt the same double sensation: the sting of being seen, and the strange mercy of being seen and not cast out. *The one who spoke in a borrowed tongue, who did not know what the words were doing.* Marisol had written it before she died, and she had written it not as accusation but as description — she had understood, somehow, from whatever distance she watched me from, that my betrayal had been a failure of language before it was a failure of loyalty. The village had adopted her understanding. They sang the verse and looked at me, some of them, and their looking held no hatred. Only the expectation that I would keep standing there, keep returning, keep being the person the verse described and also the person who had come back to answer it.

Then the fifth verse ended, and the pause came — the long, deliberate silence Marisol had built into the song, four beats of nothing, the space she had left open all those years ago in the notebook, and which she had marked with a single instruction in the margin, in our language, in handwriting I had spent a season learning to read:

*Here the last voice enters. Whoever it is.*

For five years the space had been empty. For eighteen months it had been filled by rotation — Obi once, Ruth once, Emeka most often, various elders — because the song was communal now and belonged to everyone. But tonight the villagers had decided, in the way villages decide things, by consensus so quiet and total it arrives already accomplished, that the final verse would come home to me. Not because I deserved it. Because the story required it. Because a song about a silenced voice ends badly if the voice stays silent, and everyone in that room understood narrative better than any novelist I have ever read.

The four beats of silence opened like a well.

I stood. My knees reported their age. The lamplight found me and I let it. And I opened my mouth, and here is what I can tell you about that moment, the moment toward which this whole account has been moving whether I willed it or not: I was afraid, and I sang anyway, and the fear changed nothing about the sound.

*The words we buried are not dead,* I sang. *They wait beneath the plaster. They rise.*

My voice cracked on *plaster* — the consonants of our language sit differently in the mouth than the city's soft consonants, and mine had grown lazy, and the word came out slightly wrong, and a child in the third row giggled, kindly, the way children giggle at anything honestly imperfect, and I sang on. *They rise* came out clean. The next line about the marigolds came out clean. The line about the tribunal — Marisol had somehow written a verse about a tribunal years before any tribunal existed, which remains to me the deepest mystery of her gift — came out mostly clean, one verb stumbling, recovered. And I felt what I had not felt since childhood, not once in the city, not once in the borrowed years: the language rising through me rather than being retrieved by me. Not translation. Not performance. Just speech, the ordinary miracle of it, the thing every human does ten thousand times a day without wonder — except that for me it had been taken away and returned, and so I wondered at it, standing in the lamplight, singing the last verse of a dead woman's song with two hundred living voices rising under mine like groundwater finding its level.

The final line was hers, and it was simple, and it was the hardest thing I have ever said in any language: *We are still here.*

I sang it. Without hesitation. For the first time.

And then the song was over, and the silence after it was not empty but full, the way a jar is full, and into that silence Marisol's mother rose from her seat, crossed the floor with her small unhurried steps, and took both my hands in hers. Her palms were dry and strong. She looked up at me with eyes that held the fire and the flood and everything after, and she said, in our language, slowly, so that even my limping ear could not miss a syllable:

"Welcome home, translator. Now you may begin to listen."

The room laughed and wept and rose together, and outside the marigolds held their orange in the dark, and somewhere down the valley a dog barked at the moon as dogs have always done here, in a landscape that had heard everything and forgotten nothing.

* * *

**Emeka**

Later, much later, when the benches were stacked and the elders gone home and the lamp turned low, I stayed behind to coil the cables, because someone has to, and because I like the meeting house best when it's empty — when you can hear the new timber settling and the old walls underneath it remembering.

Gideon hadn't left. They were standing at the archive wall, running a finger along the plaster edge where the old stones showed through, reading the names carved there by people dead before either of us was born. I didn't disturb them. I just plugged in the last cable and listened to the building breathe.

After a while Gideon spoke, without turning around, in our language — haltingly, the way they always do, checking each word like a man crossing ice.

"The children," they said. "Next term. Will you teach them the songs I don't know?"

"There are a lot of songs you don't know," I said, grinning, because it was true and because teasing Gideon had become one of my duties, self-appointed.

"I know." They turned, and in the lamplight their face was tired and open and younger than it had any right to be. "That's why I'm asking. Teach me the ones I don't know. All of them. I have time now."

I picked up the spare microphone, out of habit, out of mischief, and held it out across the empty room like an offering.

"Then start now," I said. "The night is long, and the tape has more on it than you've heard."

Gideon took the microphone. Outside, far down the valley, the first birds were already stirring — the ones that sing before dawn, the ones our grandmothers said carry messages — and neither of us knew yet what the morning would bring, only that for the first time in eleven years, whatever it brought would hear us speaking in our own tongues.
