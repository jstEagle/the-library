## 9. Bram Arrives Uninvited

Bram Fenwick had never in his life done anything uninvited. He was a man of invitations — invited to hold the ladder, invited to mind the desk while Elodie took her break, invited, once, to her mother's funeral, where he had stood at the back holding two umbrellas because he'd checked the forecast twice and Elodie had checked it zero times and cried anyway when the rain came.

So when he found himself on the 6:40 coach to Vellamouth, ticket bought in cash from a machine that had judged him silently with its little blue eye, he felt less like a man on a journey than like a man committing a small, forgivable crime against his own character.

He had told himself it was about the books.

That was the story he'd constructed on the walk home from the library three nights ago, after Elodie had left mid-shift without her coat, leaving the confession shelf unlocked and a note that said only *Gone to Vellamouth. Back soon.* No period after *soon*, which in Elodie's handwriting was practically a scream. She punctuated everything. She alphabetized her spice rack. A woman like that did not abandon punctuation lightly.

The books were the pretext: someone had to fetch the returns she'd left in her locker, someone had to keep the night log current, someone — this was the part he didn't say out loud even to himself — someone had to make sure she was eating. Because Elodie Thackeray, when she went home to whatever Vellamouth had done to her as a child, did not eat. He knew this the way you know things about people you have loved quietly for six years: not through confession but through observation, through the granola bar slipped into her bag on bad anniversaries, through the way she once stood in the staff kitchen staring at a kettle as if it had asked her an impossible question.

Mrs. Okafor's kitchen fire. That was what had decided him. He'd read it in the Vellamouth Courier's thin online edition while eating toast — *RESIDENTIAL FIRE ON CORDWAINER LANE, ONE HOSPITALIZED* — and then he'd looked up Cordwainer Lane on a map and found it four streets from the Thackeray house, which he knew because Elodie had drawn it for him once, drunk on half a glass of sherry, on the back of a receipt: *here's the house, here's the river, here's where Tomas—* and she'd stopped writing and folded the receipt into a very small square and put it in her pocket and never mentioned it again. He still had the receipt. He kept it in his copy of *The Shipping News*, which was either sentimental or unhinged depending on who you asked, and nobody had ever asked.

The coach smelled of wet wool and somebody's pasty. Bram watched the country go industrial and then go green and then go muddy, and rehearsed opening lines.

*Elodie, I've come because I love you* — no. Too much. She would go pale and cite the staff fraternization policy, which did not exist, but which she would invent on the spot because it was useful.

*Elodie, I've come because you left your coat* — too small. She'd know it was a lie. The coat was the lie he'd told himself, actually; the coat was hanging on the hook by the door where it always hung, and he had brought it anyway, folded over his arm like a flag of surrender.

*Elodie, I've come because Mrs. Okafor's house burned down four days after you started asking questions, and I don't think that's a coincidence, and I'm frightened for you.*

Yes. That one. That was true, and truth was the only currency she respected.

He just hoped he could get through it without his voice doing the thing it did.

* * *

Vellamouth announced itself the way old towns do: badly. A sign with letters missing (*VELLAMO TH*), a shuttered arcade, a church spire leaning like a man listening hard. The river ran through the middle of everything, wide and brown and patient, and Bram understood immediately that this town had been built around the water the way a sentence is built around a word everyone is avoiding saying.

He found the Thackeray house by the receipt, which was absurd, and by asking a woman at a bakery who gave him directions with the particular flatness of someone who has given directions to that house before, to other people, for years, and has opinions about it she is keeping behind the counter.

The house sat at the end of a lane with its windows boarded and its garden gone to seed, and it should have looked dead, but it didn't. It looked like something holding its breath.

The front door was open.

"Elodie?" His voice did the thing. He cleared his throat and tried again, lower. "It's Bram."

No answer. He stepped inside onto a floor that groaned like a ship, into a hall that smelled of dust and cold plaster and, underneath it, faintly, impossibly, of pencil shavings — the smell of her father's study, maybe, or just the smell of a house full of old paper, which in this family amounted to the same thing.

He found her in what had been the parlor, kneeling on the floorboards in a pool of afternoon light, surrounded by wallpaper.

She had stripped an entire wall of it. Strips of faded roses hung off the edge of the table like shed skins, and the plaster beneath showed a rectangle of paler paint where something had been hidden, and Elodie was sitting in front of it with letters spread around her knees in careful rows — dozens of them, yellowed, tied in bundles with string — reading by the light of her phone propped against a boot.

She looked up at him and Bram's prepared speech died somewhere in his chest, because the woman looking back at him was and was not Elodie Thackeray.

This Elodie had shadows under her eyes like bruises going violet. This Elodie's hair was pinned up with what appeared to be a pencil, which was a detail so perfectly her that it almost made him laugh, except nothing else about her was funny. This Elodie looked — there was no gentler word — *fierce*. Fierce the way weather is fierce. Fierce the way a librarian is fierce when you dog-ear a first edition.

"You're supposed to be at the desk," she said.

"It's Tuesday," said Bram. "Marguerite covers Tuesdays. You know Marguerite covers Tuesdays. You trained Marguerite."

"I left a note."

"The note said *back soon* with no period. I ran a search of your entire correspondence history and you have never once omitted a period. I concluded you had been abducted." He held up the coat. "I brought your coat. As a cover story. For myself."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth — not quite a smile, more the memory of one — and then it was gone, and she went back to the letters as if he were a delivery she'd signed for.

Bram stood in the doorway of the ruined parlor and took inventory, because inventory was how he loved people. One thermos, empty, lying on its side. Two apples, both with exactly one bite taken. A notebook open to columns in her small upright hand — dates, names, amounts, cross-references — that looked less like grief and more like prosecution. And the letters. Bundles and bundles of letters, all addressed in the same careful hand he recognized from the framed specimen above the returns desk at Halloway: *E. Thackeray, Senior Librarian (Ret.),* written in ink gone brown.

Her father's hand.

"Elodie," he said carefully, "what are these?"

"Correspondence." She turned a page. "Hidden in the wall. Behind the wallpaper. In my parents' bedroom, which is a choice, isn't it? He hid the proof of his innocence ten feet from where my mother slept, and neither of them ever—" She stopped. Breathed. Started again with terrible calm. "They're dated between March and September of the year everything happened. They're between my father and a man named Aldous Hale. Corven Hale's father. They discuss discrepancies in the harbor trust accounts. My father found them. He wrote to Hale — politely, Bram, he wrote *politely*, he underlined words like *irregularity* and *mutual discretion* — giving him every chance to fix it quietly. And then the theft was discovered, and it was my father's name on the ledger, and Hale senior testified, and Corven Hale presided over the hearing at twenty-nine years old because the regular magistrate was 'unavailable,' and my father confessed to a crime the arithmetic says he couldn't have committed, because the money he supposedly stole was already gone before the dates in his own handwriting say he found out about it."

She said all of this without stopping, in one breath, like a diver, and when she surfaced her hands were shaking and she pressed them flat against the floorboards as if steadying a boat.

Bram crouched down across from her. Up close he could see she'd been crying recently and had dealt with it administratively, wiped and filed, no follow-up required.

"How long have you been here?" he asked.

"I don't know. What day is it?"

"That's my question being reflected back at me, which means the answer is bad."

"Three days," she admitted. "Maybe four. There's a tap in the scullery that still works. I've been sleeping in my old room. The bed is very small and very honest about what time it is." She looked at him then, really looked, and her voice dropped. "You shouldn't have come, Bram."

"I know."

"This isn't library business. This isn't a returned book with a sad note in it. This is—" she gestured at the wall, the letters, the whole rotting beautiful house — "this is a loaded thing. People here got hurt. People here are *still* getting hurt. Mrs. Okafor's kitchen caught fire on Thursday, Bram. She's in the county hospital with smoke inhalation, and the fire report says electrical, and her wiring was replaced in 2019, I checked, I called the electrician, I called him *at dinner*, and he remembers the job because she paid him in jam."

"I know," Bram said again. "That's why I came. Not the jam. The fire."

"And what exactly did you plan to do about it?"

"I planned to stand next to you and be the boring one. Somebody in this operation has to be the boring one, Elodie. You've gone full archive-fire in three days. You haven't slept. You're living on single bites of apples. You've got a pencil in your hair and a prosecution in your lap and you're talking about a dead man's handwriting like he's going to answer you. I came because I love—" 

He stopped.

The word sat in the dusty air between them, enormous, having arrived without a ticket, uninvited, like him.

Elodie went very still. Outside, a crow said something rude about the situation.

"You love," she repeated.

"...the collection," Bram finished, with the courage of a man diving behind furniture. "The collection. And its custodian. Platonically. In a professional capacity."

"Bram."

"There are policies."

"There are no policies. You invented the policies. You invented the policies so we would never have to have this conversation, and now you've taken a coach to a dying town to have it in a haunted parlor, so honestly, the policies can wait their turn."

He laughed — helplessly, horribly, the laugh escaping like a bird from a cage someone forgot to latch — and she almost laughed too, he saw it fight its way up and lose, and then her face set into the fierce thing again and she picked up the topmost letter.

"Don't," she said.

"Don't what?"

"Don't be brave on my behalf. It doesn't suit you and I can't afford it. If you care about me — in whatever capacity, professional, platonic, *archival* — then the caring thing to do is go back to Halloway, log the returns, water the fern, and let me finish this."

"Finish what, exactly? Finish reading? And then what? You take them to the police? To the papers? To Corven Hale's auction and read them out loud like some kind of—" He heard himself, heard where the sentence was going, and hated it. "Elodie. Tell me you're not planning to read them aloud. At the auction. In front of the whole town."

The silence answered him. It was a well-kept silence, alphabetical, cross-referenced, twenty years in the making.

"He's selling our house," she said quietly. "He's selling it to erase the last thing in this town that remembers what he did. And Imogen Pryce says the letters prove nothing legally — statute's run, record's sealed, courts won't touch it. So the law has finished with us, Bram. The law had its turn. Twenty years ago the law had its turn and it chose him." She looked down at her father's brown ink. "All that's left is the reading. Reading aloud is the one thing I know how to do. It's the entire job. Someone leaves the truth in a book, and you find it, and you say it out loud so it exists in the air where people can't pretend otherwise. That's all this is. It's the job. Just — bigger shelving."

"That's not the job. That's a bonfire with you standing in it."

"Then hand me the matches," she said, "and stand back."

And Bram Fenwick, invited nowhere, welcome everywhere he wasn't, understood that he had come three hundred miles to stop her and was going to fail, had probably known since the bakery, possibly since the receipt, possibly since the umbrella at the funeral — and that the only real decision left to him was the one he'd been avoiding for six years.

He sat down on the dusty floorboards beside her, coat and all, and held out his hand.

"Give me the bundle marked March," he said. "If we're burning down, we're proofreading first. Your father underlined *mutual discretion*; somebody in this family has to underline the important parts properly."

Elodie stared at him. Then, slowly, she put the bundle in his hands — and her fingers stayed on his a moment longer than filing required, and neither of them pretended it hadn't.

Outside, far off toward the river, a car door closed, and footsteps started up the lane — unhurried, polished, certain of their welcome — and Elodie's head came up like a deer's.

"That'll be him," she whispered. "He knows I'm here."
