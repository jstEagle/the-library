## 2. A Name Nobody Uses

The house had a smell Isandro had forgotten she knew: cold dust and lemon polish gone rancid, the ghost of forty years of Sundays. She stood in the front hall with her coat still on, keys in her fist, and let the silence arrange itself around her the way it always had—patient, upholstered, absolute.

She had rehearsed this homecoming on the drive up from the city. Not the grief; that needed no rehearsal. She had rehearsed the *management* of it. The way she would set down her bag without hurrying. The way she would not turn on every light at once, as a frightened person might, but only the hall lamp, then the kitchen, methodical, a woman taking possession of a property rather than fleeing into one. Her father had taught her that composure the way other fathers taught cycling—one hand behind her, ready to catch, never actually catching. *A Delacroix does not flinch,* he used to say, though even that sentence had felt, even in childhood, like a translation of something said originally in another language.

She hung her coat. She opened the curtains in the parlor, and the gray light of Marrow Bay came through the glass the color of dishwater, laying itself across furniture shrouded in white sheets like a room full of patient ghosts.

Her mother had died six years ago. Her father had outlasted her by exactly the span everyone predicted and no one mentioned—the widower who declined, politely, publicly, until the end arrived in a hospital room in the city with his tie still knotted. The funeral had been three days ago. Isandro had performed it flawlessly. She knew this because three separate people told her so, gripping her hands at the reception with the particular gratitude of people relieved to watch someone else do suffering correctly.

And there had been the woman in the gray coat.

Isandro thought of her now, standing in the parlor of the shuttered house, because thinking of her was easier than opening the first box. The woman had stood at the edge of the churchyard through all of it—not the service itself, which she'd watched from beyond the lych-gate as if unwilling to bring whatever weather she carried across the threshold—and afterward she had followed the procession of mourners to the reception hall and stood by the coffee urn for two hours, drinking nothing, watching Isandro with an attention so steady it had weight, like a hand resting on the back of her neck.

Not pity. Pity she could have handled; pity was currency she knew how to make change for. This had been something else. Recognition, maybe. Or appraisal. The look of someone checking a face against a photograph kept a long time.

When Isandro had finally crossed the room to her—because her father's training did not permit letting a stranger stand unattended at the margins—the woman had said only, "I'm sorry for your loss," in a voice flat as slate, and then, before Isandro could perform her gracious nothing: "You have her hands."

"Whose?"

The woman had looked at her for a long moment, and whatever lived behind her eyes had been careful, banked, a fire kept small on purpose. "Never mind," she said. "It wasn't my place." And she had left, coat gray as the sea, before Isandro's composure could decide what to do with the exchange.

*You have her hands.* Whose hands did Isandro have? Her mother's, obviously. Everyone said so. Everyone had always said so, in that tone people used when they were confirming something already agreed upon, and Isandro had spent thirty-four years agreeing along with them.

Now she stood in the parlor and looked down at her own hands, pale against the dark wool of her sleeves, and they were just hands. They trembled slightly, which annoyed her.

She went to the kitchen and made tea she didn't drink, because ritual is a kind of scaffolding, and began, at last, the work she had come to do: the cataloging. Her father's study first. That was the discipline of it—if she gave herself an order of operations, the house could not ambush her. Study. Then the bedrooms. Then the attic, God help her. Boxes labeled in her own handwriting from the last visit, when she'd begun this project prematurely and fled after four days. *Father — correspondence, 1990s. Mother — kitchen, misc. Books — keep/donate.*

The study smelled of pipe tobacco that hadn't been smoked in a decade, preserved in the wood like a fossil. His desk was clear except for a blotter, a lamp, and a single drawer that stuck. She knelt to work it loose, and it came free all at once, spilling her backward onto the rug with a letter opener in her lap and a stack of envelopes beneath it, bound in twine, addressed in her mother's hand to an address Isandro did not recognize.

She was still sitting on the floor, twine half-unwound, when the doorbell rang.

*

No one rang the doorbell in Marrow Bay. They knocked, or they let themselves in if they were family, or they didn't come at all, because the town had decided decades ago what it thought of the Delacroix house and its silent, courteous inhabitants, and its opinion had calcified into simple avoidance. Isandro stood in the hallway looking at the bell's brass button as if it were an artifact from a drowned civilization.

Through the frosted glass: a figure. Small, still, waiting with the patience of someone who has waited a great deal already.

She opened the door.

The woman in the gray coat stood on the step with the wind pulling at her hem, and up close, in daylight, Isandro saw she was perhaps her own age, maybe younger, with a face that might once have been pretty before something had planed it down to angles and intent. Her hair was dark, cut short and practical. Her eyes were the brown of wet bark, and they moved over Isandro's face with that same terrible attention, cataloging, comparing.

"You shouldn't leave the gate open," the woman said. "Not here. People will wander in."

"People don't wander in Marrow Bay."

"They do when there's a story in it." She held out a hand. "Wren Corliss. We met, sort of, at the funeral. I'm sorry I ran off. I'd been working up to it for years and then I lost my nerve, which is humiliating, so I've come back to fix it."

Isandro did not take the hand immediately. Something in her—the trained thing, the daughter-thing, the part that read rooms before it read words—registered that this stranger had rehearsed too. That underneath the briskness was a pulse visible at her throat, quick as a bird's.

"Fix what?" Isandro asked.

"An introduction." Wren Corliss smiled, and it was not a warm smile; it was the smile of someone stepping off a ledge. "I've come about your family. About who they were. Before."

"Before what?"

"Before they were Delacroix."

The word landed between them on the doorstep, in the wind, in the smell of salt and cold stone, and Isandro heard herself say, with perfect courtesy, "I think you have the wrong house."

"I don't." Wren's voice stayed level, but her hands had found each other and gripped. "Your father's name wasn't Delacroix. It wasn't the name on his headstone, or your birth certificate, or any of the paper trail you're going to go check tonight—I know you will, I would. Before all of that, your family had another name. A different name entirely." She drew a breath, and Isandro watched her take it like a diver taking air. "Halloway."

*

Later, Isandro would try to reconstruct the sensation precisely, and fail, and try again, and fail better. The nearest she ever came was this: it was the feeling of a floor that turns out to be painted canvas stretched over a drop. Not pain. Not even fear, exactly. The specific vertigo of discovering that the solid thing you have stood on all your life was scenery—that the ground itself was part of the performance.

They ended up in the kitchen, because kitchens are where strangers get dealt with; you put a kettle on and the situation becomes domestic, containable. Isandro sat at her mother's table with her hands wrapped around a mug she had no memory of pouring, and Wren Corliss sat opposite her, coat still on, refusing tea with a small shake of the head as though accepting hospitality would compromise some necessary hardness.

"Halloway," Isandro said. She made herself say it again, tasting it. "Halloway. That's not—it's not a name from our family. There's no Halloway anywhere. I've done genealogy, actually, after Mum died. Parish records, census rolls. Delacroix goes back generations in this county. There's a Delacroix on the war memorial."

"There is," Wren agreed. "That's rather the point. It's a good name. Solid. Local. Nobody questions it." She leaned forward. "Ask yourself one question, Ms. Underhill—Delacroix, sorry. Ask yourself why your parents never spoke about their families. Not stories, not grievances, not anything. Most people have at least a difficult uncle. What did your father say when you asked about his childhood?"

And there it was—the trap laid gently in the middle of the table, because Isandro had asked, of course she had asked, every child asks, and she turned the answers over now in her memory and found them smooth, polished, empty as river stones. *We weren't close. My parents died young. It was a sad time, we don't dwell.* Her mother had been even more economical: a tightening around the mouth, a change of subject executed with such grace Isandro had mistaken it for grief rather than evasion. Forty years of questions answered with stones.

"He said they died young," Isandro said slowly. "Both of them. He said there was an accident."

"Was there a date? A place? Did you ever see a grave?"

"No graves. He said—" and here Isandro's voice did something she hated, thinned, went young— "he said they were scattered. Cremated, scattered, no marker. He said he didn't like markers."

Wren nodded, and there was no triumph in her face, which was somehow worse than triumph would have been. She looked almost gentle. "My grandmother liked markers," she said. "She believed you owed the dead a stone with their name on it, spelled right. She's got one. In the churchyard past the headland—St. Brendan's, it's called. You'll want to see it, eventually. Her name is on it, and my grandfather's, and the dates. And under the dates, the name they were born with and the name they died with, because my grandmother paid extra for that. She said names mattered." Wren's throat worked. "The name they died with was Halloway."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Somewhere outside, a gull said something ugly and wheeled away.

"That proves nothing," Isandro said. "People share surnames. Half this county is related to the other half."

"True." Wren reached into her coat—an inward flinch passed over Isandro at the movement, absurd, as if the woman might draw a weapon—and produced not a weapon but a photograph, old, soft-cornered, its colors gone the amber of weak tea. She laid it on the table between them, face up, and turned it with one finger so it faced Isandro.

Two families stood side by side in front of a stone cottage. On the left, a man and a woman and a boy of perhaps ten, squinting into sun. On the right, another man, another woman, a girl of seven or eight holding a tin bucket. Behind them, half in frame, a sign nailed to a gatepost, the letters just legible: *HALLOWAY.*

"My grandparents," Wren said, touching the right-hand family. "Edmund and Maren Halloway. The girl is my mother, Ruthie. She's alive. She doesn't know I came here today, and I'd thank you not to tell her, because she has spent fifty years building a wall around that summer and I'm not the one who gets to knock it down." Her finger moved left. "And this is your father. And your aunt Vivian. And your grandparents—or the people who were standing in for your grandparents, which is a distinction I'll come back to, if you let me."

Isandro looked at the boy in the photograph. Ten years old, cowlicked, gap-toothed, one shoulder hitched higher than the other the way boys' shoulders are when they haven't grown into themselves. She had photographs of her father at every age, albums full of them, and none of them showed a boy like this—but then, she realized with a slow cold turning in her chest, she had no photographs of him younger than sixteen. None. The albums began abruptly, mid-adolescence, as if he had been issued to the world fully formed, secondhand, with his history removed.

She had noticed that once, years ago, and her mother had said the early photos were lost in a move, and Isandro had accepted it, because accepting things was the family art, practiced daily, perfected over generations.

"The boy in this picture," Isandro said. Her voice sounded like it was coming up a long stairwell from somewhere below the house. "What was his name?"

Wren met her eyes.

"Thomas Halloway," she said. "Born 1949. And in August of 1983, according to every record that exists, Thomas Halloway died. Along with his parents, in a house fire on the coast road. Three bodies were recovered. Three funerals. Three stones at St. Brendan's, spelled right, because my grandmother's sister insisted." She paused. "Except the constable who signed the report was an old man even then, and old men near retirement sign what they're told to sign. And the bodies were burned past knowing. And somewhere in the weeks after, a young couple arrived in Marrow Bay with a ten-year-old boy and a new surname and a story about being from upcountry, and nobody in this town ever asked them a hard question again, because your grandfather—" her voice caught, steadied— "because Edmund Halloway had money, and money bought the quiet."

The kitchen seemed to tilt. Isandro gripped the table's edge, and the wood was real, grain and cold, but everything above it swam. Her father's hands teaching her to hold a pen. Her father's laugh, rare, surprising, like a door opening briefly onto a lit room before closing. Her father saying *we don't dwell,* and meaning *do not dig.* Her whole childhood reassembling itself behind her eyes with the furniture rearranged—every silence suddenly load-bearing, every non-answer suddenly an answer.

"That's insane," she said. She heard how it sounded. "That's—you've built a life on this. You've clearly spent years—" 

"Twenty-two," Wren said quietly. "Since I was twelve, and my mother finally told me the shape of it, drunk, one Christmas, and then denied it forever after. Twenty-two years, Ms. Delacroix. I'm not asking you to believe me. I'm telling you I didn't come here to hurt you, whatever it looks like. I came because my mother is seventy-six and starting to lose her words, and when she goes, the last person who remembers those people as people goes with her. And I couldn't let that happen. Somebody has to say the names out loud while somebody's left who can hear them."

Isandro looked down at the photograph. The girl with the tin bucket was laughing at something outside the frame. Her father—her father, the word itself now unstable, a chair with a loose leg—stood at the edge of the group, half-turned toward the camera, and Isandro searched his small blurred face for anything she recognized and found it, horribly, in the set of the shoulders. The hitch. The same asymmetry she had inherited, the one she'd caught in shop windows her whole life and privately disliked.

*You have her hands,* the woman had almost said at the funeral. Not her mother's hands. Someone else's entirely.

"Why tell me now?" Isandro asked. "He's dead. They're both dead. Whatever happened, whoever they were—what do you want from me?"

Wren gathered her coat around herself and stood, and for a moment she looked very tired, and very young, and very much alone in a stranger's kitchen in a town that had never wanted her family.

"I want the truth written down somewhere that isn't a gravestone," she said. "And I think you want it too. I saw your face just now. That wasn't horror, Ms. Delacroix. That was relief wearing horror's coat." She moved toward the hall, then stopped, and said, not turning around: "There's a room upstairs. Second floor, end of the hall. Locked. Your mother kept it locked your whole life, didn't she. Told you it was damp. Told you it was nothing."

Ice walked down Isandro's spine. "How could you possibly know that?"

"Because Ruthie Halloway grew up in this house," Wren said, "before it was yours. And she remembers which door her father nailed shut, and why." She pulled the front door open onto the wind and the gray water. "Check the parish records tonight. Check the stones at St. Brendan's. Then come find me—I'm at the Anchor Inn, under Corliss. I'm not hard to find. I've spent twenty-two years making sure of that."

The door closed behind her. The bell above it gave one thin, dying note.

Isandro stood in the hall for a long time. Then she climbed the stairs, one flight, then another, down the narrow corridor to the door at the end—the door she had touched a thousand times as a child and been told, gently, firmly, *that room is nothing, that room is damp, leave it be*—and laid her palm flat against the wood.

It was cool. It was silent. It was locked.

From her pocket she took the photograph Wren had left on the table after all—she discovered it in her hand without remembering picking it up—and looked again at the boy with the crooked shoulders, her father's shoulders, standing in front of a sign that said HALLOWAY.

Tomorrow, she told herself, she would go to St. Brendan's. Tomorrow she would pull the parish records. Tonight she would sit in her father's study among his careful, curated, invented life, and she would begin—with trembling hands, with the ground still tilting beneath her—to make a list of everything her family had never said.

But first she pressed her forehead against the locked door, and whispered, to whoever had nailed it shut, to whoever waited behind it:

"What are you?"
