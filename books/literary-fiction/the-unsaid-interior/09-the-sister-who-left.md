## 9. The Sister Who Left

The envelope had no return address, but Isandro knew the handwriting before she slit it open. Vivian's script was the family script—Underhill script, though Isandro had begun to doubt even that—slanted and severe, the letters leaning away from each other as if they'd been caught mid-argument. *I will come Tuesday. Do not telephone. Do not prepare. — V.*

No condolence. No mention of the funeral Isandro had watched her aunt refuse to attend from the back of the church, standing in the vestibule like a woman checking the weather before committing to a storm. Isandro had seen her there—had felt the gray shape of her at the edge of her vision the way you feel a draft—and had said nothing, because saying something would have required deciding what they were to each other now, and neither of them had touched that question in nineteen years.

Tuesday came gray and salt-smelling, the kind of Marrow Bay day when the fog sat on the water like a lid on a pot. Isandro had spent the morning in the parlor pretending to catalog—box fourteen, *miscellaneous correspondence, 1971–1974*, its contents unread because her hands had stopped cooperating somewhere around the third envelope—and when the knock came, it was three measured raps, the knock of a woman who had decided in advance exactly how much of herself she would spend on this visit.

Vivian Underhill was seventy-one and looked sixty, the way people do who have spent decades being angry at a very specific thing. Her hair was white now but still cut short and practical, and she wore a coat the color of wet slate, buttoned to the throat, though the house was warm. She did not remove it. She stood in the front hall—the same front hall where Isandro had last seen her, nineteen years ago, a suitcase in her hand and a sentence on her lips that she had never finished—and she looked at the house the way a surgeon looks at a tumor.

"You've opened the curtains," Vivian said.

"Someone had to."

"That was never the arrangement." Her aunt's eyes moved across the room with an inventory of their own, and Isandro understood, suddenly, that Vivian had been cataloging this house her whole life too—cataloging it for exits, for evidence, for the things that must never be found. "Your mother kept them drawn for a reason."

"The reason being?"

Vivian set her purse on the hall table with a click that sounded like a period. "Isandro. I did not come here to spar. I came because your father is dead and that changes the arithmetic."

"What arithmetic?"

"The arithmetic of who is left to keep things." She said it flatly, without drama, the way she might have announced a change in ferry schedules, and that flatness frightened Isandro more than any trembling could have. "I am the last one who was there. You are the last one who doesn't know. And there are people—there will be people—who will come to this house now that the door of it has lost its keeper, and everything you do between now and when they arrive will decide what happens to you."

Isandro felt the vertigo again, the one Wren Corliss had installed in her a week ago with a single word. *Halloway.* She had spent the intervening days telling herself the stranger was a fraud, a grief-tourist, a crank. She had spent them less successfully telling herself that the ledger's payments to *M.C.* were medical bills, that the burned property outside town was a coincidence of geography, that her father's will's strange clause—the room, the room, the room to remain as it is—was sentiment.

"Sit down, Aunt Vivian."

"I'll stand."

"Then I'll stand too." Isandro crossed her arms, an unconscious echo of her aunt's posture, and hated herself for it. "You sent me a letter that says you're the last one who was *there*. Where is there?"

Vivian's jaw worked. Nineteen years had not softened her; it had only taught her economy. "I watched you at the funeral," she said instead. "You were very good. Your mother would have been proud."

"That's not an answer."

"No. It isn't." And then, for the first time, something moved behind the severity—not softness, Isandro thought, but a kind of exhausted respect, the look of a chess player acknowledging an opponent who has finally stopped moving the wrong pieces. "You have your mother's stubbornness. God help you. She used it to keep things shut. You seem to want to use it to—" She stopped herself.

"To what?"

"To dig." Vivian said the word like a diagnosis. "There is a difference, Isandro, between a secret and a wound. A secret protects people. A wound needs air. Your family has spent forty years treating a wound like a secret, and it has poisoned every one of us, and now you want to open it in a house that is not only yours." Her voice rose slightly on the last words and then was mastered, pressed flat, filed away. "I came to tell you to sell. Take the money, take the apartment in the city, take your grief somewhere clean, and let this house go to strangers who will never know what happened in it. That is the mercy. That is the *only* mercy left."

"The house isn't for sale."

"It will be. When you understand."

"Then make me understand."

For a long moment the only sound was the fog horn out on the point, low and patient, the sound Marrow Bay made when it wanted to say it had swallowed bigger conversations than this one. Vivian looked at her niece—looked at her the way Isandro imagined she had once looked at her brother, at the boy who became the man who became the father who had maybe never been an Underhill at all—and something in her face came apart by a single degree.

"You were nine," Vivian said quietly. "When I left. Do you remember what I said to you at the door?"

"No."

"No. Of course not. Children remember what they're told to remember." She unbuttoned the top button of her coat, then, abruptly, buttoned it again, as if even that small exposure was more than she'd budgeted. "I said, *Some houses you leave, and some houses leave you, and you won't know which this one is until it's too late.* You looked at me and you asked if I'd come back for your birthday. And I said yes. And I didn't. And every year after that, when your birthday came, I sent nothing, because sending something would have meant explaining, and explaining was the one thing your mother had made sure I could never do."

"Made sure *how*?"

Vivian laughed—a short, terrible sound with no humor in it, the laugh of a woman hearing her own captivity described back to her. "You think the silence was a shared agreement. A family value. You think we all stood around a table and signed it." She shook her head slowly. "Silence like this doesn't happen. It's *maintained*. There are people whose whole job, whose whole life, becomes the keeping of it. Your mother was one. I was almost one. The difference between me and your mother is that I ran, and she stayed, and I have never in forty years been certain which of us was the coward."

* * *

They ended up in the kitchen, because Vivian refused the parlor—*your mother's room*, she'd said, with a flinch she disguised as a glance—and because kitchens are where families say the things parlors exist to prevent. Isandro made tea neither of them drank. The fog pressed against the window. On the table between them sat the ledger, and Isandro watched her aunt's eyes find it, and fix on it, and go still.

"You found it," Vivian said.

"You know what it is."

"I know what it cost."

"Then tell me. M.C. Who is M.C.?"

Vivian wrapped both hands around her cup as though it were something that might be taken from her. "You have a stranger in this town asking questions," she said. "The Corliss girl. I've heard. The ferryman's wife told the butcher, who told everyone, because that is what Marrow Bay does instead of having a newspaper."

"She says our name was Halloway."

"And what did you say?"

"I said she was wrong."

"And do you believe that?"

Isandro looked down at the ledger, at her own father's handwriting, at the monthly sums paid across forty years to initials that meant nothing and therefore, now, meant everything. "I believe," she said slowly, "that you came here today to stop me from finding out. And that you took a ferry and a bus and wore your funeral coat to do it. And that a woman doesn't spend that kind of effort on a lie."

The silence that followed had texture. It had weight and grain, like the fog itself. Vivian set down her cup with the same click as before, and Isandro realized the sound was not punctuation but a warning—the sound her aunt made when she was about to spend something she could not get back.

"I am going to say one thing to you," Vivian said. "One. And then I am going to leave, and you are going to let me leave, and you are not going to write to me or telephone me or come to my door, because I am seventy-one years old and I have spent my whole life paying for a night I did not choose, and I will not spend what's left of it explaining it to a child who wasn't born." Her hands, Isandro noticed for the first time, were trembling—not with age, or not only with age. "Your father was not who you mourned last week. That is the beginning and the middle of it. What he was instead—what he *did*, and what your mother did to keep it buried, and what I did, God forgive me, what I *did*—"

She stopped. Her throat worked. Outside, the fog horn sounded again, and somewhere down the lane a dog began to bark at nothing, at everything, at the smell of old fires that Marrow Bay fog always carried if you knew how to smell it.

"Vivian." Isandro's voice came out smaller than she wanted. "Please."

Her aunt stood. She buttoned her coat to the throat. She picked up her purse, and at the kitchen door she paused with her hand on the frame, and she did not turn all the way around—only enough that Isandro could see her profile, sharp as a blade against the dim hall, a woman carved out of refusals.

"You want the door your mother kept locked," Vivian said. "Everyone always wants the door. But the door is only wood, Isandro. The room behind it is only a room. What you should ask about—what you should have asked about at the funeral, before the Corliss girl ever opened her mouth, what you should have asked your father every day of your life while he was alive to lie to you—is not what's behind the door."

"Then what?"

Vivian's profile did not move. Her voice, when it came, was so quiet that the fog seemed to carry it from a great distance, from 1983, from a burning house at the edge of town, from a night Isandro had never known she was born on the far side of.

"Ask about the other child."

* * *

The door closed. Isandro heard the gate, the lane, the fog taking her aunt the way the bay took everything eventually—without malice, without effort, completely.

She stood in the kitchen for a long time. The tea went cold in its cups. The ledger lay open on the table, and she looked at it without seeing it, because two words were repeating in her head with the slow, grinding patience of a tide.

*Other child.*

She thought: my father had no siblings. She thought it the way you recite a catechism, and heard, beneath it, the catechism's hollowness. She had never actually known that; she had only ever been *told* it, told by the two people with the greatest possible investment in her never checking. Her grandparents—her *grandparents*, whichever grandparents those had been—were photographs in a hallway, faces without stories, and she had assumed the stories were simply lost, the way families lose things. Not withheld. Not *priced*.

She went to the hallway. The photographs were still there—her mother had never taken them down, had only, Isandro saw now for the first time, hung them high, above the sightline of a child, above the sightline perhaps of a visitor's casual glance. Her father at six on a beach she'd never been able to place. Her father at twelve, squinting, arm around a smaller figure.

A smaller figure.

Isandro took the frame off the wall. Her hands were shaking. Behind it, on the backing paper, in her mother's handwriting—her mother, who had labeled every box in this house, who had labeled her entire life—was a single line, small and precise and dated:

*E. and T., Halloway Beach, 1961.*

Not Underhill. *Halloway.* And the smaller figure—T.—was not her father. Her father was E. The arm around the smaller child belonged to a boy she had never seen, never heard named, never once been told existed, a boy who had been edited out of a family the way you edit a sentence that gives too much away.

She stood in the hallway holding her father's childhood in her hands, and somewhere upstairs the locked room waited at the end of its corridor like a held breath, and outside the fog was lifting off the water, and Isandro understood that she had just been handed not an answer but a countdown. Her aunt had given her one sentence and fled. The Corliss girl was somewhere in Marrow Bay with the other half of the story. And the smaller figure in the photograph—the other child, the one with no grave she knew of and no name she'd ever heard—was beginning, in the ledger's cold columns and the will's strange clause and her mother's forty years of drawn curtains, to look less like a secret and more like a debt.

She carried the photograph to the table and set it down beside the ledger, the two documents facing each other across the cold tea like strangers at an interrogation, and she reached for her coat.
