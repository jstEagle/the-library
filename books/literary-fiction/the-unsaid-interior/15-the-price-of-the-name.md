## 15. The Price of the Name

Wren had learned early that the truth was a currency you spent carefully, and that she had never been rich in anything else.

She stood at the window of the guest room—the room Isandro had given her without being asked, as if hospitality were a reflex this house still performed even after its people were gone—and watched the light come up gray over Marrow Bay. The town looked different from this side of the glass than it had in her mother's stories. In those stories it was always burning.

Her phone lay face-down on the quilt like something dead she couldn't bring herself to bury. She knew who had called. She knew what he wanted. She had known for eleven days, which was exactly how long she'd been sleeping under this roof, eating this family's bread, letting Isandro Underhill—letting *Isandro Halloway*, though the woman didn't know her own name yet—believe that grief had brought a stranger to her door out of some pure and blameless hunger for the past.

The past, yes. But not only the past.

Wren pressed her forehead against the cool pane and let herself say it, silently, in the privacy of her own skull, where no one could be wounded by the shape of it: *I came for the house.*

There. It existed now, even if only inside her. Everything else would follow from that, the way smoke follows fire.

* * *

She had been nine years old the first time she understood that her family's name was a wound other people had made and her family had been left to bleed in.

It was a Sunday. Her grandmother—her mother's mother, Alma Corliss, though *Corliss* was itself a name acquired late, a coat put on over another coat—had taken her to a church two towns over because the church in their own town asked questions. Wren remembered the questions more than the sermons: *And where did you say your family was from, before? Before the fire, before the move, before all that?* Her grandmother answered the way she answered everything, with a flatness that dared you to dig. But in the car afterward, gripping the wheel with both hands, she had said, almost to herself, "They took everything, little bird. They just left us the dying."

Wren hadn't known who *they* was. She had spent twenty years finding out.

The archive work was slow and unglamorous and it ate her twenties whole. County records. Fire marshal reports from 1983, pages water-damaged and half-legible, describing a blaze at a property on the Marrow Bay road that had killed two occupants—a man named Edmund Halloway and his wife, Ruth—while a third name appeared nowhere at all. Insurance filings. A probate file that opened and shut like a mouth. And threaded through all of it, faint as a watermark: the Underhills. A family that had arrived in Marrow Bay the same autumn the Halloways burned. A family with no history before that autumn, no cousins, no graves, no footprints in any soil but this one.

Her mother, Dana, had carried the story in fragments, the way burn victims carry skin grafts—pieces that didn't quite match the flesh around them. *My parents trusted someone,* she used to say, when she said anything at all, usually late, usually after wine. *My father worked with him. They were going to start something together, some venture, and then there was money missing and accusations, and my father said he'd ruin him, and then—* She would stop there. She always stopped there. The sentence had no ending because the ending had been stolen along with the name.

*Halloway.* Wren had whispered it to herself so many times as a girl that it stopped sounding like a word and became a place instead, a country she was heir to and could never enter. And then she had grown up and done the research and learned that countries like that don't exist anymore, that inheritance is a legal fiction, that thirty years of adverse possession and quiet title and a family that paid its taxes under a new name will erase a theft more thoroughly than any fire.

Almost thoroughly.

Because the house still stood. That was the thing. The house on the point, the one her grandfather's money had built and her grandparents had died in—or near; the reports disagreed, and the disagreement was its own kind of confession—the house had not burned. Only the outbuildings, the old boathouse wing, the records office where her father kept the papers that would have proved everything. The main house survived, and a family moved into it, and raised a daughter in it, and locked one door for forty years.

Wren had told herself, on the ferry over, that she wanted the truth. She had told Isandro a version of it that was true the way a photograph is true: accurate, cropped, lit from the best angle. *I grew up on stories of Marrow Bay. Your family borrowed our name.* All of that was real. What she had not said—what she had held back with the precision of someone who knows exactly how much weight a bridge can carry—was that truth was not the asset she'd come to collect.

The house was.

* * *

He called again at noon.

She let it go to voicemail and listened to the message with the phone flat on the table between her palms, like a Ouija board delivering bad news.

"Miss Corliss. Halloran." The voice was smooth as poured concrete. "You've had time to review the appraisal materials I sent. I'll be candid, because I respect candor: the Underhill estate sits on land my clients have wanted for a decade, and the current owners are—forgive me—sentimental, grieving, and disorganized. That combination doesn't last. When the family fractures, and families always fracture, I'd rather be standing next to you than across from you. You have a claim. Not a legal one—I want to be honest about that too—but a moral one, and moral claims make excellent leverage. Call me."

Wren deleted the message. Then she sat very still in the kitchen of the stolen house, in the stolen afternoon light, and felt the full architecture of what she'd become settle around her shoulders like a coat cut for someone else.

Leverage. He'd said it so easily. And the worst part—the part that made her stomach turn over slowly, like something drowning—was that he wasn't wrong about her. She *had* a claim. Her grandparents had died within sight of these windows. Her mother had grown up poor and nameless and flinching at church questions while the people responsible slept soundly down the lane. If anyone had a moral claim on this ground, on these rooms, on the very boards under her feet—

But a claim is not the same as a right, and wanting restitution is not the same as wanting justice, and somewhere in the last eleven days the lines had blurred until she could no longer tell which side of them she was standing on.

Because of Isandro.

That was the complication she had not planned for. She had expected the daughter of Edmund Halloway's betrayer to be either ignorant—easiest—or defensive, or coldly protective of the family myth. She had built strategies for all three. She had not prepared for a woman who cataloged her own grief in labeled boxes, who flinched at the word *Halloway* like a person touching a live wire and then, instead of pulling away, reached back to hold it. Who had walked the burned property with her in the wind and laughed—one short, startled, unguarded laugh—when they found the tin box, as if laughter were a language she was relearning after decades of silence.

Who had said, three nights ago, standing in the kitchen with dishwater on her hands: "Whatever my parents did, whatever this house is sitting on top of—I want to know it. All of it. Even if it costs me the version of them I loved."

Wren had stood there holding a towel and thought: *she means it.* And thought, one heartbeat later: *and I am going to use that.*

* * *

Vivian called at four.

Wren took the call in the front hall, standing beneath the staircase where the acoustics funneled every voice downward, and heard the aunt's voice come through thin and hard as wire: "You're still there. I told Isandro that girl should never have been given a bed in this house."

"Mrs. Underhill—"

"Don't Mrs. Underhill me. I know why you're here. You think I don't? You think forty years of keeping this family's secrets taught me nothing about the people who come sniffing at the edges of them?" A pause, and when Vivian spoke again her voice had dropped, gone almost gentle, which was somehow worse. "You want the house. Fine. Say it. Say it plainly, at least, since nobody in this family ever has."

"I want what my family lost," Wren said.

"What they lost." A laugh like a door slamming. "Child, everyone in this story lost something. The question is what you're willing to take from someone else to get yours back." And then, before hanging up: "Isandro trusts you. Remember, when it comes apart, that she trusted you. That will be the part that costs."

Wren lowered the phone. The hall clock ticked. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard settled, the old house breathing in its sleep.

*Say it plainly.*

So she would. Not to Vivian. To the one person whose opinion of her had quietly become the load-bearing wall of whatever Wren was building here. She found Isandro's note on the kitchen counter—*Gone to the county office, back by six, there's soup*—and the domesticity of it, the terrible ordinary kindness of *there's soup*, nearly undid her.

She wrote her answer on the back of the note, because some things could not be said aloud the first time, and left it propped against the salt cellar:

*I need to tell you something tonight. All of it. Why I really came. Don't read this until I'm there to say the rest.*

Then she went out to walk the point, because the truth needed an ocean in front of it, and waited for the light to fail enough to match what she was about to do.

* * *

She was halfway back up the drive at dusk when she saw the lights.

A car she didn't recognize idled at the gate, black and long, and a man stood beside it talking on a phone, his silhouette neat as a paper cutout against the last of the sky. Even at this distance something about his posture—the unhurried certainty of it, the way he looked at the house the way men look at things they intend to own—made her pulse jump.

Halloran. Here. Days ahead of any appointment, pressing his advantage, the way men like him always did when they smelled a fracture in the wood.

Wren stepped off the gravel onto the grass to go around, to think, to decide whether warning Isandro about him would count as honesty or as strategy—and that was when she saw the second figure, further up the path, half-hidden by the hedge that ran along the kitchen garden.

Isandro. Standing motionless in the blue dark, a shawl pulled over her shoulders, close enough that she must have heard—not the phone call, Wren was almost sure, not the words—but the shape of it. Her posture. Or perhaps nothing at all except Wren herself, frozen on the lawn like a trespasser caught in the act of becoming one.

"Wren," Isandro said. Her voice was level. That was the frightening part. Grief she knew how to read; this was something quieter and colder, a door closing without a sound. "Your note said tonight. But I think—" She looked past Wren, briefly, at the black car at the gate, and when her eyes came back they had changed. "I think maybe you already started without me."

The tide was going out below the point, drawing the water off the rocks, exposing everything the day had covered. Wren stood on the wet grass with her confession still folded in her pocket, unsaid, and watched the alliance they had built—two weeks of archives and ash fields and one tin box—tilt beneath her feet like a deck taking water.

Behind Isandro, at the gate, the man ended his call and began, unhurried, to walk up the drive.
