## 22. What We Say Now

The house smelled different now.

Isandro noticed it the way you notice a scar fading—not all at once, but in the ordinary light of an October morning, one year and eleven days after she had first come back to Marrow Bay with a rented car and a grief she didn't know how to carry. Then, the house had smelled of dust and sealed rooms, of forty years of held breath. Now it smelled of coffee and wood polish and, faintly, of the sea that came through windows no longer shuttered. Wren had insisted on opening every window in the house on her first night here, even in November, even with the wind off the bay cutting through the rooms like a blade. *A house has to breathe,* she'd said, wrapped in two of Isandro's mother's old blankets, her teeth chattering, her eyes bright. *Or it starts keeping secrets.*

They were cataloging again. That was the joke between them now—that Isandro Underhill had returned to this house to catalog what was never said, and had instead found a life's work. The ledgers sat open on the dining table, but these were new ones, bought at the stationery shop in town by a woman who remembered Isandro's father and still called him Mr. Underhill, because some names outlived their truths and that was not always a lie. In these ledgers they wrote things down. Everything. That was the rule, the only rule the memorial trust had, the one Isandro had drafted herself and refused to soften: *Nothing in this house is decided without being recorded, and nothing is recorded without being read aloud.*

It made for slow meetings. It made for honest ones.

"Item forty-one," Wren said, holding up a teacup with both hands as though it might go off. "Bone china, hairline crack, pattern of blue wisteria. Found in the locked room."

"Provenance?"

"My grandmother's, according to the photograph from 1979—the one where she's holding it on the porch of Halloway House." Wren turned the cup so the morning light caught the crack. "She's smiling in that picture. I keep forgetting she smiled."

Isandro took the cup and wrote in the ledger in her careful novelist's hand: *Teacup. Blue wisteria. Belonged to Eleanor Halloway. Cracked, like everything else we kept.* She hesitated over the last clause, then left it. The trust had voted—both members present, both in favor—that the catalog would not be neutral. A museum could be neutral. This was not a museum. This was a reckoning with a roof.

"Where do we put her things?" Wren asked. It was the question underneath every question now, the one they circled weekly. The east room—the locked room, though its lock had been replaced with a simple brass latch that anyone could turn—had become something between an archive and a shrine. Her grandparents' photographs hung there beside the confession Isandro had written, framed now, its pages yellowing slightly at the edges the way paper does when it is finally allowed to exist in air. Visitors came on Thursdays. Not many. Mrs. Okafor from the bakery. A history teacher from the high school who brought students twice a semester and stood very quietly while Wren told them about the fire. Once, an elderly man who turned out to have been Aldous Meek's constable partner, who stood in front of the confession for a long time and then said only, "He carried it longer than he should have," and left before they could ask which *he* he meant.

"The east room," Isandro said. "With the rest. We don't separate them anymore. That was the whole disease—separating things. The living from the dead, the truth from the family, the price from the purchase." She set the cup down. "One room. Everything together."

Wren looked at her across the table, and Isandro saw the thing she still wasn't used to seeing: someone looking at her without wanting anything performed. At the funeral, a year ago, she had been seen by everyone and known by no one. She had worn her grief like a tailored coat and every mourner had admired the fit. Wren had watched her that day from across the churchyard in a gray coat, and what Wren had seen, apparently, was the seam. The place where the performance joined the person. She'd told Isandro once, in the second month, when they were still more adversaries than anything else: "You grieved like a woman giving a eulogy at her own funeral. I couldn't decide if I hated you or pitied you." And Isandro had said, "Both, I hope," and Wren had laughed—a short, surprised sound—and that had been, though neither of them named it then, the beginning.

* * *

The developer's signs were gone from the edge of the property. They had come down in the spring, after the emergency sale collapsed—after Isandro stood in the east room with the confession in her hands and read it aloud while Vivian's lawyer checked his watch and Halloran, the man with the development plans, smiled his patient, expensive smile, and after the story went, as stories do, into the county paper and then the regional one and then briefly, absurdly, onto a podcast about inherited secrets that Isandro refused to listen to. The land was deeded now to the Halloway-Underhill Memorial Trust, its charter three pages long, its assets one burned foundation, four acres of ash soil gone soft with broom and gorse, one house with breathing windows, and two women who had no blood between them and every other tie there was.

Vivian had walked out that day and had not come back. But she wrote. Once a quarter, a card, unsigned except by the initial *V*, each one containing a single sentence. The winter one had said: *You have your mother's stubbornness, God help you.* The spring one: *The gardenia by the back steps will bloom if you'd stop letting strangers trample it.* The summer one: *I am not angry.* The autumn one, which had arrived Tuesday and lay now on the hall table where Isandro had not yet moved it: *I listened to the podcast.*

"That's practically a hug," Wren said, when Isandro showed her. "From Vivian, that's a full reconciliation."

"She'll never come through the door."

"No," Wren agreed, turning her cup slowly. "But she's listening through the wall. Some people can only love from the next room. You taught me that—you and your whole beautiful, terrible family." She said it without cruelty. That was the miracle Wren had performed, the one Isandro still marveled at: she had taken a grief that should have curdled into hatred and kept it, instead, in a state of rigorous, unsentimental clarity. She did not forgive Isandro's father. She had said so at the Thursday talks, plainly: *Thomas Underhill—Daniel Halloway—chose escape over warning, and my grandparents died in the difference between those two words. I will not forgive that. Forgiveness isn't owed just because the guilty man is dead.* But she had also said, in the same breath, the same voice: *And I will not pretend he was only that choice. He paid M.C.—Meek—for silence, yes. He also sent money, anonymously, to my mother for eleven years. Both things are true. We write down both things. That's the whole point of the room.*

* * *

In the afternoon they walked out to the burn site, as they did most days when the weather allowed. The path was worn now, a real path, trodden by their feet and by the feet of visitors. The foundation stones of Halloway House rose from the broom like the bones of something that had finally been allowed to rest. Someone—it had been Wren, the first spring, and neither of them discussed it—had planted daffodil bulbs among the stones, and in March the ruin bloomed so violently yellow that a photographer from the county paper had come out and asked if he could take pictures, and Wren had said yes, provided the caption read exactly what she gave him: *Halloway House, burned 1983. Eleanor and Marcus Halloway died here. We are done pretending otherwise.*

Today the gorse was seeding, the pods popping in the warm afternoon with small dry reports like distant applause. Wren crouched at the edge of the foundation, where the child's tin box had come out of the ash soil a year ago—the box that held the photographs of two families side by side, barbecues and birthdays, Eleanor Halloway and Marion Underhill arm in arm in a way that made the later betrayal almost incomprehensible, except that Isandro had learned that betrayal and love are rarely opposites; they are often neighbors, sharing a fence, borrowing sugar.

"We should dig again," Wren said. "Systematically. There could be more."

"There could be nothing."

"There could be nothing," Wren agreed cheerfully. "That's the fun of archaeology. You get to be wrong professionally." She had brought a hand trowel, because of course she had; Wren Corliss traveled everywhere with a hand trowel now, the way other people carried umbrellas. She worked the dark soil gently, and Isandro knelt beside her, and for a while neither spoke, and the silence between them was the good kind—the kind that had taken a year to build, silence not as a wall but as a shared room.

Isandro thought about her mother, sometimes, in this exact posture. Kneeling in this soil, or standing before the locked door, blank page after blank page. Forty years of choosing silence daily, each morning renewing the vow the way other people renew faith. Isandro had hated her for it, then understood her, then settled somewhere in the territory between—understanding as a country you visit, hatred as the home address. What her mother had never grasped, what the blank pages proved, was that silence doesn't preserve anything. It only postpones. Everything unsaid waits, and waits, and accrues interest, and comes due eventually with the whole estate attached. Isandro had simply chosen to pay the debt in her own generation rather than pass it forward, compounding, to whoever came next.

Because someone would come next. That was the other thing the year had taught her. The trust had a third member now—voted in unanimously at the June meeting, minutes recorded and read aloud per the rule. Priya Nair, the history teacher, thirty-four, no connection to either family, who had shown up on a Thursday in April out of curiosity and kept showing up until Wren said, "You know you're allowed to just be a visitor," and Priya said, "I don't want to be a visitor," and that was that. Priya was transcribing the Meek interviews. Priya had found, in the county archive, the original deed to Halloway House, signed by a name none of them had encountered before—a Halloway cousin who had emigrated in the fifties, which meant there might be living descendants, which meant the story had branches none of them had mapped.

"You're doing your thinking face," Wren said, not looking up from her trowel.

"I'm always doing my thinking face."

"Yes, but this time you're doing it at me, which means you've had an idea and you're afraid I'll say no."

Isandro laughed. "Priya found the cousin's name. Arthur Halloway. Emigrated to Toronto. He'd be dead now, but—" She turned her phone toward Wren, the genealogy forum thread glowing in the afternoon light. "There's a granddaughter. Active account. Posted last month about family history, asking if anyone knew anything about the Marrow Bay Halloways."

Wren sat back on her heels. Soil on her knees, sun in her eyes, and for a moment she was silent in the old way, the guarded way, and Isandro watched her choose—watched her take the vertigo and hold it instead of flinching. "Another one," Wren said finally. "There's another one out there who grew up on fragments."

"There's always another one. That's what I finally understand. Silence doesn't end with us unless we end it, and even then—" Isandro gestured at the foundation stones, the daffodil bulbs sleeping underground until March, the ledger waiting on the dining table with its careful entries. "Even then, the truth keeps traveling. It finds people. It found you. It found Priya. Apparently it's finding a woman in Toronto."

"Write to her?" Wren asked.

"Read it aloud first. Rule's a rule." Isandro pulled the small notebook from her coat pocket—the pocket notebook, the one she carried everywhere now, the successor to all the unsent letters—and read: *"Proposed action: contact A. Halloway's granddaughter, share the trust's records, invite correspondence or visitation. Recorded by Isandro Underhill, October fourteenth. Seconded by—"*

"Seconded," Wren said. "Obviously seconded." She reached over and tapped the notebook. "Add a line. Tell her we have her great-aunt's teacup. The cracked one. Tell her some things break and we keep them anyway."

* * *

They walked back as the light went long and gold across the bay. At the gate Wren stopped, the way she always stopped, and touched the post—oak, older than the fire, older than the lies, carved with an H that might have meant Halloway or might have meant nothing, and which they had decided, by recorded vote, to leave unexplained. Some mysteries were allowed to stay mysteries. Only the ones that hurt people required solving.

Inside, the house breathed around them. Windows open. Coffee going cold on the table. The east room latch turned easily under Isandro's hand, as it did every evening, the small ritual of it: open the room, let the air move through, close it unlocked. On the desk inside lay the manuscript she had returned to in the spring—*The Unsaid Interior*, begun as a novel about a fictional family, abandoned within a week because fiction felt like one more disguise. What she was writing now had no genre name she trusted. Memoir, testimony, inventory—it was all three, and it began with the sentence she had rewritten forty times and finally kept: *My father was buried under the wrong name, and I am the one who dug the truth up, and I have never been less sorry.*

Someday it would be finished. Someday it would sit in the east room beside the confession, another document in the growing shelf of things said. But tonight there was soup to make, and a letter to Toronto to draft, and Thursday coming with its chairs to set out, and Vivian's card to answer—because Isandro had decided, after the podcast card arrived, that she would answer every one, even if the answers came back unanswered, even if Vivian loved only from the next room forever. Next room was still adjacent. Adjacent was a direction. Directions could change.

"Isandro." Wren stood in the doorway of the east room, backlit by the last of the light, holding the cracked teacup she had carried out of the kitchen for no reason except that she wanted it near. "When the Toronto woman writes back—if she writes back—what do we tell her first? About all this?"

Isandro looked around the room: the photographs of the two families, side by side again after forty years of separation; the framed confession; the ledgers, old and new, the debt and the payment; the blank pages too, kept in their own box, because the silence itself was part of the record and deserved to be seen for what it was—forty years of paper on which nothing was written, and everything was.

"We tell her the truest thing I know," Isandro said. "We tell her: the unsaid doesn't die with you. It waits. And the moment you say it—out loud, in front of witnesses, however much it costs—that's the moment the waiting ends and the living starts." She smiled, tired and certain. "We tell her it's never too late to open a door. We know, because we opened ours."

Outside, the tide was turning, and the house stood open to the sea air, breathing.
