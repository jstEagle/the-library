## 1. Load Rating

The Harrow Bridge was sixty-one years old and Zephyrine Pemberton had been crawling over it for thirty of them, which meant he knew its body better than he knew his own. He knew the sound it made under a loaded truck—a low complaint in the steel, like an old man shifting in a chair. He knew where the rust bloomed first each spring: the north chord, third panel point, where the drainage had never worked right. He knew that if you put your palm flat against the abutment concrete on a cold morning you could feel the river's cold coming up through it, patient as anything.

He did not know, after twenty-six years of marriage, what Odile did with her Tuesdays.

This thought arrived at 7:14 a.m., uninvited, while he was kneeling on the deck with his hand on a joint seal and his clipboard balanced on one knee. He wrote *seal—minor separation, west lane, monitor* and then sat back on his heels and let the other thought come all the way in, because he had learned that thoughts were like cracks: if you didn't measure them, they measured you.

Tuesdays she said she went to the library. He had no reason to doubt this. That was the thing—he had no reason to doubt it, and no reason to confirm it either, and somewhere in the last decade the absence of reason had quietly become its own kind of evidence. Not of anything. Just of distance. You could drive a truck across distance for years and nothing would happen, and then one day you'd rate it and find it couldn't carry what it used to.

He stood up. His knees reported in their usual language. Below him the Cane River moved brown and unhurried under the trusses, carrying last night's rain toward the reservoir, toward the dam, toward everything downstream that people had built anyway.

* * *

The morning inspection ran off the standard sequence, and Zephyrine loved it for that. Sequence was mercy. Sequence meant you didn't have to decide what to look at; you only had to look.

Deck first. He walked the west lane with the sounding hammer and tapped the rail posts, listening for the dull note of delamination versus the bright ring of sound concrete. Post four rang true. Post five gave him the dull note, and he knelt and found the hairline running diagonally from the anchor bolt, thin as a hair pulled from a dark head, maybe ninety millimeters, maybe less. He measured it twice because measuring twice was a form of prayer. Then he wrote it down: *hairline, 90 mm, diagonal, non-structural, monitor.*

Hairline cracks were the honest ones. They told you exactly where they were. The dangerous cracks were the ones that hid inside the member until the member failed, but hairlines just sat there in the open saying *I am hurt, I am still here, watch me.* He had always felt a professional tenderness toward them. A man could learn something from a hairline crack about how to live, if a man were inclined to learn things, which Zephyrine had arranged his life so as not to be.

Superstructure next. He climbed down the ladder to the inspection platform under the deck, harness clipped, boots finding the familiar rungs without looking. Underneath, the bridge was a cathedral nobody visited—riveted chords going up into shadow, the cross-bracing making its long geometry overhead, swallows nesting where they weren't supposed to. He checked the connections at panel points two through nine. Paint failure at the lower chord, expected. Section loss within tolerance. At panel point six he found the weep stain he'd been tracking for four years, the rust bleeding down the web plate in a slow brown tear, and he held his caliper to the flange and got 11.2 millimeters where the drawing said 12.7.

One and a half millimeters gone in four years. Nothing, really. Everything, eventually.

He wrote it down and thought about the kitchen table.

* * *

The kitchen table had a wobble that Odile fixed every few months by folding a square of cardboard and sliding it under the short leg, and every few months the cardboard compressed and the wobble came back, and neither of them ever suggested the obvious repair, which was to actually fix the leg. This had been going on since roughly 2011. Zephyrine understood, in the part of himself he kept locked like a records room, that the table was not the problem. The table was the symptom they had agreed to treat instead of the disease, because treating the disease would require naming it, and naming it would require a conversation, and conversations between him and Odile had degraded over the years from structures into ruins you could walk through but not live in.

Last Tuesday—he was almost sure it was last Tuesday, though it might have been the Tuesday before, which was itself a kind of data—she had stood at the counter with her back to him peeling an orange in one long spiral, the way her mother taught her, and she'd said, "Marianne's daughter is getting married in June," and he had said, "That's nice," and she had said, "We're invited," and he had said, "Are we?" and she had not turned around, and the orange peel had kept coming, one unbroken ribbon, and he had watched it and thought: *there is a woman who can keep something whole from start to finish, and I am not that thing, and she knows it, and I know she knows it,* and then he had picked up his coffee and gone to check the weather for tomorrow's inspection, because the weather was something he could do something about.

*Are we.* As though he were asking whether they were invited or whether they existed. Maybe both. She hadn't answered. The peel had finished, perfect, and she'd laid it on the windowsill to dry for potpourri she never made, and that was the conversation, if you rated it. Load rating: posted. Reduced to light traffic only.

He recorded the flange measurement and moved to panel point seven.

* * *

At ten o'clock he sat in the cab of the county truck eating the sandwich Odile had made him, because she still made his lunch, had made it every workday for twenty-six years, ham and sharp cheddar and the mustard he liked, wrapped in wax paper, and he had never once thanked her properly for it, had absorbed it the way the abutment absorbed the river's cold. He ate and read the notes from the last biennial inspection, 2019, his own handwriting, younger-seeming somehow, more certain. *All primary members satisfactory. Bridge remains in fair condition. Recommend continued monitoring.*

Fair condition. There was a phrase that could stand for a lot of things. Nobody asked what fair meant. Fair meant: not failing today. Fair meant: someone is watching. Fair meant: we have decided, for now, that the cost of replacement exceeds the cost of vigilance, and we have made that decision quietly, without a ceremony, the way most decisions that matter are made.

His phone buzzed on the seat beside him. A text from Odile: *Did you take your pills?* He looked at it for a long moment. The statin and the blood pressure med, yes, he'd taken them, she lined them up by the coffee maker every morning in a little plastic tray with the days of the week on it, and he took them without thinking, and this—this pharmaceutical choreography—was apparently what intimacy had been reduced to between them: her managing his body's maintenance schedule because neither of them could manage its heart.

He typed *Yes* and sent it, and then sat looking at the single word, how small it was, how much it declined to say. Yes, and thank you for asking. Yes, and I notice you notice. Yes, and I don't know when we stopped talking about anything that wasn't a pill or a bill or the weather for tomorrow's inspection, and I'm afraid to find out, and I'm more afraid that you stopped noticing we stopped, and most afraid of all that you didn't stop noticing, that you've been keeping your own clipboard all these years, writing me down in your own shorthand, *hairline, non-structural, monitor,* waiting for the day the monitoring stops being enough.

He put the phone face-down on the seat and finished the sandwich and wiped the mustard carefully from the corner of his mouth, because she noticed that too, or used to.

* * *

The afternoon belonged to the substructure. He suited up in the waders and went down the bank to the pier footings, where the Cane River ran shallow over its gravel shoals, and he waded out with the rebar probe and sounded the scour aprons around pier two. The river here was the same river that had drowned Cane Hollow upstream before the dam went in—not the same water, water never stays, but the same channel, the same valley bones—and he tried, as he always tried and always failed, to feel nothing about that.

Scour was the killer nobody saw. Bridges rarely fell because of the loads they were designed for; they fell because the river quietly carried away the ground beneath them, year after year, invisible, until one flood came along and found nothing left holding. Every inspector learned this early. Every inspector learned to probe below the waterline, past what was visible, into the dark where the real condition lived.

He probed around pier two and found the apron stone mostly intact, some displacement at the northeast quadrant, and he wrote it down, and then he stood in the cold water for longer than the job required, looking upstream toward the reservoir, toward the drowned town he had not visited in thirty years, and he let himself think the thought he permitted himself approximately once per inspection cycle:

*Everything standing is standing on something you can't see.*

The marriage. The career. Himself. All of it bearing on foundations laid down before he was old enough to inspect them, in a valley that was underwater now, in a family whose records were lost in the flood along with the town, along with his mother's voice, along with whatever his brother Dell had become in the last minutes anyone saw him—the boy on the roof, sixteen years old, the water rising, and then the story everyone agreed to stop telling. Zephyrine had been seven. Seven-year-olds remember in fragments, and he had spent thirty years declining to assemble the fragments, preferring them loose in the drawer, rattling, harmless-sounding.

Probe the substructure, the manual said. Find out what's underneath.

He had inspected ten thousand bridges and never once probed his own.

* * *

Back at the truck at four, he totaled the day. Deck: two hairlines, one new. Superstructure: section loss at panel point six, 1.5 millimeters over four years, trending. Substructure: minor scour displacement, monitor. Overall rating, unchanged: fair. He wrote *fair* and paused over it, pen hovering, the way he sometimes paused over the word, feeling its hollowness, its administrative mercy. Fair. Still carrying. Still standing on something.

He drove home the long way, over the bridge itself, because he liked to feel a structure from inside a moving vehicle, the way a doctor might shake a patient's hand. The Harrow took his truck's weight without comment. Sixty-one years. It had outlasted the town it was built to serve, outlasted the ferry before it, outlasted the men who argued for its construction in the county minutes he'd once read out of boredom. It would probably outlast his marriage, statistically speaking. Most things did.

At home the kitchen light was on and Odile was at the stove, and she said, "There's leftovers, or I can make eggs," and he said, "Eggs are fine," and she made eggs, and they ate at the wobbly table with the cardboard shim doing its temporary eternal job, and she told him the library had a book sale Saturday, and he said, "Maybe," and the word hung there between them, meaning *probably not,* meaning *don't count on me,* meaning, if you translated it all the way down, *I don't know how to be at a book sale with you, I've forgotten the procedure, we have no load rating for ordinary happiness anymore.*

After dinner she washed the pans and he dried, their old division of labor, efficient, silent, and he noticed—really noticed, the way he'd noticed the hairline at post five—that she hummed while she washed. Four notes, descending, then a pause, then the same four notes. Over and over. Something half-remembered, or half-invented. He opened his mouth to ask what it was and closed it again, because asking would require her to stop humming, and the humming was the closest thing to music that had happened in that kitchen in years, and he was afraid of what it would turn into if he touched it. A conversation. A question with weight behind it. An opening.

He dried the pan and said nothing, and logged the omission nowhere, because he kept no notebook of unsaid things, officially, formally, in no ledger whatsoever.

Later, in bed, he heard her breathing even out, and he lay awake doing what he did instead of sleeping, which was inspecting. The ceiling had a stress crack above the window, seasonal, thermal, nothing. The house settled differently in spring. Somewhere downstairs the refrigerator cycled on, a small reliable machine in a house full of deferred maintenance.

Eleven weeks, he thought, for no reason he could name, and the number sat in the dark with him, unfamiliar, ticking, like a countdown to something he hadn't been notified of yet.

He didn't know yet that the notification was already in the mail.
