## 4. Inventory of Unsaid Things

He didn't sleep. That much was predictable, almost procedural. Zephyrine Pemberton had spent thirty years learning that structures under unexpected load behave in ways you can't guess until you've watched them, and at 2:40 in the morning he lay in the dark cataloging his own deflections: heart rate elevated, thoughts cycling, the ceiling above him doing its slow plaster arithmetic. Odile breathed beside him in the even rhythm of a person who had not heard a song from a dead woman on the radio, or had heard it and filed it away the way she filed everything—neatly, in a drawer he'd never been permitted to open.

At 3:15 he gave up on sleep the way you give up on a span that's already failed. He got up, took the good flashlight from the kitchen drawer because he didn't want to wake her with lights, and went down to the den where he kept his working notebooks—the field books, the inspection logs, the graph-paper volumes with their stiff covers that had outlasted three briefcases and one near-drowning at a culvert out past Millbrook.

He sat at the desk. He opened a fresh notebook to the first page.

He wrote the date. Then, because the habit of thirty years was stronger than the hour, he wrote *Subject:*

And then he sat there for a long time with the pen uncapped, because the subject was everything, and everything is not a subject. Everything is a failure to choose.

What he finally wrote, in the small block capitals he used for anything that mattered:

*SUBJECT: THINGS NOT SAID. AN INVENTORY.*

It was, he would admit later, the most honest engineering document of his career.

* * *

The first list came easily, which frightened him. He'd expected resistance. What he got instead was a kind of terrible fluency, the pen moving the way it moved when he was rating a girder—fast, certain, each item discrete and load-bearing.

*Things I never told Odile:*

*That my mother hummed, not sang. That she had a habit of stopping three notes from the end of any phrase, like the song was a bridge she didn't trust to hold her weight.*

*That I saw my brother's face the night of the flood. Not from the roof. From the water. That I have never been sure which of us was saved and which was left.*

*That I applied to engineering school with money I told her came from a settlement. There was no settlement. There was a man named Harrow and a check my father's union fought two years for, and I let her believe the cleaner story because the true one had water in it.*

*That I chose bridges because they end. Every one of them, every span, has two ends, and you can stand on the ground at either one and know exactly where you are.*

*That I have been keeping a second set of books on our marriage for twenty-six years. Not infidelity. Worse. Accounting.*

He stopped there, because his hand had gone unsteady, and because the lamp in the den seemed suddenly too bright, a work light on an open wound of a structure. He read the list back. It read like a punch list. Deficiencies noted. Repairs not yet specified.

That was the trick of it, he thought. That was the whole trick of his life. An inspection finds the crack, names the crack, measures the crack, photographs the crack, files the crack under a code number—and nowhere in the process is there a single line that says *fix it.* The fixing belongs to some other department. Some other man. He had been writing inspection reports on his own life for three decades and had never once submitted a repair plan.

He capped the pen. Uncapped it. The house ticked around him—joists contracting, copper pipes cooling, the water heater doing its midnight work. A house was just a bridge that stood still. Same fatigue. Same slow surrender of joints. He'd told Odile that once, early, trying to be charming, and she'd said, *Then why don't we ever inspect it,* and he'd laughed and changed the subject, and that was the marriage in one exchange, wasn't it. She asked for the inspection. He gave her a joke.

* * *

The second list took longer, because the questions were heavier than the confessions. Confessions you could carry alone; he'd proven that. Questions required another person, and the only other person who could answer them was a dead man or a drowned one.

*Things I never asked about the flood:*

*Why the sirens came late. Everyone in Cane Hollow said they came late. Nobody ever said who was late, or why, and the county report said the sirens came at the scheduled time, and the scheduled time was wrong, and nobody ever asked the county why the schedule was wrong.*

*Why my father went back for the Hendersons' boy when the water was already at the second floor, and why my mother let him, and why neither of them told us to run for the ridge instead of the roof. Was it love or was it arithmetic? Did they count the children and decide?*

*What Dell saw from the high water. What he actually saw. The town made him a boy in a rowboat, a hero, and then made him a coward in the same breath, and I never once asked him which of those he believed he was, because I was afraid he'd ask me the same question back.*

*Why Mama stopped singing after. Not gradually. All at once, like a joint sheared clean. And then the humming again that last winter, the new song, the one with no name, and why she taught it to Dell and not to me. Was I too young? Was I not to be trusted? Was I the one she was singing about?*

*What the water sounded like. Everyone who was there says it sounded like nothing. Like a train, like a wall, like God clearing his throat. But at night, when I let myself remember, it sounded like a door closing in a house I was still inside of. I have never said that out loud. I have never asked anyone if they heard it too.*

He wrote that last line and then sat with his palms flat on the page, as if he could take a reading off it. The pen had pressed hard enough to emboss the next sheet. He could feel the words from underneath, like a crack you find with your fingers before you ever find it with your eyes.

That was the thing about the flood. Thirty years of county reports and newspaper clippings and the dam going up afterward, the valley drowned on purpose this time, the town moved to higher ground and renamed itself Progress in all but paperwork—and still nobody had ever sat him down and said, *Here is what happened, here is the sequence, here is the load path of that night.* He'd been left to inspect the wreckage by feel, in the dark, with a flashlight he wasn't supposed to have.

* * *

Toward dawn he made coffee he didn't drink and started the third list, and this one he wrote standing up, because sitting felt too much like surrender.

*What the song might mean:*

*It is hers. I would know it if every radio station on earth conspired against me. The melody climbs where you expect it to fall. She did that with everything—ended sentences upward, like questions, like she was asking permission to have said them.*

*It is finished. That's what I can't get past. The humming stopped three notes short. The song on the radio goes all the way to the ground. Somebody completed it. Somebody carried it the rest of the way across.*

*Which means somebody learned it from her. Which means somebody was close to her in that last winter, closer than I was, and I was there, I was fourteen and I was there, and I don't remember being taught anything. So either she hid it from me on purpose, or I wasn't listening, and I have spent thirty years not deciding which of those is worse.*

*The words. I only caught four of them through the static and the road noise. Something about water, obviously. Something about a ledger. And a line that sounded like "what I owed I paid in silence" — which is not a lyric, that's a confession, that's a woman settling accounts from beyond the grave, and you don't write that kind of line for an audience, you write it for one person.*

*One person. The song was addressed to one person. And the man singing it on WQXR at eleven forty on a Tuesday night was singing it like it was addressed to him.*

He set the pen down. The window over the den desk had gone the gray of old concrete, that hour when the light comes up but brings no warmth with it. Somewhere down the street a paper truck made its rounds, brakes gasping at every stop.

He thought about the singer's voice. He'd told himself, driving, that grief had made the resemblance, that any baritone with a valley flat in it would have sounded like Dell, that Dell was dead, that Dell had been dead since 1979, officially, in the only sense that counted—dead to the family, dead in every conversation, a name said only in the past tense by a mother who stopped singing and a father who was already gone under.

But he'd spent his career learning to trust the instrument over the story. The gauge doesn't care what the report says. The gauge reads the strain. And the strain in that voice, the particular way the singer had leaned into the word *owed*—he knew that lean. He'd heard it across a dinner table for the first twelve years of his life, a boy who took the bigger half of everything and watched you while he did it, checking whether you'd noticed, daring you to say so.

Dell. Alive. Singing their mother's finished song on the radio like a man paying off a debt in public.

Zephyrine picked up the notebook, tore nothing out, crossed nothing out. He turned to a clean page and wrote a fourth list, the shortest one, the one that scared him most:

*Things I must do:*

*Find him.*

He looked at the word a while. Two words. The shortest span he'd ever rated, and the one carrying the most load.

Then, because he was who he was—because the man he was had been built to the same code as the bridges, redundant, over-designed, afraid of any single point of failure—he added a third line:

*And first, decide what to tell Odile.*

He heard her upstairs now, the floorboards announcing her, the bathroom pipes waking. In twenty minutes she'd come down and make her tea and read the first page of whatever book was on the counter and say something small and true about the weather, and he would sit across from her with a notebook full of her name and say nothing, again, one more time, the way he'd done ten thousand mornings running.

Unless.

He closed the notebook but didn't hide it. For the first time in thirty years of careful filing, he left it out on the desk, cover up, like a structure left deliberately unshored—visible, unbraced, waiting to see what load the day would put on it.
