## 2. Return Ticket, One Way

The bus to Puerto Sombra left at seven in the morning from a station that smelled of diesel and fried dough, and Oscar Kavanagh boarded it with a single suitcase, a folder of estate documents, and a rehearsed set of facial expressions arranged in her mind like tools on a pegboard.

She had spent the previous evening preparing, the way she prepared for everything. Not the packing—packing had taken eleven minutes, a skill refined by thirty years of moving between cities and languages and versions of herself—but the social logistics. In Puerto Sombra there would be questions. There were always questions in small towns, and they arrived wearing the costume of kindness. *Oscar, is that you? You've changed. You've not changed. How long has it been? And your mother—* and there the sentence would open like a trapdoor.

So she had prepared responses. Short ones. Polite ones. She had practiced them in the mirror of her apartment while the city hummed below, and she had noticed, with the detached interest of a professional observer, that her smile had become very good indeed. It was the smile of a woman who is fine, who is busy, who is grateful for your concern and requires nothing further. She thought of it privately as the smile that ends conversations, though she would never have said so aloud. Aloud, it was simply a smile.

The bus pulled out of the station and the city began to peel away—first the glass towers, then the shabbier honesty of the outskirts, then the long gray ribbon of highway where the sea appeared and disappeared between hills like someone checking on her.

Oscar opened the folder. Estate documents. Her cousin Rafael's letters, three of them over two years, each one more apologetic and less apologetic at once. *The taxes, Oscar, you understand. The roof alone. The house just sits there.* She understood. Understanding was her profession. She took sentences in one language and made them behave in another, and Rafael's letters were simply another dialect of the same old problem: things unsaid, arranged into grammatical order.

The auction was in six weeks. She had told herself she was going down to *prepare the house for sale*—a phrase with a clean, efficient sound, all business, no ghosts. Prepare the house. As if a house could be prepared. As if thirty years of absence could be itemized and boxed.

She looked out the window and deliberately did not rehearse anything.

* * *

Around ten, the bus stopped at a roadside café and half the passengers poured out for coffee. Oscar stayed in her seat. A woman across the aisle, returning to her seat with a paper cup, glanced at her, glanced again, and Oscar felt the moment arrive early—the moment she had rehearsed for.

"Forgive me," the woman said, in Spanish, settling into the aisle beside her with the confidence of someone who had never met a stranger. "You have a face I know. From Puerto Sombra?"

"I grew up there," Oscar said, in Spanish, and was startled by how it came out—rusty at the hinges, the vowels slightly flattened by decades of English, like a door that had swelled in the rain.

"Kavanagh," the woman said triumphantly. "You're Marisol's daughter. The one who went away."

*The one who went away.* Not a question. A category. Oscar deployed the smile. "That's me."

"Your mother—" the woman began, and then stopped, and her face did something complicated, and Oscar watched the sentence die and be replaced. "She was a wonderful woman. Everyone says so. Everyone always said so." The repetition was strange, almost defensive, as if the woman were arguing with someone who wasn't there. "Everyone."

"Thank you," Oscar said, and the smile held.

The woman squeezed her hand and returned to her seat, and Oscar sat with her pulse tapping in her throat and tried to identify what exactly had just happened. Not grief exactly. Grief's advance scout, maybe. The small cold soldier sent ahead to report on the terrain.

Everyone says so. Everyone always said so. She turned the phrase over. It had the texture of a town that had agreed on a story and was holding the agreement in place through sheer repetition. She had spent her career listening for what sentences were doing underneath what they said. This one was holding a door shut.

She opened the folder again and read Rafael's most recent letter for the fifth time and understood none of it any better.

* * *

The landscape changed the way it always did, gradually and then suddenly—the hills going gold, then green, the pines giving way to wind-bent eucalyptus, the air through the bus's faulty seal turning salted. By two in the afternoon she could smell Puerto Sombra before she could see it: fish and diesel and jasmine, the smell of her entire childhood compressed into one briny fist.

The bus came down the hill road and there it was, the town, arranged along its crescent of gray water exactly as postcards had always lied about it. The church. The plaza with its four royal palms. The breakwater where the fishing boats knocked their hulls together like old friends gossiping. It was smaller than her memory had kept it, and more worn, and more beautiful, and she hated it a little for all three.

She got off at the plaza with her suitcase and stood for a moment in the light, letting the town look at her.

And it did look. She felt it the way you feel sun through clothing. A man repairing a shutter paused. Two women at the bakery window performed the subtle rotation of heads that in small towns functions as a full interrogation. A boy on a bicycle slowed, considered her, and pedaled on, having filed her under *nobody yet.*

Oscar walked. She had planned the route to minimize encounters—up Calle del Sol rather than the longer way past the market, a straight shot, head down, efficient. But Puerto Sombra had other plans, as it always had.

"Oscar? Little Oscar Kavanagh?"

She turned. A heavyset man in a guayabera was coming out of the hardware store, wiping his hands on a rag, his face already open with delight. It took her a moment—thirty years is a long time for a face—but then it assembled itself: Chuy Mendoza, who used to sell her tamarind candy from a cart outside the school.

"Señor Chuy," she said, and the childhood honorific came out before she could stop it, and his whole face lit up like a lamp.

"Still *Señor* Chuy!" He laughed. "After all this time. Look at you. A professional woman, I can tell. The suitcase, the—what do you call it—the *look*." He gestured at her generally, at the efficiency of her. "And how is—"

He stopped. The trapdoor. She watched him step back from it with visible effort, watched him choose, and something in his kindness nearly undid her right there on Calle del Sol.

"The house," he said instead. "You're here for the house. Rafael's letters, yes? Such a shame. Such a shame, that house sitting empty." He shook his head, and then, lower, almost confidentially: "You know, people still ask about her. Your mother. After all this time, they ask."

"What do they ask?" Oscar said.

Chuy's eyebrows went up, and he seemed to genuinely consider the question, and then he smiled and patted her arm. "They ask when you're coming home," he said, which was so obviously not the answer that Oscar understood it was the only answer he intended to give. "Well. Welcome home, little Oscar. If you need anything for the house—you know, locks, hinges, the boards on the windows—my store is right here. Family discount."

"Thank you, Señor Chuy."

"Chuy," he said sternly. "Just Chuy. Some things don't change."

She walked on, and behind her she heard him telling the hardware store, in a voice pitched generously to carry, "That's Marisol's girl. She's come back for the house."

*Marisol's girl.* Not a name. A relation. In this town she would never be Oscar; she would be an appendage, a satellite, a daughter-shaped absence returned to fill itself in. She climbed the hill toward Calle Luna with her suitcase wheels clattering over the cobbles and rehearsed, uselessly, the smile.

* * *

The house at the end of Calle Luna was worse than the photographs and exactly as she remembered it, and both of these things were true at once, which is the particular cruelty of childhood homes.

The photographs Rafael had sent had shown a modest white house with blue trim, shuttered, a little tired. The reality was a modest white house that the sea had been quietly eating for thirty years. Salt bloom crawled up the stucco like frost. The blue trim had faded to the color of old denim. The shutters hung at angles suggesting they had given up at different times, in an order of decreasing loyalty. Plywood boarded two of the front windows, and someone—Rafael, presumably, in one of his bursts of practical remorse—had nailed the boards on with an optimism that had not survived the winters.

The gate stuck. She put her shoulder to it the way she had at nine years old, and it gave with the same complaint, the same *screek*, and the sound went through her like a needle finding an old scar.

*Exactly as she left it.* That was the phrase she'd been carrying, and standing in the small front yard—overgrown, the rosemary her mother had planted gone woody and enormous, gone feral, gone on without anyone—she understood that the phrase was a lie she had brought with her for comfort. Nothing was as she had left it. She had left it. That was the difference. The house had stayed and been stayed-in by time, by salt, by weather, by the slow attention of decay, which is more faithful than most people.

She stood in the yard for a long moment with the suitcase handle biting her palm.

The key was under the third flowerpot from the door, because of course it was, because her mother had kept a spare key under that flowerpot since before Oscar was born, and Rafael's letters had mentioned it with the embarrassment of a man reporting a security failure. She lifted the pot. The key was there, orange with rust, warm from the sun.

The front door opened onto darkness and a smell that stopped her on the threshold: dust, yes, and salt, and mildew, but underneath all of it, impossibly, stubbornly, *her mother's house*—the particular compound of coffee and lemon oil and laundry soap that had been the smell of being loved and hadn't gotten the news.

Oscar stood in the doorway of her childhood and let the smell do what thirty years of professionalism could not.

Then, because she was who she was, she took out her phone, turned on the flashlight, and made a list.

*Day one priorities,* she wrote. *Air the house. Check water. Inventory: main room, kitchen, mother's room, my room, storage. Contact Rafael re: auctioneer. Assess roof.*

Efficiency. It was a kindness she did herself, the way some people hum when they're frightened. She worked. She opened windows and the sea came in and moved the dust around in the light. She turned the tap in the kitchen and after a shudder the pipes produced water, brown, then gray, then merely suspicious. She swept the main room and found, behind the door, the pencil marks on the doorframe where her height had been recorded annually in her mother's hand—*Oscar, 7 años*—and she swept around them without touching them, the way you step around a sleeping animal.

The house was full of these ambushes. The kitchen table with the wobble she knew how to fix by turning it a quarter-turn. The hook by the door where her school satchel had hung, the hook still there, waiting, patient as a dog. The staircase she had counted a thousand times—fourteen steps, always fourteen—and which she now climbed without counting and then counted anyway and found were still fourteen, and was absurdly relieved.

Her mother's room was at the top, facing the sea.

She stood outside it for ten minutes. She knew this because she checked her phone after, and was embarrassed even alone, even in an empty house, to have the evidence. Ten minutes in a hallway, in her own childhood home, unable to open a door.

*Efficient people don't stand in hallways,* she told herself, and opened it.

The bed was made. That was the thing that got her—not the dust, not the light-eaten curtains, but the bed, made with hospital corners, the way Marisol Kavanagh had made it every morning of her life including, presumably, her last. A woman who made her bed for an audience of no one. Oscar sat down on the edge of that made bed and the dust rose around her and she did not cry, she noted with professional interest, she did not cry, she simply breathed, and the breathing was the crying, it was crying in another language, and she was fluent enough to understand it.

* * *

She worked until the light went gold and then blue. She inventoried the main room: furniture, serviceable, mostly to be sold. Books in Spanish, spines cracked. The radio that had delivered the news and the novels-on-air of her childhood. She inventoried the kitchen: everything as it had been, the pans hanging in their old hierarchy, the good dishes wrapped in newspaper from a year she didn't recognize, headlines gone yellow and soft.

It was in the kitchen, on that first evening, that she noticed the tile.

She hadn't been looking for anything. She was measuring the counter for the auctioneer's inventory sheet, and her hand rested on the backsplash, and one tile—small, blue, ordinary, one of a hundred—sat a millimeter proud of its neighbors. Loose. She pressed it, absently, the way you press a tooth.

The tile rocked.

Oscar looked at it for a long moment. The light was failing. The house creaked its evening creaks around her, settling into its own body the way old houses and old people do.

She did not pry it up that night. She told herself it was because she was tired, because the inventory was half done, because loose tiles in old houses were loose for boring reasons. She told herself this while writing it down in the inventory notes—*kitchen backsplash: one tile loose, check*—in her tidy professional shorthand, the shorthand of a woman who translates things for a living and has never once translated herself.

She made tea in her mother's kitchen with her mother's dented kettle, standing in her mother's kitchen at her mother's table, and drank it standing up because sitting down felt like something she wasn't ready for. Through the window over the sink—the window her mother had looked out of for forty years—the sea was going dark, and the first light of the lighthouse at the point swept around, patient, punctual, counting the town like a shepherd.

*Six weeks,* she thought. *Inventory, appraise, sell, leave.* A clean operation. In and out. She was very good at leaving; it was practically her mother tongue.

She washed the cup and set it on the rack, and her hand knew where the rack was in the dark, and she stood there a moment with her wet hand on the counter, in the house that remembered everything, and admitted, to the dark kitchen, to the loose tile, to the fourteen stairs and the made bed and the key under the third flowerpot:

"I'm not ready for any of this."

The house said nothing. It had been waiting thirty years; it could wait longer.

Upstairs, she slept in her old room, in a sleeping bag on a bare mattress, and if she dreamed, she did not write it down. And in the morning, when the light came in off the sea and lay across the floor of the kitchen like a delivered letter, Oscar Kavanagh took out the inventory sheets, and sharpened a pencil, and went to work on the house of a woman she had loved and never once asked a single question—

beginning, without knowing it, at the loose blue tile behind the counter, which was waiting for her the way everything in that house had always waited: patiently, and with something to say.
