## 4. The Man Everyone Loves

The plaza smelled of cut grass and frying dough, which is the official perfume of every celebration in Puerto Sombra and has been for as long as anyone can remember. Oscar arrived early enough to claim a spot near the bandstand but late enough to look casual about it — a calculation she had perfected over thirty years of walking into rooms where she knew no one wanted to know her.

She had not planned to come. She had planned, in fact, to spend the morning cataloguing kitchenware, a task she'd been approaching with the grim enthusiasm of a woman defusing bombs one drawer at a time. But the flyer had been stapled to the lamppost outside the house on Calle Luna, its corners already curling in the salt air: *Homenaje al Juez Tomás Reyes — 40 Años de Servicio a Nuestra Comunidad.* Forty years. She did the arithmetic without wanting to. Forty years ago she was nine years old, eating ice cream in this same plaza while her mother held her hand too tightly.

So here she was, standing behind a man whose shirt said *YO ❤️ EL PESCADO* across the back, watching workers test the microphone with the universal ritual of tapping it and saying "one, two" as though the numbers might behave differently today.

The town had turned out in force. Folding chairs fanned out across the plaza in rows that suggested geometry but delivered chaos. Women in their Sunday best fanned themselves with anything fan-shaped: programs, envelopes, a menu from La Perla. Children chased each other around the fountain, where a stone dolphin spouted water at an angle that suggested it had given up sometime in the nineties. Someone had hung bunting between the palm trees, red and white, slightly crooked, the way all civic decoration in this town was slightly crooked, as if straightness were a form of showing off.

Oscar recognized faces the way you recognize faces from a photograph you didn't know was taken of you. There was the butcher's son, now old enough to be the butcher. There was a woman who had once, memorably, told seven-year-old Oscar that she had her mother's eyes and then looked at Marisol as though confirming an inventory. Everyone was older. Everyone was rounder or thinner or grayer. Everyone was exactly where they had always been.

She pulled her hat lower and practiced being furniture.

The mayor took the stage first — a broad, sunburned man named Bautista who had been class president, deputy mayor, and now mayor, a career trajectory that in Puerto Sombra was roughly equivalent to a dynasty. He spoke for eleven minutes about service, sacrifice, and the sea, managing to mention the sea four times, each time as though introducing it to the audience for the first time.

"And now," Bautista boomed, "the man we are all here to honor. A judge who never forgot that behind every case number is a human face. A philanthropist who rebuilt our church roof, funded our library, and paid for Doña Carmen's hip surgery —" scattered applause, and somewhere a woman Oscar assumed was Doña Carmen dabbed her eyes with a hankie the size of a tablecloth — "a man who has given this town everything. Ladies and gentlemen: Judge Tomás Reyes."

The applause came up like weather.

And there he was.

Oscar had prepared herself for many versions of Tomás Reyes: the frail tyrant, the bloated caricature, the ghost shuffling toward his own statue. What she got instead was a tall man in a linen suit the color of oatmeal, silver-haired, upright, moving with the unhurried confidence of someone who has never once wondered whether he is welcome. He kissed the mayor on both cheeks. He waved off the offered chair. He stood at the microphone and waited for the applause to die down, and when it didn't die down fast enough, he raised one hand gently, the gesture of a conductor asking for pianissimo, and the plaza obeyed him.

"My friends," he said, and his voice was warm gravel, a voice built for verdicts and toasts alike. "I have only two fears in this life. One is that I will say something long enough to make you miss your lunch."

Laughter, easy and total.

"The other fear," he continued, "is that I will never be able to repay what this town has given me."

He paused there, letting it land, and Oscar watched several hundred people lean slightly forward, the whole plaza tilting toward him like plants toward sun. It was masterful. It was, she thought, the most polished performance she had seen since leaving the theater district, and she had once translated a corporate retreat keynote for a CEO who cried on cue.

He talked about the flood of '89 and how neighbors had carried neighbors. He talked about the schoolteacher who stayed late to teach fishermen's sons their letters. He talked about mothers — "the true judges among us" — and the word went through Oscar like a fishhook. He talked for fourteen minutes and made it feel like six, and when he finished, the plaque was unveiled: brass, substantial, engraved with his name and the words *SIRVIÓ CON JUSTICIA Y CORAZÓN.*

*Served with justice and heart.*

Oscar stood very still and felt something bloom under her ribs that she refused, for the moment, to name. Suspicion, probably. Or heartburn. In this town the two were often indistinguishable.

* * *

Afterward came the part of these ceremonies that Oscar dreaded most, the part she had rehearsed defenses against since childhood: the mingling. The plaza dissolved into clusters, the chairs were stacked by teenagers with the efficiency of people who have done it a hundred times, and the tables of empanadas and lemonade opened for business like a small, delicious stock exchange.

Oscar positioned herself near the fountain, holding a lemonade she didn't want, performing the role of a woman comfortably waiting for someone. It was a role she could sustain for approximately eight minutes before the loneliness became visible even to strangers.

"You'll get a sunburn standing out here like a lighthouse keeper."

The voice came from behind her, warm gravel, unmistakable. She turned and Tomás Reyes was looking at her with an expression of delighted recognition that no one should be able to manufacture on demand, and yet there it was, flawless, factory-fresh.

"Doña Marisol's girl," he said. "Of course. You have her exact way of standing — as if the ground owes you an apology."

It was, objectively, funny. Oscar felt the laugh escape before she could audit it.

"Señor Reyes."

"Tomás, please. Anyone who calls me 'señor' gets billed for my legal advice." He gestured at her lemonade. "Is that any good? They make it too sweet. Everything in this town is too sweet — the lemonade, the speeches." His eyes crinkled. "I include my own."

Up close, he was older than the stage had allowed her to see. The silver hair was thinning at the crown. There were liver spots on his hands, and his left hand trembled slightly until he closed it around the lapel of his jacket, a small correction so practiced it might have been a habit rather than a symptom. Seventy-something, maybe more. A man assembling himself in real time and doing it well.

"I'm sorry about your mother," he said, and the warmth in it seemed genuine, seemed bottomless, seemed — and this was the part that unsettled her — *calibrated*. "Marisol was one of the finest people this town ever produced. We all knew that. I hope you knew it too."

"I'm finding that out," Oscar said carefully.

"She was private. Stubbornly, gloriously private." He shook his head with something that looked like fond exasperation. "You couldn't get her to take credit for anything. I tried for thirty years."

Something shifted in Oscar's chest, a hairline crack in a wall she hadn't known she was leaning on. *Tried for thirty years.* Was that a figure of speech? An old man's loose talk? Or an inventory?

"You knew her well, then."

"Well?" He considered the question with the seriousness of a man considering a motion before the court. "In a town this size, everyone knows everyone the way you know your own hands. You stop seeing them. But Marisol—" he paused, and the pause had weight, deliberate weight, the kind of pause that lawyers bill for— "Marisol I saw. She was the conscience of this place, whether it asked for one or not."

A boy ran past with a balloon. Somewhere the band struck up again, something in three-four time, slightly too fast.

"You must come to dinner," Tomás said. It wasn't quite a question. "My wife makes a lamb that has ended friendships and started marriages. And I want to hear about your mother from someone who actually knew her. All this town ever gave me was the legend. Legends are useless. They don't tell you what a person was like at six in the morning."

Oscar heard herself say yes. She watched herself smile. Both actions appeared to originate from some department of her that had not consulted headquarters, and she found this professionally alarming — she was, after all, a woman whose entire career was built on knowing precisely what she was saying in any language at any moment.

"Wonderful." He produced a card from his breast pocket — an actual card, cream stock, embossed, a retired judge carrying calling cards like a diplomat — and pressed it into her hand. His fingers were cool. "Thursday. Seven o'clock. If you don't come, I'll send the mayor after you, and believe me, nobody wants that."

He kissed her hand, which was absurd and charming and exactly calculated to be both, and moved on into the crowd, gathering gratitude the way ships gather wake. She watched him stop for photographs, accept a child onto his hip, clap a fisherman on the shoulder hard enough to stagger him affectionately. Every gesture landed. Nothing was wasted. It was like watching a pianist who had played the same sonata for forty years: no note uncertain, no emotion unearned-looking, and yet — and this was the thought that would not leave her alone — nothing left open either. No gap anywhere through which you might glimpse the actual man.

*His kindness feels like a performance she can't audit,* she thought, and then corrected herself, because that was the translator in her talking, the professional skeptic, the woman who spent her days assuming every sentence was hiding another sentence. Maybe he was simply kind. Maybe towns like this produced men who were genuinely loved and deserved it. Maybe the postcards in her mother's shoebox meant nothing at all — maybe they were old debts of a personal nature, favors between friends, stamps bought and forgotten, a habit of gratitude that never found its mailbox.

Maybe.

But she kept hearing it, replaying it, turning it over like a coin to check for counterfeits: *I tried for thirty years to get her to take credit for anything.*

Thirty years. The ledger — whatever it was, whatever she suspected it was — spanned fifteen. The postcards spanned years she hadn't counted yet. And Tomás Reyes stood in the middle of it all wearing a brass plaque that said *JUSTICIA Y CORAZÓN*, telling a stranger that her mother had been the town's conscience, smiling the smile of a man with absolutely nothing to hide, which was, in Oscar's professional experience, the most expensive smile there was.

* * *

She walked home along the malecón, the sea doing its eternal indifferent work beside her. The light was going gold and long. Behind her, the plaza was emptying; ahead, Calle Luna waited with its boarded windows and its boxes and its shoebox of unsent words.

At the corner by the church, she stopped. Don Efraín's old boat ramp was still there, or the bones of it — pilings gone gray, the winch rusted into sculpture. She remembered it from childhood: a blue boat named *La Promesa*, the Promise, because Efraín had named it the year his first grandson was born. She remembered, vaguely, that the boat had disappeared. She had been a teenager. She had not asked why. Nobody asked why, in those days; things in Puerto Sombra simply vanished — boats, lawsuits, certain names from certain conversations — and the town absorbed the losses the way sand absorbs water.

Her mother had grown up on this street. Had walked past this ramp. Had watched, presumably, whatever had happened to Efraín happen.

Oscar took out the cream-colored card and read it again under the failing light. *Tomás Reyes. Estancia Los Almendros.* Below the name, in small type: *Consultas privadas.*

Private consultations. From a man who had just told her, in front of God and the dolphin fountain, that her mother had been the conscience of the town.

She put the card away and climbed the hill to Calle Luna, and by the time she reached the door she had already decided: tomorrow she would start with the first postcard, the oldest one, the one addressed in her mother's careful hand to a man who had just been handed a brass plaque for justice.

It began, if she remembered correctly, with a fisherman's net.
