## 9. Postcard Nine: The Schoolteacher

The ninth postcard was heavier than the others. Not physically—it weighed exactly what a rectangle of cardstock weighs, which is nothing, which is the whole problem with paper—but it sat in my palm with a gravity I couldn't explain until I read it twice and understood that this one wasn't about money at all.

My mother's handwriting filled both sides, small and upright, the letters of a woman who had learned penmanship from nuns and never let them down:

*Tomás —*

*The schoolteacher on Calle Miramar, Señora Inés Barrientos. You remember her. Twenty-two years in the classroom and you dismissed her suit in eleven minutes. Her hip is worse now; she cannot afford the operation, and she is too proud to beg, so she will not know it is coming. I have left an envelope with Dr. Fuentes marked "anonymous bequest." She will argue. Let her argue. Arguing is the only dignity we left her.*

*She taught me to read, did you know that? First grade, 1958. She made me stand up and read aloud when I wanted to hide behind the taller girls. I hated her for it. I have been standing up and reading aloud ever since.*

*Marisol*

I put the card down on the kitchen table—the same table where she must have written it—and looked out the window at the sea doing its eternal impression of indifference.

*She taught me to read.*

I had gone through thirty years of my life without once wondering who taught my mother to read. Mothers arrived pre-literate, like furniture. It had never occurred to me that Marisol Kavanagh—Marisol Villalobos then, before my father's name smoothed over hers—had been a seven-year-old girl who wanted to hide behind taller children.

And now there was a name: Inés Barrientos. A street: Calle Miramar. An operation for a hip. An eleven-minute dismissal.

The ledger in the wall had given me numbers. The postcards gave me faces. Numbers you can carry. Faces follow you home.

* * *

Calle Miramar turned out to be four blocks of houses leaning toward the water like eavesdroppers, and the third house from the corner had a garden that refused to surrender—a riot of geraniums and rosemary fighting a rearguard action against the salt wind. A hand-painted sign by the gate read SE VENDE MERMELADA, and beneath it, smaller, as if the sign itself were embarrassed: *y también limones*.

An elderly woman was on the porch, wrapped in two shawls despite the mild morning, shelling peas into a bowl with the efficiency of someone who had been shelling peas since before I was born.

"Señora Barrientos?" I asked, in the childhood language, and heard how rusty it came out, consonants softening like old soap.

She looked up. Her eyes were quick, unclouded, the eyes of a former teacher assessing whether I'd done my homework.

"Depends who's asking," she said. "If you're from the municipality, the lemons are not for sale, they're a gift, and gifts aren't taxable. If you're selling something, I already have everything, including opinions. And if you're Oscar Kavanagh—" she paused, and something moved behind the quickness, "—then you'd better sit down, because Marisol's daughter does not come to my porch by accident."

I sat down so fast I nearly missed the chair.

"You know me?"

"I knew your mother," she said, resuming the peas. "You have her chin. And her way of asking a question while pretending it isn't one. Sit. Would you like tea? Don't answer yes to be polite, I only have enough water for one pot and I'm having some."

"I'll have some anyway," I said, "if you're sharing."

That earned me half a smile. "Polite and stubborn. She was polite and stubborn too." She raised her voice toward the door. "Rosario! Tea!"

A middle-aged woman appeared—daughter or granddaughter or nurse, the taxonomy of such households being deliberately obscure—with a tray, set it down, vanished. Señora Barrientos poured with a steady hand and pushed a cup toward me.

"Now," she said. "You've found the cards."

It wasn't a question, and I was too slow hiding my surprise.

"Don't look like a fish. Delia Cruz appraised this house six weeks ago. Delia Cruz talks to everyone except the people she should talk to, and I've known her since she wet herself in my classroom in 1974, so she tells me things. She said the Kavanagh girl was going through the house alone, and she said it with a look on her face like a woman watching weather come in." She sipped her tea. "So. How many have you found?"

"Nineteen," I said. "All addressed to Tomás Reyes. None mailed."

"Nineteen." She nodded slowly, as if confirming an invoice. "There were more, I think. But nineteen is what I would expect to survive. Paper rots near the sea. So do hips." She patted her left leg without self-pity, the way you'd pat a piece of furniture that had served honorably and was now failing.

"Señora—"

"Inés. If you call me señora I'll start calling you señorita and neither of us wants that at our ages."

"Inés. Did my mother ever give you money?"

The peas stopped. For a long moment the only sound was the sea and a bee negotiating with the rosemary.

"No," she said finally. "She gave Dr. Fuentes money. Or someone did. I was told it was an anonymous bequest from a family grateful for my years of service. Which was nonsense, of course—I taught three generations of this town to read and not one family ever expressed gratitude in currency. Gratitude in fruit, yes. In jam. Once in a goat, which was a whole other problem. But never money." She looked at me over the rim of her cup. "But I knew whose handwriting was on the card Dr. Fuentes 'happened' to receive from the 'benefactors' association. I taught handwriting for twenty-two years, girl. I could identify Marisol Villalobos's capital M across a crowded courtroom."

"And you never asked her?"

"Did you ever ask your mother why she bought flour from Doña Pilar when the supermarket flour was cheaper?"

I opened my mouth. Closed it.

"Exactly," said Inés. "Some questions are rude not because of what they ask but because they imply the person owes you an explanation. Marisol didn't owe me anything. If anything—" and here her voice did something complicated, a small fracture running through the firmness, "—I owed her. Eleven minutes, Tomás gave my case. Eleven minutes! Twenty-two years of service, dismissed for refusing to change the grades of a councilman's nephew, and he looked at me over his glasses and said, and I quote, 'The court finds no merit.' No merit. As if merit were a thing you could fail to find, like lost keys, instead of a thing you make, every morning, in a room full of children who can't yet spell their own names."

She set down her cup with great care, the care of someone managing her own hands.

"So no. I didn't ask questions. I took the operation. They replaced the hip. I walked again within the year. And every Sunday for fifteen years I have lit a candle for a woman whose name I pretended not to know, because knowing would have required asking, and asking would have required answers, and answers—" she spread her hands, pea shells and all, "—answers are expensive."

* * *

We talked for two hours. Or rather, she talked and I listened, which I suspect was also her preferred classroom arrangement.

She told me about my mother at seven, the girl who hid behind the taller girls but whose hand shot up when nobody was looking. About Marisol at seventeen, translating letters for the fishermen's wives at the harbor, charging nothing, taking payment in fish she gave away again before she got home. About Marisol at thirty, back from the city with a husband nobody liked and a baby with "a face like a closed door"—me, apparently, though I choose to believe she meant it affectionately.

"She loved you," Inés said, reading my face the way she'd once read my homework. "Don't confuse love with presence. Some women love loudly. Your mother loved in envelopes."

"That's a terrible way to love someone."

"It's a terrible way to be loved," she corrected. "Which is different. From the outside it looks like devotion. Ask me how I know." She gestured vaguely at the town beyond the garden wall. "Half this town was loved in envelopes and doesn't know it. The other half knows and keeps quiet, because gratitude with questions attached stops being gratitude and starts being debt, and nobody wants to owe the dead."

"Do you think she was right? Doing it this way? Secretly?"

Inés considered this with the seriousness she must once have brought to grading.

"I think," she said, "that your mother looked at the law and saw a scale that always tipped the same direction, and she decided to become a small weight on the other side. Was it right? Scales don't ask if they're right. They just hold things up." She picked up another pod. "But I'll tell you what I think about Tomás Reyes, since you're going to ask eventually and I'd rather say it while my tea is hot."

I waited.

"He was not always what he became. I taught him too—did you know that? Class of 1951. A boy who cried when a sparrow died on the playground. He buried it behind the school with full honors. I have thought about that sparrow many times over the years, trying to locate the exact moment the boy who buried sparrows became the man who found no merit in a schoolteacher's ruined hip. And do you know what I've concluded?" She leaned forward. "Nothing happened to him. That's the horror of it. Nothing happened. He simply discovered, one ruling at a time, that he could. That the scale tipped his way and nobody weighed him back."

She sat back, suddenly tired, the afternoon arriving in her shoulders.

"Your mother weighed him back. Quietly. For years. And he let her, which tells you he knew exactly what he was. A man who accepts anonymous restitution for his own rulings is a man keeping two sets of books—one for the town, one for God. And God's accountant," she added dryly, "keeps better records than either of them."

* * *

I stayed until the light went amber over the water. At the gate, Inés caught my arm with surprising strength.

"One more thing. Since you're collecting." She lowered her voice, though the garden held no one but bees. "Delia Cruz told me something else, when she'd had enough wine to loosen the appraisal. She said buyers are circling that house who don't want the house. She said—" Inés's grip tightened, "—she said some men from the capital asked her, very politely, very officially, whether the property contained any 'documents of historical interest.' Documents. Of historical interest. To men who had never shown interest in our history before." She released my arm. "Whatever you found in that house, girl, you are not the only one looking for it. Be quicker than they are. Or smarter. Quicker is easier."

I walked home along the seawall with the ninth postcard in my pocket and Inés's warning sitting in my chest like swallowed stone.

The town was lighting its evening lamps. On the plaza, the plaque with Tomás Reyes's name caught the last sun and threw it back, polished and proud. Two men stood near it, talking, and one of them wore a suit too good for Puerto Sombra, and neither of them was from here.

They watched me pass. I smiled—the smile that ends conversations—and kept walking, and did not run, and counted the blocks to Calle Luna like a schoolgirl reciting times tables, and wondered, with a cold clarity that felt inherited, how much time my mother's secrets had left before somebody else came to collect them.
