## 1. Inventory of Small Silences

The conservatory at midnight smells of lemon oil and old rosin, and if you stand very still in the east corridor you can hear the building breathe — radiators ticking, pipes settling, the faint electric hum of the practice-room lights that no one ever turns off because turning them off would mean admitting the day has ended.

I have learned the building's breathing the way I once learned its repertoire. Room by room. Measure by measure.

My name is on a laminated badge clipped to my hip: N. WINTERBOURNE, FACILITIES. Nobody reads it. That is the point of laminated badges; they are designed to be glanced at and forgotten, which makes them the perfect costume for a woman who spent ten years trying to become unrecognizable. For a while I thought I'd succeeded. Then last Tuesday a student held the door for me with his elbow, both hands full of sheet music, and said "Thanks, miss" without looking up, and I felt something loosen in my chest that I recognized, with some annoyance, as relief.

He didn't know me. Ten years ago, three hundred people knew me. Now I am the woman who mops.

* * *

The mop bucket has a wheel that squeaks on left turns only. I've mapped it. Corridor A, turn left at the water fountain, squeak. Corridor B, turn left at the trophy case, squeak. The trophy case still holds my photograph, third shelf, between a regional chamber prize and a plaque for something choral. I am fifteen in the photograph, holding a certificate rolled like a baton, smiling the way prodigies smile — as though the future were a door already open, as though someone had promised to hold it.

Nobody had promised. That was the first thing I learned, later, at nineteen, in an office that smelled of coffee and cowardice. But we are not there yet. We are here, at midnight, with a mop.

I work the corridor from the far end back, always, so that the last room I pass is Practice Room 7. This is not sentiment. It is logistics: Room 7 is closest to the supply closet. It is also, incidentally, the room where I gave my audition at seventeen, where Voss sat behind a folding table with his chin on his knuckles and listened to me play Chopin's Fourth Ballade badly — badly, because I was terrified — and then said, *Play it again, but this time tell me the truth about it.* And I did, whatever that means, whatever seventeen-year-olds mean when they suddenly stop performing and start confessing through their hands, and he leaned back and said, *Where have you been hiding?*

I could have asked him the same question, ten years later. I didn't. There wasn't time. There is never time; that is the whole architecture of my life now, rooms I pass at speed, doors I don't open.

Tonight, Room 7 is occupied. A girl — sixteen maybe, hair in a knot skewered with a pencil, cardigan too big — is working the Ballade. My Ballade. Everyone's Ballade, I suppose; it doesn't belong to me, it never did, that was rather the point of everything that happened. She plays it the way students play it: correct, careful, afraid of the coda. She stops at the same bar I used to stop at, the one where the left hand has to decide between safety and the abyss, and she starts over, and stops again, and puts her head down on the fallboard for a moment, forehead against the wood, the universal posture of the defeated.

I keep mopping. The bucket wheel squeaks on the left turn and she looks up, startled, and I raise a hand — sorry, sorry, just the janitor — and she gives me the look students give janitors, which is no look at all, a flat polite nothing, and returns to the keys.

There was a time when a girl like her would have looked at me the way people look at weather reports before a wedding. There was a time when Master Elias Voss introduced me to visiting dignitaries as *the finest thing this conservatory has produced in forty years*, and I stood there in my good shoes believing him, which was the second thing I learned, later: belief is not protection. Belief is just the shape your naivety takes right before it breaks.

The girl plays the coda. She gets through it. It isn't much — thin tone, rushed octave passage, the pedal smeared like wet ink — but she gets through it, and in the silence after, before she can decide whether it was good or bad, I say, from the doorway, "Again. Slower. Let the bass breathe."

She turns. She stares. Janitors do not speak.

"Sorry," I say. "Ignore me."

"No," she says slowly. "Say it again?"

"Slower," I say. "You're treating the bass line like furniture. It's the argument. Everything upstairs is just decoration until the bass says what it means."

She looks at me for a long moment, and I watch her decide — the way I once decided things, quickly, with my whole body — whether to be offended or curious. Curiosity wins. She plays it again, slower, and the bass breathes, and something in the room changes temperature, and I feel the old sickness rise in me, the beautiful sickness, the one that feels exactly like flying and costs exactly what flying costs.

I finish the corridor. I wring out the mop. I do not go back into Room 7, because if I go back I will sit down beside her, and if I sit down beside her I will demonstrate, and if I demonstrate she will ask who I am, and I will say *nobody*, and it will be a lie with a mop handle attached to it.

Some doors you keep closed even when they're already open.

* * *

At 2 a.m., in the break room that smells of burnt coffee and other people's dinners, I take out the notebook.

It is nothing special. A hardcover, black, spiral-bound, bought in a multipack of three from the stationery shop on Delancey. What is special is the rule, and the rule is this: I write down only the sentences I did not say. Not diary entries. Not feelings. Sentences — whole, grammatical, addressed to somebody — that existed fully formed somewhere behind my sternum and died there unsaid. Each one gets a number. Each one gets the name of its intended recipient, even when the recipient is me. Especially then.

This is entry #1. I started the book four years ago, on a night much like this one, after a shift much like this one, and I have been filling it ever since, in handwriting that gets smaller as the pages fill, as though the sentences were learning to take up less room.

Entry #1 reads:

*To the girl in Practice Room 7:* **That was my Ballade once. I played it better than you, and it ruined my life, and I would give anything to trade places with you tonight, and none of those four facts cancels any of the others.**

I look at it for a long time. Then I close the book.

Here is what nobody tells you about silence: it is not empty. Silence is a warehouse. Things accumulate in it — whole sentences, whole years, apologies stacked to the ceiling in crates marked DO NOT OPEN — and eventually the warehouse is fuller than the life around it, and you realize you have been living in the loading dock of yourself while the real inventory sits in the dark, catalogued, waiting, growing more expensive to move every year.

I have two hundred and six entries. I know the number the way other people know their weight. Some entries are small — *To the bus driver: thank you for waiting* — and some are load-bearing walls. Entry #12, to my sister Amal, eleven words, unwritten for nine years and written finally at 3 a.m. in a Denny's parking lot, and still never said aloud, because saying them aloud would require being in the same room as her, and the same room as her requires a courage I have only ever found in notebooks. Entry #31, to myself, which took three attempts and which I still don't believe. Entry #44, to Elias Voss, one sentence, the truest sentence I own, which I wrote the week after I understood what he had done, and which I have read so many times the ink has gone soft at the edges like something breathed on.

Two hundred and six sentences. All of them addressed. None of them delivered. A postal service with one employee and no route.

* * *

At 3:15 I do my final round. This is my favorite part of the night, though favorite is a strange word for it — the way a scar might be called a favorite, being the most honest thing on the body. The conservatory empty is a different instrument than the conservatory full. Full, it is all treble: chatter, scales, doors, ambition. Empty, you hear the bass line. The radiators. The building's argument with itself about warmth.

I pass the trophy case. Third shelf. Fifteen-year-old me, holding the rolled certificate like a baton, like a torch, like something you'd hand to the next runner. I have stopped looking away from her. That took years. Now I look directly, the way you look at a photograph of someone you loved who turned out to be a stranger, or a stranger who turned out to be you.

"You have no idea," I tell her. It comes out as a whisper, because whispers are all I have left for her. "You have absolutely no idea."

She smiles her prodigy smile. The future is a door already open. Someone has promised to hold it.

Down the corridor, Practice Room 7 is dark now, the girl gone home, the piano lid closed over its strings like a mouth deciding to keep something. On the community board near the entrance — the corkboard where tutors advertise and lost gloves wait for owners — a new flyer has gone up since yesterday. I notice it the way you notice a wrong note: instantly, bodily, before understanding why.

It's printed on yellow paper. There's a photograph of a house on it.

I know the house. I grew up in the house. I know the exact pitch the screen door sings when it closes, the exact depth of the crack in the front step, the exact place on the kitchen floor where the linoleum lifts like a wave frozen mid-curl. I know whose hands painted the trim white, and I know that the piano in the front room — the upright, the heavy old upright our mother refused to let anyone move — is either still inside it or already gone.

The flyer says PUBLIC AUCTION in letters tall enough to read from across the street.

The date is three weeks from Saturday.

I stand in front of the board with my mop dripping a slow dark circle onto the polished floor — a floor I will have to clean again, and will, tomorrow, and the day after — and I do not move for a long time. Somewhere above me a radiator ticks. Somewhere behind me the building breathes. And somewhere in the black spiral-bound notebook in my pocket there are two hundred and six sentences, and not one of them, not a single one, was ever addressed to a house.

I take out the pen.

I write the number first — 207 — because the ritual holds even when the ground doesn't. Then I stand there, pen hovering, and understand that whatever sentence comes next will not fit on the page, will not fit in the warehouse, will not stay behind my sternum where the rest of them live quietly rotting.

The yellow paper flutters slightly under the vent's breath, the way paper does when it has news.

Three weeks from Saturday, they will sell the house where I learned every silence I own.
