## 7. Loyalty Is a Locked Room

Ilse Brandt kept her apartment the way she kept her reputation: curated, dustless, with nothing in it that could contradict her. The piano stood against the north wall where the light wouldn't warp its tuning. On the shelf above it, arranged by height, were the scores she had written — or completed, which was a different word, and one she had learned to say only in the privacy of her own head.

She was drinking coffee she didn't taste when the knock came. Three raps, even, unhurried. Not a courier. Couriers knocked twice and left.

Through the peephole: a man in a dark coat, gray at the temples, standing with the particular stillness of someone who has rehearsed this moment on the stairs. She recognized him before she opened the door — the janitor from Hall C, the one who had corrected young Tomas Brandwein's bowing on her quartet's second movement, the correction so precise it had traveled back to her within a day, carried by a frightened student who said, *He knew what Marta wanted there. He said "wanted," not "wrote."*

*Wanted.* She had turned that word over for a week like a stone with something underneath it.

"Mr. Kavanagh," she said, opening the door. "The night staff usually calls ahead."

"I'm not here about the floors."

"No," she agreed, stepping aside. "I didn't think you were."

He crossed her threshold the way a man crosses ice he doesn't trust — weight tested, then committed. He looked at the piano first. People always did; she'd placed it to be looked at. But then his eyes went past it, up to the shelf, and stopped on the folder at the end of the row. Manila, soft-cornered, older than everything else in the room. She had told herself a hundred times to move it into the drawer. She never had. Some part of her wanted it visible, the way a wound wants air.

"You've been listening," she said.

"To the radio. To your students." He took off his coat without being asked and folded it over his arm, an old-fashioned courtesy that sat strangely on a man holding a mop bucket eight hours a night. "To the third movement, mostly. There's a ritardando in bar sixty-two that isn't in any published edition."

"It's in mine."

"It's in hers." He said it quietly, without triumph, which was worse than if he'd shouted. "Marta used to slow there because Halina's kitchen clock ticked loud and she wrote against it as a girl. It got into her hands and stayed. No score would tell you that. Only someone who heard her play would know to write it down."

Ilse set down her cup. Her hands were steady; she made them steady, the way she made everything steady. "Who are you?"

"Edmund Kavanagh."

The name landed in the room like a dropped glass. She knew it — of course she knew it. Every student who had ever passed through Halina Vogel's studio knew it, the way you know the name of a ghost in your own house. The prodigy who walked out of the conservatory twenty years ago and out of music entirely. The scandal that no one explained to the younger students but everyone whispered about. And beneath all of it, threaded through every anecdote Halina had ever told in her lucid hours: *Marta loved him. Marta played for him. Marta died.*

"You're the janitor," Ilse said.

"I'm very good at floors."

"Why now? Why come to me?"

"Because you're going to stand on a stage in three weeks and take a bow for music that isn't yours, in front of Halina, who won't remember why she's crying." He hadn't raised his voice. That was the frightening thing. "And I needed to know, before I decided what to do about it, whether you stole her or saved her. Those aren't the same crime. I've been trying to figure out which one you committed."

* * *

She should have lied. She was good at lying; she had built a career on the load-bearing lie that the archive was hers by gift and the completion hers by right, and the architecture held because no one had ever leaned on it. But Edmund Kavanagh stood in her living room with his coat folded over his arm like a man at a funeral, and she found herself telling the truth in the flat voice of someone reading a deposition.

"Halina gave me the boxes." She turned toward the shelf but didn't touch the folder. "Three years ago. She was already losing words — she'd call me Marta sometimes, and I stopped correcting her, God forgive me, because when she thought I was Marta she talked more freely. Sketches, fragments, a movement and a half of the quartet in her hand. Half-finished. Unplayable as it stood. And one afternoon she put the whole archive in my arms and said—" Ilse paused. She had repeated this sentence to herself many times, polishing it, and it had never once sounded clean. "She said, *Take them. Someone should finish what she started.*"

"She was ill."

"She was generous. At the time, I believed both things equally." Ilse met his eyes. "I didn't steal, Mr. Kavanagh. I salvaged. There is a difference, and I have clung to it like a rail in dark water. Marta left forty pages of intention and no resolutions. I found the resolutions. I studied under Halina for six years; I know her harmonic language better than anyone alive except you, apparently. I completed the quartet. And then I let the world believe the whole of it was mine, because half a legacy is worth nothing and a whole one is worth everything, and because—"

"Because no one stopped you."

"Because no one *could* stop me. Marta was dead. Halina was forgetting. You were—" She gestured, and hated her own hand for the gesture. "Gone. Twenty years gone. There was no one left to claim her, so I claimed her. Or her work. I've never been able to separate the two in my own mind, which I suspect is the closest thing to a confession I'm capable of."

He was quiet for a long moment. Rain had started against the window, soft, indifferent.

"The corrections," he said finally. "Bar sixty-two. The viola doubling in the coda. You wrote those from her sketches?"

"From her sketches and from Halina's humming. Halina hums the pieces she can't name. I transcribed years of it." A bitter almost-smile. "You could say my entire career is built on an old woman's memory leaking through the cracks."

"And the parts that are purely yours. The fourth movement's ending. The resolution of the fugue."

"Mine." She said it firmly, and then, because he was still looking at her with that terrible patience: "Mostly mine. Built on her bones. I don't know how to weigh that. Neither do you. Don't pretend you do."

* * *

Here was the thing Ilse understood about temptation, having studied it professionally for most of her adult life: it never arrived looking like ruin. It arrived looking like tidiness. Like a door closing gently on a messy room.

She offered it to him now, and she watched herself offer it, distantly, as though reviewing a performance she'd already committed to memory.

"There's a version of this where we both win," she said. "Co-credit. *Quartet in D minor — Marta Vogel and Ilse Brandt.* Your name beside hers, restored after twenty years, in the hall where they buried your career. The conservatory would eat it alive — the returned prodigy, the posthumous collaboration, the redemption arc. They'd give you a studio by autumn. And in exchange, the origin of the archive stays between us. Halina gave it to me. That's true. How lucid she was when she gave it — that stays unexamined. My completion stands as completion, not exhumation. Nobody is prosecuted. Everybody is forgiven."

She watched him receive it. She watched the want move through him — she saw it in the way his jaw shifted, in the sudden attention of his hands. He wanted his name back. Of course he did. She had counted on it; it was the hinge of the whole offer. A man who mopped floors beneath the windows of the building that once celebrated him had spent twenty years wanting exactly one thing, and she was holding it out to him wrapped in ribbon.

"And Marta?" he said.

"What about her?"

"Her name goes first. Her sketch goes in the program notes. The archive goes to the conservatory library, catalogued, public. Halina's role acknowledged while she can still hear it."

"Yes. All of that. I'll sign whatever you draft."

"And you let me decide what the truth costs you."

Ilse laughed — one short note, genuine despite itself. "You drive a hard bargain for a man in orthopedic shoes."

"I'm told I used to be harder."

*But you'll take it,* she thought, watching him. *You'll take it, because it's the only door out of the locked room, and men who have lived in locked rooms don't ask about the lock.* She felt the relief rising in her already, the sweet chemical flood of a crisis resolved, the quartet safe, the gala safe, the story sealed with two names instead of one — a better story, actually, a richer lie, a lie with a moral at the center of it. Everyone would weep. Everyone would applaud. And somewhere under the applause, the truth would sit in a drawer like the manila folder, soft-cornered, never moved, never opened.

He picked up his coat. He unfolded it slowly, and did not put it on.

"My silence ended her," Edmund said, to the window, to the rain. "Whatever you think you know about that night — whatever Halina told you — it's worse than that, and it's mine, and I have paid for it in the only currency I had, which was absence. Twenty years of it." He turned to face her. "So understand that I know precisely what a clean lie is worth. I bought one once. The price was everything."

"Then take the honest version," Ilse said. "It's on the table."

"The honest version has your career in it. And Halina's last months spent understanding, on the days she's lucid, that her daughter's work was parceled out like an estate." He shook his head, and something in his face frightened her — not anger. Grief with a decision inside it, the same thing she'd heard in the radio announcer's voice the morning the quartet premiered, announcing a dead woman's music under a living woman's name. "I need to think. Don't call me. Don't send anything. If I'm going to burn this down or sign it, I'll do it standing in Hall C, where I do my best thinking."

He let himself out. She listened to his footsteps descend the stairs — even, unhurried, a man who had rehearsed leaving too.

Ilse stood alone in her curated room. The rain thickened against the glass. After a while she took the manila folder down from the shelf, opened it, and looked at Marta's handwriting — the slanting, impatient script, the bars abandoned mid-phrase, the little marginal note in pencil beside the unfinished coda that Halina had never been able to explain and that Ilse had never been able to forget:

*Tell E. that the silence was the answer.*

Below it, the page simply stopped.

Ilse closed the folder. In three weeks the gala. In three weeks, either the lie with two names, or the fire.

She poured the coffee out and began, very quietly, to practice.
