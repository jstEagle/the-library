## 2. Behind the Wardrobe

The house had been waiting for him the way old dogs wait — patiently, with a kind of reproachful dignity, as if to say: *you could have come sooner, and also, I forgive you.*

Casper stood at the chain-link fence at the end of Marrow Lane and looked up at it. Number 9. The windows were boarded now, the paint gone the color of weak tea, but the shape of it was unchanged. The bay window where his mother kept geraniums. The drainpipe he'd climbed at eleven and fallen from at eleven-and-a-half. The roofline against the gray morning sky like a sentence someone had started and never finished.

A demolition notice was stapled to the fence, three copies deep, curling at the edges. **NOTICE OF INTENT. STRUCTURE SCHEDULED FOR DEMOLITION. OBJECTIONS MUST BE FILED WITHIN 14 DAYS.**

Fourteen days. He'd read the notice four times since it arrived in his returned mail — forwarded twice, delayed once, addressed to a man who hadn't lived on Marrow Lane in twenty-two years. The city had been patient too.

"You're not supposed to be here," said a voice behind him, and Casper's heart performed a small, undignified leap.

It was Mrs. Okonkwo from number 12, wrapped in a coat that predated the flood wall, holding a shopping bag like a shield. She'd been his neighbor when he was small enough to be given biscuits for good behavior. She still had the biscuits, apparently; she offered one through the fence.

"I'm not breaking in," Casper said, taking the biscuit because refusing felt ruder than trespassing.

"No," she agreed. "You're standing outside looking tragic, which is legal." She squinted at the house. "They're taking it down soon. Whole street, eventually. Progress, they call it. I call it eviction for buildings."

"Did anyone go through it? After my mother—" He couldn't finish. *After your mother died* hung in the air anyway, the way unfinished sentences do in coastal towns, where everyone finishes each other's griefs for them.

"Estate people came. Took the furniture worth taking. Left the rest." She patted his hand through the fence. "There's nothing in there but dust and memories, love. And dust is cheaper."

She went on toward the shops, and Casper stood there with his biscuit, feeling the particular foolishness of a grown man who has traveled across town before his shift to look at a house. Except he hadn't come to look. He'd come for one thing.

The photograph.

It lived — had lived, presumably still lived — on the second shelf of his mother's wardrobe, behind a stack of winter sweaters that smelled of cedar and disuse. A photograph of his father, taken the summer before he drowned: standing on the pier in shirtsleeves, laughing at something off-camera, one hand raised mid-gesture as if hailing a boat or blessing the sea. Casper's own copy had been lost years ago in a move, along with a box of books he still thought about with genuine resentment. His mother's copy would be there. Things in that house stayed where they were put. That had been the whole religion of the place.

He checked the street. Empty. The fence gate was secured with a padlock that had rusted into a suggestion rather than an obstacle, and it gave when he lifted it the way Mrs. Okonkwo had probably known it would, being the sort of woman who noticed everything and reported nothing.

The front door wasn't even locked. It simply stuck, as it always had, requiring the specific lift-and-shove that Casper's hands remembered without consulting him. The hallway exhaled around him: cold air, salt rot, the ghost of lemon polish under everything, like a perfume worn by someone long dead.

He stood in the hall and let the house have its moment with him.

There was the coat hook where his father's oilskin had hung, empty now, leaving a paler rectangle on the wallpaper. There was the stair rail he'd slid down exactly once, at nine, resulting in a broken wrist and a lecture that lasted longer than the healing. There was the kitchen doorway through which his mother had called his name every evening at six, in a voice pitched precisely between invitation and summons.

And there — this was the strange part, the part that made his skin prickle — there was the silence. Not the silence of an empty house. The silence of a house that had been *tidied*. Someone had swept. Someone had closed doors carefully. The demolition notice aside, the place felt less abandoned than *staged*, like a room prepared for a play no one ever performed.

"Don't be ridiculous," Casper told himself, in the tone he used on patrons who insisted the library was haunted. (It wasn't haunted. It was old. These were different conditions.)

The stairs creaked in the same places. Fourth step, seventh, the landing board that groaned like a ship — and of course it did, everything in this city groaned like a ship, the whole town was built on the understanding that the water would take it back eventually.

His parents' room was at the front, facing the harbor. The door opened onto dimness and dust sheets, and there it was: the wardrobe. Dark walnut, taller than his mother, older than both his parents put together, with brass handles worn bright by decades of the same two hands.

Casper pulled open the lower drawer first. Sweaters. Cedar smell, undimmed. He reached past them to the back of the shelf where the photograph should be, fingers walking blind through the dark—

—and found a cavity.

Not just space. A *cavity*. The shelf's back panel had been pried loose and set slightly ajar, and beyond it the wardrobe's shallow depth gave way to something deeper, a gap between the furniture and the wall itself, a pocket of darkness that had no business existing in a house whose walls he knew like his own teeth.

His hand came out holding letters.

Dozens of them. Bundled loosely with string that had rotted to the texture of old leaves, envelopes soft with age, addressed in his mother's handwriting — he knew it instantly, that upright, careful script, the way she crossed her t's like little flags — and every single envelope bore the same name.

**Elias Vann.**

Casper sat down on the floor of his parents' bedroom, in the dust, in his good coat, and held the bundle like something rescued from a fire.

Elias Vann. He turned the name over. Nothing. No uncle, no family friend, no name from the parish rolls of his childhood. His mother's handwriting, his mother's secret cavity, and a stranger's name, over and over, dozens of times.

He counted them without meaning to. Thirty-one letters. All sealed. None stamped. None delivered. The earliest envelopes had softened with age; the later ones were firmer, written closer together, the dates — when he dared check a few, turning them gently like eggs — spanning roughly fourteen months.

Fourteen months ending, when he checked the last letter with hands that had begun, absurdly, to shake, three days after his father drowned.

Three days after. And never finished — he could feel it through the paper, somehow, or maybe he only decided it later; the final letter was thinner than the others, half-written, abandoned mid-sentence, sealed anyway, as if she couldn't bear to look at what she'd been trying to say and couldn't bear to destroy it either.

The light through the boarded window had gone from gray to the color of old ivory. Casper realized he'd been sitting on the floor for the better part of an hour, and that somewhere below, the tide was turning, and that he was due at the library in ninety minutes, and that none of these facts had any authority over him whatsoever.

He should take the photograph. That had been the mission. One photograph, in and out, tidy, contained, the whole visit filed under *closure* and shelved accordingly.

Instead he sat in the dust holding thirty-one unsent letters addressed to a man who did not exist — who could not exist, because Casper Blackwood had spent his childhood in this house, in this hallway, under this roof, and he would have *known*. Wouldn't he?

Except.

Except that his mother had hidden them behind a false panel in a wardrobe, in a cavity deep enough that a boy searching for Christmas presents — and he had searched, thoroughly, annually, with professional dedication — had never found them. She had built this hiding place the way other people built garden walls. With intent. With time.

The past, Casper thought, is not where you left it. It moves. It digs new rooms while you sleep.

He took the letters. He took the photograph too, finally, finding it wedged deeper in the cavity, face-down, as though someone had turned it away — his father laughing on the pier, oblivious, forever. He looked at it for a long moment and then, feeling like a thief in his own inheritance, slipped it into his inside pocket, over his heart, which was beating in a manner entirely unbefitting a night librarian.

At the door of the bedroom he stopped. Looked back. The wardrobe stood with its drawer open, its false panel ajar, its secret spent.

"Who was he?" Casper asked the room, quietly, the way he asked questions of books he didn't understand yet — not expecting an answer, just opening the conversation.

The house, naturally, said nothing. Houses keep their counsel. But as he pulled the front door shut behind him and heard it stick home with its familiar sigh, Casper could have sworn the whole building settled — like a dog that has finally handed over the thing it's been guarding, and can rest.

* * *

He didn't shelve them. That was the first strange decision, and it took him days to understand why it mattered.

He arrived at the library at seven, an hour early, and instead of going through the ritual — coat, kettle, cart, the gentle machinery of the evening — he sat in his office with the bundle in the center of his desk, under the lamp, like evidence. Like a patient on a table.

Ruth, the day librarian, put her head in at seven-fifteen. "You're early."

"Mm."

"You're sitting in the dark."

"The lamp's on."

"The lamp is pointed at a pile of old mail, Casper, and you're looking at it like it owes you money." She came in, poured herself coffee from his kettle without asking, which was her right and her punishment. "What is it?"

"Family papers," he said. The lie was smooth and automatic and tasted, for the first time in his life, unpleasant. Ruth looked at him a moment longer than she needed to, then left, and Casper understood that something had shifted in the room's weather.

He told himself he'd read one. Just one, to settle the question, to confirm the mundane explanation — a pen pal, a cousin, a charity, a man his mother wrote to about church raffles. People hid boring things all the time. Half of secrecy was embarrassment over nothing.

He waited until the library emptied at midnight. He waited until the insomniacs had drifted out — old Pavel with his newspapers, the student who slept in the armchairs, the woman who came nightly to read obituaries and thank him, always, for keeping the lights on. He waited until the building was down to its night sounds, the pipes ticking, the sea two streets away doing its endless arithmetic.

Then he broke the string on the oldest letter, and unfolded the thinning paper, and read:

*I don't know how to begin this except to say that I have begun it eleven times already and burned ten. You will think me foolish. You have never once made me feel foolish, which is either your great virtue or my great blindness, and after today I no longer trust myself to know the difference. What I felt on the pier this afternoon was not nothing. I am forty-one years old and I am telling you, in a letter I will probably never send, that what I felt was not nothing.*

The pier. His mother. An afternoon. A feeling that was *not nothing*.

Casper set the letter down very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

Outside, the tide was coming in. Somewhere up the coast, a house he had just robbed of its last secret stood empty in the dark. And in his hands, in his mother's careful flag-crossed handwriting, the past had stopped being a place he remembered and become a country he'd never seen — one where his mother walked a pier in the sun and felt something enormous, and folded it small, and hid it behind the sweaters, and sealed it, and kept it, and never sent it to a man named Elias Vann.

Thirty more letters lay on the desk.

He picked up the next one.
