## 5. Mara

Mara Blackwood had a rule about her brother: she thought of him on Tuesdays, and only after four o'clock, when the courthouse emptied and the day's damage could be assessed without panic. It was not cruelty. It was triage. You learned early in defense work that you couldn't bleed for everyone at once, so you scheduled the bleeding.

It was a Tuesday, quarter past four, when Casper called.

She let it ring twice — long enough to seem busy, short enough to seem kind — and picked up. "Blackwood."

"It's me."

"I know. You're the only person alive who still says *it's me* like I'd have forgotten your voice." She capped her pen. On her desk, the Okafor file sat open to a page she'd read nine times. "I've got ten minutes."

"I found some letters," Casper said. "In the house. Behind Mom's wardrobe."

The pen cap rolled off the desk. Mara watched it go all the way to the floor and did not pick it up, because if she bent down she would have to come back up, and coming back up would mean having a face, and Casper couldn't see her face over the phone but he could hear one. He always could. It was his single talent, the only one he'd ever shown any ambition toward: hearing the thing underneath the thing.

"What letters?" she said, flat as a deposition transcript.

"Mom's handwriting. A whole box of them. They're addressed to someone named Elias Vann."

There it was. The name she hadn't said aloud since she was nineteen years old and had promised herself, in the solemn way nineteen-year-olds promise things they don't yet understand, that she never would again.

"Mara? Are you there?"

"I'm here." She stood and walked to the window. Four floors down, the parking lot was doing its usual late-afternoon ballet of lawyers and bail bondsmen, everyone circling for an exit. "Casper, listen to me very carefully, because I'm going to say this once and then I'm going to say it differently until you understand it. Put the letters back."

"Put them—"

"Back. In the wall. Or burn them, honestly, burning is cleaner. Then go home and shelve something."

"You don't even know what they say."

"I don't need to know what they say." She pressed two fingers against the glass, cool and steady, as if the window might transmit the gesture through the phone line. "I was there too, you know. You weren't the only child in that house. I just did my remembering earlier than you did, and I did it on purpose, which is more than I can say for you."

* * *

Their mother had locked the doors at dusk. That was the first thing, the thing Mara carried like a stone in her shoe for thirty years. Not locked them the way people lock doors — absently, ritually, a flick of the wrist while thinking about dinner — but locked them the way banks lock doors. Deadbolt, chain, the kitchen chair wedged under the handle on bad nights, which were most nights after the funeral. Twelve-year-old Casper had slept through it or slept around it; he had a gift for sleeping through weather, literal and otherwise. But Mara had been fourteen, and fourteen is the age when you start keeping records whether you want to or not.

She remembered the envelope, too. The second thing. It came six weeks after their father drowned, hand-delivered, no stamp, no return address, just cash inside — more cash than their mother had ever held at once, Mara was sure of it, because their mother counted it twice at the kitchen table with the blinds down and her lips moving, and then put it in the coffee tin where the flour used to be. Mara had watched from the hallway. Their mother had caught her watching, and what she'd said wasn't *go to bed*. What she'd said was: *"Some things are cheaper than other things, Mara. This is the cheap version."*

Fourteen-year-old Mara had understood maybe forty percent of that sentence. Adult Mara, who spent her days negotiating the price of silence for men who deserved worse, understood all hundred percent of it. Hush money didn't arrive by accident. Hush money arrived because somebody, somewhere, was afraid of noise.

And here was her brother, three decades later, standing in a condemned house with a box full of noise.

"Mom was scared," Casper said now, on the phone, in the tone he used for shelving facts. "That's what I keep landing on. Every letter, there's this — fear in the margins. Little notes to herself. *Don't write this down. Don't send anything.* Why would she write letters she was afraid of?"

"Because she needed somewhere to put it," Mara said, "and the coffee tin was full."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer I have that won't hurt you, so let's pretend it's an answer." She sat back down at her desk, because her knees had opinions. "Casper. I love you. I want you to hear that first, before the rest of it, because the rest of it is going to sound like the opposite."

* * *

They had not always been opposite. There had been a year — she could date it precisely, the year she turned sixteen and he turned thirteen — when they were a unit, a two-person conspiracy against the quiet of that house. They'd invented a game called *Sound Check*, played entirely in whispers: one of them would make a small noise, a spoon against a glass, a laugh bitten in half, and the other had to name its danger level. Spoon on glass: safe. Laugh: medium. The front door opening past dark: catastrophic. It sounds grim, and it was, but they'd laughed doing it, quietly, in the way of children who have located the comedy inside the bunker. For one year they were each other's witness.

Then Mara went to law school on scholarship and discovered that witnesses could become advocates, and advocates got to choose sides, and choosing a side meant you never had to stand in the middle of the room again, in the draft between what happened and what was said. She chose the side of procedure. Of record. Of the beautiful, airless idea that if you wrote everything down correctly, nothing could reach out of the paper and grab you.

Casper chose shelves. Same instinct, gentler architecture.

Their mother died on a Sunday in March, eleven years ago, and at the funeral Mara had watched her brother stand at the graveside with his hands folded like a man waiting for a bus, and she had known — known the way she knew a lying client on the stand — that he had not finished whatever book their childhood was. He'd just stopped reading it. And she had decided, with the cold mercy that was her signature move, to let him.

Now he was picking the book back up, and the demolition notice was his bookmark.

"Do you remember the man from the harbor?" Casper asked. "Elias Vann?"

"No."

"Mara."

"I said no. I'm a lawyer. No is a complete sentence; look it up." She rubbed her eyes. Through the window, the parking lot had nearly emptied, and the light was doing that October thing where it made everything look like evidence. "What I remember is a mother who triple-locked a door every night for six years and flinched at the sound of boat engines. What I remember is money in a flour tin and a woman saying *this is the cheap version* like she was grateful. Whatever you think those letters are going to give you — a villain, a confession, a tidy ending where the sad music swells and we all understand each other — I promise you the real thing is uglier and it does not resolve."

"So you do know something."

"I know the shape of something. The shape is enough. Shapes are how I make my living." She pulled the Okafor file toward her and looked at it without seeing it. "Here's what happens next, in my professional opinion. You take those letters to a lockbox. Not your apartment — a lockbox. You do not call anyone else from the harbor. You do not go back to that house. And on Thursday you come to dinner at my place, and we eat the lamb thing you pretend not to like, and you tell me you put them away, and I will believe you even if it isn't true, because that's what family is for."

A long silence on the line. She could hear the library behind him — that particular hush, the hum of old radiators, someone coughing two floors down. Her brother lived inside a building whose entire purpose was keeping things safe and still and unread. Of course he did.

"You're asking me to un-know it," he said finally.

"I'm asking you to postpone knowing it. Indefinitely. It's called a continuance, Casper, and it is the most honorable tool in the entire legal system."

"And if whoever paid Mom finds out I have them?"

The question landed exactly where she'd been refusing to let it land all conversation. She thought of the flour tin. She thought of *the cheap version*. She thought of the fact that the house had stood empty for eleven years and no one had ever broken into it, though kids broke into everything in that neighborhood, and the wardrobe cavity had waited behind its plaster like a secret with a salary.

"Then you'll wish you'd taken my advice," she said, "which is why I'm giving it to you now, free of charge, which should tell you something, because I bill at four hundred an hour."

He laughed — a real one, brief, the old Sound Check laugh — and something in her chest cracked along a seam she'd caulked years ago.

"Thursday," he said. "Lamb thing. I'll be there."

"Six-thirty. Bring wine that costs more than eight dollars; you're a grown man with a municipal salary, act like it."

She hung up and sat in the emptying office for a long time, the Okafor file open in front of her, unread. Then she did the thing she had trained herself never to do: she opened the bottom drawer of her desk, the one with the lock, and took out the folder she kept there the way other people kept cigarettes — for emergencies, and out of a hopeless tenderness toward the thing that was killing her.

Inside was a photocopy, twenty-two years old, of a harbor employment ledger. One line item, highlighted in yellow gone amber: **VANN, E. — terminated, effective immediately.** Dated nine days after their father drowned. Beneath it, in her own younger handwriting, the note she'd written during the one and only investigation of her life, conducted at nineteen with a library card and a grudge: *Who signs a termination the same week as a drowning, and why does nobody remember hiring him?*

She had gotten as far as the question. She had stopped at the question, because stopping at questions was how she'd survived, and she had built an entire life on the load-bearing principle that some doors stay shut.

Her phone buzzed. Casper, texting, because he was incapable of letting a conversation end: *Found a dockworker who remembers Vann. Going tomorrow.*

Mara stared at the message. She thought about the lamb thing defrosting in her freezer. She thought about her brother walking into the harbor district with a box of unsaid things under his arm, asking questions of men who had been paid, once, to develop amnesia.

Then she picked up the phone and dialed a number she hadn't dialed in eleven years — the retired harbor clerk, the one who kept the ledgers, the one who owed her a favor from a case nobody talked about — and when he answered, she said, in her steadiest courtroom voice:

"We need to talk about Elias Vann. Before my brother gets to you."
