## 8. Demolition Eve

Mara Blackwood had spent twenty years learning to read people the way other people read weather — a glance at the horizon of a face, and she knew what was coming. So when her brother's name lit up her phone at nine in the evening, she knew before she answered that the sky over Casper had gone the color of a bruise.

"I found them," he said.

"Found what." She said it flatly, the way you say *not guilty* when you already know your client did it.

"The letters. Mom's letters. There were dozens of them, Casper said, his voice doing that thing it did when he was trying to sound calm, which was like watching someone iron silk with a brick. "Behind the wardrobe. Addressed to Elias Vann."

Mara closed her eyes. In her office on the ninth floor, the city glittered below her like something breakable, which, in her experience, everything eventually was.

"Casper," she said. "Put them back."

"You haven't even asked—"

"I don't need to ask. I need you to put them back, or burn them, or bury them at sea, I don't care, as long as no one else ever sees them."

There was a silence on the line long enough to drive a truck through. Her brother had always been good at silences. It was the one inheritance they shared from their mother, who could go quiet for a decade if the mood took her.

"You knew," he said finally. Not accusing. Worse than accusing — wondering.

"Mum made me promise."

"When you were how old? Sixteen? Seventeen?"

"Fifteen," Mara said, because lying to Casper had never once worked, and because some part of her — the tired, traitorous part that had been awake since the phone rang — wanted him to understand. "She sat me down at the kitchen table with two cups of tea going cold between us, and she said, *Mara, whatever you find out about your father, you must never let it leave this house.* And I said, what do you mean, find out? And she said, *exactly.*"

* * *

Afterward, Mara sat in her dark office for a long time, doing what she did best: nothing, visibly, while everything happened underneath.

She thought about the year their father died. She'd been nineteen, home from university for the summer, old enough to notice things and young enough to be grateful for the family talent for not noticing them. The way their mother stopped answering the door after dark. The envelope of cash that appeared in the biscuit tin one week — real money, more money than their mother had ever held at once — and disappeared the next, paid out in careful installments to a landlord, a grocer, a school fees office. The man who came to the house twice, tall and soft-spoken, who called their mother *Mrs. Blackwood* as though the title were a joke only he got, and who left each time with their mother looking like a woman who had swallowed a stone and agreed to keep it down.

Mara had been studying law by then. She'd learned the word *hush* in its proper context — not the silence itself but the price of it — and she'd done what ambitious people do with unbearable knowledge: she filed it. Sealed it. Put it in a box behind a wardrobe inside herself and got on with becoming the kind of person who could never be bought, because she'd seen exactly what being bought cost.

And Casper — poor Casper, twelve years old, standing on that pier —

No. She didn't let herself think about the pier. That was the deal. That had always been the deal.

Her phone buzzed again. Not Casper this time. A number she hadn't seen in twenty-two years, though she recognized the area code the way you recognize a scar: the harbor district, where numbers went to be unlisted.

She stared at it until it stopped ringing.

Then it started again.

* * *

The man on the phone — he wouldn't give a name, which told her he assumed she'd know it, which meant he was probably right — spoke in the gentle, reasonable tones of someone discussing a drainage easement.

"We understand your brother has come into possession of certain correspondence," he said. "Family papers. Sentimental items. We'd hate for anything so personal to become, ah, public during what is already a difficult time for the neighborhood."

"Who is *we*?" Mara asked, though she knew. She'd known since the second sentence. Some voices you hear once at fifteen across a kitchen table and never lose.

"A group of people interested in the city moving forward," he said. "In demolition meaning demolition. Walls coming down, histories coming down with them. You're a practical woman, Ms. Blackwood. A respected officer of the court. I imagine you can appreciate that some records are better left unfiled."

Here was the moment. She felt it arrive the way she felt verdicts arrive — a stillness first, then the weight.

Because here was the arithmetic she'd been running all evening, the cold ledger version: her career against her brother. Twenty years of careful, honorable, armored work — the pro bono hours, the reputation for incorruptibility built brick by brick precisely so that no one could ever do to her what had been done to her mother — weighed against one insomniac librarian who kept other people's griefs shelved because he couldn't bear his own. If she stayed silent, Casper might be frightened off. He might not. He was their mother's son; he'd inherited the stubbornness along with the silences. And if he wasn't frightened off, then the men who had once paid a widow in biscuit-tin installments would handle him the way they handled problems, and Mara would attend another funeral knowing, and filing, and sealing.

If she acted — if she called the police, made a report, put names in the record — then the record would have her name in it too. *Blackwood.* The daughter. The witness-adjacent. The woman whose testimony, if it ever surfaced properly, would unravel a case older than her career and drag half the harbor's respectable men into the light, along with every favor they'd ever been owed.

Duty said: stay clean. Stay sealed. Stay behind the wardrobe.

Love — the embarrassing, unfashionable, twenty-years-dormant love she'd been pretending was just an old ache — said: he's twelve years old on that pier, and he's been holding it alone ever since, and you are not going to let them make him hold it alone one more day.

"Mr.— whoever you are," Mara said, "I'm going to give you some free legal advice, which is more than you deserve. Anything my brother possesses, he possesses lawfully. Any attempt to intimidate him, or me, or anyone connected to us will be documented, reported, and pursued with a thoroughness that will ruin several of your mornings. Do you understand me?"

A pause. Then, pleasantly: "Demolition is tomorrow, Ms. Blackwood. Best get your affairs in order."

He hung up. She wrote down the number anyway, dated it, timed it, and noted the phrase *best get your affairs in order* verbatim, because she was, whatever else, still a lawyer, and lawyers know that threats are gifts if you wrap them correctly.

Then she picked up the phone again and dialed a different number entirely.

* * *

Detective Inspector Soraya Okonkwo had once been Mara's opposing counsel in a housing case, and they had developed the wary mutual respect of two women who'd each tried to outflank the other and settled for admiration. They met at a late-night café near the station, because Mara didn't want her office cameras catching this, and because some conversations belong in places with bad coffee and no history.

Soraya listened without interrupting, which was her gift. When Mara finished — the letters, the call, the number, the phrase — she turned her cup slowly on its saucer.

"You understand what you're handing me," she said. "This isn't a noise complaint. If I open this file, it opens *you*. Your mother's payments. Your brother as a child witness. Everything."

"I've spent my whole life keeping files closed," Mara said. "It turns out that's just hoarding with better lighting."

Something moved across Soraya's face — not quite a smile, but the place where one would live. "Your brother knows you're doing this?"

"He'll find out." Mara looked at her hands. They were steady, which surprised her; she'd expected them to shake, expected the whole architecture of her composure to rattle. But it seemed that a decision, once truly made, weighed nothing at all. It was the *not* deciding that had been heavy all these years. "That's rather the point. He's spent his life thinking everyone leaves him to carry things alone. I'd like to correct the record before the building comes down."

"And if this goes badly? If these men are protected, if the file gets buried, if—" Soraya paused, professional honesty winning out. "You could lose everything you've built."

Mara thought about their mother, who had chosen fear and gotten thirty years of locked doors. About Casper, who had chosen silence and gotten a life lived in the dead hours between midnight and morning. About herself, who had chosen duty and gotten a beautiful apartment she was never homesick for.

"Yes," she said. "Probably. But here's the thing nobody tells you about duty, Soraya. It's supposed to bend toward people. That's what it's *for*. When it stops bending, it isn't duty anymore. It's just a very tidy way of hiding."

Soraya took out a notebook — an actual paper notebook, which Mara found quietly reassuring; some truths deserved ink.

"Tell me everything," she said. "Start with the pier."

And Mara, for the first time in twenty-two years, opened the wardrobe.

* * *

It was past two in the morning when she finally called Casper. He answered on the first ring, which meant he hadn't slept, which meant he never would.

"They came to me tonight," she said. "The men who paid Mum. They know about the letters."

She heard him breathe. Heard, beneath it, the particular hush of the library around him, the shelves holding their breath.

"Mara—"

"I've gone to the police. There's a detective named Okonkwo; she'll want to talk to you before morning. Casper — listen to me. Whatever happens tomorrow, when that house comes down, you don't owe those walls your silence anymore. None of us do." She laughed, short and unsteady. "God. Twenty years of telling clients the truth sets them free, and it takes me until now to believe it."

"It's not free," Casper said quietly. "It costs."

"Yes," Mara said. "But we can afford it together."

On the other end of the line, in the sleeping library, in the condemned house waiting for dawn like a held breath, her brother began — at last, in a voice cracking like ice in spring — to tell her what he saw on the pier.

And three streets away, a car with its lights off rolled slowly past the Blackwood house, counting the windows that were still lit.
