## 2. A Name Nobody Uses

My family does grief the way we do everything else: efficiently, in dark colours, with an eye on the clock. By eleven-fifteen at the Sint-Guidon church in Anderlecht, my mother had already checked her watch four times, my uncle Dirk had shaken hands with roughly forty strangers whose names he'd absorb and discard like breath on glass, and I had been positioned—there is no other word for it—at the end of the receiving line like a piece of furniture someone had inherited and didn't quite know where to put.

"You must be Hazel," said a woman in a grey coat, gripping my hand before I could confirm or deny.

"I'm the piano tuner," I said, which is what I say when I don't want to be anyone's anything.

She was perhaps seventy, small and upright, with silver hair cut short as a hedge and eyes the colour of strong tea. She held my hand a half-second longer than Belgian politeness required, then released it and turned to my mother with a smile so composed it could have been issued by a government office.

"Madame Vermeulen," she said. "I am sorry for your loss."

The word landed in our little cluster of black wool like a dropped glass.

My mother blinked. "I'm sorry—what did you call me?"

"Vermeulen." The woman pronounced it the Flemish way, VER-muh-lun, soft at the edges, a word you'd find pressed between the pages of an old book. "That is your family name, is it not? It was your mother's."

"It's Ashgrove," my mother said, in English, loudly enough that two mourners by the pillar turned around. "It has been Ashgrove since 1968."

"Of course." The woman's smile didn't move so much as deepen, the way water gets deeper without changing its surface. "Names change. Houses change hands. Some things are more stubborn than either." She extended a hand to my uncle, who took it with the wary grip of a man accepting a fish he hadn't ordered. "Meneer Vermeulen. My condolences. Your grandmother was a woman of great discipline."

"She was," Dirk said slowly, "our *grandmother*?"

"Was she not?" A small tilt of the head, perfectly calibrated to suggest confusion while committing none. "Forgive me. At funerals one loses track of who belongs to whom."

And then she simply walked away—through the doors, down the steps, into the churchyard, her grey coat dissolving into the grey morning like sugar into tea.

There was a silence of the specific kind that families keep in reserve for moments when speaking would cost too much. My mother's jaw had gone tight in a way I recognised from childhood, from every time a neighbour asked about relatives in Belgium and received a two-word answer. My uncle was looking at the door through which she'd gone with an expression I couldn't file anywhere—not anger exactly, not fear. Something older than both. The look of a man who has heard a floorboard creak in a room everyone agreed was empty.

"Ashgrove," my mother repeated, to no one, as if reasserting sovereignty over disputed territory.

I said nothing. I had learned early that in my family, questions were a form of vandalism.

But my feet, which have always had better instincts than the rest of me, were already carrying me toward the door.

* * *

The churchyard sat behind the church like a thought nobody wanted to finish: leaning stones, wet grass, a row of pollarded limes dripping onto the gravel path. The woman in the grey coat stood some distance off, before a modest granite marker, her hands folded, her stillness so complete that a pigeon landed near her feet and seemed embarrassed about it.

I approached the way I approach unfamiliar pianos: slowly, listening first.

Up close she smelled faintly of lavender and something sharper underneath—ink, maybe, or old paper. The stone she faced read only a surname and two dates. No first name. I found that strange until I remembered that entire branches of my family tree had been pruned so cleanly that I'd grown up thinking the shape of the trunk was the whole tree.

"You followed me," she said, without turning.

"You called my mother by a name she's never used."

"And she reacted exactly as I expected." Now she turned, and up close her composure showed its seams—a tiredness around the mouth, the particular pallor of someone who has dressed carefully for a battle disguised as a funeral. "You are the grandchild. The tuner."

"People keep saying that like it explains something."

"Doesn't it?" She looked at me with an attention that made my skin prickle, the way a piano wire prickles before the felt hammer strikes. "A person who listens for a living. In this family, that must be like being a lit match carried through a paper factory."

"I don't know what family you think—"

"No," she said gently. "You don't. That is rather the point."

A bell somewhere began its flat midday count. She waited it out with the patience of someone accustomed to waiting out much larger things.

"My name is Lucienne," she said. "Lucienne Vermeulen. My mother was Margriet Vermeulen. And fifty years ago, the house on Rue de la Rosée—" she paused, watching my face, and whatever she saw there made her nod slowly, "—belonged to ours."

"The house," I said. My voice came out thinner than I intended. "You mean my grandmother's house."

"I mean the house where your grandmother lived. Yes." She smoothed the front of her coat, a small gesture, almost housewifely. "I did not come today to make claims. I came because my mother died in March, and I promised myself I would stand in a churchyard with that name spoken aloud once, properly, before I got too old to care whether it frightened anyone."

"Margriet," I said.

The name left my mouth and immediately behaved strangely in the air—as if it had been waiting a long time to be said in that postcode and now didn't know what to do with itself. Lucienne closed her eyes for just a moment. When she opened them they were brighter, and she blinked twice, briskly, refusing whatever wanted to happen next.

"You've heard it before," she said. "Or you haven't, and your face just told me that too."

"I haven't." I hesitated. "There's a photograph. In the house. Two girls on a step, and one of them—the back says—"

"*Margriet, 1943.*" She finished it for me, flatly, and for the first time her composure cracked along a visible line. "Yes. I know the photograph. There were two copies. One stayed in the house. One travelled."

"With your mother."

"With my mother." She looked past me, toward the church, where my family was emerging into the drizzle in their efficient black clusters, checking watches, summoning cars. "They will tell you I am confused. Grieving, perhaps. A fraud with good research. Your uncle especially—he has the face for indignation. Let him. But I will ask you one thing, tuner, and then I will leave you to your inheritance."

The rain thickened slightly, ticking on the limes. Somewhere beyond the wall a tram sighed past on wet rails, a long low note I felt in my back teeth.

"When you go back to that house tonight," Lucienne said, "put your hand on the radiator in the front room. The one under the window with the cracked sill. Listen to it properly—you'll know how. And ask yourself why a house that hums so honestly has kept one room silent for fifty years."

"That's not a question. That's a fortune-teller's trick."

"Yes," she agreed pleasantly. "We Vermeulens were always good with instruments."

She buttoned her coat, inclined her head to me with a formality that belonged to another century, and walked down the gravel path past my approaching relatives without a glance. My uncle stepped aside for her the way men step aside for weather.

"Hazel." My mother arrived at speed, breathless, furious in the controlled way that costs her most. "What did she say to you? What did she—did she give you anything? A card, papers, anything?"

"She told me her name."

"Her *name*—"

"Lucienne Vermeulen." I watched my mother's face as I said it, and I will spend a long time studying what happened there. Not blankness. Blankness I know; I grew up inside it. This was different—this was the look of someone hearing a sound from a locked room and realising, with horror, that they recognise the tune.

"Don't," my mother said quietly. "Whatever she wants, whatever story she's selling—Hazel, don't."

"Mother. Who is Margriet?"

And here is the thing I will carry out of that churchyard longer than any name: my mother did not say *who?* She did not frown, or laugh, or perform the innocent confusion that a person genuinely ignorant of a name would perform. She went still—all of her, even the rain running down her cheek seemed to wait—and then she said, in a voice sanded down to almost nothing:

"We're late. The car's waiting."

* * *

That night the townhouse did its usual work on me. I ate bread standing at the kitchen counter, ran my palm along the boiler pipe out of habit, and let the building murmur its long iron gossip—heat rising, water turning, the small honest complaints of a structure that had never learned to lie.

Then I climbed to the front room, the one with the cracked sill, and knelt beside the radiator under the window.

Every radiator in that house sings in the same key. I'd confirmed it my first night, palm flat against the iron, the way I check every instrument before I trust it. But this one—

This one was cold. Stone cold, in a house whose heating had been running all day, fed by pipes I could hear working diligently three metres below my knees. I traced the supply line with my fingers to where it entered the wall behind the skirting board, and there, wrapped in tape gone brittle as autumn leaf, the pipe simply stopped. Capped. Sealed. Deliberately, permanently silenced, decades ago, by hands that knew plumbing well enough to make silence look original.

I sat back on my heels in the darkening room, rain starting against the glass, and thought about a woman in a grey coat who listened to radiators the way I do, and a photograph of two girls on a step, and a mother who couldn't say a single name but couldn't quite hold her face together either.

The house hummed around me, everywhere except that one room, which kept its silence like a held breath.

In the morning, I decided, I would open the wardrobe properly. All of it. Every shelf, every seam, every place where wood might be hiding wood.

Because I had spent thirty years tuning other people's instruments, and I knew better than anyone what a muted string means.

It means somebody, once, couldn't bear the sound.
