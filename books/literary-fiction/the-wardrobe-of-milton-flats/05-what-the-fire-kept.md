## 5. What the Fire Kept

It took me four days to find her.

Four days of doing what I do best, which is nothing dramatic — no stakeouts, no trench coats. I sat at my kitchen table with a pot of coffee going bitter and a legal pad, and I made lists, because lists are how I keep the world from sliding. *Ida Corrigan.* The name was all I had, plus a grey wool coat and a key on a plain brass ring that I had not yet put in any lock because putting it in a lock would make it real.

The funeral home had a guest book. She'd signed it — *Ida Corrigan* in a hand that slanted hard to the right, as if she were always about to run — and beneath it an address in Fallowfield, a district I associated with bus depots and a pie shop my mother had once loved. I looked her up the way you look up anyone now, and found almost nothing: a decades-old listing as a seamstress; a hospital charity donation from 1996; a mention in a parish newsletter, 2009, thanking her for alterations to vestments. A woman who had spent her life hemming other people's things. There was something in that I understood completely.

I wrote it all down anyway. Four days. Then on the fifth morning I took two buses to Fallowfield and stood outside a block of flats so like Milton Flats it felt like a joke someone was waiting for me to get.

She answered on the third knock.

Up close, out of the grey coat, she was smaller than I remembered, and older — the funeral had given her a kind of architecture, grief holding her upright, and without it she seemed assembled loosely, a cardigan over a housedress, slippers, hair pinned back with what looked like a pencil. Her eyes went to my face and did the thing they'd done at the graveside: searched it for someone else and found him.

"You have his jaw," she said. "I keep forgetting. Come in before the cold does."

The flat smelled of lavender and something medicinal underneath, sweetish, like fruit going off. There were boxes along one wall, labelled in that same right-slanting hand: *LINENS. BOOKS. PAPERS — BURN.* I noticed that last one and decided not to notice it.

"I'm not staying," I said, standing in her doorway like a man at a counter he means to complain at. "I came for answers."

"Everyone says that." She shuffled toward the kitchen. "Nobody stays. Sit down, Wendell."

Hearing my name in her mouth stopped me more than any argument could have. She said it like she'd earned it. Like she'd said it before, maybe, in another life, to a boy who wasn't born yet.

I sat. She made tea I didn't want and set it in front of me anyway, and then she sat opposite with her hands folded on the table, and waited. That was the thing about her — she didn't perform. My father's whole life had been performance, forty years of a man pretending to be a man who had never heard of Milton Flats. This woman just sat there with her folded hands and let the silence do its work, and I realized she'd been letting silences do her work for a very long time.

"The key," I said, and put it on the table between us. "What does it open?"

"A wardrobe," she said.

"I know that much. *Ask him about the wardrobe,* you said. Well." My voice came out harder than I intended. "He's dead. He can't be asked anything ever again, which I suspect you knew when you chose your moment. So you'll ask me instead, or you'll tell me, or I'll walk out of here and you'll have come all this way, wept all those tears at a stranger's grave, for nothing."

"They weren't tears for a stranger," she said quietly.

"Then whose?"

She looked at the key for a long time. When she spoke again her voice had changed register — lower, flatter, the voice of someone reading from a document she'd memorized against her will.

"Your mother's name was Margaret Eastwick-Byrne. Everyone called her Peggy. She was thirty-four years old when the flats burned. She worked mornings at the bakery on Corben Street and evenings she sewed, same trade as me, though she was far better at it. She kept a wardrobe in the front room that she'd salvaged from her grandmother, and she hid things in it — money, letters, papers — because your father believed a household should have no secrets and she disagreed." She paused. "You'll want to know how I know these things. I know them because I lived above you. Top floor, back room, eleven months. Your parents took me in when the landlord forbade lodgers, and charged me four pounds a week and never once made me feel like a secret, even though technically I was one."

The tea steamed between us. Somewhere below us a door slammed and a dog started up and quit again.

"You're the lodger," I said. It wasn't a question. It had stopped being a question somewhere around the word *Peggy*, spoken by a stranger with my mother's private name in her mouth like it still fit her.

"I'm the lodger," Ida said. "And on the night of the fire, your mother went back inside to get me out. And she didn't come out. And that is the whole of it, except that it isn't, because there's the part about the door."

My hands had gone cold around the warm cup. "What part about the door."

She lifted her eyes to mine. They were pale grey, nearly colourless, and steady in a way that frightened me more than tears would have.

"When I came down the stairs — the back stairs, the only ones not burning — your father was at the bottom. In the doorway. Holding it open." Her voice stayed level, terrible, unhurried. "And when I passed him, he let go of the door. And it shut behind me. And there were people outside shouting that someone was still in there, and he stood in front of that door with his arm across it, and he did not go back."

The room tilted. Not metaphorically — I actually gripped the edge of the table, because the floor of her kitchen seemed to lean, the way a deck leans, the way ground goes when loyalty is the thing you're standing on and someone has just told you it was never ground at all.

"No," I said.

"Wendell—"

"No." I was on my feet. I don't remember standing. "That's not — you don't know what you're saying. You were half-dead with smoke, everyone was screaming, nobody knows what happened in a fire, it's chaos, people misremember—"

"I've had forty years," she said, "to check my memory. I've checked it every way there is. I dreamed it until I couldn't sleep, and then I dreamed it until I could, and it has never once changed. He held the door. Then he closed it."

"He wouldn't—" My voice cracked and I hated it, hated her for hearing it. "He loved her. Whatever else he was, whatever he became after — he carried her photograph for forty years. He never remarried. He never even *dated*. He lived in that flat like a man serving a sentence. Does that sound like a man who barred a door?"

"It sounds," Ida said, "like a man who barred a door."

The words landed with such simple weight that for a moment I couldn't breathe. She went on, gentler now, which was worse.

"Grief doesn't tell you what a man did, Wendell. Grief tells you what a man lost. Sometimes those are the same thing and sometimes—" she spread her hands, "—sometimes a man loses everything precisely because of what he did, and spends the rest of his life paying for it in the only currency he has left, which is absence. Your father paid in absence. I watched him do it. I got Christmas cards from him for six years after the fire, each one apologizing for something without naming it, until I moved and didn't leave an address, because I could not bear to be the thing he apologized to."

"Then why come back?" I shouted it, and the dog downstairs started up again. "Why come to his funeral? Why give me a key and a riddle and then drop *this* on me like — like a bill? What do you want from me?"

"What I've wanted since 1978," she said, and her voice finally broke, just once, a hairline fracture across all that steadiness. "To stop carrying it alone. Your mother carried it. Your father carried it. I carried it. Forty years, three carriers, and none of us ever set it down in the same room at the same time." She pressed her palm flat against the table, over the key. "I'm dying, Wendell. The doctors use longer words but that's the translation. I don't have time for you to believe me comfortably. I need you to believe me while it's uncomfortable, or not at all."

I turned away from her. I looked at the boxes marked *BURN* and thought about fires, about what burns and what gets boxed up instead, and I thought about my father's face at the end, in the hospital, when the morphine had loosened him and he'd gripped my wrist — my wrist, I realized now, the same gesture, a hand clamping down on a wrist — and said, clear as day, *"Don't let them open it."*

I'd assumed he meant his desk drawers. His accounts. The ordinary modest secrets of an ordinary modest man.

"Don't let them open it," I repeated aloud.

Ida went very still. "Open what?"

"The wardrobe," I said slowly, and the sentence assembled itself in my mouth with the horror of something long predicted. "It's in his flat. Her wardrobe. Scorched on one corner. Locked. I found it the day after the funeral and I haven't been able to — I put it in the spare room and I shut the door and I told myself I'd deal with it later. He said *don't let them open it* and I thought he was raving."

For the first time since I'd met her, Ida Corrigan looked afraid. Not of me. Of the thing itself, the object, sitting locked in a spare room in a flat in a city that had already burned it once.

"She hid things in that wardrobe," she said. "Money. Papers. Letters. If it survived the fire, then some part of her survived it too, and your father knew it, and he kept it locked for forty years rather than—" She stopped herself. Pressed her lips together. Started again, quieter. "There are letters in there with my name on them. I know there are. She told me once, near the end, that if anything ever happened to her, I should look in the wardrobe. I never did. I ran instead. I was very good at running."

The anger was still in me — I want to be honest about that, because honesty is the one inheritance I got clean. I was furious at her, at her flat voice and her checked memory and her certainty, furious at the sheer arithmetic of it: that this woman had lived and my mother hadn't, that survival could look like a barred door, that my father might be both the man who carried the photograph and the man behind the door, and that both men had worn his face at dinner tables where I ate my supper and asked nothing, asked nothing, asked nothing for forty years of Sundays.

But under the fury, something else had begun, quietly, the way water finds a crack.

Because here is what I knew, standing in that lavender-smelling kitchen with my mother's name in a stranger's mouth: my father had kept the wardrobe. Of everything he might have saved, everything he might have kept or sold or buried, he had carried a scorched piece of furniture out of the ashes of his old life and kept it locked beside him for forty years, and died gripping a stranger's wrist saying *don't let them open it.*

Guilty men destroy evidence. I'd seen enough of human nature in forty years of writing postcards to a ruin to know that much. Guilty men burn the letters.

They don't keep them. They don't guard them. They don't spend four decades within arm's reach of the one object that could convict them.

Unless the letters weren't evidence. Unless they were something worse. Unless whatever my mother had hidden in that wardrobe was a thing my father couldn't destroy and couldn't read and couldn't live without, all at once.

"I'm going to open it," I said.

Ida nodded slowly. "I know."

"Not because I believe you." I picked the key up off the table. It was warm from lying there between us, warm as if the argument itself had heated it. "Because I don't. Because I think you're wrong, or mistaken, or cruel, or all three, and the only way I'll ever sleep again is if that wardrobe proves it."

"And if it doesn't?" she asked softly. "If it proves the other thing?"

I stood there with the key in my fist and the ground still tilting under me, my father's jaw aching in my own face, and I heard myself say the truest thing I'd said in eleven years of postcards:

"Then I'll wish I'd never found you. But I'll open it anyway."

She rose, unhurried, and fetched her coat from the hook by the door — the grey coat, the funeral coat — and buttoned it with fingers that trembled only slightly, and said:

"Then take me with you. I've been running from that wardrobe since 1978, and I am done running."

* * *

We took the bus back across the city in a silence that wasn't comfortable and wasn't hostile either — something newer than both, two people strapped into the same falling elevator. She looked out the window at the streets sliding past and I watched her reflection in the glass and tried to see my mother's lodger in it, a pregnant girl of twenty-two climbing stairs past our door for eleven months, eating at our table, learning my mother's laugh through a wall.

"Peggy used to sing while she sewed," Ida said suddenly, not turning from the window. "Badly. She sang the same four songs and forgot the words to all of them and made up new ones. Your father pretended it drove him mad. He used to stand in the hall listening when he thought no one saw."

"That's not the man you described."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. That's the trouble with the truth, Wendell. It's never just one man."

The flat smelled of dust and my father when I opened the door — that smell of soap and wool and old paper that had outlived him by a week and would not last much longer. Ida stepped inside and stopped, very still, in the narrow hall, and I understood that she was smelling it too: the ghost of a household she'd eaten in, sung in, fled from.

The spare room door was shut. I'd shut it myself, five days ago, and told myself it was tidiness.

I put the key in the lock. It turned with a small, dry click, like a full stop.

Inside, the wardrobe stood against the wall where I'd left it, scorched corner facing the window, catching the afternoon light. Ida made a sound behind me — small, involuntary, the sound of a woman meeting a ghost she'd been promised didn't exist.

I reached for the brass lock at the centre of the doors, and my hand was shaking, and hers came to rest on my shoulder, light as ash.

"Whatever's in there," she said, "she put it there on purpose. For someone to find. Remember that."

The lock gave. The doors swung open onto darkness, and the smell that came out was camphor and paper and, faintly, impossibly, smoke — forty years old and still leaving.
