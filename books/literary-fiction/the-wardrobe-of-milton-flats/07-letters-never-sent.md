## 7. Letters Never Sent

**Wendell**

The hospital had a smell Wendell decided he could live inside: antiseptic laid over something older and sweeter, like flowers kept too long in a closed room. He came on a Tuesday because Ida Corrigan told him to come on a Tuesday, and he found that at fifty-three years old he was still obedient to women who gave instructions as if the world had already agreed to them.

She was sitting up when he arrived, the wardrobe key on a chain around her neck now — she'd had it drilled and threaded herself, she said, because hospitals lost things and she did not intend for this to be among them. On her lap, squared like a hymnal, sat the bundle of letters.

His mother's handwriting. He'd seen it on envelopes his whole childhood — birthday cards, notes in lunchboxes, the grocery lists she wrote in that slanted, urgent cursive as though the words might escape if she didn't pin them down. But these were different. These were addressed to *Miss I. Corrigan*, over and over, year after year, and none of them bore a stamp.

"Forty-one," Ida said. "I counted them again this morning. Forty-one letters, and not one of them posted." She looked up at him. "You sent four thousand postcards to an address that didn't exist. She wrote forty-one letters to a woman she believed was dead. Your family has a talent for it, Wendell."

"Don't," he said.

"I'm not accusing. I'm observing. There's a difference, though I'll grant it's narrow." She patted the chair beside her bed. "Sit down. We're going to read them."

"Out loud?"

"Out loud. Silent reading is how you keep things for yourself. I've spent forty years keeping things for myself and look where it's got me — a room with a view of the car park and a heart that's filing its notice." She held out the first envelope. "You read. My voice tires. And besides—" something moved across her face, quick as a bird's shadow, "—I want to hear what she sounds like coming out of someone who loved her without owing her anything."

He took the envelope. It was heavier than paper should be. The postmark space was empty; the date, written by hand in the corner, said *March 1979* — eight months after the fire.

"Dear Miss Corrigan," he read. "They tell me writing to the dead is a madness, but they also told me the fire was nobody's fault, so I have stopped consulting them."

Ida made a sound that was almost a laugh.

*The insurance man came again today. He asks questions with his hat in his hands, which is supposed to make me trust him. Where were you sleeping, Mrs. Eastwick-Byrne. Who else lodged here. Was there a second exit. I say no, no, no, and he writes it all down as if my answers were worth more than my house.*

*I keep your room as it was. The landlady complains but the landlady owes me nothing and I owe her less. Your hairbrush is still on the sill. I cannot bring myself to move it. It seems that moving it would be agreeing to something.*

Wendell stopped. His throat had closed around the sentence.

"Keep going," Ida said quietly. "It gets worse before it gets better. Most true things do."

*If you are somewhere and can hear me, I am sorry. That is the whole letter, really. Everything else is just furniture around it. I am sorry. I am sorry. I am sorry.*

Yours, M. Byrne.

The ward hummed around them — a trolley rattling past, a phone ringing unanswered two rooms down. Wendell realized he was gripping the letter hard enough to crease it and made himself loosen his fingers.

"She thought you were dead," he said.

"For eight months, apparently. Then the next one will tell us why she changed her mind." Ida nodded at the bundle. "Go on. Second one. June '79."

***

**Ida**

The second letter was shorter, and harder.

*Dear Miss Corrigan,* Wendell read. *A woman from the old neighbourhood saw me at the market and said, "They never found her, did they." Not a question. A kind of weather report. I said no, they never did, and she clicked her tongue and bought her onions.*

*I have started dreaming about doors. In every dream there is a door and I know what is behind it and I know what will happen if I open it, and every night I open it anyway. You would think a person could learn.*

*The reason I write again after saying I wouldn't: the police came back. They are asking about the night itself now, not the building. They asked my husband where he was standing. I watched him answer and I could not tell you if he lied. That frightens me more than the fire did — that I lived beside a man for nine years and cannot read him on the one subject that matters.*

*If you are alive, stay un-found. I mean that kindly. Some things are safer in the ground.*

M. Byrne.

Ida listened with her eyes half-closed, and it was strange, hearing her own history read back to her in a dead woman's voice through a stranger's mouth. She remembered March of '79 dimly — she'd been in Hull by then, or Leeds, some city with wet streets and a name she used that wasn't hers. She remembered the hairbrush on the sill. She had forgotten the hairbrush until this moment and now it was so vivid she could feel the bristles.

"You knew she thought you were dead," Wendell said. "When you finally contacted her."

"I didn't contact her." Ida opened her eyes. "That's the thing you keep getting wrong, and I don't blame you for it. I never wrote to her. I never rang. I came back once, in '81, stood across the street from the new place you lived, and turned around and went home. She found *me*. Nineteen eighty-four. A letter arrived with no return address, just my name spelled correctly, which only three people in England could do."

"And you didn't tell me any of this at the funeral."

"At the funeral you wanted a villain. I could see it. You'd brought one with you in your pocket, ready to hand it to me or take it back, whichever hurt more." She reached for the third envelope. "Read the one from '84. Then we'll talk about villains."

***

**Wendell**

The 1984 letter was on different paper — better paper, the kind his mother would have called *good*, saved for occasions.

*Dear Miss Corrigan,* he read. *I know you are alive because grief is a debt collector and mine has just been paid in part. A nurse from the burns unit wrote to me — do you remember Nurse Okafor? Neither did I, until she described a patient who survived a house fire in '78 and would not give her real name. She described your hands. I would know those hands anywhere; you mended my blue dress with stitches finer than the factory's.*

*I am not going to ask where you are. I understand hiding better than most people; I have been hiding inside my own kitchen for six years.*

*But I must ask one thing, and then you may burn this letter and I will consider the transaction complete. What happened at the door? You were the last one out. My husband was the last one behind you. I have asked him and asked him and he gives me the same sentence every time, word for word, like a recording: "There wasn't time." Six years of "there wasn't time."*

*Tell me if there wasn't time. Or don't, and I will know.*

M. Byrne.

The ward was very quiet. Somewhere a machine kept time for somebody.

"She never got an answer," Wendell said. His voice came out flat, unfamiliar. "Did she?"

"No."

"Because there *wasn't* time?"

Ida looked at him for a long moment. Outside the window, a gull hung motionless over the car park, riding air that wasn't going anywhere.

"There was time," she said. "That's the whole horror of it, Wendell. There was time. What there wasn't was—" she searched, and he watched her refuse several words before settling on one, "—room. For what he chose. Read the fifth one. She worked it out herself, eventually. Women usually do. We just work it out alone, which is the other crime."

***

**Ida**

He read the fifth letter standing up, as if sitting had become impossible. She understood that. Some sentences require you to be on your feet.

*Dear Miss Corrigan. I have stopped beginning these letters with apologies, since apologies imply the possibility of repair and I am no longer sure what is broken can be located, let alone mended.*

*I have worked out what happened at the door. I won't put it in writing. Even unsent, even hidden in a wardrobe, some things should not exist in ink. But I want you to know that I know, and that knowing has not killed me, though it has rearranged the furniture of me considerably.*

*Here is what I cannot work out, and it keeps me up past the sleeping hours: whether he barred that door because he is a coward, or because someone made it necessary. There is a difference between a man who saves himself and a man who is made to choose, and I have been married to this man for fifteen years and I still cannot tell you which one walked out of that fire.*

*If you ever read this — and I think, somehow, that you will — you are the only person alive who knows which one he is. I am asking you to tell me. I have been asking for six years. This is the last time I will ask.*

M. Byrne.

Wendell lowered the page. His hands were steady — she noticed that, catalogued it, filed it away with everything else about him. Steady hands, careful face, a man who had organized his grief into drawers decades ago and dusted them weekly.

"She asked you," he said. "Directly. And you never answered."

"I answered the only way I could." Ida touched the chain at her neck, the little key warm against her collarbone. "I stayed silent. Silence is an answer, Wendell. It's just one that takes forty years to arrive."

"That's not an answer. That's a cruelty."

"Yes," she agreed pleasantly. "It is. Welcome to the family business."

He flinched. She hadn't meant it as a blow — or she had, a small one, the kind you administer to check whether a limb still works. It worked. He sat back down.

"Your mother spent nineteen years writing to me," Ida said, "and I spent those same years not answering, and your father spent them not speaking, and you spent them mailing postcards to a ruin. Four people, four silences, one wardrobe." She gestured at the bundle. "We are not going to add a fifth silence. So here is what we'll do. We'll read them all. Every one. Today, tomorrow, however many visits it takes. And when we reach the ones where she asks her question — and she will ask it again, she was not a woman who mistook persistence for rudeness — I will answer it. Out loud. To you first, because you've earned it secondhand and I haven't the strength left to earn anything firsthand."

"Why not just tell me now?"

"Because the letters are the road to it. Skip the road and you get the destination without the journey, and people who get destinations without journeys do terrible things with them." She smiled, and knew it was the smile that had unsettled him at the funeral. "Also, frankly, because I'm a coward in my own way. Everyone in this story is. That's rather the point of it."

***

**Wendell**

They read until visiting hours ran out. Letter seven, from 1987, contained a recipe for soda bread ("in case you are somewhere with flour, which I pray you are"). Letter twelve, from 1991, reported that Wendell — *my son, who does not know you existed* — had left home and taken a job with an insurance firm, "which I mention only because the universe has a sense of humour and I have learned to report its jokes." Letter nineteen, from 1996, was a single paragraph: *Still here. Still sorry. Still yours in whatever this is.* Letter thirty-one, from 2003, mentioned the wardrobe — *I have moved it twice now and it follows me like a conscience with hinges* — and Wendell had to stop then, briefly, and stand at the window with his back to the bed while Ida pretended to sleep.

By the twenty-second letter he had stopped feeling like a man reading documents. He felt like a man eavesdropping on a marriage — not his parents' marriage, exactly, but a triangle of marriages: his mother married to her guilt, Ida married to her flight, his father married to his sentence. And himself, outside it all these years, posting little rectangles of truth into a void, believing he was the only one keeping vigil.

"We're the same," he said, somewhere around letter thirty-five. "All of us. She hid letters in a wardrobe. I mailed postcards to ash. He—" he gestured vaguely eastward, toward the cemetery, toward the flat he hadn't yet cleared, "—he hid whatever he hid inside his own skull for fifty years."

"Unsent truths," Ida said. "It's the family coat of arms. You should have it embroidered on something."

"It's not funny."

"No," she said, and the humour drained out of her face like water from a cracked basin. "It's the least funny thing I know. Which is why I laugh at it. The alternative is what happened to your father, and look how that ended — a man so full of unsaid things that when he finally died, the silence just poured out of him and filled the whole church."

He gathered the letters, squared them, tied the ribbon the way it had been tied. At the door he paused.

"The last one," he said. "Number forty-one. It's sealed. She sealed it and dated it — the week before she died — and never opened it herself, or you'd have opened it too."

Ida's hand went to the key at her throat.

"I know," she said.

"Forty years and you never opened it."

"I was saving it." Her voice was steady but her eyes had gone somewhere else entirely — down a stairwell, into smoke, toward a door. "Some things you have to be ready to survive twice."

He stood in the doorway, holding forty years of a dead woman's voice against his chest, and heard her say, softly, almost to herself:

"Come Thursday. And Wendell — bring the photographs. If we're going to walk through that night, we'll need to see who was standing where."

***

Thursday was four days away. Wendell spent three of them in his father's flat, finding the photographs, and on the fourth morning, standing in the hospital corridor with the shoebox under his arm, he understood that what he was carrying was not pictures.

It was evidence.

And Ida Corrigan, propped up in her bed with the morning light on her face, looked at the box and then at him and said, "Before we open that, you should know — whatever you find in there, your father was not the man in the last photograph. He was the man in all of them."

She nodded at the lid.

"Open it. Let's see which one you find first."
