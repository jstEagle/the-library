## 1. Tenant

The amuse-bouche arrives on a spoon the color of old ivory, and I write: *a whisper of smoked roe over crème fraîche, the salt arriving late, like an apology.*

It is a good sentence. I have been writing sentences like this for eleven years, and they have a machine-tooled perfection that editors describe, in their breathless way, as *incisive*, *luminous*, *devastating*. What they mean is that my metaphors are precise and my verdicts are final. What they do not know is that I could write them asleep, drunk, or dead, because for three years now I have not tasted anything at all.

The spoon goes into my mouth. The roe is presumably briny; the crème fraîche presumably cuts it with its cool fat. My tongue registers temperature, texture, the faint mechanical fact of pressure. It is like reading a letter written in a language I once spoke fluently and have since forgotten — I can parse the grammar, but the meaning is gone. Somewhere behind my sternum, a door has closed, and through it I can hear the party still going.

"Chef asks if you're enjoying," says the server, who is nineteen and has the earnest terror of someone whose livelihood depends on my mood.

"It's lovely," I say, and it is not even a lie, exactly. Lovely is a word about surfaces, and surfaces are all I have left.

* * *

The restaurant is called Saltglass, which is the kind of name restaurants give themselves when they want you to think of weather and honesty. The chef is twenty-six, a woman named Priya Nair with burn scars up both forearms like a story she's stopped telling, and everyone in the industry agrees this review will make her career or end it before it starts. My editor, Douglas, said as much on the phone this morning, his voice doing the thing it does when he wants something without asking: "Mirabel. This one matters. I need the full weight of you."

The full weight of me. I look down at my notebook, at seven courses rendered in immaculate prose, and I feel the specific shame of a translator working from a text she cannot read. Course four was a turbot poached in brown butter dashi, and I wrote that it *dissolved on the tongue like a rumor*. Course five was venison with a juniper glaze, and I wrote that it *arrived with the gravity of a confession*. These are true sentences in the way that weather reports are true. They contain no me whatsoever.

There is a technical term for what happened to me, or rather there isn't, because the neurologists gave up and used phrases like *functional* and *idiopathic* and *these things sometimes resolve on their own*, delivered in the gentle tone reserved for people whose bodies have made a decision their minds haven't authorized. Three years ago I sat across from my brother Theo at a table in a restaurant very much like this one, and he said something to me, and I ate dinner, and by morning the world's flavors had packed their bags and left without leaving a note.

I never told anyone. You cannot be a restaurant critic who cannot taste; it would be like being a piano tuner who is deaf, except worse, because at least deafness is legible. Mine is invisible, private, a betrayal of my own body that mirrors — though I do not let myself finish that thought — some other betrayal.

So I have become the finest mimic in the profession. I taste with my memory, which is vast and well-stocked. I remember what smoked roe does. I remember juniper, cold and resinous, like biting a winter forest. I assemble reviews from these recollections the way a widow sets a table for a guest who isn't coming, and no one has ever known, and the knowing is the loneliest part.

Course eight is a dessert of burnt honey and black sesame, and I write: *the bitterness held against the sweetness like a grudge.* Then I put down my pen, because my hand has begun to shake, and I realize I have just described my family.

* * *

Room 214 smells the way all these rooms smell: salt-damp plaster, old carpet, the ghost of cigarettes from decades when the hotel permitted such things. The Meridian Hotel sits at the crooked end of the promenade where the town gives up on pretense, and my mother lived here for the last six years of her life as though she were in witness protection from her own history. Room 214. She chose it herself. I never asked why, and now the question has nowhere to go.

Friday, the house closes. Not this room — the actual house, on Cormorant Lane, the one Theo and I grew up in, empty now for two years, sold to a developer who sent a man in a fleece vest to walk its rooms saying *load-bearing* and *open concept* in the reverent tone other men reserve for cathedrals. The lawyer, Ms. Okonkwo, needs my signature by Friday noon. Everything inside goes to the skip or the auctioneer. My job, this week, is to decide which memories have resale value.

I have been at it since ten. Cardboard boxes from the pharmacy, each one labeled in my mother's small upright hand — a hand so controlled it looked printed: KITCHEN, MISC., THEO'S ROOM (DO NOT), and then nothing, the instruction unfinished, as though even her labels had secrets.

Her clothes went first. A woman who owned forty identical gray cardigans turns out to be easy to pack. Her books went second, spines cracked at the same pages, Marguerite Yourcenar and a shelf of poetry I didn't know she read. It was the third box that slowed me — photographs, hundreds of them, sorted by decade into biscuit tins, and I had gotten through perhaps thirty when the radio on the dresser, which I hadn't touched, which I'd assumed was broken along with everything else in this room, crackled once and began to play.

Not music, at first. Static, the sea caught between stations. Then a melody.

I knew it before I knew I knew it. That is the only way I can describe it — the way you know your own name shouted from another room. Four notes rising, a fifth falling back, then that little turn at the end, the phrase that circles itself like a bird deciding whether to land. Theo's melody. Theo's song, the one he wrote the summer everything ended, humming it through the house on Cormorant Lane until it got into the walls, into the water pipes, into me. He never recorded it. He never performed it. He played it twice for anyone but himself — once for our mother in the kitchen, and once, half-finished, for me — and then he left, and the song left with him, and for nineteen years it has existed nowhere in the world except the inside of my skull.

And now it is coming out of a hotel radio in a room where my mother died, clear as a bell, note for note, including the turn at the end that Theo never finished writing.

I sit down on the bed, which complains under me, and I listen to a song that was never sung, sung.

The station identifies itself in a voice like warm syrup — *you're listening to the overnight hours here on WQST* — and plays something else, something ordinary, and I am left holding a photograph I don't remember picking up.

It is my mother. She is maybe thirty-five, standing in front of the house on Cormorant Lane, laughing — actually laughing, head thrown back, one hand pressed to her collarbone, a laugh so large it seems to have required a second person to cause it. And beside her, half-turned toward her laughter like a man leaning into sunlight, is a stranger.

He is tall, dark-haired, with Theo's exact smile, and I have never seen him before in my life.

I search the photograph for my father and find him absent. I search it for myself and find myself absent. There is only my mother, radiant in a way I have no memory of her ever being, and this man, and behind them the kitchen window where — I know this without knowing how I know it — the song was written.

The radio hisses. Downstairs, somewhere in the Meridian, a door closes. And the melody begins again in my head, uninvited, patient, pulling me backward through nineteen years toward a kitchen that no longer exists, where a boy I betrayed is humming, and a stranger I've never met is making my mother laugh like the world was a kind place after all.
