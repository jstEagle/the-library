## 2. The Song That Was Never Sung

The radio had been dead when Mirabel arrived. She was almost certain of it.

She had checked the room the way she checked everything now — methodically, without appetite. The clock on the nightstand ran four minutes fast and had stopped at some hour she hadn't bothered to read. The lamp's shade was the color of weak tea. And the radio, a squat brown thing with a cloth grille gone gray as moth wing, sat on the dresser with its dial frozen between stations, its cord coiled beneath it like something sleeping. No cord in the wall. She'd noticed that. She noticed everything; it was her profession to notice, even now, especially now, when noticing was all that remained of eating.

So when the music started, mid-afternoon, while she knelt in a nest of newspaper from 1987 wrapping her mother's good glasses one by one, Mirabel did what any reasonable person would do. She looked for the source.

There wasn't one.

* * *

She kept wrapping the glasses. Her hands knew the work: fold, tuck, nestle, tape. The glasses were cut crystal, wedding presents from people whose names her mother still remembered and whose faces Mirabel never had. Each one chimed faintly against the newsprint, a small wet sound, like ice settling in a glass across a quiet restaurant — and Mirabel catalogued the sound automatically, the way she catalogued everything, *high register, brief decay, faintly melancholy*, and then hated herself a little for doing it. Three years since she had tasted anything. Longer than that since she had tasted anything gladly. But the machinery of description went on running in her head like a factory after closing, lights off, machines still turning out product no one would ever buy.

*Delicate attack. Clean finish. Nothing underneath.*

That was how she'd described the tasting menu last night, in her notebook, in language so polished it could have been engraved on the menu itself. Eight courses. She had written about the scallop's *sea-sweet restraint*, the beet gel's *earthy whisper*, the verbena oil that arrived *like weather*. Her editor would have wept with pleasure. Any editor would have. It was flawless. It was also, she understood perfectly well, the literary equivalent of describing a photograph of food to someone who was starving. She had eaten nothing. She had tasted less. The words came anyway, obedient and hollow, and she signed the check with a pen that left dents in the page because she pressed too hard, always too hard, as if pressure could substitute for everything else.

The music played on.

It was coming from everywhere and nowhere — from the walls, from the radiator, from the space behind the plaster where old hotels keep their histories. A piano first. Then a voice, low and unfinished, humming more than singing, the melody circling back on itself the way a dog circles before lying down.

Mirabel's hands stopped over the crystal.

She knew this song.

Not the way you know a song on the radio — casually, collectively, the way everyone knows everything now. She knew it the way you know a scar. Because there was no recording of it. There had never been a recording. There had been one summer, one kitchen, one boy sitting at the piano they'd pushed against the wall so the door could open, playing the same eight bars over and over while their mother chopped onions and pretended not to listen, which was how she listened to everything.

Theo had called it *the song that wasn't finished yet*, and he'd said it the way he said everything that summer, with a grin that dared you to ask what he meant. He was seventeen. He wrote it in the kitchen because the kitchen was the only room in the house where nobody lied — or so he claimed, though by August Mirabel would learn otherwise, would learn it so thoroughly that the word *kitchen* would taste of iron for years afterward, if taste had stayed with her, which it hadn't.

*Eight bars.* She could have written them down from memory. She had tried once, years later, in a hotel room in Lyon, and the notation had come out wrong, flat, like a photograph of moving water. Some things refuse transcription. They live only in the bodies of the people who heard them, and when those people die or go silent, the songs die too.

Except this one wasn't dead. This one was playing, impossibly, cordlessly, from a radio that had no power, in a room where her mother had spent the last eleven months of her life not speaking of the past, and Mirabel knelt among the newspaper and the crystal and felt the floor tilt gently, like a boat taking a swell.

*Fold, tuck, nestle—*

No. Her hands wouldn't do it. Her hands were listening.

* * *

The melody moved into its second phrase, and Mirabel's whole body flinched, because the second phrase was new. Theo had never gotten past the second phrase. He'd play the eight bars, then stall, then laugh and say *tomorrow*, and tomorrow he'd play the eight bars again. The second phrase existed only as a promise. Their mother used to hum a version of it sometimes, wrong on purpose maybe, or wrong because she'd only ever heard him reject his attempts — and Mirabel had spent twenty years unable to decide whether the tune in her mother's humming was memory or invention.

But this — this was neither. This was the phrase as Theo must have heard it inside himself, resolved at last, completed, the note landing where he'd always wanted it to land. Mirabel recognized it the way you recognize, in a stranger's face on a train, the chin of someone you buried.

She stood up. Newspaper slid from her lap. A glass tipped and she caught it without looking, without caring, her attention somewhere else entirely, somewhere with yellow curtains and a ceiling fan that wobbled and a boy at the piano saying, *listen, listen, I've got the turn now, I swear I've got the turn—*

"Stop," she said aloud, to the room, to the radio, to whatever was happening. Her voice sounded strange in Room 214, small and official, the voice she used with waiters. "This isn't funny."

Nothing answered. The song continued, patient, unhurried, as if it had all the time in the world, as if it had been waiting eleven months in this room for her to arrive with her boxes and her tape gun and her flawless, empty vocabulary.

She crossed to the dresser. The radio sat exactly where it had sat. The cord lay coiled beside it, unplugged, its prongs bright with disuse. She picked the radio up. It was light as a loaf of bread. She turned it over. The battery compartment was empty — she shook it and something rattled, but when she opened the little plastic door there was nothing inside except a single dried bay leaf, curled like a comma, the kind their mother used in the soup.

Mirabel set the radio down very carefully, the way you set down something that might be dreaming.

* * *

Here is what she did not do: she did not leave the room. She understood, distantly, that leaving would be the sane response, that there were explanations — a neighbor's radio through the wall, a phone playing in some forgotten pocket, the building's bones conducting sound the way old buildings do. She constructed each explanation and set it aside like a folded napkin, and stayed.

Because the song was pulling.

That was the only word for it. Not the way music pulls, generally, sentimentally. This was specific. Physical. Each phrase opened a door in her and something walked through it before she could decide whether to allow it. The opening bars put her at the kitchen table, age twelve, doing homework she wasn't doing, watching her brother's shoulders move above the keys. The turn — that impossible, long-awaited turn — put her in the garden, later, much later, the summer going brown at the edges, Theo standing at the fence with his back to her, and herself saying words she had spent two decades refusing to remember in full.

The melody reached the place where the third verse should be, and stopped.

Just — stopped. Not faded. Stopped, the way a sentence stops when the speaker decides you've heard enough.

The silence afterward was enormous. Mirabel became aware of sounds returning in layers: traffic below, gulls, the tick of the stopped clock deciding, absurdly, to tick again. Four minutes fast. Still four minutes fast. Somewhere in the building a vacuum cleaner started up, mundane and merciless.

She looked down at the box she'd been packing. Her mother's handwriting showed on the flap in fading ballpoint: *KITCHEN — MISC.* Inside, beneath the crystal, lay a stack of photographs bound with rubber band, and Mirabel had seen them yesterday and passed them by, the way you pass a door you've decided not to open. Now she lifted them out. The rubber band crumbled at her touch, dry as the bay leaf.

On top was a picture she didn't remember, though it must have lived in this house all her life: her mother, young, younger than Mirabel was now, standing in front of the sea wall with her head thrown back, laughing — actually laughing, mouth open, unguarded, a laugh Mirabel had never once witnessed in forty-odd years of daughterhood. And beside her, hand resting on her shoulder with an ease that made Mirabel's chest hurt, stood a man she did not know. Tall. Dark-haired. Smiling at her mother as if she were the punchline to the only joke worth telling.

A stranger.

Her mother had never laughed like that. Her mother had folded herself smaller every year Mirabel knew her, until by the end she was mostly silence and hands and the smell of bay leaves. And here she was, luminous, mid-laugh, with a man who was not their father touching her shoulder like he had every right.

Mirabel turned the photograph over. On the back, in pencil, in a hand slightly different from the one on the box:

*E. and me, the pier, before.*

Before.

Before what?

Behind her, in the empty radio, in the walls, in the water-stained ceiling of Room 214, she could almost hear the eight bars beginning again — patient, unhurried, willing to wait — and Mirabel Zhou, who had made a career of knowing exactly what she was tasting, understood that she had just been served the first course of something she could not yet name.
