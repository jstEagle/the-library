## 3. Photographs of a Stranger

The photographs were at the bottom of the third box, beneath a nest of yellowed tea towels and a bread tin that still smelled faintly of rust and aniseed.

Mirabel had been sorting by category all morning — linens to donate, papers to shred, dishes to the church sale — with the brisk efficiency she brought to everything now, the efficiency that had replaced feeling the way scar tissue replaces skin. She had a system. Systems kept the hands busy and the heart unemployed.

But the photographs had no system. They arrived in no order at all: a beach holiday from 1978, her father squinting into the sun with his hat pushed back; Mirabel herself at six, gap-toothed on a pier; Theo as an infant, furious and red in a christening gown. And then —

Her mother.

Not her mother. A woman wearing her mother's face, wearing it loosely, like a coat borrowed for the evening. She was laughing — not the closed-mouth, apologetic smile Mirabel knew from thirty years of Sunday dinners, but laughing, head thrown back, throat bare, one hand pressed flat against the chest of a man who was not their father.

The man was lean where her father was broad. He had dark hair going silver at the temples and a way of leaning toward the camera as though he'd just said something worth hearing. In the photograph he was looking not at the lens but at her mother, at the laughing woman, with an expression Mirabel recognized because she'd spent three years reading it on the faces of couples in restaurants: the look of someone watching a person they love be happy, and being fed by it.

She turned the photo over. The ink had faded to the color of weak tea, but the handwriting was unmistakable — her mother's small, upright script, the same hand that had written grocery lists and birthday cards and *call your brother*:

*August. The Point. D— says I laugh like a gull.*

*D—.* Not even a name. An initial, guarded even in private ink.

Mirabel sat down on the floor of Room 214 without deciding to, the box between her knees, the sea pressing its gray shoulder against the window behind her. There were more photographs beneath the first. Her mother on a seawall, mid-laugh again, caught in the act of it. Her mother and the man — D— — standing before a door painted the blue of shallow water, his hand resting on her shoulder with a familiarity that made Mirabel's stomach tighten. Her mother younger than Mirabel was now, radiant in a way that seemed almost indecent, as if she'd stolen the light from somewhere and hadn't been caught yet.

In none of them did she look like the woman who had raised Mirabel. That woman had been careful. That woman spoke in half-sentences and apologized to furniture when she bumped into it. That woman had sat across from her children at dinner like a guest who wasn't sure she'd been invited.

*Who are you,* Mirabel thought, holding the laughing stranger up to the window light. *Who is this woman, and what has she done with my mother?*

The radio on the dresser had gone quiet since morning, but she found herself listening for it anyway, the way you listen for a sound you're afraid of. Nothing. Just the radiator ticking and the gulls working over the harbor.

She should put the photographs in the discard pile. That was the system. But her hands wouldn't obey, and so she sat on the fading carpet of Room 214 turning pictures over like a fortune-teller who didn't want to know what the cards said, and somewhere between one photograph and the next, the room began to dissolve.

* * *

It began with the smell. That was how the memories always came — not as pictures first but as smells, because smell was the sense that never learned manners, that walked straight through the front door of the mind without knocking. Aniseed and rust from the bread tin. Then something else: garlic hitting hot oil, the green bite of parsley stems, the particular sweetness of tomatoes cooked past patience until they gave up their acidity entirely.

The kitchen on Marlowe Street. August, because the windows were open and the curtains breathed in and out like lungs.

Mirabel was fourteen. She knew this because of the height of things — the counter hit her at the hip rather than the waist — and because Theo was seventeen, which meant he was insufferable, which meant he was happy. He stood at the stove with a wooden spoon held like a microphone, singing nonsense lyrics about their mother's soup, improvising, terrible, glorious.

*"Simmer down, simmer down,"* he crooned, *"your mother's got a secret and it's drowning in this town—"*

"Those aren't words," Mirabel said from the table, where she was supposed to be doing homework and was instead drawing mustaches on the models in her mother's catalogues.

"They're better than words." Theo spun, spoon outflung, flinging tomato onto the window. "Words are finished things. This—" he gestured at the pot, at himself, at the whole steaming golden afternoon— "this is still happening."

Their mother stood at the sink with her back to them, washing parsley, and here was the thing Mirabel had forgotten for twenty years and remembered now with the force of a physical blow: their mother was humming. Humming along with Theo's nonsense, low, off-key, her shoulders moving slightly, and when Theo changed key to test her she followed him, unerringly, as though the melody lived in her too and had only been waiting for someone to come dig it out.

"You know this song," Theo accused, delighted. "Mum. You *know* this song."

"I don't know any such thing," their mother said to the sink. But she was smiling. Mirabel could see it in the reflection of the dark window — the smile arriving on her mother's face like weather from another country, unannounced, unexplained.

"It's yours," Theo said. "I'm writing it for you. It's your song. Every family should have one."

"We have hymns," their mother said.

"Hymns belong to God. This belongs to us." He tapped his chest with the spoon. "Verse two goes—" and then he sang something wordless, rising, a phrase that climbed and climbed and refused to resolve, hanging there at the top of itself like a bird that had decided against landing, and their mother went very still at the sink, spoon dripping, and said, quietly, in a voice Mirabel had never heard before and would spend decades trying to name:

"Where did you get that?"

"I made it up," Theo said. "Obviously."

"No." Their mother turned around. Her face had closed — not angry, nothing so useful as anger — shut, like a shop pulling down its grate at closing time. "You didn't make that up. Nobody makes that up. Where did you hear it?"

Theo laughed, uncertain now. "Mum, I literally just—"

"Play it again," she said, and her voice cracked in the middle of the sentence like ice underfoot, and she gripped the edge of the counter, and Mirabel understood, even at fourteen, even without the vocabulary for it, that she was watching her mother stand in a doorway between two lives and feel the wind from both of them at once.

But Theo played it again. Of course he did. He was seventeen and the song was his and he played it again, softer, watching her, and their mother listened with her eyes shut and her lips moving around words she didn't say, and when it ended she opened her eyes and looked at Mirabel — directly at her, for once, as if Mirabel were a person and not a piece of the furniture of childhood — and said:

"Some songs are borrowed, love. You have to give them back."

And then she'd dried her hands and asked about homework, and the doorway closed, and the summer went on.

* * *

Mirabel came back to herself sitting on the floor of Room 214 with a photograph stuck to her damp palm and her heart going hard against her ribs.

The light had moved. An hour, maybe more, gone — swallowed whole, the way the memory had swallowed her, and she couldn't account for it, couldn't account for any of it. Her legs were stiff. Her eyes were wet without her permission.

She looked down at the photograph in her hand. It was one she hadn't examined yet: her mother and the man — D— — at a table outdoors, wineglasses, a guitar leaning against a chair. The man was holding the guitar by the neck, not playing it, just holding it the way you hold something you intend to pick up soon. And her mother was looking at him with her mouth open, mid-word or mid-song, and her eyes—

Her eyes were the eyes from the kitchen window. The weather-from-another-country eyes. The woman Mirabel's mother had been before she became careful.

*Some songs are borrowed. You have to give them back.*

Mirabel set the photograph down with exaggerated care, the way you handle something that might go off. Anger arrived late, the way it always did for her — first the shaking, then the heat climbing her throat, and finally the thought, fully formed, incandescent:

*He took her from us.*

Because that was the shape of it, wasn't it? Some man, some summer, some initial on the back of a photograph — and after him, the careful woman, the closed shop, the mother who hummed only when she thought no one could hear and stopped the instant she was caught. Whatever this D— had been, whatever had happened at whatever Point, he had taken the laughing woman away and left them the stranger. Left them the silence. Left Theo with a song he'd been told to give back and had refused, had carried it like contraband all the way to the end of his life, unwritten, unsung, unfinished.

Her brother had died with a stolen song in his mouth, and this man had put it there.

Mirabel gathered the photographs into a stack with hands that weren't quite steady. She told herself she'd sort them tomorrow, file them properly, assign them to the correct pile in the correct system. She told herself the deadline was Friday and feelings were not on the inventory.

Then, behind her, the radio on the dresser — dead all day, unplugged since spring, she was almost certain it was unplugged — cleared its throat of static and began, softly, unmistakably, to play.

The melody climbed. Climbed and climbed and refused to land.

Verse two. The verse their mother had recognized.

Mirabel turned around slowly, the stack of strangers' photographs pressed to her chest, and waited to find out what the song wanted from her next.
