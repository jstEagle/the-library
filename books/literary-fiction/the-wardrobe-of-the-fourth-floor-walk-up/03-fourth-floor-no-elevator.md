## 3. Fourth Floor, No Elevator

There is no dignified way to say this: I chose the café because it had chairs I could leave from quickly.

I had rehearsed the conversation on the métro — Line 4, standing, one hand on the pole, the other clutching a folder that contained the only proof I owned of my own existence. In my head, Oscar Joubert was a tall man with his father's jaw and his mother's silences, and he would listen to me the way people listen to weather reports: politely, without believing a word, and then go about his day.

In person he was shorter than I expected. Grief does that to people; it takes an inch or two off the top and gives it to the shoulders.

He arrived late, which surprised me until I realized he'd probably circled the block twice first, deciding whether to come at all. He sat down across from me like a man taking a seat in a dentist's waiting room — upright, cooperative, prepared for pain.

"Vivienne," I said again, because names are doors and you sometimes have to knock twice.

"Oscar," he said, as if we were being introduced at a party neither of us wanted to attend.

I ordered a coffee I didn't want. He ordered nothing. The waiter wrote down my order anyway, out of professional courtesy to the concept of hope.

"You said you have a right to be here," Oscar said. "At the cemetery."

"I did."

"That's not an explanation."

"No," I agreed. "It's a claim. Explanations take longer." I opened the folder. "You have somewhere to be?"

"I have a floor to mop at six."

"A man with priorities."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, more like a smile's distant cousin waving from across a street. I decided to count it.

"Marcel Aubry," I said. "That was his name before it wasn't. Before he became Marcel Joubert, husband of Hélène, father of two sons, resident of a house with good bones and bad heating. For nine years — nine, I've done the arithmetic so many times I could do it in my sleep, and some nights I have — he also lived at 14 Rue des Tilleuls, fourth floor, no elevator, with a woman named Colette Marchand. Me. Well — her, first. Then me."

The coffee arrived. I wrapped both hands around the cup because my fingers needed employment.

Oscar said nothing. He had the stillness of a man who has spent years being invisible and has gotten very good at it. I recognized the technique because I'd invented my own version: I made myself loud instead. Two children of the same architect, built to opposite specifications.

"You're going to need to say something eventually," I told him. "Or at least blink."

"I'm blinking," he said.

* * *

Here is what I knew growing up, in the order I knew it:

That Papa worked late. That Papa traveled for work. That Papa had another life entirely, which Maman knew about and had decided, through some treaty I was never shown the terms of, to tolerate rather than detonate. That our last name was Aubry on documents and something softer at home, a name spoken quietly, like a password. That when I was nine, the arrangement ended — not with a scene but with a subtraction. One day there was a coat on the hook by the door, and the next day the hook was empty, and Maman took the coat to a charity bin and never explained anything, because explaining, in her economy, was a luxury good.

"He left us," I said now. "Or he left her. The verbs get complicated. What I know is that he came back once, when I was eleven, stood outside the building for an hour, and didn't come up. Maman watched him from the window and made me practice my scales so I wouldn't look."

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because three weeks ago Maman died too, and I found out she'd been writing letters. To your mother. For thirty years. Polite ones. Horrible ones. One of each per decade, roughly." I pulled the photograph from the folder and set it face-down on the table between us, like a card in a game where only one of us knew the rules. "And because your mother never answered a single one, and then last month she died, and I thought — someone has to be in that room when the truth gets unpacked. It shouldn't just be you and your brother dividing up furniture."

Oscar looked at the face-down photograph the way you look at a dog you've been told doesn't bite.

"What is it?" he asked.

"Turn it over."

"I'd rather you tell me."

"It's better if you turn it over. Trust me. I turned it over four times myself before I believed it."

He turned it over.

It was a Sunday in April, judging by the light — that flat, forgiving spring light that makes even ugly streets look like they're considering becoming beautiful. My father stood in front of 14 Rue des Tilleuls holding a baby, and the baby was me, and he was looking down at the bundle in his arms with an expression I had spent my whole life trying to find in photographs of him looking at anything else. His other hand rested on the iron railing. Behind him, in the ground-floor window, hung a pair of curtains.

Oscar went very still. Not the practiced stillness of the janitor, the invisibility he wore like a uniform. This was different. This was the stillness of a man whose floor has tilted.

"The watch," he said finally. His voice had gone quiet in a way that suggested the words were being lowered on ropes. "The watch on his wrist."

"Do you know it?"

"It was his father's. He used to say—" Oscar stopped. Started again. "He used to let me hold it. When I was small. He'd unstrap it and put it on my wrist and it would slide halfway up my arm, and he'd say, *One day this will fit you.*"

"He said that to me too," I said. "Word for word. Maman kept the watch after he died. It's in a drawer with batteries that don't belong to it."

Oscar stared at the curtains in the background of the photograph. I watched him recognize them — watched it happen in real time, the way recognition moves across a face like weather.

"Those are my mother's curtains," he said. "She sewed them herself. She complained about them for twenty years and re-hung them every spring anyway."

"Yes."

"They were in *your* window."

"For nine years, yes."

He put the photograph down very carefully, the way you set down something that might be load-bearing. Then he looked at me, and I saw him doing the terrible arithmetic — not the dates, those were simple, but the implications, which were not. Every childhood has a floor plan. You grow up inside it and you think you know every room. And then someone hands you a second set of blueprints and says, *by the way, there was another wing.*

"There's a version of this where you're lying," he said.

"There is. There's also a version where I'm lying about lying, which is worse. But look at the curtains, Oscar. Look at the watch. I'm not asking you to believe me. I'm asking you to believe the upholstery."

That got the cousin-of-a-smile again, briefer this time, and sadder.

"My mother," he said slowly, "kept a box of photographs in the wardrobe upstairs. Fourth floor. She never let us touch it. Étienne thought it was money. I thought—" He shook his head. "I thought it was nothing. I thought it was just things old women keep."

"And now?"

"Now I think my mother sewed a second set of curtains and never told anyone why."

* * *

We sat there for another hour, and I will be honest: most of it was silence. But it was a different quality of silence than the funeral, where his brother Étienne had looked at me like a stain on the family plot. This silence had furniture in it. We were both sitting inside the same fact, and the fact was large enough to hold two people, which was more than I'd expected.

Near the end, he asked, "Did he love you?"

It was the question I'd been dreading, because I'd spent thirty years answering it in the dark.

"He loved us the way you love a place you visit," I said. "Beautifully, and part-time. And he loved you the way you love a place you live, which means he could hurt you daily and call it home." I closed the folder. "I don't know which is worse. I've stopped ranking them."

Oscar nodded, and then, unexpectedly, he said: "Fourth floor, no elevator."

"What?"

"Our house. The one being sold. The wardrobe nobody has the key to is on the fourth floor, and there's no elevator. My mother's rule was always that whatever was up there stayed up there." He rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand, a gesture so much like my father's that I nearly knocked over my cold coffee. "The sale contract excludes it. The lawyer couldn't explain why. Now I know why. She knew exactly what was up there, and she wanted it to go into the ground with the house, sealed, like everything else."

"So we open it," I said.

"We don't even have a key."

"Then we find one." I stood, put money on the table for coffees neither of us drank, and allowed myself the small vanity of a exit line I'd been composing since the cemetery. "Oscar. Your father held me in front of those curtains and he held you in front of yours, and he told us both the same lie about a watch. Whatever is in that wardrobe belongs to both of us. Half-siblings share, apparently. It's in the bylaws."

He walked me to the door, and just before the bell above it rang, he said, almost to himself:

"She dusted that wardrobe every week. Forty years. You don't dust something weekly unless—"

He didn't finish. He didn't have to. Some sentences complete themselves in the listener.

That night I slept badly, which was normal. But I am told — by the dark circles under his eyes the next morning, by the way he opened the door of the house before I'd finished knocking — that Oscar did not sleep at all. He had spent the night, I suspect, walking the rooms of his own memory and finding new doors in walls he'd known his whole life, all of them locked, all of them humming faintly, like a piano someone is playing one floor below.
