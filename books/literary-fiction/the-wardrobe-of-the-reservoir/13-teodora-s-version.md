## 13. Teodora's Version

The house smelled of dust and eucalyptus the night I told him. Eucalyptus, because I had hung cut branches from the lintels the way our grandmother did before a death, and dust, because no one had lived in Casa Marchetti since the year the government sent men with clipboards to tell us what our lives were worth.

Wendell sat in the kitchen with the notebook open in front of him, and I watched him not read it. He had been not-reading it for three days. His finger rested on the underlined sentence the way a man rests his hand on a wound he is afraid to look at directly, and his face had the particular grey of someone who has spent the night composing accusations against the dead.

I had watched that face before. I had watched it on my mother's face, and on my mother's mother's, and I knew that if I let him leave the valley with that expression still on him, he would carry it for the rest of his life, the way we carried the water stain on the north wall of this kitchen — a map of a country that no longer existed, drawn by something that had happened to us without our consent.

So I put the kettle on. It seemed important, somehow, to begin with the ordinary. With water in a pot, with the click of the burner, with the small domestic sounds that Lucia had loved and that I had spent forty years resenting her for loving.

"You've decided what she was," I said.

He looked up. My cousin. My almost-brother, if you believed the arithmetic of the village, which I had stopped believing at nine years old and started believing again at fifty, and which I was about to hand over to him like a live coal.

"You've decided she was a woman who ran off with a ferryman and left a child behind, and now you've found the proof, and it's written in her own hand, and you hate her for it."

"I don't hate her," he said, in the flat voice of a man who hates.

"You do. It's all right. I hated her for years. I'm very good at it. I could teach a course." I set two cups down. The cups were hers — thick white porcelain, the glaze crazed with hairline rivers, chipped at the lip because she refused to throw anything away that still held coffee. "But before you hate her, you should know what I know. And you should know that I've known it since I was nine years old, and that I have kept it in my mouth for forty-three years like a stone, and that the stone has worn my teeth down to nothing, and that I am tired, Wendell. I am so tired of the weight of it."

* * *

Let me begin where I have always begun, in the summer of 1974, in the kitchen where I am sitting now, though then the walls were white and the window looked out on vines instead of water.

I was nine. Lucia was twenty-six and the most beautiful woman in Rescarola, which is not a boast about my family but a fact about the village, which had perhaps four hundred souls and a beauty standard set by the mountains. She had a laugh that came from low in her chest, and she sang while she sewed, and the men of the valley had been circling her for a decade like moths who had learned patience.

She was married by then. Three years married, to a man from over the ridge — Wendell's father, the one whose name he carries, the one whose face he carries, and I want to say this plainly before the rest of it: that man was not cruel, and he was not a fool, and whatever else you learn tonight, learn that too. He was a man who worked the terraces and came home smelling of soil and sweat and wanted, more than anything in this world, a child. And Lucia gave him one, and loved him in the way you love a house you live in — faithfully, and without ever once mistaking it for the sky.

Bruno was the ferryman. Before the dam, before the water, there was a river — the Ribeira Funda, cold and quick, and the bridge was old, and when it flooded in spring the only way across was Bruno's flat-bottomed boat. He was twenty-nine that summer. He had hands like tree roots and he read books, which in Rescarola made him either a saint or a lunatic, and he had a way of looking at a woman as though she were a sentence he intended to spend his whole life translating.

I saw them together exactly once, and I was not supposed to see them at all.

It was August. The heat had driven everyone to the river, and I had gone down to the willows to catch the little silver fish that gathered in the shallows, the ones we called *as reluzentas*, the shining ones, because they turned in the water like coins. I was crouched in the reeds with my skirt hiked up and a jar between my knees when I heard voices from the bend where the river went narrow and the willows made a green room of shade.

Lucia was sitting on the bank with her shoes off and her feet in the water. Bruno was standing in the shallows beside the boat, and he was reading to her. Not poetry — I would learn later it was a novel, some translated thing, because Bruno was the kind of man who read novels in two languages and Lucia was the kind of woman who listened to them as though the words were being poured into her. And she was laughing. Not the laugh she gave at the market, not the laugh she gave my uncle over cards, but a laugh I had never heard, low and helpless and young, the laugh of a girl who had forgotten she was supposed to be a wife.

And then he stopped reading, and she stopped laughing, and the silence that came down over that bend of the river was so complete that I could hear the water moving over her ankles, and Bruno reached out and touched her wet hair, and she closed her eyes and turned her face into his hand like a plant turning toward the sun, and I understood, at nine years old, with the jar of shining fish forgotten between my knees, that I was looking at the truest thing I would ever see.

I ran home. I said nothing. Children learn early in a village what is not to be said.

* * *

The winter of that year, the government's letters began arriving. I remember the envelope itself — grey, official, with a crest — and I remember my father reading it at the table and then reading it again, and then setting it down with both hands flat on either side of it, the way you set down something that might go off.

The valley was to be flooded. Every house, every field, every grave. The church would be dynamited — they left the tower, in the end, some concession to the petitions, some engineer's arithmetic about what the water could hold — and the village would become the bottom of a lake that would light the lamps of cities none of us would ever visit.

They offered money. That is the part the histories get wrong, or rather the part they state and do not explain. They offered money, real money, more money than any family in Rescarola had ever held in its hands, and they offered it on a schedule: the earlier you signed and left, the more you received. There was a date — I have it still, burned into me, the fifteenth of March, 1975 — and after that date the price dropped like a stone down a well. And after the last date, there was nothing at all. You left with what you could carry and the state's blessing and not one *escudo* more.

Every family in the valley sat at its table that winter and did the arithmetic of its own dissolution.

Lucia's husband signed early. I don't blame him — hear me, Wendell, I do not blame him, and you will not either when I finish — because he had a brother already settled in Coimbra, and work waiting, and a child to think of, and the money would buy a proper apartment with a bathroom that worked and a street with a name. He signed in January. He was a man who did the reasonable thing at the reasonable time, and the reasonable thing that winter was to drown your village on paper before the water did it in fact.

Lucia did not sign.

* * *

I found out why in February, because I was nine and curious and because I had learned to read upside down, which is a skill every child of a household of secrets acquires without being taught.

She had the government's papers in her sewing basket, under the thread. I pulled them out one afternoon when she was at the well, and I sat on the floor of this kitchen — this kitchen, the same four walls, though then they were white — and I read the forms, or the parts of them a nine-year-old can read, and I understood that her page was blank. She had not filled in her name. And beside the forms, folded into them, was a letter in Bruno's handwriting, and I will not tell you what the letter said because I read only the first line before I heard her at the door and put it back, and that first line has been enough for forty-three years: *Se ficares, eu fico* — if you stay, I stay.

She came in with the water jar on her hip and found me sitting on the floor with the sewing basket open, and I have never in my life seen a face change the way hers changed. It was not fear. That is what I want you to understand, what took me decades to understand. It was not the fear of a woman caught. It was the stillness of a woman who has been caught and has decided, in the space of one breath, that she is done hiding — and who is measuring, in that same breath, whether a nine-year-old can be trusted with the weight of it.

She set down the jar. She sat down across from me. And she said, in Rescarolese, because she always spoke the deep things in Rescarolese, the way you'd keep a fire in the oldest hearth of the house:

*"Sabes o que é a aldeia?"* Do you know what the village is?

And I said, because I was nine and literal, that the village was the houses and the church and the terraces.

And she shook her head, and she took my hands, which were still cold from the well rope, and she said: the village is the words. The village is that we say *chuv'na petra* and not the official Portuguese, that we say it and the rain on the stone smells of home. The village is that when your grandmother dies, the word we bury with her is a word no one in Coimbra or Lisbon or any city with a bathroom that works will ever say again. The water can take the houses. The water cannot take the words unless we let it — and letting it starts the day the last person decides the words are not worth staying for.

And then she said the thing I have kept like a stone in my mouth:

*"Eu fico para que ele nasça aqui. Para que a primeira palavra dele seja desta terra."*

I stay so that he is born here. So that his first word belongs to this earth.

She was pregnant. Three months, perhaps. She had not yet told her husband — or she had, I never learned, and it is the one question I would ask her across any distance, through any water, if the dead take messages. But she had done the arithmetic at her own table, alone, and the sum was this: if she left in January, she left with money and a husband and an apartment in Coimbra, and her child was born a city child with a city tongue, and Rescarola died a month later in her mouth like a language in a drowning throat.

And if she stayed — if she stayed past the fifteenth of March, past the last date, past the dropping of the price to nothing — then her son was born on Rescarolese soil, in the final legal year of the village, and his first word would be a word from down here, from the deep country, from the oldest hearth.

She stayed. She gave up the money — all of it, the entire evacuation payment, the price of a life, and she was not rich, Wendell, she was the daughter of a seamstress and she sewed for other people's weddings until her eyes went — and she gave up Bruno, because Bruno had a boat and a mother and a life on the river, and when the water came the river would go with it, and a man cannot stay for a woman who has decided to stay for a child that may not be his. *Se ficares, eu fico*, he had written. She did not let him stay. That was her sacrifice, the one no one saw, the one that looked from the outside exactly like faithlessness: she sent him away to save him from the drowning, and she took the village's contempt onto herself like a coat, because contempt was lighter than grief and easier to carry in front of a child.

The village called her the woman who stayed for stubbornness. Then, after her husband left alone in February and the whispers started, they called her worse. And she sewed, and she sang, and she carried her belly through that last spring like a woman carrying a lantern through a house that was being dismantled room by room, and on the twelfth of April, 1975, in this house, in the room above this kitchen, with the midwife from the next valley because ours had already gone, she gave birth to a son on Rescarolese soil.

She named him Wendell, which is not a Rescarolese name and not a Portuguese one, and when my grandmother asked her why, she said: so that no one will ever be able to guess from his name which country he belongs to. He will have to choose. And then she laughed — that low laugh, the river laugh, the one I had heard through the willows — and she said, *que pelo menos escolha com as palavras certas* — may he at least choose with the right words.

* * *

The kettle had gone quiet. The kitchen was dark now except for the lamp, and the water stain on the north wall, and the notebook lying between us on the table like a third person who had been listening.

"She asked me to burn it," I said.

Wendell's head came up.

"After the funeral. She was dying for eight months, you were in Lisbon, you came when you could, but the last three weeks it was me. And one night, very clear, the way she was clear at the end, she took my hand and she said: the notebook, in the desk. When I'm gone, burn it. Not read it — burn it. And I said yes. I said yes because you say yes to the dying, it's the last kindness, you let them believe their affairs are in order and their wishes are iron." I turned my cup a quarter-turn on the table. The porcelain made a small sound against the wood. "And then I didn't.

"Because I read it first. I told myself I'd read it to be sure there was nothing in it that would hurt you, which was a lie, Wendell, I read it because I had carried her secret for forty-three years and I wanted, for once, to hold the whole of it in my hands. And I read the translations, half-finished, beautiful, her handwriting going shaky at the edges. And I read the sentence. The one you can't finish. And I understood that she wrote it for you and could never send it, and that burning it would be the second drowning — that the water took the village, and I would be taking the last word of it. And I could not. I am a coward, and I could not, and I have regretted it and thanked God for it in the same breath every day since."

I looked at him then, really looked, and I saw that he was crying without any noise, the way men of our family cry, as though the tears were a private administrative matter.

"She was not faithless," I said. "She was the most faithful person this valley ever produced. She was faithful to a handful of words and a square of ground and an unborn child's first syllable, and she paid for it with everything, and she let you believe — she let all of us believe the easy, ugly version — because the true version would have put a weight on you no child should carry. She took the contempt so you could have the language. That was the trade. That was her whole life, the trade."

The lamp flickered. Outside, the reservoir lay flat and black under the moon, holding the church tower in its throat.

Wendell put his hand flat on the notebook, over the underlined sentence, the way you'd put a hand on a grave.

"Then teach me," he said. "The words I've lost. I can't read her. I can't — I've lost the words, Teodora. She stayed so my first word would belong here, and I've let them go one by one, I've been *counting* them going—"

"Then we start tomorrow," I said. "There are three of us left who spoke it from the cradle, and one of them is Serafina Costa, and Serafina has been silent about your mother since 1975, and I think — I have always thought — that her silence and mine are the same silence, and that it is time we both broke it."

I blew out the lamp, and we sat a moment in the dark together, two keepers of one stone, while the water held the village below us and the house around us held its breath.
