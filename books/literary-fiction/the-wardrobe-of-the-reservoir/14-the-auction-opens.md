## 14. The Auction Opens

The morning of the auction, Casa Marchetti wore its price tags like a disease.

Wendell saw them from the road before he saw anything else — small yellow rectangles, laminated against the damp, wired to the gate, the doorposts, the olive tree that had stood when his mother was a girl. *Lot 1. Lot 2. Lot 3.* The house itself was Lot 4, and the land around it, all nine hundred and forty square meters of terraced hillside that had once grown beans and bitter oranges and the particular silence of a village that knew it was already condemned, was Lot 5. Someone had walked the property with a clipboard and a roll of wire and had decided, item by item, what the past was worth.

He stood at the gate a long time. The reservoir glittered below, flat as hammered tin, and the tip of the church tower broke its surface the way it always did now, that gray finger pointing at a sky that had taken everything from it. Bidders were already arriving. He could hear their car doors, their voices carrying up the hill in the bright, carrying air of a valley that no longer had a village to absorb sound.

* * *

Teodora had not slept. She would have said so anyway, to anyone who asked, but no one asked, and so she said it to the kitchen — the kitchen that was no longer hers, had never legally been hers, that the auctioneer's people had measured yesterday with a laser device that clicked and gave them numbers she refused to look at.

She had polished nothing. She had wanted, for one furious hour at dawn, to smear the windows with ash, to let the dust sheets fall, to make the house so ugly with grief that no one would want it. But the house was already beautiful in the way that abandoned things are beautiful, the way that ruins are always more photogenic than the living room they replaced. The morning light came through the east windows and lay across the floorboards in long gold planks, and the dust motes turned in it like the slowest snowfall in the world, and she understood that she could not make this place unlovely. Grief had already done its decorating. All that remained was the sale.

She heard the first cars. She straightened her back the way her mother had taught her, the Rescarolese way, spine like a rower's, shoulders that could carry a crate of eels up from the water without complaint. Not that anyone in this valley had carried eels in fifty years. Not that the water had eels in it anymore, or anything else that remembered the river.

She went to the door to let the strangers in.

* * *

They came in pairs and clusters, and Wendell watched them the way you watch gulls work a fish shoal — with a professional's detachment and a fisherman's dread.

There was a couple from Porto, he could tell by the vowels, a man in a linen jacket that had never been near linen's original purpose and a woman who walked through the rooms touching nothing and photographing everything. She stood in what had been his mother's sewing room and said, *the light is extraordinary, you could put a studio here,* and Wendell's chest did something complicated and painful, because the light was extraordinary, had always been extraordinary, and his mother had sat in it for thirty years making coats for people in a town that had forgotten her village existed, and now the light was a selling point and the coat hooks were Lot 12.

There was a younger man, alone, who moved through the house with the quick hungry economy of someone who had done this before. He measured doorways with a tape. He opened his phone and spoke into it in a low stream of figures — *load-bearing, probably, see the beam, that's original chestnut, we could open this whole wall* — and Wendell followed him at a distance, a ghost haunting his own biography, listening to his childhood described in square meters.

And then there was the developer.

Wendell had seen his type before, in Lisbon, in the old neighborhoods that kept getting younger. This one was called — the auction catalog said, the catalog that Wendell had read forty times until the paper went soft as cloth — Álvaro Quintela, and he arrived not in a car but in a kind of arrival, with an associate and a surveyor and a confidence so complete it didn't need to raise its voice. He was perhaps fifty-five, silver at the temples, dressed in the uniform of serious money, which is to say clothes that looked inexpensive and were not. He walked the house slowly. He did not measure anything. He stood in the main room, the one with the hearth where Lucia had once — Wendell remembered it suddenly, the memory arriving like a body surfacing — where Lucia had once roasted chestnuts on a wire rack and sung a song about a girl who married the river, and Quintela stood there and looked at the ceiling beams and then out the window at the reservoir and said, to no one, to the room, to the water:

"You could see the lake from a terrace here. Wraparound. The tower —" he nodded at the church tower's tip, visible from the window, that gray finger — "that's a feature. People will pay for a view of that. Ruins are romantic from a distance."

*From a distance.* Wendell felt the words go into him like a splinter. Everything in his life, he thought, had been true from a distance. His mother's story. His language. The village. All of it legible, even beautiful, as long as you stood far enough away that the details dissolved. It was only up close that you could see the sentence in the notebook, the underlining, the twice-pressed pen that had nearly torn the paper.

Quintela caught him looking. He had that gift, the men of that class, the ability to feel attention on them like weather.

"You're family?" Quintela asked. Not unkindly. His voice had the warmth of a man who was good with sellers.

"Cousin," Wendell said. It was close enough. It had been close enough his whole life.

"Then I'm sorry," Quintela said, and Wendell believed that he was, in the way that a tide is sorry for the sand. "These sales are never easy. But a house that no one lives in is just a slow fire. Better it becomes something. Better the water keeps some company."

He moved on, toward the kitchen, where Teodora stood, and Wendell stayed by the hearth, in the place where the chestnuts had been, and looked at the window, and thought: *the tower is not a feature.*

* * *

By noon the house was full.

It was the strangest thing Wendell had ever witnessed, and he had witnessed the exhumation of his own childhood from a locked desk, and that was saying something. Forty, fifty people moved through Casa Marchetti like water through a wreck. The auctioneer's staff had set up a table with coffee and pastries on the terrace — pastries, Wendell noted, from the town, the valley towns, the resettlement towns, nothing that had ever existed in Rescarola — and people ate them standing up and talked in the low voices of a funeral that had been scheduled on a working day.

An old woman — not old enough, Wendell corrected himself, not old enough to remember, but old — stood in the corridor upstairs and said to her husband, *imagine the work, imagine the damp,* and the husband said, *that's why the price,* and Wendell passed them on the stairs and heard his own breathing.

The rooms had been tagged. He had known this, Teodora had told him, but knowing and seeing were different animals. Little yellow stickers, like the ones on fruit, like the ones on corpses in a morgue, on the doorframes: *Lot 7, Lot 8, Lot 9.* His mother's sewing machine had a sticker. The desk — the desk was Lot 23, and someone had already tried the drawers, and the scratch marks of their impatience were fresh over the old scratch marks of his own, from the night he had forced it, the night the notebook had come into his hands like a live coal.

He found himself standing in the doorway of the room he had slept in as a child, the summers, the last summers, before the water came for the valley and the family scattered like seed. It was a small room. It had a window that looked down at the river, at what had been the river. Someone had written on the wall inside the wardrobe — he had forgotten this, had lost it the way he lost the words, and now it came back whole — someone, probably himself, probably eight years old, had written in pencil inside the wardrobe: *aqui mora um urso,* here lives a bear. The pencil marks were still there. Faint. Patient. Thirty years of patience in graphite.

A bidder came up behind him. The man from Porto, the one with the tape.

"Original wardrobe?" the man asked, already looking past Wendell at the answer.

"Yes," Wendell said.

"Good bones, this house," the man said, to the house, or to himself, and wrote something on the back of his catalog.

Wendell stepped out of the room. He could not be in the room. There was a bear in the wardrobe, and the bear was him, and the man wanted the bones.

* * *

Teodora watched Quintela the way she had once watched the water rise — with a ledger in her head and no power at all over what the numbers did.

He was good. That was the worst of it. He was respectful, he was warm, he asked her questions about the house that no one had asked her in thirty years — *which way did the prevailing wind come from, did the well run dry in August, who built the terrace walls* — and she answered because the answers were in her and wanted out, and she hated herself a little for every answer, and answered anyway. He wrote everything down. He had a surveyor photograph the terrace walls. He said, *the stonework alone,* and touched the wall with two fingers, gently, the way you touch a horse before you buy it.

"You grew up here," he said. It was not a question.

"No," Teodora said. "I grew up two kilometers east, in a house the state built, with a number for a name. But my mother was born in this one. And her mother. And her mother's mother, probably, if anyone had written it down, which no one did, because writing things down was for people who weren't going to be flooded."

Quintela looked at her for a moment. Whatever was behind his eyes, he kept it there.

"And you're not bidding," he said.

"I have no money," Teodora said. "I have a house with a number for a name and a pension and a brother who—" She stopped. She had been about to say *a brother who is a coward or a fool, I haven't decided which.* She had been about to say it to a stranger, which was how she knew how far the morning had gone.

Quintela nodded, and moved toward the stairs, and Teodora stood in the kitchen where her mother had rolled pasta on the board that was Lot 19, and felt the house around her like a coat being taken off her shoulders by polite hands.

* * *

At four o'clock the light went long and gold and the bidders began to drift out, and Wendell found Teodora on the terrace, alone, looking down at the water. The coffee table was a wreckage of cups. Somewhere below, the church tower held its finger up to the darkening sky, and the first wind of evening moved across the reservoir and made the water shiver the way a horse shivers off flies.

"Teodora."

"I know," she said. "I know what you're going to say. You're going to say you can't. You're going to say the money is for your old age, for Lisbon, for the apartment, for the life you built out of the pieces. You're going to say that a house is not a language, that you can't speak a house, that keeping it would just be another way of drowning." She said it all in one breath, in the flat reciting voice of someone who had been rehearsing an argument for weeks and had decided, at the last moment, to perform both sides.

"Something like that," Wendell said.

She turned. In the low light her face was all planes and shadow, their mother's face, their grandmother's face, the Rescarolese face that the water had never gotten and never would. Her eyes were dry. He understood that the dryness was costing her.

"Tomorrow at ten o'clock," she said, "a man is going to buy this house. Maybe the Porto couple. Maybe the one with the tape measure. Probably Quintela, because Quintela has the money and the patience and the vision, and vision is just grief that's been put to work." Her voice did not rise. That was the terrible thing about Teodora; she had inherited all of Lucia's fire and none of Lucia's smoke. "And they will pull out the wardrobe with the bear in it. And they will sand the terrace walls that my grandfather laid stone by stone with his own hands, stone by stone, in the year of the flood, because he believed until the last day that the water would be reasonable. And the tower will be a feature. And in five years there will be people drinking wine on a wraparound terrace, looking at the water where our village is, and they will say, *how beautiful, how peaceful,* and they will mean it, Wendell, they will mean it with their whole hearts, because they will never know what they are looking at. That is what peace is. Peace is not knowing."

The wind moved. Down at the shore a mooring rope knocked against a post, once, twice, like a hand on a door.

"So I am asking you," Teodora said. "Not as your cousin. As the last person alive who remembers the smell of this kitchen. Bid, or walk away. But if you walk away, walk away all the way. Don't stand at the gate. Don't come back to look at the terrace lights. Don't keep the notebook in a drawer in Lisbon and visit it on Sundays like a grave. If you let them have it, let them have all of it, and let the water take the rest of you too." She looked at him then, fully, and her voice finally cracked, one hairline fracture down the middle of the steel. "Or bid. Use the money. Lose the money. Be a fool. Lucia was a fool. Everyone who ever loved this valley was a fool, and the water took them anyway, and I would rather be a fool on this hill tomorrow than a sensible woman in a numbered house for the rest of my life."

Wendell said nothing. There was nothing in him that was finished yet. There was the notebook in his bag, and the sentence in the notebook, and the fragments he had gathered like a man gathering spilled grain in the dark — *the boat, the name, the word for forgive* — and there was tomorrow, ten o'clock, and there was the money, eleven years of careful translation, sitting in an account in Lisbon like a lifeboat tied to a dock.

"Think about it tonight," Teodora said. She turned back to the water. "You have until ten o'clock. After that, the gavel decides who we are."

He left her there. At the gate he stopped, once, and looked back at the house, at the yellow tags going gray in the dusk, at the lit window of the kitchen where Teodora stood without moving, keeping her vigil over the last house of Rescarola on its last night as a house.

And below, in the dark water, the church tower pointed at the first stars, and Wendell thought he heard — though it was only the wind, only the wind against the stone — a sound like a voice, saying a word he almost knew, in the language he was learning to carry.
