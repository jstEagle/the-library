## 1. The Radiator Speaks First

The radiator in the front parlor spoke before the piano did.

Gideon Villalobos stood with his hand flat against the cast iron, the way other men might press an ear to a door. The metal was warm — someone had kept the boiler running even now, even condemned, and that alone told him something. You don't heat a house you've given up on. You heat a house you're still arguing with.

"Cast iron's original," he said to the room, though no one had asked him anything. "Nineteen-twenties, maybe older. They ran it hot for years and then let it go cold, probably during the war or just after, when coal was rationed. Then somebody got it working again and ran it too hot out of relief." He took his hand away. "You can see it in the scaling. First thirty years of loving it too much, then ten years of neglect, then forty years of making up for it."

The room said nothing back. Rooms never did, not in words. But he'd learned to read them anyway, the way his mother had read weather off the backs of her hands.

He turned to the piano.

It was an upright, a Schiedmayer from what looked like the fifties, walnut veneer gone dull at the edges where hands had worn through the finish. It sat against the wall beneath a rectangle of unfaded wallpaper — a picture had hung there once, and whoever took it down hadn't bothered to repaint, because why would they? The city had posted its condemnation notice on the door downstairs six months ago. The family was moving out in stages. What remained was this: the piano, two chairs, a crucifix over the doorway, and a smell of boiled potatoes and floor wax that had soaked so deep into the plaster it would outlast the building itself.

A woman came in carrying a cardboard box labeled VERHULST — KITCHEN in ballpoint. She was maybe seventy, small, quick-moving, with the forearms of someone who'd carried things her whole life.

"You're the tuner," she said. Not a question. She set the box down and looked at him with the frank appraisal of a woman who had raised four children and buried one husband and did not have time left over for nonsense. "My son said come Saturday. He didn't say you'd be talking to the radiators."

"They talk first," Gideon said. "Pianos take longer."

She squinted at him. He watched her decide whether he was mad or merely Belgian, and settle — provisionally — on the second.

"It's going to the new place," she said, nodding at the piano. "My son's flat in Jette. My husband bought it in 1963, secondhand, from a music teacher who was dying. He paid too much. I told him so at the time." A pause, and then, because she was the kind of woman who finished her sentences: "It was the last thing we bought together that wasn't a coffin."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. He had a good death. Quick. Some people get a long goodbye and it ruins them." She looked at the piano the way you look at an old dog. "Can you make it sound like it used to?"

"No," Gideon said. "Nobody can make anything sound like it used to. But I can make it sound true. That's usually enough."

She snorted — almost a laugh — and went back out for another box.

* * *

He opened the piano and began the work, which was really listening wearing work clothes.

The Schiedmayer had been tuned regularly until roughly fifteen years ago, then irregularly, then not at all. The pitch had sagged to somewhere south of A-440, the way old pianos do, settling into their own private key like old men settling into their own private opinions. Several hammers were grooved hard enough to leave string-marks in the felt. One bass string had a dead spot — corrosion, probably a spill, water or beer or tears, and there was no way to know which, though he found himself constructing a story anyway: someone crying over this instrument sometime in the nineties, head down near the bass strings, where the low notes live, where grief keeps its furniture.

He worked top to bottom, mute wedge by mute wedge, and while his hands did the mechanical part his ears did the rest.

This was the thing about him, the thing clients found either charming or unsettling depending on their temperament: Gideon could hear a building. Not metaphorically — or not only metaphorically. Pipes have voices. Radiators have accents. Every house in Brussels is a body that has swallowed sixty, eighty, a hundred years of lives, and the plumbing is its throat, and if you put your hand on it and shut up long enough, it will tell you everything.

The townhouse told him plenty.

The supply pipes under the stairs had been replaced twice — once in copper, once more recently in those plastic composites that hum at a different frequency, flatter, less honest. But the originals were still there behind the lath, lead, and he could hear the difference when water moved: the old pipes carried a lower, rounder note, a note with history in it. Someone had bathed children under that line. He could tell from the mineral ring pattern on the accessible joint near the cellar door — years of hard use, high volume, small bodies splashing. Three children, he guessed. Maybe four. Close in age, because the wear was continuous rather than seasonal.

The kitchen radiator had been painted over eleven times. He counted the ridges at the valve, each coat a year or two, each coat a spring cleaning, a woman on her knees with a brush, doing it properly because that was how it was done. Eleven springs. And then the paint stopped changing, which meant either she died or she stopped caring, and the dust told him which: after the eleventh coat there was a layer of grey film, undisturbed, gentle. She died. Or she left. The radiator didn't distinguish. Radiators are honest but not omniscient.

Upstairs — he went up during a break, uninvited, telling himself it was professional habit — the bedroom radiators told a colder story. Two rooms heated all winter, every winter, right up to the end. One room barely heated at all, and one not at all, its radiator cold-rusted, seized, a valve fused with disuse. Somebody had slept alone up here for years, keeping only the minimum of rooms alive around them, narrowing their life down room by room like a man folding a map.

"That was my parents' room," said a voice behind him, and he nearly dropped his flashlight.

The son — the one who'd hired him, mid-forties, cardigan, the tired face of a man managing his mother's move and his own guilt simultaneously — stood in the hallway holding two coffees.

"The small one," the son said, nodding at the half-heated room. "My brother's. He left in '89. Went to Canada. We don't—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "They kept it ready. Twenty-five years they kept the radiator ready. My father bled it every October. In case."

"In case he came back," Gideon said.

"In case he needed somewhere to come back *to*. There's a difference." The son handed him a coffee. "My mother says you talk to houses."

"I listen. Talking is mostly me filling in."

"And what does ours say?"

Gideon looked down the stairwell, at the banister worn pale in two arcs — two hands, decades, descending side by side. He thought about saying it. He had the whole sentence ready; he always had the whole sentence ready. *Your house says your parents loved each other quietly and grieved loudly and kept a room warm for twenty-five years for a man who may never have thought about it once, and that is either the saddest thing I've heard this month or the most faithful, and I can't decide.*

What he said was: "It says good things about your father's boiler maintenance."

The son laughed, a short surprised bark, and went downstairs, and Gideon stood in the cold room drinking coffee he hadn't asked for, doing the thing he always did, the thing that had become so automatic it hardly registered as a choice anymore: swallowing the true sentence whole and handing people the wrapper.

* * *

By four o'clock the piano was in tune — true pitch, true voicing, the dead bass string flagged for replacement — and Madame Verhulst-that-was, the mother, came and sat on the bench and played.

She played badly. Stiff fingers, hesitant, a hymn she clearly knew by heart and hadn't performed in years. But when she hit the middle of the keyboard the piano answered her clean and round, and her face did something complicated — pleasure and grief arriving on the same nerve — and she played the hymn through to the end without stopping, which he suspected was the point.

"He tuned it himself, you know," she said, closing the lid. "My husband. Bought a kit from the music shop. Did it wrong for thirty years. Every autumn, wrong, with great confidence." She patted the wood. "You've done it right. It sounds like a stranger's piano now. Better. Stranger's."

"Thank you."

"My son says you're good. He says you hear things." She looked at him directly, and Gideon recognized the look — it was the look of a woman standing in a house full of boxes, deciding what could be carried and what had to be left. "Do you want it? The piano?"

He blinked. "I have pianos."

"You have tools. A man with tools and no piano is a carpenter with no table." She stood, briskly, before he could answer, which he suspected was deliberate. "Think about it. It needs a family. Even a family of one."

At the door, she paused with her hand on the frame, on the paint worn to bare wood at shoulder height — generations of hands leaving in exactly that spot — and said, without turning around:

"There's a woman on my street. Odile Vermeersch. Ninety-one years old and sharper than the condemnation notice on this building. She has a piano nobody tunes and a tongue nobody uses anymore, if you understand me." Now she turned. "I'm going to give her your number. You'll hate it. Do it anyway."

And she was gone, into the van, into the moving day, into the rest of her life, and Gideon stood in the doorway of the condemned house holding his tuning hammer like a man who'd been handed something heavier than a tool.

* * *

He walked home through Anderlecht as the light went the color of weak tea.

His street was narrow and damp and he loved it without ever having said so, even to himself, out loud, in words. His flat was above a closed friterie, two rooms, and the radiator in the main room knocked three times whenever the boiler cycled — knock, knock, knock — a stutter in the system, air in the line, and he had never fixed it because the knocking was the closest thing he had to a roommate.

He made coffee. He sat. The radiator knocked its three knocks and he knocked back, twice, out of some private liturgy he would have denied under oath.

On the table was the photograph.

Not the photographs — those lived in a drawer, in an envelope, in the dark, where he kept them the way you keep a tooth that came out wrong. Just the one, the one he allowed himself, face up, because tonight the house on Rue Verhulst had rattled him: a room kept warm for twenty-five years for a man who never came. A radiator bled faithfully every October, in case.

In the photograph his father was maybe thirty. Lucien Villalobos, though the name on the back, in his mother's handwriting, was just *him, 1978*, as if even she had started abbreviating the man before he left. He stood outside a café with his collar up, laughing at something outside the frame, and he had Gideon's exact eyebrows, and beyond that the resemblance dissolved into guesswork. Gideon had spent nineteen years — no. Ten years. Ten years since he'd found the envelope at nineteen, since his mother's illness had loosened her grip on the secrets, since she'd said *the box is in the wardrobe, if you want it, I won't watch you open it* — he had spent ten years circling this stranger the way a tuner circles a false note: locating it precisely, refusing to fix it.

He had written letters. Fourteen of them. They lived in the same drawer as the rest of the photographs, stamped, unsent, addressed to an address in Antwerp that a detective — a cheap one, hired with savings he'd never admitted spending — had confirmed was real. Somewhere in Antwerp a man with his eyebrows received nothing, and Gideon composed nothing, and both of them called it peace.

The phone rang.

He looked at it. Old landline, beige, the kind that outlives its era through sheer stubbornness. He didn't recognize the number, which meant it was a client, which meant it was safe, which meant he could answer it and be competent for eight minutes and hang up having said nothing true, which was his specialty, his art form, the one discipline in which he was unquestionably gifted.

He picked it up on the fourth ring, at the point where answering became rude and not answering became a decision.

"Villalobos. Piano tuning."

"Gideon?" A woman's voice, old, dry, unhurried, with an accent he couldn't place — not quite Brussels, not quite anything, a voice from some street that no longer officially existed. "This is Odile Vermeersch. Madeleine gave me your number. She said you talk to houses."

"I listen," he said. "Mostly."

"Well. I have an upright piano that hasn't been tuned since the Germans were a rumor, and I have a great deal else besides, and I pay cash, and I talk too much, and none of these things bother you yet because you haven't met me." A pause, in which he heard her smile. "When can you come?"

He looked at the photograph. His father laughed at something outside the frame, forever.

"Thursday," he said. "Afternoon."

"Thursday afternoon," she repeated, as if filing it somewhere permanent, and hung up, and the radiator knocked its three knocks, and Gideon sat in the dark with the true sentence rising in him again — *something is starting, something has already started, you can hear it coming up the pipes* — and, as always, said nothing at all.
