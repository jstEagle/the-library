## 2. Ten Years of Almost

The piano in the Sint-Guido community hall had been tuned last in 1998, and it sounded like it. I lifted the lid and the smell came up at me — felt, dust, the ghost of someone's cigarettes from a decade when people still smoked indoors without apology — and I put my ear close to the soundboard the way other men put their ear to a train track.

"Take your time," said the woman from the parish council, checking her watch.

"I always do."

That was a lie. I took exactly as long as the instrument required and not one minute more, because taking longer would mean being somewhere longer, and being somewhere longer meant being asked things. How long have you been doing this? Are you from Brussels originally? Your name — Villalobos — is that Spanish? And then the question under all the questions, the one nobody actually asked but everybody's face asked: *and who are you, exactly?*

I am a piano tuner. That is the whole of my biography that I hand out for free.

* * *

Ten years ago, almost to the month, I found the photographs.

I was nineteen and my mother had been dead eight months, and I was in her house on the Rue de Fiennes doing what orphans do, which is open drawers. Not looking for anything. Looking is too purposeful a word. I was excavating, the way water excavates — slowly, without intent, finding the low places.

The photographs were in a biscuit tin behind her winter sweaters, which should have told me something right away. You don't hide what you've forgotten. You hide what you remember every day.

There were eleven photographs. In ten of them there was a man I did not know, and in the eleventh there was a man I did not know holding a baby who could only have been me.

He was not handsome in any way the magazines would recognize, but he was *vivid*, the way certain buildings are vivid — he seemed to take up more light than the room offered. Dark hair going gray early at the temples. A laugh in three of the photos, caught mid-sound, mouth open, eyes shut, as if laughter were something that happened to him rather than something he decided to do. In one he stood outside a narrow brick townhouse with a green door, one arm slung around a woman I didn't recognize either, and both of them were squinting into a sun that fell on some street I'd never walked.

On the back of that one, in my mother's handwriting — I knew her handwriting the way you know your own pulse — it said only: *Lucien, rue du Cure, 1979.*

Lucien. My father had a first name. It had been in my birth certificate all along, printed there like a word in a language I'd never been taught, and I had read past it ten thousand times.

I sat on my mother's bedroom floor with the tin in my lap until the light went out of the window, and then I turned on the lamp and kept sitting. At some point I noticed I was humming. I do that. When something is too large to feel, I hum, low, a note below hearing, and I didn't learn it from anyone I ever met.

Here is what I did not do: I did not cry, or shout, or throw the tin across the room, or call anyone. Here is what I did instead, over the following weeks and months and years: nothing, exquisitely.

* * *

"You're brooding," Odile said.

I looked up. My landlady was standing in the doorway of the community hall with a canvas shopping bag on her arm and an expression on her face like weather coming in from the sea. She was eighty-nine then, small and upright as a chair, with white hair cut short and hands that looked like they'd done every kind of work except the kind that apologizes.

"I'm working," I said.

"You're brooding over a piano." She came down the aisle and set her bag on a folding chair. "That's different from working. Working sounds like this—" she rapped a knuckle on the hall's radiator, a bright hollow clang, "—and brooding sounds like this." She pressed her palm flat against the same radiator and held it there, listening through her skin, which was a thing she did that I had never seen anyone else do and which unsettled me every time, because it was so nearly my own trick, as if we'd learned it from the same teacher.

"The radiator's fine," I said.

"The radiator is always fine. It's the tuner who rattles." She peered into the piano's open throat. "You've been at this one two hours. It needed forty minutes."

"It needed two hours."

"Gideon." She said my name the way she said everything, flat and final, a stamp coming down on an envelope. "Your phone has rung four times since I got here. Four times you looked at it and let it die. Whoever it is will keep calling until you answer or until they understand, and believe me, they will understand. Silence teaches very fast."

"It's nobody."

"Nobody doesn't call four times."

I turned back to the pinblock. There was a string in the treble — the F above middle C — that wanted to drift sharp no matter what I did, and I gave it a quarter turn and listened to it fight me. Some strings are like that. They hold their grudge against whatever pitch the world has agreed on.

Odile watched me for a while. She had a way of watching that made you feel audited. Then she opened her canvas bag, took out a thermos and two cups — two, always two, she'd decided years ago that I drank coffee whether I agreed or not — and poured.

"Sit," she said.

"I'm nearly done."

"Sit anyway. The piano will wait. Pianos are patient; it's why they outlive us." She handed me a cup. "Who calls a man four times?"

I drank the coffee. It was terrible, chicory-heavy, the way she'd made it since the rationing taught her to stretch things, and I have never once told her it was terrible, because telling her would start a conversation about why I drink it every day.

"My father," I said, to the coffee, to the string, to nobody. "Possibly. It's a number I don't know. It rings twice, stops, rings again. Like someone picking it up and putting it down."

"And you don't answer."

"No."

"Why not?"

Because answering is a door, and doors swing both ways. Because for ten years I had built an entire architecture out of not-knowing, load-bearing walls of maybe, and one phone call was a wrecking ball with a dial tone. Because the question I'd rehearsed ten thousand times — *why did you leave* — had worn smooth as a river stone from handling, and a rehearsed question asked aloud comes out sounding like what it is: a performance. And I couldn't bear to perform it badly. Or at all.

"Wrong number, probably," I said.

Odile looked at me over the rim of her cup for a long moment. Then she said, in a voice I would think about later, much later, when I understood everything it had been carrying: "In my experience, wrong numbers are the rarest kind."

* * *

At twenty-two I wrote him the first letter.

I say *him*, but I didn't know where *him* was. I wrote it to a man who existed only as a name on a photograph and a vacancy in my life, and I wrote it in longhand on lined paper, thirty-one pages, and it began *Dear Lucien* and ended *Gideon*, and between those two words I laid out everything: the childhood, the mother, the birthdays, the way I'd found the tin, the way his absence had a shape I'd been living inside like a house. I told him I wasn't angry. Thirty-one pages proving I wasn't angry, which is how you know you are.

I sealed it. I addressed it to *Lucien Villalobos, Brussels* — the whole city, as if the postal service might simply hand it to whichever door he stood behind — and I carried it to the post office on the Place de la Vaillance, and I stood in line, and when I reached the counter I bought stamps for a bill instead, and walked home, and put the letter in the biscuit tin with the photographs.

Over the next decade I wrote him forty-one letters. I know the number because I counted once, drunk, at thirty, and then counted again sober to make sure the drunk version hadn't exaggerated. Forty-one letters in a biscuit tin behind a sweater I kept moving from drawer to drawer so the hiding place would stay fresh.

Some letters were furious. Most were reasonable, measured, adult — I was very good at reasonable by then, it's amazing what you can build out of deflection if you practice daily. A few were just lists: things I wanted to ask him. *What was your mother's name? Do you hum? Did you teach yourself to hear buildings, or does everyone and only I noticed? Why the green door — did you live there? Is the woman in the photograph the reason? Was it me? Was I a reason or an obstacle?*

That last pair I never let myself write more than once a year. Rationing. Even hoarders ration.

The phone calls started three years ago. Twice a year, sometimes three times, always the same pattern: two rings, silence, two rings again. Never a voicemail. Once, in a moment of weakness I have never repeated, I answered on the second ring, and there was breathing, and then a voice — a man's voice, older than my mother would be now, roughened like a road — said, "Is this—" and hung up.

*Is this.* Is this what? Is this you? Is this mine? Is this the son I drove past every day for forty years inside my own head?

I sat on my bed with the phone in my hand for an hour. Then I went and tuned a piano in Schaerbeek for a family with four children who used it as a coat rack, and the work steadied me the way it always does, because a piano asks only one question — *are you in tune with yourself* — and answers it honestly, and forgives you in fifths.

* * *

The F above middle C finally settled. I closed the fallboard and gathered my tools, and Odile was still in the folding chair, still watching, her cup empty, her face doing something complicated that I pretended not to see, because pretending not to see is my native language and I'm fluent.

"There," I said. "Done."

"Mm." She rose, joints announcing themselves like a ship in harbor. "You know what my husband used to say? He repaired clocks. Forty years, clocks. He said a stopped clock isn't broken, it's *refusing*. There's a difference. Broken things want fixing. Refusing things want asking."

"That's very poetic for a clock repairman."

"He was Flemish from the Marollen, we're allowed one poetry each." She picked up her bag. "Four times, Gideon. Next time, answer. Or next time, don't — but then stop letting it ring where I can hear it, because I'm old, and old women shouldn't have to listen to a young man's cowardice ringing out loud. It's bad for the blood pressure."

She marched toward the door. At the threshold she stopped, and without turning around she said:

"You know, I knew a man once who left without packing. There's a word for it, in the old speech of my streets. A whole word, one word, for leaving without packing. Nobody uses it anymore. Nobody under eighty even remembers it exists." She adjusted the strap of her bag. "Come by Sunday. Bring your ears."

And she was gone, down the steps and into the cold, leaving me alone in the hall with a tuned piano, an unanswered phone, and the sudden, irrational certainty that somewhere in this city there was a word I needed, and that it had been waiting for me the way the photographs had waited — patiently, in the dark, behind the sweaters.
