## 20. Eleven Days Becomes One

The doctor was honest the way a hammer is honest. He did not dress it up. He sat on the edge of his chair with his hands folded between his knees and said, *Madame Vermeersch, we are no longer treating an illness. We are keeping you comfortable.* He said it in careful French, the schoolroom kind, and Odile thought: even here, at the end, nobody speaks to me in my own tongue. There is no one left who could.

She thanked him. She had been thanking men her whole life for telling her what she already knew.

The heart was failing. That was the phrase they used — *failing* — as though it were a student who hadn't studied, a shop that hadn't paid its suppliers. Her heart had worked eighty-nine years without complaint through two wars, one widowhood, and forty winters of coal smoke, and now it was *failing*, and everyone spoke about it gently, in the passive voice, as if it were happening to someone slightly removed from her.

Eleven days, maybe. The doctor didn't say eleven days. The doctor said *weeks, perhaps* with a small hesitation before *perhaps* that told her everything. But Odile had done arithmetic all her life — on ration cards, on rent books, on the ledger of what people owed each other and never repaid — and she read the numbers behind his face.

Eleven days.

And the bus route died in eleven days too. She had seen the notice pasted to the shelter on the corner, the city's neat blue lettering announcing the rerouting with effect from the first of the month, as if a street could be rerouted, as if fifty years of footsteps could be issued a new timetable. They would take Bus 46 away and with it the last thread stitching the old quarter together, and the dialect would have nowhere left to stand. A language needs a street the way a coat needs shoulders.

Well then, she thought, lying in her own bed because she had refused the hospital — refused it flatly, with a word so old and sharp the district nurse laughed out loud before covering her mouth — well then. We will not waste them.

* * *

Gideon came every morning at nine, and she made him wait outside until exactly nine, because a schedule is a spine and she intended to die standing up, more or less.

He had changed. She had watched it happen over these weeks the way you watch weather move across the canal — slowly, then all at once. When he first sat at her kitchen table he was a man made entirely of almosts: almost asking, almost staying, almost a son to somebody. Now he arrived with the recorder in its padded case and the notebook swollen with loose pages, and when he spoke her words back to her he no longer flinched at the sounds. His mouth had learned. His mouth had learned what his father's mouth had known, though Gideon still couldn't say Lucien's name aloud. That would come, or it wouldn't. She was not going to be there either way, and she had stopped pretending this didn't cost her something. It cost her plenty. She simply declined to pay it in front of him.

"Today," she said, when he'd settled into his chair with the recorder running and the red light burning like a small obedient eye, "we do the words for coming home."

His pen paused over the page. "We haven't finished the ones for leaving."

"We finished those years ago, both of us." She let that sit. "Leaving is easy. Every language has a hundred words for leaving and children learn them before they can walk. Nobody teaches the words for arriving. You have to earn those."

She pulled the blanket higher on her knees. Her hands had gone thin as winter branches, blue-veined, spotted like old wallpaper, but they still moved when she talked, the way they always had — cooking hands, counting hands, hands that had slapped a husband's arm affectionately and once, memorably, a debt collector's face.

"*Terugkomme*," she said. "To come back. Plain enough. But listen — *terugkommen na dees*. To come back to this. To a place, yes, but also to a state. You come back to a kitchen, to a smell, to a version of yourself you thought was lost. Say it."

He said it. The vowels were nearly right. Nearly.

"The second syllable goes down," she said. "Like a step. Like you're stepping down into the house. *Na dees.* Again."

He stepped down into it. This time it landed, and she felt something loosen in her chest that had nothing to do with the failing heart — or everything to do with it, since time was the only currency left and he was spending it well.

"Good," she said. "Now the hard one."

* * *

They worked through the morning. Rain came against the window in the fine sideways way it had, the Anderlecht rain that never committed to falling properly, just drifted and reconsidered, and she taught him the word for that too — *motregen*, drizzle, but the old word, the street word, which meant also a mood, a grayness worn inside a person. *Hij heeft motregen in hem.* He has drizzle in him. She had used it about her husband, Pieter, in the last year of his life, and saying it now she heard her own young voice under the old one, two women layered like paint.

By noon her breath was short. She knew the signs; she had nursed half the street through their endings. She raised one finger and Gideon stopped mid-word, attentive as a tuning fork.

"Rest," he said.

"I don't pay you to tell me to rest."

"You don't pay me at all."

"No," she agreed, pleased. "Which makes my instructions binding."

But she rested. She let him make the soup — he had learned, God help her, he had actually learned, and the *soep met balletjes* was respectable now, the meatballs seasoned right, though he still browned the onions too fast, impatient, like his father before him, though she would carry that comparison to her grave unspoken. She ate three spoonfuls to keep him from worrying and pushed the bowl away with the authority of a woman who had fed a neighborhood.

In the afternoon they went back to work, because there were entries left.

* * *

It was during the afternoon session that her voice began to go.

Not all at once. It thinned, frayed at the edges, like cloth rubbed thin at the fold. Words she had said ten thousand times came out wrong-shaped, and she heard herself mispronounce her own mother tongue and felt a fury so clean and total it frightened even her. Her body was taking the language away piece by piece, the same way the city was taking the bus, the same way the decades had taken everyone she'd ever spoken it with — by increments, by rerouting, by funerals.

"Gideon." Her voice cracked on the second syllable of his name and she hated it. "If I lose a word — if I say it wrong — you write down what I *meant*. Not what I say. Promise me."

"I promise."

"Because the dictionary doesn't record the dying. It records the word. The dying is just how we get there." She closed her eyes briefly. "My mother lost French first, you know. At the end she had only Marollien left, and the priest couldn't understand her confession, and she died with sins on her soul because the Church had sent a Fleming." A dry sound that was almost a laugh. "I swore I'd die bilingual if it killed me."

"You're doing better than bilingual," Gideon said quietly. "You're dying trilingual. There's me mangling all of them."

She opened her eyes and looked at him — this tall, careful, stubborn man sitting in Pieter's old chair with his father's hands and none of his father's cowardice, or rather all of it, conquered daily, which is the only courage worth the name — and she thought: *there. That one. That is the entry that matters, and there is no word for it in any language I own.*

"Dictate," she said.

* * *

The last days folded into each other. Nine o'clock, the recorder, the red light. The district nurse came and went and learned to stop suggesting the hospital. Neighbors knocked softly and left bread, flowers, bottles of geuze that Odile ordered Gideon to hide from herself and then to produce, one small glass, at four o'clock, medicinal.

On the fourth day she taught him the words for homecoming proper. Not *terugkomme* — the mechanics of return — but the deep ones. The word for the feeling of your own street after long absence, which was also the word for the smell of rain on warm stone. The word for a door left unlocked for someone expected. The word for setting a place at table for the dead, which doubled as the word for hope, because in the old quarter those had never been separated.

"They'll ask why," she said, breathless after the longest entry yet. "Whoever finds these recordings. Some student, some archivist. They'll ask why a language needs a word for setting a place for the dead. And you'll say—" she pointed at him with one crooked finger— "you'll say: because the dead set places for us first. Everything else is repayment."

He wrote it down. His hand shook slightly. Good, she thought. Let it shake. Steady hands write lies.

On the sixth day she corrected his pronunciation of her own name. He had been saying *Vermeersch* with a soft Flemish *g* creeping into the middle of it, some ghost of standard Dutch, and she stopped him mid-sentence and made him say it six times until the *r* rolled off the roof of the mouth the way it did in the old streets, rough, unapologetic, a sound like a cart on cobblestones.

"That name walked from Bruges to Brussels in 1840," she said. "It survived two spelling reforms and one German occupation. It will survive you."

"Yes, Madame Vermeersch."

"Better." She settled back against the pillows. "Now. The word for staying."

* * *

There was, she told him, no single word. Of course there wasn't. Staying was not one act but a thousand small refusals to leave, renewed daily, and the old speech had broken it apart the way a jeweler breaks a chain to price the links.

A word for staying when staying costs you. A word for staying put out of stubbornness, which was affectionate, almost proud. A word for staying that had curdled into imprisonment and was used mostly about marriages and mortgages. And one more — she made him lean close to catch it, her voice down to embers now — a word that meant *to stay for someone*, to remain in a place not because of the place at all but because of who was in it.

"That one," she whispered, "is the rarest. That one most people never use correctly in their whole lives. Because to use it right you have to mean it."

"Say it again," Gideon said. "Slowly."

She said it again. Slowly. And watched him write it in the notebook, and watched him understand, and said nothing further, because a teacher knows when the lesson has landed and anything more is just noise over the bell.

* * *

On the eighth day the doctor came again and used the passive voice more than ever, and after he left Odile asked Gideon to bring the ledger down from the shelf — her ledger, the old account book where she had recorded, across sixty years, every debt and payment of the quarter, flour and favors and feuds, kept in a shorthand of her own invention that no one alive but Gideon could now read.

"Read me the last page," she said.

He read. Old names. Settled accounts. A column of small crosses marking debts forgiven rather than repaid.

"There's room," he said.

"There is indeed." She looked at the ceiling, where the water stain had bloomed into a new continent overnight. "Write today's date. Then write: *all accounts current.*"

He wrote it. She heard the nib scratch and thought: sixty years of bookkeeping, and it comes down to four words. Not balanced — she was no fool, life was never balanced, it was only survived — but *current*. Up to date. Nothing owed that could still be paid, nothing owed that couldn't be forgiven.

"Now the notebook," she said. "Last entries. My voice won't last the week and we both know it, so we do them tonight, all of them, and tomorrow—" she paused, and the pause cost her breath— "tomorrow I rest."

"What's left?" he asked.

"Three words." She held up fingers that trembled and despised themselves for it. "One for goodbye that isn't goodbye. One for the moment just before dawn. And one—" her voice caught, frayed, held— "one I've been saving."

* * *

They worked until the lamp burned low and the rain turned serious, real rain at last, drumming on the glass like a neighbor demanding entry. She gave him the word for goodbye-that-isn't-goodbye, the phrase mothers called down stairwells, meaning *until I see you again, and I will see you again, and don't argue with me.* She gave him the pre-dawn word, the hour her generation had trusted above all others, the hour when the quarter was honest.

And then she lay still a long time, listening to her own heart conduct its failing business, and Gideon waited, patient, the recorder turning, the red light steady.

"The last one," he prompted gently.

"I know what it is," she snapped, and then, softer: "I know what it is. Give me a moment. Even dictionaries get to breathe."

She looked at him sitting there in the lamplight, this man who had come to her as a stranger and turned out to be the last speaker of a house she'd loved, the inheritor of a guilt that was never his, the keeper now of every word she had. Outside, far off toward the depot, a bus sighed through the wet streets — the 46, running its old route, one night fewer remaining.

"The word," she said finally, "means *I stayed because of you.* It means: the place was hard, the times were hard, every reason to leave stood at the door with its hat on — and I stayed because of you. It is not a word for lovers. It is bigger than that. It is the word you say to whoever made staying bearable." Her breath whistled faintly. "I have used it twice in my life. Once to Pieter. Once to this house, the day I decided not to sell it."

"And now?"

"Now I'm going to teach it to you," Odile said, "and you are going to use it correctly, someday, to someone — a street, a woman, a dead man, I don't presume to say — and when you do, you'll pronounce it properly, because I will haunt you otherwise."

She taught it to him. He repeated it back, once, twice, until the sounds sat right in his mouth, until the word belonged to him the way a key belongs to a lock it was cut for.

Then she said, "Turn that machine off. We're done working."

He switched off the recorder, and in the sudden silence she reached out and took his hand — hers cold as canal water, his warm, startled — and held it, and neither of them said anything at all, because the final entry needed no recording. It was already written somewhere no fire could reach.

"Tomorrow," she murmured, drifting, "we don't work. Tomorrow you go to the depot. Find out which platform the 46 leaves from. Its last night is coming, boy, and I intend to be difficult about it from my deathbed — I want to hear my streets one more time, every stop announced in the old tongue, and I want *you* to be the voice."

Her eyes were closing. At the door he paused, and she heard him hesitate — the old habit, the almost — and then, for the first time in all these months, he didn't almost.

"*Tot ziens,*" he said, in her language, in her mother's language, in the language of the condemned streets — *until I see you again.*

And Odile Vermeersch, alone in the dark with her failing heart and her finished ledger, smiled and answered him the way her mother had answered from stairwells a century ago:

"*En ik zie u nog wel.* And see you I shall."
