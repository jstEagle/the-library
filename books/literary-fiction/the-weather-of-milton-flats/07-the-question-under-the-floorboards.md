## 7. The Question Under the Floorboards

The house went quiet the way a church goes quiet after everyone has gone home—still holding all that had been said in it, but no longer willing to say anything itself.

I stood in my kitchen with my coat still on, the funeral program creased in my pocket where I'd worried it into softness. Outside, the November dark pressed against the windows like something patient. The dishes from the reception sat unwashed in the sink because Maren had brought them over—casserole dishes, a punch bowl, three kinds of pie plates—and I hadn't been able to face them yet. Washing them would mean the day was over. As long as they sat there, my mother was still technically being mourned by someone.

Daniel Okafor's voice was still in the house too, though he'd left an hour ago. *Nyström-Voss.* He'd said it twice before Anders shut him down, and both times it had gone through me like cold water through a cracked pipe—finding every channel I'd spent fifteen years sealing.

I took off my coat. Hung it on the hook. Did not wash the dishes.

Instead I did what I always did when the name surfaced, when the past knocked at the door in some new disguise: I went to the floorboards.

* * *

The letter lived under the third board from the window in my bedroom closet, beneath where the shoe boxes used to sit before I moved them to make the hiding place look like nothing. It was Elias who taught me about floorboards—not as hiding places, exactly, but as archives. When we were children he kept his treasures there: a hawk feather, a marble with a twisted ribbon of green inside, a baseball card gone soft at the corners. "Paper burns," he told me once, eight years old and grave as a judge, "but wood remembers where you put things."

He'd been wrong about the paper. Wrong in the way that mattered most.

I knelt on the closet floor with the overhead light off, working by the spill from the bedroom lamp, and pried up the board with the flathead screwdriver I kept in the closet for this purpose and no other. The nail groaned. The board came up the way it always came up—reluctant, then suddenly, like a decision being made.

There it was. The tin. A Prince Albert tobacco tin, rusted at the seams, light as a bird's bone. Inside: the letter. The only thing Elias ever wrote to me after he left, if you could call leaving what he did. If you could call it writing, what the fire had left of it.

I carried the tin to the bed and sat with it in my lap for a long time before I opened it. This was part of the ritual too. There were years when I couldn't open it at all, when I just let it sit against my thighs, warming slowly to my body heat, and that was enough—that communion of weight. Other years I opened it within minutes of coming through the door. Tonight I lasted maybe four minutes.

The envelope was half-burned. That was the first thing, always the first thing—the scorch along its bottom edge, the corner gone entirely, so that the address read *Beatrix Nyst* and then nothing, just char, as though even the fire had wanted to claim him mid-name. The postmark was legible: three days after the night everything changed. Mailed from two towns over, from a place called Halden, which I had looked up once on a map and found to be barely more than a crossroads, a grain elevator and a Lutheran church and a gas station. He had gotten that far. Two towns. Forty miles. And then he had stepped off the map of the world forever.

Inside, the paper was worse than the envelope. The bottom third of the single sheet was burned away, and what remained was water-stained where someone—I never learned who—had apparently tried to put him out, or put the letter out, or both. The ink had run in places. But the top held. The top was his handwriting, that slanted, urgent hand that looked like it was always trying to get ahead of itself:

*Bea —*

*I know what you did.*

Four words. Four words and then the burn took over, and then, surviving above the scorch line like figures on a hillside after a flood, a few more lines:

*—don't come looking. Whatever they tell you happened, I was never—*

And then nothing. The rest of him was ash.

Fifteen years I had read those fragments. Fifteen years I had turned them over the way you turn a stone in your pocket until it wears smooth, trying every arrangement of the missing words. *Whatever they tell you happened, I was never afraid. I was never here. I was never sorry.* Each version made a different man of him. Each version made a different crime of mine.

But the first four words never changed. *I know what you did.* He knew. He had known from the beginning—from that night, or the morning after, or somewhere in the terrible interval between—and he had written to tell me so, and then he had written nothing else, ever again, and the silence had done the rest of the telling.

* * *

Here is what I did instead of sleeping that night: I sat on the edge of my bed with the letter in my hands and I conducted, once again, the trial that has no verdict.

The prosecution went first, as it always does. Exhibit A: I struck the match. Not literally—I want to be precise about this, precision is all I have left—but I set it in motion. I soaked the rags. I chose the night, the dry October week when the fields were stubble and the wind came down off the ridge smelling of dust and ending. I knew my father kept the ledgers in the back office of the house, knew he checked them on Sundays, knew that without them he was a man with no proof of anything except what people remembered, and memory, I had learned, could be arranged. I was twenty-three years old and I thought fire was a clean instrument. Fire takes everything, I told myself. Fire doesn't keep records.

Exhibit B: my father's ledgers. What was in them I will not write down even now, alone, at midnight, in my own house, with the door locked. Some sentences are load-bearing walls; remove them and the whole structure comes down. Let it stand that my father, Henrik Nyström-Voss, kept accounts the way other men keep diaries, and that what he accounted for was not money—or not only money—and that the family name we stopped using was not changed for a fresh start, whatever Maren says, whatever the story says. We changed it the way you change a bandage you're afraid to look under.

Exhibit C: Elias. Seventeen years old. Asleep down the hall. Who woke, who saw, who understood faster than I did what the fire would do to us—to me—and who made a choice in the smoke and the noise while I stood in the yard watching our house go up like a lantern being lit from inside. He didn't ask me. I want that on the record. He never asked me, never negotiated, never extracted a promise. He simply walked into the trees behind the burning house and became a boy who ran away, and the town, which needed a story more than it needed a son, gave him one: troubled Elias, wild Elias, the one who was always going to leave anyway. And then, when no body turned up, the softer verdict: dead, probably. Poor Nystroms. Lost their house and their boy in the same season.

The defense rests, as it always rests, on this: I was twenty-three, and I thought I was saving us, and I did not know the price would be paid in installments across fifteen years, by everyone, forever.

But here is what the defense cannot answer, what it has never been able to answer, what keeps the trial running night after night like a machine no one can switch off: Elias knew. And knowing, he paid. And I let him.

That was the stone in my chest. Not the fire. The fire was over in forty minutes. The stone was the arithmetic after—the ledger of my own, the one I kept in the dark under the floorboards of myself, where the entries never balanced and never closed.

* * *

Sometime after two I got up. I don't remember deciding to; my body simply arrived in the kitchen, and my hands were doing a thing I had watched them do from a great distance many times before: crumpling the letter.

This happens perhaps twice a year now. In the early years it was nightly. I would hold the letter over the sink, over the stove burner, over the woodstove in the old days, and my hands would begin the work on their own—folding, crushing, the paper crackling softly like something alive being hurt. The logic was always the same and always flawless: burn it, and the evidence dies. Burn it, and the last thing in the world that says *I know what you did* stops saying it. Burn it, and I am free to be the woman the town believes in—quiet Beatrix, sad Beatrix, the one who lost her brother and her mother and her name and never complained. Burn it, and the trial adjourns for good.

Twice a year, my hands try. Twice a year, I stop them.

Tonight I stood at the kitchen counter with the letter crushed to a fist-sized knot in my right hand, and I looked at the stove, and I even reached out—this part is true, I reached out and turned the dial, and heard the gas catch with its soft blue *whump*, and held my hand with the letter in it over the grate, feeling the heat climb toward my knuckles like something rising to meet me.

And then I smelled it. Or imagined I smelled it—the scorched-edge smell, the smell that lives in the paper permanently now, baked into it that night, so that the letter carries its own death around with it like a scent. And underneath that smell, fainter, impossible, the ghost of another smell: woodsmoke and October and the particular sweetness of a house giving itself up. My nose has never forgiven that night. It finds the fire in everything.

I turned off the burner.

I stood there in the dark kitchen with the gas ticking as it cooled, and I uncrushed the letter one fold at a time, smoothing it against the counter with the heel of my hand, the way you smooth a sheet over someone sleeping. The creases would never fully come out. They never do. The letter is mostly crease now, mostly scar tissue, held together at the burn line by a fragility that means each unfolding might be its last. One day I will open it and it will come apart in my hands like a moth's wing, and I don't know what I'll do then. Probably carry the pieces. Probably carry them the way I've carried the whole.

Because here is the truth I arrive at every time, at the end of every trial, at the bottom of every floorboard: I don't keep the letter as punishment. Punishment would be easy. Punishment has a shape, a sentence, an end date. I keep it because it is the last thing Elias ever gave me, and because those four words—*I know what you did*—are the only honest sentence anyone in my family has spoken in fifteen years. The whole architecture of our lives—the new name, the rehearsed stories, Maren's bright deflections, Anders's flinty loyalty, my mother's beautiful, terrible silence—all of it stands on the ground that Elias cleared when he walked into the trees. And I am the only one left who visits the foundation. I am the keeper of the question under the floorboards.

My mother died without asking me. That is the thing I could not think about at the funeral and can barely think about now. Fifty-one weeks ago, in the hospice room that smelled of lavender and plastic, she had gripped my wrist with a strength that surprised us both and looked at me with eyes already halfway to elsewhere, and she had said, "Beatrix." Just that. My name. The whole name, not Bea, not the shortened public version. And I had leaned close and waited for the confession or the absolution or the accusation—waited for her to finally open the floorboard of herself—and she had said, "Take care of the postcards," and closed her eyes.

She knew about the postcards. She had known for years, I think. And that was her sentence for me: not forgiveness, not exposure, but *continuance*. Keep writing. Keep practicing the words you can't say. Carry it.

I smoothed the letter flat one final time and refolded it along its scar-lines and put it back in the tin, and the tin back under the floorboard, and pressed the board down with my palm until the nail found its grip. Wood remembers where you put things. Elias taught me that. Elias, who is either dead in an unmarked grave or alive under some other sky, depending on which hour of the night you ask me.

* * *

I slept maybe two hours, and woke at dawn to a light the color of dishwater, and lay listening to the house tick and settle, and thought about Daniel Okafor.

*Nyström-Voss.* He had said it at the graveside like a man testing whether a lock still worked. And Anders had moved on him fast, too fast, the way you move on a wasp—not angry so much as terrified of what happens if it lands. I had watched my brother's face do something I'd never seen it do in public: flicker. For half a second, under the grief and the black wool, Anders had looked like a man hearing a floorboard creak in a house he believed empty.

And Daniel had looked at me. Across the raw brown earth, across the lowered box and the folded chairs, he had looked at me with an expression I recognized because I wear it myself, in mirrors, at 3 a.m.: the expression of someone carrying a question they've promised someone they'd ask.

He claimed to be a friend of Elias. A friend of a dead man, or of a vanished one. Either way, a friend of the question.

I got up. I made coffee I didn't drink. I stood at the window watching the frost burn off the roofs across the street, and I admitted to the empty kitchen what I had not admitted to anyone, including myself, in fifteen years:

I was tired. Tired of the ritual, tired of the tin, tired of the trial that never adjourned, tired of being the keeper of a question whose answer had calcified into furniture. My mother was in the ground. The last witness to the arrangement—the arranger herself—was gone. Whatever held the family story together now was habit, and habit is just fear that's forgotten why it started.

If Daniel Okafor had come to Milton Flats to dig, then some part of me—a part I'd fed postcards for fifteen years, a part that wrote *Dear Elias, today I almost told you* every Sunday like a woman doing penance in installments—some part of me wanted to hand him the shovel.

The phone rang.

I looked at it for three rings before I answered, because callers at dawn after funerals are never bearers of ordinary news. The voice was male, unfamiliar, careful.

"Ms. Nystrom? This is Daniel Okafor. I'm sorry to call so early." A pause, and I heard him choose his next words the way you test ice. "I found something. At the property yesterday, after you dropped me off. And I think—" another pause, longer, the ice creaking—"I think you should see it. Before your brother does."

I stood very still. Behind me, down the hall, the closet door stood open a hand's width, and beyond it, under the floorboard, the letter lay in its tin, saying what it always said.

"I know what you did," I said—not to Daniel, not aloud, but to the quiet kitchen, to the frost, to the fifteen-year silence. Then, into the phone: "All right. Tell me where."
