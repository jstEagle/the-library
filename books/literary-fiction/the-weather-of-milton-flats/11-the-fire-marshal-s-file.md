## 11. The Fire Marshal's File

The Milton Flats Public Library occupied the old Carnegie building on Corliss Street, its sandstone facade gone the color of weak tea, and it smelled the way all small-town libraries smell: of paper slowly surrendering to time and of the particular dust that gathers in the seams of radiators. Daniel Okafor had been in a hundred libraries like it across four states, and he had learned that the truth of a town lived not in its churches or its courthouses but in the microfilm cabinet nobody bothered to lock.

He arrived at nine-fifteen on Monday morning, an hour after opening, because arriving too early announced intention. He wore the same gray jacket he'd worn to the funeral—funerals and libraries, he'd found, required the same costume of respectful neutrality—and he carried a canvas tote bag containing nothing but a legal pad and two pens, because a man who carried a camera into a library looked like a journalist, and a man who looked like a journalist got remembered.

The librarian was a woman in her sixties named Dolores, according to the laminated nameplate, with reading glasses on a beaded chain and the unhurried competence of someone who had answered every possible question for forty years and could no longer be surprised. She was reshelving biographies when he approached the desk.

"I'm doing some family research," he said, which was true in the way that mattered. "Historical. Newspaper archives."

"Microfilm's in the back room," she said, already turning back to her cart. "Index cards are in the green binder. If you need anything after 1985 you'll want the digital terminal, but I'm guessing you don't."

"Why do you guess that?"

She glanced at him over her glasses with something almost like amusement. "People doing family research always start before they were born. It's the ones who start after who are usually lawyers." She let that sit for a moment. "Green binder. Back room. The machine sticks sometimes—jiggle the lever on the left, don't force it."

* * *

The *Milton Flats Courier-Dispatch* had published weekly until 2009 and twice weekly after that, and the green binder held its index in a librarian's careful hand, entries going back to 1961. Daniel turned pages until his finger stopped on the entry he had driven eleven hours to find:

**FIRE — NYSTROM PROPERTY, COUNTY RD 12 — OCT 1998 — see also ARSON INVESTIGATION, CORONER'S INQUEST (none), MISSING PERSONS**

Three entries. He wrote down the reel numbers and fed the first spool into the reader, jiggling the lever as instructed, and the machine hummed to life and threw the past onto the screen in grainy silver-gray.

The first article ran October 14, 1998, three days after the fire. He read it twice, then a third time, forcing himself to read it the way a stranger would—not the way a man reads who already knows how the story ends.

**BLAZE DESTROYS NYSTROM FARMHOUSE; SON UNACCOUNTED FOR**

The house of Gustav and Ingrid Nystrom-Voss on County Road 12 was destroyed by fire late Friday evening. Firefighters from Milton Flats and two neighboring departments responded but were unable to save the structure. Gustav Nystrom-Voss, 54, was treated at the scene for smoke inhalation. His wife Ingrid declined treatment. A son, Elias, 19, has not been located, and authorities ask anyone with information regarding his whereabouts to come forward.

No mention of other children. No mention of a daughter. Daniel sat back in the creaking chair and let that absence settle over him. Fifteen years later, Beatrix Nystrom would tell him, in the dark on her porch, about the postcards she wrote every week to a burned address. But in October of 1998, the newspaper of record had looked at this family and seen only the people worth naming—the father, the mother, the missing son. The daughters had been invisible even then. Erasure, he was learning, was rarely a single act. It was a practice. A discipline. Something a whole town could get good at.

The second article ran October 21. Shorter. **INVESTIGATION CONTINUES INTO FARM FIRE; FAMILY DECLINES COMMENT.** One paragraph, and one sentence in it that made Daniel's pen stop moving: *Sources close to the investigation indicate the blaze is being treated as suspicious.*

Suspicious. Not arson yet. Suspicious—a word that did its work quietly, the way a rumor does, softening the ground before the verdict arrived.

The third article ran November 4, and it was the one he had come for.

**ARSON RULED IN FARM FIRE; AUTHORITIES SEEK ELIAS NYSTROM-VOSS FOR QUESTIONING**

Daniel read it standing up, though there was nowhere to go. The state fire marshal's office had determined the fire was incendiary in origin—accelerant patterns consistent with a poured trail, ignition source concentrated in the room where, the article noted delicately, "business records were stored." Elias Nystrom-Voss, described as having left the family home "under circumstances the family declines to discuss," was sought for questioning. Warrant pending. Anyone with information—

Daniel copied the article out by hand, word for word, including the byline: *R. Calloway, staff.* Then he sat back down and read the rest of the reel, month by month, hunting for what came next.

What came next was nothing.

That was the thing that made the hair rise on his forearms, there in the cold back room with the radiator ticking. There was no follow-up. No arrest. No inquest. No trial, no plea, no dismissal. In the last week of November, a brief item noted that the warrant for Elias Nystrom-Voss remained active. In December, a year-end roundup mentioned the fire in a list of unsolved matters. And then January came, and February, and the name appeared nowhere again—not in the police blotter, not in the courthouse column, not anywhere. A young man wanted for questioning in an arson investigation had simply been released from the record like a bird from a hand.

Cases didn't close themselves. Daniel knew that much. He had spent enough years around institutions—prisons, courts, the slow bureaucratic mills that ground human lives into case numbers—to know that a file either moved forward or it died, and when it died young, somebody had smothered it.

* * *

He spent Tuesday at the county courthouse, and the courthouse taught him the second half of the lesson.

Records requests in small counties were a matter of knowing which window to stand at and how long to stand there without apologizing. Daniel stood at the records window for forty minutes while a deputy sheriff with a mustache older than Daniel's career processed a title search for a farmer ahead of him, and when his turn came he asked, mildly, for the case file on the 1998 Nystrom-Voss fire investigation.

The deputy typed. Frowned. Typed again.

"That one's sealed," he said.

"Sealed?"

"Court order." The deputy said it the way men say things they've said many times, the words worn smooth. "Ninety-nine. Judge Hargrove. You can look at the order itself if you want, that part's public."

Daniel looked at the order. It was one page. It cited "privacy interests of surviving family members" and "the welfare of minor children in the household"—and there it was, the mechanism, elegant as a trap. Somewhere in that household there had still been minors in 1999. Anders, maybe; Maren certainly. Seal the file to protect the children, and the children grow up inside the seal, and the seal never comes up for air because everyone protected by it has learned to prefer the dark.

But sealing a court file was not the same as sealing everything, and Daniel had learned patience in places where patience was the only currency. The fire marshal's office was a state agency, and state agencies kept their own copies. The marshal who had signed the determination—D. R. Kowalczyk, per the newspaper—had retired sometime in the early 2000s, but agencies kept their determinations, their scene photographs, their lab reports on accelerant samples. Those were public records under the state's open-records act, unless someone had thought to seal them too.

Someone had thought of it. But someone else, apparently, had not gotten around to it, or had judged that a fifteen-year-old arson file in a dying farm town was not worth the paperwork. When Daniel's written request came back—eleven days later, in a manila envelope postmarked from the regional office—it contained a photocopy of a photocopy, sixty-one pages, some of them so faint the type floated like ghosts.

He took it back to the motel on Route 9, the one with the sign missing its L, and he laid the pages out on the bed in order, and he did not sleep for a long time.

* * *

The file told its story in fragments, the way bodies tell stories in morgues.

Incident report, October 11, 1998. First unit on scene at 11:47 p.m. Structure fully involved. Occupants accounted for except one.

Scene photographs. Daniel spread these out carefully, handling them by the edges. The house he had visited with Beatrix—the scorched lot, the chimney standing like a headstone—was here in its final hour, lit orange from within, sparks streaming off the roof into the dark like a reversed snowfall. And then the aftermath shots, taken at dawn: the collapsed east wing, the melted husk of a tractor, and the interior of the room where the accelerant pattern was found. Even in black-and-white photocopy, the pour pattern was unmistakable—a fan-shaped burn spreading outward from the base of a metal cabinet, the classic signature of liquid fire walking across a floor.

Lab report. Gasoline. Common unleaded, no additives, no traceable markers. Of course not. Whoever poured it had used gas from a can, the most ordinary weapon in rural America.

And then the interview summaries, and this was where the machinery began to show its gears.

There was an interview with Gustav Nystrom-Voss, conducted October 13, two days after the fire. The summary was terse, but certain phrases survived the summarizing: *subject evasive regarding contents of destroyed records.* *Subject stated business files were "personal" and declined to enumerate.* *Subject became agitated when asked about son's recent activities.* And then, appended in different ink, a handwritten note in the margin, unsigned: **G.N.V. pushing hard to close. Called the marshal's office twice this week. Ask K. what's going on.**

Daniel read that note five times. Somebody inside the investigation had been uneasy. Somebody had noticed the father leaning on the process and had written it down, and the note had survived, orphaned in a photocopied page, the fossil of a doubt.

There was an interview with Ingrid Nystrom-Voss—or rather, a record that an interview was attempted and declined. There was an interview with a neighbor, a Harlan Vetch, who reported seeing "a vehicle, dark-colored, leaving the property around eleven" on the night of the fire, heading toward the county road. The vehicle was never identified. Or rather—the summary said—the lead was "pursued without result."

And there was the timeline, and the timeline was the thing that broke the case open in Daniel's hands like a lock finally giving.

Elias Nystrom-Voss had last been seen, per the family's statements, on the afternoon of October 9. The fire occurred the night of October 11. The warrant issued October 29. And the case was closed—closed, administratively, suspect at large—on December 18, 1998, barely ten weeks after the fire, with the suspect unlocated, the vehicle unidentified, the accelerant can unrecovered, and the motive officially listed as—"Daniel flipped to the page, though he already knew what it would say"—*undetermined; believed financial.*

Believed financial. Believed by whom? By a father who had pushed hard to close, whose business records had burned in the room where the fire started, whose own potential culpability the file acknowledged nowhere and everywhere. The file's logic was a circle drawn by a careful hand: the son had access, the son had fled, therefore the son. But access proved nothing—everyone in that house had access. Flight proved nothing—or rather, flight proved fear, and fear is a country with many borders. And the fleeing had happened, if the family's statement was honest, *before* the fire, which meant Elias had run before there was anything to run from, unless he had set the fire and then run, unless, unless—the file offered no unlesses. The file had stopped asking questions on December 18, 1998, and every question Daniel asked now, he asked alone, fifteen years downstream, holding a dead man's doubts in a motel room that smelled of carpet cleaner.

Except Elias wasn't dead. That was the knowledge Daniel carried alone, the stone in his pocket through every library and courthouse and motel corridor: Elias was alive, and sick, and serving a sentence under another name for a crime that might have been this one or might have been another, and either way the sentence had been built on this foundation—this thin, hurried, leaned-upon foundation of a case closed in ten weeks by pressure Daniel could now almost see, like a handprint pressed into wet cement and smoothed over badly.

* * *

On Thursday he drove out to see the retired fire marshal.

Kowalczyk lived twelve miles north of town in a prefabricated house with a flagpole and a garden gone to seed for the winter. He answered the door in a flannel shirt and suspenders, seventy-some years old, eyes pale and level, and when Daniel introduced himself and said the words *Nystrom-Voss fire, 1998,* the old man's face did something complicated and quick—like weather crossing water—and then went still.

"You're the one at the funeral," Kowalczyk said. Not a question. "Ingrid's funeral. I heard about you."

"I was there."

"Huh." The old man stood in his doorway for a long moment, weighing something. Then he stepped back and held the door wide. "Well. Come in before the heat gets out. I've been waiting fifteen years for somebody to come ask me about that fire, and now that it's happening I find I need to sit down for it."

They sat at a kitchen table under a fluorescent light, and Kowalczyk poured coffee neither of them drank, and Daniel laid the photocopied file between them, and the old man put his hand flat on the pages like a man touching the face of someone he'd once loved and failed.

"You want to know why it closed fast," Kowalczyk said. It wasn't a question either.

"I want to know who closed it."

"Gustav Nystrom-Voss closed it." The old man said the name without heat, the way you say the name of a force of nature. "I ruled arson because it was arson—you don't get a pour pattern like that from a wiring fault, and I've been doing this since before you were born. But who set it?" He shook his head slowly. "Everybody in that family knew something. The boy took off days before the fire—that's in your file, and it's true, I had a deputy confirm it at the bus station in Halden. Days *before.* You understand? Whatever that boy was running from, he was running from it before there was any fire to run from. Which means either he set it and ran, which never made sense—he'd have run after—or he ran from whatever the fire was meant to destroy."

"The ledgers," Daniel said quietly. "The records in the cabinet."

Kowalczyk looked at him for a long moment. "You know about the ledgers."

"I know Gustav Nystrom-Voss was not the man the town thought he was."

"No," the old man said. "He was not." He turned his coffee cup a quarter-turn on the table, a small ritual gesture, a man buying time. "I wanted to keep it open. I wrote it up that way—open, active, persons unknown. And then I got a call from the county attorney, and then a call from above the county attorney, and then Judge Hargrove sealed my file, and I was told the matter was resolved to the satisfaction of the interested parties, and I was sixty-three years old and two years from my pension, and I let it happen." His voice didn't waver, but his hand, on the table, had gone white-knuckled around the cup. "I have regretted it every day since. Whatever that boy did or didn't do, he deserved better than a case that died of embarrassment. And whoever set that fire—" he lifted his pale eyes to Daniel's face, and held them there, and Daniel felt the weight of thirty years of fire scenes behind the look, all the burn patterns and pour trails and human wreckage the old man had read like scripture— "whoever set it is still carrying it. People think fires go out. Fires don't go out. They just move somewhere you can't see them and keep burning."

Daniel gathered the pages slowly, squaring their edges against his knee. Outside the window, the November fields lay brown and stubbled to the horizon, and a wind was coming up, moving through the dry grass in long silver waves, the way wind moves through anything that has been cut down and left standing.

"There's one more thing," Kowalczyk said as Daniel reached the door. "You didn't hear it from me, and it's not in any file. But the night of the fire, Harlan Vetch saw a dark vehicle leaving the property. We never identified it. But I'll tell you what I've thought about for fifteen years." The old man's voice dropped, not for drama but for accuracy, the precision of a man testifying at last. "Gustav Nystrom-Voss owned a dark vehicle. And Gustav Nystrom-Voss's truck was never once mentioned in any statement, any interview, any page of that file. You tell me how a man's own truck goes unexamined in an arson on his own land, and I'll retire a second time."

Daniel stood in the doorway, the file heavy against his ribs like a second heartbeat.

Because he could tell him. He was beginning to be able to tell him. The pieces were clicking together in the dark of him, gear into gear into gear, a buried machine waking up—and at the center of the machine, the one person the file never interviewed, the daughter the newspaper never named, the woman who wrote weekly letters to a burned address and whose hands had shaken on the scorched lot when he'd asked her one question too precise to be innocent.

Beatrix.

He had to know. And there was only one person alive who could tell him—and only one way to ask that wouldn't send her running the way her brother had run, days before the fire, from something the rest of them were still burning in.

He got in his car and drove back toward Milton Flats, toward the small house on Alder Street where a box of fifteen years of undelivered postcards waited in the dark, each one a confession rehearsed and swallowed, each one addressed to a man who had known all along.
