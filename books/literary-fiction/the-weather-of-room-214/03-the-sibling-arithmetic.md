## 3. The Sibling Arithmetic

The phone rang at 11:47 on a Tuesday night, which meant it was either an emergency or Caspian. In the Montrose family the two had long since become indistinguishable.

Wendell was at the reference desk of the Halloway Free Library, third hour of the night shift, watching the rain do its slow arithmetic against the tall windows—one drop, then two, then a number too large to count. The overhead lights were dimmed to their evening amber, and the reading room held only its usual population of the quietly displaced: a man in a canvas coat asleep with his cheek on an open atlas, a teenager highlighting a biology textbook with the fervor of someone cramming for a life she hadn't yet agreed to, and Marisol, who wasn't sleeping tonight but sitting by the radiator with her hands wrapped around a paper cup, staring at nothing with the particular patience of someone who had made peace with waiting.

Wendell let the phone ring twice more before answering. There was no one to disturb—the rule at the desk was that calls were permitted so long as they were conducted in the voice one used for finding books for strangers: low, level, faintly apologetic.

"Caspian."

"Wendell." His voice came through flattened by distance and cell towers, all the weather stripped out of it. "You got my email?"

"I got your email."

"And?"

"And I read it."

A pause on the line, the sound of Caspian recalibrating. He had never known what to do with Wendell's answers, which were always true and never useful. "The auctioneer needs a signed inventory authorization by Friday. I'm emailing you the form. You sign it, you scan it, you send it back. That's it. That's the whole thing."

"Okay."

"Okay meaning yes, or okay meaning you're going to make this difficult?"

"Okay meaning I'll sign it."

"Good." Papers rustled somewhere in Caspian's world—Caspian was always in the middle of papers, always mid-shuffle, as though his life were a filing system he'd inherited from their father and could never quite bring to order. "Odile's flying in the fourteenth. Her flight lands at 6:40, so if we do the walkthrough at eight that gives us—"

"Caspian."

"What."

"Do you want to talk about it? Any of it? The house, or—" Wendell stopped, because the sentence had already gone further than any Montrose sentence went, and finishing it felt like stepping onto ice they could hear cracking under the words. "Or anything."

The silence that followed had texture. It had grain, like old varnish. Wendell could hear their brother breathing on the other end of the continent, forty-nine years old, a man who ran a logistics company in Denver and whose hands, when Wendell tried to remember them from childhood, existed only as fists or as instruments—holding a wrench, holding a receipt, holding the door of the moving truck the day their father's things left the house on Marrow Street.

"There's nothing to talk about," Caspian said finally. "Dad left. Mom's in a home. The house costs eleven hundred dollars a month to sit empty. We sell it, we split it three ways, we're done. That's not cold, Wendell. That's just what happened."

"I didn't say it was cold."

"You were going to."

Outside, the rain thickened. The man with the atlas shifted in his sleep and the book slid an inch toward the table's edge; Wendell watched it hang there, teetering on its small cliff, and did not move to save it yet.

"How is she?" Caspian asked, and for one unguarded second his voice cracked open like a drawer, showing something soft and disordered inside—a child's sock drawer, mismatched and kept anyway. "Mom. Have you been out there?"

"I went Sunday. She called me your father again."

Another silence, this one longer, and Wendell understood that Caspian was doing what all three of them did when the family's one forbidden subject surfaced: he was standing very still, the way you stand still when a wasp lands on your sleeve, hoping it will lose interest and fly away without either of you having to acknowledge the other.

"The form," Caspian said. "Friday. And don't forget Odile wants the good bedroom furniture appraised separately, she thinks it might be worth something."

"It's not worth anything. It's veneer over particleboard."

"Then tell her that. I'm not getting in the middle of it."

He said it automatically, the way other people say *bless you* after a sneeze, and Wendell felt the familiar small ache open beneath the sternum—the ache that had no better name than *family*. Because that was the middle, wasn't it. All three of them lived permanently in the middle of each other, arranged like load-bearing walls, holding up a structure none of them would ever call a house.

"I'll send the form," Wendell said.

"Thursday," Caspian said. "Not Friday morning. Thursday."

"Thursday."

"Okay." And then, because Caspian Montrose could not end a call without converting blood into business: "Also the locksmith. For the back door. I booked him for the fifteenth, nine a.m., so be there or tell Odile to be there, somebody has to sign for it."

Click. No goodbye. There never was. Goodbye was a sentence, and sentences were not how the Montroses traveled.

* * *

Afterward Wendell sat at the desk for a while with the phone still warm against their ear, listening to the library breathe. The building had a nighttime respiration all its own: the boiler ticking in the basement like a patient heart, the radiators pinging as they cooled, the stacks settling by degrees into their dark. Thirty years of night shifts had taught Wendell the building's whole vocabulary, every creak and sigh, and Wendell had come to prefer it to most human conversation, because the building never pretended its noises meant something they didn't.

At 12:30, the phone lit again. Not a call. A text. Odile.

Wendell opened it the way you open a bill.

**odile:** flight confirmed. 6:40 arrival. rental car or will someone pick me up

Wendell typed: *I can pick you up.*

**odile:** ok

Three minutes passed. Then:

**odile:** does C have the inventory form handled

*I'll send it Thursday,* Wendell wrote.

**odile:** good

And then, forty minutes later, when Wendell had almost returned to the cataloging queue:

**odile:** mom's meds. does fernwood handle refills or do we

*They handle it. I checked last month.*

**odile:** good

That was all. That was everything. Wendell set the phone face-down on the desk and looked at it the way an archaeologist looks at a shard—this small glazed fragment of a civilization, from which the whole culture could be reconstructed. *Flight confirmed. Form handled. Meds handled. Good.* Four texts, twenty-six words, and not one of them a sentence in the sense that Wendell meant it—not one string of words that began in one person and arrived, alive, in another. Odile texted the way air traffic controllers spoke: all vectors and clearances, keeping three lives from colliding at altitude, never once saying what any of it was *for*.

Wendell had once, years ago, tested the system. It had been Thanksgiving, Odile visiting from Portland, the two of them alone in the kitchen of 214 Marrow Street while Caspian watched football with the volume too high, and Wendell had said—casually, leaning against the counter, casualness being the only safe vehicle for such cargo—"Do you ever miss him? Dad?"

Odile had been drying a dish. She had dried that dish for a very long time, thoroughly, professionally, until Wendell understood that the question had been received and processed and filed under *not actionable*, and then she had said, "Can you hand me the other towel? This one's saturated."

And Wendell had handed her the towel. Of course they had. That was the arithmetic: one question asked, zero questions answered, balance maintained. The family ledger closed every night at zero. It had closed at zero for thirty years, through holidays and funerals and hospital waiting rooms, and Wendell had begun to suspect that the zero was not emptiness but design—that somewhere in the founding documents of the Montrose family, in the constitution drafted the night their father's suitcase hit the porch, there was a clause, unwritten but ironclad: *Nothing shall be said that cannot be unsaid, and nothing shall be felt that requires saying.*

* * *

At 2 a.m., during the dead hours when even the insomniacs went home, Wendell took out the shoebox.

It lived under the desk now, in the same kneehole where it had lived under the kitchen table on Marrow Street, migrated across three decades and two apartments like a hermit crab carrying its shell. Inside: letters. Unsent letters, thirty years of them, each one addressed and stamped and sealed and then, at the last moment, denied—slid into the box instead of the mailbox, the way you might catch a word behind your teeth a half-second too late. Letters to their father at addresses that were guesses. Letters to Vera that said everything the visits couldn't. One letter, near the bottom, written in a child's slanted hand, the ink gone brown with age, addressed simply to *The Rain*.

Wendell didn't take it out tonight. They only rested their hand on the box's lid, feeling the cardboard's cool give, the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping animal.

The truth—which Wendell admitted to no one, least of all the shoebox—was that Wendell understood Odile perfectly. Understood the fragments, the vectors, the saturated towel. Because Wendell had built the same architecture, just in a different material. Where Odile built schedules, Wendell built silences. Where Caspian built ledgers, Wendell built a library of things never sent. Three siblings, three currencies, one exchange rate: everything convertible into nothing, nothing ever converted back.

They were not a cold family. That was the thing no outsider would ever believe. Their mother had loved them ferociously, had sewn Halloween costumes and learned every teacher's name and driven through ice storms for piano recitals, and their father—before whatever happened had happened—had been a man who laughed with his whole torso. The cold wasn't in them. The cold was *between* them, a weather system that moved in the night their father left and never lifted, and all three children had grown up inside it the way fish grow up inside water, never once thinking to name the wet.

Wendell thought about the letter from the lawyer, found last week in the returns bin—someone had wedged it between a returned cookbook and a water-damaged paperback, and Wendell had known before opening it what it was, the way you know thunder before the flash. *Re: Estate of Vera Montrose / Property at 214 Marrow Street.* Six weeks to auction. Six weeks until strangers walked through the rooms where Wendell's entire interior life had been built, bidding on the light fixtures.

Six weeks, also, until the three of them would have to be in the same house at the same time, and the house itself—the house that remembered everything, the height marks on the doorframe, the handwriting on the pantry wall—would be standing right there among them, unable to keep its mouth shut, full of sentences none of them had ever spoken.

*We communicate exclusively in arrangements,* Wendell thought, watching the rain. *Never in sentences.*

An arrangement was a plan for avoiding something. A sentence was a plan for reaching someone. The Montroses had thirty years of the first and a lifetime deficit of the second, and Wendell had begun, in the small hours of the night shift, to wonder what the interest on that debt came to. Whether it was the kind of debt that could still be paid. Whether their mother, whose mind was thinning to gauze, might be the only member of the family now honest enough to receive it—since she would not, could not, remember it tomorrow.

There was a terrible freedom in that thought, and Wendell put it down carefully, the way you put down a knife you've caught yourself looking at too long.

* * *

At 3:15, the man with the atlas woke, gathered his coat, and shuffled out past the desk. He paused, as he always did, and nodded—not thanks exactly, more a recognition between members of the same nocturnal order—and Wendell nodded back. The teenager left at 3:40. By 4, the reading room held only Marisol and the radiator's tick and the rain, which had gentled to something almost conversational.

Marisol came to the desk sometime after four, cup in hand, moving with the unhurried economy of someone who had learned to take up little space. She was perhaps fifty-five, perhaps sixty; her hair was gray at the temples and her eyes were quick and brown and missed nothing, which was the strangest thing about her, because people assumed the homeless saw nothing, when in fact they saw everything—they were the only ones awake for all of it.

"You get a phone call tonight," Marisol said. It wasn't a question. She'd been sitting close enough to hear the murmur, and besides, she noticed everything.

"My brother."

"Mm." She lowered herself into the chair across the desk, an honor she granted herself maybe once a month. "Family."

"Logistics," Wendell said.

"That's what I said." She smiled a little, around the rim of her cup. "My sister used to call me like that. Every call was a schedule. Never once asked how I was sleeping." She turned the cup slowly. "You know what I figured out, working nights all these years? People think the night is empty. It's not empty. It's just where everybody puts the things they can't say in daylight."

The rain tapped the glass. Somewhere in the stacks, the boiler ticked.

"Your brother," Marisol said, "he ask you how you are?"

"He asked if I'd sign a form."

"Mm-hmm." She nodded slowly, unsurprised, unsurprising. "And your sister?"

"She asked who's picking her up from the airport."

Marisol looked at Wendell for a long moment, and there was something in her look that Wendell recognized and dreaded in equal measure—the look of a person about to see you, which is a far more dangerous thing than being seen. "You know," she said gently, "you could answer those questions anyway. Even the ones nobody asks."

"They wouldn't know what to do with the answer."

"No," Marisol agreed. "Probably not. First time." She rose, tucked the chair in with strange courtesy, and stood there a moment in the amber light, a woman the world had misfiled and who had made her peace with the wrong shelf. "But you keep answering anyway. That's how it starts. Somebody in the family has to go first, and it's never going to be the one with the ledger."

She drifted back toward the radiator, toward her corner, her cup steaming faintly in the cold air of the great room, and Wendell sat at the desk with her words settling over them like the rain outside—slow, persistent, finding every seam.

On the desk lay the lawyer's letter. Six weeks. Wendell picked up a pen, pulled a sheet of library letterhead toward them, and wrote at the top, in the small careful hand of thirty years' practice: *Dear Caspian —*

And then sat there, pen hovering, while the rain counted down the nights.
