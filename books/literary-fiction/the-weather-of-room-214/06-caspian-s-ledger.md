## 6. Caspian's Ledger

Caspian Montrose arrived at the Starlite Diner eleven minutes early, which was not punctuality but a strategy. He needed time to arrange the table before the others arrived—time to choose the booth at the back, the one with the sightline to the door and the wall at his spine, the one where no one could sit behind him. He ordered coffee he didn't want, black, because ordering something gave him a reason to be sitting there alone that had nothing to do with nerves. He spread his documents on the formica like a man laying out instruments before surgery.

He had a folder. Of course he had a folder. The folder was manila, tabbed, and contained three copies of everything: the auction house contract, the appraisal summary, a timeline printed in fourteen-point type because Odile claimed she couldn't read anything smaller on her phone anymore, and a one-page executive summary he'd drafted four times and rewritten until it sounded less like a eulogy and more like a transaction. That was the trick of it. You could put almost anything in front of people if you dressed it as arithmetic. Grief was unbearable. A line item was not.

The waitress refilled his cup without asking. He liked diners for exactly this—the impersonal service, the way nobody wanted your story. The Starlite hadn't changed since he was nineteen and worked the grill summers home from state college. Same vinyl booths, same pie case humming under fluorescent light, same smell of grease and burnt coffee that had soaked into the walls so thoroughly it had become a kind of architecture. His father used to bring them here on Saturday mornings, back when Saturdays still meant something, and order three eggs over easy and let Caspian have the corner toast, the piece with the most butter surface area. Caspian had not thought about the corner toast in twenty years and now here it was, arriving uninvited, sitting down across from him like a ghost with an appetite.

He checked his watch. Wendell would be early too. Wendell was always early; it was one of the few things about his youngest sibling that Caspian could predict with confidence, and he hoarded predictions the way other men hoarded tools. Odile would be late by exactly enough minutes to make her lateness seem accidental rather than chosen.

* * *

Wendell came in at five past the hour, coat damp from a rain that had started sometime during their drive over and showed no intention of stopping. They stood just inside the door for a moment, letting their eyes adjust, and Caspian watched them do the same survey he'd done—the room, the exits, the table—and felt an unexpected flare of kinship that he immediately suppressed. Kinship was not on today's agenda.

"Wendell." He nodded at the seat across from him.

"Caspian." Wendell sat, unwound a scarf they'd knitted themselves, badly, in some fit of domestic ambition years ago. "You got here first."

"I always get here first."

"You do," Wendell agreed, and there was something in their voice—not quite accusation, more like notation—that made Caspian's jaw tighten. Wendell had this habit of agreeing with you in a tone that suggested you'd confessed to something. Thirty years of night shifts had given their voice a certain quality, soft and level, the voice of someone who spent their days whispering in the stacks and their nights among people who whispered for darker reasons. It was a voice that made you want to fill silence, and Caspian did not fill silence. Silence was his native language. He'd been fluent since he was thirteen years old.

"Odile?" Wendell asked.

"Landing at six. She'll be here by seven if traffic cooperates."

"That's forty-five minutes."

"It's Odile."

Wendell smiled faintly and looked at the menu, though Caspian knew they wouldn't order. Wendell never ordered at these things. They'd nurse a tea for two hours like communion, taking small sips at moments of tension, a kind of self-administered sedation. Caspian found the habit infuriating and, in some chamber of himself he kept locked even from himself, familiar. He recognized avoidance when he saw it because he'd built a cathedral out of it.

He opened the folder anyway. Might as well start. Every minute Odile wasn't here was a minute he could spend talking about things that were safe: square footage, reserve prices, buyer's premiums.

"So." He slid a copy of the timeline across the table. "Auction's confirmed for the sixth. Preview day is the fourth. I've already signed the listing agreement with Hargrove & Finch—they came recommended by two separate sources, which mattered to me, because I don't want some cowboy operation turning Mother's—" he paused, recalibrated, "—turning the estate into a circus."

"The estate," Wendell repeated.

"The house, Wendell. The house and its contents. That's what an estate is."

"I know what an estate is." Wendell turned the timeline toward themselves and read it top to bottom, slowly, the way they read everything, as though the words might contain a second document hidden between the lines. "You've allocated ninety minutes for the walkthrough."

"Ninety minutes is generous. We grew up there. We know where everything is."

"Do we?"

Caspian looked up. Wendell was watching him with those library eyes, patient and terrible, and Caspian understood that the question was not about the location of furniture.

"We know where everything is," he said again, flatter, daring them to make it into something.

Wendell let it go. That was the arrangement, the great silent treaty the three of them had ratified without ever signing: you don't push, I won't push, and we'll all die politely. Caspian returned to his papers with relief that tasted like shame.

* * *

Odile arrived at 6:52, phone pressed to her ear, finishing a call to someone named Deb about something called the Henderson file, and she stayed on the call while she slid into the booth beside Wendell, holding up one finger to them both—*one moment*—a gesture she'd been making to her family for so long it had worn smooth as a river stone. She smelled of airplane air and a perfume Caspian vaguely remembered costing more than his first car. Her hair was perfect. Everything about Odile was perfect the way a showroom kitchen is perfect: beautiful, expensive, and arranged so that no one would ever actually cook in it.

"—no, tell him the contingency has to be in writing or there's no deal. Yes. Monday. Love you too." She hung up and smiled at them both, bright and brief as a camera flash. "Sorry. Traffic from the airport is a war crime."

"You're late," Caspian said.

"I'm here, aren't I?" She picked up the timeline. "What am I looking at?"

"The plan."

"There's a plan?"

"There's always a plan," Wendell said quietly, and Odile laughed—a real laugh, short and surprised—and for one strange second the three of them were almost warm. Then Odile reached the appraisal summary and the warmth closed over like water.

"They're selling the piano?" she said.

"The piano appraises at twelve hundred dollars. The moving costs exceed its value. It goes in the general lot."

"It was Mother's piano."

"It was a Kimball upright manufactured in 1974, Odile. There are ten thousand of them in church basements across America."

She looked at him the way she'd looked at him when they were children and he'd explained, with diagrams, why her belief in Santa Claus was statistically indefensible. Not hurt. Recalibrating. Odile didn't do hurt in public. Hurt went into a drawer somewhere in Scottsdale, in a house with a husband who traveled, filed under a heading Caspian had never been permitted to read.

"Fine," she said. "The piano goes in the general lot. What else?"

And that was the meeting, really, for the better part of an hour. Caspian walked them through it all—the reserve price, the commission structure, the division of proceeds into three equal shares (he'd insisted on equal; unequal money was how families detonated), the disposal of the hazardous materials in the garage, the question of Vera's remaining effects at Fernwood. He answered every objection before it was raised. He had anticipated Odile's concern about the neighbors' perception and prepared a paragraph on discretion. He had anticipated Wendell's concern about—well, he'd assumed Wendell would have a concern, and he'd prepared a generic response about how everyone's feelings were valid and also the auction was happening.

The rain thickened against the windows. The diner filled and emptied around them in the indifferent rhythm of places that serve everyone. Somewhere behind the pie case a radio played something from the eighties, low enough to be weather.

It was going well. It was going exactly according to plan. And then Wendell set down their tea, aligned the cup precisely with the edge of the table, and said:

"When are we going to talk about Dad?"

* * *

The word landed on the table like a dropped glass.

Caspian kept his eyes on his papers. He became aware of his own heartbeat, steady, deliberate, a metronome he maintained through practice. Behind him the door opened and closed, letting in a gust of wet cold and the sound of the parking lot, and he counted—one, two, three—until it swung shut again.

"What about him?" he said.

"He's in everything we're doing here. Every decision we've made about that house has been shaped around a hole where he should be. We're pricing his workbench, Caspian. We're cataloging his tools. We're selling his desk, and none of us has said his name in thirty years, and I think—" Wendell's voice stayed level, maddeningly level, "I think maybe we should ask Mom. While she can still answer. About that night. About why he left. Before the auction, before the house is gone, before there's nothing left to ask about."

Odile had gone very still beside them. Stillness, in Odile, was a scream.

"She wouldn't understand the question," Caspian said.

"She might."

"She has dementia, Wendell. She calls you by my father's name. You want to sit down with a woman who thinks it's 1987 and interrogate her about the worst night of her life? What exactly do you imagine that produces? A clean answer? Closure?" He heard his own voice rising and pulled it back down, professional, reasonable. "Here's what it produces. It produces confusion. It produces distress. It produces a woman crying in a care facility with no memory of why she's crying, and then it produces a staff member calling me, and then it produces a bill. That's what it produces."

"You don't know that."

"I know her charts. I'm her healthcare proxy. I've read every note Fernwood has ever written about her, which is more than either of you can say, because visiting your mother is apparently a quarterly obligation, so forgive me if I don't defer to your expertise on what she can and can't handle."

That was cruel and he knew it was cruel the moment it left his mouth, and he watched it land—watched Wendell's face do something small and complicated, watched Odile turn her wedding ring once around her finger—and he did not take it back. Taking it back would require explaining why he'd said it, and explaining why he'd said it would require opening a door he had nailed shut at thirteen years old with his whole body.

"Mom doesn't need to be asked anything," Odile said finally, softly. "She needs to be comfortable. That's it. That's the whole job now."

"The whole job," Wendell repeated.

"Yes."

"For thirty years the whole job has been not asking. Don't ask about Dad. Don't ask why there's no photographs. Don't ask why Mom flinches at rain. Don't ask, don't wonder, don't remember out loud. I've worked thirty years in a building full of records, Odile. I know what happens to things nobody writes down." Wendell looked at each of them in turn. "They stop having happened."

Caspian gathered his papers. It was a gesture he'd rehearsed in a hundred meetings—the tidy close, the signal that discussion had concluded whether or not agreement had been reached. He squared the pages, tapped them twice against the table, and slid them back into the folder with a crispness that was itself a sentence.

"The motion fails," he said. "Two to one. We sell the house, we don't disturb Mom, and we move forward. Are we done?"

Nobody answered, which he took for yes.

* * *

But driving home afterward, wipers slapping, the folder on the passenger seat like a sleeping animal, Caspian found his hands were not steady on the wheel, and he pulled into the empty lot of the closed garden center to sit with that fact.

Because here was the thing he had not said at the diner. Here was the thing under the ledger, beneath the line items, in the margin where the accountant's hand trembles.

He knew why the motion failed. It failed because he'd voted it down, and he'd voted it down not for Vera's comfort, whatever he'd claimed, but for his own. Because Caspian Montrose, age fifty-four, controller of a mid-sized logistics firm, keeper of folders and timelines and fourteen-point type, did not want to know what his mother remembered. He had spent his entire adult life constructing a version of that night that was load-bearing—that held up the house of who he was—and he could not afford an inspection.

There had been a window, that night. He remembered the window. He remembered rain coming through it sideways, and the sound it made, and the way his mother stood in the hallway with her arms wrapped around herself like she was holding her ribs together. He remembered Wendell, small, eleven years old, standing at the top of the stairs. And he remembered—this was the part, this was the part he kept in the deepest drawer—he remembered that after his father's taillights disappeared, his mother had looked at Wendell, and Wendell had looked at her, and something had passed between them that Caspian had never been given and had spent forty years pretending not to want.

Whatever Wendell said that night—or didn't say—it had rearranged the family. He knew that the way you know a wall is load-bearing: by what collapsed when you tried to lean on it.

He restarted the car. The rain had thinned to a fine drifting mist, the kind their mother used to call *softer than sorry*, and Caspian sat with his hands on the wheel and thought about the locked desk drawer still waiting in the study at 214 Marrow Street, sealed since before any of them could remember, scheduled now to be sold to a stranger with everything else.

He would vote to leave it locked, he knew. He would vote it down two to one if he had to.

And he also knew, with the certainty of a man reading his own ledger's final page, that Wendell was not finished asking.
