## 12. Caspian Breaks Something

The garage smelled the way it had always smelled—oil and sawdust and something faintly sweet that Caspian had never been able to name and had stopped trying to name around age nine. It was the smell of his father's hands, he supposed, though he would never have said so aloud. In the Montrose family you did not say things like *this smells like Dad*. You said nothing, or you said something useful, like *the extension cord's in the blue bin*, and you let the smell do its own quiet work on you in private.

He'd arrived at 214 Marrow Street at seven in the morning with a rented pickup, forty contractor bags, a pry bar, and a spreadsheet. The spreadsheet was titled MARROW ST — GARAGE — PHASE 1, and it had columns for KEEP / SELL / DONATE / DISPOSAL, and it was the most honest document anyone in his family had produced in thirty years, because it admitted what they were actually doing here: sorting a life into categories.

Wendell was already there when he backed the truck up the driveway. Of course Wendell was already there. Wendell lived like a man perpetually early for an appointment no one else remembered making, standing on the cracked concrete apron in a cardigan despite the October cold, holding two coffees as if they were peace offerings from a country that had run out of everything else.

"I didn't know which way you take it," Wendell said, handing one over.

"Black."

"I know. I got it black anyway." A pause. "I always get it black anyway."

Caspian took the cup. It was hot enough to hurt, and he was grateful for that, for something in the morning with edges.

They worked in parallel for the first hour, the way they did everything—side by side, not together. Wendell took the shelving along the north wall, lifting down jars of screws sorted by size into coffee cans labeled in their mother's handwriting, her looping capitals gone soft and uncertain toward the end of her time in the house. Caspian took the far corner, where the snow tires leaned against the wall like tired animals and a child's bicycle hung from a hook by one wheel, too small for any Montrose still living, rusted to the color of dried blood.

He did not look at the workbench.

This required strategy. The workbench occupied the entire east wall, four meters of scarred maple bolted to the studs, with a pegboard above it where every tool hung inside a painted outline of itself—a hammer-shaped ghost, a wrench-shaped ghost, each tool returned nightly to the silhouette of its absence. His father had made those outlines with a fine brush on a Sunday afternoon in, Caspian guessed, 1979, before Caspian was born, before anything. The pegboard was the most organized thing his father had ever done, and Caspian had spent his whole life understanding that fact without letting himself understand it: a man who could give every tool a home and then leave all of them behind.

"You're doing the corner by the bench last," Wendell observed, not looking up from the coffee cans.

"I'm doing it in order of difficulty."

"That's not the order of difficulty. That's avoidance wearing a schedule."

Caspian set down the bag he was filling. "You want to psychoanalyze me, there's a form at the library, I'm sure."

"There's no form," Wendell said mildly. "There's just me, noticing."

That was the trouble with Wendell. Thirty years of night shifts had given them the patience of a lighthouse—not aggressive, not moving, just relentlessly present, sweeping the same beam over the same water until whatever was out there either sank or surfaced. Caspian had built his entire adult life on the principle that nothing surfaced if you kept the water moving fast enough. Work. Numbers. The ledger of the estate auction, balanced to the penny. His wife's questions answered before she asked them. His own questions never asked at all.

He went back to the snow tires.

* * *

By eleven they had filled the truck bed once and made a run to the donation center, and the garage had opened up around them like a wound being debrided—cleaner and worse at the same time. Now there was no avoiding the middle of the floor, where a tarp lay over something large, and under the tarp, Caspian knew without looking, was the riding mower his father had bought the summer before he left, one season of use, payments still owed when the payments stopped being made.

And now there was no avoiding the workbench.

Wendell had drifted toward it, because Wendell drifted toward everything painful the way other people drifted toward windows in a fire—as if drawn by the very thing that should repel them. Their fingers moved along the pegboard outlines, hovering, not touching. "He painted these himself," Wendell said. "I used to think that was so strange. A man drawing pictures of where his tools should go."

"It's called organization."

"It's called a man telling his tools what he expected of them." Wendell turned. "Did he ever tell us?"

"Don't."

"I'm just asking a question, Caspian. People ask questions. It's a thing that happens between—"

"In this family it isn't." He heard his own voice come out flat and final, a drawer closing, and hated how practiced it sounded. Hated that he had a practiced voice for this. That was the thing no one understood about silence—it wasn't the absence of speech. It was a skill. It had technique. It required maintenance, like the pegboard, like the mower, like everything his father had left behind that someone still had to keep up.

Wendell looked at him for a long moment with those librarian eyes that catalogued overdue lives, and then, mercifully, turned back to the shelf and began carrying jars to the donate pile.

Caspian stood alone in front of the workbench.

Up close, the maple top was a topography. Cigarette burns—he'd forgotten his father smoked, or had he buried that, filed it under DISPOSAL in some internal spreadsheet? Rings from cans of beer set down without coasters. A groove worn near the vise where his father's hip had leaned ten thousand times. And there, low on the front edge, a row of small drilled holes with dates beside them in black marker, faded almost to nothing: the height marks. Not on a doorframe like normal families—his father had drilled the garage wall, said a doorframe was temporary, said a man's growth should be recorded where the real work happened. CASPIAN, AGE 6. CASPIAN, AGE 8. WENDELL, AGE 5, standing on the paint can, wobbling, laughing. ODILE refusing to be measured at all until she was taller than the mark she wanted to beat.

His father had measured them against the wall where he fixed things, as if they were projects. As if they were also things he might one day repair, or abandon mid-repair.

Caspian picked up the sledgehammer.

It wasn't a decision, exactly. It was more like the moment a dam decides—the water has been pressing for years and the concrete has only ever been postponing. He told himself he was clearing the bench to move it, that the legs were bolted and the fastest way was demolition, that this was logistics, Phase 1, column D, DISPOSAL. He told himself all of this in the half second it took to swing.

The first blow took off the front-left leg and the sound was enormous, a gunshot of maple and grief, and the whole bench lurched like a ship struck below the waterline. Jars fell from the shelf above and burst across the floor, screws and washers skittering everywhere, decades of sorted order suddenly indistinguishable from chaos.

"Caspian!" Wendell spun around, arms full of coffee cans.

The second blow broke the top free of the back wall. The pegboard came down with it, whole, swinging from one nail, the tool-ghosts swaying—hammer, wrench, level—all the silhouettes of everything his father had promised to keep in place.

The third blow he didn't remember deciding on. It landed on the top itself and split the maple along a grain line that had been waiting thirty years, and Caspian stood there breathing hard with the hammer still raised, fifty-three years old, in his dead father's garage—or not dead, that was the thing, wasn't it, they didn't even know if he was dead—and the silence he had maintained for four decades lay broken on the floor in pieces smaller than any jar.

Wendell set down the cans very slowly. They didn't shout. They didn't rush him. They stood among the scattered screws and watched him with the terrible gentleness of someone who has waited a long time and knows better than to startle the animal that has finally come out.

"Do you feel better?" Wendell asked quietly.

"No." His voice cracked on it. "No, I feel—" He lowered the hammer. His hands were shaking. "I saw him, Wendell."

The garage went very still. Even the pegboard stopped swaying.

"Nineteen ninety-eight," Caspian said. "Maybe ninety-nine. Tulsa. I was there for the quarter close, some acquisition due diligence, and I came out of a steakhouse at eleven at night and he was pumping gas at the station across the street." He heard himself describe it in the language he used for everything, transactions and closings, because it was the only dialect he had left. "Older. Gray. Same posture though. You'd know the posture. Leaning on the car like the car was holding him up instead of the other way around."

Wendell had gone white. "You saw him. You *saw* him." Not a question. A recalculation. Caspian could practically hear the ledger updating, some long-standing entry finally reconciled.

"He was maybe forty feet away. Under the lights. I could have crossed the street in ten seconds." Caspian looked down at the wreckage, at the height marks now facing the ceiling, CASPIAN AGE 8 upside down in the sawdust. "I stood behind my rental car until he finished pumping and drove away. Then I went back inside and paid for dinner on the company card and flew home the next morning and I never—I have never told a single living person. Not Marianne. Not anyone."

"Why?" Wendell's voice was barely above the hum of the refrigerator motor. "Why wouldn't you tell us? We spent years—we had detectives, we had the missing-persons filing, Mom put his face on flyers, Caspian. She papered three states with his face."

"I know."

"She died a little every year wondering if he was alive. She's losing her mind in Fernwood right now and part of what's going is whatever she built to survive not knowing, and you *knew*, you had a data point, you had a *sighting*, and you sat on it for twenty-five years because—why? Because why, Caspian?"

"Because if I told you," he said, and the sentence came out of him like a splinter coming out, slow and tearing, "then it would be true. Out loud. In the family. And everything we built on top of not-knowing would come down." He gestured at the ruined bench, absurdly, as evidence. "All of it. Every Christmas where nobody mentioned him. Every time Odile changed the subject with a joke. Every time you filed something instead of saying it. Every rule this house runs on. I wasn't keeping a secret from you. I was keeping the building standing. Somebody has to keep the building standing, Wendell. That's the job nobody wants and everybody needs."

"And how's the building?" Wendell asked softly. "How are *you*?"

And that—that was the question that finished him. Because the honest answer was that he was a load-bearing wall himself, cracked through, holding up rooms he no longer believed in, and he had known it since Tulsa, since the gas station, since the moment he chose the silence over his father's face and understood that he had become his mother's son in the worst way: a person who protected the family by disappearing from it.

He sat down on the fallen bench top. It held. Of course it held; it was maple; his father had bought good wood. He put his head in his hands and did something he had not done since he was eleven years old, which was cry in front of another member of his family.

Wendell sat down next to him. Didn't touch him. Just sat, shoulder an inch from his shoulder, in the ruins of the sorted world, among the screws that would never again find their coffee cans.

"He looked okay," Caspian said finally, wiping his face with the heel of his hand. "That's the worst part. He looked *okay*. Fed. Rested. Like a man who'd been sleeping well for twenty years while Mom—" He couldn't finish it.

"We tell Odile," Wendell said. Not asking.

"Not yet. Please. Let me—" He laughed, wet and awful. "Let me practice. Isn't that pathetic? Fifty-three years old and I need to rehearse telling my sister the truth."

"It's not pathetic," Wendell said. "It's a first draft. First drafts are allowed to be rough." They stood, and offered him a hand up, and he took it, which was itself unprecedented, a transaction with no column for it. "Come on. We'll sweep up. We'll bag the pieces. Sellable salvage, probably—someone will want the vise."

"The vise," Caspian repeated, and found, unbelievably, that he was smiling. "God. The vise."

They worked another hour in the broken garage, side by side, and for the first time in his life it felt like together rather than parallel. He was still raw, still shaking a little, but underneath the shaking was something he didn't have a word for and suspected Wendell did and would never say, because Wendell was kind.

He was backing the truck out at dusk, headlights sweeping the driveway, when he saw the figure walking up Marrow Street pulling a suitcase—small, wheeled, the kind of suitcase a person takes when they are not planning to go back.

Odile.

She stopped at the edge of the light, looking thinner than her photographs, and called out in a voice carefully arranged to sound casual:

"So. I came early. Don't make it a thing."
