## 3. Letters in a Dying Tongue

The house smelled of my mother for three days after the funeral, and then it began to smell of dust instead, and I understood that grief has a schedule of its own and does not consult the living.

I had meant to leave on Monday. The ferry ran twice daily from Vessing — once at dawn in a fog so thick the horn sounded like something drowning, and again at four in the afternoon when the light came slantwise off the water and made even the harbor's rust look gilded. I had a flat in the city with a radiator that knocked like a polite ghost, a half-finished manuscript, a life assembled out of things that did not require me to stay anywhere. But on Sunday morning I opened the wardrobe in her room and found the boxes.

Not boxes, exactly. Tins. Biscuit tins, the kind every coastal household hoards past all reason, stacked in the corner behind her winter coats, each one wrapped in a tea towel against the damp. Seven of them. My mother had been dead nine days and she was still keeping secrets the way other women kept linen — folded, pressed, stored against a future she would not see.

I sat down on the floor of the wardrobe with my back against the cold plaster and opened the first tin the way you open a door you have been told not to open: slowly, with an apology already forming.

Letters. Dozens of them, bundled with string gone soft as hair. And beneath the letters, notebooks — cheap school exercise books, their covers mottled with salt bloom, filled edge to edge with her handwriting.

The handwriting itself stopped me first. I had known my mother's hand my whole life: the careful, upright script she used for shopping lists and condolence cards, the slightly looser version on birthday checks. This was neither. This was smaller, quicker, leaning hard to the right as if the words were walking into weather. And it was not in English.

It was in the old tongue.

I want to be honest here, since honesty is the thing this whole story is about: I could not read it. Not properly. I recognized it the way you recognize a face across a street at dusk — the shape of it, the music of it, but none of the detail. Vessing dialect, the tongue of the headlands, the language my grandmother had prayed in and my mother had been taught to be ashamed of. I had maybe forty words of it left in me. *Sea. Bread. Come home. Enough.* The vocabulary of a child, which is what I had been when the household switched languages, sometime around my seventh year, the way a boat switches flags entering foreign water.

My father had called it "the old talk." He'd said it the way he said everything — lightly, with that smile of his that ended conversations before they started. *We're Lockridges now, love. Lockridges speak proper.* And my mother had laughed, because she always laughed, and after that the only place the old talk lived in our house was under her breath, in the kitchen, when she thought no one was listening — counting stitches, cursing the drain, singing half a lullaby before catching herself.

She had written whole books of it anyway. In secret. In biscuit tins behind her coats.

* * *

I carried the tins down to the kitchen table one at a time, both hands, like they were live coals or communion. The rain came on around noon, the fine sideways drizzle that Vessing calls weather the way other places call it nothing at all, and I did not turn on the lamp. I worked in the grey.

The letters were correspondence. That much was clear from the layout — salutations, dates, signatures — though the names took me a while. The hand that wrote to her was different from hers: older-fashioned, heavier, the letters pressed deep enough to read by touch. I traced one signature with my fingertip until the shapes resolved.

*Kell.*

Ada Kell's family. Ada Kell, who had stood in my mother's parlor two nights ago and said a name over the canapés that made my uncle Bram's face go the color of uncooked dough. She had said it to us — to me, to Bram, to the whole cold room — and I had heard it as an insult, as a stone thrown through thirty years of glass. Now here it was, in ink, dated decades back, addressed to my mother in the intimate second person, page after page after page.

They had written to each other. For years. My mother, who spoke to no one in the old talk, who performed her English at the harbor dinners like a woman playing an instrument she'd been told to hide — my mother had a correspondent. A friend. Someone she wrote to in the language of her childhood, in that quick right-leaning hand, filling pages I now held while wearing her dead sister-in-law's cardigan because I hadn't packed warm enough clothes for a funeral.

I couldn't read them. That was the cruelty of it. She had kept this one thread of herself alive for fifty years and I was holding the evidence and it might as well have been in Sumerian.

But translation is patient work, and I am, whatever else is true of me, a professional reader. I made coffee I didn't drink. I got a legal pad. I started with the shortest letter and the dictionary problem — no dictionary exists; the dialect was never written down officially, never standardized, never forgiven — and so I did it the way scholars do dead languages: by frequency, by context, by guessing and checking and guessing again. By noon I had six words. By dark, twenty. Some I recovered the way you recover a forgotten tune — hearing my grandmother's voice say them, feeling my own mouth remember. Others I simply stared at until they surrendered.

And slowly, between the words I knew and the words I was learning, the shape of the correspondence emerged. It was not, primarily, affectionate. Or rather — there was affection in it, deep and worn smooth like beach glass — but the letters were also something else.

They were records.

* * *

The exercise books were worse. Better. Worse.

Where the letters wandered, the notebooks were exact. Columns. Dates going back to 1968. Sums — real sums, in real currency, some entries crossed out and corrected with a precision that told me my mother had checked them more than once. Names beside the sums. I recognized some of the names from the harbor: men whose grandchildren now sat on the council, whose family crests hung carved over the hotel bar, whose boats had names painted in gold leaf. I recognized others from the churchyard we had stood in last week, fresh earth over old earth over older earth.

I copied out one entry entire, sounding the words aloud in the empty kitchen, my accent atrocious, my voice strange to myself:

*Vinter 1971. Fjorton pund till Kell-familjen, för vad de förlorade vid muren. Han sa att det var allt han kunde få dem att acceptera. Det var inte allt han hade.*

Fourteen pounds to the Kell family, for what they lost at the wall. He said it was all he could get them to accept. It was not all he had.

I sat with that sentence for a long time. Outside, the tide was coming in over the shingle with its sound like slow applause, and somewhere down the road a dog was barking at nothing, the way dogs do here, at the fog, at the ghosts, at the general condition of being alive in Vessing.

*Vad de förlorade vid muren.* What they lost at the wall. The seawall. Everyone in town knew the seawall — the great concrete arm built along the eastern shore the winter of '71, the project that had made reputations, including, prominently, my father's. It had saved the harbor district. It had been celebrated in the regional paper with photographs of men in hard hats shaking hands. What nobody celebrated, what nobody so much as mentioned in print, was what lay behind it: the low fields east of the wall, the smallholdings, the plots that had flooded every spring since anyone could remember and drained again, until the wall redirected the water and the water did not drain. Fields that belonged, if land can belong to anyone, to families like the Kells.

My father had built his name on a wall that drowned his neighbors' fields. And my mother had written it down. Every payment, every date, every name — recorded in a dying language, in biscuit tins, behind winter coats, for fifty years.

Why keep records you never intend to use? I turned the question over all evening. Because silence without witness isn't peace, it's just a slower way of drowning? Because she wanted someone, someday, to know she had known? Because a woman who cannot speak can still write, and writing is a door left unlocked?

Or — and this thought arrived late, unwelcome, certain — because she had always intended someone to find them. Me. She had known I would come back to sort the effects. She had known where I would look, or hoped I would look, or trusted that the child who became a writer would eventually go poking through the wardrobe. The tins weren't hidden. They were *filed*.

* * *

By the third day I had worked through perhaps a tenth of it, and my notebook of translations had grown its own architecture: columns of guessed meanings, question marks, arrows, marginal notes in my own hand that looked increasingly like hers, leaning rightward into weather. I dreamed in fragments of the dialect. I woke saying a word I couldn't translate and lay in the dark tasting it.

There was a letter near the bottom of the third tin, undated but newer — the paper whiter, her hand less steady — that I saved for last without deciding to. When I finally unfolded it, the opening line was one I knew. Not from study. From childhood. From the hundred times she'd said it kissing my forehead, though she'd only ever said it in English, softened, domesticated:

*Du hör hemma här, även när du inte vet det.*

You belong here, even when you don't know it.

Below it, the letter went on for two full pages in that failing hand, and I could make out almost nothing — except that my name appeared, twice, spelled in the old fashion, and that near the end, in larger letters, underlined twice, was a phrase I could not crack but whose shape I recognized from the other letters, from the entries about the wall, from everywhere:

A word I had already translated once. *Sanningen.* The truth.

She had written me two pages about the truth, and underlined it, and I could not read a single sentence of the rest.

I sat in the kitchen until the light failed completely, the letter in my lap, the rain starting up again against the window with its patience, its terrible patience. Then I put on my coat, because there was exactly one person in Vessing who still spoke the old talk fluently, and I had seen her face at the wake, and I knew she had been waiting thirty years to be asked.

The coast road was black and blowing when I set out toward the Kell house, and for the first time since the ferry brought me back, I did not smile at anyone.
