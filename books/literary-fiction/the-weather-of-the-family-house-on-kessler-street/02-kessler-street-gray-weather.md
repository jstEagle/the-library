## 2. Kessler Street, Gray Weather

The taxi let Mirabel off at the corner of Kessler and Ninth because the driver refused to go further. "That street," he said, gesturing at the pothole that had apparently achieved municipal significance since her last visit, "eats suspensions."

"Then it's consistent," Mirabel said, and paid him.

She stood on the curb with one suitcase and a garment bag containing the black dress she'd bought three years ago for a funeral she'd been rehearsing in her head ever since her mother's diagnosis. The dress had been altered twice—once when grief made her stop eating, once when a restaurant opened in Lyon and she flew back eating again—and it fit now, which felt like a small betrayal of everyone involved.

The house on Kessler Street was exactly as gray as the sky above it, which either meant the weather had come down to meet it or the house had learned to reach up. The clapboard siding had gone from white to the color of old dishwater. The porch steps sagged in the middle like a tired spine. The fig tree in the side yard—the grandmother's fig tree, though Mirabel had spent her whole childhood thinking of it simply as *the tree that dropped things*—was bare and black against the clouds, its branches contorted into shapes that looked, this time of year, less like a tree than like handwriting.

She catalogued it all without meaning to. It was what she did; it was how she had made a living for fifteen years. Square footage. Condition of roofline, visible curling on the south-facing shingles. Foundation: unknown, probably criminal. She could write the listing herself. *Charming fixer-upper in transitional neighborhood, original details throughout, sold as-is, estate sale.* The words came automatically, professionally, and she noticed—with the distant, clinical interest she brought to everything now—that they were easier to think than any other kind.

*Sold as-is.* Like her.

The front door opened before she reached it, and Ruben stood there in a suit that fit him badly in the shoulders, the way his suits always had, because he had their father's build and kept buying clothes cut for someone more apologetic.

"You took a cab from the airport?" he said. "I would've picked you up."

"I didn't want—" She stopped. She didn't know how to finish that sentence honestly. *I didn't want to sit in a car with you while we both pretended this was a normal thing, brothers and sisters, picking each other up at airports.* "The driver said the street eats suspensions."

"It does. Come in before you become part of the local economy."

He hugged her, briefly, the way you hug someone across a fence. His chin smelled of barber shop and coffee. Then he stepped back and looked at her suitcase instead of her face, which was going to turn out to be the theme of the weekend.

"The guest room's made up," he said. "Mom kept it ready. I don't know why. Nobody ever—" He stopped himself, jaw working, and Mirabel understood that they were both going to be doing a great deal of stopping themselves over the next four days, and that the stopping itself would be the real conversation.

* * *

Inside, the house did what the house had always done, which was to smell faintly of lemon polish over something older and unnameable, some base note of the building itself, wood and plaster and decades of cooking soaked so deep into the walls that no amount of paint would ever get it out. Mirabel stood in the front hall and breathed it in and waited for something to happen in her chest.

Nothing happened. That was the thing no one told you about grief, or maybe they told you and you didn't believe them: sometimes it wasn't a wave. Sometimes it was a flatline. Her mother had been dead nine days and Mirabel felt about it the way she felt about the weather in Cleveland—regrettable, expected, not personally addressed to her.

She began, without deciding to, the inventory.

Front hall: coat hooks, four, one shaped like a duck, chipped. Console table with the drawer that stuck, still sticking. Wallpaper, faded roses, peeling at the seam near the light switch where Ruben had thrown a shoe in 1994 and she had never once been blamed for it, an injustice she intended to raise at the appropriate time, which was possibly not at a funeral.

Living room: the good sofa, plastic-covered until 2003, then uncovered and immediately regretted. The television, enormous and obsolete, that their mother had refused to replace on the grounds that it "still worked," a phrase that in their house constituted a complete philosophy of life. The piano nobody played, closed, with a doily on top and a photograph of the grandmother—the only photograph of the grandmother anyone owned, taken in 1968 outside a church, a small severe woman squinting into a sun that looked, in the print, like it belonged to another country entirely.

Mirabel touched the piano lid. Dust came away on her fingers. She rubbed it between thumb and forefinger and thought: *dust, household grade, undisturbed approximately two weeks,* and then thought: *you are appraising your dead mother's living room,* and then thought nothing at all, which was better.

Ruben appeared in the doorway with two glasses of water, because neither of them drank before funerals, a family tradition consisting entirely of things they didn't do.

"Casseroles are in the fridge," he said. "Eleven so far. Mrs. Okafor brought two."

"Two casseroles?"

"She said one was for the funeral and one was for after, when it gets quiet." He said this evenly, but Mirabel saw his throat move. "Delia across the street has been organizing people. There's a sign-up sheet. It's color-coded."

Of course it was. Delia Pruitt had lived across from the house longer than either sibling had been alive, and ran the block the way small nations run themselves: efficiently, affectionately, and with an iron sense of who owed whom.

"How is she?" Mirabel asked.

"Old," Ruben said. "Same as ever."

They stood in the living room holding their waters, and the silence between them was the old silence, the sibling silence, the one that could hold twenty years or hold nothing depending on how you leaned into it. Mirabel leaned carefully.

"The funeral home wants the photos by tomorrow," Ruben said finally, eyes on the mantel. "For the display board. I've got the ones from the album in the hall closet, but I figured you'd want to look through them first. In case there's anything you—" He stopped himself again. Third time. He was getting good at it. "In case there's anything."

"In case there's anything," Mirabel agreed, and heard how it sounded, and hated it a little, and did it anyway.

* * *

Upstairs, the house changed key. Downstairs was public space, funeral-ready, arranged for company. Upstairs was where the actual family had happened, and walking down the corridor was like walking through a sentence with most of the words redacted.

Her mother's room: bed made with hospital corners even though no one had slept in it for nine days, because their mother had made her bed every morning of her life including, presumably, the last one, and Ruben hadn't had the heart to undo the work. Mirabel stood in the doorway and catalogued: quilt, hand-sewn, fading; dresser, walnut, water ring on top from a glass nobody ever admitted to; window facing the fig tree, curtains open, as if the room were keeping watch.

She went in. She sat on the edge of the bed. She waited for the wave.

What came instead was a list. Thread count of the sheets. Age of the mattress, estimate seven years, replaced during the illness, insurance-covered. The small clock on the nightstand, analog, ticking, set four minutes fast because her mother had believed lateness was a moral failing and had built a buffer into the very furniture of her life.

Mirabel lay back on the quilt and stared at the ceiling crack she'd known as a child—the one shaped like a river delta, the one she used to imagine flooding—and felt absolutely nothing, and the nothing was so total, so polished, so *complete*, that somewhere underneath it a very small voice observed that this was exactly how she wrote about food she couldn't taste. *Bright notes. Lovely tension. A dish that announces itself.* Language laid over emptiness like wallpaper over a crack.

She got up before the thought could finish.

* * *

The guest room—her room, formerly, though the house had reassigned it years ago the way institutions reassign offices—was made up with sheets she recognized. Her mother had kept buying them the same pattern for thirty years: pale blue, small white flowers, the kind of bedding that communicated a philosophy rather than a preference. On the pillow sat a folded towel and, on top of the towel, a note in her mother's handwriting.

Mirabel's hands went cold around the note before she'd read it, because her mother had died nine days ago and yet here was fresh handwriting, ink barely a year faded, and the handwriting was so alive, so full of its old impatient slant, that for one second the flatline jumped.

*M — towels are new, don't use them to take off makeup like last time. R knows the funeral arrangements. Be civil. Both of you. — Mom*

Be civil. Both of you. As if she'd known. As if she'd written it yesterday, from wherever mothers go, watching her two adult children circle each other in the hallway below like weather fronts that refuse to merge.

Mirabel laughed—one short, ugly syllable—and then pressed the note flat against her sternum and stood in the middle of her childhood bedroom and felt, if not grief, at least its shadow pass over the wall.

* * *

Downstairs, the doorbell rang, and then rang again, because whoever it was had never learned that the bell worked. Mirabel found Ruben already opening it, and beyond him on the sagging porch stood a woman Mirabel didn't know: elderly, small, dressed in funeral black that looked worn-in rather than purchased, the way clothes look on people who attend many funerals because they have outlived the people who mattered.

Behind her, at the bottom of the steps, waiting, stood Delia Pruitt, silver-haired and formidable, holding a casserole dish wrapped in a tea towel like a diplomatic pouch.

"This is Mrs. Adeyemi," Delia announced, in the tone of a woman introducing a head of state to a town she technically governed. "From the community center. She knew your grandmother."

Something moved through the hallway then—not a draft, though the front door was open, not quite a sound. Ruben's hand tightened on the door edge hard enough that Mirabel, watching, saw the knuckles change color.

"We're so sorry for your loss," Mrs. Adeyemi said, and her accent was round and deliberate, each word placed like a stone in a stream. "Your mother was kind to me once, long ago, when kindness was expensive." She paused. "Please give our condolences to the Okonkwo-Vasquez family."

The name went through the hallway like a stone through a window.

Nobody corrected her. That was the strange part, the part Mirabel would turn over later, at three in the morning, in the blue-flowered sheets. Because there should have been a correction—it was practically reflexive, it was *family law*: *we're just Vasquez, the hyphen was never official, Grandma dropped it at Ellis Island or wherever, it never counted.* Someone should have said it. Ruben should have said it. Their mother had spent forty years saying it, cheerfully, firmly, to census takers and school secretaries and the woman at the DMV, until the saying of it wore smooth as river stone and nobody questioned it anymore.

But Ruben said nothing. He stood there with his bad-fitting suit and his white knuckles and looked at a point approximately six inches to the left of Mrs. Adeyemi's shoulder.

And Mrs. Adeyemi, for her part, watched him not say it, and something in her face—patient, sad, unsurprised—suggested she had come here today specifically to say the name out loud in this hallway, and to see what would happen.

"Well," said Delia briskly, into the silence, handing over the casserole as if it were a peace treaty, "it'll keep till tomorrow. Come on, Adé, I'll walk you back, my hip's acting up and I want sympathy."

Mrs. Adeyemi turned at the bottom of the steps. She looked up at the house—all of it, the sagging porch, the dishwater siding, the black scrawl of the fig tree against the gray sky—as if taking attendance. Then she looked at Mirabel, directly, the way nobody had looked at her in days.

"The tree still stands," she said. It wasn't small talk. It had the weight of a sentence translated from somewhere else, carried across an ocean and set down carefully on a wet sidewalk in front of a dying house. "That's more than I expected."

And then she and Delia were crossing the street, and the door was closing, and Ruben was carrying the casserole into the kitchen with the careful stiffness of a man transporting something explosive.

"What was that?" Mirabel asked him.

"What was what?"

"*Okonkwo-Vasquez.*"

"That's just—" He set the dish on the counter harder than necessary. "Old people. They remember things wrong. You know how Grandma's name got shortened, everybody mangles it, it doesn't *mean* anything."

It was the longest speech he'd made all day, and it had the specific over-polished quality of language laid over a crack.

"Okay," Mirabel said, because she was tired, and grieving adjacent, and the fight could wait. Everything could wait. That was her whole condition lately: everything, waitable.

But that night, lying in the guest room under the flowers-that-communicated-a-philosophy, listening to the house tick and settle around her like an old animal rearranging itself, she found her mind returning to the moment in the hallway the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Not the name itself—she'd heard the name before, in fragments, in the hushed arguments of her childhood, *that's not our name anymore, don't ask*—but the silence afterward. Two adults, raised in the same house, trained in the same reflex, and neither of them had fired.

Ruben knew something. Or Ruben feared something. With Ruben those had always been the same thing.

Outside, the gray weather held, neither raining nor clearing, the sky doing what the house did, which was to endure without committing. And beneath the window, at the edge of her hearing, the fig tree moved in the wind with a sound like pages turning.
