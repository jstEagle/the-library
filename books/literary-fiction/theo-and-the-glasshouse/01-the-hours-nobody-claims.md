## 1. The Hours Nobody Claims

The Halcyon Branch Library closed at nine, but Ivo Oyelaran never left until two.

This was not devotion. He had stopped believing in devotion years ago, somewhere between the second and third time he'd watched the day staff leave in a bright spill of coats and laughter while he stayed behind to reconcile the returns cart. It was simply that the hours after midnight belonged to no one else. The city above the library — the insomniac city, the city that slept in shifts and grudges — had its own population of the unclaimed: cab drivers eating rice out of foil trays under sodium lights, nurses walking home with their badges still clipped to their hips, old men who sat in all-night laundromats watching other people's clothes go around. Ivo counted himself among them. The night shift was not a job so much as an address.

He worked the returns first, because returns were honest. Books came back damaged, dog-eared, coffee-ringed, occasionally water-warped into the shape of someone's grief, and none of it was his fault and none of it required him to be anything other than accurate. He logged each one into the system with the small green scanner that clicked like a beetle against the desk. *The Collected Poems of W. B. Yeats.* Overdue forty-one days. *A History of Irrigation in the Northern Valleys.* Overdue six days. *What We Talk About When We Talk About Love.* Never checked out at all — someone had slipped it through the overnight slot like a baby into a basket, and now it was his problem, which meant it was his.

It was the returned books that held the confessions.

Ivo had discovered this in his first year, when he found a bus ticket folded inside a paperback romance, dated three years before the book's return stamp, as if someone had been carrying their regret around inside the novel for safekeeping and finally decided to leave it where they'd found their comfort. Since then he had learned to read the margins the way other men read faces. People wrote things in library books they could not say anywhere else. A pencil note beside a passage on forgiveness: *he never asked me to stay, so I didn't.* A phone number on a receipt tucked into a biography, circled twice, then crossed out with such force the pen had torn through to the next page. A pressed flower in a field guide to wildflowers, flattened to a brown ghost of itself, marking the chapter on something called honesty weed.

He did not report these things. There was no procedure for them. Instead he kept a notebook — a plain black one, unlined, bought from the stationery shop on Delmar Street whose owner slept standing up behind the counter — and into it he copied the unsaid things of strangers, transcribing them in his small, even hand. He told himself it was archival work. Librarians preserved what mattered; that was the whole trade. Nobody had ever written down these sentences for the people who needed to hear them, so Ivo did, in the only way available to him, which was silently, and too late.

The notebook lived in the bottom drawer of the circulation desk, beneath the rubber bands and the dead pens, and by now it was more than half full. He thought of it as the inventory. Not of lost books — the library had a separate ledger for those, with fines and forgiveness schedules — but of lost sentences. Things said into paper instead of air. He had developed, over seven years of nights, a taxonomy he never spoke aloud: there were confessions (I took the money), there were renunciations (I am not coming back), there were the ones he privately labeled *addresses* — notes that named a person who would never read them, sent into the world like letters posted without stamps, trusting the postal service of chance.

Tonight the returns cart held eleven items, and the third one gave him pause: a hardcover history of the region's glasshouse industry, its dust jacket gone, its spine sun-bleached to the color of weak tea. Someone had checked it out eight months ago under a card that no longer existed in the system, and here it was, come home the way cats come home, without explanation. Inside, on the flyleaf, in ballpoint, someone had written a single line:

*They say the water remembers. I hope it forgets.*

Ivo read it twice. Then he opened the black notebook and copied it down, and his hand, usually so steady, hesitated over the page for reasons he chose not to examine. Water. He hadn't thought about water in years, except as weather, except as the sound the pipes made in the walls of his apartment at four in the morning, a knocking he had long ago stopped investigating.

His father had died a week ago tonight.

Not died — that word still refused to sit flat in his chest, like furniture delivered to the wrong address. His father had *stopped*, on a Tuesday, in a hospital across town that Ivo had visited exactly once in the last nineteen years, and the stopping had been announced to him by a voicemail from a woman whose name he recognized as the current wife, or perhaps the former wife; the marriages had been difficult to track from a distance, and Ivo had kept the distance carefully, the way you keep a fence you built yourself and are proud of and ashamed of in equal measure.

He had not gone to see the body. He had not yet decided about the funeral, which was — he checked the date on the library's community board, because funerals were announced there alongside bake sales and tax clinics — tomorrow. Tomorrow at eleven, at Resthaven, which was not a restful name for a place but apparently an accurate one.

There was a list. Of course there was a list; Ivo was a man of lists, of ledgers, of systems that turned chaos into columns. In the back of the black notebook, upside down from the confessions, he kept a different inventory: everything he had never said to his father, and everything he had never asked. The two categories had merged over the years, because an unsaid question is just an unsaid statement wearing a question mark, and both of them corrode at the same rate.

*Why did you stop calling.*

*Did you know I got the job at Halcyon. Did anyone tell you.*

*When you drove me to the coast that summer — I was eleven — why did we turn around before the water. You said the car was making a noise. The car was fine. I've always known the car was fine.*

*Was it my mother. Was it her leaving, or was it before her leaving. What happened before I could remember.*

*Who is Oyelara.*

That last one had appeared on the page recently — three nights ago, actually, in handwriting slightly less controlled than the rest, as if it had been added during a moment when the hand was not fully consulted. He had no memory of writing it. That was not true either; he remembered the pen in his fingers, the lamp, the particular silence of the library at 1 a.m., but he could not remember *deciding*, and the distinction unsettled him. The name had surfaced the way names surface in dreams — familiar, freighted, sourceless. He had heard it once, maybe twice, in childhood, spoken low in kitchens by relatives who went quiet when he entered the room, and it had lodged in him like a splinter too deep to grip.

Oyelaran was the name on his library badge, his lease, his taxes. Oyelaran was the name his mother had given him and the school had misspelled and he had corrected, patiently, hundreds of times, until the correcting became part of his identity, this small daily act of insisting on accuracy. But *Oyelara* — shorter by a letter, lighter by some invisible weight — belonged to another country of the family, one that existed only in lowered voices.

He closed the notebook. The clock above the periodicals read 12:40. Outside, rain had begun, the fine persistent kind that made the city sound like it was whispering to itself, and through the tall front windows Ivo watched the streetlights turn the wet asphalt into a river of amber. Somewhere out there the insomniacs were moving through their own unclaimed hours. Somewhere out there was a funeral home with the lights off, holding his father the way a returned book holds a confession — quietly, temporarily, waiting for someone to open it.

*

At 1:15 he made tea in the staff kitchen, which was really a closet with a kettle and a poster about handwashing that had faded to the color of nothing. He drank it standing at the window that looked onto the reading room, and he let himself do the thing he only allowed himself at night, alone, when the building's daytime personality had drained away and it became what it truly was: a body of shelved voices, breathing dust.

His father had loved libraries. That was one of the few true things Ivo could still hold without it cutting. Not in the way patrons love them, gratefully, passively — his father had loved them the way sailors love harbors, as places to wait out weather. Ivo's earliest coherent memory was of being small in a branch library not unlike this one, watching his father read standing up, one finger moving down the page, and feeling — this was the strange part, the part he had never put into words, not even into the notebook — *safe*. Not happy. Safe. As if the building itself had agreed to protect them for the length of a chapter.

Then the years had done what years do. The calls thinned. The visits contracted to holidays, then to funerals of people Ivo barely knew, then to nothing but birthday texts that arrived late and said only *Hbd son,* no punctuation, as if punctuation were a commitment his father couldn't afford. And Ivo, who forgave strangers in margins every night, had extended his father no such mercy. He had filed him. *Estranged,* the form would say, if forms asked. *Relationship: estranged.* Two syllables doing the work of nineteen years.

Now the file was closed by death, and death, Ivo was discovering, does not accept overdue slips. There was no returning your father. There was no logging him back into the system, no fine to pay, no grace period. Whatever remained unsaid remained unsaid permanently, and the inventory in the back of the notebook had become, overnight, an archive — the worst kind, the kind that can no longer grow, only be reread.

He rinsed the cup. Dried it. Put it away handle-outward, because order was the one language he spoke fluently, and tonight the language was failing him, and he needed to speak it anyway.

*

The last hour he spent on the shelf-reading, walking the stacks with the trolley, reshelving the strays. This was his meditation, if he had one: the geography of the collection mapped onto his feet, Dewey decimals as a nervous system, every book in its place except the ones people had pulled out and abandoned face-down on tables like birds fallen mid-flight. He righted them. He always righted them.

In the 900s — history, the section he avoided on principle and haunted on compulsion — he found the glasshouse book's twin, another copy, correctly shelved, and stood looking at it longer than the task required. Glasshouses. Structures built to make impossible climates possible, to coax tropical things to grow in cold countries, transparent buildings whose entire purpose was to let light in while keeping the weather out. His grandfather, he knew — or had been told once, at a table of adults, in that tone adults used for family facts they wanted children to half-hear — had owned one. Had *kept* one, which was a different verb, a verb with hands in it. There had been land outside the city, and palms, and something about water that made his aunt stop talking and start slicing bread very precisely.

The rain thickened against the high windows. Somewhere in the building a pipe knocked — that same four-in-the-morning knock, arriving early tonight, as if the building too had something unsaid.

Ivo took down the shelved copy of the glasshouse history and carried it to the front desk, and under the lamp he read the first chapter standing up, one finger moving down the page, the way a man reads in a harbor waiting out weather. The chapter was about the great estate glasshouses of the northern valleys, their rise and ruin, and halfway down the second page a photograph stopped him: a long vaulted structure, iron ribs and panes of glass, palms pressing against the roof like prisoners or parishioners. The caption named it only as *a private conservatory, demolished.* No owner. No name. Just the architecture of keeping, already fallen.

He looked at the photograph for a long time. Long enough that the tea went cold in the closet behind him, long enough that the clock moved from 1:50 to 2:04 without his noticing. Something in the image itched at him — not recognition, nothing so dramatic, but a familiarity of light, the way the glass caught the sky, as though he had stood inside such a place once, as a child, holding someone's hand.

He photocopied the page. He did not know why. He folded the copy into the black notebook, into the back section, beneath the list of unsaid things, directly under the entry he could not remember deciding to write:

*Who is Oyelara.*

At 2:20 he shut down the lights wing by wing, front to back, the ritual he performed every night, darkness following him like a tide. At the door he paused, keys in hand, and looked back at the reading room — the empty chairs, the sleeping computers, the returns cart with its one remaining item, the stray copy of the glasshouse book he had decided, without deciding, to keep until tomorrow. Until after the funeral. He would log it properly then. He was a careful man. Everything would be logged properly, eventually, in its place.

Except, of course, the things that couldn't be.

Outside, the rain had softened to mist. The city lay steaming under the streetlights, half-asleep, wholly awake. Tomorrow at eleven there would be strangers in dark clothes saying his father's name in the past tense, and Ivo would stand among them holding his invented composure like an umbrella, and he would learn, though he could not yet know it, that among those strangers would be a woman who would look at him across the grave and call his family by the name nobody used — and set down, on the memorial table, face-up, a photograph.

But that was tomorrow. Tonight there was only the walk home, the mist, the keys going cold in his pocket, and the notebook riding against his ribs like a second heartbeat, heavier than paper should be, fuller than any library he would ever work in.
