## 10. Borrowed No Longer

My mother's kitchen smells of cardamom and burnt toast, which is to say it smells exactly as it did when I was nine, which is to say that some things survive even the people who swear they've buried them.

She makes the toast anyway. We both pretend not to notice.

"Your grandmother burned everything," Ingrid says, scraping at the black edge with a knife. "Bread, letters, bridges. She had a talent for it."

"She kept forty years of postcards behind a wardrobe."

"That's not keeping. That's hoarding with good posture." She sets the plate in front of me and sits down across from it, not eating, watching me eat instead, which is its own kind of language—one I'm only now learning to read. "Did you bring any of them?"

"I gave them away."

All of her stillness changes quality. It's the translator's trick I inherited from her without knowing: how to go motionless while something enormous moves through you. "Gave them where?"

"To Mara Kovac. Aurel's granddaughter. And the last one—" I take a breath. My mother's kitchen, my mother's knife still in her hand, and I am about to read aloud from a postcard written by a woman who exiled this woman from her own family for telling the truth. "The last one was addressed to me. I read it out loud. At the house. Then I called you."

"You called me," she says, "and read our dead mother's apology down a phone line while I stood in my kitchen holding a dish towel."

"Yes."

"And then you hung up before I could say anything, because we're both cowards in different dialects."

I laugh. It startles both of us. She almost smiles—the Brightwater smile, the one that ends conversations, except that between us now it seems to be trying to begin one instead.

"I'm sorry," I say. "For thirty years of silence. For inheriting it so well."

Ingrid looks at the burnt toast, at my hands around the plate, at some middle distance where the past presumably keeps its office hours. "I spent my whole life thinking I was the one who'd been thrown out," she says finally. "Turns out she was the one standing outside the whole time, posting letters to a door that never opened." She pushes the plate an inch toward me. "Eat. You're too thin. Grief is no excuse for poor nutrition; your grandmother would have said so, and then hidden a letter about it behind the wardrobe."

* * *

Vellamouth in October is a town made of weather. The harbor café has put its tables inside; the gulls have organized into committees; the light comes off the water at an angle that makes everything look remembered rather than seen.

Mara meets me at the library steps. She has her grandfather's jaw and none of his silences—or rather, she has spent hers, spent them all on the walk over, because when she sees me she talks like a woman who has been holding a breath since September.

"They're up," she says. "Come and see."

Inside, past the lending desk where Tomas pretends very badly not to be watching, there is a glass case against the north wall. The Kovac letters—Ulyana's letters, the ones addressed to Lida that named him, that carried his name through four decades of struck-through lines and careful margins—are laid out under glass, dated, annotated in Mara's small square handwriting. The frame above holds the earliest one, 1963, the ink gone brown, the dialect curling off the page like smoke.

*Mira sestra,* it begins. *My dear sister.* Words I translated once alone in a cold kitchen, and here they are in public, in daylight, laminated against forgetting.

"The librarian helped," Mara says. "Tomas. He catalogued everything. He says the dialect section of the archive has tripled since we opened the case." She glances at me sideways. "He also asks about you approximately every eleven minutes."

"Tomas is thorough."

"Tomas is a disaster," she agrees warmly. "He wrote you a letter. He's rewritten it four times. I'm not supposed to know that."

We stand in front of the case together. Two families' shame, framed and lit, and strangers stopping to read it—a schoolgirl copying words into a notebook, an old man mouthing the dialect like tasting something he half remembers. Ulyana wanted those words buried. Instead they are becoming the town's.

"She'd be furious," I say.

"She'd be relieved," Mara says. "It takes one to know one."

* * *

Tomas's letter finds me two days later at the guesthouse, because he apparently does not trust the postal service he works for, or because he wanted to watch my face when I read it, or both. It is written in three languages, moving between them mid-sentence like a man crossing a river on stepping stones, and the fourth language—the dying one, ours—appears only twice, carefully, at the beginning and the end, like a hand offered and a hand held.

*You told me once you were losing it,* he writes. *Word by borrowed word. But I've been reading what you sent to the archive, and Freya—you didn't lose it. You were carrying it somewhere. It just took you forty years and a box of postcards to arrive.*

I sit with that longer than I should admit. Then I get out my notebook—the working copy of the novel I've been translating for six months, a slim Norwegian book about a lighthouse keeper's daughter, contracted, deadline-ridden, due in English—and I do the thing I have not dared to do since I was a child being corrected at Ulyana's knee.

I begin translating it again. Into her language.

It goes badly. It goes beautifully. The dialect has no word for *deadline*, which tells you everything about why it died, but it has three words for the way light falls on water at different hours, and a word—I find it on the second evening, in a postcard from 1971—for the specific grief of saying goodbye to someone who is still standing in front of you. There is no English equivalent. There may need to be.

I work until the lamp gives the room a gold rim, and when I look up, Tomas's letter is propped against the teapot, and I understand that I am answering it. Not tonight. But soon. In all four languages.

* * *

The house sold on a Tuesday, to a young couple from inland who want "a project." The estate agent sent me a photo: scaffolding already up, the wardrobe carried out to the garden on two men's shoulders, gaping open like a mouth that has finally finished speaking. Behind it, the wall where the panel used to be is just a wall. Whatever it was hiding is in the light now, distributed—some to the archive, some to Mara, one to my mother, who keeps it in her kitchen drawer beside the scissors and reads it on Sundays, she tells me, "for tone."

The buyer asked if the house was haunted. The agent, bless her cheerful mercenary heart, said, "Only by correspondence."

On my last morning in Vellamouth I walk down to the harbor. The café owner nods at me like a regular, which after six weeks counts as citizenship. Mara waves from the fish quay. Somewhere behind me Tomas is probably drafting letter number five.

At the end of the pier I stop and say it—not aloud, not quite, but in the place where aloud begins:

*I'm coming back,* in her language. *The words are coming back. Thank you for the box. Goodbye, Mormor. Hello.*

Because that's the thing nobody warns you about endings: done properly, they don't close. They open. A door swings wide and the draft comes through and suddenly there are letters to answer, a mother to visit again, a novel in a dying tongue that refuses to die, a librarian rewriting his fifth draft, a town that has adopted a dead woman's secrets as its own living history.

I take out my notebook and write the first line of the next translation, standing up, in the wind, spelling it the way Ulyana spelled it—wrong according to the dictionaries, right according to her.

Behind me, someone clears their throat.

Tomas, holding an envelope, wearing the expression of a man whose fourth draft has just been promoted to first.

"I rewrote it," he says. "Again."

"I heard," I say, in his language, in mine, in hers. "Read it to me anyway."
