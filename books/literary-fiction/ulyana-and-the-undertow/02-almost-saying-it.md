## 2. Almost Saying It

The review ran on a Thursday, as his reviews always did. *A Study in Restraint* — nine hundred words on a tasting menu at a restaurant called Fjellheim, where the chef deconstructed a reindeer heart into something that looked, Zephyrine wrote, like "an argument the forest was finally willing to have with itself." The sentence had been quoted twice on social media before noon. By evening it was on a poster in the restaurant's window, set in letters the size of hymnals.

He had not tasted the reindeer heart.

He had not tasted anything in three years.

Zephyrine sat at his kitchen table in Oslo with the newspaper spread before him and read his own review the way a forger studies his best work — with pride, yes, but also with the low hum of dread that comes from knowing exactly how good you've gotten at this. The prose was immaculate. That was the problem. It was too immaculate, too certain of itself, a review written by a man who clearly remembered what butter did to leek when the heat was patient. He remembered everything. That was the cruelty of it. Taste had left him the way a lover leaves who takes nothing with them: no note, no warning, just an absence so complete that the rooms they once filled now seemed to have always been empty.

What remained was memory, and memory was generous to a fault.

He ate his lunch while he read — two slices of bread, unbuttered, because butter was a lie he couldn't afford to tell himself at home. He chewed and felt texture, temperature, the faint sweet dust of wheat, and nothing else. It was like eating photographs of bread. He swallowed anyway. A body expects to be fed even when the rest of you has resigned.

The phone buzzed against the table. His editor, Astrid. He let it go to voicemail and listened to the message while looking out the window at the rain coming down over Grünerløkka in long gray threads.

*"Zeph. Loved the piece. Loved it. But call me back, because I want to talk about the verb 'to bloom' — you used it in the cod course, and I could swear you used it in the halibut piece in March, and I could swear —"* a pause, the sound of her deciding whether to say the next thing — *"I could swear you borrowed it from Berg's review in 2019. Call me."*

He didn't call her back. Not yet.

Instead he opened the folder on his laptop that he thought of, privately, as the reliquary. Fourteen years of reviews, his own, arranged chronologically, and alongside them a second folder he had never named aloud: the sources. Screenshots of other critics' sentences going back decades. Scans of menus from restaurants closed twenty years before he ever held a fork professionally. Notes from his own childhood kitchen in Vardø, transcribed in a teenager's handwriting — *the dill goes in off the heat or it turns to hay, Ulyana says* — which he had mined so often that certain phrases had worn smooth as river stones.

Three years of this. Three years since the trial ended and his tongue went silent, and every week he had produced eight hundred words of authority about food he could not taste, stitched together from the dead language of other people's dinners. He had told himself it wasn't fraud. He had told himself it was translation — that he was translating memory into the present tense, that the meal in front of him merely confirmed what the meal in his head already knew. Chefs cooked from tradition; why shouldn't a critic cook from recollection?

But lately the seams were showing. Lately Astrid's red pen kept finding the same words blooming in different gardens.

He got up and stood at the window. Rain. The city below him doing its gray Tuesday business. Somewhere down there people were eating lunch and tasting it, tasting it without ceremony, without gratitude even, and he hated them a little, and then hated himself for hating them, and then went back to the table and began, mechanically, to draft his response to Astrid.

*I can explain,* he typed. Then deleted it. Explain what? That his palate had died of grief? That grief was not a recognized occupational hazard? That the most decorated critic in the country had been reviewing meals the way a blind man reviews paintings — faithfully, lovingly, from description?

His phone buzzed again. Not Astrid this time. An email, automated, from a law firm in Finnmark whose name he hadn't seen in years.

*Subject: Estate of U. Eriksen — Notice of Forced Sale.*

He read it standing up. He read it twice. The house in Vardø — the yellow house on the harbor road with the blue door Ulyana repainted every spring whether it needed it or not — had passed through some tangle of distant kin and municipal bureaucracy after her death, and now, debts of administration unpaid, it would be sold at public auction in nineteen days. Nineteen days. The email included a photograph taken by the auctioneer's assessor: the house under a sky the color of dishwater, gulls wheeling above the chimney, the blue door flaking.

He sat down slowly, the way old men sit down.

Ulyana was dead fourteen months. He had learned of it by accident — a death notice in a regional paper, three lines, no cause given, no service listed. He had not gone north for the funeral. He had told himself the ferries were difficult to book in February, which was true, and that she would have wanted it that way, which was a guess dressed up as knowledge. He had lit no candle. He had written no eulogy. Instead, that same week, he had published a rave review of a new bistro in Majorstuen, praising its "almost maternal generosity," and had gone home afterward and eaten plain bread in the dark.

Now the house itself was being sold out from under the past, and something in Zephyrine's chest — some muscle he hadn't known he still owned — clenched around the news like a fist around a hook.

* * *

That night he dreamed of the kitchen in Vardø, and the dream was more vivid than any meal he'd reviewed in three years.

In the dream it was winter and he was nine years old, sitting on the counter with his legs dangling, watching Ulyana work. She was a large woman built along the lines of a fishing boat — broad in the beam, weathered at the prow — and she moved through her kitchen with the economy of someone who had fed crowds her whole life and resented not one plate of it. Her hands were enormous. They looked like they should be clumsy and were instead the most precise instruments he had ever seen, before or since.

"Smell first," she said, in the dream as in life, holding a spoon of fish stock under his nose. "The tongue is a gossip. The nose is a witness."

He breathed it in: the sea reduced to its essentials, the sweetness of onion gone gold in butter, dill like a green rumor. In the dream he could taste it. In the dream his mouth flooded and he said something childish and ecstatic and she laughed, a laugh like a hatch opening, and ladled him a bowl, and outside the window the Barents Sea worked at the rocks with its gray patience, pulling back and pushing in, the undertow that had taken men from that coast for as long as there had been men on it.

"Ulyana," dream-Zephyrine said, "why do we feed people who don't ask?"

She didn't answer in words. She took the bowl from his hands, refilled it fuller than before, and set it in front of him again, and the look she gave him was the whole answer, and he understood it then completely, and understood, waking at four in the morning in his cold Oslo flat, that he had spent fourteen years failing to explain it in print.

He lay in bed while the city made its early noises. The trial came back to him the way it always did at this hour — not the courtroom, which he barely remembered, but the moment after, walking out of the courthouse into slanting rain with the verdict still warm in his ears, mentor ruined, wages recovered, justice served with a stamp on it. And the taste of the coffee he bought at the kiosk across the street simply — gone. Not bitter. Not bland. *Gone*, the way a word goes if you repeat it until it breaks. He had assumed shock. Shock lifts. This had stayed three years, a tenant who paid nothing and broke nothing and would not leave.

The doctors had used phrases like *functional* and *psychogenic* and *in some cases spontaneous remission*. They had said the word *grief* carefully, the way you hand someone a knife point-first.

He had never told anyone the full truth: that the taste hadn't died at the verdict. It had started dying earlier — the day he decided to testify, the day he chose the law's version of Ulyana's rule over Ulyana's version. Feed first, ask after, she had taught him. He had asked first, fed never, and his tongue had filed its resignation the same season.

* * *

Astrid cornered him on Friday. She did it the way she did everything, with espresso and no preamble, installing herself at his kitchen table uninvited because, as she put it, "your office has chairs but no honesty."

She was sixty-one, chain-vaped, brilliant, and had edited him for eleven years. She picked up the printout of Thursday's review and laid it flat between them like evidence at a hearing.

"'Bloom,'" she said. "'Bloom' appears in your cod course, your halibut course, and — I checked — Henrik Berg's 2019 piece on Maaemo. Word for word. You know what else? 'An argument the forest was finally willing to have.' That construction — noun, relative clause, personification of a landscape — that's you, fine, but the rhythm of it, Zeph, I've read that rhythm somewhere and I can't find where and it's driving me insane."

"It's a common construction."

"Don't insult us both." She leaned back. Outside, the rain had stopped and the light did that thin Scandinavian thing where it forgives nothing but illuminates everything anyway. "Here's what I actually think. I think you haven't tasted anything in a long time. I don't know how long. I noticed the bread thing last year — you ordered the cheese board at that dinner and wrote two lines about it that could have been written by anyone, and Zeph, you once wrote six hundred words about a single apricot. Six hundred. I framed it."

He said nothing. The silence stretched. Astrid, to her credit, let it.

"I'm not accusing you," she said finally. "I'm telling you what I see. Your prose is better than it's ever been and it's also — hollow. Technically perfect. Emotionally borrowed. Like listening to a great singer lip-sync." She tapped the review. "So either you tell me what's going on, or you take some time, or you keep filing these beautiful corpses and one day somebody sharper than me notices, and then it isn't a conversation anymore, it's an obituary."

"The house I grew up in is being auctioned," Zephyrine said.

It came out of nowhere, even to him. Astrid blinked.

"In Vardø," he said. "The woman who raised me. She died last year. I found out from a death notice. I didn't go to the funeral. I've been writing about food for fourteen years and I couldn't manage to get on a ferry for the only person who ever taught me what food was *for*."

Astrid studied him for a long moment. Whatever she saw, she had the grace not to name it.

"When's the auction?"

"Nineteen days."

"And you're going."

It wasn't a question, but he treated it as one, because the honest answer was *I don't know*, and the truest answer was *I am already on the ferry, I just haven't bought the ticket yet.* He heard himself say: "I need to see the kitchen."

"Then go," Astrid said, standing, gathering her coat. "File nothing for three weeks. If anyone asks, you're working on a long feature. Which you will be." At the door she paused. "And Zeph — whatever happened to you, whatever you're not saying — the longer you almost say it, the louder it gets."

* * *

He booked the ferry that night, or rather, discovered at midnight that he had booked the ferry — that some part of him had navigated to the booking page, entered his name, chosen a cabin, and paid, all while the rest of him sat watching the cursor blink in an empty document titled *ULYANA — NOTES*.

The confirmation email sat in his inbox like a small lit window in a dark house. *Departure: Saturday, 07:40, Hurtigruten northbound. Cabin 214.* He read it three times. He had made no decision. That was the strange mercy of it — for three years every sentence he'd published had been deliberated to death, assembled clause by clause from borrowed timber, and here, finally, was an act with no drafting, no revision, no second opinion. He had simply done the true thing by accident, the way a man sleepwalking might walk out an open door into air that wakes him.

He packed badly and quickly. Warm clothes he hadn't worn since the trial. A notebook. The printed email from the law firm, with the photograph of the yellow house, the blue door, the gulls. At the bottom of the bag, wrapped in a sweater, he placed the one object he owned from that kitchen: a wooden spoon, birch, handle worn to silk by Ulyana's thumb, which he had stolen — there was no other word — when he left Vardø at eighteen and had never once used.

He slept two hours. At five he gave up, made coffee he wouldn't taste, and drank it anyway, standing at the window while Oslo turned from black to pewter. Southward the city slept on. Northward, behind all that distance, a house waited with its doors chained — the email had mentioned chains, a court officer's precaution — holding whatever Ulyana had left in it. Drawers. Ledgers. Maybe nothing. Maybe the answer to a question he had carried so long it had grown into his spine: *what did she do, exactly, all those years, feeding half the town under false names — and why did it work, and why does the law have no shelf for it?*

At seven-forty the ferry pulled away from the terminal, and Oslo slid backward into the rain, and Zephyrine stood at the rail with the wind taking his hair apart, watching the coastline assemble itself plank by plank, island by island, toward the north.

Somewhere around Lofoten, a steward offered him dried fish as a snack, the way they do, and he accepted a piece automatically, out of fourteen years of professional habit, and put it on his tongue.

Nothing. Salt-colored nothing.

But the smell — the smell went through him like a harpoon line: cold water, smoke, iron, his mother-country of odor, and beneath it something older, something he suddenly understood he had been circling for three years without naming. Not hunger. Not grief, exactly.

Obligation.

The ferry drove north into the darkening water, and Zephyrine Voss stood at the rail eating a fish he could not taste, going home to a house that no longer belonged to anyone — least of all, he suspected, to him.
