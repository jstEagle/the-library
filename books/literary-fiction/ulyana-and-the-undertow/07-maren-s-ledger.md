## 7. Maren's Ledger

The ledger lived under the third floorboard from the window, the one that creaked, and Maren had been taught to lift it with a fish knife, not a screwdriver, because a screwdriver left marks and a fish knife left nothing but the smell of herring, which nobody in Vardø would ever question.

She had gone down for it before Zephyrine Eriksen even knocked.

That was the thing she couldn't explain to anyone, least of all herself: she had known he was coming the way you know a storm is coming — not by seeing it but by the way the gulls went quiet and the water changed its mind about its color. Ulyana would have said the house told her. The house had opinions; Ulyana had said so often enough, standing in the kitchen with her hands in flour up to the wrists, claiming the floorboards complained about damp and the chimney sulked when it wasn't fed birch. Maren had rolled her eyes at fourteen. At seventeen she caught herself apologizing to the stove.

So when the man came up from the ferry with his city coat and his city face, walking past the seawall like he didn't see her standing on it — like she was a post, a bollard, a piece of the town's furniture — Maren had already pried up the board and taken the ledger out and put it in her school bag, under the math homework she never did, because some part of her had understood before he reached the chained gate:

He had come back for the books. And the books were hers now.

* * *

Ulyana had found her the winter Maren was eleven, the winter after her mother stopped coming home before dark and started coming home with explanations instead. Maren remembered it as a smell before anything else — fish stock and dill drifting down Havnegata at nine at night, when every other kitchen in Vardø had gone cold hours ago. She'd followed it the way cats follow things, without dignity or plan, to the green door of the house on the harbor side, the one everyone knew about and nobody mentioned, and Ulyana had opened the door before she knocked.

"You've been standing out there four minutes," Ulyana said. "In this wind. Come in before the soup takes offense."

That was how she talked. As if food had feelings, as if weather held grudges, as if the whole world were a household that needed managing. Maren had stepped inside and the warmth had hit her like a wave and she had thought, with the terrible clarity of children: *this is what my mother's kitchen used to be.*

Ulyana never asked why she'd come. That was the first rule, though Maren wouldn't learn it was a rule until much later, because Ulyana never announced her rules. She just did them, the way she salted water — without measuring, without apology, and always exactly right.

*Feed first, ask after.* Maren learned that one by watching. A man came to the door in February with a face like a dropped plate and Ulyana sat him down and put a bowl in front of him and talked to him about the price of diesel for twenty minutes while he ate, and only afterward, when his shoulders had come down from around his ears, did she say, gently, "Now. Who is she, and how long has it been going on?" Because of course it was a woman. It was always, eventually, a woman or a boss or a debt, and Ulyana could tell which by how a man held his spoon.

Maren learned the ledger second. It was a school exercise book, blue-ruled, the kind that cost thirty kroner at the Coop, and Ulyana kept it under the floorboard because, she said, "A record is a dangerous thing, little one. It should live somewhere humble." Every meal that left the kitchen under a false name went into it. Not the meals she sold — those were honest business, cash in the tin by the till. The ledger was for the others. *Fish soup, 4 portions, "for the church raffle" — given to Ingrid B., bruised again.* *Barley porridge, 6 portions, "staff meals" — Halvorsen family, leaving Friday, no time to pack food.* *Bread, 2 loaves, "burnt, unsellable" — they were not burnt. Old Sverre, who took what wasn't his from the Coop and could not say why.*

"Why do you lie in the book?" Maren had asked once, tracing the neat columns. "If it's secret anyway."

"Because a lie with a purpose is a tool," Ulyana said, "and a truth written down is a weapon someone else can pick up. I feed people the law has failed. If I wrote it plainly, I'd be handing the law a list of its failures, and the law doesn't fix failures, little one. It punishes them." She had tapped the page with one flour-white finger. "The law is a net. It catches what fits the mesh. Everything else slips through, and everything that slips through comes to my door eventually, smelling of sea and shame. So we write them down sideways. So they stay people, not cases."

Maren hadn't understood all of it then. She understood the parts she'd seen. She'd seen Ingrid B.'s husband at the pub being charming, and Ingrid B. at the harbor the next morning with sunglasses in January. She'd seen the Halvorsen kids at the ferry terminal with their suitcases tied with string, waving at a town that had already begun forgetting them, deported back to a country the oldest daughter barely remembered. She'd seen old Sverre stacking firewood against Ulyana's wall all one October, plank by plank, saying nothing, repaying two winters of bread the only way a proud thief knows how — and Ulyana letting him, counting nothing, thanking him once, briefly, the way you thank weather for rain.

The ledger held all of it. Eleven years of it. And when Ulyana died — quickly, between one tide and the next, in the chair by the window with a half-knitted sock still on the needles — the ledger became Maren's, along with the floorboard, the fish knife, and the rules.

She was sixteen. She had not wanted any of it. She had wanted, more than anything, for the woman in the chair to open her eyes and say, "You've let the stock go lazy, little one," and Maren would have taken that scolding over an inheritance the way you'd take bread over gold.

* * *

Now there was a stranger in the kitchen.

Not a stranger, exactly — that was the problem. Zephyrine Eriksen moved through Ulyana's kitchen like a man moving through his own memory, touching things without picking them up, and every place his fingers hovered near, Maren wanted to slap them away. The ladle on its hook. The chipped blue bowl. The window latch that you had to lift *and* push, no one ever guessed.

He stood where Ulyana used to stand, and the kitchen didn't object, and that felt like betrayal by architecture.

"You were her student too," Maren said. She'd broken the chain off the gate with bolt cutters borrowed from her uncle's boat shed, and she regretted it already, and she'd do it again, because the alternative was letting him stand outside looking through windows like a tourist at a wreck. "Everyone keeps saying that. 'He was raised here.' Like it makes him family."

"Doesn't it?" He said it carefully, the way outsiders said everything in Vardø, weighing each word like coins.

"No." She pulled the ledger from her bag and set it on the table between them, and watched his face change — watched the careful drain out of it. "Family stays. Family gets fed. You want to know what this house was? Read it. But you read it here, at this table, and you don't take it anywhere, and when you're done you tell me why a grown man who had all this"—she swept her arm at the pots still hanging, the range Ulyana had polished the week she died, the shelf of jars labeled in three scripts—"*left*, and only came back when there was something in it for him. An auction. A house. Papers." Her voice cracked on the last word and she hated it. "There are people in this town who loved her and got nothing, and you get the *house*."

He looked at the ledger the way starving men look through restaurant windows. She knew that look. She'd worn it herself at eleven.

"She taught me," Maren said, quieter, because the anger had burned down to the coals underneath and the coals were worse. "For six years. Feed first, ask after. Never let the law do what a table can do better. Salt the water like the sea is watching. She made me memorize the soups before she let me touch the bread. She said bread forgives, soup doesn't." She swallowed. "And then she died, and I'm supposed to be the last one, and the last one shouldn't be seventeen and should not have to explain herself to a critic who writes about food he—"

She stopped. Too far. His face did something small and terrible, a door closing behind his eyes, and part of her — the part Ulyana had trained, the part that asked after — wanted to take it back.

But another part, the part that had stood on the seawall watching him walk past like she was furniture, let it stand.

* * *

He read the ledger for a long time. The light moved. Outside, the tide turned and the harbor began its slow exhale, boats settling on their lines, and Maren pretended to make tea and mostly listened to the sound of pages turning in a dead woman's handwriting.

"She hid the names," he said finally. "'For the church raffle.' There is no church raffle."

"There hasn't been since 2011. She kept using it anyway." Maren set his tea down — strong, sweet, the way Ulyana made it for people who'd had bad news, and she realized only after she'd done it that she'd made it that way for *him*, and resented her own hands. "It was code. Raffle meant a woman getting hurt. Staff meals meant a family running. Burnt bread meant—" She hesitated. "Burnt bread meant someone who stole. Someone the town had decided to hate. She fed them anyway and called it burnt so God wouldn't notice."

Zephyrine's finger stopped on a line. Maren leaned despite herself and saw it: *Bread, 2 loaves, "burnt" — Z.E., age 9, nightmares again. Extra butter.*

The silence stretched so long the kettle ticked as it cooled.

"I remember the bread," he said. His voice had gone strange, thin as ice in March. "I thought—I used to think she baked it badly on purpose so I wouldn't feel like charity."

"She never baked anything badly in her life." Maren heard her own voice shake. "That was the point. Nothing she gave you was ever second-best. That's the whole rule, don't you understand? Anyone can give leftovers. The law gives leftovers — whatever falls through the mesh, whatever's cheap to process. She gave the good bread. To the ones who deserved it least, by everyone else's counting. *Especially* to them." She yanked the ledger toward her, protective, absurd. "You got extra butter. Do you know what Ingrid B. got? Fish soup with saffron. Saffron costs more per gram than the fish. She spent grocery money on saffron for a woman whose own husband broke her wrist, because—" Maren stopped, breath ragged.

"Because," Zephyrine said softly, "the meal is the argument."

Maren stared at him. "What?"

"That's what she said. Once. When I was—" He rubbed his face. "I must have been ten. A man came to the door and she fed him and I asked why, he'd done something, everyone said so, and she said, *the meal is the argument, little one. The verdict comes later, if it comes at all. First you prove they're worth feeding. Then the world has to argue with you.*"

The kitchen was very quiet. Somewhere under the floorboards the house creaked, settling, or listening.

"That's the sentence," Maren said suddenly, because it landed in her all at once, the way Ulyana's lessons always had — sideways, late, complete. "The one the clerk was talking about. At the auction office. Everyone says she died owing a sentence. I thought it was gossip." She gripped the ledger's soft cover. "It wasn't a debt. It was a lesson she never finished teaching. She was writing it down. For whoever came back."

"And if no one came back?"

"Then it was for me." Maren lifted her chin. "I'm the last student. Whatever she owed, I inherit it. That's how debts work in this house."

Something crossed his face — not guilt exactly, guilt was too comfortable a word for it. More like a man discovering the floor of a room he'd thought he knew.

"The attic," he said. "I haven't been up yet."

Maren looked at the stairs, at the narrow door with its swollen frame, at the dust-sheeted dark above where Ulyana had kept the things she was still deciding about. Her whole body said *no*. Her whole body had been saying *no* since the ferry docked — no to him, no to the chain cut, no to the tea with extra sugar, no to the way the kitchen had warmed around him like it remembered.

But Ulyana had also taught her this, without ever saying it: *you don't get to choose who hunger sends to your door. You only choose whether the door opens.*

"Take the lamp," Maren said. "The stairs lie about their edges. And Zephyrine—" He paused on the bottom step, and it was the first time she'd said his name aloud, and it tasted like a word from the ledger, sideways-written, true anyway. "Whatever's up there. You read it *here*. At this table. Where she fed you."

He climbed into the dark. Maren stayed below with the ledger open on her knees, on the page with the extra butter, and did not let herself cry until she heard the floorboards overhead begin to creak — and then only a little, and only because the house was listening, and the house, she was almost sure, approved.
