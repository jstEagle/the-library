## 24. The Boat Registry

The harbor archives occupied the ground floor of the old customs house, a building that had been everything in its life — customs office, naval depot, briefly a discotheque in the eighties according to a plaque nobody had paid for — and was now a repository for the paperwork of drowned things. Fishing licenses. Cargo manifests. The bureaucratic sediment of a port that had stopped being a port and started being a memory with parking meters.

The woman at the desk had a cardigan the color of weak tea and the expression of someone who had spent forty years watching people arrive looking for their pasts and leave looking slightly embarrassed about it.

"Vessel registry," I said. "Sales of vessels. Nineteen eighty-nine."

"Name?"

"The boat's or mine?" I asked, and she almost smiled. Almost. It was the kind of smile you'd need sonar to detect.

"The vessel."

"*La Sirena Rota.* The Broken Mermaid." My father had named her that as a joke, or a prayer, or both — the way men name boats after the thing they're afraid will happen. She had been broken from the day he bought her, secondhand, hull patched with optimism and fiberglass. He loved her anyway. Or he loved what she stood for: the idea that a man could go out onto the water every morning and come back with something.

The woman typed with two fingers, each keystroke landing like a small verdict. "Registry transfers are in the back room. You'll need to fill out a request slip. Pencil only."

"Pencil only," I repeated. "Of course. We wouldn't want anyone amending history in ink."

She looked at me over her glasses with the weary patience of a person who had heard every joke the archives had ever produced and found all of them wanting. "It's so the records don't get marked up."

I filled out my slip. *Researcher: Beatrix Vasquez. Purpose: personal.* There was no box for *purpose: exhumation*, though it seemed like there should be.

* * *

The back room smelled of dust and cold paper and the particular melancholy of bound volumes no one had opened since the last time someone came looking for something they wished they hadn't found. The registry books were enormous — ledgers the size of tombstones, their spines cracked, their pages the yellow-brown of old teeth. Each entry was written in a different hand, because registries are kept by clerks and clerks die and are replaced by other clerks, and so the record of one town's maritime life is really a relay race of handwriting, baton passed from pen to pen across decades.

I found 1989 and ran my finger down the transfer column. March. April. May.

My father died on the fourteenth of June. I knew this the way I knew my own blood type — not as information but as constitution. I had been eleven. Marisol had been fifteen and had already begun his career of knowing things and saying nothing, which at the time I had mistaken for grief and which I now understood was a much older profession than that.

I found the entry on the last page of May.

*La Sirena Rota. Trawler, 9.4 meters, wood hull, engine Perkins 4-107. Seller: Tomás Vasquez. Buyer: [name smudged, water damage]. Consideration: recorded. Date of transfer: 31 May 1989.*

Two weeks before he died.

I sat down on the stool provided — a stool, singular, as if the archives anticipated exactly one seeker at a time, one grief per room — and read the entry again. And again. The way you reread a sentence that has changed shape between readings, the way you test a loose tooth.

Two weeks before he died, my father sold his boat.

Except he hadn't. That was the thing. That was the thing sitting in the middle of the room like a stone dropped through the floor of everything I knew.

Because I remembered June of 1989. Not all of it — I was eleven, memory is a colander — but I remembered this: my father still went down to the harbor every morning those last weeks. I remembered him leaving before dawn in his rubber boots, and I remembered Marisol going with him sometimes, and I remembered my mother standing at the sink saying, "He shouldn't be going out, look at him," in the dialect, in that low worried register she used when she thought we weren't listening. And I remember — this is the part I would swear to, the part I have carried for thirty years without knowing it was evidence — I remember the boat coming back in the evening. Lights on the water. The sound of the engine idling into the dock. If the boat had been sold on the thirty-first of May, then whose boat was coming home every night in June?

Unless the sale wasn't real. Unless the registry said *sold* the way a stage says *forest*: painted, declared, useful for the scene being staged.

A dead man can't contest a bill of sale. A dying man barely can. And a man with debts — my father had debts, this much was true, this much even the loyal version of the family story admitted; creditors had come to the door twice that spring, men with clipboards and the flat politeness of people who have taken many houses — a man with debts might well want his most valuable asset moved out of his own name before the clipboards turned into court orders.

But a man doesn't sign his own boat away while he's still sailing it. Not unless someone is holding the pen for him.

I looked at the seller line again. *Tomás Vasquez.* The signature beside it was cramped and slanted, the signature of a man who wrote rarely and angrily. I knew my father's hand. Every child of a certain kind of household knows their father's hand the way they know his cough — from the shopping lists, the notes left on the table, the birthday cards signed with the effort of a man performing affection in cursive. His letters were tall and narrow, leaning right like fence posts in wind. He crossed his t's with a single violent stroke that often took out the neighboring letter as collateral damage.

This signature was careful. Round. Almost gentle. The *T* was looped. My father did not loop. My father considered looping a form of vanity.

Someone else had written my father's name.

I didn't feel shock, exactly. Shock is sudden; this arrived more like weather, a front moving in that had been forming offshore for chapters. What I felt was closer to recognition — the click of a lock I hadn't known I'd been carrying. Odalys at the kitchen table that spring, papers spread out, telling me to go play. Odalys who "handled things." Odalys who could talk to the creditors in their language and to my mother in ours and to the bank in a third dialect made entirely of reassurance. Odalys, who had been at our house nearly every day that May because my mother was spending her days at the hospital and someone had to keep the household upright, and Odalys kept things upright the way she kept everything: by deciding, unilaterally, what the truth should be allowed to weigh.

Or maybe not Odalys. Maybe my father himself, lucid enough to plan, weak enough to dictate. Maybe a lawyer. Maybe any of the hundred plausible explanations that a reasonable person would accept and a daughter of Ferro Street never would, because we are not raised to be reasonable about our dead; we are raised to defend them.

I copied the entry into my notebook, pencil only, and then — because the tea-cardigan woman was watching and because some instincts are deeper than irony — I requested a certified copy of the page. It cost me eleven units and a form in triplicate. Bureaucracy, I have come to believe, is just grief with a filing system.

* * *

Outside, the harbor was doing its afternoon performance: gulls, tourists, a man selling roasted nuts from a cart with the confidence of a man selling diamonds. The water lay flat and silver, indifferent, the way water always is when you need it to testify.

I sat on a bench with the certified copy in its official envelope and called Marisol. It rang six times. I counted. Counting rings is a habit of translators; we are trained to notice duration, to know that a pause is never empty, it's just speaking another language.

"What," he said. Not *hello*. Marisol answered phones like a man defending a position.

"I'm at the harbor archives."

A pause. Long enough to be a sentence. "Why?"

"Because Lumen's landlady says she went to the harbor town where the boat was sold. Because the letters mention the boat. Because everything in this family eventually comes back to that boat, Marisol, including you, including your mysterious arrangement with the developer, including whatever it is you've been not-telling me since before I had words for not-telling."

"You've been reading too many of Mami's letters."

"They're very good letters. She was very good at saying things to the one person she couldn't say them to." I unfolded the copy against my knee. "The boat was sold on the thirty-first of May. Papá died on the fourteenth of June."

Silence. The kind of silence that has furniture in it.

"I know," Marisol said finally.

The world tilted a degree and settled. "You *know*."

"I was fifteen, Bea. I went with him to the office. I sat outside the door." His voice had gone flat, the flatness he used for logistics, for deflection, for the grammar of avoidance I'd catalogued years ago and never managed to translate. "There were papers. There was a man from the bank and a man Odalys brought, and Papá was tired, he was so tired, he could barely hold his head up. And afterward he didn't talk about it. Nobody talked about it. And then he died and there was a funeral and there was you, eleven years old, asking questions, and Mami saying *the sea takes what it takes*, and I thought—" He stopped.

"Thought what?"

"That if I said what I saw, it would break her. She was already broken. So I put it away." A breath. "That's what I do, Bea. You know that. I put things away. It's the family business. Odalys does it with documents, Mami did it with letters, I do it with—"

"With everything."

"With everything," he agreed, and for once there was no armor in it.

"Whose name was on the signature line?" I asked. "Did you see who signed?"

Another pause, longer, and I understood that I had reached the edge of what Marisol would give me today, maybe the edge of what he had. "I saw Odalys holding the pen," he said. "I didn't see her write. But Bea — Papá couldn't write his own name by then. His hands shook too bad. He hadn't signed anything in weeks. Mami signed his forms. Everyone knew that."

"And Odalys signed this one."

"I have to go," Marisol said, which in Marisol meant *you've gotten close to something and I need the distance to decide whether to protect you from it or from myself.*

"Marisol. Two weeks. He sailed her for two weeks after the registry said she was gone. Whose boat was he sailing?"

"Ask Odalys," he said, and hung up, and the dial tone — even now, even with mobile phones, some hangups produce a dial tone in the soul — hummed in my ear like a struck string.

* * *

I walked the length of the harbor because walking is what I do instead of crying, and crying is what I do instead of speaking, and speaking — well. Speaking is the thing I've been rationing for three years, and the reserves were running strange and low.

The fishing fleet was smaller now, mostly pleasure craft, a few working boats flying the flags of regulations my father would have found hilarious. At the far end of the pier, an old man was mending nets with the unhurried competence of someone whose hands predate his opinions. I watched him for a while. In another life, in the loyal version of the story, that could have been my father, sixty and salt-cured and still going out, still coming back with something.

In the true version, my father spent his last two weeks sailing a boat that legally belonged to someone else, and I will never know whether he knew. Whether he sat at that wheel understanding that his name had been lifted off his one possession like a label soaked off a jar, and consented — because he was dying and tired and trusted his sister-in-law, or because he was too far gone to understand, and the trust was entirely Odalys's invention, exercised on behalf of a man who could no longer grant it.

Both versions broke something. One broke his dignity. The other broke hers, and mine, and Marisol's, and my mother's, in a chain reaction that took thirty years to reach the surface, like something sunk that finally, finally gives up its air.

The letters made sense now. The ones I'd read — the early ones, the pleading ones — my mother writing to Lumen: *come home, I cannot carry this house alone, there are papers, there is always papers, and I do not know what I am signing anymore.* I had read that line as metaphor. Translators do that; we reach for the figurative because the literal is too heavy to lift. But my mother had not been writing poetry. She had been writing testimony, addressed to the one person who might have stopped it, and the letter had been intercepted, and the boat had been sold, and the man had died, and the family had folded the whole affair into the shape of tragedy — *the sea takes what it takes* — when the sea had taken nothing. The sea had been robbed, and blamed for it.

And Lumen. Lumen who vanished that summer, who became a myth, a cautionary tale, a hole in the deed. Had she run because she'd learned what Odalys did? Had she been sent away? Was her disappearance not a mystery at all but a consequence, the way a splash is the consequence of a stone?

I stood at the rail at the end of the pier until the blue hour came on — that hour I keep returning to in this story like a tide, the hour my mother rode the bus home, the hour the light forgives nothing but makes everything beautiful anyway — and I took out my notebook and wrote, in English, because the dialect still wouldn't come:

*They sold the boat while he was still sailing it. They sold the boat and let him sail on, believing, or not believing, and called it saving the family. And when he died they buried the sale under the funeral and the funeral under the grief, and grief makes excellent landfill. Thirty years. Thirty years of everyone protecting everyone from the truth, and calling the protection love.*

Then underneath, in smaller letters, the question that had been forming since the archive, since the bus, since the landlady who said Lumen *talked like the sea*:

*What did Mami know, and when did she stop forgiving Odalys — and why did she never once say it out loud?*

I closed the notebook. The gulls were settling. Somewhere behind me the nut vendor was packing up his cart, folding his little empire into a hand truck.

One person alive knew the whole of it. One person had held the pen, kept the deed, intercepted the letter, shattered the plate, edited the summer. One person had spent thirty years being the family's memory, and memory, when it lies long enough, becomes a kind of prison with the keeper inside it too.

I picked up my bag with the certified copy inside — my father's name, in a stranger's hand, stamped and sealed and undeniable — and I started toward Ferro Street, toward the porch where Odalys sat every evening like a lighthouse for a coast that had stopped needing one.

It was time, I thought, to ask her to read the water.
