## 14. What Idris Hid

The song, he decided, was a map.

He had spent the night with the notebook open on his knees, the lamp turned low, reading the second verse until the words stopped being words and became instructions. *The gulls know where I keep it. Under the boards where the tide comes in. Look where the water goes and you will find what I could not say.* Water-damaged ink, a boy's careful handwriting gone soft at the edges, and beneath it all — Emrys was certain now, certain the way you are certain of weather — a place. Idris had never in his life written anything without burying a second meaning in it. Even his school essays had come home with jokes folded into the margins, jokes only Emrys was meant to find. *Look where the water goes.* The house stood on the rise above the strand, and when the storms came — Emrys remembered this with his body, the way you remember cold — the water came up under the floor of the back room. Idris's room. Their father had complained about it every winter for eleven years. *The sea in the house again, like living in a wreck.* They had pulled up boards twice to dry the joists. Idris had watched the whole operation both times, sitting on his bed with his chin on his knees, saying nothing, smiling at nothing.

Emrys had thought, at fourteen, that his brother was bored.

He woke on the sofa in the front room with his neck at a wrong angle and the grey light of the village morning pressing at the curtains, and for a moment he did not know where he was, and then he did, all at once, the way grief always finds you: completely, and with the smell of salt in it. The house smelled of held breath. Forty years of it. He put the kettle on because his hands needed a task, and while it heated he stood in the kitchen looking at the door to the back room, and the door looked back at him the way doors do in old houses — patiently, with all the time in the world.

*You could leave today,* said the part of him that had kept him safe for thirty years, the translator's voice, the voice that moved meaning from one language to another without ever having to own it. *You could sign the papers, take the cheque, go back to London, and let the strangers who buy this house pull up whatever boards they like. They'd find it or they wouldn't. It wouldn't be your secret anymore.*

But it had never been his secret, that was the thing. That was the whole terrible architecture of his life. Everyone else had known the shape of it — Carys, Ifor, perhaps the whole village in its silent way — and he alone had been kept outside his own family's weather. He was done being outside. Whatever Idris had hidden, he had hidden it for someone. And Emrys was beginning to suspect — had been suspecting since the second verse, since *what I could not say* — who that someone was.

The kettle boiled. He did not make the tea.

* * *

Idris's room was the smallest in the house, a box room tacked onto the back where the sea could reach it, and in the years since their parents died it had become the room where things went to be forgotten: the broken pram, boxes of tax papers, a rolled carpet gone the colour of weak tea. Emrys had to move all of it, hauling the carpet out into the hall, shifting boxes, breathing dust that tasted of every winter he had not been here. The floor was old pine, boards the width of a hand, some of them darker than the others where the damp had come up over the decades and dried, come and dried, the tide's own signature.

He knelt on the cold boards and looked for the water's path.

It was not difficult, once he let himself think like the sea. The damp had always come from the seaward corner, under the window, and run diagonally toward the chimney breast, following the fall of the joists. The darkened boards made the route legible — a stain like a river on a map, a river of winters. He followed it on his knees, pressing each board with his palms, feeling for give, for the looseness that comes when a nail has been drawn and driven again, or not driven at all.

The fourth board from the corner gave.

Not much. A whisper of movement, a sound like a held breath released. Emrys sat back on his heels and looked at it for a long time. His heart was doing something he did not like, a slow heavy knocking, as if someone were at a door far away in his chest and had been knocking for years.

There were no nail heads visible. The board sat flush with its neighbours, seamless, innocent. But it moved. He fetched the crowbar from the kitchen — the same crowbar, he realised, that their father had used on these very boards to dry the joists, still hanging on its nail in the scullery, still wearing its collar of rust — and he worked the edge in gently, gently, the way you open something you are afraid of waking.

The board came up with a groan of old wood. Beneath it: darkness, and the smell of the sea's memory, and something pale.

Not money. Not the tin of letters Carys must have imagined, the evidence Ifor had been paying all these years to find. A bundle, wrapped in a plastic bread bag gone brittle with age, tied with the blue twine their mother used for the beans in the garden. And under the bundle, flattened against the joist, a second thing: a child's tin whistle, its fipple clogged with salt, the paint worn away on the holes where a thumb had pressed and pressed.

Emrys knew the whistle. He had bought Idris that whistle with his own saved pocket money at the fair in Pwllheli, the summer they were eleven and nine. He had not thought of it in forty years and now it rose in him whole, complete, with the smell of candyfloss and diesel attached, and he had to put his hand flat against the floor to steady himself.

He carried the bundle to the window because the light was better there, and his hands were shaking, and he could not get the twine undone, and then he did.

* * *

There were seven letters.

They were written on the lined paper from Idris's school jotter, torn out unevenly, folded in thirds. The ink had feathered where the damp had reached through the bag, but the handwriting was Idris's — that forward-leaning, impatient hand, the letters running downhill like boys racing — and the name on each one stopped Emrys's breath before he read a single word.

*Dear Emrys.*

Every one of them. Seven letters, and every one of them addressed to him.

He sat down on Idris's bed — the bed frame still there, bare, the mattress long gone — and held the letters without opening them, the way you hold a bird that has flown into a window, afraid that your grip is the thing that will finish it. The light came through the glass grey and slow. Somewhere below, the tide was doing its work against the rocks, that sound you stopped hearing when you lived here and heard forever once you left.

He opened the one on top. It was dated the sixth of August — six days before the boat, five days before the end of everything he had understood about his own childhood — and it began, in his brother's downhill hand:

*Dear Emrys, you were never the reason.*

He read it four times. The first time the words would not stick; they slid off his mind like rain off glass. The second time they stuck and hurt. The third and fourth times he was crying, quietly, the way he did everything, without witnesses, without sound, without ever once in fifty-four years having learned to do it properly, out loud, in front of another person — and the letter said:

*Dear Emrys, you were never the reason. I know you'll think you were, because you think everything is your fault, you've been carrying the whole family's weather since you were small and nobody ever asked you to put it down. So I'm writing this so you can't. Whatever happens on Friday, whatever they tell you, I want it written down somewhere that it was my idea, my plan, my boat, my storm to walk into. Carys and I are going and I'm not asking permission any more. If it goes wrong it goes wrong because I wanted too much, not because my brother loved me too much. Don't let them bury you with me. You're the one who can say things. Say them.*

Emrys put the letter down on his knee and looked out the window at the sea and could not have said, if a gun had been at his head, what he was feeling, because it was not one feeling but all of them at once, the whole drowned summer surfacing at speed.

*You're the one who can say things.*

He, Emrys, who had not spoken a full sentence of Welsh aloud in thirty years. He, who had built a whole life out of other people's words, moving meaning between languages like a man ferrying passengers across a river he himself never crossed. Idris had thought he could speak. Idris had believed it the way you believe in weather, without argument, and had written it down and buried it under the floor where the tide came in, and had died — had gone out into the storm, Emrys understood now, not to die but to force the world to let him go, and the storm had made the argument final — believing that his brother was the one with the voice.

And Emrys had spent the rest of his life proving him wrong.

* * *

The other letters were variations, rehearsals, a boy trying to get a hard thing said and saying it differently each time, the way Emrys himself revised translations, hunting for the sentence that was true. In one, Idris apologised for leaving. In another he did not apologise at all and was magnificent about it, seventeen years old and furious and free on paper. In a third he wrote about the song — *the one I only ever played for Carys, and once for you when you were pretending to be asleep, don't think I didn't know* — and said he was writing a second verse for it, a verse that was a key, and that he was leaving the key where the water went, because water keeps things better than people do, water never tells.

The last letter was different. Shorter. The handwriting had changed, tightened, the downhill run gone, each word placed like a foot on wet rock. It was dated the eleventh of August. The day before the boat.

*Dear Emrys. If you're reading this then I didn't come back and Carys didn't tell you, and I'm sorry for both of those things, but especially the second. She'll have her reasons. She's better at keeping things than either of us. Forgive her before you forgive me, and forgive me first, because I asked for this. All of it. Even the storm. Especially the storm. You were never the reason. You were the reason I thought I could survive wanting this much. — I.*

*Forgive her before you forgive me.*

Emrys sat in his dead brother's room with seven letters fanned across his knees like a losing hand of cards, and understood that he had been forgiven before he had even sinned — that Idris had known, six days before it happened, that Emrys would tell their parents about the plan to run away, that Emrys would do it out of love, out of terror, out of that terrible eldest-child instinct to keep everyone safe even from their own happiness, and that Idris had forgiven him for it in advance, in ink, under the floor, where the water kept it all these years without telling.

Carys had carried the secret to protect Idris's memory. Ifor had carried his own grief and grievance, circling this house for decades, paying to keep it unsold, believing something was hidden here that would set his account right. And Emrys had carried nothing but the absence of knowing, the shape of a guilt he couldn't name, and had built his whole silence on top of it like a house on a fault line.

Three people, circling the same buried thing, for forty years. And the thing itself had been a blessing all along. It had been forgiveness, folded in thirds, waiting under the boards where the tide came in, patient as water.

He laughed — one sound, cracked, nothing like laughter — and pressed the letters to his face, and they smelled of salt and dust and pencil shavings and his brother, or his brother's ghost, or the part of his brother that forty years of sea air could not reach.

* * *

He was still sitting on the floor of Idris's room when the light began to fail, the letters read so many times he could have translated them into Norwegian, into anything, into the language of his exile. He had decided, in the slow way decisions crystallise in old houses, what he had to do. Not sign. Not run. He would read them aloud — here, in this room, in Welsh, the words coming back to his mouth like tide-borne objects, some of them broken, all of them his — and then he would take them to Carys, and to Ifor, and he would say the whole of it out loud, before witnesses, before the house passed to strangers and the floorboards became someone else's floorboards.

He was wrapping the letters back in their brittle bag, carefully, the way you handle a thing that has already survived more than it should have, when he heard the front door.

Not opened — tried. The handle turning, the door meeting the lock, a pause. Then a key — Bronwen's key, the agent's key, the one that opened this house for viewings — and footsteps in the hall that were not Bronwen's. Too heavy. Too slow. A man's tread, the tread of someone who had walked this floor a thousand times and expected it to be his.

Emrys stood up in the darkening room, the letters against his chest, and heard the footsteps stop at the bottom of the stairs, and heard Ifor's voice come up through the floorboards — the same boards, he thought absurdly, the same pine, carrying it the way they had carried the tide all these years — rough and low and not entirely steady:

'I know you're up there, Emrys Ashgrove. We need to talk about what your brother left in this house. And I'm not leaving without it.'
