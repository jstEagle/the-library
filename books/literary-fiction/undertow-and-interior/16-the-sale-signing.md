## 16. The Sale Signing

The solicitor's office in Pwllheli smelled of toner and old carpet, a smell that had nothing to do with the sea, though the sea was visible through the window if you leaned far enough to the left — a grey stripe between two buildings, like something glimpsed through a keyhole. Emrys had been leaning that way for ten minutes.

Mr. Rhys Parry-Jones — *Dewi*, he insisted, everyone called him Dewi — had laid out the documents in a fan across his desk with the tenderness of a man arranging a hand of cards he already knew would lose. Completion statement. Transfer deed. Land Registry forms. A pen, not cheap, not expensive, resting on the top sheet at an angle Emrys suspected was deliberate. Solicitors knew about pens. They knew that a signature was a small death, and they offered you the instrument of it the way an undertaker offers a spade.

"Everything's in order," Dewi said. "The buyer's solicitor confirmed this morning. Funds are cleared. Once you sign, completion follows at twelve noon."

Twelve noon. As if the house were a hostage whose release had been negotiated to the hour.

Emrys picked up the pen. It was heavier than it looked. He turned it once between his fingers and thought: *this is how it ends, then. Not with a wave or a fire or a dramatic collapse, but with ballpoint ink on machine-made paper, in an office above a shop that sells mobile phones.*

Across the desk, Ifor sat with his cap on his knees.

They had not spoken since the harbour wall, since the night Ifor's kindness had come apart in his hands like wet rope — *the bailer was gone, boy, taken out before ever he launched* — and then clammed shut when Carys walked through the pub door. Two weeks of silence. Two weeks of Emrys living half in London and half in the house on the hill, sleeping badly among boxes, reading Idris's letters over and over until the paper went soft as cloth at the folds. He had brought one of them with him today. It sat in his inside pocket, against his chest, like a coal that refused to go cold.

Ifor looked older than he had at the pub, or perhaps Emrys was simply seeing him properly now, without the beer and the dim light softening things. The hands were enormous, scarred along every knuckle, resting on the cap as if the cap might bolt. His face was the colour and texture of a fence post that has stood through forty winters. But the eyes were quick, and they went from the pen in Emrys's fingers to Emrys's face and back again, patient as gulls.

"You'll want to read it all first," Dewi said gently. "Take your time."

Emrys read. Or rather, his eyes moved over the words while some other part of him listened — listened to the rain starting against the window, fine and sideways off the bay; listened to the radiator ticking; listened to Ifor breathing, slow and deliberate, a man conserving himself for something.

*The property known as Tŷ'r Aber, Cwm Eilir...*

He had learned the house's name before he'd learned his own, probably. *Tŷ'r Aber.* House at the river-mouth. Their mother used to say the house drank from both waters, the river and the sea, and that was why no one in it could ever quite settle — salt water pulling one way, fresh the other. He had thought it a pretty fancy when he was young. Now it felt like a diagnosis.

His father had bought the house with herring money and stubbornness. His mother had kept it alive with borax and hymns. Idris had filled it with music that leaked under doors and drove their father to shout and their mother to pretend she wasn't crying with pleasure. And Emrys — Emrys had filled it with silence, thirty years of it, so thorough that when he walked in now the silence rose up to meet him like a dog that knows its master.

*Dear Emrys, you were never the reason.*

The letter pressed against his ribs.

"Mr. Ashgrove?" Dewi said.

Emrys realised the pen was uncapped. He didn't remember uncapping it. His thumb had a faint blue-grey smudge on it, ink bleeding where the clip met the barrel, and there was something obscene about that — the sale marking him before he'd even agreed to it.

He looked at Ifor.

Ifor was looking at the window, at the rain, but Emrys had the sudden, unshakeable sense that the man had been watching him the whole time, the way fishermen watch weather — not staring, just tracking, reading signs others miss.

"Ifor," Emrys said, and his voice came out wrong, too high, and he tried again. "Ifor."

The old man turned. "Aye."

Up close his eyes were pale, almost colourless, the blue boiled out of them by decades of glare off the water. There was a small white crescent of scar above his left eyebrow that Emrys did not remember from the summer that mattered, though surely it had been there; everything about Ifor had been there, all those years ago, hauling nets, mending pots, laughing at Idris's songs on the quay while the younger men pretended not to be moved.

"I want to ask you for something," Emrys said. "And I have no right to ask it."

Dewi made a small motion, halfway to intervening, and thought better of it. This was not, strictly speaking, a matter for conveyancing.

"The week," Emrys said. "Give me one week. Don't complete today. Let me stay in the house seven more days, and then sign whatever you want, sell it, burn it, I don't care. One week."

The rain thickened against the glass. Somewhere below, the phone shop played its tinny jingle to the street.

Ifor did not answer at once. He put his cap back on his head, which Emrys understood, obscurely, to be a form of settling in — a man putting on his working clothes for difficult work.

"And what will you do with your week?" Ifor asked. His Welsh was the old Welsh, the harbour Welsh, vowels worn smooth as pebbles. Emrys hadn't heard it spoken to him directly in years, and it went through him like cold water through a wetsuit, finding every gap.

"I'm going to say some things," Emrys said. "Out loud. In that house. Things that should have been said thirty years ago."

"To whom?"

"To Idris." He swallowed. "To my parents, maybe. To whoever's listening. To myself, if it comes to that."

Ifor studied him for a long moment. Then he said, in English, deliberately, the way a man switches languages to make a thing official: "You found them, then. Whatever it was you found."

It wasn't a question. Emrys felt the floor tilt slightly.

"How do you know I found anything?"

"Because you've got the look." Ifor tapped his own chest, once, with two blunt fingers. "My brother had that look for years after. Carrying papers he couldn't show anyone. You've been walking around this village a fortnight like a man with a stone in his boot who can't decide whether to take his shoe off." He paused. "I've paid good money, these last years, keeping that house unsold. Waiting for someone to find what I couldn't find. And now you sit there with ink on your thumb and a letter-shaped lump in your jacket, and you ask me for a week."

Emrys's hand went involuntarily to his breast pocket. Ifor saw the gesture and something moved behind his eyes — not triumph, nothing so clean as that. More like hunger meeting food it has waited too long for, so long that the appetite has curdled into something else.

"What did he leave?" Ifor asked quietly. "What's in the house, Emrys?"

And here was the moment, Emrys thought later, where he could have lied, or deflected, or deployed the smile — the smile Nia called *the smile that ends conversations*, the one he'd polished over three decades of dinner parties and editors and strangers asking after his accent. He felt it begin at the corners of his mouth, the old reflex, the drawbridge starting to rise.

He let it fall.

"Letters," he said. "Unsent. Written the week he died. Addressed to me." He drew a breath that hurt. "One of them is addressed to whoever else might come looking. I haven't opened that one yet. I was saving it. I don't know why. Perhaps because I was afraid of what it says. Perhaps because—" He stopped, started again. "Perhaps because I wanted one more thing in that house that wasn't finished. Something still waiting. Because when everything's finished, the house is only a house, and then it goes, and then—"

"Then it's done," Ifor said.

"Then it's done."

The two men looked at each other across the fan of documents, across forty years of parallel silences — Emrys silent in London, Ifor silent in the village, each believing himself the sole keeper of the drowned summer, neither knowing the other had been circling the same wreck all along. Three of them, really, counting Carys. Three people orbiting one absence, like moons that never compared their skies.

Dewi cleared his throat. "If the parties wished to defer completion," he said carefully, "we could execute a simple postponement agreement. Seven days. Both parties' solicitors would need to—"

"No solicitor," Ifor said. "Paper's had enough of my money." He turned to Emrys. "You'll give me your word. One week, and then you sign, no games, no second delay. My word's cost me enough in this life. I'll not spend yours cheaply, but I'll have it."

"You have it," Emrys said. "One week. And Ifor — the letter, the one addressed to whoever came looking. When I open it, I'll tell you what's in it. Same day. Whatever it says."

Ifor nodded slowly, twice, the way men accept terms at sea, where nodding more than twice invites argument with the weather.

Then he did something Emrys did not expect. He reached across the desk and closed Emrys's hand around the pen, folding the fingers over it, and held them shut for a moment with his great scarred fist.

"Not yet," he said. "Seven days. Then you sign it like you mean it, boy. No hovering. Your brother never hovered. He decided things and lived in them, right or wrong." His grip tightened, briefly, painfully. "Learn from him, even now. Especially now."

He let go, took up his cap — no, he was wearing the cap; he took up instead a folded oilskin packet from beside his chair, which Emrys had taken for a lunch — and stood. At the door he stopped, his back to the room, rain-light silvering the shoulders of his coat.

"He sang at my wedding, you know," Ifor said, without turning round. "Idris. Nobody asked him. He just stood up between the pudding and the speeches and sang, and half the chapel cried, and my wife said afterwards it was the only true thing said all day." A pause. The rain filled it. "That's what's in the house, Emrys. Not furniture. That kind of thing. See you don't let the new people sand it out of the walls."

Then he was gone, down the stairs, heavy boots on thin carpet, and the street door banged, and through the window Emrys watched him walk away into the rain without hurrying, a man who had just given away the only thing he'd been hoarding, and who was discovering, perhaps, how light a man can feel with empty hands.

* * *

Dewi drew up the postponement in twenty minutes, typing with two fingers and total serenity, and Emrys signed it standing up because he couldn't bear to sit still. Outside, the rain had settled in for the afternoon, the fine persistent drizzle the English call weather and the Welsh call *glaw mân*, little rain, though Emrys noticed, with a small shock, that the word had arrived in his mind whole and warm, unbidden, like a bird landing on a shoulder.

He walked. He didn't go back to the house — not yet. He walked down through the town and out along the front, past the chippy and the shuttered amusement arcade, past the war memorial with its list of names softened by lichen, and out onto the promenade wall where the sea worked at the stones below with its patient, indifferent industry.

One week.

Seven days. He counted them like coins. Tomorrow he would go to Carys — no, today, this evening; there was no time left for the decent intervals. He would take her the unopened letter, or he would open it with her, or he would ask her to open it, because some things needed more than one pair of hands, the way a heavy boat needs two to launch. Saturday he would start saying the words aloud, the ones he'd been drafting in his head like a man rehearsing a speech for a court that would never convene. Sunday — Sunday he would read Idris's letters in every room, as he'd somehow always known he must, letting the voice fail and recover, fail and recover, until the house had heard everything.

And on the seventh day he would drive to Pwllheli and sign, and mean it.

The rain ran down his face and he didn't wipe it away. In his inside pocket, the letter against his chest seemed to warm, or perhaps that was only his own heat reflected back. *Dear Emrys, you were never the reason.* He had read those words perhaps two hundred times now, and they still landed like the first time, still knocked the wind out of him — absolution arriving thirty years late, from a dead boy, in handwriting that slanted leftward as if leaning into wind.

But there was the other letter. The one addressed not to him. *To whoever finds this,* Idris had written on the envelope, in the same leftward-slanting hand, and beneath it, smaller: *and if it's Emrys, you'll know why I wrote it anyway.*

He had carried it for two weeks without opening it. He told himself he was being careful. He told himself it deserved ceremony. But the truth, which surfaced now as he stood at the promenade wall watching the grey water fold over itself, was simpler and worse: as long as that envelope stayed sealed, the house still held a secret, and as long as the house held a secret, it was still, in some sense, Idris's house — still occupied, still defended, still refusing to become merely property. Opening it was the true sale. Everything else was paperwork.

Tomorrow, then. Tonight. With Carys, if she would come; alone, if she wouldn't.

He turned back toward the town, and that was when he saw her — Carys Vaughan, standing outside the post office across the road under a green awning, holding a parcel she had plainly stopped carrying anywhere, watching him with an expression he had last seen on her face the summer they were nineteen: the look of someone counting waves, waiting for the one she'd have to stand up in.

She crossed the road without checking the traffic, the way she always had, as if the world owed her safe passage.

"One week," she said, when she reached him. Rain beaded on her grey hair. She was not smiling. "Bronwen rang me. She rings me about everything, that girl, she always has." Her eyes searched his face. "So it's time, then. What you have to say."

"It's time."

"Then come to the chapel gate at six," Carys said. "Not the house. The gate. There's a reason, and you'll understand it when we're standing there." She hesitated, and for a moment the postmistress fell away and Emrys saw the girl from the drowned summer, fierce and frightened underneath. "Bring the letter, Emrys. The one you haven't opened. Bring it unopened. Some envelopes want witnesses."

She turned and walked away into the rain, and Emrys stood at the wall with the sea at his back and six hours between him and the gate, and the letter against his heart ticking like something alive.
