## 19. Out Loud, Before Witnesses

The tide was going out when they gathered, and the light came up the way it does on that coast — not arriving so much as being remembered, grey becoming silver becoming a thin gold laid along the water like a seam of ore. Emrys had not slept. He had spent the night in the empty house reading Idris's letters aloud until his voice gave out around three, and then he had sat in the kitchen with the notebook open on the table, listening to the beams settle above him as though the house were clearing its throat, preparing to speak too.

Now he stood on the shingle below the sea wall, where the stones were black and wet and gave underfoot with that small complaining clatter he had known since childhood. The village behind him was still asleep. Gulls argued over something on the tideline. The air smelled of salt and bladderwrack and the cold iron smell of a receding tide, and beneath all of it, faintly, of woodsmoke from Ifor's chimney.

They came down one by one.

Carys first, of course. She wore her good coat, the dark green one, and she walked down the slipway with the unhurried certainty of a woman who has waited thirty years for an appointment and intends to be punctual for it. Her hair was grey now, cut short, and the wind took at it without effect. She did not look at him until she stood beside him, and then she looked at him fully, steadily, the way she had looked at him across the chapel gate weeks ago — no accusation in it anymore, only attention, which was worse and better at once.

Then Bronwen, who had closed the estate agency for the morning, an act she described on the phone the night before as *closing it properly*, with an emphasis Emrys understood to mean forever, or at least for this. She carried a thermos and four paper cups and set them on the sea wall like a small altar, because even at the end of things someone must think of tea.

Then Nia. She had arrived the evening before, unannounced, standing in the doorway of the house with her bag still on her shoulder and an expression Emrys could not read until she said, "I'm not here to fight. I'm here to listen." She had slept in his parents' old room, the good spare bed, and come down at five without being asked. Now she stood a little apart, arms folded against the cold, watching him with the particular patience of a woman who has translated difficult men before and knows the work cannot be rushed.

Ifor came last, walking slowly along the top of the sea wall from the harbour end, leaning on a stick he didn't strictly need but had earned. He was dressed as if for church, or as if for a funeral, which perhaps amounted to the same thing this morning. His face was unreadable in the flat early light, weathered into grooves that could hold grief or obstinacy or both. He stopped six feet away and said nothing. That was the agreement. They had made it yesterday in the solicitor's office, over the unsigned papers: one week, and this morning, the shore, and whatever Emrys had to say, he would say it out loud, before witnesses, in the language it happened in.

The tide sucked back over the stones. Somewhere behind them a gull dropped a mussel on the rocks and the crack of it went through the silence like a shot.

Emrys's mouth was dry. He had rehearsed this — God knew he had rehearsed it, three days of it, Carys correcting his vowels gently in her kitchen while the kettle screamed itself hoarse, the old hymnal open between them like a shared map of a country they'd both been exiled from. Words had come back to him like objects borne in on the tide: some whole, some broken, some beautiful and some merely functional. *Cael* — to have, to get, to be allowed. *Cuddio* — to hide. *Cyfaddef* — to confess, though the word carried also the sense of acknowledging, of admitting into company, and he had thought more than once that this was the truest word in the language, because that was what confession was: an admission. You admitted the truth the way you admitted a guest. You opened the door to it.

He looked at the four faces. Bronwen's, young and grave. Nia's, steady, her breath making small clouds. Ifor's, carved and waiting. Carys's, open as a gate.

And he began.

---

"Rwy'n mynd i siarad yn Gymraeg," he said, and his voice cracked on the second word like ice on a puddle, and he stopped, and breathed, and started again. "Rwy'n mynd i siarad yn Gymraeg am fod hyn wedi digwydd yma, yn yr iaith hon. Ac wedyn fyddaf i'n ei ddweud mewn Saesneg. Am fod y ddau ohonoch"—he nodded at Nia—"yn haeddu clywed y gwir."

*I am going to speak in Welsh because this happened here, in this language. And then I will say it in English. Because both of you deserve to hear the truth.*

The words came slowly. Not haltingly, exactly — he had done the work — but with the deliberate weight of stones being placed one upon another, each one tested before it bore the next. His accent was rusted. He could hear the English underneath it, the forty years of London sitting in his mouth like silt. But it was Welsh. It was the language Idris had sung in, the language their mother had scolded in, the language of the boat and the harbour wall and the summer everything broke.

"Hafan haf 1974," he said. "Roedd Idris yn ysgrifennu caneuon. Roedd Carys yn gwrando. A fi—"

He stopped. The tide filled the pause.

*A fi.* And me. There it was, the sentence he had been circling for thirty years, the sentence every other sentence had been built to avoid. He looked at Carys and she gave him the smallest nod, permission or courage or both.

"A fi," he said again, "roeddwn i'n cyfieithu. Cyfieithais i bethau nad oedd neb wedi gofyn i mi eu cyfieithu. Dyna'r broblem. Dyna bob tro'r broblem."

*I translated. I translated things no one asked me to translate. That was the problem. That was always the problem.*

He told it then, all of it, in the order he had finally learned it rather than the order he had lived it, because the lived order was a lie of sequence and the learned order was the truth. He told how Idris and Carys had planned to leave — the van, the money saved in a tobacco tin, the ferry, the songs Idris believed would feed them in some city where nobody knew their fathers. He told how he had found out, and how he had told their parents, and how he had told himself at the time — he made himself say this part, made himself stand in the cold and say it — that he was protecting Idris. Saving him from himself. Keeping him from throwing away school and home and the family for a girl and a guitar and a dream with no floor to it.

"Ond dyna ddim y gwir," he said, and his voice shook now, and he let it shake. "Y gwir oedd bod Idris yn mynd i fy ngadael i hefyd. Yn mynd i adael y tŷ. Yn mynd i adael fi ar fy mhen fy hun gyda'r rhieni hynny, gyda'r distawrwydd hwnnw, gyda'r bywyd bach hwnnw. Roeddwn i wedi fy nhyfu arno fel planhigyn ar waliau tŷ. Ac pan glywais i am y cynllun, nid ofn am Idris oedd yn fy nghyffwrdd i. Ofn am fy hun."

*But that wasn't the truth. The truth was that Idris was going to leave me too. Leave the house. Leave me alone with those parents, with that silence, with that small life. I had grown up on him like ivy on the walls of a house. And when I heard about the plan, what touched me wasn't fear for Idris. It was fear for myself.*

Nia made a sound — not a word, just a small exhalation, the sound of a woman hearing a thing she had suspected for years and hearing it confirmed is its own kind of wound. Emrys did not look at her. He couldn't, not yet. He kept his eyes on the water, on the line where the sea met the sky and neither would give ground.

"Dywedais i wrth y rhieni," he said. "Nos Wener. Cyn i Idris a Carys fynd. Dywedais i wrth Mam ei bod hi'n gallu stopio fo. Ac roedd Mam yn gallu stopio fo. Roedd Mam yn gallu stopio unrhyw beth."

*I told the parents. Friday night. Before Idris and Carys could go. I told Mam she could stop him. And Mam could stop him. Mam could stop anything.*

He told how the house had turned to stone around Idris that weekend — the locked drawer, the confiscated tin, the father's silence heavier than shouting, the mother's tears deployed like weather. How Idris had not raged. That was the thing Emrys needed them to understand, needed the sea itself to witness: Idris had not raged. He had gone quiet, quieter than the house, and he had smiled at Emrys across the Sunday table with a smile Emrys had spent three decades refusing to interpret, and on Monday night he had taken the boat out.

"Ni oedd damwain yn unig," Emrys said. "Doedd e ddim eisiau boddi. Doedd e ddim eisiau marw. Ond doedd e ddim eisiau dod yn ôl i'r tŷ hwnnw chwaith. Roedd e eisiau iddyn nhw — ni — deall fod y bywyd hwnnw'n ei ladd e mor dda â'r môr. Felly cymerodd y cwch all i'w hysbrydoni nhw. I wneud iddyn nhw fethu ag anadl am funud. Ac yna daeth y storm yn real."

*It wasn't only an accident. He didn't want to drown. He didn't want to die. But he didn't want to come back to that house either. He wanted them — us — to understand that that life was killing him as surely as the sea could. So he took the boat out to frighten them. To make them lose their breath for a minute. And then the storm became real.*

Ifor shifted. Emrys heard the stick move on the shingle and knew what was coming and made himself turn to face it.

"The bailer," he said in English, deliberately, letting the word sit there in the cold air between them, plain and terrible. "You told me, Ifor. In the pub. The bailer was gone from the boat that night. Taken out beforehand." He switched back, the Welsh coming easier now, as if the English had been a rock in the river and now the current ran free again. "Gwyddwn i beth hynny'n golygu. Golyga hynny nad oedd Idris yn mynd allan am awr. Roedd e wedi penderfynu na fyddai'n dod yn ôl os na fyddai'r storm yn ei droi'n real. Roedd y cwch heb bailer yn datganiad. Datganiad a ysgrifennwyd mewn dŵr."

*I know what that means. It means Idris wasn't going out for an hour. He had decided he wouldn't come back unless the storm turned real. A boat without a bailer is a statement. A statement written in water.*

Ifor's jaw worked. For a long moment he said nothing, and then he said, in Welsh, his voice rough as netting: "Dywedais i ddim byd am hynny am ddegawdau. Dywedais i ddim byd wrth neb ond ti, wythnos diwethaf, am fod dy frawd yn haeddu cael ei gofio'n gywir." He looked out at the water. "Mae gen i frawd fy hun sy'n colli popeth ar ôl y noson honno. Felly dwi wedi bod yn dawel. Ond dawel ddim yn gywir."

*I said nothing about that for decades. I've said nothing to anyone but you, last week, because your brother deserved to be remembered rightly. I have a brother of my own who lost everything after that night. So I have been silent. But silent isn't the same as right.*

Emrys nodded. "Diolch," he said. Thank you. The smallest word, and the hardest.

Then he turned to Carys, and this was the part he had practised least, because it was the part that could not be practised, only undergone.

"Carys," he said. "Ar y traeth y noson honno. Chi oedd chi yma."

*Carys. On the beach that night. You were here.*

She did not flinch. "Do," she said. Yes. Just that. Giving him the fact the way you hand someone a stone: here, hold it, feel its weight.

"Chi welsoch y cwch," he said. "Chi glywsoch Mam a Dad ar y traeth. Chi wyddoch beth oedd wedi digwydd — nad damwain pur oedd e. A chi dewisoch ddistawrwydd. Dros ddegawdau. Pam?"

*You saw the boat. You heard Mam and Dad on the beach. You knew what had happened — that it wasn't purely an accident. And you chose silence. For decades. Why?*

And Carys Vaughan, postmistress, keeper of the village's letters and its silences, answered him in a voice as level as the horizon.

"Am fod y gwir yn lladd dau," she said. "Os dywedwn i beth roedd Idris wedi'i wneud i'r cwch, byddai pawb yn meddwl ei fod e wedi bwriadu marw. Byddai Mam a Dad ti wedi carrio hynny. Byddai ti —" she paused, and her eyes did not soften so much as sharpen with kindness, which is a different thing — "byddai ti wedi carrio hynny ar ben dy zwaen dy hun. Roedd dy zwaen ti'n ddigon trwm yn barod."

*Because the truth kills two. If I'd said what Idris had done to the boat, everyone would have believed he meant to die. Your mother and father would have carried that. You — you would have carried that on top of your own guilt. Your guilt was heavy enough already.*

"Ac roedd gen i addewid," she added quietly. "Addewid dan y dŵr."

*And I had a promise. A promise under the water.*

Emrys knew the verse now. Had deciphered it from the water-blurred page with his heart hammering like surf: the second verse addressed to the one who promised to keep the secret *under the water*, where the drowned keep what the living cannot carry. Idris had written his own confession and entrusted it to the sea, and Carys had been the sea's witness, holding it for thirty years without once letting it surface.

"Gwnaethoch chi ddim byd ond cadw addewid," Emrys said, and his voice failed on the last word and he let it fail, let the silence hold it, because some sentences are finished by what you cannot say. *You did nothing but keep a promise.*

Then he turned, at last, to Nia.

---

He spoke to her in Welsh first, because the order mattered — truth first in the tongue it grew in, then carried across — and because he wanted her to hear the music of it, the way the language rose and fell like the tide it lived beside, before he gave her the plain freight of the English.

"Nia," he said. "Wythnos ar hugain yn ôl, dywedais i wrthych chi fy mod i'n mynd i drin tŷ mam a dad. Celwydd. Roeddwn i wedi clywed cân. Cân Idris. Ar y radio. Caniad nad oes record ohono, gan mai dim ond dau berson yn y byd oedd yn ei adnabod, ac un ohonyn nhw wedi bod yn farw ers tri deg blwydd."

*Nia. Twenty-eight days ago I told you I was going to settle my parents' house. A lie. I had heard a song. Idris's song. On the radio. A song with no recording, because only two people in the world knew it, and one of them has been dead for thirty years.*

He watched her take that — the arithmetic of it, the impossibility, the fact she had already half-believed it from the nights he'd spoken Welsh in his sleep, words rising out of him like bubbles from a drowned place.

"Ac wedyn," he went on, "gwnaethoch chi enwi'r patrwm. Yn y ffôn. 'Rydych chi'n cyfieithu er mwyn peidio byth â siarad.' Doedd gen i ddim ateb y pryd hynny. Mae gen i un nawr."

*And then you named the pattern. On the phone. "You translate so you never have to speak." I had no answer then. I have one now.*

He drew a breath that seemed to come from below the waterline of himself, from some deep cold stratum where the boy he had been still stood on this same shingle with his hands in his pockets, translating everyone's feelings because he could not voice his own.

"Fi oedd y brifdrwg," he said. *I was the chief culprit.* "Dim ond hynny. Fi oedd y babell. Fi oedd y llais yn y drws. Tri deg blwydd rydw i wedi byw gyda hynny heb ei ddweud, ac mae'r distawrwydd wedi bwyta fi fel y môr yn bwyta'r clogwyni. Powdr i bowdr. Ac rydw i wedi meddwl am flynyddoedd fod y distawrwydd yn amddiffyniad. Nage. Roedd y distawrwydd yn ymosodiad. Roeddwn i'n ymosod ar fy hun, bob dydd, yn y ffordd fwya tawel bosibl."

*That's all. I was the bell. I was the voice at the door. For thirty years I have lived with that without saying it, and the silence has eaten me the way the sea eats the cliffs. Grain by grain. And I have thought for years that the silence was protection. No. The silence was assault. I attacked myself, every day, in the quietest way possible.*

The tide had turned; he felt it rather than saw it, the pull changing under the stones, the whole grey body of the sea beginning its long return. The light was stronger now, laying a path of hammered gold from the horizon almost to their feet, as if the water were offering them a road.

He looked at each of them once more. Bronwen, who had closed her office. Ifor, who had bought a week with mercy. Nia, who had crossed a country to watch a man do the one thing he feared more than drowning. Carys, who had kept a promise to a dead boy for longer than most marriages last.

And then he said the last of it, the sentence the whole speech had been built to reach, and he said it in Welsh and then, immediately, in English, himself, deliberately, because translation was his life's work and he would not let anyone else — not time, not memory, not silence — do it for him anymore.

"Felly heddiw, ar y traeth hwn, rwy'n cyfaddef," he said. "Cyfaddef yn gyhoeddus, o flaen tystion, fod fi wedi bradychu Idris. Fod fi wedi bradychu Carys. Fod fi wedi bradychu fy hun am ddegawdau. Ac rwy'n gofyn am ddim. Dim maddeuant. Dim cosb. Dim byd. Rwy'n siarad er mwyn siarad. Er mwyn bod y gwir wedi'i ddweud allan, yn yr awyr agored, yn yr iaith y digwyddodd y cyfan ynddi — ac wedyn yn yr iaith rwy'n byw ynddi nawr. Dwywaith. Allan. Yn y golau."

*So today, on this beach, I confess. Publicly, before witnesses, that I betrayed Idris. That I betrayed Carys. That I betrayed myself for decades. And I ask for nothing. No forgiveness. No punishment. Nothing. I speak for the sake of speaking. So that the truth has been said out loud, in the open air, in the language it all happened in — and then in the language I live in now. Twice. Out loud. In the light.*

And then, in English, his voice steadier than it had any right to be:

"I betrayed my brother. I told our parents of his plan to leave, not to save him but because I could not bear to be left. He went out in a boat without a bailer to make us understand what that house was doing to him, and the storm made it death, and I have spent thirty years calling it an accident because the truth was a weight I refused to lift. Carys kept the secret to protect me, and I let her, without ever knowing I was letting her. I hid inside another language. I hid inside other people's stories. I hid so well I nearly disappeared. This morning I stop hiding. That is all. That is everything."

The words went out over the water and did not come back.

For a long moment nobody moved. The gulls wheeled. The tide crept up the shingle with its small industrious whisper, taking back the stones one by one, rearranging the evidence of their standing there. Then Nia crossed the wet rocks and took his hand, and did not squeeze it or speak, just held it, warm against his cold one, and that was enough, that was more than enough.

Bronwen wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand and pretended she hadn't. Ifor leaned harder on his stick and looked out at the water for a long time, and when he finally spoke it was in Welsh, and his voice was not angry, which frightened Emrys more than anger would have.

"Wyt ti'n gwybod," Ifor said slowly, "beth fydd hyn yn ei olygu i'r pentref?"

*Do you know what this will mean to the village?*

Emrys shook his head honestly. He did not know. He knew only that the truth, once spoken, does not stay where you put it; it moves through a community the way the tide moves through a harbour, finding every channel, filling every hollow, lifting some boats and stranding others.

"Bydd rhai yn dweud bod Idris wedi'i ladd ei hun," Ifor said. *Some will say Idris killed himself.* "Bydd rhai yn dweud bod y teulu wedi'i ladd e. Bydd rhai —" and here his voice caught, and he mastered it the way men of his generation master everything, brutally and in private — "bydd rhai yn cofio fy mrawd i, a'r fferm a gollodd e, a'r blynyddoedd y cwestiynnwyd amdano ar ôl y noson honno. Mae'r gwir fel y môr, Emrys. Mae'n codi popeth sydd wedi bod ar y gwaelod. Ac nid pob peth ar y gwaelod yw dy garreg di."

*Some will remember my own brother, and the farm he lost, and the years he was questioned after that night. The truth is like the sea, Emrys. It lifts everything that has lain on the bottom. And not everything on the bottom is your stone.*

"I know," Emrys said. "I know it isn't."

But even as he said it, he saw it — saw the ripple already moving outward from this beach toward the houses waking above them, toward the chapel and the shop and the memorial stone on the headland with his brother's name spelled wrong on it these thirty years, toward every family in the village that had its own version of that summer sealed under its own floorboards. The confession was not finished. It had only begun to travel.

Carys stepped forward then and stood beside him, facing the water, shoulder almost touching his, the way they might have stood at nineteen if the world had been otherwise.

"Nawr," she said quietly. *Now.* "Mae'r gwir wedi'i ddweud. Nawr mae'n perthyn i'r pentref."

*The truth has been spoken. Now it belongs to the village.*

And far up the lane, where the houses began, a door opened — Emrys heard it, a small sound carrying on the morning air — and a figure came out and stood looking down at the four of them on the shore, and did not move, and did not go back inside.
