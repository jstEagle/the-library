## 21. Keys on the Kitchen Table

The morning of the handover came in grey and silver, the way the village liked to dress itself for occasions it didn't want to make a fuss about. Emrys woke in his childhood bed — he had stayed the night again, though the week Ifor granted him was nearly spent — and lay listening to the house perform its small orchestra of settling beams and ticking pipes. Forty years of held breath, and now, at last, the exhale.

He had expected to sleep badly. Instead he had slept like a stone dropped into deep water, down past the light, past the cold layers, to some floor where nothing moved. It occurred to him, lying there with the salt-smell of the room in his nostrils, that this was what forgiveness did. Not the kind you asked for. The kind you found in a biscuit tin under a floorboard, written in a dead boy's careful hand — *Dear Emrys, you were never the reason* — and carried around for days until its weight redistributed itself through your body and became something you could stand upright with.

He got up. He made tea in the old brown pot because the kettle in this house had never been trusted with anything so modern as speed. He drank it standing at the kitchen window, watching the harbour do its slow grey breathing, and he let himself think, deliberately, of everything he was about to leave behind in these rooms. That was the discipline of it. You didn't slip out of a life; you walked out through every room and looked at what was in each one.

* * *

By nine he had the kitchen table cleared. This was the work he'd given himself the night before, and it took longer than he'd thought, because clearing a table in this house meant deciding, object by object, what forty years of silence had accumulated.

His mother's biscuit tin went into a box for the charity shop — empty now, its secret long since promoted to daylight. His father's tide tables, annotated in pencil with weather that had never arrived. The chipped blue jug that had held wildflowers every summer of Emrys's childhood and had never once, in his memory, held water. He wrapped each thing in newspaper Bronwen had brought from the post office, and the paper crackled with a sound so ordinary it was almost obscene, the sound of a household being made portable.

But the notebook he handled differently.

Idris's songbook sat on the table where Emrys had left it after the night of reading aloud, when the house had answered him in creaks and settling beams and he had gone from room to room giving Idris's letters back their voices. The cover was swollen with old water, warped into a shallow bowl, as if the sea had tried to drink it and given up halfway. The spine was cracked. The pages inside were soft as cloth, some of them fused at the edges where the damp had welded them together, and opening the book required a gentleness Emrys had not known he possessed until he needed it.

He turned to the last finished song. *Cân yr Dŵr Dan.* The water-under song. The one the radio caller had hummed into the small hours of a London night and cracked Emrys's life open like an egg.

It looked different now. For thirty years the song had existed in Emrys's memory as a fragment — eight bars, the wrong chord in the second phrase that Idris had refused to fix because, he said, the gulls sang flat too and nobody told them they were wrong. But here on the page was the whole architecture: the verse Emrys had known, and beneath it, recovered from the blur of water-stain like a face emerging from fog, the second verse. *Under the water I will keep it / under the water where you swore / the secret sleeps but does not drown, my brother / the tide brings back what it took ashore.*

Emrys had copied the words out in his own hand during the week, in Welsh, checking each one against the hymnal and against Carys's patient corrections, and then — this had cost him more than anything — he had written the English beneath, line by line, in his own translation. Not the polished translator's version he would have produced for a publisher. A plain one. The kind you'd say to someone across a kitchen table.

He left the book open to that page. Open, so that whoever came next would find it not hidden but offered. The notebook was the only object in the house he would not pack, would not wrap, would not carry away. It belonged to the house the way the damp belonged to the house, the way the view of the harbour belonged to it. And Ifor — strange, stubborn, grieving Ifor, who had paid year after year to keep strangers out of these rooms while he searched for whatever he believed Idris had left behind — Ifor deserved to find it. Let him sit at this table with his big fisherman's hands and read what his brother's friend had written the week before the water took him. Let him learn that what Idris hid was not treasure or blame but a letter of forgiveness, unsent, addressed to the wrong person by half — or perhaps addressed to exactly the right person, since Ifor's own grievance had never been named in any of it.

There was a justice in that which no courtroom could have arranged.

* * *

Bronwen arrived first, at ten, in her estate agent's coat that she wore like a uniform she'd outgrown. She stood in the doorway of the kitchen and looked at the bare table, the open notebook, the boxes stacked by the door, and something moved across her face that was not professional at all.

"You've done it properly," she said. In Welsh. She'd been speaking Welsh to him all week, patiently, correcting him gently when he reached for a word and found the shelf where it used to be standing empty. *Rydych chi wedi gwneud e'n iawn.* You've done it right.

"I've done it as well as I can," Emrys answered, and heard, with a small private astonishment, that the sentence had come out whole. Untranslated. There had been a time — three weeks ago, a lifetime ago — when he would have built the sentence in English first and ferried it across. Now the Welsh rose in him like groundwater finding an old channel. Some of the words still came up broken. Some came up not at all. But enough came. Enough.

"Are you keeping anything?" Bronwen asked. Her eyes went to the notebook. "Besides—"

"One page." He touched his jacket pocket, where the letter lay folded against his chest. He had taken it out last night and read it once more by lamplight, and then put it back, and understood that he would be putting it back, in one pocket or another, for the rest of his life. "One page only. The rest stays with the house."

"He'd have wanted that," Bronwen said. Then she flushed slightly, as if she'd claimed a knowledge she didn't have. "I mean — I don't know what he'd have wanted. I was six."

"You knew the song," Emrys said. "That's knowing enough of him."

She smiled — the warm, accusing warmth she'd carried since the day he walked back into her office — and busied herself with the inventory clipboard, giving him the gift of pretending there was paperwork to attend to.

* * *

Ifor came at eleven, and he came slowly.

Emrys watched him come up the lane from the kitchen window: a broad, weathered man walking like a man approaching a grave, pausing twice on the short slope, looking once back at the harbour as if to check whether the sea was watching too. When the knock came it was soft, two knuckles, apologetic.

They stood in the kitchen facing each other across the cleared table, and between them lay the open notebook, and both men looked at it before either spoke.

"I read it," Ifor said at last. His voice was rough gravel over something softer. "Last night. Bronwen gave me the key yesterday evening, said you'd left it — said you'd want me to see." He laid one hand flat on the table beside the book, not touching it, the way you'd rest your hand near a sleeping animal. "Thirty years I thought there was money in this house. Or papers. Something of his that proved—" He stopped. Started again. "My brother lost his boat over that summer. Over the questions people asked. I came here for years looking for something that would say it wasn't our name that broke that night."

"And you found a song instead."

"I found a song instead." Ifor's laugh was short and wet. "And a letter forgiving a man who wasn't me. And you know what? It's worth more than money. Don't tell anyone I said that. I've a reputation."

"You have a terrible reputation," Emrys agreed. "Everyone at the harbour wall says so."

"They're right." Ifor straightened. He looked around the kitchen — at the blue jug in its newspaper, at the window, at the brown teapot — and Emrys saw him doing the same arithmetic Emrys had done all morning, room by room, object by object, except that for Ifor every sum came out in the currency of a grief he'd never been allowed to name publicly. "You'll leave the book?"

"Open to that page."

"The second verse." Ifor shook his head slowly. "All these years I hummed the first one without knowing there was more. Like reading half a letter." He looked up. "Like being everyone in this village, basically."

Emrys laughed — actually laughed, out loud, in this kitchen where laughter had been rationed like wartime sugar — and the sound surprised them both.

They signed nothing here; that happened at the solicitor's in the afternoon. But the keys — the heavy iron key for the front door, the brass one for the back, the small new one for the meter box — Emrys had set them on the table beside the notebook, and now he pushed them across the wood toward Ifor with two fingers, a distance of eighteen inches that had taken thirty years and one drowned boy and one found letter to cross.

"Not yet," Ifor said. "After the office. After it's mine proper. Then you hand them to me and I'll know they're real." He paused at the door on his way out, and turned. "You'll come to the memorial stone? Saturday. When the new carving's done. Carys is seeing to the spelling."

"I'll come," Emrys said. "Idris. With the *s*. He always said the extra letter was the sea getting into his name."

"Aye," said Ifor. "Well. The sea gets into everything eventually. That's rather the point of it."

* * *

Carys came at noon, and she came into the kitchen without knocking, because she had been coming into this kitchen since she was seventeen years old and doors had never stood between them that mattered.

She looked thinner than she had at the chapel gate, or perhaps Emrys had simply learned to see her properly since. Her hair was grey now where it had been dark, cut short and practical, postmistress hair. She set a paper bag on the table — bara brith, still warm, from the bakery that had changed hands four times and never changed its recipe — and then she stood with her hands flat on the table's edge, on either side of the open notebook, framing it.

"You're leaving it," she said. Not a question.

"It lives here."

"It lives here," she repeated, and closed her eyes briefly. When she opened them they were bright and steady. "Do you know what I did with my half of the secret, all those years? I kept it the way you keep a coal alive under ash. Fed it a little every day. Just enough. Because if it died entirely, then Idris died entirely, and if it burned openly, then—" She stopped. "Then you burned. And I couldn't bear that either. Nineteen years old and carrying two people in my hands, and I chose wrong, or I chose right, or there was no choosing that wasn't wrong. I've had fifty years to decide and I still haven't."

"You chose to protect us both," Emrys said. "It's what he'd have asked."

"It's what he *did* ask." Her voice caught on it. "In the song. Under the water, where you swore. He wrote his own secrecy into music so it would outlive all of us, and it did, and it dragged you home across three hundred miles by the ear. Songs are worse than ghosts. Ghosts stay where they're put."

They cut the bara brith and ate it standing, butter melting into the warm slices, and talked of small things — the memorial stone, Nia arriving tomorrow for the service, the way the swallows had come back late this year — and the smallness of it was the point. They had spent their large words already, on the shore at dawn, in front of witnesses, in two languages. What remained was bread and butter and the pleasure of speaking plainly in a kitchen where plainness had been contraband for half a century.

At the door, leaving, Carys turned back.

"What will you do with the page?" she asked. "The one you kept."

Emrys put his hand over his pocket. "Read it," he said. "For the rest of my life. Until I know it by heart better than I know the song. And then read it again."

"Good," said Carys. "That's the correct answer. There'll be a test later."

* * *

Late afternoon, alone again, Emrys walked the house one final time.

Not searching now — that work was done, the letters found, the floorboard replaced, the secret given its daylight. This was the other walk, the slower one, the one he'd promised himself at the window that morning. Through every room, looking at what was in each one.

His parents' bedroom, stripped to its boards, holding nothing now but light and the smell of lavender gone ghostly. The parlour where the radio had played that late-night programme three hundred miles away and one lifetime ago. The stairs whose fourth step had always announced visitors. And last, Idris's room, small and north-facing, where the floorboard gap was now just a floorboard gap, and the window looked out on the lane and the harbour wall and the grey, patient, forgiving, unforgiving sea.

Emrys stood at that window a long time.

He thought about how a house holds a family the way the tide holds what it takes: not maliciously, not kindly, just thoroughly, turning everything over and over in the dark until the sharp edges come off and what washes back up is smooth enough to hold in your hand. He had spent thirty years trying to leave this place behind and had succeeded completely, the way you succeed at holding your breath. The breath had been waiting the whole time. The language had been waiting. The truth had been waiting, under the water, where the song said it slept but did not drown.

He took out the letter one more time. He didn't need to unfold it fully; he knew the opening lines the way he now knew the second verse, the way he knew the shape of the wrong chord. *Dear Emrys, you were never the reason.* Eight words. His brother had spent his last living week writing those eight words and had died before posting them, and the paper had waited under the floor for fifty years to do its work, patient as water.

He folded it away. He touched his pocket once, lightly, the way you'd touch a wound to confirm it's healing.

Downstairs, the kitchen was dimming into evening. The notebook lay open on the bare table, its warped pages drinking the last of the light, the second verse visible, legible, finished. Beside it, waiting for tomorrow's ceremony at the solicitor's office, the keys made a small iron-and-brass constellation on the wood.

Emrys stood in the doorway and looked at them — the notebook and the keys, the song and the surrender, side by side on the scrubbed pine — and understood that this was the true signing, here, tonight, and the solicitor's office tomorrow was only the receipt.

"Noson da," he said aloud to the empty house. Good night. His voice sounded strange and full in the bare rooms, and the house answered, as it had begun to answer all week, in the creak of a beam and the tick of cooling pipes — not words, never words, but attention. Witness. A held breath finally, gently, let go.

Tomorrow he would sign. Tomorrow Ifor would take the keys and the house would pass to a man who had circled the same silence for decades and would now live inside its answer. Tomorrow Nia would arrive, and Saturday they would stand at the memorial stone with its corrected spelling, and the village would watch a name come right after fifty years of being wrong.

But tonight — tonight Emrys climbed the stairs, avoiding the fourth step out of habit and then, deliberately, setting his foot square upon it, letting it announce him to no one, to everyone, to the house itself.

Outside, the tide was coming in. It sounded, as it always had, like pages turning.
