## 15. Efua's Version

Efua's flat was above a shuttered bakery on Coriander Street, and the smell of forty years of bread had soaked so deeply into the walls that even now, with the ovens cold, the stairwell exhaled something warm and faintly sweet, like a house remembering. She opened the door before I knocked the second time.

"You called twice," she said. "I don't answer twice."

"I know. I'm sorry."

"Come in before the neighbors see you. They already think I talk to myself."

The room was small and fiercely ordered—books squared to the edges of shelves, a single chair angled toward the window as if someone had recently been asked to sit in it and had refused. On the table between two cups of tea she had already poured lay a cassette recorder, older than mine, its plastic gone amber with age. Beside it, a notebook. Not her mother's grammar notes—I knew those by heart now, the careful loops, the marginal diagrams of verb-stems branching like river deltas. This was a child's notebook, the cover soft with handling, the handwriting inside large and round and patient, the hand of a girl who had been taught one thing at a time and told never to forget it.

"You said you had a fragment," I said.

"I said I had a lullaby." She sat down across from me and did not touch her tea. "There's a difference. A fragment is dead. You find it in a drawer and you label it. A lullaby is alive. It has a job. It was sung to me every night for six years by a woman who was not my mother, in a language my mother pretended not to speak, and I didn't learn it—I *absorbed* it, the way you absorb damp. And then my mother took me away from that woman and we came here, and for thirty years I have hummed it to myself in the dark without knowing what I was humming." She turned the notebook toward me. "Until your letters started arriving at the hospice, and she started asking about me, and I remembered that words have meanings, and meanings can be found out."

I looked down at the page. The sentence was there, written phonetically in Efua's own system, syllable by syllable, with stress marks and a line under the vowel she wasn't sure of. And beneath it, in English, a translation. My chest went tight before I finished reading the first word, because I knew the shape of it. I had heard it three nights ago in the half-dark of Amara's room, when she was somewhere between sleep and waking and mistook me for a woman thirty years dead, and she had spoken it aloud—slowly, tenderly, terribly—and I had written it down afterward in the corridor with shaking hands, sound without meaning, a shell with nothing inside it.

It was the same sentence.

Not approximately. Not a variant. The same syllables, the same stresses, the same falling cadence at the end that Amara's voice had made even half-lucid, even dreaming. Efua had carried it since childhood, worn smooth as a stone in a pocket, and she had never known what she was carrying.

"And your translation," I said. My voice came out wrong, flattened.

"It took me four months and two linguists at the university who kept trying to give me papers instead of answers." She tapped the page. "Read it."

I read it. The English sat there in her round, patient handwriting:

*What you did, I carry it for you, so your hands stay clean.*

"That's not what she told me," I said.

"No," Efua said. "I imagine it isn't."

* * *

Here is what I had, and I laid it all out on her table because I no longer trusted my own head to hold it: my notebook, open to the page where I had transcribed Amara's midnight version phonetically, and beneath it the meaning she had given me two weeks earlier, in daylight, in her clear teaching voice, the voice she used for salt and thread and the names of colors.

*What I did, I confess it: I bought our lives with other lives.*

Two sentences. Identical sounds. Opposite meanings.

"They can't both be right," I said.

Efua laughed, and it was not a kind laugh—it was the laugh of a woman who had spent decades being lied to elegantly. "Of course they can't. One of us is wrong. Or—" she leaned forward, and the lamplight caught the gray at her temples, "—one of us was lied to. Or both. Or neither, which is worse."

"Explain that last one."

"The language my mother speaks—the language she is dying in—does it have a neutral way to say things? Think before you answer. You've sat in that room longer than I ever did."

I thought of the verbs that changed depending on what you had done to endure. I thought of how Amara had once corrected me eleven times over a single word for *left*, insisting that leaving a place and abandoning a place were different verbs, and that confusing them was not an error but an accusation. I thought of the sentence itself, how it had felt in my mouth when I'd practiced it alone in my kitchen—how it seemed to tilt depending on where I put the weight, like a plank over water.

"No," I said. "There's no neutral form. That's why she calls it untranslatable. It doesn't mean one thing. It means whichever thing the speaker chooses."

Efua sat back. "Then she hasn't taught you a sentence. She's taught you a weapon with two handles. And she's handed each of us one end."

* * *

She made more tea while I tried to make the arithmetic work. It wouldn't. If the sentence meant confession—if Amara had built her whole final project around forcing a stranger to carry her guilt past the edge of her own death—then the lullaby was a lie, or a mask, or an earlier draft of herself that she had abandoned. But if the lullaby was true—if the sentence was absolution, a promise to carry another's burden—then everything Amara had told me in that dying room was a fabrication, and I had spent months learning the grammar of a lie, syllable by devoted syllable.

Unless.

"Who sang it to you?" I asked. "You said a woman who wasn't your mother."

Efua's hands went still around her cup. For a long moment the only sound was the radiator ticking, and downstairs a dog barking at nothing, and the building settling into its bread-scented memory.

"A neighbor," she said finally. "In the village. Before." She said *before* the way people say a name they've sworn off. "My mother worked. There was a woman who kept me in the evenings, and she sang. My mother forbade the language in our house—said it would get us killed, said some things survive better untranslated." She stopped. Looked up sharply. "Why are you making that face?"

Because my mother had said those exact words to me, in that exact cadence, standing in a kitchen in a city a thousand miles from any village, refusing to teach me a tongue I hadn't known she had. Some things survive better untranslated. I had carried the sentence for twenty years as evidence of her silence. Now it turned out she might have been quoting.

"What was the woman's name?" I asked.

Efua told me.

And the floor of the world tilted, quietly, the way a boat tilts when you realize the water has been coming in for some time.

* * *

I need to set down what happened next carefully, because memory has begun doing to me what translation does—choosing, arranging, deciding what survives. But certain facts held their shape.

Efua's keeper, the singer of the lullaby, the woman who had pressed absolution into a child's ear night after night until it became part of her breathing—that woman had lived, for two years, at 14 Marrow Lane.

My postcards' address. The address that burned. The address I had written to weekly for nine years, filing each unsent card into an inventory of everything unsaid, believing I was mailing letters to a ruin, to an absence, to the sealed vault of my mother's refusals.

"She moved away before the fire," Efua said, watching me. "Your mother. I've done the arithmetic too, letter-writer. Don't think I haven't. Two women, one village, one war, one language between them—and then both of them end up in the same city, and one of them keeps my mother's secrets and the other one raises you on silence, and neither of them ever says the other's name." She pushed the notebook toward me again. "And now my mother is dying and she hires a professional stranger to learn her sentence, and that stranger turns out to be the son of the only other person alive who knew it. You don't believe in coincidence. Neither do I. So tell me—who arranged this? Her, or fate, or you?"

"I answered a referral," I said. "A hospital slip. I didn't know her name until I walked in."

"But she knew yours." Efua said it gently, which was worse. "Think. A dying woman who has spent fifty years making sure no one connects her to that village, who forbade her own daughter four words of comfort—and the first thing she asks a stranger for is not a letter but *lessons*. She chose you. The question is whether she chose you because of who your mother was, or despite it. And whether the sentence she gave you is a confession or a gift depends entirely on which."

I looked down at the two versions lying side by side on the table, hers and mine, mirror images in a language only three people living could read—and one of them was upstairs in a hospice bed right now, breathing shallowly, keeping whichever truth she preferred behind her teeth.

"There's a third possibility," I said slowly. "That she gave me the confession because she thought I deserved the truth. And sang the lullaby to you because you deserved mercy. Same sentence. Different audiences. Translation isn't fidelity—it's choice. Maybe she's been choosing all along, for everyone, for fifty years. Deciding who gets which version."

"Then she decided my whole childhood for me," Efua said, and her voice cracked along an old fault line. "She decided I would be a woman humming nonsense in the dark. She decided I would believe she had no tenderness in her. She let me hate her rather than let me carry one true thing." She stood abruptly, went to the window, stood with her back to me. When she spoke again her voice was level, and that levelness cost her visibly. "Tomorrow you're going back to that room. I can see it in your face. You're going to sit by her bed and say nothing, the way you always do, the way your inventory trained you. So I'll say it for both of us: ask her. Ask her which version is true. Make her choose in front of a witness. Because if she dies with both versions still standing, then neither of us will ever know whether we inherited a sin or a blessing, and I will not do that. I will not spend the rest of my life humming."

* * *

I walked back across the city through streets gone blue with early dark, and the sentence rode in my chest the whole way, tilting, tilting. At a crossing I caught myself mouthing it, testing the weight forward onto the first syllable—confession, a hand raised against her—and then backward onto the last—absolution, a hand extended. The same sounds. The same breath. Nothing in the language itself to arbitrate. All the courts adjourned; the judge was whoever spoke.

At the hospice, the night nurse told me she was sleeping, and that she had asked for paper. I stood outside her door a long time, listening to the machines keep their small faithful time, holding two truths that could not both be true and knowing that tomorrow one of them would have to die in front of me.

Under her door, before I left, I slid a single sheet. On it I had written the sentence once, in her phonetics, exactly as she had taught me—and beneath it, in English, both translations, side by side, with a blank line after each.

*Choose*, I wrote at the bottom. *While you still can.*

Then I went home and took out the inventory book, and for the first time in nine years I could not decide which column an entry belonged in.
