## 18. Last Light, Last Word

The hospice called at eleven at night, and by the time Xanthe arrived the corridor smelled of antiseptic and something older, sweeter—the smell of a room where someone is being allowed to leave at her own pace. The night nurse, a broad-shouldered man named Dev with kind eyes and the practiced calm of someone who had watched many rooms empty, met him at the door.

"She's been asking for you. Not by name—she keeps saying a word. *Sɛnkyerɛn?* Something like that."

"That's *teacher*," Xanthe said, and was surprised to hear it come out of him without hesitation, without the half-second of translation he still needed for most of what he'd learned. "Or close to it. It means the one who shows the road."

Dev nodded. "She's lucid in patches. The doctor says hours, maybe less. She's refused the morphine drip since sundown." He said it carefully, watching Xanthe's face. "She said she needed her mouth to be hers."

Of course she did. Xanthe felt something in his chest tighten—not grief yet, not yet, but the recognition of grief approaching, the way you feel a wave before it breaks, the undertow pulling at your ankles while the water still looks calm.

He went in.

The room was dim except for the lamp over the bed and the blue wash of the window, where the sky had not yet decided between night and morning. Amara lay propped against pillows, smaller than he had ever seen her, her hands folded on the blanket like two birds that had stopped flying. Her eyes found him at once. They had not dimmed. If anything, they were brighter now, the way coals are brighter just before they go to ash.

"You're late," she whispered.

"I came as fast as—"

"Late," she said again, and there was, unbelievably, the ghost of her old severity in it. "I have been dying for eighty-nine years. You could not arrive on time for the last hour of it."

He pulled the chair to the bedside and sat, and took her hand, and she did not pull it away. Her skin was cool and paper-fine. He could feel the pulse in her wrist, quick and shallow, a bird's pulse, a moth's.

"What word did you want?" he asked.

She smiled. It cost her something; he could see the cost in the tendons of her neck. "I wanted to see if Dev would find you. He did. Good. The word travels now." Her fingers tightened, a fraction, an entire sentence. "We have time for one more lesson. Maybe. Maybe not even one. So we will not waste it on the alphabet."

* * *

She taught him, in that last hour, the way she had taught him everything: through objects. But the room held few objects—a water glass, a basin, the lamp, the blanket, her own body—and so she taught him with those.

"The glass," she said, lifting one finger toward it. "Say it."

"*Nkyene*—no. Wait." His tongue stumbled. "*Kɛseɛ*—no." He closed his eyes, hating himself. "*Nsa*—no, that's hand—"

"Stop." Her voice was thin but it cut clean. "You are reaching for the word the way you reach for a postcard. From the top of the stack. The newest one. The word is not in the newest stack. It is in the oldest one. Go down. Down past your mother's English. Down past your English. Down to the bottom, where the water is cold. What is the glass?"

He went down. It felt like drowning in reverse—descending through layers of himself, through years of inventory and silence, past the burned house, past his mother's closed mouth, past his own careful, unsent sentences, down to a place where language was not a tool but a substance, where a glass was not a glass but a vessel, a holder, a thing-that-keeps, and the word rose out of the cold water and into his mouth:

"*Dɛsen.*"

Amara's breath went out of her in something that was almost a laugh. "There you are," she said. "There you are. You see? It was in you all along. I only had to make you go and get it."

"The lamp," she said. "*Nsoromma.* The star that lives in a house. We did not have a word for lamp that was only a lamp. A lamp is a star we caught and tamed. Say it."

"*Nsoromma.*"

"The blanket. Say it."

"*Nsã.*"

"The hand." She turned her palm up in his grip. "Say it."

"*Nsa.*"

"Good. Now the hard one." Her eyes held his. "Me. Say me."

He knew the word. He had known it for weeks. It was one of the first she'd given him, back when the lessons were still a bargain struck in bad faith, two people each hiding what they wanted. But now, saying it—now, with her pulse flickering under his fingers like a signal from a ship going down—the word would not come. It sat in him like a stone at the bottom of a well, too heavy to lift.

"Say it," she said. "Xanthe. Say it now. While I can hear it."

"*Wo*—no, that's you." His throat closed. "*Me—*" and then nothing. The grammar failed him. The pronouns failed him. He sat in the failing light of a dying language and could not say *me*.

Amara watched him struggle with something like tenderness, and something else underneath it—the old sharpness, the teacher who had survived a war by knowing exactly what people could and could not do.

"You cannot say it," she said, "because you have spent your whole life not saying it. Your mother did that to you. She took the word *me* out of your mouth before you ever learned it, and she filled the space with postcards." Her hand turned over, gripped his. "So I will say it for both of us. Listen."

She spoke then, low, in the language, and he did not translate it, because there was nothing to translate it *into*—it went into him the way water goes into dry ground, all at once, without negotiation. He understood it the way you understand a hand on your shoulder. It meant: *I see you. You are the one I made. Whatever you carry of me, carry it whole.*

"Say it back," she whispered. "Not the meaning. The sounds. The meaning can wait. The sounds cannot."

And he said it back. His accent was imperfect. His voice broke in the middle. But the sounds went out of his mouth and into the room, into the blue window, into the last hour of the world as it had been, and Amara closed her eyes and listened the way you listen to rain after a long drought—not to understand it, only to be under it.

* * *

The sky over the window began, very slowly, to consider the color of dawn.

Amara drifted in and out. In the out-times her face went smooth and young, and once she said, in English, "Efua, put the pot down, you'll burn yourself," in a voice so ordinary, so domestic, that Xanthe's chest seized—because here was a whole life compressed into one sentence, a kitchen, a child, a war waiting outside the door of it. In the in-times she came back sharp and exact, and she used the sharpness to teach.

"The tenses," she said, surfacing. "You have been avoiding the tenses. I have watched you avoid them for weeks. You do it the way your mother did it. You keep everything in the present because the present cannot accuse anyone."

"I don't—"

"Listen." Her breath caught, steadied. "In my language, the verb changes depending on when. But not the way your languages do it. Not past, present, future. We have a tense for *what was done to us*. We have a tense for *what we did to survive*. And we have a tense for *what we are still doing about it now*. Three tenses. Most people live in only one and pretend the others don't exist." Her eyes, fever-bright, fixed on him. "Your mother lived in *what was done to her*. I lived in *what I did*. Efua lives in *what she is still doing about it*. And you, Xanthe—"

She stopped. Coughed, a small dry sound, like paper tearing.

"You live in the tense that hasn't been invented yet. The one where you get to choose."

"The sentence," he said. The words came out before he could stop them, and he saw her hear them, and he saw her decide—something shifting behind her eyes, a door opening onto a room she had kept locked even from herself. "Amara. The sentence. Tell me what it—"

"Not yet." Her grip tightened, and for a dying woman her strength was astonishing, was infuriating. "Not while I can still change my mind. That is the whole point of it, do you see? The sentence has no neutral form. It must be spoken one way or the other. Accusation or absolution. And the speaker chooses." Her breath rasped. "I will not choose for you. I have spent my whole life choosing for people. It is the thing I am answering for."

"Then teach me the sounds again. Only the sounds."

She did. She gave him the sentence in fragments, the way you give someone a key in pieces because the whole key will not fit through the slot of the hour. The first phrase, rising. The middle, which turned on a sound he could only make by letting his throat do something English had never asked of it. The last phrase, which fell, which settled, which was—she made him repeat it eleven times—*the sound of a door closing gently, not slamming*. He wrote nothing down. He was afraid that if he looked away from her mouth to reach for the notebook, he would lose the mouth.

The window went from blue to grey to the first thin gold.

* * *

It happened mid-lesson. Later he would understand that this was the only way it could have happened—that for Amara Osei, the last fluent speaker of a language that had lived for a thousand years and was now living in the mouth of one dying woman and two broken students, the only possible death was in the middle of a word.

She was teaching him the word for dawn.

"*Anopa*," she said. "But that is only the clock's dawn. The other dawn—the one before it, the one where the light is not light yet but the dark already knows—is *ahemmerɛ*." She shaped the word slowly, syllable by syllable, and he repeated it, and she corrected his vowels with the last of her precision, "*ahemmerɛ*, not *ahemera*, the second syllable falls, let it fall—"

"*Ahemmerɛ.*"

"Again."

"*Ahemmerɛ.*"

"Again. You will need this word more than any other. Every day of your life there will be a moment that is this moment, the one where it is not yet light and not yet dark, the one where you have to choose which one you are standing in—"

Her voice stopped.

Not faded. Stopped, mid-syllable, the way a radio stops, and for one terrible second the room was so quiet Xanthe could hear the lamp.

"Amara."

Her eyes were open. They were looking at the window, at the gold coming up over the rooftops, and her lips were still moving, shaping something—he bent close, he put his ear almost to her mouth, and what came out was not a word he knew. It was not a word in any grammar. It was a sound, one sound, rising and then falling, and he understood with a clarity that felt like being struck: she was speaking the language to someone who was not in the room. Someone who had come for her. Someone she had been waiting eighty-nine years to say one more thing to.

Her hand was in his. He held it. He held it the way you hold a rope, the way you hold a door, the way you hold a word in your mouth that you are not ready to say. Her pulse went under his fingers—quick, quick, slower, slower—

"*Ahemmerɛ*," she whispered. The second syllable fell. She let it fall.

And then the pulse was a flutter, and then it was a pause, and then it was a pause that did not end, and Xanthe Okonkwo sat in the gold light of a dawn he had just learned to name and held the hand of the last speaker of a language, and felt the language leave the world through her breath—except for what lived in him, and in a woman across the city who didn't yet know her mother was gone.

He did not call the nurse. Not at first. He sat and held her hand through the pause, through the long pause, because he had learned enough grammar by now to know that a pause is not an ending; it is a tense. It is the tense where you are still doing something about it.

Then Dev came in, quiet, and did what nurses do, and the room filled with the machinery of after, and Xanthe stood by the window with the notebook in his pocket and the sentence in his mouth like a coal he could not swallow and could not spit out.

* * *

He walked home through the waking city without remembering any of the streets. The world was unbearable in its ordinariness: a man hosing down the pavement, bread smell from a bakery, a child arguing with her mother about a coat. All of it continuing. All of it in the present tense, as if nothing had ended at dawn, as if a whole way of saying *dawn* had not just gone out of the only mouth that held it whole.

At his flat he did what he always did. He sat at the desk. He took out a postcard. His hand hovered over it—and then he understood what his hand was doing, and he put the postcard down, because there was no address left to write to. 14 Marrow Lane was ash. Amara Osei was a body in a hospice bed being made ready for strangers. The inventory of unsent things had run out of addressees.

Except one.

He opened the notebook instead, to the page where he had written nothing, where the sentence lived only in his memory, in his throat, in the falling of its second syllable. *Accusation or absolution. The speaker chooses.* She had not chosen. She had refused, with the last of her strength, to choose for him—and now the choice sat in him like a stone, like the word *me* had sat in him, too heavy to lift.

His phone buzzed. Efua. He stared at her name for a long time, the screen glowing in the grey morning light of the room where everything unsaid had been filed for thirty years. He thought about what he would say. *Your mother died at dawn. She was teaching me the word for it.* He thought about the lullaby Efua had learned as a child, the fragment with its meaning inverted, the two versions of the sentence that could not both be true. He thought about the funeral, which would be in three days, where someone would have to stand up and say something, and where the safe thing—the English thing, the false thing—was already assembling itself in his head, smooth and hollow as a postcard.

He answered on the fourth ring.

"I know," Efua said, before he could speak. Her voice was strange—flat and vast, like the sea before weather. "The hospice called. Xanthe." A pause. In the pause he could hear her breathing, and under it, faintly, almost certainly imagined, he could hear the second syllable of *ahemmerɛ* falling. "There's something you need to know. About the sentence. About what my mother told me it means. Before you stand up at that funeral and open your mouth."

"I'm listening," he said.

And she began to tell him.
