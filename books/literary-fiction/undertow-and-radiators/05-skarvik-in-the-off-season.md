## 5. Skarvik in the Off-Season

The train stopped running to Skarvik in 1998, which meant the last leg of my journey was a bus driven by a man who talked the entire forty minutes about his gallbladder. I learned more than I wanted to know about laparoscopic surgery and nothing at all about what I was walking into, which felt like an appropriate exchange rate.

I had told myself I was coming for practical reasons. Edda Lindqvist was old, her letters were getting stranger, and someone ought to check on her. I was a professional of other people's instruments; I knew how to listen to things that were failing. That was all this was. A service call.

The lie had a nice even tone to it, like middle C. I let it ring.

* * *

Skarvik in October is a village holding its breath. The summer houses were shuttered, their windows reflecting grey water back at the grey water, so that the whole coastline seemed to be looking at itself and not liking what it saw. The tourist café had a sign in the window that said SEE YOU NEXT YEAR with a smiley face drawn in marker, and the smiley face had faded to a smudge, so it looked less like a greeting and more like a warning from someone who had given up.

I walked from the bus stop with my tuning kit over my shoulder and my notebook in my coat pocket, where it always rode, heavy as a brick. Thirty years of inventory. Some people keep diaries; I kept silences, catalogued and cross-referenced. *Item: never say Ingrid's name aloud. Item: never explain why you left. Item: never answer when someone asks if you dream in Norwegian.*

The harbor was still there, obviously. Harbors are stubborn. But the boats were fewer, and half of them looked like they'd been hauled up not for winter but for good, their hulls going soft as old fruit. The breakwater stood where it had always stood, arm's-length black stone reaching out into the sound, and I made myself look at it the way you make yourself press on a bruise—to prove you can, to prove something.

It didn't work. It never works. The breakwater looked exactly like the last day of everything.

* * *

My childhood home had become a rental property, which is the fate of most childhoods eventually. There was a new name stenciled on the mailbox—*HYTTE 4, Utleie*—and a plastic sign advertising weekly rates, and a keybox bolted beside the door with a code panel gone yellow from salt air. Someone had painted the front door a cheerful maritime blue. My mother would have hated it. My mother had hated every color except the color of worry.

I stood in the yard for longer than a person with any dignity should stand in a stranger's yard. The garden fence was the same one, or its descendant—my father had built it the year I was born, and fences here don't die, they just get repaired by successive generations of men who don't talk much. The birch tree by the gate had gotten enormous. Trees do that when you're not watching. It's their whole strategy.

And then, underneath the wind off the water, under the gulls and the creak of a mooring line somewhere, I heard it.

The radiators.

The house was occupied—or at least heated—and through the wall came the low three-note figure I had known before I knew language itself. Every radiator in Skarvik sang some version of it; the plumbers who installed them in the fifties must have all been cut from the same melancholy cloth. But ours had a particular phrase, a rising-falling-settling pattern, and my grandmother used to say the house was counting us. *One, two, three. Everyone accounted for.*

It was singing it now, for strangers. For tourists who would sleep in my bed and never know whose nightmares had seasoned the mattress springs.

I put my hand flat against the fence board and listened until the notes resolved and repeated. One, two, three. Except we weren't three anymore. We weren't even two. The house was counting ghosts and getting the arithmetic wrong, and there was a part of me—a part I did not invite but which arrived anyway, unannounced, like relatives—that wanted to knock on the door and tell whoever was inside: *You're living in a sentence that hasn't been finished. You're renting the pause.*

I took my hand off the fence. I had a room booked at the guesthouse above the shop, because sleeping in my own childhood house as a paying customer seemed like a joke even I wasn't cruel enough to play on myself.

* * *

The shop was called Nilsen's when I was a girl, which was a problem then too, though nobody said so out loud. Now it was called Skarvik Handel, which solved nothing and pleased everyone, because renaming things is how villages apologize without apologizing.

A bell over the door announced me. Inside it smelled exactly as it had always smelled—coffee, diesel, dried fish, floor wax—the smell of a place that has decided change is for other people. The shelves had rearranged themselves slightly in thirty years, the way furniture does in dreams.

Behind the counter was a woman about my age with a fishmonger's forearms and a cardigan that had survived several administrations. She looked up, said "Welcome," in the automatic voice of the seasonally doomed, and then stopped.

She looked at me for a long moment. I watched recognition arrive in stages, like weather coming in off the water: first the squint, then the tilt of the head, then the softening around the mouth that precedes either a hug or a catastrophe.

Then she said it.

"Ingrid?"

The bell was still faintly swinging behind me. The freezer hummed. Somewhere in the back a radio played a song about summer, because even radios in Skarvik refuse to accept the calendar.

"No," I said. My voice came out level, which cost me. "Petra. Petra Whitlock."

The woman's hand went to her mouth, actually to her mouth, like a character in a silent film. "Å nei. Å, nei, forgive me." She came around the counter wiping her hands on her apron as if she might need them free for emergency embrace. "Petra. Of course. You have her face now—you understand, you *have* her face. When you were girls you didn't, Petra was the sharp one, Ingrid was the—" She stopped herself, which was kind, and then kept going anyway, which was Skarvik. "You look so much like her. At that age. Before."

Before. The word sat down between us and refused to leave.

"It's all right," I said. "People make the mistake."

"People shouldn't." She shook her head hard, twice, like a dog shedding water. "Thirty years and I still do it. Your mother does the same thing, you know. Walks past the window and I think—" She caught herself again, and this time the catch held. "Solveig is coming back, did you know? For Edda. The whole village is talking about it. Well. The whole village talks about everything. That's the problem with a village, isn't it. And the point of one."

"I heard she might come," I said, which was true, in the sense that I had read it in Edda's second letter between the photograph and the sentence I couldn't translate.

"Kari," the woman said, extending her hand. "Kari Vold. We were in school together, but you wouldn't remember, I was two years below and mostly quiet." She pumped my hand with genuine warmth, and I searched her face for the child I should have known and found only weathered adult, and felt the specific vertigo of realizing that everyone from your past has continued existing without your permission.

"I'll take a room," I said. "For the week. Maybe longer."

"Longer is good." Kari was already pulling a ledger from under the counter, an actual paper ledger, because the guesthouse registration had apparently also declined to enter the century. "We're dead in October. You could have the whole upstairs and sing opera at midnight and no one would complain except the gulls, and they complain regardless."

She found me a pen. I wrote my name in the ledger, and writing *Petra Whitlock* in a Skarvik book felt like signing something legally binding, like countersigning a treaty with my own past.

"You're here for Edda," Kari said. Not quite a question.

"For Edda," I agreed.

"She won't know you at first. Then she'll know you better than you know yourself. That's how she is now—the memory goes backward and forward both, like tide." Kari tore off a key from a board of keys behind her, each tag hand-labeled in fading ink. "Room three. Watch the third stair, it complains."

Everything here complains, I almost said. That's how it tells the truth.

* * *

Room three was clean and narrow and smelled of lavender deployed aggressively against mildew. The window faced the harbor, which meant it faced the breakwater, which meant the view came with a surcharge I hadn't known I was paying. I set my tuning kit down on the dresser—my mutes, my tuning hammer, my felt strips, the small professional religion of my life—and unpacked nothing else. Unpacking means staying. I wasn't ready to conjugate that verb.

I sat on the bed with my notebook and opened it to a fresh page, because that is what I do instead of praying.

*Item,* I wrote. *The fishmonger called me Ingrid.*

Underneath, after a while:

*Item: I corrected her. This is technically true and morally irrelevant.*

The radiator under the window ticked as it warmed, gathering itself, and then began its figure. Three notes. Rising, falling, settling. The same phrase our kitchen wall had sung, the same phrase every pipe in this village carried like a shared bloodstream. I had spent thirty years telling clients that radiators were just physics—expansion, contraction, water finding its level—but I had never once believed it, and sitting there in room three with the harbor going dark outside, I understood with total clarity why I had chosen a profession built on listening to houses.

Houses don't lie. Houses just repeat what happened in them, patiently, forever, at sixty-eight degrees Fahrenheit, waiting for someone with the right ear.

Our house was singing its three-note warning for strangers now. But warnings are addressed to whoever can hear them. Maybe they'd always been addressed to me.

*Item,* I wrote, and my pen hesitated, and then I made myself finish it: *Item: tomorrow, Edda Lindqvist. Bring the notebook. Bring all of it.*

Outside, the last ferry light crossed the sound, and the village settled into its off-season silence—not empty silence, but held silence, the way a congregation holds its breath before the organ starts.

Somewhere down the hill, a radiator began to sing.
