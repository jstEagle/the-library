## 12. The Radiator Song of the Church

The church piano was a Bösendorfer that had been left to rot in a sea of salt air for thirty years, which is to say it was a Bösendorfer in the way a drowned man is still technically a swimmer. Pastor Vold had called me on a Tuesday, his voice carrying the particular embarrassment of a man asking a favor of someone he'd rather not be seen with.

"We heard you were back," he said. "Temporarily."

"Temporarily," I agreed, and let him think that was reassurance.

I could have refused. I want that on the record, wherever records are kept—the inventory of unsaid things has an appendix now, and it begins with *I could have said no*. But the piano sat in the church where my grandmother had been baptized and married and buried without once being asked her opinion of any of it, and there is a kind of gravity in old buildings that no one escapes by refusing. Also, I needed the work. Grief and guilt both bill monthly, and neither accepts installments.

So on Thursday morning I carried my tools up the hill past the fishmonger's shuttered stall—Bjørn raised a hand as I passed, and I raised mine back, and we both pretended the other hadn't flinched—and into the church, which smelled of candle wax and cold stone and, underneath it all, faintly, of iron. The radiators ran along the walls like ribs under skin, cast-iron monsters from some decade when the congregation had money and faith in equal measure. They were off. The pastor had assured me they'd stay off until the heating season proper began in November.

They were singing anyway.

* * *

You have to understand what I mean by *singing*, because I know how it sounds. A radiator that is off makes no sound; this is physics, and I have never once argued with physics. But a building is not only its pipes. A building is what its pipes have carried, the way a violin is not only wood but every note ever drawn out of it, and if you've spent thirty years with your ear against other people's walls you learn to hear the residue. The Victorian house in chapter three of my life—I mean my actual life, though sometimes the two blur—had taught me that. Its pipes had hummed my grandmother's kitchen back at me: the same three-beat rhythm she tapped with a wooden spoon against the pot rim, the same argumentative syncopation of a family talking around a thing instead of through it.

The church was doing something else.

I set my case down by the piano and stood still and listened, the way you'd stand still at the edge of water you suspected held something. The sound was very low, below hearing almost, more vibration than tone, coming up through the floorboards and into the soles of my feet like a pulse. And it had a shape. Not the kitchen's shape. Something older, slower, with a falling cadence at the end of each phrase—a phrase that ended, always, unresolved, the way a sentence ends when the speaker dies before the verb.

I knew it. That was the terrible part. I stood in my dead grandmother's church with my tuning fork in my hand and I knew the melody, because Edda Lindqvist had sung it to me eleven days earlier in a care home that smelled of lavender and institutional despair, tapping it out on the armrest of her chair while teaching me the word I would not yet translate.

*A lament for the drowned,* she'd said. Or rather she'd sung it, twice, and then looked at me with those pale eyes and waited for me to ask what it meant, and I had asked about verb conjugations instead, because cowardice is a discipline and I am very well trained.

Now the church itself was singing it, through pipes full of nothing but cold air and forty years of sediment, and I thought: buildings remember too. I had built a whole life on that idea. I had simply never considered what my own village might remember, or whom it might hold responsible.

* * *

The piano needed everything. I started with the bass strings, muting them with felt and listening for the false beats, and for an hour the work held me the way work does—mercifully, completely, the hands busy so the mind can pretend to be elsewhere. There is a reason tuners are hired rather than merely tolerated. We come into your house and make things true, and we don't ask what happened in the rooms we pass through.

But the radiator-song kept threading under the piano's voice. Every time I struck a note and listened to it decay, the decay had company—that low falling phrase beneath it, patient as tide. And somewhere around the third octave I stopped working and just sat on the piano bench with my hands in my lap like a child at a recital she hasn't practiced for.

Because here is what I finally admitted, sitting there: the melody wasn't ambient. It wasn't the building groaning or the wind finding a gap in the stonework. It was structured. Four phrases, descending, each ending on that unfinished cadence, then a silence exactly long enough for an answer that never came. Call-and-response, Edda had called it, when I asked why so many songs in the old language ended mid-thought. *The second voice is expected. When it doesn't come, the silence is part of the song.* She'd said it lightly, but she'd watched me while she said it, the way you watch a door you expect to open.

Four phrases. Then silence. Then four phrases again.

I got up and walked the length of the church, following the sound to its source, which was absurd, because the source was everywhere—the whole iron skeleton of the building humming it, floor to rafters. Near the north wall, behind the second pew from the back, the vibration was strongest. I put my palm flat against the radiator there, and the metal was cold, and it sang into my hand anyway.

That was the pew my family had used. Third from the front actually, but after the funeral we'd moved to the back, all of us, silently, the way water finds its level, and no one ever moved us forward again. Ingrid's mother had sat across the aisle. She had looked at me during every service for the rest of her life—not often, not dramatically, just a glance, once per hymn, the way you check a wound. She died when I was twenty-two, in Trondheim, and I learned of it from a newspaper clipping my mother mailed without comment, which was its own kind of sentence.

I took my hand off the radiator. My palm had gone numb with cold, or with something else.

* * *

Pastor Vold came by at noon with coffee in a thermos and the manner of a man visiting a sick relative he doesn't intend to help.

"How is she?" he asked, meaning the piano, and I gave him the honest answer, which was that she'd survive but never again hold her pitch through a damp winter, and he nodded as though I'd delivered a sermon.

"You're staying at the old Solheim place?" he said, pouring coffee into a cup he'd brought for himself and then, after visible calculation, offering me.

"The rental, yes."

"It's good you came back." He said it the way people say *it's good weather*, meaning nothing, committing to nothing. "For Edda. She was a teacher here, you know. Before my time. Sunday school, and the Norwegian lessons for the fishing families' children." He paused. "And the other lessons."

I didn't ask. He didn't elaborate. We stood in the church together drinking coffee while its bones sang a lament neither of us acknowledged, and I understood that Pastor Vold knew the melody too. Everyone over fifty in Skarvik knew it. They'd grown up inside it. It was in the walls the way the lie was in the town: structural, load-bearing, never discussed.

"There's a memorial service in the spring," he said suddenly, looking at the piano rather than at me. "For the drowned. We do it every year. Ingrid's name is read, among others." A pause with weight in it. "Halvard Nilsen usually doesn't attend."

"He wouldn't be welcome."

"No," Vold said, and then, with the mildness of a man setting down something heavy very carefully: "No one ever decided that, exactly. It's just understood." He screwed the cap onto his thermos. "It's strange, isn't it, the things that get understood without anyone deciding them."

He left. I stood alone in the church with my cooling coffee and the song, and I thought: he knows. Not the specifics—maybe not even consciously—but pastors develop the same ear I have, an ear for the note the congregation won't sing. Thirty years of reading Ingrid's name aloud and watching half the pews look at the floor. He had been conducting a lament whose second voice was missing, year after year, and he had begun, perhaps, to wonder who had swallowed it.

* * *

I went back to the piano and worked until my fingers ached, because the alternative was thinking, and thinking had become a hazardous occupation. By late afternoon the instrument was nearly true—temperament set, unisons clean, the middle register singing the way Bösendorfers do, that dark warm bloom like a voice remembering something pleasant. I played a chord to test it, then another, and then, because the church was empty and empty churches invite blasphemy, I found myself picking out the melody.

The lament. Edda's four descending phrases with their unfinished endings.

My hands knew more of it than I did. That startled me. I'd heard it sung twice, eleven days ago, and yet under my fingers it unfolded complete—phrases three and four arriving as if they'd been waiting in the tendons since childhood, since some afternoon in this same church when I was small and my grandmother had hummed it, thinking I wasn't listening, thinking the language she'd buried was safe inside a tune no one remembered the words to.

Except I did remember the words. Some of them. They surfaced as I played, rising like drowned things do eventually, bloated and unmistakable: *the water takes, the water keeps, the water gives back only what it wants.* And then the fourth phrase, the one that ends without resolution, the one that asks its question into the silence—

I stopped playing. My hands were shaking, which annoyed me. Hands should be reliable; they're the one part of me I've always trusted.

The fourth phrase was a name. It had been a name all along. The lament wasn't generic—it was specific, local, composed or adapted for this coast, and its unanswered final line was a question posed to the sea, and the sea had answered thirty years ago with a body on the rocks below the breakwater, and the town had decided the answer was Halvard Nilsen because the truth was a girl of fourteen standing in a constable's office inventing a boat.

I sat with that. The radiators sang. Outside, the light went the color of old brass, and a gull argued with something invisible, and the tide turned with a sound like a long exhale.

Here is what became inescapable, sitting on that bench in the church where my lie had been repeated every Sunday for three decades in the form of a name read aloud and a glance across the aisle: the grief and the lie shared one melody. They had always shared it. The town mourned Ingrid through the same four phrases it used to condemn Halvard, because the condemnation was folded into the mourning like poison into honey—you couldn't sing one without the other, couldn't weep for the girl without pronouncing the guilty man, and so the whole village sang its grief with a falseness in it, a beat that never resolved, year after year, until nobody alive could hear the falseness anymore except the ones who'd caused it.

I had caused it. Me, fourteen years old, terrified of a constable's questions and of my mother's face through the door, reaching for the one detail that would end the interrogation: *there was a boat.* Three words. I have tuned pianos in six countries and I can tell you that three wrong notes, placed early enough, ruin everything downstream. Nothing you do later—no amount of correct voicing, no careful regulation—fully removes them. You can only retune from the foundation, string by string, and hope the frame holds.

The frame was holding. That was the horror of it. Halvard Nilsen's frame, holding for thirty years under a weight it was never built for.

* * *

I locked the church at dusk and walked down the hill with my case, and at the bottom, where the road bends toward the harbor, I saw him. Halvard, at his gate, coiling rope with the slow economy of a man who has nothing left to hurry for. He saw me. He lifted his hand—not a wave, exactly. An acknowledgment. The way you acknowledge weather.

I lifted mine back and kept walking, and behind me the church settled into evening, its pipes going quiet one register at a time, the way a singer closes her mouth between verses.

At the rental house I put my key in the lock and the radiators inside woke as the boiler cycled, three notes, the warning, the same three notes they'd sung when I was fourteen and packing a bag I told myself was temporary. But tonight, for the first time, I heard what came after the third note. A fourth. Faint, uncertain, like a voice remembering itself.

Or like a question, waiting for its answer.

I stood on the doorstep in the dark with Ingrid's name in my mouth and Halvard's rope-coiling hands behind my eyelids and Edda's unfinished cadence in my ears, and I understood that the song was not going to release me. It had waited thirty years inside iron and stone and tendon, and it was patient, and it wanted its second voice.

Tomorrow, I thought. Tomorrow I would go back to Edda and ask her to teach me the words I'd been refusing.

The door opened under my hand before I'd finished turning the key.
