## 13. Halvard's Version

The nets don't care what people say about you. That's the first thing I'd tell you if you asked me how I've survived thirty years in Skarvik after everyone decided I killed a girl. The nets only want to know if your hands are steady. They don't ask where you were on the fourth of August 1989. They don't look away when you come into the shop. They just hang there in the shed, smelling of salt and patience, waiting to be mended, and there is a certain mercy in that.

I was mending them the morning Petra Whitlock came back to the village. I didn't see her myself — old Berg at the fish stall told me, in the way Berg tells everything, which is sideways and with a great deal of herring involved. *You'll never guess who's off the train.* And then he looked at me with those watery eyes of his, and I guessed.

Petra Whitlock. Ingrid's shadow. The one who left before the funeral flowers had even wilted, as if grief were a tide she could outrun if she got to the city fast enough.

I put down my netting needle and sat for a while.

You have to understand something about being accused. Not charged — accused. The law never touched me. Two constables came, asked their questions, looked at my engine, found it cold and dead and full of seawater where seawater had no business being, and went away again. No charges. No trial. But Skarvik is a small place, and a rumor in a small place is like water in a bilge: it finds every seam, and it rises, and eventually you are bailing all day just to keep your head above the story they've decided is true.

I bailed for thirty years.

* * *

Let me tell you about the day itself, because nobody has ever actually asked me for the day itself. They asked me questions, yes, but questions are not the same thing. A question is a door someone opens so they can look for what they already believe is inside.

It was hot. That's the thing nobody remembers now — they remember grey water and a grey sea and a grey grief, because that's how you're supposed to remember a drowning. But it was golden. It was one of those days Skarvik gets maybe four times a summer, when the light comes off the water so hard you have to squint, and the whole village smells of tar and sunscreen and somebody burning fish on a grill.

My boat was the *Solveig's Promise* then — named for my mother, not for Solveig Whitlock, though I'll admit the coincidence did me no favors later. Forty years old, that engine, and temperamental as a cat in rain. I'd taken her out past the breakwater around two o'clock to check the marker buoys, alone, because my deckhand had a wedding to go to in Bodø and I was too cheap to refuse the work.

Around three, maybe half three, the engine coughed twice and died.

Now, any fisherman will tell you: an engine dying is not an event. It's a Tuesday. You drift, you swear at it, you open the housing, you find the problem or you don't, and meanwhile you're not going anywhere fast because the sea off Skarvik in August is usually asleep. I opened the housing. There was water where water shouldn't be — a cracked line, I thought, or worse. I got on the radio. Static. I tried again. Static, and then a voice so far away it might have been God mumbling in his sleep.

And here is the detail that mattered, the detail that decided everything, and I want you to hold it carefully because I have held it for thirty years and it has worn grooves in me: I could not start the engine, and the current was taking me along the outside of the breakwater, parallel to shore, at the speed a man walks when he isn't in any hurry.

That's it. That's the whole crime. A dead engine and a slow current, on the one afternoon in thirty years when a current needed to be somewhere else.

* * *

I saw the girls before they saw me. Or I saw one girl and the idea of another — they were down by the rocks past the breakwater, two small figures, and one of them went into the water. I remember thinking, idly, the way you think things on a good day: *braver than me.* The water past the breakwater is colder than it looks. It looks like bathwater on a day like that. It is not bathwater. It is the North Sea wearing a disguise.

I shouted something — a wave, a hello, I don't know. My voice doesn't carry over water the way you'd think. Sound skips off the surface when the air is warm. Everything I said that afternoon skipped, skittered, went out flat and died a few meters from my mouth.

Then the second girl went in, and I lost sight of both of them behind the breakwater stones, and I went back to my engine, because that is what a sensible man does. He fixes his engine. He does not stand on a drifting boat staring at children playing in the sea, because if you do that in a village like this, people talk, and I was already a man people talked about — a bachelor, forty-one, odd, keeps to himself, talks to his nets. You learn early which behaviors cost you. Standing on a boat watching children swim would have cost me.

So I fixed the engine. It took — I know this because I have gone back over it ten thousand times, I have timed it in my sleep — it took perhaps twenty minutes. Maybe twenty-five. When I got her running again, the current had carried me a good stretch down the coast, and I turned back toward the breakwater because I'm not a monster, whatever else I am, and I wanted to make sure the girls were clear of my wake.

The beach was wrong.

I can't describe it better than that. Beaches have a grammar, and this one had lost its verbs. People standing in clusters. Someone running. A woman on her knees in the sand with her arms wrapped around herself like she was trying to keep her own body from coming apart.

I cut the engine and drifted in and shouted, and this time the sound carried, because grief travels better than greetings, and a man on the rocks — Torvald, I think, Torvald Bakke — shouted back at me:

*"Where were you?"*

Not *a girl is missing.* Not *have you seen anything.* Where were you. As if the question had been waiting for a boat to attach itself to, the way a drowning man attaches himself to driftwood. As if the village had been holding its breath for years, waiting for something terrible to happen so it would finally have somewhere to put all the fear it kept in the cellar.

I told him. Dead engine, cracked line, drifting, here is the housing, come and look, smell the seawater in it. And men did come and look, some of them, and some of them nodded slowly, and every single nod had a hinge in it, a little conditional clause: *if what you say is true.*

If.

Thirty years of *if.*

* * *

They dragged the water for two days. I helped. Did you know that? I rowed one of the drag boats myself, with Torvald Bakke sitting in the stern watching me the way you watch a man who might steal the oars. We pulled Ingrid up on the second evening, from the deep channel just past the breakwater, where the undertow lives — the one the old people had a word for, the word in grandmother Eriksen's language that nobody would teach us boys because we weren't the right blood for secrets.

She looked peaceful. Everyone said so. Peaceful, like sleeping. I have never repeated this to anyone, but I will put it here, in the privacy of my own telling: she did not look peaceful. She looked like a girl who had been pulled somewhere she did not want to go, by something stronger than she was, and the sea had smoothed her face afterward the way it smooths everything, out of what I can only assume is politeness.

Her scarf came up two days later, snagged on the breakwater stones near the channel mouth. Red wool, knitted by her grandmother — by Edda Lindqvist, though I didn't know her name then, just knew her as the old woman who spoke the secret language. The constable logged it as evidence and then, because there was no case, un-logged it, and it sat in a box in the station at Bodø until someone sent it back to Skarvik with a form letter, and the form letter sat on the constable's desk, and nobody claimed it, because claiming it meant standing in a room and saying *this belonged to the drowned girl* out loud.

I claimed it. Eventually. A year later, almost. I said I'd found it on my boat, which was a lie, my first lie in the whole affair, and I have often thought it strange that the one lie I ever told in that entire summer was told in order to take custody of a piece of evidence against myself. I suppose I wanted something of hers to keep safe. I suppose when a village takes your name away, you need to hold on to something true with your hands.

I have kept it thirty years. Wrapped in oilcloth, in the bottom drawer under the spare netting needles. Some nights I take it out. Wool holds a shape, did you know that? Thirty years folded and it still holds the shape of a fourteen-year-old girl's neck.

* * *

Here is what I understood, in the weeks after, watching the story assemble itself without anyone ever quite saying it: a village cannot survive an accident. An accident means the sea is indifferent, and if the sea is indifferent, then every boat that goes out goes out unprotected, and every mother on this coast would have to live with that. But a guilty man — ah. A guilty man is a load-bearing wall. You can hang the whole village's safety off him. As long as Halvard Nilsen did something, then the sea does nothing, and the boats go out, and the mothers sleep.

So they built me. Not maliciously, most of them. The way you build anything: one beam at a time. *He was always strange.* Beam. *His engine was broken, wasn't it?* Beam. *He was right there.* Beam. Nobody laid a single brick and said, *there, that's the house where we keep our innocence.* They just kept handing each other materials until the thing stood up on its own, and then they moved in, and they've lived in it thirty years, and they call it the truth.

The strangest part — the part I'd laugh at if laughing were available to me — is that the person who built the biggest beam never said a word in public. Petra Whitlock. The constable talked to her, everyone knew that; she'd been on the beach, she'd been Ingrid's friend, she was the last one to see her alive except the sea. And whatever Petra said in that room, the story hardened overnight. Before her statement, I was a man whose boat had drifted. After it, I was a man who had been *seen.* Seen doing what, exactly, was never specified. Seen was enough. Seen is a wonderful word. It requires no content whatsoever.

I met her once, after. At the shop, the winter after, when she was sixteen and already halfway to becoming a ghost. She looked at me — I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have — she looked at me the way you look at a stove you're afraid is still hot. Not hatred. Fear. And I thought at the time: *she believes it. The poor child believes it, and no one has corrected her, and I am the monster under her bed now, and I will carry that for a child who won't even look at me properly.*

What I did not think — what it took me twenty more years to think — was: *why is she afraid? What did she see? What did she say?*

A fourteen-year-old girl drowns, and her best friend leaves the village within the month and never comes back for thirty years, and no one in this village — not one of us, including me, including especially me — ever asked why. We were too busy living in the house we'd built.

* * *

Berg says she's staying at the old guesthouse. Says she's been seen going up to Edda Lindqvist's room at the care home every afternoon, carrying books. Says the old woman's mind is going, going, and the Whitlock girl is learning the language before it dies.

Learning the language.

I stood in my shed with that information and let it move through me like cold water through a wader boot. Because here is a thing I have never told anyone, a thing I will set down now and then fold this telling away like the scarf: I heard them, that last day. The girls. I heard them talking as my boat drifted past the breakwater, before the second one went into the water, and they were not speaking Norwegian. They were speaking the other language, the secret one, the one that sounds like water deciding something. And one of them — the living one, the one who is now back in this village after thirty years — called something across the water. To me, I thought at the time. A greeting, maybe. A child's joke.

Or a warning. I have wondered for thirty years whether it was a warning. Whether Ingrid was already past the breakwater by then, already in the channel, already in the grip of the thing the old language has a single word for and Norwegian needs a paragraph to fail at describing. Whether that call across the water was a girl asking the only adult in earshot for help, in the only language she had that felt big enough for the asking.

I didn't answer. I didn't understand. I went back to my engine, because that is what a sensible man does.

So. Petra Whitlock is learning the language. From the last throat that speaks it. And I am an old man in a shed with a red scarf in a drawer and a question I have carried so long it has grown into my skeleton.

There is a version of events where I burn the scarf and die quiet, the way I have lived, and the village gets to keep its house, and I get to keep what's left of my name, which is to say the plot of ground where a name used to be.

And there is a version where I walk up to the guesthouse, knock on her door, and put thirty years of oilcloth and wool into the hands of the only other person who was there.

I sat with the netting needle in my lap until the light went out of the shed, and then I went inside, and I took the drawer open, and I held the scarf, and I did not put it back.
