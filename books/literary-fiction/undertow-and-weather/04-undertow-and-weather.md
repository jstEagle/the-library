## 4. Undertow and Weather

The storm arrives on a Tuesday, the way the worst weather does—without ceremony, without warning worth heeding. I'm at the desk when the lights stutter, when the rain goes from percussion to pressure, when the drains on Delancey give up their argument and the water starts looking for lower ground. The library basement is the lowest ground for six blocks.

I go down with a flashlight and a roll of plastic sheeting and find the water already ankle-deep, black and patient, lifting the returns carts off their wheels like a tide deciding what belongs to it. The overflow from the 700s floats past me—architecture, mostly, and a book on Japanese gardens that has been checked out twice in ten years. I stand there in the dark and let it come. There is a strange mercy in a flood. It doesn't ask what you were keeping down here. It just takes.

I call the emergency line, and a man with a tired voice tells me to get upstairs and not to risk myself for inventory. I stand in the doorway of the reading room and watch the rain write its long gray sentence down the windows, and I think: I have been keeping something in a basement too. Five years of it. And it is flooding now whether I open the drawer or not.

Teo hasn't spoken to me in nine days. Not since the letters. Not since I laid them on the dining room table like evidence in a trial neither of us wanted to hold. *You're doing this at her funeral week,* he said, and his voice had that terrible steadiness he gets, the one that means he is holding the roof up with his hands. *You're doing this now.*

I hadn't answered. There was no answer that wasn't either a surrender or a weapon.

I take the bus across the drowned city to the care home on the hill, the one place in the neighborhood the water hasn't reached. The nurse at the desk says my mother has had a hard afternoon but is calm now, sitting by the window, watching the storm come down the valley.

"She's more herself in bad weather," the nurse says, and smiles apologetically, as if she's said something unprofessional. "Some of them are. The barometer, maybe. Or maybe they just like knowing the sky can still surprise them."

I go in.

* * *

My mother is small in the chair by the window, wrapped in the blue shawl I bought her in Lisbon a lifetime ago, when I still thought travel was the same thing as escape. She turns her head when I enter, and I know—before she says a word, before anything—I know that she is *here*. The way the fog lifts off the water all at once. The clarity is total and I understand, with a librarian's instinct for the final return, that it will not last. Windows like this don't stay open.

"Isandro," she says. Not Lena. Not the aunt. Not the woman from the market she sometimes greets as her sister. My name, whole, pronounced correctly, with the stress she always insisted on. *Is-AN-dro.*

"Mama."

"Sit down. You're dripping."

I sit. My coat is soaked through. Outside, the rain has become a single continuous sound, the city erased behind it.

"You've been angry with me," she says. "For a long time. You think I don't know. You come every Thursday and you hold my hand and you are so careful, and careful is just anger wearing its good coat."

I open my mouth. Nothing comes out that is adequate. She has never spoken like this—not to me, not in the five years of the fading, maybe not ever.

"Vera came," she says. "The Aldous girl. They told me. I heard her at the funeral, saying the name. Marchetti-Aldous. Like a bell." She looks at her hands. "I thought I would be afraid, hearing it. I've been afraid of it for fifty years. But it was just a name. It was just my own name, said out loud."

"Mama—"

"I was married before your father." She says it plainly, the way she might tell me the year a house was built. "I was twenty-three. His name was Emil Aldous. He was kind and he was weak and he loved me in the way that people love a door they're standing in front of—not the door, just the possibility of what's behind it. And I was already in love with someone else, and I was a coward, and I married him anyway because my mother told me a girl with my prospects couldn't afford to wait for weather."

The rain fills the silence. I don't move. I am afraid that if I move, the window will close.

"I left him after two years. There was no divorce that the family would permit, so we did the other thing—the quiet thing, the arrangement. He kept the name off the papers and I kept the life. And there was a child." Her voice doesn't break. It goes very level, the way water goes level before it goes over the edge. "Lena. Elena Aldous. My daughter. Your sister, Isandro. Half, the way they say it, as if the halves were less."

I am thirty-four years old and I feel like the flooded basement, everything I stored down there lifted and turning slowly in the dark.

"I know," I say. "The letters. I found the letters. I've known for—"

"I know you've known." She turns and looks at me, and her eyes are the sharpest thing in the room. "You think I lost everything, but I only lost the recent things. I have known for two years that you knew. You read them the way you read everything—completely, silently, and then you shelve it." She almost smiles. "I waited for you to ask me. All this time. I thought, she's a librarian, she'll come to the desk eventually. She'll ask the question."

"I couldn't. It wasn't mine to—"

"It was *exactly* yours to ask." For a moment she is fierce, the woman from the photographs, the celebrated one, the one who stood on stages. "A secret kept from a child is a theft. I stole from you, and from Teo, and I told myself it was protection, and protection is the word cowards use for control. I'm not going to do it from this chair. I'm not going to let the disease take it before I give it."

She reaches over and takes my hand. Her grip is stronger than it has been in years.

"Her name is Elena. She lives in Porto. She has two sons and she paints, badly, and she writes to me every Christmas and I have written back every Christmas and neither of us has ever said the word mother in a letter, and that was my failure, not hers." She presses my hand. "I am telling you so that you don't have to carry it like a body. Carry it like a key. Do you understand the difference?"

I understand. I have understood, maybe, my whole life, without knowing what I was understanding.

"What do I do with it?" My voice comes out small.

"That," she says, "is the only part that's yours. Not the secret. The choosing." She looks back at the window, at the storm doing its work on the world. "I chose silence and it cost me Lena and it cost your father the truth and it cost you five years of Thursdays where you couldn't look at me. I'm not asking you to be better than me. I'm asking you to be *later* than me. That's all inheritance is, Isandro. Doing it a little later, a little better, until one of us finally gets it right."

The fog is already coming back in. I can see it happen—her eyes losing their focus by degrees, the sharpness going soft at the edges like a photograph left in the sun. But she holds on a moment longer, and she says, clearly, deliberately, like a woman signing her name:

"Tell your brother. Not for me. For him. A man should know what he's made of."

And then she is looking at the rain, and she says, gently, to no one and to me: "Is it still storming? I do love weather. It's so honest."

* * *

I find Teo at his apartment at nearly midnight, standing in his kitchen with the lights off, watching the water sheet down his windows. He doesn't ask why I'm there. He pours a second glass of the whiskey he's clearly already been into and sets it on the counter in front of me, which is the closest thing to forgiveness our family has ever produced.

"She was lucid," I say. "Completely. It won't last, Teo. It might be the last time."

He goes very still.

"She told me everything. Not because I asked. Because she wanted to give it to us before it was taken." I wrap my hands around the glass. "Her name is Elena Aldous. She's our sister. She lives in Porto. And I'm not going to the papers, I'm not going to the aunt, I'm not going to Vera. I'm telling you, here, because you're my brother and you should hear it from me and not from a flood."

He is quiet for a long time. The storm hammers the glass. When he finally speaks, his voice is wrecked and steady at once.

"I don't forgive you."

"I know."

"I don't forgive *her*."

"I know."

He looks at the window for a long moment, and then he pulls out the chair beside him at the counter, and he says, "Sit down. Start at the beginning. Start with the letters."

I sit. I stay. Outside, the water keeps rising, and the two of us sit in the bright kitchen above the drowned street, and I begin to tell him what the tide has finally given back.
