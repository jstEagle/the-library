## 9. Letter Seven, Water-Stained

The letters lived in the tin on the kitchen table now, arranged in a fan like a losing hand of cards. I'd taken to checking on them in the morning the way you'd check on a sleeping animal — not because they needed anything, but because you want to know it's still breathing, still there, still yours to be responsible for. Forty-one letters. Eilish's handwriting marching across each envelope with the confidence of a woman who believed, apparently for years, that she had said something worth saying, and said it, and then put it in a drawer.

Forty-one stamps, never cancelled. Forty-one addresses, never reached. It occurred to me that my grandmother had been a piano tuner of sorts herself — she'd struck the note over and over and never once let it out of the room.

Maren was still asleep upstairs, or pretending to be, which in Maren's case was a form of work. She'd been packing with the ferocity of a woman being paid by the box, which in a sense she was, though I didn't know the details of that yet. I only knew that every morning the tape gun came out a little earlier, and every morning the fan of letters on the table got a little more compressed, as if she were tidying them toward the bin by degrees.

I made tea. I sat down. I picked up the seventh envelope because it was the one that had been bothering me.

You could tell it was different before you touched it. The others were the pale buff of old paper kept in the dark; this one had a tide mark across it, a brown bloom like something had drowned and dried. Water-stained. The paper had gone soft and translucent at the bottom corner, and where the ink had run, Eilish's careful hand had blurred into ghosts of itself.

I don't know why I hesitated. I'd read six of these already — alone, at night, the way you eat something you're not supposed to have. But this one felt heavier before I'd read a word, the way a house feels different before a pipe bursts. Some part of me, the part that listens to radiators for a living, had already heard it coming.

I opened it.

*Dear Nuala,*

*You will have heard by now, or you will not have heard at all, which would be like you. Either way I have to write it down because I cannot say it in this house, and I cannot say it in any house, and so I will say it to paper, which at least has the decency to hold still.*

*I am carrying a child, Nuala. I am four months gone and I am leaving, and I need you to understand that these two facts are not separate.*

The kitchen got very quiet. Not the ordinary quiet of an old house in the morning, which is really a collection of small sounds agreeing to be quiet together — the fridge, the pipes, the sea two streets over. This was the other kind. The kind where the house stops pretending.

I read it again. *I am four months gone.* The words didn't change. That's the trouble with ink; it has no manners. It just sits there being true while you stand over it asking it to be something else.

*I know what Da will say and I know what the town will say and I know what you will say, which is the only one of the three that will cost me anything. But Nuala, I cannot stay in that house and I cannot lose this child, and it has become plain to me that I cannot do both. I have thought about it every way there is and there is no way. So I am choosing the way that lets me walk.*

*The father is married. You know him. I will not write the name because names in this town are a kind of currency and I am done paying.*

I put the letter down. I picked it up again. The tide mark had eaten through part of the next paragraph, and I had to hold the page at an angle to the window light to make out the words beneath the stain, the way you read a watermark on a banknote.

*— will be born in the spring, in a place where no one knows me, and I will give the baby —*

The rest was gone. Eaten. The water had taken the middle of the sentence and left the edges, and I sat there holding the page up to the light like a man divining, trying to read a sentence that no longer existed. *I will give the baby* — give the baby what? Up? A name? Away?

I knew. Of course I knew. You always know; the not-knowing is just a courtesy you extend to yourself. But I sat there anyway, turning the page, as if the missing words might have migrated to the back, might be hiding in the watermark, might be legible if I held it at the right angle to the morning.

*I will not ask you to forgive me. I will only ask you to imagine, for one hour, that you are me, and that the town is the town, and that Da is Da, and then tell me what you would have done differently. I have waited fifty years — no, I have waited four months — for someone to ask me that question, and no one has, and so I am asking it of you, and of the paper, and of whoever finds this, which God forbid is no one.*

*Your sister,*
*Eilish*

*P.S. The child will have a better life. This is the sentence I will build the rest of my life on top of. I am telling you so you know the foundation is a sentence, and to be gentle with it.*

I sat at the kitchen table for a long time. The tea went cold, which is the traditional way to mark the passage of terrible news.

My grandmother had had a baby. Not my father — my father was born in 1962, I'd seen the certificate, I'd framed it, I'd hung it in the hall like the dutiful archivist of a family that had never once thanked me for the framing. This was another child. A child born in the spring of 1971, in a place where no one knew her, and given away. Given *away.* The word sat in the ruined sentence like a stone in a shoe.

Neither of us knew. Maren didn't know. I didn't know. And it wasn't a small thing, was the thing of it. It wasn't a debt or a disgrace or an affair with a married man — though it was that too, and the married man was *someone we knew*, someone whose name Eilish had withheld with the same care she'd withheld everything else. It was a whole person. A person who would now be in their fifties, walking around somewhere, with Eilish's hands or Eilish's temper, and no idea.

The inventory habit is an old one with me. I keep lists of unsaid things the way other men keep receipts — in a notebook, in pencil, dated. *Things not said to Maren: that I heard her crying in the car the night of the funeral. That I know she thinks I chose the house over her. That she's right.* Pages and pages of it, going back years. It's not a virtue. I want to be clear about that. It's not some noble discipline of restraint. It's a hoarding. It's what you do instead of speaking when speaking has been priced out of your reach.

But this — this went on a new page. I got the notebook out of my coat and wrote, in pencil, because some things you don't want to commit to in ink:

*Things Eilish did not say: that there was a fourth child. That she was four months gone when she left. That the father was married, and known to us. That the baby was given away in the spring of 1971. That the foundation of her whole life was one sentence, and she told us nothing of the load it was bearing.*

I looked at what I'd written and felt the awe go out of it, the way heat goes out of a radiator when the system shuts down for the season. Because that's the sequence, I've learned. First there's awe — the vertiginous, almost holy awe of touching something true that was hidden. You feel chosen. You feel like the house itself led you to the wardrobe, loosened the floorboard, left the tin where your hands would find it. The dead were saving it for you.

And then, on the far side of the awe, always, comes the anger. Because the dead didn't save it for you. The dead just didn't say it. There was no ceremony in the silence, no intention, no gift. There was only a woman with a secret and a tin and forty-one stamps, and a lifetime of choosing the drawer over the door.

She'd had fifty years. Fifty years of Christmases, of my father's birthday, of Maren's christening — Maren, who she'd held as a baby, who'd been *named* for her, God help us, Maren Eilish Halloran, carrying her grandmother's name like an heirloom while the grandmother sat on a whole human being she'd never mentioned. Fifty years of Sunday dinners where she'd looked at her grandchildren across the roast and said *pass the potatoes* with a person missing from the table, a person she had made and surrendered, and not one word. Not one.

I understood it. That was the worst part. I understood it the way I understand a house — you can see exactly why the wall is cracked, you can trace the settling, the bad drainage, the corner that catches the wind, and knowing why doesn't fix it. She'd been nineteen, maybe twenty. Pregnant by a married man in a town that would have eaten her. She'd chosen the only exit she could see, and the toll was the child, and she'd paid it, and then she'd spent fifty years not saying so because — and here my pencil stopped — because what would saying it have done? Un-given the baby? Un-broken the town's opinion of her? The silence wasn't pride, or not only pride. The silence was structural. Load-bearing. She'd built her life on a sentence, she'd told us so herself, and you don't go announcing the foundation.

But God, the *cost*. The cost paid forward, down the decades, to all of us. To my father, who died thinking he was the youngest. To Maren, who was named for a woman who was two women and told us she was one. To the child herself, whoever and wherever she was, who had spent fifty years possibly wondering — and I know how that wondering works, I've tuned pianos in enough houses where someone was wondering — why she was the one given away.

I heard Maren on the stairs. Her tread is unmistakable — quick, flat, businesslike, a woman descending toward a task. I had perhaps ten seconds to decide what to do with the letter, and I did what I always do, which is nothing, which is leave it exactly where it was, face-up, on the table, because some part of me — the part that had been hoarding unsaid things for thirty years — was tired of being the only one who carried them.

She came in with the tape gun already in her hand, which was its own kind of message.

"We should do the front room today," she said. "The buyer's agent wants—"

"Maren."

She stopped. She looked at the letter. I watched her read it the way you watch weather come in — the change moving across her face in stages, confusion first, then attention, then something that wasn't confusion or attention anymore.

"She had a baby," Maren said. Not a question. Her voice had gone very level, which is what Maren's voice does instead of shaking.

"In the spring of 1971. She gave it away."

Maren put the tape gun down on the table. Carefully, the way you put down something when your hands aren't steady and you don't want anyone to know. She picked up the letter and read the ruined paragraph, holding it to the light exactly as I had, and I understood that we were the same in this, at least — two people trying to read through a water stain, trying to recover a sentence the water had taken.

"The middle's gone," she said.

"I know."

"*I will give the baby* —" Maren's voice did the thing it never does. It cracked, just at the edge, just once, and then she was level again, leveler than before, the way a floor is flatter after it's been refinished. "There was a person, Orsen. A whole person. And she just —" She set the letter down and looked at the tin, at the other thirty-four letters still unopened, and I saw her do the arithmetic I'd been doing all morning: how many more of these were there, how many more people, how much more family we didn't know we had. "Who was the father?"

"She wouldn't write the name. She said names in that town are a kind of currency."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one she left."

Maren sat down across from me. For a moment neither of us said anything, and the house did its morning quiet around us, and somewhere in the walls a pipe ticked as it warmed, the small honest sound of water going where it's sent.

"I used to think the worst thing she did was leave," Maren said finally. "That was the whole story, wasn't it? Eilish left, and everything after was fallout. That was the story we were *given*." She turned the envelope over in her hands, studying the stamp, the never-cancelled stamp, the fifty-year-old intention. "There was never one story. There were two. There was the one we got and the one that happened, and she kept them in separate rooms her whole life."

"Like the house," I said.

"Like the house," Maren agreed, and for a second we were in agreement about something, which frightened me more than the letter had.

Then she stood up, and picked up the tape gun, and said she'd start on the front room, and asked if I'd be up to help by ten. And I said I would, which was a lie, or would become one, because I sat at that table for another hour with the tin in front of me, not opening letter eight, not packing anything, just writing in my notebook, the pencil moving through the morning.

*Things we do not know: her name. Where she was born. Where she went. Whether she was told anything at all, or whether she, too, was given a story instead of the truth. Whether she is alive. Whether she ever wondered. Whether she ever stood outside a house that might have been hers and didn't knock.*

I closed the notebook. And then I opened it again, because there was one more line, and it had been forming all morning in the place where my anger lived, and it wouldn't be honest to leave it out:

*Things I am angry at the dead for: everything above. And for making me the one who knows now. Knowing is a job. Nobody asked me if I wanted it.*

I was still sitting there when Maren called down from the front room, her voice carefully casual, asking me to come look at something she'd found behind the skirting board — and I knew, before I'd even stood up, before I'd taken the first step toward the stairs, from the particular pitch of her voice, the one the house and I both recognized, that whatever was behind that skirting board was going to change the shape of everything we'd just learned.
