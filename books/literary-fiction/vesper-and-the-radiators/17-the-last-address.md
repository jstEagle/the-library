## 17. The Last Address

**Orsen**

Letter forty-one was the one that refused to make sense.

I had spent three weeks learning the handwriting the way I learn a piano — first the obvious things, the strong downstrokes, the places where Eilish pressed hard enough to emboss the page. Then the subtler failures: the way her *r* collapsed when she was tired, the way her sentences shortened when she was frightened, as if fear made her economical. By letter thirty I could date a page within a season. By letter thirty-eight I could tell you what she'd had for breakfast, roughly, from the tremor in her loops.

Letter forty-one broke all of it.

The address was written in a hand I didn't know — steadier, rounder, someone else's pen, someone else's nerve. And the name was no one. Not Nuala, not Síofra, not any Halloran or Byrne or Doyle from the parish registers Maren had started keeping like a detective's wall of string. The name was *Bríd Nolan*, and the address was Ashgrove House, a care home forty minutes inland, past the turnoff for the coast road everyone in our family had apparently spent fifty years not taking.

"That's not a relative," Maren said. She said it the way you say *that's not a load-bearing wall* — hopefully, right before the ceiling comes down.

"Then why is it in the tin?"

"Everything's in the tin. That's the whole problem with the tin."

We had been through the letters twice now, cross-referencing, building the chronology Eilish never gave us. Letter seven — the water-stained one, the one that had rewritten every Christmas story we'd ever been told — mentioned a baby. *The baby is settled and it is better this way and I will not write of her again, I am telling you I will not, and if you love me you will let me keep that promise to myself.* Eilish had been twenty-three. The letter was dated March 1969. The child, if she lived, would be fifty-six.

Maren had done the arithmetic out loud the night we read it, and then had gone very quiet, and then had said, "She'd be older than me," as if that were the impossible part. Not that Aunt Eilish — tidy, cardiganed, scone-producing Eilish, who had died when I was nineteen smelling of lavender and camphor — had carried and surrendered a child. That the child would be *older than Maren*. Grief does its own bookkeeping.

Now the last letter sat between us on the kitchen table, and neither of us touched it.

"Bríd Nolan," Maren read, though I'd already read it four times. "Nolan was the surname on the adoption paperwork angle, maybe? Síofra said the parish kept records."

"Síofra said the parish kept *everything* and shared *nothing*."

"Well. One of those things is about to change." Maren stood, tucked the letter into her bag with the efficiency of a woman who had once removed a splinter from my hand before I'd finished saying *ow*. "We're driving out tomorrow. Before I lose my nerve."

"You don't lose your nerve. You lose your temper. Different equipment entirely."

"Orsen." She paused at the door, and for a second the briskness dropped off her like a coat she'd forgotten she was wearing. "What if she doesn't want to know?"

"Then she'll tell us," I said. "And it'll be the first honest sentence anyone in this family has said in fifty years, so we should probably be grateful."

* * *

**Maren**

Here is what nobody tells you about care homes in the Irish midlands: they are always in a place with a beautiful name and nothing else. Ashgrove House was at the end of a lane lined with actual ash trees, so at least the sign wasn't lying, but the nearest shop was four miles back and the silence had a texture to it, a felted quality, like the whole landscape had been upholstered for people who no longer needed sharp edges.

I had dressed for it wrong and I knew it. I'd put on the navy blouse, the one that says *I am competent and also sympathetic*, the blouse I wore to bank meetings and to the solicitor and once, memorably, to tell my husband I was taking the spare room. Orsen had worn the same jumper he always wore, the grey one with the cuff he'd half-unravelled and then, characteristically, refused to either fix or fully destroy. It hung from his wrist like a small surrender.

"You could have ironed something," I said.

"You could have not," he said, which was fair.

The woman at reception had the particular kindness of people who work in buildings where everyone is waiting for something. When Orsen explained — and I'll give him this, he explained it well, the letters, the family, the fifty years, without once making it sound like a television drama — she said, "Bríd's having a good day," in a tone that told us there were other kinds of days, and led us down a corridor that smelled of polish and lilies and something underneath both, something older, like a house remembering.

And then there she was, in a chair by a window, and I felt the floor of my understanding go soft.

She had Eilish's hands.

I want to be precise about this, because I am a precise person and precision is the only religion I've kept. It wasn't the face — the face was her own, broader, a face that had done outside work, weather and wind in it. But the hands, folded in her lap, were the hands from the photographs in the hall at home. The same knuckles. The same way the thumb lay over the fingers. I had spent my childhood watching those hands play the piano at Christmas and butter scones and smooth the tablecloth, and here they were, fifty-six years old, in a stranger by a window.

"Hello, Bríd," the receptionist said gently. "These two have brought you something. Would you like them to sit?"

Bríd Nolan looked at us the way you look at weather — assessing, unbothered, already deciding whether to bring in the washing. "Sit, so," she said. "I'm not doing anything."

* * *

**Orsen**

I had rehearsed this. I want to admit that, because everything afterward happened unrehearsed and I think the difference matters. In the car I had a whole opening — *we found a box, we've been delivering letters, we believe you may be* — a little speech, tidy as a set list. Maren had listened to me run through it once and said, "You sound like a man reading a gas meter," which was accurate and unkind and true.

But when I sat down across from her and took out the letter, none of the speech arrived. What arrived instead was the radiator-thing, the habit I don't talk about at parties, which is that I listen to rooms. And Ashgrove's room was saying: *someone has been patient here a long time. Someone has been waiting without knowing what for.*

"Bríd," I said. "My name is Orsen. This is Maren. Eilish Halloran was our grandmother."

Something moved across her face — not recognition, more like the shadow of a door being tested.

"She died a long time ago," Maren said. "But she left letters. Letters she wrote and never sent. And this one —" Orsen's throat did something. I let it. "This one is addressed to you."

Bríd looked at the envelope for a long time. Longer than politeness. Longer than curiosity. The length of time a person looks at something when they are checking it against a wound.

"I was adopted," she said at last, conversationally, as if reporting the price of bread. "Fostered first, then adopted when I was four. The Nolans were good to me. They're both gone now." She turned the envelope over in those hands. "Nobody ever came looking before. I used to think that meant I wasn't worth looking for. Then I decided it meant I was free, and I liked that version better."

"We didn't know about you," Maren said, and her voice cracked on the *know*, and I understood that she wasn't apologizing — she was grieving, in advance, for the version of herself that had existed ten minutes ago. "Until three weeks ago. There was a letter. She wrote about — she wrote that she'd had a baby. In 1969. She said she wouldn't speak of it again."

"She kept that promise," Bríd said. "Fifty-six years. That's a kind of discipline, isn't it? Or a kind of cowardice. I've gone back and forth." She held out her hand. "Well. Give it here. I'm not getting younger."

* * *

**Maren**

She read it the way you'd read a bill, at first — quick, scanning, the face closed. I watched her and thought: *she's going to hand it back. She's going to say thank you and turn to the window and that will be that.* I know that move. I invented that move. It's the move of a person who learned early that the safest response to a gift is to decline it before it can cost anything.

Then she got to the middle, wherever the middle was, and her breathing changed. I know that change too. It's the sound a house makes when a radiator that's been cold for years suddenly, without anyone touching the valve, begins to warm.

Orsen had told me once — back when we were speaking in full sentences, back before the nine years — that he could hear a family's history in the pipes. I'd laughed at him. I'd said, *Orsen, they're radiators, not séances.* But sitting in that upholstered silence in Ashgrove House, watching a stranger read my grandmother's handwriting and slowly come apart and slowly reassemble, I understood that he'd never been talking about plumbing. He'd been talking about this. About how everything a house holds eventually finds its way back into circulation.

"Oh," Bríd said. Just that. *Oh.* Like a woman finding the light switch in a house she thought she knew.

Then, quieter: "She came. Once. When I was — I must have been five, six. There was a woman at the gate. Dark hair, lovely coat. She didn't come in. She gave me a half-crown and told me I had good strong hands." Bríd looked down at her lap, at the hands themselves, as if they might corroborate. "I thought for years she was an angel. The nuns encouraged it. Then I thought I'd made her up." She looked up at us, and her eyes were dry but her voice wasn't. "I didn't make her up."

"No," Orsen said. "That was her. That was exactly her."

"She stood at the gate," Bríd repeated. "Fifty years I've been telling that story and now it turns out the woman at the gate was my mother, and she stood at the *gate*." She laughed — one syllable, sharp as a struck key — and then the tears came anyway, without drama, the way rain starts in the west, just suddenly there, and she let them run while she read the rest of the letter, and none of us said anything, because there is a kind of crying that needs the room to stay quiet the way a tuning fork needs the room to stay quiet.

When she finished, she folded the letter along its original creases, precisely, and held it to her chest.

"Are there others?" she asked. "Letters?"

"Forty," Orsen said. "Forty to her sister, and this one. We've been delivering them. To the people they were meant for."

Bríd nodded slowly. Then she said the thing that rearranged the whole afternoon:

"Then there's someone else who should hear this. Because if Eilish Halloran was my mother, then I've a brother or sister out there who's been walking around all this time not knowing they've got *me*." She smiled, and it was Eilish's smile, the one from the photographs, tilted and private. "And I'd like to see their face when they find out."

* * *

**Orsen**

We drove back in a silence that wasn't empty. That's the only way I can put it. For nine years Maren and I had specialized in silences that were full of everything unsaid — lead-lined silences, silences you could store things in. This one was different. This one was full of something said, something enormous, and we were both just sitting inside it, letting it resonate.

It was Maren who finally spoke, somewhere past the ash trees, eyes on the road.

"Forty letters left to deliver," she said. "To one person. Nuala's dead. Síofra has hers. The collector has —"

She stopped.

The car did not stop. The car continued at a law-abiding fifty kilometers an hour down the N67. But something in me stopped, the way a note stops when you lift the damper pedal — the string still vibrating, but caught.

"The collector has what, Maren?"

The road unspooled. She drove. I watched her hands on the wheel — our grandmother's hands, I understood now, everyone in this family had our grandmother's hands, we were a whole lineage of hands that knew how to hold things and not let go.

"Three," she said. "He has three. I was going to tell you. I *was* going to tell you, Orsen, I had a whole —" and here her voice did something I had never heard it do in forty-odd years of knowing her, which was fail. "I had a whole speech. I've been rehearsing it for two weeks. You'd be amazed how good the speech was."

The radiators of the house were forty miles behind us, but I swear I heard the system change pitch.

"Tell me the speech," I said. "We've got forty minutes."
