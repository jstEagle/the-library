## 20. The Sisters' Letters, Delivered

The cemetery at Kilbrack sits on a rise above the town like a sentence placed carefully at the top of a page. You can see the harbor from it, and the harbor can see you back, which I have always thought was the point. My grandmother Eilish is buried in the third row from the wall, under a granite stone that lists her dates and nothing else — no *beloved mother*, no *resting in peace*, just the arithmetic of her life. Whoever chose that stone made a decision, and I suspect the decision was Nuala's, made by proxy, or possibly by nobody, which is its own kind of verdict.

I had been dreading this morning for eleven days, which is roughly how long it took Maren to stop speaking to me in complete sentences after the fight, and then to start again, haltingly, the way water finds its way back into pipes after a hard freeze. The thaw was not graceful. There were cracks. But on Thursday she stood in the kitchen of the house we now owned together — God help us both — holding the folder of Nuala's letters that Síofra had finally surrendered, and she said, "You should read them to her. Out loud. She waited long enough."

"She's dead," I said.

"Yes," said Maren. "That's never once stopped anyone in this family from having a conversation."

So here I was, standing in a churchyard with a wind off the sea that could strip paint, holding two sisters' correspondence like a man holding live wires, waiting for the other attendees to arrive.

* * *

Síofra came first, as I knew she would. She drove up in a small blue car that sounded like it was clearing its throat, parked with the precision of a woman who had backed trailers into tighter spots than this, and got out wearing a coat the color of wet slate. She was seventy-one now and walked like someone who had spent her life on boats — slightly wide of center, braced for weather.

She carried a thermos and two folding stools, because Síofra did not believe in standing through anything longer than a hymn.

"Your grandmother," she said, by way of greeting, nodding at the headstone, "had terrible handwriting. Did you know that? Like a spider drowned in ink."

"I did know that," I said. "I've been reading it for months."

"Then you've earned your sainthood." She set up the stools facing the grave, then turned one slightly toward me, so we'd be angled together rather than opposite, which I understood to be a courtesy and also a strategy: whatever happened next, we would face it side-on, like people watching a storm come in.

The half-sister arrived twenty minutes later, in a taxi from the care home, and seeing her walk up that grassy slope toward us did something to my chest that I am still not prepared to describe accurately. Her name was Deirdre. She was fifty-three. She wore a cardigan over her shoulders despite the wind and moved slowly, one hand on the arm of the driver who had insisted on accompanying her, and when she reached us she looked at the headstone for a long time before looking at either of us.

"So this is her," Deirdre said. Not a question. "This is where she ended up."

"This is where they put her," Síofra corrected gently. "Where she ended up is still under negotiation."

Deirdre laughed — a short, startled sound, as if laughter had been rationed to her early in life and she was still making it last — and something in the sound made Síofra reach out and take her arm, and Deirdre let her, and I stood there with forty-one letters in a canvas bag and thought: *this* is what the house has been humming about all along. Not grief. Assembly.

Maren arrived last, of course. She parked badly, which for Maren was a form of prayer, and came up the hill carrying flowers she had clearly bought at the petrol station — carnations dyed an orange that does not occur in nature — and thrust them at me without comment. I put them in the stone vase bolted beside the grave, because even a hostile grave deserves water, and arranged them as well as orange carnations can be arranged, which is to say: present.

"Are we doing this or are we admiring the view?" Maren asked.

"We're doing this," I said.

* * *

Here is what I had decided, in the sleepless arithmetic of the past week: I would not read all of them. Forty-one letters from Eilish, nineteen from Nuala — sixty documents of a fifty-year silence, and no congregation alive could stand for the full liturgy. Instead I had chosen seven. Three of Eilish's, four of Nuala's, alternating, so that the exchange would feel like an exchange, which was the entire scandal of it: two women writing to each other across forty miles and never posting a word, each one composing replies to letters that existed only in their own kitchens.

Two sisters, each waiting for the other to apologize first. Pride as inherited architecture. I had lived in that building myself for nine years; I recognized the floor plan.

I took out the first letter and my hands were not steady, and I noticed that Síofra saw, and did not comment, and passed me the thermos cap full of coffee instead, which is the Irish sacrament for trembling.

"Eilish Halloran," I read, "writing to her sister Nuala, March 1968. Never sent."

"'Nuala,'" I began, and stopped, because hearing my grandmother's voice come out of my mouth in a churchyard, in front of the sister she wronged and the daughter she gave away, was like playing a piano that hasn't been tuned — every note true and unbearable at once. But there was nothing for it except to keep going, so I kept going.

"'I have started this letter eleven times. I have burned four of them in the stove and hidden the rest under the lining paper in the wardrobe, which I know you will think is madness, but I cannot send them until I know what it is I am actually apologizing for, and I begin to suspect I will die not knowing, so here is my best attempt: I am sorry for leaving. I am sorry for the way I left. I am sorry that I let Da tell the town his version before I told you mine, though in fairness to him, I never gave anyone a version at all. Silence is a door and everyone walks through it and hangs their own coat.'"

The wind moved through the yew trees. Deirdre had gone very still.

"'You will want to know why I went,' the letter continued. 'The truth is I was frightened, and the truth is I was angry, and the truth is those are the same feeling wearing different coats. I will write again when I am braver. Your sister, Eilish.' And then, underneath, smaller: 'P.S. Tell the boy his dog is with Mrs. Feeney. He'll know which boy.'"

"That was our father," Síofra said quietly. "She remembered the dog." She pressed her knuckles to her mouth for a moment, then lowered them. "Go on."

* * *

I read Nuala's reply next — the reply that never traveled, dated April 1968, found behind the same wardrobe in a different tin, because apparently the sisters had shared not only a childhood but a filing system.

"'Eilish,'" I read. "'I received your letter, which is to say I wrote it for you, which is to say I have been answering you in my head for six years and it is a relief to finally get some of it down on paper even if the paper goes nowhere. You ask what you are apologizing for. Here is my list. One: the business. Two: Christmas 1961. Three: you know very well what happened at Christmas 1961. Four: I forgive you for all three, but I wanted it written down somewhere that I said it first, because if I say it out loud you will argue with me, and I am too old to argue with you and win.'"

Maren made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth.

"'I do not forgive you for leaving without telling me,' the letter went on. 'That one I am keeping. A woman is entitled to keep one thing. — Nuala.'"

"One thing," Síofra repeated. She shook her head slowly. "She kept it her whole life. She'd bring it out at Christmas like the good china. *And another thing your grandmother never—*" She caught herself, smiled at the grave. "Sorry, Mam. Old habits."

"Don't apologize," Deirdre said. It was the first thing she'd said since the letter began. "Keep the habit. Somebody should."

* * *

The middle letters were harder. I had chosen them deliberately, which felt like a betrayal of the dead and a service to the living simultaneously, and I have made peace with the fact that those are usually the same act.

I read Eilish's letter from November 1969 — the one that mentioned the pregnancy in the oblique, terrified grammar of a woman writing around the largest fact of her life. I did not read the whole thing. Some sentences belong to the person they're about, and Deirdre was standing right there, hearing her origin described in her mother's handwriting for perhaps the fourth time ever, and I watched her hold herself the way you hold a hot cup: carefully, with both hands.

But I read the ending, because the ending was for everyone:

"'I did not give her away,' Eilish wrote. 'I want that written down somewhere, even if the somewhere is a drawer. I gave her to a family that could feed her, which is not the same thing, and I have spent every day since learning the difference, and I will spend every remaining day paying for the confusion. If you ever meet her, Nuala — and I pray you do, and I pray you don't, because I don't deserve to be forgiven twice in one lifetime — tell her the stove was always warm. Tell her I thought of her every time I tuned the piano, which was every day, because a piano out of tune is a lie you can hear.'"

I closed that letter and looked at Deirdre, and Deirdre looked at the headstone, and after a while she said, "She was right about the piano. That's exactly the kind of thing she'd say." And then she sat down on Síofra's spare stool and put her face in her hands, and Síofra put her hand on her back, and neither of them explained themselves, and none of us required it.

* * *

There is a particular quality to reading aloud in a graveyard. The acoustics are honest. There's nowhere for the voice to hide — no soft furnishings, no sympathetic walls — just grass and granite and open sky, so everything you say arrives exactly as pitched. I have tuned pianos in worse rooms. I have tuned pianos in better ones. But I have never played to an audience like this: one sister dead, one sister's daughter, one daughter given away, one granddaughter who flew nine hundred miles to buy flowers at a petrol station, and me, the tuner, the keeper of inventories, finally reading the ledger out loud.

Because that's what it was, I realized somewhere around the fifth letter. An inventory. Sixty items, entered over fifty years, never reconciled. Every accountant knows the books must balance eventually or the whole enterprise is fiction. My grandmother and great-aunt had run a double-entry system of grievance and forgiveness, each keeping her column private, each assuming the other's column was all debt. They died solvent and unaware of it. All that was left was for someone to read the entries aloud and close the accounts.

So I read Nuala's letter from 1970, the furious one, the one where she called Eilish a coward in three languages and then, in the postscript, asked after Eilish's health in a fourth. I read Eilish's reply from 1971, the last one, the one that simply said: *"I hear you kept the one thing. Keep it. I'll keep mine too. We'll be even in the ground."*

They were even in the ground. They had been even in the ground for decades, lying in cemeteries forty miles apart, balanced to the penny, and nobody had done the audit.

"They never posted them," Síofra said, when I finished. "All that. Fifty years. And neither of them ever posted a single one."

"No," I said.

"Do you know why?"

I thought about it. I thought about the wardrobe, and the lining paper, and the tin box with its rusted lid, and the way a house holds what a family won't. I thought about Maren's nine-year absence and my nine-year inventory and the phone calls we didn't make and the letters we didn't write and the apology I rehearsed in the shower for most of a decade and never delivered.

"Because posting it means finding out you were wrong," I said. "As long as the letter's in the drawer, you might be right. The drawer keeps the possibility open. It's the cruelest piece of furniture in any house."

Síofra looked at me for a long moment. Then she looked at Maren, and Maren looked at the harbor, and the harbor looked back, impartial as ever.

"Well," Síofra said. "Thank God somebody finally audited."

* * *

We stayed until the light went long and gold — vesper light, though I didn't have the word for it yet; I wouldn't find it until later, in the front room, at the piano — and the cold came up through the ground and informed us that the dead were done receiving visitors. Síofra folded the stools. Deirdre touched the headstone once, briefly, the way you touch a doorframe you're not sure you're allowed to lean on. Maren stood at the edge of the plot with her hands in her pockets and said, quietly, to the granite: "You could have just posted them, you impossible woman." Which was, I think, the closest thing to a eulogy anyone in our family has ever managed, and Eilish would have hated it and kept it forever.

At the car, Síofra stopped me with a hand on my sleeve.

"There's one more thing," she said. "When I cleared out Mam's house in the spring, there was a box in the attic. Photographs, mostly. But there was a piano tuning fork in it. Stamped with a name — Halloran & Son. I assumed it was yours."

"It's not mine," I said. "Ours broke years ago. Or—" I stopped. Ours hadn't broken. Ours had gone missing, sometime in the nineties, and Aunt Eilish had said not to worry about it, and I hadn't, because I was nineteen and worried about other things.

Síofra held it out. A tuning fork, dark with age, the stamp worn but legible. *Halloran & Son.* The year stamped beneath it was 1948.

"She kept it fifty years," Síofra said. "I don't know what that means. But you tune things. Maybe you do."

I took it. It was heavier than it looked, the way everything in this story had turned out to be.

"I'll strike it tonight," I said. "Against the radiators. See what pitch the house has settled on."

"And if it's changed?"

I looked back up the hill at the headstone, at the orange carnations defying botany in their vase, at the two women walking down toward the harbor road, shoulder to shoulder against the wind.

"Then the books balance," I said, "and it's time to tune to something new."

That night, back in the emptying house, I struck the fork against the kitchen radiator and listened. The note that came back was one I had never heard the pipes sing before — low, warm, resolved, like a chord finally allowed to finish. I got out my inventory book, turned past all the pages titled *Unsaid*, and opened a fresh one.

I wrote the date at the top. Then I sat there, pen hovering, trying to decide what the new page should be called — and that was when I heard Maren's key in the front door, earlier than she'd said, carrying something under her arm wrapped in a tea towel, and I understood that whatever we named this next chapter, we were going to name it together.
