## 3. The Question Arranged Around

### Ada

The invitation went out the way all our invitations did: as a fait accompli.

*I've asked Dr. Okafor to adjust Mother's medication schedule,* I wrote to my brother and sister. *She has lucid evenings now. Real ones. If we're going to do this, it should be while she can still be present for it.*

Cormac replied within four minutes — he always replied fast when he wanted you to think he hadn't agonized — with a single word: *Fine.* Junie took two days and then sent a photograph of a casserole with no message at all, which in Junie's language meant *yes, and I am frightened, and I will feed us so we don't have to look at each other.*

So on a Thursday in late October we converged on Vesper's room at Fernwood House, three adults in their thirties carrying dishes like peace offerings to a war none of us would admit had been fought.

I had spent five years as a night librarian learning how silence is built. It isn't built from nothing. It's built from small load-bearing refusals — the question swallowed at the dinner table, the name never said, the room everyone walks around. Our family was an architecture of that. We were, I had always believed, very good at it.

I was about to learn what the building had been holding up.

### Cormac

Here is what nobody tells you about being the oldest: you spend your whole life believing you're the wall, and then one evening you find out you were the thing the wall was hiding.

I brought the good wine, because if we were going to commit familial arson I refused to do it with a Merlot that tasted like pencil shavings. Ada met me in the corridor outside Mother's room, and my little sister — who once waited out a full year of my marriage failing without asking me a single direct question, who is the most fluent not-asker I have ever known — looked me in the eye and said, "Before we go in. Tell me your piece."

"My piece," I said.

"You have one. Everyone does. Junie's been rehearsing hers in the car park for twenty minutes; I watched her mouth moving at the windshield."

"That's just how she prays."

"Cormac." Ada's voice didn't rise. That was the terrifying thing about Ada; her voice never rose, it *descended*, like water finding the crack. "A man at the funeral called her Mrs. Aldous. Nobody uses that name. She got letters under that name. You knew something about the letters."

I set down the wine because my hands had opinions of their own.

"I knew," I said, "that there was a night in 1994 that we do not talk about. And I knew that after it, Dad sat me down — I was sixteen — and told me that whatever I remembered about that night, I should remember it wrong. On purpose. He said it was the kindest thing anyone would ever ask of me." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "And I've been remembering it wrong for thirty years, Ada. So well that I don't know anymore which parts are mine."

Ada went very still, the way she does at the reference desk when someone asks her a question the catalogue can't answer.

"Then we'll ask her," she said. "While she still knows the answer."

### Junie

I burned the casserole. Not badly — badly would have been a mercy, an excuse to order pizza — but enough that the smell of it followed me down the corridor like a small accusation. I am forty-one years old and I cannot bring a dish to my mother's care home without burning it, because my hands shake when I'm frightened, and I was frightened, and everyone was going to pretend otherwise because pretending otherwise is our whole religion.

Mother was sitting up when we came in, and she was *luminous*. That's the only word. The fog that lived behind her eyes had drawn back like a tide, and she saw us — really saw us, all three at once, which hadn't happened since before the diagnosis — and she clapped her hands together like a girl.

"There you are," she said. "All my children. Sit, sit. Junie, love, you've brought food, you're an angel." Then she looked at Ada, and her face did something I will be turning over for the rest of my life. It softened into a grief so specific, so *addressed*, that I felt like a trespasser watching it.

"Oh, my darling," she said to Ada. "You came back. After everything, you came back."

"I'm here, Mum," Ada said carefully.

"Not Ada," Mother said, patting the bed beside her. "Liesl. Liesl, sit. We have to talk about the house. About what happened to the house."

### Ada

The name went through me like cold water through cloth.

*Liesl.* I had found it in the letters — *my Liesl, my Liesl, come home* — in handwriting that shook and then steadied and then shook again. I had assumed Liesl was a friend from the other life, the stranger's life, the Aldous life. A woman Mother had loved and lost somewhere in the years before us, or alongside us, in the second ledger of her existence.

But Mother wasn't reaching for the past. She was looking directly at me. At my face, my actual face, and calling it Liesl's.

"Tell me the truth," Mother said, gripping my wrist with a strength the illness had been saving up. "Did they ever find out it was you?"

The room went silent in the way our rooms had always gone silent — but this time I heard its architecture, every beam of it, and understood that we had built it around this exact moment without knowing.

"Mum," I said. "What was me?"

Junie made a sound like a hinge. Cormac set his glass down too hard. And Mother — lucid, oh God, lucid as noon — looked at me with such tenderness and such terror that I finally understood I had been fluent in the wrong language my entire life. All those years of not-asking. All that skill. And the question had been waiting behind my own face the whole time.

"What did we survive?" I asked aloud. My voice cracked on *survive* like ice on a pond. "Mum. What did it cost?"

### Cormac

She told it plainly, which was the worst part. No drama left in it after thirty years of erosion — just the facts, worn smooth as river stones.

The house on Aldous Street. October 1994. Ada was seven. There had been a fire — not arson, not tragedy in the cinematic sense, a stupid electrical fire, the kind that takes houses and occasionally takes more. Ada had been pulled out by a neighbor. Mother had gone back in — everyone agreed on this — gone back in for Ada, who was already out on the lawn, and stood in the smoke calling a name that wasn't Ada's.

Because in the smoke, concussed by a falling beam, she had become someone else. Or rather, she had become *only* someone else — the self she'd been before us, the Aldous wife, the woman with the daughter named Liesl. The blow took our mother and left the earlier woman standing in the wreckage, asking strangers where her child was.

"They told me it was the smoke," Mother said, looking at Ada-Liesl, at both of them at once. "That it would clear. Your father said — he said if we gave her time, gave her quiet, no shocks, no questions—" Her voice frayed. "He built the silence around you, Ada. Around all of you. Because the doctors said the memories might return, and if they returned and the first thing she learned was that her real daughter had nearly died in that fire—"

"She'd break properly," Junie whispered. "Not just forget. Break."

"So we never spoke of it," Mother said. "For thirty years I let you all believe I was simply private. I was a bomb with the pin held in by a family." She laughed, wet and terrible. "And now the illness is doing what the fire started, and I find I'd rather tell you than protect you. Isn't that a thing? Thirty years of silence and the dementia talks more than I ever did."

### Junie

Nobody moved for a long time. The casserole went cold on the sideboard, smelling of its own small failure, and I thought: *we arranged our whole lives around protecting Ada from a truth, and the truth is that we protected ourselves too.* Cormac got to be the steady eldest instead of the boy who'd been ordered to misremember. I got to be the easy one instead of the eight-year-old who'd seen Mum walk back into the smoke. Even Mum got a version of herself she could live inside.

Only Ada had been kept outside the arrangement entirely. Only Ada had spent thirty years thinking the silence was about *her* — her fault somehow, her weight to carry — when it had been built to hold her up.

"Mum," Ada said at last, and her voice was wrecked and level at once, "the letters. The Aldous letters. Who wrote them?"

Mother's face changed again — and this time it wasn't confusion, and it wasn't grief.

It was guilt.

"Liesl writes them," she said. "Every week. She's never stopped writing to me, all these years. She thinks I'm dead, you see. Someone told her I died in the fire." She turned to Ada, urgent, gripping her hand. "You have to tell her I'm not. You have to find her and tell her I'm alive. Promise me, darling. Promise me you'll find Liesl."

* * *

We walked out into the car park in the blue dark, three people wearing the same family like clothes that no longer fit. Junie sat down on the low wall and put her head in her hands. Cormac stared at nothing.

Ada stood apart from us both, phone already lit in her hand, the letters' return address glowing on the screen — an address she had carried for weeks without understanding why.

"It's forty minutes away," she said quietly. "She's been forty minutes away this whole time."

She looked up at us, and my little sister — the keeper of silences, the librarian of everything unsaid — was already putting on her coat.

"I'm going tonight," Ada said. "Somebody has to stop being quiet first."
