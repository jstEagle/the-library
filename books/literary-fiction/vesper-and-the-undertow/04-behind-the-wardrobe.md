## 4. Behind the Wardrobe

The wardrobe came out of the wall on a Tuesday, and Priya would remember that later — how ordinary the day was before it wasn't.

They'd been at the site since seven. Tom had the kettle going on a camp stove in what used to be the kitchen, and Priya was working through her list with a pencil stub behind her ear: survey the rear boundary, photograph the drainage run, mark the two stumps the tree surgeon hadn't touched. The lot smelled of rain and char and something green underneath it all, something pushing up through the ash like it had been waiting twenty years for permission.

"Tea," Tom called, and she held up one finger, because she'd reached the wardrobe.

It stood against the back wall of what had been Vesper Thackeray's bedroom — they knew that now, from the council records and from Mrs. Okonkwo down the lane, who talked to Priya over the fence every time she came out to weed. The fire had taken everything around it. The roof was gone, the floor was gone, the walls were gone down to knee height, and yet the wardrobe stood upright in its corner like a man who'd refused to leave a sinking ship. Oak, dark as wet earth, its varnish blistered but intact. A keyhole without a key. Carved along the top rail, someone had done a running vine pattern, worn almost smooth by hands opening and closing it across decades.

"It's beautiful," Priya said, when Tom came to stand beside her.

"It's four hundred pounds of beautiful," he said. "And it's standing where our stairs go."

She ran her palm along the vine. "We could keep it."

"We could keep a photo of it." He said it gently. He'd learned, in the three months since they'd bought the plot, to say things gently to her about this place. She loved him for it and resented it in equal measure, which she suspected was what marriage was.

* * *

Moving it took the whole morning. They couldn't lift it — the thing was built like a coffin for a giant — so they walked it, tilting it onto its edges and pivoting, inch by inch, away from the wall, out into the open ground where the light hit it properly for the first time in who knew how long. Priya kept expecting it to fall apart. It didn't. It moved with the stubbornness of something that intended to survive.

When they finally set it down and turned back to the wall it had guarded, there was a gap in the plaster.

Not a gap exactly. The fire had stripped the wall to brick and lath, and behind where the wardrobe's back panel had pressed flush, a section of lath had come loose — or been loosened, once, by human hands. Priya crouched. The cavity between the studs went back further than it should have, deeper than a wall needed to be, and inside it, wedged tight, wrapped in a plastic bag gone brittle as autumn leaves, was a tin.

"What is it?" Tom called from the kettle.

"I don't know."

She worked it free with both hands. It was a biscuit tin, the old kind, rectangular, with a faded picture of sailing boats on the lid. Rust had bloomed along the seams in orange continents. It was heavier than an empty tin should be, lighter than tools. When she shook it, very gently, it made the sound paper makes — a soft, dense whisper, like wind through reeds.

Tom came over wiping his hands on his jeans. He looked at the tin for a long moment, and Priya watched his face do the thing it did when he was deciding whether to be practical or kind, and saw him lose the argument with himself.

"Open it," he said.

* * *

Inside: letters. Dozens of them. Bundled loosely with rubber bands that had perished into sticky threads, packed in order — she could tell they were in order, somehow, the way you can tell a queue has been standing a long time. Each envelope was addressed in the same hand, a small, careful, slightly old-fashioned script:

**Vesper Thackeray, 14 Gullwing Lane.**

Priya sat down on an upturned bucket. The morning had gone strange around her. Every envelope bore a stamp. Not one of them bore a postmark.

"They were never sent," she said.

Tom leaned over her shoulder. "Maybe they're drafts. Maybe he kept copies."

"No." She turned the top envelope over. The stamp was stuck down properly, licked and pressed, the gum long dried. The flap was sealed. "These are finished. These are ready. He just—" She stopped, because she didn't know how to finish the sentence, and the not-knowing felt enormous.

There were more than forty. She counted them twice, not touching them more than she had to, feeling suddenly like an intruder in a way she hadn't felt even when they'd first walked the burned rooms. Forty-odd sealed letters, addressed to a dead woman, hidden in a wall behind a wardrobe that had stood through a fire. Someone had put them here. Someone had climbed — no, not climbed; the wardrobe backed right up against the cavity, you'd only need to slide it forward six inches — someone had slid the wardrobe aside, tucked the tin into the wall, and pushed the wardrobe back. And then left them there. For years. Through the demolition surveys, the sale, the permits. Through Priya and Tom signing their names to this ground.

"Vesper Thackeray," Tom read aloud. "That's the sister. The one who died." He'd done the research early on; he liked knowing the history of a place before changing it. "So these are from—"

"The brother. Oscar." Mrs. Okonkwo had told her about Oscar too, in the way neighbors tell things, sideways and incomplete: *the brother lived in the city, never came back, not once, not even for the anniversary.* Priya had filed it away as grief. Now it looked different. Now it looked like a correspondence conducted entirely in one direction, with a wall for a postbox.

"We should hand them in," Tom said. "Council. Or the solicitor handling the estate, if there is one. It's not really ours, is it? It's evidence, sort of. Or an archive. Something."

"Yes," Priya said.

But she was already holding the earliest letter — the bottom of the pile, the oldest rubber band, postmarkless and patient — and reading the date in the corner. Twenty years ago. Two weeks after the fire.

Two weeks after the fire, someone had written to Vesper Thackeray, stamped the envelope, sealed it, and hidden it in her bedroom wall.

"Priya." Tom's voice had gone careful again. "You're doing the face."

"What face?"

"The face where you've already decided something and are waiting for me to catch up."

She laughed, but it came out wrong. She looked down at the tin in her lap, at the rust and the boats and the whisper of all those unsaid things, and the wonder she'd felt a moment ago — the pure storybook thrill of finding treasure in a wall — curdled slowly into something else. Because treasure was meant to be found. That was the point of treasure. But whoever hid this had hidden it *well*, behind furniture, inside a wall, in a house that then burned to the ground around it, and if Priya and Tom hadn't needed those four hundred pounds of oak out of the way of their staircase, it would have gone into a skip with the rubble and the landfill and nobody would ever have known. This wasn't a message waiting to be discovered. This was a message buried alive.

Except — and here was the part that made her skin prickle — except that it *hadn't* been buried well enough to be safe. Plastic bag or no, a wall cavity in a coastal town was damp. Twenty years of salt air. The tin had rusted through at one corner; she could see the edge of the bottom letter gone brown and soft. Whoever hid these letters must have known, on some level, that time would get in eventually. That the sea would read them first, if nothing else did.

Or maybe they hadn't thought about it at all. Maybe hiding them had been the whole act — the sealing, the stamping, the sliding of the wardrobe — and the future hadn't entered into it. Priya knew that kind of act. She had a glovebox full of it.

"Tom," she said. "I want to find him."

He didn't sigh, which she appreciated. He sat down on the bucket beside her, so their shoulders touched, and looked out at the lot — the low walls, the stumps, the wardrobe standing in the open light with its vines worn smooth. Somewhere down the lane a gull was making its case about something.

"The council will take weeks," he said. "The solicitor might not exist anymore. And meanwhile these are rotting in a biscuit tin." He rubbed his jaw. "You're not wrong. I just want to say, for the record, that I know how this goes. You find the writer. You knock on his door. And then we're in it. Whatever this is, we're in it."

"I know."

"He might be—" Tom gestured vaguely at the tin, at the whole impossible arithmetic of it. "Forty letters to a dead woman, Priya. He might not be well."

"He might be the most well person either of us has ever met," Priya said. "He might be the only person honest enough to write forty letters he knows he'll never send. I don't know. That's why I want to find him." She looked at the top envelope again, at the small careful script, and felt the strangest sensation: that she was being written to. That the letters had been addressed to the house, and she was the house now.

Tom was quiet for a while. Then he took out his phone, photographed the tin from four angles — he documented everything; it was why their projects ran on time — and said, "Thackeray's not a common name. If he's alive and findable, we'll find him."

"And until then?"

"Until then," he said, "we don't read them."

"No," Priya agreed. "Of course not."

* * *

They didn't read them. That was the plan, and for most of the afternoon it held. They finished the boundary survey. Tom marked the stumps. Priya put the letters back in the tin, in order, and put the tin in the boot of the car, under a blanket, where it rode home through the dark like a passenger.

But at the flat, carrying it up the stairs, she felt the weight shift — the rusted corner, the softened letter at the bottom — and understood that *not reading them* was also a decision, and possibly a worse one. Paper didn't wait politely. Paper rotted. If the bottom letter was already going brown, then every month they spent being respectful was a month the earliest words — maybe the truest ones, maybe the ones written closest to the fire — dissolved a little further toward nothing.

She set the tin on the kitchen table. Tom looked at it, then at her, and got two glasses and the good bottle of red, because he was Tom.

"One," he said. "If we check that the bottom one isn't disintegrating — purely for conservation purposes — that's not reading. That's triage."

"That's exactly the kind of lie people like us tell," Priya said, and opened the bottle instead.

Later, when Tom had gone to bed, she stayed at the table with the last of the wine and the tin in front of her, and she did not open it, and the not-opening filled the room like a third person. She thought about her father's handwriting, which she hadn't seen in nine years and could still draw from memory — the way his *r*s leaned like men walking into wind. She thought about the envelope in her glovebox, going soft at the creases, and how she told herself every year that she was leaving it unopened out of anger, when the truth was closer to fear: that the letter might say something she'd have to answer.

Whoever wrote these forty letters had answered nothing for twenty years. Had built a whole life of answering nothing. And then, at some point, had carried the answers here and hidden them in a wall — which was either surrender or the opposite of surrender, and Priya couldn't tell which, and wanted desperately to know.

She went to bed. She lay awake listening to the sea, which sounded from this distance like a crowd leaving a stadium.

In the morning, over toast, Tom slid his phone across the table. On the screen was a search result, one line long, from a small-town community newsletter, four years old: a photograph of a man in a raincoat at a bus stop, caught mid-turn, half his face blurred by motion. The caption read: *Oscar Thackeray, visiting family graves.*

"Family graves," Priya said. "Plural."

"There's a cemetery on the headland road," Tom said. "I checked. There's a Thackeray plot. Parents, I'd guess. No Vesper — she'll be in the churchyard by the harbor, Mrs. Okonkwo mentioned it once."

Priya zoomed in on the blurred half-face. Raincoat, gray at the temples, shoulders hunched like a man perpetually braced against weather. There was no address, no city, nothing. Just the fact of him: alive, four years ago, ninety minutes from their kitchen table, standing at a bus stop in the rain.

"So we write to him," she said. "Care of the newsletter. Care of the cemetery. We write and say: we found your box. We have your letters. They're safe. Would you like them back?"

Tom nodded slowly. "And if he doesn't answer?"

Priya looked at the tin on the table, at the rusted corner, at the boats sailing their faded sea.

"Then we'll know," she said, "exactly how he's spent twenty years. And we'll decide what to do with a box full of words nobody was ever supposed to hear."

She got a pen. She got a card. And she found, to her complete lack of surprise, that her hand hovered over the blank space for a long, long time — because it turned out that writing to a stranger about his own unsent letters required a skill neither of them had practiced: saying the true thing, plainly, in the first line.

She started four times. On the fifth, she wrote: *Dear Mr. Thackeray. We found your box.*
