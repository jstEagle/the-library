## 20. The Garden

Priya stood at the edge of the bare lot with the revised plans rolled under her arm and the wind pushing at her collar, and she thought: this is what an argument looks like after it's been won by nobody.

The demolition had taken four days. The wardrobe had gone last, on a flatbed, with Oscar watching from the sea wall like a man seeing off a ship. Tom had shaken his hand afterward — actually shaken it, both hands, the way you greet someone at a wedding — and Priya had cried a little, stupidly, into her sleeve, and then the lot had just been a lot. Dirt and foundation scars and one stubborn line of old garden wall along the north edge that the excavator couldn't quite bring itself to touch, or that Tom hadn't asked it to.

For two weeks the plans had sat on their kitchen table in their current form: the build pushed forward, the frontage running hard to the pavement, maximum footprint, minimum sentiment. That was Tom's version. It was a good version. It was the version they'd bought the plot for.

And every morning Priya looked at it and saw the strip of land along the north wall — six metres wide, twenty long, useless for parking, awkward for anything — sitting there in the drawings like a held breath.

"We give it away," she said, over eggs, three weeks after the walls came down.

Tom didn't look up. "Give what away."

"The frontage strip. The bit along the old wall. We make it a garden. Public. A little gate, a bench, something that grows." She turned the plans toward him and laid her pencil across the strip like a fence going up. "We keep everything else. We lose maybe two parking spaces' worth of value and we gain—" She stopped, because she didn't have the word yet.

Tom studied it for a long time. He was better than people thought at this, at letting silence do work. "A garden needs a gardener," he said finally. "Neither of us knows anything about plants."

"Mrs. Okonkwo does."

That got him. He smiled despite himself, the way he always did when Mrs. Okonkwo came up, because the woman had adopted them within a month of moving in, arriving with a tin of shortbread and a complete dossier of the street's history delivered in a voice like warm honey poured over iron.

"And a name," Priya said. "It'll need a name."

Tom put down his fork. He looked at the pencil lying across the strip of ground, and then out the window, toward the sea he could just see between the roofs, the grey glitter of it, the undertow going out and coming back.

"Vesper's," he said.

She'd been waiting for him to say it, and it still landed like a bell being struck.

* * *

Oscar heard about it from Mrs. Okonkwo, who heard about it from the postman, who heard about it from Tom, which meant the whole town knew before Oscar did, which was how things worked here and always had.

"A garden," Mrs. Okonkwo said, standing in his doorway with a bag of seed potatoes she had no reason to be carrying and every intention of leaving behind. "Along the old wall. With Vesper's name on it."

Oscar held the door. He had gotten better, these past weeks, at standing in doorways without gripping the frames.

"They asked me," she went on, "what to plant. And I told them rosemary. Because your sister—" She paused, and he watched her decide not to soften it, which was the thing he loved about her, if love was the word; she fed people and told them true things and called both of those duties. "Because Vesper loved the smell. Used to come past my kitchen window on summer evenings when I had it growing and stop dead and say, *Mrs. Okonkwo, your whole house smells like a memory I haven't had yet.*"

He laughed. It came out of him sideways, surprised, and Mrs. Okonkwo nodded as if to say, yes, that's the one, that's the sound we've been waiting twenty years for.

"She said that?"

"Word for word. That girl collected smells the way other girls collected records." She pushed the potatoes into his hands. "You'll grow these. Don't argue. A man needs something in the ground that answers back."

After she left he stood in his kitchen holding the bag, and the light came through the window the way it did in late afternoon, low and gold and slightly accusing, and he thought about the strip of ground along the old wall where the hydrangeas used to go wild, where Vesper had once buried a time capsule that was probably a jam jar containing two marbles and a lie about a boy named Callum.

They were putting her name on it. In letters. On a sign. Where strangers would read it and say it aloud, *Vesper's Garden*, and she would become a place instead of a wound.

He sat down at the table — the table, the one where seven thousand postcards had begun their impossible journey to a burned house — and pulled a sheet of paper toward him, and wrote a cheque. Then he sat with it for a long time, because a cheque has a name on it, and his name was a thing he had spent twenty years keeping out of ink.

In the end he wrote to the bank, and the bank wrote to a solicitor, and the solicitor wrote to Tom and Priya's project account, and by Thursday there was a donation in the fund for the garden from something called *The Gullwing Trust*, amount generous, origin untraceable.

Priya read the letter twice, looked at the return address, which was a PO box, and smiled at nothing.

"He thinks we don't know," she said.

"Do we know?" Tom asked.

"We know."

* * *

The planting happened on a Saturday in pale, undecided weather, the kind of sky that couldn't commit.

Oscar came down early, before anyone else, because he wanted to see the ground first. Just the ground. The strip along the old wall had been turned over and raked, the soil dark and wet-smelling, and someone — Priya, he guessed, from the careful spacing — had marked out beds with string and pegs. At the far end, against the surviving wall, a bench. Simple, oak, facing the sea.

He stood there a while with his hands in his pockets. Twenty years ago this had been a side garden full of Vesper's chaos: herbs gone leggy, a washing line strung with dresses, a bicycle rusting into the hydrangeas because she'd decided walking was more honest. He had forgotten the bicycle until right now, standing on the exact spot where its wheels had been, and the forgetting and the remembering arrived together like a tide meeting itself.

"You're early," Priya said, coming through the gate they'd hung the week before — a proper gate, painted green, that swung without squeaking because Tom had oiled it three times out of some private devotion.

"So are you."

"I never left, basically. I slept in the car Tuesday night. Don't tell Tom." She came to stand beside him and looked at the turned earth the way she looked at everything now, he'd noticed — as if the ground might open and hand her something. "The sign comes today. Tom's collecting it at ten. He's been rehearsing how to carry it so it doesn't get scratched. He's very worried about the sign."

"It's a good sign?"

"It's the best sign. Oak again. The lettering's done by hand — there's a woman up the coast who does it, she wouldn't take payment once she heard whose name it was. Said she remembered Vesper from school. Said Vesper once talked her out of dropping art." Priya glanced at him. "Everyone remembers her. Did you know that? Everyone has one story and they all keep them like coins."

"I'm starting to," Oscar said. "I spent twenty years thinking I was the only one who kept her. Turns out the whole town had a drawer."

People arrived the way people arrive in small towns: gradually and then all at once. Mrs. Okonkwo with trays of rosemary cuttings wrapped in damp newspaper, enough to hedge the entire wall. The retired fire captain, Mr. Aldous, in a cardigan, carrying a bag of compost and refusing all help with it. Neighbors Oscar half-recognized, faces rearranged by two decades but eyes the same. A woman who introduced herself only as *Deirdre, I was in the choir with her* and then pressed a small paper packet into Oscar's hand — sweet pea seeds — and walked away before he could manage more than a nod.

By eleven the strip was a working site of the gentlest kind. Kneeling figures. Trowels. Mrs. Okonkwo directing operations with the serene tyranny of a woman who had raised six children and forty gardens. Oscar knelt where he was pointed and pressed rosemary cuttings into the earth, and the smell came up off his hands, sharp and resinous and utterly specific, and he understood suddenly why she'd planted it — why Mrs. Okonkwo had chosen this above flowers, above roses, above anything beautiful and brief.

Rosemary doesn't bloom and fade. Rosemary is green all year. Rosemary is *remembrance*, the old language of flowers said, but more than that, rosemary is a smell that stays on your hands after you've touched it, and that was Vesper exactly: you brushed past her once and carried her for days.

"Your sister," Mrs. Okonkwo said, kneeling beside him with the ease of a much younger woman, "would have hated a solemn occasion. So we won't have one. We'll have dirt under our nails and tea at four. That's the whole ceremony."

"That's the best ceremony," Oscar said.

At noon the van came back and Tom unloaded the sign, and everyone stopped, the way crowds do, without arranging to.

It was simple. Oak, waist-high on two posts, set near the gate. Hand-lettered in deep green:

**VESPER'S GARDEN**

And beneath, smaller, in the same careful script, a line Priya had chosen and Tom had approved and the lettering woman had rendered like scripture:

*Open to all. Come talk.*

Oscar read it twice. The second time his vision blurred and he let it, standing in a crowd of neighbors with soil on his knees, because there was no one left in this town who needed to be protected from the sight of him crying. Maybe there never had been.

*Come talk.* Her words. From the envelope, from the wardrobe frame, from the dead girl's patient handwriting: *You were always better at writing to me than talking to me. Come talk.* They'd put it on the sign. They'd made her last sentence into an invitation for strangers.

He found Priya's eye across the bed of cuttings.

"Thank you," he said, and it wasn't enough, and they both knew it wasn't enough, and she nodded anyway, because some thank-yous are just placeholders for a debt that gets paid in years instead of words.

* * *

Priya stayed after everyone left.

Tom had gone for tea things; Mrs. Okonkwo had pronounced the planting *done for today, done properly, none of this fussing*; and the garden sat in the late light with its new sign casting a long thin shadow across the turned earth. Priya walked the length of it slowly, touching the top of the old wall, reading the beds like a page.

Her glovebox was forty miles away, in a car parked outside a flat in another town, and inside it, beneath the insurance documents and the ancient sunglasses, nine years of her father's letters sat in their envelopes, unopened, addressed to her in handwriting that had gotten shakier each year according to the postmarks she allowed herself to notice.

She had watched Oscar read his letters aloud in a gutted house by lantern light, and she had watched him open his sister's reply, and she had watched him stand in the rubble of a verdict he'd built his whole self around — and somewhere in all that watching, quietly, without announcing itself, her own excuse had died. *I'm not ready* had worked for nine years. It had stopped working sometime around the moment an old man in a cardigan brought compost to a stranger's memorial.

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow she'd drive down and collect the glovebox. Tonight—

Tonight she took out her phone and photographed the sign, and sent it to no one, and stood there a moment longer in the smell of rosemary and wet earth.

Then she heard footsteps on the path, and turned, expecting Tom with the tea.

It was Oscar. He'd come back through the gate with something in his hands — a postcard, she saw, blank side up, and a pen, and he had the expression of a man arriving at a door he'd circled for weeks.

"Priya," he said. "I need a favor. And I need you to tell me if it's mad."
