## 10. Reading Aloud

The lie, when it finally arrived, was smaller than Ansel had expected. He had imagined needing an elaborate architecture of excuses — permissions, forms, a letterhead from the library — but what came out of him on Vesper Marlowe's doorstep that gray Thursday was simply this: "The historical society wants to document correspondence from the neighborhood. I thought — since you knew my aunt — you might want to be involved."

Vesper looked at him for a long moment, the way she always did, as though checking his face against some earlier version of it. Then she stepped aside. "Involved," she repeated, tasting the word. "Well. Come in before the rain does."

It had, in fact, begun to rain — a fine, needling drizzle that had hung over the city for three days, the kind of weather Aurelio Sandoval would have called *a mackerel sky, ma'am, rain by evening, count on it*. Ansel climbed the narrow stairs behind her, the box of letters in a canvas bag slung against his back like something smuggled. Which, he supposed, it was.

He had told himself he was bringing them to return them. That was the story he'd rehearsed on the bus. But he had also brought a notebook, a pencil, and the reading glasses he'd begun needing at thirty-four, and when Vesper settled into her chair by the window — the one with the crocheted afghan, the one that faced the light — he found himself saying, "Would you like me to read you one? Just so you can tell me if it's worth archiving."

And she said, "Read it, then. I haven't got all afternoon," which was a lie too, and they both knew it, because she had nothing but afternoons now, whole weather systems of them.

He opened the box. He chose letter twelve — not the earliest, not the ones that burned, but one from the middle of the first decade, when Aurelio's handwriting had loosened into confidence. He cleared his throat like a man about to make a fool of himself.

"*Dear Vesper — Today I said on television that the low pressure system moving in off the coast would bring 'unsettled conditions through the weekend.' What I meant was that I miss you, and that the word 'unsettled' was invented for people like me, who carry their weather on the inside. You would have laughed at that. You always laughed at the right things, which is rarer than you think. Most people laugh at the wrong things or not at all, and then the world goes quiet in a way that isn't peace. — When I can say the sentence, I'll come. — A.*"

The rain ticked against the window. Vesper sat very still, her hands folded on the afghan, her face turned slightly toward the gray light.

"Well," she said at last. "He always did talk too much about the weather."

"Should I stop?"

"I didn't say that." She adjusted her hands, unfolded them, folded them again. "Read the next one. If we're doing this, we're doing it properly."

* * *

Vesper had expected it to hurt. That was why she had said yes — or one of the reasons, the one she was willing to examine. Pain was an old acquaintance; she knew its visiting hours. What she had not expected was the strangeness of hearing Aurelio's voice come out of a stranger's mouth.

The boy — Crane, Ansel Crane, the nephew, though of course he wasn't a boy at all, he was a grown man with tired eyes and careful hands — read in a low, even voice, without performance. He did not do the accent. He did not do the weatherman's broadcast warmth. He read the letters the way Aurelio had written them, which was quietly, as if each one were being slipped under a door.

And she sat in her chair and let it fall on her.

That was the image that came to her, unbidden, somewhere around the fourth letter: standing in a summer storm when she was a girl, out past the fence line, letting the rain come down on her bare arms until her mother shouted from the porch. Not running. Not hiding. Just standing in it, face up, because there was a kind of honesty in getting wet that no umbrella could give you.

She was being rained on now. Forty years of letters, and she stood in them.

"— *the marigolds you planted the year we met are probably dead by now, but I find I can't drive past a garden center without slowing down, which the other men at the station find hilarious. Let them laugh. A man who doesn't slow down for marigolds is a man who has forgotten something important.*"

She laughed — a short, cracked sound that startled them both. Ansel looked up. "I'm sorry," she said, pressing her fingers to her mouth. "It's just — he never told me that. About slowing down. He would have died before telling me that."

"Maybe that's why he wrote it down."

She looked at him then, really looked, and thought: *you are too young to say things like that and mean them, and yet.* There was something in the boy's face she recognized, though it took her a moment to place it. It was the look of a man carrying something heavy and pretending it was light. She knew that look. She had worn it for nine years, waiting.

"Another," she said.

* * *

They established a schedule without ever discussing it. Tuesdays and Thursdays, two o'clock, when the light through the flower shop's painted window went the color of weak tea. Ansel would arrive with the box and the notebook, and Vesper would have tea already made — she never asked if he wanted any, she simply poured it, and he drank it, and this too became part of the architecture neither of them would admit to building.

He read letters fourteen through eighteen that first week. Nineteen he read slowly, dreading it, because nineteen was where the sentence lived — or failed to live, the paragraph where Aurelio circled it like a man circling a fire.

"*There is a sentence in my mother's tongue,*" Ansel read, "*and I have tried thirty times to write it here, and thirty times the paper refuses it. It is not that the words are long. It is that the sentence does a thing that English sentences do not do — it says the wrong and the love in the same breath, and means both, and the meaning lives between them like a bird between two hands. When I say it aloud, it is understood. When I write it, it is only spelled. Forgive me. When I can say the sentence, I'll come.*"

Vesper's teacup was halfway to her mouth. It stayed there, suspended, for a long moment before she set it down without drinking.

"He had a word for it," she said. "The language. He'd say a word sometimes when he was tired, or happy — a word that sounded like water going over stones. I asked him once what it meant and he said, 'It means I can't tell you.'"

Ansel wrote nothing in his notebook. His pencil hovered over the page and then retreated, ashamed. He was half-fluent now in that language's grammar, had been teaching himself from library grammars and a borrowed dictionary for months, and he understood — with a humility that felt like vertigo — that no amount of vocabulary would get him across. The sentence was a door that only opened from the speaking side.

"Read twenty," Vesper said. Her voice had gone quiet in a new way. "And don't stop at the hard parts. He never did."

* * *

Ansel walked home through the rain that Thursday and did not feel the rain. He felt the specific, dangerous warmth of a secret being fed. Because that was what he was doing — he knew it now, had known it since the second letter, since Vesper leaned forward in her chair and the lamplight caught the wet in her eyes and she said *don't stop at the hard parts*. He was feeding her the past one spoonful at a time, and she was opening her mouth like a bird, and she did not know that the hand feeding her had stolen from the nest.

She believed the letters were burned. She had told him that in their first real conversation, with a shrug that had cost her something. *He probably burned them, or she did — your aunt. Women like us, we burn things. It's cleaner.* And he had nodded, and said nothing, and gone home and slept through the morning as always, and woken with the box's weight still on his chest.

He could tell her. That was the exit, and he saw it every time, lit like a fire door. *Vesper, I found them behind my aunt's wardrobe. Vesper, my aunt hid them from you for thirty years. Vesper, I don't know if that was mercy or theft, and I don't know which of us she was protecting.* He could say it any Tuesday. Any Thursday. The door was always there.

And every time, he read another letter instead.

* * *

Vesper, for her part, had begun to live in two tenses. In the present tense there was the tea, the chair, the boy with the careful voice, the rain. In the other tense — the tense she had no grammar for, the tense Aurelio's sentence would have needed — there was 1971, and a television studio, and a man in a powder-blue jacket pointing at a map of high pressure and saying her name under his breath when he thought the microphone was off.

She caught herself dressing for the readings. That was the first sign, and she noted it with something like alarm. She had chosen the blue blouse on Tuesday. On Thursday she'd pinned her hair up the way she used to wear it, and then taken it down again, furious at herself, and pinned it up anyway.

He was a stranger, this nephew. He was half her age twice over. He read someone else's love letters in a borrowed voice and she was *dressing* for it.

But it was not him she was dressing for, she told herself. It was the rain. It was the standing-in-the-storm of it, the forty years of it, the chance at last to feel the whole weight of what had fallen on her and never once been measured. Aurelio had left her a drought — a silence with her name on it — and now, letter by letter, the drought was ending.

She did not ask where the letters had come from. That was the second sign, and she noted that one too, later, in the small cold hour when honesty keeps its appointments. She did not ask, because asking might close the door. She had spent nine years learning what it was to wait for a man to come through a door, and she would not do it again — not even by asking a question that was hers to ask.

On the fourth Thursday, letter twenty-three ended with the usual promise — *when I can say the sentence, I'll come* — and Vesper heard herself say, aloud, before she could stop it:

"And did he? Did he ever come?"

The room went very still. Rain on the glass. The kettle ticking as it cooled.

Ansel's eyes came up from the page. She watched him weigh something — she saw it happen, the small machinery of it behind his face — and she thought, with a clarity that frightened her: *he knows something. He has known it since the first day, and I have been too thirsty to see the well is poisoned.*

"No," Ansel said. "Not yet."

*Not yet.* She turned the phrase over like a stone found in a garden. Not *no*. Not *he never did*. *Not yet* — as if the letters were a forecast, as if somewhere in the boy's canvas bag the weather had not finished arriving.

She should have demanded the truth then. Seventy-eight years of living had earned her that much; she had buried a husband, a sister, a flower shop, and she had earned the right to look a lying man in the eye and say *tell me what you're carrying.*

Instead she said, "Read twenty-four."

And he read, and she listened, and outside the rain came down on the painted window, on the shuttered shop, on the whole gray city, steady as a debt, and neither of them moved to close the door against it.
