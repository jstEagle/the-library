## 1. The Smile That Ends Conversations

The trick to crying on cue, Tamsin had learned by nine, was not to think of anything sad. Sadness was a well you could fall into and never climb out of. The trick was to think of something small and specific instead — the exact blue of a matchbox, the sound a kettle makes just before it decides to scream — and let your body do the arithmetic of grief on its own. The body was always willing. It wanted, most days, an excuse.

So at the front of the chapel on Delancey Street, with two hundred people arranged in rows like witnesses, Tamsin thought about the color of the matchbox their mother had kept by the stove for eleven years, never used, never thrown away, and their eyes did what the room required of them.

"She's at peace now," said a woman Tamsin didn't recognize, gripping their hand with both of hers as if returning something borrowed. "You were such a devoted child."

"I was," Tamsin said, and smiled.

It was a good smile. They had practiced it in bathroom mirrors across three states: warm enough to be kind, closed enough to be final. The smile that ends conversations. People received it the way they received a receipt — proof of transaction, nothing further owed. The woman released their hand and moved along the receiving line toward the casserole of the next bereaved.

Eleven years of practice. Eleven years of funerals, actually, if you counted the first one, which no one else had been allowed to count because it had happened without bodies, without a service, without so much as a newspaper line beyond *three dead in Halloway house fire*. Tamsin had attended that funeral alone, at fourteen, standing in a field of wet grass looking at a burned foundation, conducting the entire eulogy inside their own skull while a county investigator's radio crackled somewhere behind the treeline. Every funeral since had been rehearsal. This one — their mother's, the real one, the one with a name spelled correctly in the program — was the last performance of a long run.

*Marisol Vasquez Reyes*, said the card stock. Beloved mother. Survived by one son.

Tamsin had chosen the pronoun themselves, years ago, when the paperwork required it. It sat in the mouth of strangers like a stone they'd been handed and told was bread. *Your brother.* *He must be devastated.* Tamsin nodded. Devastated, yes. Devastation was available upon request.

* * *

The reception was held in the parish hall because Marisol had been Catholic in the way of women who keep one saint's card tucked behind a bus pass — not devout, but hedged. There were ham sandwiches and a urn of coffee that tasted like the urn. Tamsin moved through the crowd doing the work: accepting condolences, redirecting questions, deploying the smile.

"You look just like her," said an old man with hearing aids the size of buttons.

"Everyone says that."

"She talked about you all the time. At the clinic. She was so proud."

This was true, and it landed strangely, like a letter delivered to the wrong address. Proud of what, exactly? Of the quiet tenant in 4B who paid cash for everything and worked freelance typesetting under a name that appeared on no school record anywhere? Of the person who visited every Sunday and watched telenovelas with her and asked careful, frictionless questions about nothing? His mother — his mother — had spent eleven years loving someone she believed was her son, and Tamsin had let her, because the alternative was explaining, and explaining was a door, and doors opened from both sides.

"Thank you," Tamsin said. "She was proud of me too." Which was also true, in the flattened way that survived translation.

A cousin from the Yonkers side cornered them near the sandwich table. "So what happens with the apartment?"

"The lease runs through spring. I'll deal with it."

"And her things?"

"I'll deal with it." The smile. The cousin drifted off, satisfied or deflected, impossible to know which, and Tamsin understood suddenly that they would spend the next month dealing with it — the clothes, the dishes, the matchbox — alone, in an apartment where every object would now be evidence of a woman who had died without ever being told the truth. Or almost without. Mothers knew things. Tamsin suspected, some nights, that Marisol had known everything and had chosen, out of love or exhaustion or both, to leave the fiction standing between them like a load-bearing wall neither dared knock on.

There was a moment, near four o'clock, when the hall emptied enough that Tamsin could stand at the window and watch the light do its late-October business on the street below. A man across the room was watching them. Tamsin registered him the way you register weather — peripherally, without commitment. Gaunt, forty-something, wearing a suit that fit him the way rented tuxedos fit people, which is to say correctly and without any relationship to his actual body. He wasn't holding a drink. Nobody at a funeral reception holds nothing unless they're working.

Tamsin turned back to the window and gave him, reflected in the glass, the smile.

He didn't look away. That was the strange part. Everyone looked away. The smile was built to be looked away from.

* * *

That night, back in the apartment on Halsey Street with its radiators knocking like a polite ghost, Tamsin performed the other ritual. The private one. The real one.

The postcards lived in a shoebox under the bed, beneath a winter sweater that hadn't been worn since the move from Newburgh. On top of the stack was the current week's card, already written, already stamped, waiting for morning. Tamsin took it out and read it again in the lamplight.

*Mama — The chestnuts are early this year. Saw a man selling them off a cart on Myrtle and stood there breathing the smoke until he asked if I was going to buy some. I bought some. They tasted like being eight. I hope wherever you are there is a cart.*

It was addressed, as all of them were, to **Vasquez Family, 14 Corliss Lane, Halloway**. An address that had not existed since June of 2013, when it became a black rectangle of foundation and ash that the county eventually graded over and seeded with grass. No mail carrier delivered there. No mailbox stood at the end of that nonexistent driveway. The postcards went into the blue box on the corner of Halsey and Patchen every Sunday evening, and they went into the great indifferent river of the postal system, and they went nowhere.

Tamsin had done the math once, drunk on cheap vermouth at twenty-three: five hundred and seventy-two postcards, give or take the weeks grief had made writing impossible. Five hundred and seventy-two small rectangular lies addressed to the dead. Because that was the thing Tamsin had never managed to say aloud, even inside their own skull, even at the burned foundation at fourteen: the family on those cards didn't exist. Not anymore. The Vasquez family of 14 Corliss Lane had died in the fire, officially, and the person who walked away from it — sixteen then, carrying a duffel bag and a new name and a story Uncle Marek had rehearsed with them until it ran smooth — had been assembled afterward from spare parts. Tamsin was the spare parts. Aurelia was the fire.

And still, every Sunday, the postcard. Still, every week, the fiction maintained in ballpoint pen: *Mama*, written to a woman who had died three years ago in a hospice bed believing her child was named Tamsin; *Papa*, dead longer than that, dead in ways that had nothing to do with smoke; *I'm eating well, I'm sleeping well, the city is loud but I've found the quiet streets.* Letters home to a house that was a garden now, if it was anything. Letters to a family that had never, in this configuration, drawn breath.

Sometimes Tamsin imagined a postal worker in Halloway — some route driver who'd been there long enough to remember — pulling these cards aside, reading the address, feeling the hair rise on their arms. *Three people died here. Who keeps writing to them?* It was the closest thing to a confession Tamsin had ever filed. A crime reported to a place where no one could investigate it.

They put the card back in the box and lay down on top of the covers, still half in funeral clothes, and listened to the radiator knock.

The apartment was very good at silence. Tamsin had selected it for that property, the way other people selected apartments for light. Silence was a material you could build with, if you were patient. You laid it down in courses, like brick: don't tell the landlord your history; don't give the bodega guy more than weather-talk; date people briefly and leave before the question *so what's your family like?* could get comfortable. Eleven years of masonry. What stood at the end was a life — small, clean, load-bearing, entirely hers and entirely hollow, a set dressed for a play that had closed before opening night.

Tomorrow she would mail the card. Tomorrow she would begin the month of *dealing with it*: the apartment, the clothes, the saint's card behind the bus pass. Tomorrow the machinery of erasure would grind forward one more rotation, smooth as it had always been, and Tamsin would ride it the way you ride anything that's been carrying you long enough — without thinking, without looking down.

Except.

Except the gaunt man in the ill-fitting suit, who had not looked away from the smile. Except the way he'd stood there holding nothing, watching Tamsin cross the parish hall like a man confirming an identification. Except that when Tamsin had finally left, just past five, he had been standing on the steps of the church, and he had said — quietly, not a question, not quite a greeting —

"Aurelia."

Just the name. Just the name, spoken into the cold air of Delancey Street for the first time in eleven years, in a stranger's mouth, with the certainty of a key entering a lock.

Tamsin had kept walking. Had gotten three blocks before their legs remembered how to shake.

Now, in the dark of the good silent apartment, Tamsin got up, went to the shoebox, and took out the week's postcard. Held it under the lamp. *Mama,* it began, in handwriting that had been designed, carefully, deliberately, to resemble no one's.

Somewhere across the city, a man knew a word that should have burned in 2013.

Tamsin turned the card over. The stamp was already on. The address was already written. All those Sundays, all those years, mailing letters to the dead — and for the first time, Tamsin wondered who had been reading them.
