## 3. Postcards to Ash

The card went into the sink before Tamsin could think better of it, and then they stood there with the lighter they kept for candles they never lit, and the sink was dry, and the card sat in the basin like a small white animal waiting to be told what it had done wrong.

**JONAH REYES.** **HALLOWAY COUNTY RECORDS.** A phone number. No email. No title beyond that. A man who said a name out loud in a room full of casseroles and folded flags of grief, and then walked out of it like a man who had paid a bill.

Tamsin flicked the lighter. The flame caught the card's corner and the corner curled, browned, went black. Reyes. The name meant nothing. The name meant everything. The name was a door with a man standing in it, and the man had said *Aurelia* the way you'd say a password you'd been given by someone who shouldn't have given it.

The card burned fast. Tamsin ran the tap and the ash went down in a gray spiral, and they watched it go, and their hands were steady, and their hands were not steady. Both things were true. Both things had been true for eleven years.

They dried their hands on the dish towel their mother had bought at the market on Ninth Street, back when the mother still bought things, back when the mother still believed in kitchens. The towel had lemons on it. Tamsin had never once thought about the lemons until tonight, and now they couldn't stop.

*You're Aurelia Vasquez.*

Eleven years since anyone had said it. Eleven years since Tamsin had said it, even alone, even into the dark, even as a test — *my name is* — and stopped, always stopped, at the space where the name should go. The name was a room they'd sealed off. You could stand outside it. You could press your ear to the wall. You did not open the door, because behind the door was a house on fire, and behind the fire was a family, and behind the family was a girl of fourteen who had walked out of a burning town with a new name in her mouth like a stone she couldn't swallow and couldn't spit out.

Tamsin turned off the light and went to bed and did not sleep.

* * *

The fire was in March. March of 2013, a Tuesday, though everyone always said it like a Sunday, like a day of rest and ruin. The house on Corben Lane went up at 2:14 in the morning, and by 3:30 there was nothing left of it but a chimney standing in its own smoke like a monument to something that had never been a home at all.

That was the story. That was the story everyone in Halloway told, and told, and told, until the telling wore the grooves so deep that no other story could be driven into them. The Vasquez house. Three dead. Father, mother, daughter. A candle, probably. Old wiring, probably. A tragedy, and the town grieved it properly, flowers at the fence line, ribbons on the mailbox of the neighbors, a plaque eventually, because Halloway was a town that honored its dead the way it honored everything: thoroughly, and then not at all.

The dead were buried in the ground of the story. The living were buried in the story too.

Tamsin had been in the back seat of a car that night, headed east, a duffel bag of clothes that their mother had packed with the calm of a woman performing a task she'd rehearsed in her head for weeks. The mother had said, *Don't look back at the town, mija, it's just a town.* And Tamsin hadn't looked back. That was the discipline of it. That was the whole religion: don't look back, don't write it down, don't say the name, and when people ask — and they will ask — your family died in a fire.

*Your family died in a fire.* Fourteen years old and handed a sentence like a coat too heavy for her, told to wear it forever.

The mother had done the wearing better. Or worse. The mother had worn it until the coat was skin. In the city, in the small apartment with the good light the mother never sat in, the mother had told the story to neighbors, to caseworkers, to the woman at the pharmacy who asked after family — *we lost everything in the fire, my husband, my daughter* — and her voice never cracked, and that was the terrible part, that it never cracked, that the lie had gone in so deep it came out smooth as bone.

Tamsin had started the postcards that first autumn. Not for the dead. The dead didn't need postcards. The postcards were for the story — a way of keeping it fed, keeping it warm, keeping it alive in the one place it could be safely alive: an address that no longer existed. 14 Corben Lane, Halloway. A house that was a black square in the ground. Tamsin wrote to it the way you'd write to a grave, except graves don't get mail, and that was the point, that was the beautiful sealed-off point: nothing would ever come back.

One a week. Sometimes two. *Dear Mama and Papa,* and then whatever the week had been. The weather. A book. A woman on the bus with a parakeet in a knit cap. The small inventory of a life that was itself an invention, because the life was an invention, because Tamsin Vasquez — the real one, the living one — was a fiction maintained at a dead address.

The mother had found the first stack once, years ago, in a shoebox under the bed. She'd read three of them, Tamsin remembered, standing very still by the window. Then she'd put the lid back on the box and said only, *You should use more stamps than that, they'll come back marked undeliverable.* As if the danger was the postal service. As if the danger was anything so small.

* * *

At 1 a.m. Tamsin got up and took the shoebox down.

It had migrated over the years — under the bed, to the closet, to the top shelf, back to the bed — but it had never gotten heavier, which felt wrong, because Tamsin had put eleven years into it. Postcards, all of them, in the order they were written, because the order mattered, because the order was the only chronology of the true life that existed anywhere. The apartment had no photo albums. The apartment had no diaries. The apartment had this: four hundred postcards addressed to ash.

Tamsin sat on the floor with the box between their knees and the lamp off, because the light made it too much, and read them by the streetlight coming through the blinds, in stripes, like reading through bars.

*Dear Mama and Papa — Week 1. I got a library card. The librarian didn't ask questions. I checked out a book about the ocean. I miss the river.*

The river. The Halloway River, brown and slow, that went past the tannery ruins and under the old rail bridge where the kids used to dare each other to jump. Tamsin had jumped once, at twelve, from the low beam, and come up gasping and triumphant, and their father had been on the bank pretending to be furious and failing completely. *Aurelia Vasquez,* he'd said, wading in, *you are going to be the death of me.*

He had not been wrong, and he had not been right. It was the other way around, and it was not death, it was this — a floor, a shoebox, a name like a stone.

*Week 14. Mama, I made a friend at school. Her name is Beth. I didn't tell her anything real. I'm sorry. I know you'd say that's the right thing. I hate that it's the right thing.*

Beth had lasted four months before Tamsin ended it — ended it the way they ended everything, with the smile, the good smile, the smile that ends conversations. *I'm fine, I'm just busy, I'll see you around.* Beth had stood in the school corridor holding two sodas, one for Tamsin, and Tamsin had walked past them both, and the look on Beth's face was a tuition payment Tamsin was still paying off.

*Week 31. The mother is better this month. She made soup. She said my name — the new one — and for a second it sounded like it could be mine.*

The new name. Tamsin. It had been chosen in a diner two hundred miles from Halloway, the mother flipping through the phone book with a shaking hand, looking for a name that was common, forgettable, a name that would slide through the world without catching on anything. *Tamsin,* she'd said, testing it, and Tamsin had thought: it's a coat. It's a coat I can wear. And it had been. It had been a good coat, warm enough, roomy enough, and you could live in a coat if you never took it off, and never took it off, and never —

*Week 88. Papa, I dreamed about the house. Not the fire. The house. The kitchen, the yellow wall, you burning the toast. I woke up and for one second I didn't remember any of it and I was just a girl in a bed and it was the best second of my year.*

*Week 140. I mailed this one from a different post office, just to see. It felt like a crime. Everything real feels like a crime now.*

*Week 203. Mama died in the story again today. Someone at work asked about family. I did it perfectly. I got the tone right and everything. I came home and threw up. I'm telling you because you're the only ones who know it's a story, and now you're a story too, and I don't know who I'm telling the truth to anymore.*

The stripes of streetlight moved. A car passed. Somewhere below, the city did its city things, indifferent, enormous, safe in its indifference. That was what Tamsin had bought with eleven years of silence: indifference. Safety priced at everything.

* * *

Here was the arithmetic of the fire, the arithmetic Tamsin had never once written down and had never once stopped calculating.

Three people died at 14 Corben Lane on March 12, 2013. The town believed it. The county believed it. There were, presumably, records — a report, a file, a line in some ledger of Halloway's dead. And the records were wrong, and the wrongness had been arranged, and the arranging had been done by adults, by the mother and the father and — Tamsin's mind skated off this, always skated, like a stone off ice — by *him*, by the uncle, by Marek, whose face Tamsin could summon only in fragments: a big laugh, a ring that clicked against coffee cups, a hand on the father's shoulder at some family dinner, saying *you worry too much, brother, I'll take care of it, I always take care of it.*

He had taken care of it. That was the thing about Marek. He took care of things the way fire took care of a house.

Tamsin had been fourteen and told nothing and told everything. Told: get in the car, don't pack what you love, pack what you need. Told: your family died in a fire. Told: never Halloway, never the name, never anyone from before. And the fourteen-year-old had done it, had done it *well*, had done it so well that the doing had become the person, and now the person was thirty-five and sitting on a dark floor with a shoebox, and a stranger had walked through a funeral and said the sealed name out loud, and the seal was broken, and Tamsin could hear the room behind the door.

Because here was what Tamsin knew, and had always known, and had kept in the sealed room along with the name: the fire had not been an accident. The fourteen-year-old had known it from the back seat, from the mother's rehearsed calm, from the duffel packed before any flame existed. The adult had known it from the money.

*The money.* Tamsin's hands went still on the postcards.

Every month, on the first, a deposit. Fourteen hundred dollars, exact, unvarying, eleven years running. From an account with a name attached: Marek Vasquez. *From your uncle,* the mother used to say, in the early years, when Tamsin asked. *He's good to us. He's the only one.* And the mother had never once explained how an uncle who supposedly believed his brother and sister-in-law and niece were dead — dead in the fire, dead in the story, dead in the ground of the telling — kept sending money to the living.

Tamsin had not asked. That was the contract, unsigned, unwritten, understood: the money comes, the questions don't. Eleven years of the contract. Eleven years of taking it, spending it, living on it, and telling themselves it was family, it was love, it was the one thread of the old life that hadn't burned.

But a man had stood at the funeral reception today with a card from the Halloway County Records office and said *Aurelia Vasquez* like he'd been authorized to say it, like the name had a file, like the file had a drawer, like the drawer had just been opened by someone who was asking questions. And the questions were going to travel, the way questions do, down the wires and into the town and up to the one man who had always taken care of things.

Tamsin sat in the striped dark and understood, with the slow cold clarity of 3 a.m., that the money had never been love. The money was a retainer. The money was the monthly fee for silence, and Tamsin had been on the payroll since they were fourteen, and the payroll meant that somewhere there was an employer, and the employer had a ring that clicked against coffee cups, and the employer had burned a house once and could burn a life just as easily.

The postcard in Tamsin's hand was from Week 260. *Dear Mama and Papa — I think someone was following me today. Probably nothing. Probably my nerves. I crossed the street twice and he crossed twice and then I lost him at the market. I didn't tell the mother. She's had a good month.*

Tamsin hadn't told the mother. The mother was dead now — actually dead, in the ordinary way, the heart simply stopping its work at seventy-one in a hospital with good light — and there was no one left to not tell. There was no one left at all, except an uncle who paid, and a stranger who asked, and a shoebox of letters addressed to a house that had been a black square for eleven years.

Tamsin put the postcards back in order. Week 1 to Week 572. Five hundred and seventy-two weeks of a life told to no one. Then they sat with the box on their knees and did not put it away.

The card was ash in the drain. But ash wasn't gone — ash was what remained when the burning was done, and Reyes remained, and the questions remained, and somewhere in Halloway a file was open with a dead girl's name on it, and the dead girl was sitting on the floor of a rented apartment holding the only evidence in the world that she had ever been alive.

They could burn the box. That was the move, the safe move, the move the mother would have made. Burn it, flush it, scatter it, and go on being Tamsin, go on being the coat, go on being safe and small and paid.

Tamsin looked at Week 1. *I miss the river.*

They didn't burn the box. They packed it, instead, into a canvas bag, deep, like contraband, like a thing you carry across a border — which it was, which it had always been — and then they sat on the edge of the bed as the sky went gray at the edges, and they did not sleep, and they did not decide, and the decision made itself the way the river used to make its way to the sea: not by choosing, but by being what it was.

In the morning there would be a deposit in the account. Fourteen hundred dollars, exact, from a man who believed — who had arranged to believe, who had paid for everyone to believe — that his niece was ash.

Tamsin was going to find out what he'd do when the ash started writing back.
