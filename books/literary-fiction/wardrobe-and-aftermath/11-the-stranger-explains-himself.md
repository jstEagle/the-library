## 11. The Stranger Explains Himself

Jonah Reyes had a rule about waiting: never do it in the place you're expected. So when Tamsin Vasquez finally called the number on the card he'd left at the funeral—three weeks later, voice flat as river ice, saying only *the diner on Corliss Street, four o'clock*—he arrived at three and took the booth in the back corner with his face to the door, then moved, at ten to four, to the counter stool nearest the register, where he could watch the whole room without seeming to.

He'd learned that from his father, who had learned it from worse men. *If someone wants to find you, let them walk past you first.* His father had said it as a joke, mostly. The last year of his life, not so much.

The bell over the door rang at 4:02.

Tamsin came in the way Jonah had seen frightened people come in for twenty years—shoulders up, eyes doing one fast sweep of the exits before settling into a performance of casualness. They wore a gray coat too good for Halloway and carried nothing except themselves, which told him they'd left the postcards somewhere safe. Smart. He'd have been disappointed otherwise.

They didn't see him at the counter. They looked at the back booth—the obvious one—and something in their posture loosened half an inch, deciding he hadn't come after all, or was late, or—

"I'm over here," Jonah said. "Coffee's better by the window anyway."

The half-inch of looseness snapped back tight. Tamsin crossed the floor like someone crossing a frozen pond, sat across from him at the little table by the glass, and put both hands around the menu without opening it, the way people hold things when they need proof their hands are empty.

"You're the one from the funeral," they said.

"Jonah Reyes." He slid a business card across—not the county records one this time; that had been Dee's card, borrowed cover, and he wasn't proud of borrowing it, but the funeral had been the only place he could get close without Tamsin bolting. This one just said his name and *Reyes Claims Analysis* and a phone number. "Independent insurance investigator. I work fire cases."

"I don't have any insurance."

"Nobody's asking you to file a claim." He kept his voice low and even, the tone he used with people whose houses had burned down. It was the same tone, he'd realized long ago, whether the house was wood or a whole life. "I asked you here because I think we've been reading the same story from different ends of it. And I think you already know how your end goes."

Tamsin's jaw did something small and controlled. "I don't know what you mean."

"Yeah," Jonah said. "You do. But let's start with mine, because mine's easier to say out loud."

He turned his coffee cup a quarter turn on its saucer. He'd rehearsed this in the car, in motel rooms, in the shower, for years—rehearsed it so many times the words had gone smooth as river stones—but rehearsal meant nothing. It never did.

"In 2009 my family lived in a duplex on Delmore Avenue, upstate. My mother, my stepfather, my little sister Lucia. I was nineteen, living two towns over, working nights." He said it plainly, because plainness was all he had to offer and all he trusted. "There was a fire. Official cause: electrical, faulty wiring in the wall behind the dryer. Insurance paid out. Case closed in eleven days."

Tamsin's hands had gone still around the menu.

"What actually happened," Jonah continued, "was that my stepfather had been doing books for a man named Corrigan, who did books for a development group that bought distressed properties along the river corridor. My stepfather found out the development group was a laundering operation—shell companies buying cheap, insuring high, burning what wouldn't sell. He told Corrigan he was going to the state. Nine days later, wiring fault."

He watched Tamsin's face while he said it. Watched the color leave and come back wrong.

"The investigator who closed my family's case," Jonah said, "was named Halvorsen. He later went to work for a county office downriver. Halloway County."

The name landed. He saw it land.

"There is no version of this conversation," Tamsin said quietly, "where I keep pretending you're a stranger."

"No. There isn't. That's why I picked the funeral. You perform grief beautifully, by the way—I mean that as a compliment. Nobody performs it that well unless they've had practice at something adjacent to it."

"That's a cruel thing to say to someone at their mother's funeral."

"It's a cruel thing to know about yourself," Jonah said. "I'm sorry. But I've spent nine years learning how these stories go, and I can't afford politeness anymore. Politeness is how the file stays closed."

*

He laid it out for them then, right there between the ketchup and the napkin dispenser, because he'd learned that the middle of a public room was safer than any private one—witnesses everywhere, no dark parking lots, no sudden cars. He kept his voice under the diner noise and spoke in the flat cadence of a report, because that was the only way he could get through it.

Nine years. That was the number. Nine years since he'd pulled the insurance file on his own family's death and found the investigation thinner than a phone book page. Nine years of following the money upriver, company by shell company, name by nominee, until the shells stopped being names and started being one name.

Marek Vasquez.

"My stepfather's operation was small-time," Jonah said. "Corrigan was a franchise. Somebody taught him the method, and the somebody taught him how to make investigators comfortable, and the somebody's signature style shows up in eleven fires across three counties between 2006 and 2013. Accelerant patterns disguised as appliance failures. Investigations closed fast. Buyers appearing within eighteen months at thirty cents on the dollar. And in every single file, somewhere—a consultant's fee, an appraisal, a records request, a quiet settlement—one shell or another with Marek Vasquez's fingerprints on the paperwork."

"And you've taken this to—" Tamsin started.

"Everyone. State police twice. The AG's office once, before the election replaced everyone. A federal task force that thanked me for my time and dissolved six months later." He smiled, and knew it wasn't a warm smile. "You know what every single one of them said? Some version of: *Mr. Reyes, this is compelling, but you have no witness.* Documents are ghosts. Ghosts can be explained. A ledger can be a forgery. A pattern can be coincidence. What nobody can explain away is a person standing in a courtroom saying *I was there, and here is what happened to me.*"

"And you think I'm that person."

"I think you're the only one left alive who knows what happened in the Halloway house in 2013, and I think you've spent eleven years mailing postcards to the grave of the person who could have testified." He leaned forward. "Yes. I read about the postcards. Not surveillance—don't look like that. Your uncle's mail arrangements are sloppy once you know to look. A forwarding order on a burned address is a strange thing to maintain, Tamsin. Someone wanted to know if you were still writing. Which means someone always knew you were alive."

The silence stretched. Outside the window, a delivery truck idled, and the light through the glass lay across the table like something spilled.

"My father died in that fire," Tamsin said at last. "My mother died three weeks ago of everything that came after. There's no one to testify for. There's no one left."

"There's you."

"I'm not a witness. I'm a—" Their hand made a small gesture, taking in the coat, the city shoes, the whole assembled self. "I'm what survived instead."

"I know exactly what that is," Jonah said. "I've been it for nine years. Let me tell you what it costs, because maybe nobody's told you the price list. It costs every relationship you don't start because starting things means explaining yourself. It costs every job you quit before anyone gets close enough to ask about your family. It costs birthdays spent composing alibis and funerals performed like auditions. And the worst part—the part nobody warns you about—is that after enough years, you can't remember whether you're hiding from them or hiding from the truth. Because those start to feel identical."

Tamsin was looking at him now, really looking, the way survivors recognize each other across crowded rooms.

"Why me?" they said. "Why now? You've had nine years. Why did you wait until my mother's funeral to walk up to me?"

"Because your mother's death changed the math." He said it gently, because it needed gentleness even though it was arithmetic. "While she was alive, you were a survivor with a dependent. Now you're a survivor with a choice. And because Marek knows she's gone. He'll have recalculated too. Men like him don't grieve; they audit. The moment she died, you stopped being a managed asset and started being a loose end. I'd give you a month, maybe two, before he decides the allowance is more expensive than the alternative."

"You talk like you know him."

"I know his paperwork the way you know your own handwriting." Jonah paused. "And I know what happened to the last person who tried to testify against the Corrigan operation. He didn't die. That would have been simple. He had a breakdown, lost his job, lost his wife, and now lives in a facility outside Scranton where he tells the nurses the same story every day and nobody believes him. They didn't kill the truth. They made it unbelievable. That's the house style."

Tamsin laughed—one short, terrible note, quickly suppressed. "That's the most honest thing anyone's said to me in eleven years."

"I have more honesty where that came from. Fair warning: it doesn't get better."

*

They sat with it awhile. Jonah ordered a second coffee he didn't want, because the ritual of it gave the silence something to do. Through the window, Halloway went about its afternoon—a hardware store, a church sign, the river glinting past the end of the street like a held breath.

"If I talk," Tamsin said finally, "what happens to me? Actually happens. Not the movie version."

"You testify. You live under your real name for the first time in eleven years. You lose whatever small safe life you built, because it was built on sand and sand doesn't survive daylight." He met their eyes. "And you get to stop writing postcards to a dead address. I can't tell you what that's worth. I genuinely don't know. I've never gotten there myself."

"You haven't testified."

"Not yet. My case dies without you. Yours dies without me. That's the whole alliance." He spread his hands. "I'm not offering friendship. I'm not offering safety—there isn't any, and anyone who offers you safety in this is selling something. I'm offering a file nine years thick, a records clerk inside the county archive who's already risking her job, and one living witness. That's the entire hand. Play it or fold it."

"The records clerk," Tamsin said. "Dee Okafor. She wrote to me."

"She's braver than both of us combined, and she has less protection than either. Remember that when you're deciding."

Tamsin looked out the window for a long time. When they turned back, their face had done something Jonah recognized from mirrors—the calculation where fear and exhaustion cancel each other out and what's left is neither.

"One question," they said. "Answer it straight. Did my father set that fire?"

And there it was. The question underneath all the other questions, the one Jonah had turned over for months, because he'd read the 2013 file until he could recite it, and the file said what files like that always said: accelerant, origin point near the kitchen, investigation closed in thirteen days, Halvorsen's signature on the report like a butcher's stamp.

"I don't know," Jonah said. "Here's what I know. The origin point was the kitchen. Your father's study was upstairs. If he set it, he set it in the one room of the house where he'd have the least control and the least chance of getting himself out—which is either incompetence or staging, and Marek's operations don't hire incompetent. Here's what else I know: three days before the fire, your father withdrew eight hundred dollars in cash and made a call from the payphone outside the Halloway pharmacy that lasted forty minutes. To the state attorney general's office. They have no record of the call. Records from that office, that year, were later—"

"—handled," Tamsin finished. Very quietly.

"Handled," Jonah agreed. "So either your father tried to blow the whistle and got burned for it, or he was made to look like the kind of man who burns his own family. Either way, the fire was built to be read as his confession. And either way, the only person who can say what really lived in that house is sitting across from me holding a menu they never opened."

Tamsin set the menu down at last. Folded their hands on top of it, empty now, deliberate.

"The money," they said. "Every month. From Uncle Marek. Eleven years."

"Hush money has excellent customer retention," Jonah said. "It's the only industry where loyalty is guaranteed by fear."

"I thought it was kindness. For a long time I thought—" They stopped. Started again, flatter. "What do you need from me first?"

"Nothing tonight. Nothing this week. First you decide—really decide, alone, in whatever room you trust—because once you're in, you're in, and I won't pretend otherwise." He stood, left bills under his cup, and gave them the truth as a parting gift, because they'd earned it and because lies were the enemy's currency. "But decide fast. Because yesterday morning, a black SUV with dealer plates drove past the memorial garden on Ash Lane three times. Slow. And this afternoon, my motel room was entered while I was at breakfast. Nothing taken. Everything read."

He watched the words go in. Watched Tamsin understand that the machinery had already started turning, that the funeral stranger and the searched motel room and the slow SUV were all one thing now, one weather system moving over the town.

"They know I'm here," Tamsin said.

"Marek knows you're here. Those are different problems, and you should worry about the second one first." Jonah shrugged into his coat. "Diner's neutral ground, so I'll say this here and not again: whatever you decide, don't sleep at the motel tomorrow night. Book out. Move. And if a deputy offers you a friendly ride anywhere—"

He stopped. Tamsin had gone very still, in the particular way of a person remembering something that happened hours ago and only now understanding it.

"A deputy stopped me this morning," they said slowly. "By the garden. Said I looked lost. Offered directions. Offered a ride into town." A pause. "I said no. I walked."

Jonah exhaled. "Then you've already survived your first day. Get some rest, Aurelia."

The name hit them like a hand on the shoulder from behind—they actually swayed, just slightly, just enough for him to see it—and he felt the old guilt and the older certainty wrestle briefly and settle, as they always settled, on certainty.

"That's not my name," Tamsin said.

"Not yet," Jonah said. "But it's the only one worth having. Think about which one you want to be buried under."

He left a five beside the coffee cups and pushed open the door into the cold, and behind him he heard the bell ring once more—Tamsin, a minute later, stepping out the opposite way, toward the river, toward the garden, toward whichever name they'd choose—and Jonah Reyes stood a moment on the sidewalk of a town he hated, breathing air that smelled faintly of woodsmoke, and got out his phone to text Dee Okafor four words:

*They listened. Clock is running.*
