## 13. Dee's Risk

The sealed cabinet lived in the basement of the Halloway County Records Annex, behind a door with a keypad that Dee Okafor was technically authorized to open and absolutely not authorized to use for what she was about to do.

She stood in front of it at 6:47 on a Thursday evening, long after the last clerk had gone home, holding a cup of cold coffee she'd been carrying around all day like a prop. The basement smelled the way basements in government buildings always smell—damp concrete, old toner, the faint chemical sweetness of dehumidifiers running against time. Fluorescent lights hummed overhead in a rhythm that had started to feel, after four years down here, like a second pulse.

Her supervisor's memo was still folded in her back pocket. She'd read it eleven times.

*Effective immediately, all materials related to case 2013-FR-0417 (Vasquez) are restricted pending review by county counsel. Access requires written authorization from the Sheriff's Office.*

Pending review. She knew what that phrase meant now. She'd learned it the way you learn anything important in a small town—not from a statement but from a silence. Two weeks ago, after Tamsin Vasquez came back to town looking like a ghost who'd taken up jogging, Dee had pulled the fire file for the third time and found it thin. Not missing—thin. Pages renumbered. A lab report on the accelerant analysis reduced to a single paragraph with no chain-of-custody form attached. And the investigation closure letter, signed by a detective who had retired to Florida two months after the fire and died there before anyone thought to ask him anything.

Someone had gotten to the file before her. Someone with better access and worse intentions.

And then yesterday, her supervisor—a decent man, a tired man, a man whose boat payment existed because of a loan from First Halloway Savings where Marek Vasquez had banked since before Dee was born—had called her into his office and slid the memo across the desk without meeting her eyes.

"Just procedure," he'd said. "County counsel wants everything tidy."

"Since when does county counsel care about a twelve-year-old fire?"

"Since somebody started asking questions." He'd finally looked at her, and what was in his face wasn't anger. It was apology. "Dee. Take the vacation days. You've got them accrued. Go see your sister in Columbus."

That was when she understood the shape of it. They weren't sealing the file to protect it. They were sealing it so that when it quietly disappeared, no one would be able to prove it had ever been whole.

She set the coffee down on a filing cart and keyed in the code.

* * *

The scanner was slow. That was the first problem. The annex had budgeted for a new one three years running and each year the money had gone somewhere else—to roof repairs, to a records-management software subscription nobody used, to things Dee suspected she could trace back through procurement if she were the kind of person who traced things. She was, as it turned out, exactly that kind of person. But tracing took time, and time was the thing the humming fluorescents kept counting off above her.

So she photographed pages with her phone instead, which was worse than scanning in every way except speed, and she worked in an order she'd decided on the walk over: originals first, copies second, and the ledger third.

The ledger wasn't part of case 2013-FR-0417. It was part of something dumber and more damning: the property transfer records. Deeds didn't get sealed, because deeds were boring, and boring was the best camouflage corruption ever invented. For six weeks she'd been pulling them one at a time, in ordinary daylight, wearing her lanyard, doing her job—the parcel where the Vasquez house had burned; the shuttered feed store on Corliss Street; the flooded duplexes off the river road; the old cannery. Every property touched by fire or flood or foreclosure in the years surrounding the blaze, and every one of them bought within ninety days of its ruin by one of four LLCs whose registered agents were all the same law office in a strip mall outside Carbondale, and whose funding traces—she'd followed the money through the county's own financing statements, cross-referenced with state incorporation filings she'd requested under the public records act, politely, twice—led back to a holding company whose sole officer was listed as M. Vasquez.

Four shell companies. One man. A pattern of buying grief at auction prices.

She'd printed the summary table at home, on her own printer, at two in the morning, and hidden it inside a hollowed-out copy of the *Historic Structures Survey: Tri-County Region, 1998*, which was the only book in her apartment nobody would ever voluntarily open.

But the deeds alone were circumstantial. What she needed was what connected Marek to the fire itself—and that meant the sealed file, the real one, the version that existed before someone thinned it out upstairs.

It took her forty minutes to find it. The original lab report was there, complete with chain-of-custody forms, signed by a chemist named Osgood who had since moved to Oregon. Gasoline, mixed with something the report called "a medium petroleum distillate consistent with kerosene." Poured in three locations, including the base of the rear exterior door—from the outside. The word *outside* was circled in the original investigator's handwriting, with a note: *Entry point? No forced entry found. How did pour pattern originate EXTERIOR if fire determined accidental electrical?*

Below that, a supplemental page Dee had never seen in any copy: a request from the lead investigator to subpoena financial records of Vasquez Brothers Development, dated nine days after the fire. And below that, stamped across the request in red:

*WITHDRAWN — ORDER OF COUNTY COUNSEL — R. HALLORAN*

Halloran. County counsel. The same name on the memo restricting the file now. Twelve years, and the same hand on the lock.

Dee sat down on the concrete floor with her back against the cabinet and photographed every page twice, once straight-on and once at a slight angle with the corner of the folder visible, so no one could later claim the images were fabricated. It was a trick Jonah Reyes had taught her, back when their alliance was still mostly suspicion and shared coffee. *Document the document,* he'd said. *Prove the proof.* He'd lost his family to a fire just like this one, in a town two counties over, and he carried his grief the way other men carry a wallet—in constant contact with the body, checked for constantly.

Her hands were steady until they weren't. Somewhere around page forty, photographing the witness statement of a neighbor named Rosa Delgado—*I saw a truck I didn't know parked by the house that week, dark colored, maybe a Ford, two men I didn't recognize*—her vision blurred and she had to stop and press the heels of her hands against her eyes and breathe.

Because here was the thing about Dee Okafor, the thing she'd never said out loud to Jonah or to Tamsin or to anyone: she loved this work. Not the corruption, obviously, not the menace—but the archive itself, the deep green ocean of paper, the idea that a county keeps a memory whether or not anyone consults it. She'd taken this job at twenty-six because she believed records were a promise the present made to the future. *We will remember what happened to you.* That was the promise. That was the whole job.

And tonight she was learning what the promise was worth, and to whom, and she was breaking it open anyway, because a promise that protects the guilty is just a lock on the wrong door.

* * *

She lost the job on a Monday, and it happened so fast and so politely that for two days she kept expecting to wake up from it.

There was no confrontation. No dramatic discovery. At 9:15 in the morning, her supervisor appeared at her desk with a printout of the keypad log—her code, 11:04 p.m., cabinet seven, forty-three minutes—and laid it face-up beside her stapler the way you'd lay down a losing hand.

"County counsel flagged the access," he said. "They run audits now." He wouldn't look at the shelf behind her head. "Dee, I have to suspend you pending termination. There's a hearing, but—" He finally looked at her, and again it was apology, and again the apology changed nothing. "Don't bother contesting it. Halloran already drafted the paperwork. It's dated Friday."

Dated Friday. Before the audit. Which meant the audit hadn't caught her; the audit had been built to catch whoever looked, whenever they looked, and she had walked into a trap that had been baited with her own conscience.

"I copied those files because they're evidence of arson," she said, keeping her voice level, keeping it clerical, the voice she used for difficult patrons. "A man burned his own brother's family out of a house and bought the lot for eleven thousand dollars, and your county counsel withdrew the subpoena that would have proved it, twelve years ago, and is still sitting in that office drawing a salary—"

"Stop." His voice cracked on it. "Please. I've got a daughter starting college. I can't hear this right now. Just—clean out your desk after five."

She cleaned out her desk at 4:40. She took nothing that belonged to the county except the truth, which she had already taken, which was in a waterproof pouch in the bottom of her bag along with her phone and the hollowed-out survey book, and which no one searched for because no one imagined the quiet archivist with the lanyard would have done anything but look.

In the parking lot, her phone buzzed. Jonah.

*Heard. You okay?*

She typed back: *Better question is whether I'm useful. Meet me at the diner in an hour. Both of you. I'm handing it off.*

Then she sat in her car in the emptying lot and did the math on her life. Twenty-nine years old. Four years of service, gone. A suspension letter that would follow her through every county hiring system in the state. A savings account that would cover four months of rent if she was careful, three if she wasn't. And somewhere across town, a developer with a marina and a lawyer on retainer was learning her name, if he hadn't already, and adding it to whatever list he kept.

She was surprised by how little fear she felt, and how much relief. For weeks she'd been living two lives—the careful clerk and the midnight copier—and the seam between them had been fraying for a while. Now there was only one life, and it fit strangely well, like clothes cut for someone braver who had turned out to be her.

* * *

The diner on Route 9 did its pie at four o'clock and its best business at five, and by the time Dee slid into the corner booth, the windows had gone amber with early autumn light. Tamsin arrived first, hood up, moving like someone who'd learned to enter rooms sideways. Jonah came in ten minutes later, ordered coffee he wouldn't drink, and sat with his back to the wall, facing the door, facing the parking lot, facing everything.

Dee put the pouch on the table between the napkin dispenser and the salt.

"Everything's in there," she said. "The original lab report with full chain of custody. The withdrawn subpoena, signed off by Halloran himself—that's the keystone, that's the man who sealed the file then and sealed it again last week. Rosa Delgado's witness statement about the truck. And this." She slid the folded summary sheet out of the hollowed book and flattened it on the formica. Four columns. Four LLCs. Eleven properties. One name surfacing under all of them like a body under ice. "Marek bought the ruins. Every one. Cheap, fast, through layers clean enough to pass any casual audit. He didn't just burn his brother out—he made a business of it. The fire was the first transaction."

Tamsin stared at the table of numbers the way people stare at x-rays of themselves. Dee watched them take it in—the flinch at the purchase price of their childhood lot, the long stillness, the breath that finally came out through the nose.

"You copied all of this knowing what it would cost," Tamsin said finally. It wasn't quite a question.

"I knew what it might cost. There's a difference, and the difference is you don't get to choose outcomes, only actions." Dee wrapped her hands around the refilled coffee cup, mostly for warmth. "Besides. I didn't lose my job for copying files. I lost it because Halloran was waiting for someone to care enough to look. Somebody was going to trip that wire eventually. Better me than some kid intern with no backup and no idea what they'd found."

"That's not better," Tamsin said quietly. "That's just you."

Jonah turned the summary sheet toward himself and studied it with the flat attention he gave everything, though Dee had learned to read him well enough to catch the tell: his thumb pressing white against the paper's edge. "This connects to my file," he said. "Different county, same structure. Same strip-mall law office as registered agent—I checked that address months ago. If we can show the LLCs funded a single source, and that source matches the disbursements in my case, we're not telling one story anymore. We're telling a system."

"So we take it to the grand jury," Dee said. "Tamsin testifies, I authenticate the records, you connect the pattern—"

"And Marek's lawyers spend three weeks explaining that LLCs are legal, development is legal, and grieving families sell cheap because grief makes bad negotiators," Jonah said. "Which is why the records aren't enough. Records say *what*. We need someone who says *who*." He looked at Tamsin, and didn't say the rest, because the rest was obvious: *we need you, saying your own name, in a room where it counts.*

Tamsin picked up the summary sheet, held it a moment, and set it back down with strange gentleness, the way you'd handle something breakable that belonged to someone else.

"The mail," Tamsin said. "You said the postcards went somewhere. That they were intercepted."

Jonah's jaw tightened. "I'm close. Another few days. If I can show Marek's operation was collecting mail addressed to a burned-down house—if he's known for twelve years that survivors were alive and kept paying to keep them quiet—then the hush money stops being deniable and becomes a confession with a monthly deposit slip." He drained the coffee he hadn't touched. "Give me until the weekend."

Outside, the light was going. Dee gathered the pouch, the book, the sheet, and divided them—copies to Jonah, copies to Tamsin, originals staying on her body, because if the county wanted its archive back it could come find it in the one place it had never bothered to protect: a person.

At the door, Tamsin stopped her with a hand barely touching her sleeve.

"Why?" Tamsin asked. "You didn't know us. Twelve years ago you were a teenager somewhere else. Why burn down your own life for ours?"

Dee considered the question honestly, standing in the amber light with her whole future folded in a waterproof pouch against her ribs.

"Because I spent four years keeping a promise for people who never intended to keep it," she said. "Somebody has to be the record now. Might as well be me."

Tamsin nodded slowly, and something moved across their face—gratitude, yes, but underneath it, resolve hardening into something Dee recognized from her own mirror that morning.

"Then hold onto them tight," Tamsin said. "Because tomorrow morning, I'm going to call Uncle Marek and accept his dinner invitation."
