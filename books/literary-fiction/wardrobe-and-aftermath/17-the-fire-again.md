## 17. The Fire Again

The call came at 6:47 in the evening, which told Marek everything before the caller said a word. Nobody called him at dinner with good news.

He was in his office above the marina, the one with the long window facing the water, where the boats sat in their slips like patient money. On the desk: two contracts, a glass of Macallan he hadn't touched, and a photograph of his brother's family taken the summer before everything. Marek kept it there the way a man keeps a loaded gun in a drawer—not to use, but to remember what he was capable of.

He listened. He said, "Say that again, slower." He listened some more. Then he said, "No. Don't do anything. I'll handle it," and hung up, and sat for a long moment with his hand flat on the desk, as if steadying himself against weather only he could feel.

The girl. Aurelia. Tamsin. Whatever name she was wearing this week.

She'd come back to Halloway. She'd gone to the county annex. And this afternoon, according to the man who watched the parking lot because Marek paid him to watch the parking lot, she'd met with Dee Okafor for over an hour, and when she left, she was carrying a folder.

Not a folder from the gift shop. Not a brochure about the town's revitalization initiative, which Marek had personally funded and personally neutered. A records folder. The kind with the string closure. The kind you signed for.

He poured the Macallan and didn't drink it. He held the glass up to the window and watched the light come through it amber and slow, and he thought about his brother, who had also once sat at a desk with a drink he wasn't drinking, three days before the fire, and had told Marek on the phone: *I'm going to the authorities, Marek. I don't see how there's any other way. You can come with me or you can explain yourself after.*

Marek had explained himself after. That was the whole of his adult life, really—a long, patient, meticulous explanation, conducted in the language of property.

* * *

Here was the thing people got wrong about Marek Vasquez, and he had let them get it wrong for thirty years: they thought he was a man who loved money. He didn't. Money was just the instrument. What he loved was solvency. Order. The feeling of a ledger in which every column balanced, every debt was either paid or scheduled, and every risk had been priced, hedged, or eliminated.

The fire had been a risk elimination. That was all. His brother had been going to talk to the authorities about the business—the loans, the shell entities, the way Marek had been moving money through the family company like water through a broken pipe for the better part of a decade. And a man in that position, a man facing exposure, has exactly two options: he can let the exposure happen, or he can remove the witness.

There were, of course, gradations. You didn't have to kill anyone. You could simply arrange for a life to become untenable—credit ruined, licenses suspended, a marriage stressed until it cracked along its natural fault lines. Marek had tried that first, actually. He had spent two years trying that first, and his brother had responded the way he responded to everything, which was with a maddening, immovable decency. He'd taken the second job. He'd sold the car. He'd told his wife the truth, or enough of it, and she'd held the family together with her hands.

So the fire. Marek had not lit it. He wanted that understood, by himself, in the privacy of his own head, where he conducted all his most honest accounting: he had not lit it, had not held the can, had not struck the match. He had made a call to a man in Ellsworth who made calls to other men, and he had paid an invoice labeled *site remediation*, and eleven days later his brother's house was ash and his brother was a hero who died trying to save his wife and daughter—except that the wife and daughter hadn't died, because Marek, in what he still told himself was his one act of mercy, had gotten them out first. Had arranged it. Had been standing at the end of the driveway in the dark with his engine running while they came out the back with nothing but what they wore.

He'd put them in a car. He'd given them a name that wasn't theirs. He'd set up the deposits. Eleven years of deposits. He had been, by any honest measure, their provider, their protector, and their jailer, and the beauty of the arrangement—the thing he'd marveled at, quietly, all these years—was that all three roles used the same money.

And now the girl was in Halloway, carrying a folder.

* * *

At seven he made the calls. Not many. That was the discipline. A man in Marek's position never made many calls, because many calls were a pattern, and patterns were what investigators found when they had nothing else.

First: his attorney, to confirm, in the abstract, the retention schedule for county records. "If a document is copied but the original is sealed," Marek asked, "which one has standing?" His attorney, who was careful and expensive and had never once asked Marek a question he didn't want to answer, said the original had standing, copies had evidentiary weight only insofar as they could be authenticated, and why did Marek ask. "Curiosity," Marek said. "I'm thinking about donating to the historical society."

Second: the man in Ellsworth, who was older now, slower, more expensive. Marek didn't say *annex*. He said, "The county's got that records building off Corliss Street. The old one, with the addition in back. I'm told the electrical in that addition is a disgrace. I'm told it's a wonder it hasn't gone up already." A pause. "I'm told that by people who'd know."

"You want it gone," the man said. Not a question.

"I want the town to be safe," Marek said. "I care about this town."

Third: the sheriff. This was the shortest call. Marek didn't ask for anything. He said, "Dale. You hear anything about a fire over on Corliss Street, you make sure the response is... unhurried. Water pressure's terrible in that part of town anyway. Everybody knows it." And Sheriff Dale Pruitt, who had been on Marek's payroll in one form or another since the year of the fire, said, "What fire?" and Marek said, "Exactly," and they hung up like two men who had discussed the weather.

Then he sat with the photograph again. His brother. His brother's wife, who had died in March, in a city apartment, alone, and whose funeral Marek had not attended because attending funerals of the people you've destroyed is a special kind of exposure, and Marek did not do exposure. His brother's daughter, standing at the edge of the photograph, half out of frame, as if even at nine years old she'd been trying to leave.

*She's carrying a folder.*

He thought about the strangeness of it. Eleven years of deposits, and she'd never once come to him. Eleven years of postcards to a dead address—yes, he knew about the postcards, he'd known about them from the second month, because he had the mail at the Halloway route watched the way a man watches a wound to make sure it's healing—and she had written every week to a house that wasn't there, to a family that was officially dead, to a fiction he had built and she had maintained, and he had found it, in a way, touching. Proof the arrangement worked. Proof she understood, at some level beneath language, that the past was a place you visited only by mail.

But now she'd come back in person. And she hadn't come to him. She'd gone to a records clerk with a folder and a stranger with questions, and that meant she wasn't grieving anymore. Grieving people write postcards. This was something else. This was a woman building a case.

Marek respected that. He wanted that on the record, in the ledger of himself: he respected it, the way you respect a fire crossing a firebreak.

And because he respected it, he understood it precisely. She didn't want money. Money he could have handled—money was his native language, he could have tripled the deposits by Friday and she'd have taken them, people always took them, taking was easier than knowing. But she didn't want money, which meant she wanted the thing money was for. She wanted the columns to balance. She wanted him, in the end, exposed.

There was no hedging that. There was no pricing it. There was only elimination.

* * *

At 9:20 he drove out to Corliss Street himself. He didn't like to, but a man checked his own work.

The annex sat behind the main records building like a shy relative: a low brick addition from the seventies, flat-roofed, windowless except for one high strip of glass block, its fire door propped open with a cinderblock because the closer had been broken since the Obama administration. He'd walked past it a hundred times over the years. He'd even been inside once, for the sesquicentennial exhibit, and remembered thinking: *all of it, paper. Every debt this county ever owed or denied, and it's all paper, and paper burns.*

The parking lot was empty except for a sedan he didn't recognize, parked too far from the light. The watcher's car. Good. The watcher would confirm the building was empty. Marek had been explicit about that—explicit with himself, at least. Empty building. He had drawn a line there eleven years ago and he drew it now: no people. His brother had been a problem; the people in the house had been witnesses; witnesses were eliminated. But he had gotten them out. Whatever else was true of him, he had gotten them out, and the girl was alive because of it, and if the annex had to go, it would go at two in the morning with no one inside it, and the county would lose some old paper and Marek would lose a problem, and the columns would balance.

He stood in the dark at the edge of the lot for four minutes by his watch. The building sat there, patient as a boat in a slip. Somewhere inside it, he supposed, were the originals of whatever the Okafor woman had copied—the fire report, the sealed investigation, the invoices, God knew what else. Eleven years of his careful architecture, and it all came down to a room full of paper with a broken door closer.

He thought: *I built half this town.* And it was true, that was the maddening thing, it was true—he had built the marina and the medical plaza and the renovated riverfront that the governor had come to cut the ribbon on. He had given this town its second act. And the town had repaid him with a records clerk who didn't know when to stop and a niece who wouldn't stay buried.

"You should have taken the money," he said quietly, to the building, to the girl, to no one.

Then he got back in the car and drove home, and on the way he called the man in Ellsworth one more time and said, "Tomorrow night. After the custodian leaves, before the night crew at the paper plant changes shift. And make it electrical. Panel fire, starts low, climbs the wall. Slow enough that the department gets a good look at how old that wiring is."

"You've done this before," the man said, and there was something in his voice—not admiration exactly. Recognition.

"Once," Marek said. "A long time ago."

"And it worked."

Marek looked out at the river, black and flat, holding the lights of the town upside down. "It worked," he said, "until it didn't."

* * *

He slept badly, which was rare, and woke at four and went down to the kitchen and made coffee he didn't drink and stood at the window over the water.

This was the hour he did his real accounting. Not the money—the money took care of itself, had for decades. He accounted for himself. He had learned it from his father, who had kept a leather book and written in it every morning, and Marek had found the book after the funeral and read it cover to cover and found, between the columns of figures, sentences like *paid the mill, overpaid again, weakness* and *was hard on the boy, necessary*. A man who audited himself. Marek had built his whole life on that foundation: that if you looked at yourself honestly, regularly, without flinching, you could not be corrupted, because corruption required self-deception and you had none.

So he looked.

He had eliminated his brother. He had exiled his sister-in-law and his niece into a life so small they could not be seen from space. He had bought a sheriff, a zoning board, two councilmen, and a newspaper editor, and he told himself it was civic management, that every town this size ran on some version of it, that he had simply professionalized what others did drunkenly. He had burned a building full of paper scheduled to die anyway. He had never, not once, hurt a person who didn't place themselves in the way.

And the girl. He had gotten her out. Whatever else was true.

He stood at the window until the sky went gray, and he noticed that his hands were steady, and he was relieved by that, and then he was disturbed by his relief, and then he poured the coffee out and went to work, because the work was the thing that had always held him together, the columns and the closings and the calls, and today there was so much to do: the donation to the historical society to announce, the insurance question to think through, the story about the wiring to seed with the editor at the *Courier* before lunch. A fire was not an event. A fire was a project with a communications plan.

At 8:15 his assistant buzzed to say Dee Okafor was on the line, and he smiled, actually smiled, and picked up.

"Miss Okafor. This is a pleasure."

"Mr. Vasquez." Her voice was flat and careful, the voice of a woman reading from a script she'd written that morning. "I'm calling because as of yesterday I'm no longer employed by the county, and I wanted you to hear that from me."

"That's unfortunate. You were good at your work."

"I copied some files," she said. "Before I left. Copies of things in the annex. And I made sure people know I have them. My sister has a set. My lawyer has a set. A reporter in Augusta has a set. So if anything happens to me, or to the copies, or—" a breath— "or to the annex, it doesn't matter. The paper's already out of the building."

Marek sat very still. Outside the window, a heron lifted off the water with an unhurried violence, and the morning went on being beautiful.

"You're telling me this," he said, "why?"

"Because I want you to know it's too late," Dee Okafor said. "Whatever you're thinking about doing. It's too late. The paper's out of the building."

And Marek, who had already made the call, who had already set the clock running on a fire that would begin in roughly eleven hours, heard the words land in the room like coins into a deep well, and understood—with the strange, weightless clarity that comes only to men who have made an irreversible error and cannot yet admit it—that she was telling the truth, and that it didn't matter, and that he was going to burn the building anyway.

Because the fire had never really been about the paper.

He said, "I'm sorry to hear about your job, Miss Okafor. If there's anything I can do—"

"There isn't," she said, and hung up.

He sat for a while. Then he picked up the phone and called the man in Ellsworth and said, "It's still on. Tonight." And the man said, "You heard what she said?" and Marek said, "I heard it," and hung up, and looked at his hands, and they were still steady, and that frightened him more than anything else had in eleven years.

Because he knew what it meant. He had read it in his father's leather book, in the margins, in his father's small hard hand, next to the entry for the worst year of the old man's life: *the hands stay steady too long, that is how you know.*

Outside, the river ran. The boats sat in their slips. And across town, in a room full of paper that no longer mattered, the oldest solution in Marek Vasquez's ledger was already moving toward him, patient as water, wearing the face of history about to rhyme.
