## 18. Smoke Over Halloway

### Dee

Dee Okafor had worked in the county archive long enough to know the building's moods—the way the old HVAC groaned at two a.m., the way the fluorescent over aisle nine flickered like it was trying to say something. So when she came back for the copied originals at eleven forty that night, she noticed immediately that something was wrong with the light.

It wasn't coming from inside.

She was still half a block away, her car parked crooked on River Street because she'd been too nervous to parallel park properly, and the windows of the annex were glowing orange from within. Not the flat amber of sodium lamps. This light moved. It breathed.

Her first thought, absurdly, was of her mother's kitchen in Lagos, the gas burner catching when the wind gusted through the window screen. Her second thought was: *the files.*

She didn't remember getting out of the car. She remembered the heat hitting her face at twenty feet, a wall of it, pushing back against her like a hand laid flat against her sternum. The annex door was metal and it would hold for a while yet—she could see that much through the smoke curling out under it—but the roof vents above were already coughing black.

"Hey!" she screamed, though there was no one to scream at. "HEY!"

The fire had been set well. That was the thing that frightened her most, standing there in the parking lot with her phone shaking in her grip. It wasn't sloppy. It wasn't a cigarette or a fault in ancient wiring. The flames were climbing the east wall in a neat, patient column, the way fires climb when accelerant has been poured along the baseboards by someone who understood that the county's night crew wouldn't come around until six.

Someone had wanted this building gone tonight, and someone had known exactly what was in it.

Dee dialed 911 with her thumb and then, before the dispatcher even finished speaking, hung up and dialed Jonah Reyes instead. Because the ledger—the photocopies, the certified extracts, the three weeks of her life spent hunched over a copier with her heart hammering like a fist on a door—was sitting in a gray filing box on the second floor, in the corner carrel where she'd hidden it behind a decade of unclaimed probate records.

And because she already knew, with the cold certainty of a woman who had just lost her job for exactly this reason, who had lit the match.

* * *

### Jonah

Jonah was awake when the call came. He was always awake now; sleep had become a country he visited briefly and without enthusiasm. He'd been sitting at the motel desk with the Marek file spread out around him like a card game he was losing—photographs, property transfers, the insurance claim histories going back fifteen years—and when Dee's name lit up his phone, his stomach dropped before he answered, because nobody called him at midnight with good news.

"The annex," Dee said. "It's burning. Right now. I'm here."

"Are you inside?"

"I'm *outside*, obviously."

"Stay outside." He was already pulling on his jacket, one-armed, grabbing the room key. "Dee. Listen to me. The copies—are they in your car?"

A pause. In the background, faintly, sirens beginning somewhere across town, still far off, still theoretical.

"No," she said. "They're in my carrel. Second floor."

He closed his eyes. "You hid them in the building you were stealing them from."

"I hid them in the building nobody checks because nobody cares about probate records from 2009, which is exactly why—" Her voice cracked, not with fear but with fury, and he found himself almost grateful for it. Fury was fuel. Fear just sat there. "Jonah, those are certified copies. If the originals burn and the copies burn, we have nothing but my word and Tamsin's memory, and Marek's lawyers will eat both."

"I know what we'd have." He was moving now, down the motel corridor, out into the wet dark. October air, the river smell, and above the rooftops to the northeast—a glow. Not sunrise. Sunrise was hours off and came from the east. This was north, toward the civic complex, and it pulsed.

"Get back from the building," he said. "When the fire department comes, tell them everything except the box. Then find me."

"And if the box burns?"

He stood in the motel parking lot with the fire's glow staining the low clouds, and he thought about his sister Marisol, who had been nineteen, and about the insurance investigator in El Paso who had told him, gently, that sometimes buildings simply burn, and about the eleven years since, and about the word *sometimes* and how it could be purchased.

"Then we do it the hard way," he said. "But it won't burn. Dee—it won't burn, because whoever set this wanted the *originals* gone, and they don't know about your copies. They think they're erasing everything. People who think they're erasing everything get careless about the rest."

He hung up and drove toward the fire, and only at the last intersection did it occur to him, with a chill that had nothing to do with the open window, that if Marek's people knew enough to burn the annex tonight, they might also know enough to be watching who came to look at it.

* * *

### Tamsin

Tamsin saw the glow from the bridge.

They had been walking—unable to sit still in the motel, unable to sleep, the dinner with Marek still stuck in their throat like a fishbone. Three hours since they'd left his house on the bluff, three hours since he'd smiled across the roast and said *I've doubled the deposit, Aurelia, family takes care of family,* and Tamsin had given him the smile, the ending smile, the one their mother used to call *the smile that closes doors.* They had walked out into the dark afterward with their hands trembling and their mind strangely clear, and they had walked, and walked, and now they stood on the middle of the Halloway Bridge with the river sliding black beneath them and the sky to the north turning the color of a wound.

For one full second, Tamsin's body made the decision for them.

*Run.* Every nerve said it. Eleven years of training said it. Run the other way, go back to the city, pack the small life into its small boxes, mail no more postcards, become no one again, become whoever was next. Their legs actually turned. Their feet actually took two steps southward, toward the bus station, toward the safe direction, toward survival as they had always practiced it—which was to say, survival as retreat, survival as amputation, survival as the slow subtraction of everything that might burn.

Then they stopped.

Because they knew whose fire this was. Not whose hands, maybe, not whose matches—but whose logic. The same logic that had come for the house on Calder Street when their father talked about going to the authorities. The same solution, applied again, to the same problem: *a Vasquez who talks.* And this time the thing burning wasn't a home with their family asleep inside it. This time it was paper. Evidence. Dee's three weeks of stolen nights. Maybe Dee herself.

Tamsin stood on the bridge and felt terror move through them like weather, and then felt something else arrive underneath it, quiet and enormous, like bedrock under topsoil.

*You ran once,* they thought. *You were nine years old and someone put you in a car and you ran for eleven years, and it kept you alive, and it cost everything, and it did not save anyone else.*

They turned north.

They ran toward the fire.

Later, they would try to describe the feeling and fail. It was not bravery; it did not feel like courage, which they imagined felt like wings. It felt like setting down something heavy after carrying it so long the weight had become part of the body. Each step toward the glow was a step out of the wardrobe, out of the false name, out of the postcards addressed to ash. The heat reached them a block away, pressing against their face, and instead of flinching they leaned into it, the way you lean into a door you have finally decided to open.

The annex was fully involved when they arrived. Fire trucks were screaming up Civic Center Drive, and a ragged line of neighbors stood in the parking lot in bathrobes and jackets, faces lit orange, everyone doing the small-town arithmetic of *whose building, whose fault, whose night is ruined.* Dee Okafor stood apart from all of them, arms wrapped around herself, staring at the roof.

Tamsin crossed the lot at a dead run and caught her arm.

"Dee—the box—is it—"

Dee turned, and in the firelight Tamsin watched her face do something complicated: relief, then grief, then a hard bright anger that looked, Tamsin realized, exactly like their own reflection.

"It's upstairs," Dee said. "Second floor, northwest carrel. And before you ask—yes, I know. I know what I did. I know why it's burning." Her voice was steady and terrible. "Marek is burning my career tonight, Tamsin. He can burn the building. He doesn't get to burn the truth."

"How do you know he can't?"

"Because I'm not stupid, I'm an archivist." For the first time, something like a smile. "There are three sets of copies. One's in my trunk. One's in a post office box in Corvallis under my aunt's name. And one—" she nodded at the flames, at the collapsing roar of the roof giving way— "one is wherever Jonah decided to hide it, because I never told him where mine were and he never told me where his went. We learned that from watching you."

Tamsin stared at her. "From me?"

"You wrote to a dead address for eleven years," Dee said. "You kept every copy. You built a paper trail out of love letters to a ghost. You're the most documented invisible person in this state." The firelight moved across her face. "We just did what you taught us."

* * *

### Jonah

By the time Jonah reached the scene, the annex was beyond saving and everyone knew it. The firefighters weren't fighting so much as containing—hosing down the exposures, keeping the flames off the main courthouse, letting the old wooden annex die. Water arced through the smoke and turned to steam. The smell was ink and varnish and a century of county paper becoming weather.

He found Dee and Tamsin at the edge of the lot, and the sight of the two of them together—Tamsin's face streaked with soot they must have gotten leaning past a barricade, Dee's jaw set like poured concrete—gave him a feeling he hadn't had in eleven years, which was the feeling of not being alone in a doorway at 3 a.m. watching something burn.

"They'll sift the ruins," he said, coming up beside them. "Arson investigators will be in there by morning. They'll find the pour patterns, the origin point. That's evidence too—not our evidence, but evidence. Somebody will read that fire the way I read the El Paso file."

"Somebody always reads it eventually," Dee said. "That's the whole point of archives. Even burned ones."

"Tamsin." Jonah turned to them. "This changes the clock. Marek just committed arson against a government building while a grand jury is being empaneled. He's panicked. Panicked men make mistakes, but they also make moves—he'll come for you next, and he won't use fire, he'll use lawyers, or worse, he'll use the sheriff's office. You need to decide. Tonight. Whether you testify."

Tamsin looked at the burning building for a long moment. Embers were rising off the roof in spirals, dozens of them, climbing the smoke column like sparks off a struck flint, rising and rising until they cooled and went dark. Tamsin watched one climb higher than the rest, watched it hang there against the black, and Jonah saw their expression change—not into peace, nothing so gentle, but into decision, which is harder and lasts longer.

"He already came for me," Tamsin said. "Eleven years ago. Tonight I'm just returning the visit." They pulled the rubber band off the box of postcards they'd been carrying all week like contraband, and held it against their chest. "First thing tomorrow, I walk into the county clerk's office and I say my name. My real one. Out loud, on the record, spelled correctly."

"Aurelia Vasquez," Dee said quietly. Testing it. Giving it back.

"Yes," Tamsin said, and the word came out steady, and above them the annex roof folded inward with a sound like a held breath finally released, sending up a tower of sparks that everyone in the parking lot turned to watch—everyone except Tamsin, who kept their eyes on the two people standing beside them, the first witnesses of the new life, while behind them eleven years of silence went up in smoke.

"Tomorrow," Jonah said, and shook their hand, and did not let go right away.

Somewhere across town, a phone was ringing in a marina office. None of them heard it. But all of them, in their separate ways, were already imagining the man answering it—and the exact moment his collar would stop sitting straight.
