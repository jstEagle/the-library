## 1. The Inventory

The shoebox lived on the top shelf of Petra's closet, behind the winter boots they never wore because this was California and the boots had been an impulse purchase during a rainstorm in 2019, and beside a hatbox that contained nothing but the hatbox's own history. It was a plain box, once home to a pair of shoes Petra could not remember owning—brown leather, size nine, some earlier self's idea of professionalism—and it held, as of last Tuesday, five hundred and twenty-one postcards.

Five hundred and twenty-two, technically, if you counted the one still sitting half-written on the kitchen table, which Petra did not count until it was finished. The rule was: finished or it doesn't exist. Ten years of rules like that, small and load-bearing, and the whole structure of a life balanced on them.

Petra took the box down on Sunday mornings, which was not a rule so much as a gravity. They carried it to the table, set it beside the cold coffee, and lifted the lid with both hands, the way you'd lift the lid off something sleeping.

Inside, the carbon copies lay in loose chronological drift, though every January Petra re-sorted them by year and rubber-banded each twelve-month sheaf together, so that now the box looked like a bundle of gray twigs. The copies themselves were flimsy things, thin as onion skin, the ink faded from black to a tired brown. On the back of each one, in the space where an address would go, Petra had written the same thing for ten years:

*The house, Larch Street.*

Not a street number. Not a city. Just *the house*, as if the postal service might know which one, as if mail were a kind of prayer that found its recipient by intention alone.

The front of each card was different. That was the other rule. You couldn't send the same picture twice; that would be lazy, and whatever else this was, it was not lazy. Petra had bought postcards at gas stations and museum gift shops and the rotating wire rack outside a pharmacy in Eureka that seemed to stock nothing printed after 1987. There were lighthouses. There were sunsets over bodies of water that may or may not have been oceans. There was a card depicting a giant artichoke with a smiling face, purchased in a moment of weakness outside Castroville, which Petra had almost not copied because it felt disrespectful to the project, whatever the project was, and then had copied anyway, because the artichoke was laughing and somebody in that burned-out kitchen had laughed like that once, high and surprised, and it seemed important to keep a record of the fact that laughing had happened here, in this family, historically speaking.

The message side was where the real inventory began.

Petra sorted the way their aunt Marguerite used to sort buttons: not by any system an outsider would recognize, but with total confidence in a logic that revealed itself only after an hour of handling. Today's logic was weather. Petra pulled out every card that mentioned rain.

*It rained all week. I stayed in and read a book about glaciers, which felt right.*

*I got caught in the rain without my coat and stood there like an idiot letting it happen. You'd have said something dry about wool.*

*Rain again. The gutters sound like applause.*

That last one was from year three, back when Petra still believed the ritual might loosen its grip if they just kept showing up to it. Year three had been full of metaphors. Year seven had been mostly grocery lists. *Bought eggs. Thought of the kitchen.* There was no kitchen anymore; there hadn't been a kitchen in twenty-two years. There was a lot, chain-link fenced, weeds up to the sills of nothing, and yet the sentence *thought of the kitchen* required no clarification, because there was only one kitchen in the world that meant anything, and both of them—the writer and the unread recipient—knew exactly which one.

Except the recipient didn't know anything. The recipient was ash and a foundation slab and whatever the city had hauled away in 2003. That was the whole point, or the whole problem, or possibly both, filed under the same heading the way grief and habit tend to share a file.

Petra counted the rain cards. Fourteen. Then they counted the cards that mentioned Dree—eleven—and the ones that mentioned their mother Vivian—six, all careful, all circling, none landing anywhere. Then, because counting was its own kind of ritual and rituals breed like rabbits, they counted the total again and got five hundred and twenty-one, which matched, which was somehow both comforting and faintly disappointing, like a fortune cookie that says what you expected.

One postcard a week. Fifty-two weeks. Ten years, minus the week in 2016 when Petra had the flu and slept through Sunday and Monday and woke Wednesday with the wild conviction that they'd missed something unforgivable, and had written two cards that week to make it up—one to the house, and one, absurdly, to the flu, which was not mailed either, and which lived in the box between a card about a county fair and a card that said only *I'm fine, stop asking*, addressed to nobody Petra could identify even at the time.

The carbon paper had been Marguerite's idea originally, or rather, Marguerite's stationery. Petra had inherited a desk drawer of supplies when the old woman moved into assisted living—a tin of paperclips, a blotter, two boxes of carbon sheets, the kind of office archaeology nobody does anymore. Petra had taken the carbons out of a practical impulse, the impulse of a person who writes things down, and then discovered what the carbons made possible: proof. A record. If you wrote to a house that didn't exist, at least the writing itself existed, twice-removed, ghost of a ghost, but legible. You could hold it up later and say: see. I said things. All these years, I kept saying things.

To whom was another question. To the house. To whoever had been in the kitchen that night. To the version of themselves who'd stood on the lawn in a borrowed jacket watching the windows go orange, age eleven, holding a garden hose that someone had pressed into their hands as if water were going to matter.

Petra did not think about the fire while sorting. That was a rule too, though an unwritten one, enforced by the body rather than the mind: the chest tightened, the jaw set, and the hand reached automatically for the next card, the next year, the safe territory of weather and groceries and the smiling artichoke. The fire lived at the bottom of the box the way sediment lives at the bottom of a lake, and Petra's method, refined over a decade, was to skim the surface and call it swimming.

But today the sorting went long. The coffee went cold and then Petra made more coffee and let that go lukewarm too, and somewhere around the 2019 bundle—the year of the boots, the year everything was fine, allegedly—they came across a card they didn't remember writing.

That wasn't possible. Petra remembered all of them. That was the curse of the practice: perfect recall of five hundred trivial messages, each one tagged in memory like Marguerite's buttons. But there it was, in their own handwriting, dated October of that year:

*Dear house — I dreamed the garden door was open. In the dream I wasn't scared. I walked in and the kitchen was whole and someone was standing at the stove with their back to me and I woke up before they turned around. Ten years and I still don't know who I'm hoping it is.*

Petra sat with that for a while. The apartment was quiet in the way apartments are on Sunday mornings when everyone else is at brunch performing happiness. Downstairs, a dog expressed opinions. Somewhere a car alarm began and gave up.

Here was the thing Petra had never written on any card, carbon or otherwise: the ritual worked. That was the humiliating truth of it. Writing to a burned-down house every week for ten years had kept something intact that would otherwise have collapsed, and Petra knew this the way you know your own pulse. As long as the postcards kept going—to the house, to no one, to the sealed vault of that night—the silence in the family remained orderly. Contained. Architectural. Everyone knew where the silence was; it had an address. You could visit it weekly. You could leave your small offerings of weather and eggs at its door and walk away unburned.

The alternative was asking. And asking was the one thing the family had agreed on, without ever agreeing, without ever saying a word about it in anyone's living room: you don't ask. You don't ask what happened the night of the fire. You don't ask why Uncle Raymond left town the following spring and never came back, not even for funerals. You don't ask why Vivian changes the subject to precipitation with the smoothness of a croupier sweeping chips off a table. You don't ask why Theo's father, Gerald, always parked down the block from family gatherings, as if proximity were a debt he was avoiding collection on.

You write a postcard instead. One a week. Ten years running. Armor and wound, wound and armor, indistinguishable after enough winters.

Petra finished the coffee. Outside, the light had gone from morning to noon without ceremony, the way it does in late summer, spending itself freely. They looked at the empty postcard on the table—the week's card, still blank, waiting—and picked up the pen.

The picture side showed a bridge in fog. Fine. Bridges in fog were reliable; they suggested passage without promising arrival, which was the correct emotional register for correspondence with a ruin.

*Dear house,* Petra wrote. *Nothing much to report. Weather mild. Work fine. I reorganized the—*

The pen stopped.

This happened sometimes, rarely, maybe twice in ten years: the sentence simply refused to continue, not because there was nothing to say but because there was too much, backed up behind the pen like cars behind an accident. Petra stared at the fog. The dog downstairs had achieved silence. The carbon sheet lay ready beneath the card, prepared to make its faithful copy, its proof, its little twin.

*I reorganized the,* Petra wrote again, and stopped again, and understood suddenly that they were tired. Not sleepy-tired. Structural-tired. The kind of tired that comes from holding a roof up with your shoulders for a decade and calling it posture.

They put the pen down. They did not finish the card. For the first time in ten years, the week's postcard sat unfinished on the table, and the world, notably, did not end. No lightning. No phone call from the void. Just the fog on the bridge, the cold coffee, the box of gray twigs with its lid off, breathing.

Petra should have felt freed. Instead they felt exposed, the way you feel when a wall you've leaned on turns out to be a door.

They were still sitting there, staring at the half-sentence, when the mail slot clacked and an envelope dropped onto the mat. Legal-sized. Heavy cream stock. Petra's name typed out in a font that had opinions about serif.

They opened it standing up, which was instinct, the body wanting to be vertical for bad news.

It was from a probate firm in the town Petra had spent ten years writing to. Aunt Marguerite's estate, the letter said, was settled at last, and her property—the house on Corliss Avenue, the last intact family property, with its attic and its rooms and its decades of accumulated everything—would go to public auction at month's end.

And below that, in a paragraph of its own, in the same tidy type, as if it were a scheduling detail and not a detonation:

*Please note the Larch Street parcel (formerly [address]) is scheduled for demolition and grading within the same period, per municipal order.*

Petra read it twice. Then a third time, slower, the words rearranging themselves each pass like furniture in a dark room.

The lot was coming down. The address on five hundred and twenty-one postcards was about to stop existing even as an absence.

And Marguerite's house—Marguerite's wardrobe, Marguerite's drawers, Marguerite's papers, whatever an old woman keeps when she has spent a lifetime being the family's memory—was about to be sold to strangers by the boxful.

Petra looked at the shoebox. At the unfinished card. At the phone, which sat on the counter looking innocent, and which rang, at that exact moment, with Dree's number glowing on the screen, because sisters have radar for precisely the wrong moments.

Petra let it ring twice. Then, with the envelope still in one hand and the pen still uncapped on the table behind them, they answered.
