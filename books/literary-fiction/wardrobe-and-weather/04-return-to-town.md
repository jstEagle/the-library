## 4. Return to Town

The drive took six hours, which was exactly long enough for Petra to rehearse every possible version of the trip and none of the one that actually happened.

They had planned it like a heist, in a way. In and out. The auction preview was Saturday; they would arrive Friday evening, sleep badly in some motel with a continental breakfast, attend the preview with the detached air of a professional appraiser—Petra had once interviewed an appraiser for a novel and could do the voice, all *provenance* and *condition reports*—and leave Sunday before anyone could ask them anything at a dinner table. A surgical strike on their own childhood. They'd even packed light: two shirts, a jacket, the shoebox riding shotgun in the passenger seat like a small, obedient dog.

What actually happened was that Petra missed the turn off the highway because they were thinking about Aunt Marguerite's handwriting, and the detour took them through Millbrook—the next town over, where Dree had her first kiss behind the feed store and swore Petra to secrecy for eleven years—and by the time Petra got back on track they were hungry and disoriented and driving past a hand-painted sign for a diner called The Blue Plate Special, which was either charming or a threat.

They stopped. They ate a grilled cheese that tasted like 1997. They tipped too much, the way you do when you're trying to buy absolution from a town.

It was nearly dark when they crossed into Harlow proper, and Petra understood immediately that they had made a tactical error of the heart: they should have arrived in daylight. Towns are different at night. At night, Harlow didn't look changed or unchanged—it looked *unresolved*, like a sentence someone had left hanging. Streetlights came on in the same stuttering sequence Petra remembered from childhood, as if the grid itself were still counting to ten before committing. The hardware store was now something called a Wellness Collective. The movie theater was still the movie theater but its marquee read LIVE MUSIC FRIDAYS in letters that had clearly been installed by an optimist.

Petra drove past Larch Street without turning onto it. That was deliberate. That was strategy. You don't walk past the grave on the first night; you acclimate. You let the town get used to your car.

The Pine Rest Motel sat at the edge of town where the road gave up and became farmland, and it was exactly what motels in novels are supposed to be, which Petra found almost insulting in its fidelity: a long low building the color of weak tea, a neon sign missing its second E so it advertised PINE R ST, ice machine humming like it knew something. The desk clerk was maybe nineteen and did not recognize the name Thornbury, which was, Petra reflected, the entire point of having left.

"Room's quiet," the clerk said, sliding the key card across. "Except the ice machine. And sometimes the highway. And Mr. Okafor in four watches TV with the volume up because of his hearing."

"So not quiet," Petra said.

"Nope." The clerk said it cheerfully, like a man delivering good news.

Room 7 smelled faintly of chlorine and older cigarettes, a smell Petra decided to think of as *atmosphere*. They set the shoebox on the dresser, squared it with the edge, then moved it into the drawer, then took it back out because hiding it felt like lying to no one, which was a specialty of theirs but not tonight. They hung up the two shirts. They stood at the window looking at the parking lot, at the single sodium lamp turning everything the color of old broth, and told themselves firmly that tomorrow they would be efficient. Preview. Wardrobe. Out.

They slept the way you sleep in motels: in installments, with commercial breaks.

* * *

Morning arrived gray and apologetic. Petra drank the in-room coffee, which tasted like it had been brewed from a photograph of coffee beans, and put on the jacket, and stood in the doorway of room 7 for a full minute doing nothing, which is a thing people do when the next thing is large.

Larch Street was a twelve-minute walk from the motel if you cut behind the church, and Petra knew this the way you know things from a former life—not remembered, exactly, more *resident in the body*, the way your feet still know a staircase in a house that's been sold twice. They walked. The morning was cool enough to justify the jacket and warm enough to make them regret it, and the light had that washed-out quality particular to towns built near water, though Harlow wasn't near water and never had been, and Petra had never been able to explain the quality of the light to anyone who hadn't grown up under it.

The church was still there. The oak on the corner of Larch and Vine was still there, bigger now, its roots buckling the sidewalk in the same place they'd always buckled it—one of those details so stable it felt like a personal message. *We kept your crack.*

And then the lot.

Petra had known, of course. They'd driven past it last night without turning, and they'd seen the aerial view on the county assessor's website during a three a.m. session they thought of as research and everyone else would call spiraling. Knowing and seeing turned out to be different animals entirely.

The house was gone. Obviously. It had been gone for twenty-two years. But knowing a thing and standing in front of its absence are separated by a distance no amount of knowing can close. The lot was maybe half an acre of weeds going gold at the tips, foxtail and thistle and something climbing and ambitious that had already swallowed the remains of the fence. Chain-link ran around the perimeter, sagging in places, propped up in others by stakes that had been driven in by someone who cared briefly and then stopped. There was a NO TRESPASSING sign, rusted to a soft orange, and beneath it a smaller sign, newer: **AUCTION — SEE COUNTY LISTING**. Someone had tied a strip of yellow surveyor's tape to the gate, and it moved in the wind with a small dry persistence, like a person clearing their throat before saying something difficult.

That was it. That was the whole of it. Weeds, wire, tape.

Petra stood across the street with their hands in their jacket pockets and felt the strangest sensation, which was not grief exactly, or not only grief, but a kind of vertigo of scale—the understanding that an entire architecture of silence, twenty-two years of careful not-asking, had been built to cover a rectangle of dirt that a determined goat could clear in an afternoon. All those postcards. Ten years of weekly postcards mailed to a foundation and some foxtail. The post office had surely been returning them, or binning them, or—no. Petra had addressed them to the house, and the house had no mailbox, and somewhere in the machinery of the postal service there was presumably a procedure for mail addressed to places that don't exist, and Petra had never asked what that procedure was. Some questions you keep fed so they stay alive.

A crow worked at something in the weeds. A truck went by, slowed slightly—the universal instinct of drivers passing a pedestrian in a small town, cataloguing them—and moved on.

"You'll want to watch the thistle," said a voice. "It's vicious this year."

Petra turned.

An older woman stood on the porch of the house directly opposite the lot, holding a watering can, wearing a cardigan the color of canned peas. She was small and round and her hair was white in the specific way that suggested it had been pink once, on purpose, decades ago, and simply given up. Petra did not know her. Petra knew her instantly.

"Mrs. Okonkwo," Petra said, before the name had cleared customs. "You used to—" They gestured vaguely, a gesture meant to encompass roses, complaints about the Thornburys' hedge, lemonade distributed during the fire's aftermath with a gravity appropriate to a field hospital.

"I did use to." Mrs. Okonkwo set down the watering can and came to the edge of her porch steps, and her face did something complicated—rearranged itself around recognition the way water rearranges around a dropped stone. "Well," she said. "Well, look at you."

Here is what Petra had spent ten years constructing: invisibility as a discipline. In the city, they were a person of no fixed narrative—a writer, sure, but writers are invisible by trade; you sit in rooms and watch people and nobody watches you back, or if they do, they see a generic middle-aged person in a good jacket. Petra had cultivated this. Had *watered* it, like Mrs. Okonkwo's roses. Being unrecognized was a skill, and Petra had gotten good, and part of getting good was forgetting what it cost.

So when this small woman in the pea-colored cardigan looked across the street at them and her whole face opened—not politely, not socially, but *entirely*, the way faces open at airports and funerals—Petra felt it physically, a kind of pressure behind the sternum, the shock of being seen after years of chosen invisibility. It was like walking out of a dark theater at noon.

"You look just like her," Mrs. Okonkwo said, warmly, terribly. "You know that? Just like Marguerite. Same way of standing, even. Like you're waiting for somebody to finish a sentence so you can correct it."

"That's—" Petra started, and found they had no ending for the sentence. Their throat had closed in a manner they found professionally humiliating. They were a writer. Words were the tools. And here was a retired woman with a watering can reducing them to a person making small strangled sounds on a public street.

Mrs. Okonkwo, mercifully, talked enough for both of them, which seemed to be her lifelong vocation. "I said it at the funeral, I said it to your sister—Dree, is it, Dree has her father's chin but she's got Marguerite's way of listening, which is to say she listens like she's taking notes for later. And now here you are." She beamed. "For the auction, I expect. Everybody's talking about it. Half the town wants the piano. The other half wants whatever's in the attic, on account of Marguerite being Marguerite."

"On account of her being—" Petra managed.

"A keeper." Mrs. Okonkwo said it with the simple authority of a woman who had lived opposite three generations of Thornburys and filed them all correctly. "She never threw anything out. Your aunt. Forty years I lived across from that woman and I never saw her put so much as a jar lid in the trash without considering it first. Some people called it clutter." She picked up the watering can again, held it against her hip like a holstered weapon. "I called it a record."

Something went through Petra then, cold and quick, a fish turning under the surface of a pond. *A record.* But they only said, "You knew her well?"

"Well enough to borrow sugar and not well enough to borrow anything else." Mrs. Okonkwo smiled, and for just a moment the smile had edges. "That was the arrangement on this street, honey. Everybody friendly, everybody private. Your family especially. Lovely people. Very quiet." She watered a geranium that did not appear to need it. "Quiet like a lake is quiet. Looks calm till you think about what's under it."

A truck rumbled past. The yellow tape at the gate lifted and fell.

"I should go," Petra said, because staying felt dangerous in a way they couldn't have defended in court. "Thank you, Mrs. Okonkwo. For the—for remembering me."

"Oh, I remember everybody." She said it lightly, but Petra heard the other sentence underneath it, the one she wasn't saying, the one that might have been *I remember the night of the fire, too, and I remember who came out of which door,* or might have been nothing at all, just a lonely woman enjoying company. With lakes, you couldn't tell. That was the point of lakes.

"Come by before you leave town," Mrs. Okonkwo called as Petra crossed back toward the sidewalk. "I've got photos. Nobody ever wants the photos until forty years later, and then everybody wants the photos."

Petra raised a hand without turning around, an acknowledgment that committed to nothing, and walked.

* * *

They walked for an hour afterward, not going anywhere, letting the town reacquaint itself with them street by street. The school. The park with the bandstand that had never hosted a band in living memory. The pharmacy, still the pharmacy, miracle of miracles, its window display now dominated by an inflatable cartoon thermometer nearly the size of a man. Gentle humor was available everywhere if you looked; the town was quietly ridiculous, the way all towns are, and Petra clung to the ridiculousness like a handrail. It was easier than the other thing.

Because the other thing kept surfacing. *Just like her. Same way of standing.* Twenty-two years of careful erasure, undone by a woman with a watering can in under a minute. And worse—worse was the word *record*. Marguerite had kept things. Everyone said so. The wardrobe, the attic, forty years of considered jar lids. And now the wardrobe was going up for auction in thirty-six hours, tagged and lot-numbered, and Petra was going to stand in that house tomorrow among strangers while Marguerite's whole careful archive was priced per item.

Back at the motel, Petra sat on the bed with the shoebox in their lap and did not open it. They knew what was inside. Four hundred and some carbon copies, one per week, ten years, each one beginning *Dear Larch Street* because you can't write *Dear House* and you can't write *Dear Ashes* and there is no salutation for a place. Each one describing weather, mostly. Weather and small news. *Rained here Tuesday. Finished chapter nine. The maple on my block is turning early.* Never once: *Who was in the kitchen? Who went out the garden door? Why does nobody say her name in front of Mother?*

The ritual had been armor. Petra had always known that. What they were only now beginning to suspect, sitting in room 7 with the ice machine churning its slow opinion outside, was that armor is also a container. It keeps things out. It also keeps things in, pressed close against the skin, all that time.

Their phone buzzed. Dree.

*Heard you're in town!!* Two exclamation points, which from Dree was a controlled substance. *Theo says the preview's worth seeing. Dinner Sunday? Before you bolt?*

Petra looked at the message for a long time. Then they typed: *Maybe. Looking at the lot today.*

They sent it before they could soften it, and then sat watching the screen, waiting for the deflection to arrive. It took ninety seconds.

*There's not really a lot to look at lol. See you at the preview?*

*There's not really a lot to look at.* Petra set the phone face-down on the bedspread and laughed once, a short sound with nothing in it, and reached for the shoebox after all—because tomorrow the wardrobe, and tomorrow Theo with his friendly watchfulness, and tomorrow the house full of Marguerite's labeled ghosts. But tonight, one postcard, week four hundred and eighty-one, and for the first time in ten years, Petra didn't know how to begin it.
