## 8. Theo's Offer

The call came at seven in the morning, which Petra had learned was Theo's way of making a thing feel official. Nobody called at seven in the morning to chat. People called at seven in the morning to sell you something, or to tell you someone had died, or—apparently—to propose a conspiracy dressed as a family reunion.

"Petra! You're up." He sounded delighted, as though her consciousness were a gift he'd personally arranged.

"I'm always up at seven," Petra said, sitting on the motel bed with the phone pressed to their ear and the curtains still dark against the window. "I'm fifty-three, not seventeen."

"Fifty-two."

"Thank you for the correction. It's the only birthday present anyone's given me in years."

Theo laughed, and it was a good laugh, warm and unguarded, and that was the problem with Theo. He made you want to believe him before he'd said anything worth believing. He'd been doing it since they were children, when he could convince an entire backyard of cousins that the creek behind Marguerite's house ran all the way to the ocean if you followed it long enough, and then lead them two miles through brambles to prove it, arriving scratched and triumphant at a drainage ditch he'd describe, without a flicker of irony, as "technically tidal."

"So here's my thought," he said. "And hear me out before you say anything, because I know you're going to want to say something."

"That's usually how conversation works."

"Hear me out anyway." A breath, the sound of him arranging himself. "The house. Marguerite's house. I've been thinking about it, and I think we should buy it. All of us. Together."

Petra pulled the blanket over their knees, though the room wasn't cold. "Buy it ourselves. The whole family."

"The four of us who are left who'd care. Me, you, Dree, and Aunt Vivian's trust can chip in if she wants—I already asked her lawyer, he says it's clean. We pool the money, we outbid the developers, we keep it in the family." He said *keep it in the family* the way other people said *amen*. "And then nobody has to deal with strangers walking through Marguerite's bedrooms measuring for drywall. Doesn't that sound better? Doesn't that just sound—simpler?"

There it was. *Simpler.* Petra looked at the ceiling of the motel room, at the water stain shaped vaguely like a hand reaching for something.

"It sounds expensive," Petra said.

"It's not, split four ways. And I can front more than my share if anybody's short. I'm not being noble about it, I just—" another breath, and now Petra could hear the eagerness underneath, the thing moving beneath the warmth like a fish under ice, "—I just think some things shouldn't get sold off in pieces. To strangers. You know?"

"I know," Petra said, because it was true, and because agreeing was easier than saying what they actually thought, which was: *Why does this matter so much to you, Theo? Why did you find me at the preview within ten minutes of my arrival? Why do you keep using the word 'simple' like it's a rope you're throwing me?*

"When were you thinking?" Petra asked instead.

"This week. The auction's Saturday. I'd need to know by Thursday, honestly, so I can talk to the bank." A pause, and then, softer, coaxing: "It would be good, wouldn't it? Having one place that's ours. One place where everything's still where she left it."

*Everything's still where she left it.* Petra thought about the wardrobe, the tags in Marguerite's careful script, the dress that smelled faintly of smoke. They thought about the shoebox back home, ten years of carbon copies addressed to a house that no longer existed, and how every single postcard had been, in its way, an attempt to keep things exactly where they were left—which is to say, buried.

"Theo," Petra said carefully, "can I ask you something?"

"You just did."

"A real one."

A silence on the line, brief but audible, like a door easing shut somewhere in his house.

"Sure," he said. "Anything."

"Why now? You've had ten years to buy this house. Ten years while Marguerite was alive, even. Why does it matter this month?"

The pause that followed was longer than the first one. Long enough that Petra could hear a clock ticking in Theo's kitchen, or maybe it was his own pulse transmitted down the wire; long enough for Petra to understand that the question had landed somewhere he hadn't prepared for, and that he was now deciding between answers.

"Because she's gone," he said finally, and his voice had changed—not colder, exactly, but more managed. "Because once it's sold, it's gone forever, and I guess I'm the kind of person who doesn't figure out what things mean until they're about to be taken away. That's not a crime, Petra."

"No," Petra agreed. "It's not a crime."

"And you'll think about it? Thursday?"

"I'll think about it."

"Good. Good." The warmth came back, reinstalled like software. "Hey—it was really good seeing you at the preview. It's been too long. We should do dinner while you're in town. All of us. Like old times."

*Like old times* meant Dree there as a buffer, meant Vivian's charming sealed presence, meant everyone performing the family at close range. Petra said they'd think about that too, and hung up, and sat for a long time watching the water stain on the ceiling reach for whatever it was reaching for.

* * *

They went to the diner at noon, ostensibly for lunch, actually because the motel coffee tasted like a punishment and because sitting alone in that room with the wardrobe's memory was becoming its own kind of weather. The diner hadn't changed. Same vinyl booths the color of mustard, same pie case fogged with condensation, same waitress—Dolores, older now, her name tag held on with tape—who squinted at Petra for three full seconds before saying, "Thornbury. Larch Street Thornbury," with the particular reverence small towns reserve for people who left and came back looking slightly haunted.

Word traveled fast in towns like this. Petra had been back four days and already felt catalogued.

They were halfway through a grilled cheese they weren't tasting when Theo walked in, spotted them, and raised both hands in a gesture of comic surrender, as though to say *I come in peace, I promise, put down your weapons.*

"Stalking me now?" Petra said as he slid into the booth across from them.

"Dolores makes the best patty melt in the county. This is my office." He flagged her down without looking, ordered by pointing, and turned back with the easy grin. "Also, yes. Slightly stalking you. I wanted to catch you before Thursday."

"You could have called."

"I did call. You gave me nothing on the phone. You're a stone wall on the phone, Petra, you always have been. In person you at least have tells." He leaned forward, elbows on the table, and studied them with open affection. "You look tired."

"I'm sleeping in a motel across from a weed lot. Everyone looks tired doing that."

"The Fairview's not that bad."

"The Fairview has a hot tub that's been 'coming soon' since 2011. There's a sign. It's yellowed."

That got the laugh again, and for a moment it was almost easy, the way it had been easy at the preview, the two of them drifting through Marguerite's rooms trading memories like kids trading baseball cards. Theo holding up a hideous ceramic owl: *She won this at the fair, 1987, she was so proud of it.* Petra finding a hatbox of gloves: *She wore these to Dad's funeral and cried the whole time and never told anyone why she kept them.* Tenderness, real tenderness, threaded through with that watchfulness Petra couldn't shake—the sense that Theo's eyes tracked not just the rooms but Petra's reactions to the rooms, filing each one away.

The patty melts arrived. Theo ate like a man refueling between important meetings, efficient and cheerful, and then set down his half and folded his hands, and Petra recognized the posture: this was the meeting part.

"Here's the thing," Theo said. "I don't think you're going to say yes. Not straight out. So let me say my piece and then you can say yours, and we'll still be friends at the end of it, okay?"

"Okay," Petra said, wary now, the grilled cheese going cold.

"I think you came back here to be angry." He said it gently, no accusation in it, which somehow made it worse. "And you're entitled. God knows. But anger needs a place to live, Petra, and if the house gets sold to some developer and bulldozed into condos, your anger's got nowhere to sit down. It just floats around. Whereas if we own it—if it stays ours—you can come stand in it whenever you want. Ask whatever you want. Look at whatever you want." He spread his hands. "I'm not offering you a house. I'm offering you permission."

The word *permission* sat on the table between them like a coin someone had flipped and refused to call.

"That's generous," Petra said slowly.

"It's meant to be."

"And it's also a cage with very nice wallpaper."

Theo didn't flinch. That was the remarkable thing—he didn't flinch at all. He just nodded, once, as if Petra had confirmed something he'd already priced in. "You found the wardrobe tags," he said.

Not a question. The diner noise seemed to recede around them, the way sound does underwater.

"Some of them," Petra said. "They're beautiful. She labeled everything. 'Worn the night of the storm.' 'Burnt sleeve, kept anyway.'"

"She labeled everything," Theo agreed. "Forty years of it. She was the family archivist whether we asked her to be or not." He picked up his patty melt, considered it, set it down again. "Here's my piece, since I promised. Whatever you think those tags add up to—whatever story you're building in that motel room—I'm asking you to hold off. Just hold off until after Saturday. Let us buy the house. Then open every drawer, read every tag, turn over every goddamn stone if you need to. I'll help you. But don't do it in public. Don't do it in a way that—" he searched for the word, and Petra watched him search, watched him choose, "—that costs people who didn't choose anything."

"Costs them what?"

"You know what."

"I genuinely don't, Theo. That's the thing. I don't know what anything cost, because nobody ever told me anything. That's sort of the entire problem."

He looked at them for a long moment, and something moved across his face—something that might have been pity, or might have been fear wearing pity's coat. "You were eleven," he said quietly. "You were a kid. Some doors are locked for a reason, and the reason isn't cruelty. Sometimes the reason is that what's behind the door doesn't stay behind the door once you open it. It comes out. It comes out and it spreads around the whole house and everybody breathes it."

"Is that what happened?" Petra asked. "Twenty-two years ago? Did something come out and spread around?"

The waitress appeared with the coffee pot, cheerful, oblivious, filling cups that neither of them touched, and the spell broke—or rather, Theo broke it, gratefully, visibly, thanking Dolores with a smile so quick and bright it could have cut glass. When she moved away, he was already standing, pulling bills from his wallet, laying them on the table with a flourish.

"Lunch is mine," he said. "Think about the offer. Thursday." He shrugged into his jacket, then paused, one hand on the booth's edge, and for a second the cheerfulness dropped entirely and Petra saw underneath it: a tired man, roughly their own age, carrying something heavy in a way he'd gotten very good at disguising. "For what it's worth," he said, "I loved her too. Marguerite. Whatever you think I'm doing, it's not that."

Then he was gone, the bell over the door chiming behind him, and Petra sat alone in the mustard booth with two untouched coffees and the word *permission* still lying on the table like a coin nobody would claim.

* * *

Back at the motel, Petra took out the shoebox.

It lived in the bottom of their suitcase, wrapped in a sweater, and taking it out always involved a small ceremony: clearing the nightstand, washing their hands, sitting cross-legged on the bed the way they'd sat cross-legged on their childhood bedroom floor twenty-two years ago, learning to fold grief into rectangles small enough to mail.

This week's postcard was due. Wednesday. They'd never missed a week in ten years—not through flu, not through funerals, not through the year Dree stopped speaking to them for reasons Dree never named and Petra never asked about. The ritual was the one load-bearing wall in their life, and they knew it, and they knew it was a strange kind of architecture, and they built it anyway.

They took out a blank card—a view of a lighthouse somewhere in Maine, bought in bulk, chosen for no reason—and uncapped the pen.

*Dear Larch Street,* they wrote. *Weather here is turning. The neighbors' maple has gone the color of—*

The pen stopped.

Petra stared at the sentence. The maple. The maple sentence. They had written some version of the maple sentence eleven times in ten years; autumn demanded it the way tides demand the shore. And today it wouldn't come, because today the words felt like what they had always, secretly, been: not letters but lids. Weather as a way of saying nothing beautifully, week after week, decade after decade, to a house that couldn't answer and wouldn't have, because the house had learned its silence from the family that built it.

*Theo offered me permission today,* Petra wrote instead, the pen moving before the thought finished forming. *He used the actual word. Permission. As if the truth were a room I needed a key to, and he happened to have copies.*

They stopped again. Their hand was shaking slightly, and their heart was going hard, and the motel room felt smaller than it had that morning.

Below the unfinished letter, without deciding to, Petra began a list. Not a postcard. A list. The pen moved fast, pressing hard enough to emboss the cardboard:

*Who oiled the garden door?*

*What did Vivian see?*

*Why did Marguerite keep a dress that smelled of smoke for twenty-two years?*

*What was Theo's father afraid of?*

*Why does Theo need this house bought before Saturday?*

*Why did Dree stop speaking to me, and why did I never ask?*

*What am I protecting, and whose idea was it?*

Seven questions. Petra read them over twice, feeling the strange vertigo of a person reading their own handwriting and not recognizing the person who wrote it. Then they slid the card into the shoebox—not with the mailed copies, but loose, on top, the first document in the box that had never been intended for anyone else's eyes, including a house's.

Outside, the light was going amber over the Fairview's empty pool, and somewhere down the highway a truck blew past, dragging its long sound behind it. Thursday was tomorrow.

Petra closed the shoebox lid and understood, with a calm that surprised them, that whatever they said to Theo tomorrow, it would not be yes.
