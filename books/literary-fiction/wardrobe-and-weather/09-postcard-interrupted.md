## 9. Postcard Interrupted

The motel room had two lamps and Petra had decided, over the course of four nights, that they hated the one nearest the bed. It gave off a light the color of weak tea, and it buzzed at a frequency just below hearing—a sound you didn't notice until you tried to sleep, and then couldn't stop noticing. The other lamp, over the desk by the window, was fine. So Petra had begun conducting their life on the desk side of the room: coffee there in the morning, phone calls there, the shoebox open there like a patient on an examining table.

Tonight the shoebox was open, and the postcards were fanned out in front of them, and Petra could not write.

It had started simply enough. Sunday was postcard night—had been for ten years, five hundred and twenty-odd Sundays, a streak that would have impressed a monk. The ritual had a liturgy. First the card itself: bought wherever Petra happened to be, always with a picture, never blank-backed stock from an office supply store. A lighthouse. A diner. A mural of local birds. Then the message, three or four lines, written small because the cards were small and the things unsaid were large. Then the address, which never changed:

*The House, 44 Larch Street.*

No name. No *To Whom It May Concern*. The house had burned when Petra was nineteen; the lot had been weeds for most of Petra's adult life; the postal service had long since stopped pretending to deliver to it. And yet every week a postcard went into a mailbox somewhere in America, addressed to a building that no longer existed, and every week Petra kept the carbon copy—the old-fashioned kind, blue-black paper that took an impression, bought in bulk online from a company that seemed genuinely puzzled by the orders—and filed it in the shoebox under the week's date.

Ten years. Petra had once done the math: if each card held forty words of unsaid thing, that was twenty thousand words. A novella of silence. A short book, but dense.

They picked up this week's card now. It was a good one, found at a gas station outside town: a painting of a storm front rolling over wheat fields, all bruise-purple and gold. Appropriate. Everything this month was weather.

*Dear House,* they wrote.

The pen stopped.

This was not unusual in itself. Some weeks the opening line came hard, and Petra would sit with the pen hovering until something arrived—*The rain here is the kind that makes you want to call someone,* or *I saw a dog today that looked like yours did, before.* The ritual had never required eloquence. It required only showing up, the way you show up to a grave.

But tonight the pen didn't hover and then descend. Tonight it hovered and stayed hovering, and Petra became aware, gradually, of what the trouble was: the salutation was a lie. Not a comfortable lie, not the benign fiction the whole ritual was built on, but a lie that had gone bad, curdled sometime in the last week without their noticing. There was no house. There hadn't been for twenty-two years. And more than that—there was no *dear*. You couldn't be dear to a foundation slab and a chain-link fence. Petra had been writing love letters to a hole in the ground for a decade, and calling it grief, and maybe it had been grief, once, but lately—

Lately it had been armor. They could see that now, the way you can see a scar's shape only after the wound stops hurting. Every Sunday, forty words poured out onto a card addressed to nobody, and the act of writing them down meant Petra never had to say them aloud, never had to aim them at a face. Dree's face. Theo's face. Their mother's face, serene behind her tea. The postcards were a pressure valve, and pressure valves exist so boilers don't explode, and Petra had spent ten years being very carefully not-exploded.

They set down the pen.

The motel room hummed its sub-audible hum. Outside, the parking lot light made a yellow island of the asphalt, and moths performed their idiot devotion against it. Somewhere down the highway a truck changed gears. Petra sat with the unfinished card—*Dear House*, and nothing after—and felt something shift in their chest that was not quite pain and not quite relief. More like the moment when a tooth that has ached for years finally cracks, and the crack hurts worse than the ache ever did, and yet some animal part of you thinks: *finally.*

* * *

They got up and walked to the window, because that is what people do when they cannot do the thing in front of them. The motel faced the highway on one side and a strip of scrubby field on the other, and beyond the field, half a mile off, you could see the dark shape of the water tower and, below it, the roofs of the older streets. Larch Street was out there. Marguerite's house was out there too, still standing for another few weeks, full of tagged clothing and other people's bids. The demolition notice for the Larch Street lot was folded in Petra's jacket pocket like a stone they'd decided to carry.

Five days ago they'd stood at the chain-link and read it. *STRUCTURE SCHEDULED FOR DEMOLITION. OWNER NOTIFIED. OBJECTIONS MUST BE FILED IN WRITING WITHIN THIRTY DAYS.* As if anyone could object. As if the family would file anything in writing; writing was Petra's department, and look what they wrote.

Petra thought about Theo at the estate preview, his hand resting on the wardrobe door the way you'd rest a hand on a horse you were calming. *Keeps things simple,* he'd said. Pool the money. Buy the house. Keep it in the family. And the word *simple* had sat wrong in Petra's ear ever since, the way a note sits wrong when the instrument is slightly out of tune—nothing you could prove, just a frequency your body rejected. Because nothing about their family was simple. Simple was the product. Simple was what everyone had been manufacturing, together, for twenty-two years, with the tireless efficiency of a factory running three shifts: simple, ordinary, tragic only by accident, nothing to see here, have you heard about the weather.

And here was the thing Petra could not stop thinking, the thought that had broken the pen mid-salutation: *Marguerite wasn't keeping it simple either.*

The tags. They kept coming back to the tags. *Worn the night of the storm.* *Burnt sleeve, kept anyway.* And the dress with the date—the date, the fire's date—written in Marguerite's upright hand, and the smell of smoke that had survived thirty years of cedar and mothballs. Petra had stood in that bedroom with the dress in their hands and felt the floor tilt. Because Marguerite had been a record-keeper. Marguerite, who never asked a direct question in her life, who deflected like everyone else, who had smiled her way through every holiday of Petra's childhood—that woman had been running her own archive. Labels instead of letters. Wool instead of words. But an archive. Proof that someone else in the family had also been unable to let it go, and had also been unable to say it, and had found a third way: she'd sewn the truth into linings and hung it in the dark and waited.

Waited for what? For someone to come read the clothes. For someone exactly like Petra, maybe. Or maybe for no one. Maybe the archive was Marguerite's own postcard to a burned house—one more letter addressed to nobody, mailed to no one, kept anyway.

Petra turned back to the desk. The card lay there, *Dear House* and white space, the storm front rolling over nothing.

They didn't finish it. That was the decision, though it didn't feel like a decision; it felt like setting down something heavy and discovering your arms remember how to be empty. Ten years of Sundays, and the streak ended not with a bang but with a blank card, left face-up on the motel desk like a note announcing a departure.

But the hand wanted to write. That was the strange part. The compulsion didn't leave with the ritual—it just lost its address. Petra sat back down, pulled a sheet of the motel's lined paper from the drawer (the pad said COMPLIMENTARY, which struck them suddenly as hilarious, complimentary paper, free of charge, free of charge), and began, without deciding to, to write.

Not a postcard. A list.

*Questions I have never asked anyone.*

They wrote the heading and then stared at it, and then the questions came—not all at once, but in a trickle that widened, the way water finds a seam in concrete.

*Who was in the kitchen?*

That was first. It was always first, underneath everything, the question the whole family was built around like a city built over a fault line. The night of the fire, twenty-two years ago, Petra had been nineteen and half-asleep upstairs, and had come down to heat and noise and the smell of burning plastic, and had seen—or half-seen, memory being the unreliable witness it was—a figure moving through the kitchen. Someone calm. Calm was the detail that had haunted them: everyone screamed that night except one person, and that person walked, unhurried, out through the garden door, and Petra had never been sure they'd seen it at all, and the family had never given them any help being sure, because the family had closed over the night like water over a dropped stone.

*Why did Uncle Robert's debts disappear the summer after the fire?*

Petra knew about the debts the way you know things in families—overheard fragments, a cousin's careless remark years later, the general atmospheric knowledge that Robert had been in trouble with men whose names weren't printed anywhere. And then, abruptly, he hadn't been in trouble anymore. And then the insurance money had arrived. Petra had been nineteen and grieving and had not done arithmetic. They were forty-one now, and the arithmetic was sitting up and begging.

*Why did we stop going to Aunt Marguerite's for Christmas?*

Small question. Telling question. The Christmases had stopped the same year as the fire, and no one had announced it; the tradition had simply evaporated, like everything else. Families don't vote on these things. Families just quietly reroute, and ten years later no one remembers the original road.

*What did Mom see?*

Their pen pressed harder on this one, nearly through the complimentary paper. Vivian had been there that night—had been in the garden, actually, which was its own strangeness, standing on the patio in November in a cardigan, and when the fire trucks came she'd been composed in a way Petra had found eerie even at nineteen. Composed, and then sealed. Thirty seconds of looking at the fire and a lifetime of not discussing it. When Petra had visited last week and said the word *fire*, Vivian had pivoted to rainfall averages with the smoothness of a talk-show host, and Petra had let her pivot, because that was the choreography, that was the dance everyone knew the steps to.

*Why does Theo want the house so badly?*

*Why does Theo need us all to want it together?*

*What am I protecting him from? What is he protecting me from?*

*Did Marguerite know?*

*If she knew, why didn't she say?*

*If I know—if I've known all along, in the place where you keep things you won't look at—why haven't I?*

The list went on past the first page onto the second. Petra wrote until the questions thinned out, and then sat looking at what they'd made: two pages of handwriting, slanting downhill the way their writing always did when it sped up, a census of everything unsaid. Twenty-two years of family dinners and funerals and graduations, and here was the actual conversation, the one happening underneath the whole time, transcribed at last in a motel room that smelled of industrial lavender.

It should have felt like breaking something. Instead it felt like the crack in the tooth: terrible, and clean, and overdue.

* * *

Around eleven, the phone rang. Petra knew before answering that it would be Dree—Dree called at night, Dree always called at night, breezy as a radio host, and Petra understood now that the timing was tactical: nighttime calls were harder to end, and harder to think clearly during.

"Hey," Dree said. "You still alive out there? Motel life getting to you yet?"

"It's growing on me. Free paper."

"What?"

"Nothing. How's home?"

"Oh, you know." Papers shuffled; Dree was doing something else while talking, as always. "Work stuff. Sam's got a cold. Listen, weird question—Theo called me today. About the house thing. He's really pushing this co-buy idea, huh? He asked if you'd gotten back to him."

"He asked. I didn't answer."

"Yeah, well." A pause with texture in it. "He mentioned you've been spending a lot of time at the preview. Going through Marguerite's stuff."

"I'm going through my aunt's belongings at a public sale, Dree. It's not a crime."

"No, I know, I know. It's just—" The shuffle stopped. For a second Dree's voice lost its radio-host polish, and underneath it Petra heard something older, something from the era of shared bedrooms and whispered after-lights-out conferences. "It's just, Mom said you asked her about the fire."

The word landed in the room between them. *Fire.* Neither of them had said it to each other in years—maybe ever, directly, one-on-one. It sounded strange in Dree's mouth, unpracticed, like a word in a language she'd studied as a child.

"I did," Petra said.

"And?"

"And she told me about the weather."

A breath of laughter from Dree, automatic, and then it caught, and didn't complete itself. Silence on the line. Petra looked at the two pages of questions lying on the desk, and at the unfinished postcard beside them, and made a choice they would later understand was the real subject of this entire decade: they didn't put the pages away.

"Dree," they said. "Can I ask you something?"

"You just did, like six times."

"A real one." Petra picked up the pen. Held it. "Do you remember the garden door?"

Another silence, longer. When Dree spoke again, the breeziness was gone entirely, and what remained was careful, and frightened, and awake. "Why are you asking me that?"

"Because I'm tired of asking the house."

"The—what?"

"Never mind." Petra looked at the storm-front postcard, unsent, unsayable. "Forget it. Get some sleep. Tell Sam I hope the cold clears up."

They hung up and sat in the tea-colored quiet. Down the hall, the ice machine dumped its load with a crash, and a door opened and shut, and the ordinary machinery of strangers' evenings went on around them. On the desk, the list waited. *Who was in the kitchen?* At the bottom of the second page, beneath all the questions, Petra's hand had written one more line without their quite noticing, the pen moving on its own errand, and they read it now for the first time:

*Tomorrow: go back to the house. Check the garden door.*

They hadn't remembered writing it. But reading it, they felt the rightness of it settle over them like a coat—like one of Marguerite's coats, maybe, with its lining full of sewn-in truths. Tomorrow. The house was still standing. The auction was Saturday. Whatever was in that house—whatever Marguerite had hidden, whatever someone might still be coming back for—would be rubble soon enough.

Petra turned off the buzzing lamp, left the good one burning, and set the unfinished postcard on top of the shoebox, face-up, where they could see it from the bed.

*Dear House,* it said. And then, in the morning light it would get tomorrow, in ink that hadn't dried yet when they fell asleep, one more line, added in the dark without ceremony, the smallest possible rebellion:

*I'm done writing to you. I'm going to start asking them.*
