## 10. The Garden Door

The trouble with a town that remembers you is that it also remembers your errands. Petra had been in the hardware store on Delancey for less than four minutes when the man behind the counter said, "You're a Thornbury, aren't you. You've got the look. Your aunt Marguerite used to come in here every October and buy a case of lightbulbs, like she was preparing for a siege."

"Maybe she was," Petra said, and bought the flashlight.

They had not planned to go inside the house. That was the thing about plans; they were like weather reports, accurate right up until the moment you stepped outside. Petra had planned to walk past the Larch Street lot the way you walk past a former lover's building—quickly, chin up, already composing the sentence you'd use to describe it later. But the lot was not the lot anymore. The chain-link had a gap in it, wide as a doorway, and someone had bent the wire back with what looked like care, folding the sharp ends under so no one would get cut.

Someone had made an entrance. Someone had made it *considerate*.

Petra stood at the gap for a long moment, holding the hardware bag against their chest like a shield, and thought about all the postcards in the shoebox. Ten years of *Dear house, I hope you are well.* Ten years of addressing the dead. And here was the evidence, folded back wire and all, that somebody else had been talking to the house too—and not with postcards. With visits.

They went in.

* * *

The house on Larch Street had been Marguerite's, and Marguerite had been the kind of woman who believed that a house was a garment you lived inside. She'd said so once, at a Thanksgiving when Petra was eleven: "A house should fit you the way a coat fits you. Snug in the shoulders. Room in the pockets." The family had laughed, the way families laugh at the aunts who are right. The house had fit her. Now it hung empty on its lot like a coat on a hook, sleeves still shaped by the arms that had gone.

Petra walked the perimeter first, the way you'd circle a sleeping animal. The porch boards were silvered with weather. The front door had a padlock on it—new, brass, bright as a coin in all that gray—and Petra's stomach did a small cold drop, because a new padlock meant a new keyholder, and the estate lawyers had said nothing about a padlock.

But the padlock was not locked. It was looped through the hasp and closed, but the hasp itself was loose, one screw half-out, the whole arrangement theatrical. A lock that locked nothing. Petra had spent ten years writing to an address that received no mail; they knew a performance of security when they saw one. This was a lock for the neighbors. This was a lock that said *nothing to see here* in the loudest possible way.

Which meant the real entrance was elsewhere.

The garden door was at the back, where the kitchen had been, where the fragments lived—the fragments Petra had been carrying for twenty-two years like stones in a coat pocket. Someone in the kitchen. Someone leaving through the garden door. The smell of smoke arriving like a guest who had let themselves in.

The garden door was a Dutch door, top half and bottom half, painted once a green that time had aged into the color of old celery. Petra put their hand on the latch and their heart put up its hand too, waving, saying *excuse me, excuse me, are we sure about this?*

"We're sure," Petra said aloud, to their heart, to the house, to nobody.

The latch lifted. The door opened without a sound.

That was the wrong part. That was the part that made the hair on Petra's arms stand up and introduce themselves. A door that had been shut for twenty-two years, in a house that had been empty for twenty-two years, should have shrieked like the whole concept of the past. This door opened the way a door opens in a home where someone is expecting you.

Petra crouched. The lower hinge—brass, green with patina everywhere else—was clean. Not just clean. *Oiled.* A dark, fresh gleam at the pin, the metal wet-looking, the way metal looks an hour after it's been touched with machine oil, not a year.

Someone had oiled this hinge. Recently. Someone had come to this door with a little can in their pocket and knelt in the weeds and tended it, the way you'd tend a grave, or a wound, or a secret you still needed to use.

"Okay," Petra said. Their voice came out smaller than they'd have liked. "Okay."

* * *

They did not go straight in. They walked the garden first, because the garden had been Marguerite's kingdom and because Petra's legs wanted a minute to renegotiate. The beds were gone to weeds, but the bones of it survived—the rose arch rusted into lace, the stone birdbath holding a cup of last week's rain, a single determined tulip coming up through a crack in the flagstones like a woman pushing open a door she'd been told was locked.

Petra stood under the rose arch and let the fragments come, the way they always came, in the wrong order, with the edges worn soft from handling.

The night of the fire. Petra had been fourteen. There had been a family dinner at Marguerite's—there was always a family dinner at Marguerite's, the whole architecture of the family arranged around that table like planets around a patient sun. Petra remembered the table. Petra remembered the pie, lemon meringue, because Marguerite believed lemon meringue was the only pie that required courage. Petra remembered going to the bathroom, and coming back, and the kitchen doorway standing open and dark, and a voice in the kitchen—low, urgent, a voice they had known, a voice they had *named* for themselves at fourteen and then, over twenty-two years, carefully unnamed, sanded down, made into a shapeless *someone*.

Someone in the kitchen. Someone leaving through the garden door. The door swinging shut with that same silence Petra had just heard, that oiled, practiced silence. And then, later, smoke. And then, after that, the family's great collective project: the building of the wall. Brick by brick by Sunday dinner by *we don't talk about that, sweetheart, it was an accident, everyone's safe, eat your pie.*

Petra had spent ten years writing postcards to a burned house. It occurred to them now, standing in Marguerite's ruined garden, that they had never once written a postcard that asked a question. Every card had been weather and memory and love, a decade of *hope you are well* to an address that could not answer. They had built their own wall, brick by brick by carbon copy, and called it devotion.

The tulip nodded in the wind. Petra wiped their face with the heel of their hand and went inside.

* * *

The kitchen was a museum of absence. Cabinets open like dropped jaws. A refrigerator humming—*humming*—and Petra stood very still and listened to that hum and understood, in a slow cold slide, that the electricity had never been turned off. The house had been kept. All these years, the house had been *kept*, like a coat kept in a closet for someone who might come back, or like evidence kept for a trial that never came.

The flashlight came out of the hardware bag. The beam found the counter, the sink, a dish towel folded over the oven handle—folded, recently, because dust does not lie and there was no dust on the towel. The beam found the floor and the floor said the rest: footprints in the fine gray felt of twenty-two years. Boot prints, medium size, coming and going in a path worn almost clean between the garden door and the cellar stairs. Not one visit. Not ten. A *route*. Someone had been walking this path so long the dust had given up and made a road.

Petra stood at the top of the cellar stairs and had a conversation with themselves that was brief and entirely one-sided.

The flashlight went first. Petra followed.

* * *

Cellars are honest. That's the thing about them. A house can lie with its curtains and its paint and its carefully arranged living room, but a cellar just holds what it holds. Marguerite's cellar had held jars of preserves in its working years—Petra remembered the shelves, the jewel-colored ranks of them, the plum and the quince and the famous damson that Vivian had once said was "too good to share, which is the whole problem with your aunt." The shelves were still there. The jars were gone. But the shelves had been *dusted*.

The footprints led to the back corner, where the coal chute had been bricked over sometime in the sixties, and stopped.

Petra played the light along the brick and found it: a hairline seam, a rectangle's worth of mortar slightly newer than its neighbors, and at knee height, a brass plate screwed into the brick with two screws so old they'd gone the same color as the wall. On the plate, in a script Petra knew better than their own handwriting—knew from a decade of wardrobe tags, from auction labels, from the margins of every cookbook in the estate—two words, small and unhesitating:

*Not yet.*

Petra crouched in front of it for a long time. The cellar cold came up through their knees. Somewhere above them, water moved in a pipe, the house's old circulation, still running, still keeping.

*Not yet.* Not *never*. Not *nothing here*. Not the family's whole vocabulary of *accident* and *let it be* and *why dig all that up, sweetheart.* *Not yet.* Which was a different word entirely. *Not yet* was a promise with the date left blank. *Not yet* meant Marguerite had known what was behind the brick, had put it there or guarded it, and had been waiting—for what? For courage? For a death? For a fourteen-year-old with a flashlight and a shoebox full of unsent mail?

The beam trembled. Petra let it.

They thought about Theo at the auction preview, friendly and watchful, his laugh arriving a half-second before his eyes agreed to it. They thought about Dree at the diner, reciting the family like a speech she'd rehearsed in the car. They thought about the padlock that locked nothing, the hinge that opened like a greeting, the route worn through the dust by boots that came and came and came.

Someone was still visiting this house. Someone was still *tending* it. And whatever Marguerite had bricked into the back corner of her cellar, she had not bricked it in to bury it. She had bricked it in the way you put a letter in a drawer. The way you keep a dress with a burnt sleeve. The way you write *tell me what you saw* and never mail it.

The past wasn't past. The past had a key above the door frame and a route through the dust and a schedule.

* * *

Petra found the key on the way out, because of course they did. It was exactly where keys go when someone wants them found by the right person and nobody else: above the garden door, in the gap between the frame and the siding, taped flat with a strip of cloth tape gone amber with age. Petra's fingers knew before their mind did. The key was small, brass, old-fashioned, the kind that fit a diary or a strongbox or a door in a cellar.

They stood in the doorway with the key in their fist and the house breathing around them—the refrigerator humming its one long note, the wind trying the rose arch like a fiddler tuning up—and Petra understood that fear had quietly left the room. What remained was something steadier and more dangerous. Purpose, arriving the way weather arrives: not all at once, but with a pressure you can feel in your teeth.

Tomorrow they would come back with a hammer and a better flashlight and a thermos of coffee, because whatever was behind *not yet* deserved to be met sober and prepared. Tonight they would go back to the motel and do the one thing they had done every week for ten years.

They would write the postcard. And this time, for the first time, they were going to write a question on it.

Petra pocketed the key, pulled the garden door shut behind them—it closed on its oiled hinge without a sound, the house keeping its own counsel—and walked out through the considerate gap in the chain-link into the blue beginning of evening, behind them a house that was not empty at all, and had never been.
