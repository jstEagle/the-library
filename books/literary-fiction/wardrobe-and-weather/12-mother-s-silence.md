## 12. Mother's Silence

The assisted-living facility was called Rosewood Commons, which Petra thought was a lie told twice—once about the roses and once about the commons. There were no roses. The courtyard held three ornamental junipers in mulch rings, and the commons was a hallway with a coffee station nobody trusted. But the lobby smelled of cinnamon rolls on Fridays, and it was Friday, and Petra had learned years ago that you visited Vivian Thornbury when there was sugar in the air.

Vivian's apartment was on the second floor, end unit, with a window that faced the parking lot but caught the afternoon light for one golden hour, and Vivian had arranged her whole existence around that hour like a plant. She sat in her good chair by the glass when Petra knocked, wearing a cardigan the color of weak tea and a brooch shaped like a wren.

"There she is," Vivian said, opening her arms before Petra had fully crossed the threshold. "Or there they is. I can never get the grammar right and I refuse to try."

"You're doing fine."

"I'm doing *magnificently*, thank you." She kissed Petra's cheek and held them at arm's length, studying their face with the frank appraisal of a woman who had spent decades reading people for a living—not literally, but effectively. "You look tired."

"I drove up this morning."

"From where?"

"From town. I've been staying at the Starlite Motel."

Vivian's eyebrows performed a small, elegant lift—theatrical surprise, calibrated to communicate *I have opinions but will not yet share them*—and then she turned toward the kitchenette. "I have shortbread. The nice aide, Marisol, brings me shortbread even though it's against some policy or other. Sit down before you fall down."

Petra sat. The apartment was small and fiercely curated: two armchairs, a lamp with a fringed shade, photographs arranged on the mantel in strict order of importance. A wedding photo of Vivian and Petra's father, dead nineteen years now. Dree's graduation. A picture of Petra at maybe nine, holding a dog that had never actually existed except in the photograph, borrowed from a neighbor for the occasion because Vivian wanted a picture of her child with a dog and would not be deterred by reality.

There were no pictures of Aunt Marguerite. Petra noticed this the way you notice a missing tooth in an old smile.

"Marguerite's house went up for auction," Petra said, when the shortbread had been distributed and the tea poured. "Last week. I bought the wardrobe."

Vivian stirred her tea though there was nothing in it to stir. "That old wardrobe. It weighed as much as a car. Your aunt used to say if the house ever caught fire, she'd save the wardrobe first and let the rest of us fend for ourselves."

She said it lightly. She said everything lightly; that was her gift and her fortress. But the sentence landed in the room and sat there between them, enormous and unacknowledged, like an elephant that had learned to hold its breath.

"She kept labels on everything," Petra said. "Inside the clothes. Little tags in her handwriting. *Worn to Eleanor's wedding. Burnt sleeve, kept anyway.*"

"Mm," said Vivian. "She was always writing things down. Your aunt believed a thing wasn't real until it was recorded. I'm the opposite. I believe a thing stops being real if you refuse to discuss it long enough." She smiled brilliantly. "It's worked beautifully for seventy-one years."

"Mom."

"Is the shortbread bad? You're not eating the shortbread. Marisol will be wounded."

"The shortbread is excellent." It was. It was buttery and slightly stale and perfect. "I went to Larch Street."

And there it was—the change. It happened fast, faster than Petra could track, the way weather changes on a plain: one moment clear, the next a wall of gray sliding over the sun. Vivian set down her cup with a small ceramic click and turned her face toward the window, toward the parking lot and its one golden hour, and said:

"Terrible wind we've been having. Marisol says the junipers are going to come down one of these days. They're badly rooted. Everything here is badly rooted, if you ask me, but of course nobody asks me, they just ask if I'm taking my pills."

"You went to Larch Street too," Petra said. "Didn't you. Before the auction."

The silence lasted four seconds. Petra counted. Four seconds was a long time for Vivian Thornbury, who could fill any gap with talk the way other people filled ice cube trays.

"I don't know what you imagine I do all day," Vivian said finally, "but I assure you it involves very little driving."

"That's not a no."

"It's not anything. It's a lovely day out. Look at that light." She gestured at the window with her chin. "You know, when your father and I were courting, he used to drive me out past the reservoir just to watch storms come in. He'd time them. He had a theory that you could see a storm coming twenty minutes before it arrived if you knew how to look—there'd be a greenish tint under the clouds, and the birds would go quiet all at once, like someone had turned down their volume. He was usually right. He was insufferable about being right." She laughed, a real laugh, warm and sudden, and for a moment Petra forgot what they had come to do and simply sat in it, the laugh, the light, the smell of shortbread and lavender sachets.

Then Vivian looked at them again, and her eyes were kind and absolutely closed, like beautiful shutters.

"Petra," she said gently. "Tell me about you. Tell me about the book. Are you still writing?"

"I'm always writing."

"What's this one about?"

"A family," Petra said. "That doesn't talk."

"Mm. Depressing. Make sure there's a dog in it. Readers forgive anything if there's a dog."

* * *

They talked for an hour about nothing, and it was pleasant, and it was maddening. Vivian was lucid—sharply, unmistakably lucid. She remembered the name of the cardiologist's receptionist's daughter. She remembered that Dree had called Tuesday and that Dree had sounded "fine, which means either fine or the opposite of fine, with your sister those are the same voice." She remembered the exact year the reservoir froze hard enough to skate on and the fact that Petra had once, at eleven, put a frog in her handbag as a joke and Vivian had discovered it three days later in church.

But every road Petra built toward the fire, Vivian paved over with something else. Weather. Food. Gossip about the man in 214 who played his television at full volume because, he claimed, he liked knowing other people existed. The roads were smooth and endless and led nowhere, and they were built so skillfully that Petra sometimes lost track of the fact that they were roads at all.

At half past four, Petra tried again. Not with a question—with a story.

"I found a dress in the wardrobe," they said. "With a tag dated October ninth."

The teacup did not tremble. Vivian's hands had never trembled in her life; Petra suspected they wouldn't tremble at her own funeral. But something behind her face went very still, the way birds go quiet before a storm, like someone turning down their volume.

"October ninth," Vivian repeated pleasantly. "Was that a Tuesday? Your father always said nothing important ever happens on a Tuesday."

"Mom. It was the night of the fire."

"Yes," Vivian said. "I know what date the fire was. I was there." She smoothed her cardigan over her knees, a small domestic gesture, tidying herself the way she tidied everything. "Everyone was there. That was rather the point of it."

"And nobody has ever said one word about it since. Twenty-two years."

"Hasn't it been lovely?" Vivian said, and smiled, and the smile was so radiant and so completely armored that Petra felt something crack in their chest—not anger, exactly, though anger was in there somewhere, but a kind of grief with teeth. Because the terrible thing, the thing Petra couldn't explain to anyone who hadn't grown up inside this family, was that it *had* been lovely. The silence worked. The silence had held the family together like mortar holds bricks—you didn't notice it, you only noticed when it started failing, and then you noticed it everywhere, and you understood that the wall had been the house all along.

"Mom," Petra said quietly. "I found letters. In Marguerite's attic. Letters she wrote to you and never sent. Dozens of them. All asking the same thing."

Vivian's face did not change. But her hands stopped moving.

"*Tell me what you saw,*" Petra said. "Over and over. For years. She wrote to you the way I wrote postcards to a burned-down house. Did you know she kept them? Did you know she never mailed a single one?"

For a moment—one long, suspended moment—Petra thought the wall might move. Vivian looked at her son, her child, her strange quiet youngest, and her eyes were wet at the rims, just barely, just enough to catch the last of the golden hour, and her mouth opened—

—and closed.

"Well," she said briskly, standing, gathering cups, moving to the sink with the efficiency of a woman who had raised two children and buried a husband and outlasted every difficult conversation of the twentieth century. "It's nearly five. Marisol gets worried if I'm agitated at five. She thinks agitation interferes with my sleep, and she's probably right, though frankly everything interferes with my sleep, including sleep."

"Marguerite died not knowing," Petra said to her back. "She spent her whole life asking and you never answered. And now she's gone, and the house is sold, and the lot's coming down, and there is nobody left to ask except you."

Vivian rinsed a cup. Set it in the little rack. Rinsed another.

"You want to know why I won't talk about it," she said, without turning around. Her voice was level, almost cheerful. "I'll tell you why. Because I was thirty-four years old, and I stood in a garden, and I saw what I saw, and I made a decision that night, on the grass, in my bare feet, that I would carry it alone so that everyone else could put it down. And I have carried it for twenty-two years, and my back is strong, and I am *fine*, and the single most annoying thing you could do to me—more annoying than the television man, more annoying than the pills—is to try to take it off my shoulders now, when I've gotten so good at the weight."

She turned around. She was smiling. Her eyes were dry now, sealed, shutters drawn against the evening.

"So let's talk about something else. Tell me about the motel. Is it the one with the sign that buzzes? I always loved that sign. It was like a moth singing."

* * *

Petra stayed another hour. They ate dinner in the dining hall—overcooked chicken, canned peas, a roll that was mostly crust in spirit—and Vivian charmed the table. She told the woman from 209 a story about a hat fire at a 1978 Easter service that had the whole table laughing. She complimented the aide on her braids. She asked after everyone's grandchildren by name. She was, Petra thought, watching her mother preside over the linoleum kingdom like a deposed queen making the best of exile, the most alive person in the building and the most alone, and both of those things were choices, and she had made them on the same night, in the garden, in her bare feet.

At the door, Vivian hugged them. She hugged hard, longer than usual, her chin tucked into Petra's shoulder, and for a moment she was just small and warm and smelling of lavender and tea, and Petra's throat closed.

"Come back Sunday," Vivian said. "Bring the shortbread next time. Fair is fair."

"I will."

"And Petra." She held them at arm's length again, and her face was composed, pleasant, final. "Whatever you think you're digging up—it was buried on purpose. By people who loved each other. Remember that part. Whatever else you find, remember that we did it out of love."

"Did it work?" Petra asked. "The love. Did it work?"

Vivian considered the question with genuine seriousness, head tilted, the wren brooch catching the hall light.

"Ask me again sometime when it isn't Friday," she said, and kissed their cheek, and closed the door with a click soft as a teacup coming down on its saucer.

In the parking lot, the sky over Rosewood Commons had gone the color of wet slate, and the wind had picked up, worrying the badly rooted junipers. Petra sat in the car a long while without starting it, thinking about bare feet on grass, about a decision made in the dark that had become a house that everyone lived in.

Their phone buzzed. A text from Theo: *Heard you went to see Vivian. She okay?*

Then, a minute later, before Petra could answer: *Some things are better left where they are. Trust me.*

Petra stared at the screen, at the words sitting there in their neat gray bubble, and felt the last warm fog of the visit burn away entirely.

Theo hadn't asked *what did she say*. He'd asked *is she okay*—which meant he already knew what she'd say. Which meant he already knew what there was to say.

Petra started the car and pulled out onto the road, toward the Starlite, toward the wardrobe waiting in the motel room like a body, toward the coat whose lining they hadn't yet opened.
