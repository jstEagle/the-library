## 15. Confrontation at the Lot

Petra had rehearsed the conversation eleven times on the drive over, which meant they had rehearsed it zero times, because rehearsing was just a more elaborate way of avoiding. The photograph sat in their coat pocket, folded in its evidence bag—a sandwich baggie from the motel's complimentary breakfast bar, because Petra owned no evidence bags and the universe had a sense of humor about that. The train ticket was tucked behind it. *R. Halloway. Departing 6:40 a.m., the morning after.*

Theo's truck was already at the Larch Street lot when Petra pulled up, parked crooked across from the chain-link, as if he couldn't quite bring himself to face the house directly. Or what had been the house. The lot itself was mostly weeds now, tall and gold-green, with the blackened stub of the old foundation showing through like a bone through skin. Someone had wound plastic flowers around the chain-link at intervals—marigolds, petunias, all of them sun-bleached to the color of old teeth. Petra had never noticed them before. They must have been there for years.

Theo was standing at the gate with his hands in his jacket pockets, looking at the weeds the way men look at things they're pretending not to look at.

"You know," Petra said, coming up beside him, "when I was a kid I thought those were real flowers someone kept planting."

"They were real, the first year." Theo didn't startle. He'd heard the car; he'd had time to arrange his face, and he'd arranged it into something gentle and tired. "Mrs. Okafor planted them. Then she moved to Tucson and her nephew kept buying plastic ones because he figured the gesture mattered more than the horticulture. He wasn't wrong."

"You've been talking to Mrs. Okafor's nephew."

"I've been talking to everybody. It's a small town, Petra. Talking to everybody is the local industry."

The wind moved through the weeds with a dry whispering sound, like an audience shuffling programs. Petra put their hands in their own pockets, felt the baggie crinkle, and decided not to rehearse anymore.

"I went through the wardrobe," they said.

Theo nodded slowly, as if this were a normal thing to say at a vacant lot on a Tuesday. "I figured. You bid on it like a woman possessed. Well. Like a person possessed. I don't want to presume."

"Theo."

"I'm just saying, four hundred dollars for a wardrobe with a broken drawer—"

"*Theo.*" Petra took the photograph out of their pocket. They meant to do it with dignity, with the slow gravity the moment deserved, but their hands were shaking and the baggie was slippery and the whole gesture came out like someone producing a coupon. Theo looked at it anyway. He looked at it for a long time. He did not reach for it.

The photograph showed four people on the porch of the Larch Street house, squinting into summer light. Petra's mother, Vivian, young and laughing at something outside the frame. Marguerite, arm slung around her, wearing the green dress. And two men—Petra's father, dead now eleven years, and beside him a broad, easy-smiling man with a cigarette tucked behind his ear. Robert Halloway. Theo's father. The ticket behind the photograph was dated the day after the fire, one-way, and it was in Robert Halloway's name, and it had been sewn into the lining of Marguerite's winter coat with stitches so small and so patient that she must have done it by lamplight, over hours, the way you do a thing you intend to survive you.

"It was in the coat," Petra said. "The camel coat. The one labeled *'too warm for this climate, kept anyway.'*"

"I know the coat," Theo said quietly.

"I know you know the coat."

The wind came again. Down the street a dog was barking at nothing in particular, with the tireless commitment of a dog who has made barking his life's work.

"My father set the fire," Theo said.

Petra had expected—had braced for—denial. Had prepared, in the eleven rehearsals that weren't rehearsals, a whole arsenal of follow-ups for denial. What they had not prepared for was the flat, immediate, exhausted truth of it, delivered to the weeds, and it knocked the air out of them worse than any fight would have.

"He set the fire," Theo said again, as if saying it twice made it more possible, "because he owed eleven thousand dollars to a man named Duval who had already broken his hand over a smaller debt, and the house was insured, and my mother had left him in March, and he was—" Theo stopped. He laughed, a small terrible sound. "He was *twenty-six*, Petra. That's the part that gets me. Everyone in this story was so young. Your mother was twenty-four. Marguerite was thirty-one and she was the only adult in the room, and she wasn't an adult, she was just the oldest child."

"Someone was in the kitchen." Petra's voice came out strange, thin, like a recording of their voice played back. "The night of the fire. I was eight. I saw someone come through the garden door."

"I know."

"You *know*?"

"I know because my father told me. Not then. Eleven years ago, two weeks before he died. He'd had the stroke, and he couldn't drink anymore, and apparently when Robert Halloway couldn't drink, the truth came out the way water comes out of a cracked pipe—slowly, and all over everything." Theo finally turned to look at Petra, and his face was wrecked in a very controlled way, the way a room looks after it's been tidied by someone crying. "He said there was a girl in the kitchen. He said he told her to go upstairs. He said she went."

Petra's mother. Vivian, twenty-four, in the kitchen at night, and a man with a cigarette behind his ear telling her to go upstairs, and her going, because what else does a twenty-four-year-old do when a grown man tells her to go upstairs, and then the house—

"She never said," Petra whispered. "In forty years she never said."

"Of course she never said. Nobody ever said. That was the whole *architecture* of it." Theo gestured at the lot, at the weeds, at the plastic flowers, at the entire town. "Your mother saw him and said nothing. Marguerite figured it out—she was the only one who ever figured it out on her own, from the insurance paperwork, and God knows how long that took her—and she said nothing. My mother knew and left anyway and said nothing. And the rest of us—" he spread his hands— "the rest of us were raised inside the shape of the thing that wasn't said. You don't have to be told a secret to be built by it. You just grow around the hole."

"Marguerite kept records." Petra held up the baggie. "She sewed his ticket into her coat. She labeled her *dresses*, Theo. She labeled a dress with the date of the fire and it still smells like smoke. She was keeping a file. She was building a case for thirty years and never once filed it."

"She was waiting for someone to ask her."

"Nobody asked."

"Nobody *asks*." Theo said it with a bitterness that surprised them both; it seemed to surprise him most. He looked down at his boots. "That was the deal, you understand. That was the arrangement. My father set the fire, and the family closed around it like water closing over a stone, and the price of the arrangement was that Robert Halloway got to stay at every Christmas, every Fourth of July, every terrible potluck, laughing his big laugh, and nobody ever looked at him too long. And then he died, and the arrangement didn't die with him, because arrangements never do. They just transfer. To the next generation. To—" he tapped his own chest, once, "—the custodian."

"You."

"Me." He said it the way other men say *the oldest* or *the responsible one*. "I've been holding the lid on this thing since I was nineteen years old. Do you know what that's like? Do you know what it's like to love your father and know what he did, and to love everyone he hurt and know they know you know, and to have all of that live in a room in you with no windows?" He shook his head. "I proposed buying the house because I thought if we owned it together, we could control it. I thought if we kept it in the family, it would stay quiet. That was the only tool I was handed, Petra. Containment. My father's whole inheritance to me was *containment*."

"And now the demolition's moved up," Petra said, "and your tool doesn't work anymore, and here we are at the lot."

"Here we are at the lot."

Petra looked at the photograph again. In it, Robert Halloway's smile was enormous and uncomplicated, the smile of a man who had not yet owed anyone anything, or who had already learned that a big enough smile could stand in for payment. Petra had loved that smile as a child. Uncle Robert, who lifted them onto his shoulders, who called them *champ* in a voice like a friendly landslide. The fury arrived then, all at once, late and total—the fury of an eight-year-old who had seen a shape in the kitchen and been folded into the silence about it for thirty years without ever being told there was a silence to keep. They had spent a decade writing postcards to a burned house. They had mailed four hundred and some letters into a hole in the ground, and the hole had a name, and the name had smiled at them from shoulders-high.

"You let me do that," Petra said, and their voice cracked on it. "You knew. You watched me—everybody watched me—write to a house. You watched Dree do her little polished speech about how tragedy just *happened* to us, acts of God, weather. You sat in diners and let us perform our whole lives around a man you knew was an arsonist."

"Yes," Theo said. Simply. No defense offered, none available.

"I hate you a little right now."

"I'd think less of you if you didn't."

And there it was—the pity, arriving uninvited, tangling itself into the fury like two colors of thread in the same knot. Because Theo was standing in the wind at the site of his father's crime, holding the lid with both hands, and his arms must have been so tired. Because he was thirty-nine years old and had never once been allowed to be anyone's nephew instead of anyone's alibi. Because Marguerite had sewn a ticket into a coat instead of mailing a letter, and Vivian had gone upstairs instead of screaming, and Dree had memorized a version instead of asking a question, and Petra had written four hundred postcards instead of making a phone call—and every single one of them had chosen the container over the contents, the wardrobe over the body, the weather over the fire.

The whole family, Petra thought, was a house where everyone kept their coats on so no one would see what was sewn into the linings.

"Marguerite wrote letters," Petra said. "To my mother. Unmailed. I found them in the attic of her house. She wrote the same question over and over, thirty years of it. *Tell me what you saw.*" They looked at Theo. "She never sent them. She died with a question in a shoebox. I'm not going to do that. I'm not going to be the third generation of this family that dies holding a question like it's a coat we're keeping warm for somebody."

"Petra." Theo's voice dropped, and there was something in it Petra had never heard from him—something young, and scared, and pleading. "The demolition is Friday. The house is coming down either way. Whatever we say now goes into rubble. Can't we just—" He stopped himself. He heard himself. He closed his eyes. "God. Listen to me. *Can't we just not.* That's the family prayer. That's the grace we say before every meal."

The question was in Petra's throat now. It had been forming all the way over, through the eleven rehearsals, and it was simpler than any of them, and larger. It was not *who set the fire*—that was answered, had been answered in the weeds, in nine flat words. The question was the one underneath, the load-bearing one, the one the whole careful architecture of silence had been built to keep from ever being asked out loud:

*Why did we all agree not to know?*

And it could not be asked here, at the lot, to Theo, to the custodian. It had to be asked where the family was. All of them. In one room. With the coats on, so they could be taken off.

"Dree's birthday dinner is Thursday," Theo said, reading Petra's face with the terrible accuracy of a man who had spent his life reading faces for signs of questions. "At the Roadhouse. Everyone's coming. Vivian's coming. It was supposed to be—normal. One last normal thing before the lot goes."

"Thursday," Petra said.

"Petra. Whatever you're thinking—"

"I'm not thinking anything." They put the photograph back in their pocket, next to the ticket, next to the question. "I'm done thinking. That's the whole problem with our family, Theo. We've been thinking for thirty years. Somebody has to stand up at a birthday dinner and *ask*."

Theo looked at the lot for a long moment. Then he did something Petra had never seen him do: he reached through the chain-link and straightened one of the plastic marigolds, adjusting it with two fingers, tenderly, the way you'd fix a flower on a grave.

"Friday," he said. "The demolition's Friday. So if you're going to burn the whole arrangement down, you've got about forty-eight hours before they do it for you."

* * *

Petra sat in the car for a long time afterward, watching Theo's truck pull away, watching the weeds bow and rise, bow and rise. In their pocket, the photograph pressed against their ribs like a second heartbeat.

They took out a postcard—one of the last in the glovebox, the motel's complimentary ones, a picture of the town water tower—and wrote, in the small handwriting of ten years' practice:

*Dear Larch Street —*

Then they stopped. The pen hovered. The ritual, broken since the night in the motel, stayed broken; there was nothing left to tell a house.

Instead, on the back of the water tower, Petra wrote a single line, not addressed to anyone yet, just set down like a stone on a path:

*Thursday. I'm going to ask.*

They looked at it for a while. Then they started the car.

Two days. Forty-eight hours. Somewhere across town, Dree was polishing her version, and Vivian was choosing her weather, and the demolition crew was fueling its machines—and Petra drove back to the motel with the windows down, letting the wind take the smoke smell out of their hair, or trying to, or pretending to.
