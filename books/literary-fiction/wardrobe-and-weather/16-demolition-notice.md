## 16. Demolition Notice

The notice came on a Tuesday, folded inside a plain envelope with no return address, which was how Petra knew before opening it that it wasn't from the county. The county sent its notices in windowed envelopes, barcoded, indifferent. This one had been handled by a person. There was a coffee ring on the corner of the paper, faint and brown, and Petra held it up to the motel room's fluorescent light like an artifact, as if the ring itself might testify.

*Notice of Accelerated Demolition Schedule,* the letter read. *Due to revised contractor availability, demolition of the structure at 114 Larch Street is advanced from October 30 to October 9. All parties with standing are advised accordingly.*

Five days. The house would come down in five days.

Petra sat on the edge of the motel bed—the bedspread the color of weak tea, the air conditioner rattling its one note—and read the letter three times. The words didn't change. The date didn't change. Somewhere out past the parking lot, past the highway interchange, past the weedy chain-link fence they'd stood at two weeks ago feeling the shock of being recognized by a neighbor who remembered their face better than they'd wanted to be remembered, the house stood with its fresh-oiled hinge and its hidden key and its cellar footprints, and now it had five days left to stand at all.

They thought about Marguerite's letters. *Tell me what you saw.* Twenty years of that question, written and rewritten in an attic, never mailed, never answered. And now the answer was going to be buried under rubble on a schedule set by a contractor's calendar, and Petra could feel the window closing—not metaphorically, not in the way writers say *window closing* when they mean opportunity, but literally, physically: a house with rooms in it becoming a lot without them, the dust settling into permanence, the footprints erased by machines.

The phone rang. Dree.

Petra let it ring twice, then picked up. "You've heard."

"Heard what?" Dree's voice did the thing it always did, the brightening, the little upward inflection that was supposed to sound casual but sounded, to Petra now, like a door being propped open with a chair. "Oh—Petra, hi. I was just—I saw something online about the schedule changing. Is that real? Five days?"

"It's real. I'm holding the letter."

"Who even sends—" Dree stopped herself. Petra could hear her rearranging her face, the way she did at family functions, the smile arriving a half-second before the sentence. "Well. Okay. So that's that, then. The county finally does something fast for once in its life."

"Dree." Petra said her sister's name the way you'd set down a heavy object, carefully, so it wouldn't break anything. "The hinge was oiled. Someone's been going into that house."

A silence on the line, long enough that Petra heard the motel's ice machine cycle on through the wall, its laborious grinding, its shudder and stop.

"You've been reading too many of your own postcards," Dree said finally, and laughed, and the laugh had a crack down the middle of it. "Look. Can I come by? Theo's here, actually—we're both at Mom's, we were helping her with—she's fine, she's fine, don't worry, she just likes the company. We could all—"

"I'll come to you," Petra said, because they did not want Theo in the motel room, near the wardrobe, near the coat with the lining Petra had not yet opened again but had not stopped thinking about since the night they'd found the photograph and the train ticket sewn flat against the wool, dated the day after the fire, bearing the name of Theo's father.

---

Vivian's assisted-living apartment smelled, as always, of lavender potpourri and the ghost of cigarettes decades quit. Dree opened the door before Petra knocked, which meant she'd been watching the parking lot, which meant she'd been waiting with her stomach in a knot and pretending otherwise. Behind her, Theo rose from the loveseat with his hands already half-raised, palms out, the universal posture of a man arriving at a conversation he hoped to end before it started.

"Petra." He said their name warmly. He was good at warmth; Petra had to give him that. It was real warmth, or it had been once, or it was the fossil of warmth compressed under twenty-two years of weight until it looked exactly like the living thing. "You got the letter too, then."

"We all got the letter." Vivian spoke from the armchair by the window without turning around. She was doing her nails, or pretending to; the bottle of pale pink polish sat open on the side table, untouched. Outside her window, October was assembling itself out of gray light and falling leaves. "Five days. Well. Houses fall down eventually either way. That's what houses do."

"Mom." Dree's warning tone, soft but unmistakable.

"What?" Vivian turned then, and her face was serene, charming, absolutely sealed—the face Petra had studied across a hundred visits, the face that changed the subject to weather whenever the fire came up, the face that had said, only days ago, in a voice Petra still hadn't finished recovering from: *You don't ask because the answer rearranges everyone.* "I'm allowed an opinion about architecture. I lived in three of them."

Theo gestured Petra toward the kitchenette, and they went, because it was easier than refusing, and because part of Petra wanted to hear whatever he was going to say, wanted it the way you want to hear a diagnosis even when you already know.

He poured coffee nobody had asked for. His hands were steady. That steadiness frightened Petra more than shaking would have.

"The county's moving fast because the developer leaned on them," Theo said quietly. "Which means the developer wants this done quietly, which means—" he slid a folded paper across the counter, "—there's no more time for whatever process you're in. Whatever you think you're doing. The house comes down Friday. After that there's nothing to investigate, nothing to find, nothing to argue about. It's just a lot. Grass grows. People forget."

"People forget," Petra repeated.

"That's not a threat, Petra. It's a mercy." He looked at them with those eyes so much like his father's—Petra saw it now, couldn't unsee it, the same gray, the same hooded patience—and his voice dropped further. "My dad was a sick man. Sick with debt, sick with pride, sick with the kind of desperation that makes a man do a terrible thing and then spend the rest of his life being almost decent to make up for it. He was almost decent. He coached my Little League team. He paid for Dree's braces when Uncle Ray couldn't. You know that. You grew up on his shoulders at the fair."

"I know," Petra said. "That's why this has been so hard."

"And it can be over." Theo touched the folded paper between them. "Let it go. Let the house go. What does keeping it open get anyone? Marguerite's dead. Mom's—" he nodded toward the living room, toward Vivian, "—she's earned her quiet. Dree can't—Dree will come apart, Petra, you know she will, she's built her whole self on top of the official version, and if you pull that thread in public, in front of everyone, at some dinner—"

"I haven't decided anything about a dinner."

"You're deciding it right now. I can see you deciding it." He smiled, and the smile was begging wearing a suit. "Five days. Please. Let it be rubble. Rubble doesn't testify. I'm asking you as family. I'm asking you as the last person whose childhood I care about preserving."

From the living room, the sound of the television coming on, volume low, and Dree's voice pitched bright over it: "Mom, look, they have that channel with the old movies—"

And Vivian's voice, dry as autumn: "I know what channel the old movies are on, Dree. I watched them before you were born."

Petra looked at the folded paper. It was a copy of the demolition permit. At the bottom, in Theo's handwriting, someone had written a phone number and the words *call me before Friday.*

"They oiled the hinge," Petra said. "Someone goes in that house, Theo. If it's not you—"

"If it's me?" He held their gaze, and for one unguarded second something moved behind his face, something tired and old and young all at once, a boy standing in a garden doorway. "Then maybe I go in because it's the only place my father exists anymore. Did you think of that? Maybe I sit in his kitchen. Maybe that's all it is." He straightened. "Or maybe you should ask yourself why you need it to be more than that. You've spent ten years writing to a burned house, Petra. Don't tell me about needing things to stay haunted."

The words landed harder than any blow could have, because they were true, and Theo knew they were true, and he used them gently, which was somehow worse.

---

Petra walked home from Vivian's—or walked *back*, back to the motel, back along the route that passed Larch Street because every route in that town passed Larch Street if you let it—and stopped at the chain-link fence as the light went long and amber across the weeds.

The house stood in its last week, paint peeling in long curls, windows clouded with grime, the garden door visible from here, small and white and shut. Five days. Petra thought about the wardrobe in the motel room, hauled there like a body after the auction, its tagged garments hanging in the narrow closet space, each label in Marguerite's handwriting a small act of testimony: *worn the night of the storm. burnt sleeve, kept anyway.* And the dress with the date, smelling faintly of smoke after twenty-two years, smoke that would not wash out, smoke that had been kept on purpose.

Marguerite had kept records. Marguerite had written letters she never mailed. Marguerite had circled the same question for twenty years and died with it circling. And now the last physical place where any of it had happened was scheduled to become gravel on Friday morning.

Footsteps behind. Petra didn't turn; they knew the gait already, the careful walk of a sister approaching something she'd rather walk around.

"He begged you," Dree said. Not a question. She came to stand at the fence, a foot away, both of them looking at the house. "Theo. He called me after, crying, actually crying, did he tell you that? He said you wouldn't let it go. He said you were going to ruin everything."

"Is that what you think too?"

Dree was quiet for a while. A car passed, slow, the driver craning at the house the way people crane at accidents. Everyone in town knew the story's shape if not its contents: the fire, the family, the silence. The shape was public. Only the contents were locked.

"I think," Dree said slowly, "that I built a life on top of a foundation I never inspected. And I think you're standing down in the crawlspace with a flashlight, and I hate you for it, and I love you for it, and mostly I'm terrified that you're right." Her voice frayed on the last word. "Because if you're right, Petra—if Dad knew, if Mom knew, if Aunt Marguerite knew and wrote it all down and *still* we never talked about it—then every Christmas, every funeral, every casserole somebody brought over—"

"Was real anyway," Petra said. "Dree. It was real anyway. Love isn't a load-bearing wall. It can hold things up even when the truth gets in."

"You don't know that." Dree turned, and her eyes were wet and furious. "You write postcards to a *burned house*. You've spent ten years talking to nobody because talking to somebody was too dangerous. And now you want us all to stand up at some table and say the unsayable thing with five days left, like it's a deadline instead of a—instead of a wound—"

"Somebody oiled the hinge, Dree."

"I know." Barely a whisper. "I know who."

Petra turned. The amber light caught Dree's face, and there was no deflection left in it, no brightness, no rehearsal—just the raw architecture showing through the wallpaper at last.

"Friday," Dree said. "After Friday there's no house, no hinge, no footprints, no proof of anything except what Marguerite hid in wool. If you're going to ask your question—your big question, the one you've been sharpening for ten years—you have to ask it *before*. While the house is still standing to hold it. Because after that—" she looked at the garden door, small and white and shut, "—after that it's just us, and our memories, and you know what our memories are worth. Half-fragments. Vertigo. Somebody in the kitchen. Somebody leaving through the garden. Nobody agreeing on anything ever again."

The wind moved through the weeds along the fence line, a dry whispering, like paper turning.

"So ask it," Dree said, and her voice broke clean through, and she was crying now, openly, in public, on a sidewalk in front of the house where they'd grown up next door to a secret. "Ask it before the window closes. Ask it while there's still a *there* there. But Petra—God help you, God help all of us—be ready for what the answer does. Because Mom told you. The answer rearranges everyone."

She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, briskly, the way she'd done since she was seven, and walked away up Larch Street without looking back, her shadow stretching long ahead of her toward the ordinary evening world of lit kitchens and unasked questions.

Petra stayed at the fence until the streetlights came on. The house stood against the darkening sky, holding its rooms, holding its dust, holding its oiled hinge and its hidden key and whatever boyhood grief walked through that garden door in the evenings to sit in a dead man's kitchen.

Four days.

In the motel room that night, Petra took out a postcard—the week's postcard, the ritual, ten years unbroken—and wrote the address first, out of habit: the house on Larch Street, an address that hadn't received mail in twenty-two years. Then they sat with the pen hovering, and thought of Theo's steady hands, and Dree's cracked-open face, and Vivian at her window saying *houses fall down eventually either way.*

They didn't finish the postcard. Instead, on the back, beneath the impossible address, they made a list. Names. A date—Thursday, two days before the machines came. A single line at the bottom, underlined twice:

*Who set the fire, and why did we all agree not to know?*

Petra read it aloud once, alone, to the motel room, to the wardrobe with its labeled ghosts hanging in the closet, to the smoke that had waited twenty-two years in the seams of a blue dress to be heard.

It sounded, spoken aloud, like a door beginning to open.
