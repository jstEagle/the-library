## 4. Ada

I had rehearsed the funeral for eleven years, which is a strange thing to admit about a stranger's death, but Edith Wrenfield — Edith Underhill, Edith of the good coat and the careful hands — was never quite a stranger to me, and I had never quite been a stranger to her.

I stood at the back of St. Brannoc's with my collar up and let the rain do what rain does in Saltmere, which is to say everything slowly and without apology. The church smelled of wet wool and lilies past their argument, that sweetish rot underneath the perfume, and I thought: she would have hated this. She would have hated the lilies especially. She kept geraniums. Red ones, brutal and ordinary, in a window box that I once saw from a train platform when I was nineteen and too cowardly to knock on her door. Geraniums said something about a person that lilies never would.

The mourners filed in like weather. Aunts first, then the cousins, then the men who had known her through her husband's business and called her Edie in voices that suggested they had never once been invited to. And then the children — the famous ones, or famous-adjacent, because everyone in that church knew Felix Underhill's byline even if they'd never read him, and Margot's name was on half the plaques in the county's conservation offices.

They looked like her. That was the thing nobody warns you about grief at second hand: it ambushes you through resemblance. Felix had her mouth, the one that turned down at the corners even when it smiled, as if smiling were a transaction it was conducting reluctantly. Margot had her spine. I watched Margot move down the aisle receiving condolences the way you receive parcels you didn't order, and I thought: yes. That is exactly how she stood when she was angry. Exactly that stillness, that refusal to be moved an inch she hadn't chosen.

I had come to see them, though I'd have denied it under oath. I had told myself all the way down on the train that I was coming for the house, for the papers, for the simple administrative business of a family closing its accounts. But the truth is uglier and simpler: I came because dead people stop being able to refuse you. While Edith lived, every letter I wrote came back unopened or not at all, and every year the silence hardened into architecture, into a wall with her on one side and me on the other, and now the wall had a door in it shaped like a coffin, and I walked through it wearing my mother's grey coat.

* * *

My mother had died believing her father's name meant nothing to anyone anymore. That was the cruelty of it, or maybe the mercy — Vera Voss, who spent forty years saying *we were never important, Ada, we were never anybody*, while keeping his letters in a biscuit tin under her bed like relics, like contraband, like the only currency she trusted.

Thomas Voss. Say it fast and it sounds like nothing. Say it slow, the way my mother said it, once, after two sherries at Christmas when I was sixteen — *Thomas Voss* — and there was a whole country inside it. There was a man who built things and lent money he couldn't spare and laughed too loudly in rooms where laughter wasn't welcome, and there were two families, and there was a woman named Edith who stood in his kitchen in the photograph I have carried across three counties today, laughing with her head thrown back like a girl who has never once been afraid.

That woman became someone else. That is the whole story, or the shape of it, and I am getting ahead of myself, because at the churchyard I hadn't earned the middle of the story yet. At the churchyard I had only the edges.

* * *

Afterward, when the congregation spilled out onto the wet gravel and the umbrella-bloom opened over the relatives, I did the thing I had decided to do somewhere outside Taunton, staring at rain on glass: I used the name.

"Wrenfield," I said to the aunt — a small fierce woman with her brother's eyebrows, who introduced herself as Celia and shook my hand like she was testing its construction. "You must be the aunt. I'm so sorry for your loss. I knew Edith — Mrs. Wrenfield — before."

The word went into the air between us and sat there.

Celia blinked. Rain ticked on the umbrella above us. Somewhere behind her a cousin was telling a story about Edith's driving, doing the voice, getting the laugh.

"Before," Celia repeated. Not a question. A door held very slightly ajar, out of politeness, out of the funeral-day habit of admitting anyone who arrives carrying sorrow.

"Before," I agreed, and gave her nothing else, and she — here was the marvel, the thing I will think about for the rest of my life — she did not correct me. She patted my arm with a glove gone dark at the fingertips from the wet, and she said, "She had such a gift for beginnings, our Edie," and moved on to the next mourner as if she had said nothing at all.

As if she had said everything.

I stood there with the rain finding the gap between my collar and my hair and understood, in one cold drop down the back of my neck, that the name was not entirely news to these people. It was a word they had agreed not to hear. You could set it down in the middle of a funeral and it would lie there like a coin on a counter, and no one would pick it up, because picking it up would mean counting what it was worth.

* * *

He followed me. Of course he followed me; he was always going to follow me; I had counted on it the way you count on water going downhill.

Felix Underhill came around the corner of the church at a walk that wanted to be a run, and stopped about four feet away, and looked at me with his mother's eyes doing something hers had never done, which was search.

"You said Wrenfield," he said.

"I did."

"That isn't—" He stopped. Recalibrated. I could see the critic in him taking over from the son, the professional instinct to describe precisely rather than feel imprecisely. "Nobody here uses that name."

"No," I said. "Isn't that interesting."

Rain ran off the church gutter between us in a thin bright rope. Behind him the mourners clustered under their umbrellas like a slow black flower opening and closing. His sister was watching from beneath the lychgate, arms crossed, her face doing the arithmetic of strangers at funerals: who are you, who invited you, whose grief are you spending.

"My name is Ada Voss," I said, because he deserved at least that much, and because saying it aloud in Saltmere churchyard felt like setting a match to paper just to watch whether it caught. "I knew your mother before she was your mother. Before she was an Underhill. That's all I can tell you today."

"Why today?"

"Because today she can't tell you I'm lying." I hadn't planned to say that. It came out with more edge than I'd intended, and I watched it land, watched him flinch the way people flinch at truth that arrives dressed as cruelty. "I'm sorry. That was unkind. But you'll want to check everything I ever tell you against her version, and she's the only witness either of us trusted, and now she's—"

I gestured at the church. At the flowers. At the whole wet apparatus of ending.

He didn't answer for a long moment. Rain filled the silence the way it does, generously, taking the blame for it.

"She never mentioned anyone," he said finally. "Anyone at all, from before. There wasn't a before. There was her wedding, and then there was us."

"Yes," I said. "That was rather the point of her."

And I saw it hit him — not the meaning yet, but the shape of the meaning, the way you see lightning's branch before you hear the thunder explain it. He took a step back. Not away from me, exactly. Away from the sentence.

* * *

Margot intercepted me at the gate. Of course she did. Spine first.

"I don't know you," she said. It wasn't hostile, precisely. It was procedural. She said it the way a customs officer says *anything to declare*.

"No," I agreed. "You don't. Your mother made sure of that."

Something moved behind her face — quick, quickly suppressed, a fish under ice. "If you're here about the estate, speak to the solicitor. Mr. Halloway, in Saltmere. If you're here for any other reason—"

"I'm not here for money."

"Everyone says that."

"They do," I said, "because usually it's true and they're ashamed of it. I'm not ashamed of anything I've come for, Ms. Underhill, but I'm not ready to say what it is, because if I say it wrong, in this weather, on this day, you'll spend the rest of your lives defending a version of your mother that I'll have damaged with bad timing. I'd rather be rude than careless."

Margot looked at me for a long time. She had her mother's way of looking, level and unhurried, as though the world were a document she was proofreading. Whatever she found in my face, she filed it.

"The wake is at the house," she said at last. "Two o'clock. You're not invited."

"I know."

"Then why are you still standing here?"

"Because I'm leaving this," I said, "and I wanted to leave it where you'd find it yourselves. Not handed over. Found. There's a difference, and the difference matters more than you can possibly know yet."

I put the photograph on the flat stone top of the gatepost, weighted it with the smallest stone from the wall, and walked away down the lane without looking back, which cost me more than the whole day had so far.

* * *

Here is what the photograph shows, since I will not be there when they turn it over, and someone should say it plainly at least once:

A summer afternoon, late, gold going to amber. Two young women in front of a house — not this house, not the tidy grey Underhill house with its geraniums and its respectability, but a low white-walled place with a blue door and a fig tree against the south side, a house I have visited twice in my life and both times pressed my palm flat against that door as if taking a pulse. My grandfather's house. The house where everything happened and nothing was written down.

One of the women is my grandmother, Ruth — younger than I ever knew her, hair loose, one shoe off and dangling from her fingers, mid-laugh at some joke the camera arrived too late to hear.

The other woman is Edith.

Edith Wrenfield, aged perhaps twenty-two, head thrown back, throat bare, laughing with the total unguardedness of a person who has not yet learned what her laugh will cost her. Her arm is through my grandmother's arm. They are leaning into each other the way friends lean when the friendship is new enough to still feel like luck.

On the back, in my grandmother's handwriting, faded to the brown of old tea: *E. & R., Voss house, June.* No surname for E. There never needed to be one, then. Everyone knew which E. Everyone knew whose house.

That is what I left on the gatepost for the children of the woman who erased us: not an accusation. An invitation. Proof that their mother had been someone before she was theirs — someone loud, someone loved, someone with a friend whose family name she would one day take like a coat and wear until the original owner froze.

I did it knowing what it would do. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is the whole inheritance I'm carrying. I left it because I was tired of being the only one who knew, and because grief makes people brave in ways they'll regret, and because some part of me — the part that takes after Thomas Voss, according to my mother, usually as a warning — wanted to watch the ground shift under their feet the way it shifted under ours thirty years ago.

Quiet provocation. That's what I'd call it if I were being kind. A stone lifted, and whatever lives underneath it left blinking in the light.

* * *

I watched from the end of the lane — I know, I said I didn't look back, but that was at the gatepost, and this was later, from behind a hedge like a spy in my own melodrama — I watched the cars go up to the house for the wake, and the lights come on in the grey windows, and the last of the daylight go off the sea like a lid closing.

At some point a figure came out onto the porch. Even at that distance I knew it was Felix; he had his mother's way of standing in doorways, one shoulder against the frame, as if doors were things you negotiated with rather than passed through. He bent. He picked something up off the step.

For a long time he didn't move. The porch light made a small yellow room around him and he stood in it absolutely still, the way people stand when the floor has quietly become water.

Then he turned the photograph over. I saw his thumb move across the back of it, reading the handwriting, and even from a hundred yards I swear I felt it, the exact moment the name on the back of a photograph becomes a question with teeth.

He looked up. Out toward the lane, toward the dark, toward — absurdly, inevitably — me, though he couldn't have seen me, couldn't have known. He looked straight down the line of the hedge as if the evening itself had asked him a question and he intended to answer it.

I turned up my collar and walked to the bus stop in the rain, and behind me Saltmere lit its windows one by one against the weather, and in one of them, I knew, a man was holding a picture of a house that wasn't theirs, and a name that was mine, and a mother who had been, once, before everything, somebody else's friend.

He would come and find me. Men like Felix always come and find you; they can't help it; curiosity is the appetite they can't lose, even the ones who've lost every other.

I only hoped, watching the road for headlights that weren't there yet, that when he came, he'd bring his sister. Because the letters were never really for him alone — and neither, whatever Edith had spent her life pretending, was the truth.
