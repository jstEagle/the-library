## 7. What the Critic Cannot Taste

The last thing Felix Underhill ever tasted was a spoonful of his mother's soup.

He would spend months trying to make the memory behave — to arrange it in sequence, cause before effect, so that it might be entered into evidence like anything else in his life: dated, sourced, defensible. But memory refused the discipline he imposed on restaurants. It kept rearranging itself. Some nights the soup came first and the telephone second; other nights the phone was already ringing when he lifted the spoon, and the taste dissolved not on his tongue but somewhere behind it, in the dark anteroom where flavor became thought.

What he could never rearrange was the soup itself. Chicken, but not chicken broth exactly — she had always called it "cleansing broth," an inheritance from her own mother, made with a whole bird, a parsnip, too much dill, and a lemon cut in half and left to float face-down like a small drowned sun. She had made it every time one of them was ill, and once, memorably, when Felix at nine had come home having told his first lie, standing in the kitchen with his school tie still knotted and his ears scarlet, and she had set a bowl in front of him without a word and sat across from him until he'd eaten all of it and confessed.

So when she called him that Tuesday in late October — *Felix, love, I've made too much again, come take some off my hands* — he went, because he always went, because the ritual required no decision any more than breathing did. He took the train out to Saltmere with an empty container in his canvas bag like a man going to collect a debt.

She looked well that day. That was the thing he would return to later, turning it over like a stone with something underneath it: she looked *well*. Her color was good. Her hair had been done — Margot took her on Thursdays, but this was a Tuesday, so she must have gone alone, which meant she was feeling strong, which meant — he understood only much later — that some part of her had been tidying herself for departure, the way you clean a house before guests.

"You look thin," she said, which was what she always said.

"I'm eating constantly," he said, which was what he always said. "It's my job."

"Eating isn't tasting," she said, and laughed, and ladled the soup.

He remembered the steam. He remembered lifting the spoon. He remembered thinking, with the professional detachment that had become his native language, that the dill was slightly past its best — that she'd bought it Friday and it was fading by Tuesday — and that he would say nothing about it, because there were things you said and things you kept, and after forty years of saying things about food for money he had developed a private conviction that the kindest criticism is the one swallowed whole.

The soup touched his tongue.

And nothing happened.

Not badness. Not absence-as-flavor, the way water has an absence, or the way a room smells when it's been cleaned of smell. Nothing. His tongue reported temperature — warm, salt-warm — and texture, and then stopped, the way a clock stops, mid-tick, as though someone had put a hand over the works.

He lowered the spoon. He took another mouthful. He closed his eyes and did the thing he did in restaurants when a dish was shy and needed coaxing into speech: he breathed through it, let it coat the palate, waited for the retronasal lift, the bloom.

Static. Warm static.

"Felix?" His mother was watching him from across the table, her hands folded, her face doing something complicated. "Is it all right?"

"It's perfect," he said, and heard his own voice arrive from very far away, fluent and hollow, a man reading his lines off a card. "It's exactly right."

He finished the bowl. He praised the dill. He kissed her cheek at the door — her skin smelled of talc and lemons, and even then, even in that moment, he registered that he could no longer tell whether the lemons were real or remembered — and he walked to the station with the empty container knocking against his hip, and somewhere between her gate and the platform he began, quietly, methodically, to panic.

* * *

The first three days he told himself it was a cold.

Everyone knows the flattened world of a head-cold: food goes mute, wine goes dead, coffee tastes like warm pennies. He had a slight pressure behind the sinuses, or believed he did; belief is enough when you're building a shelter. He bought decongestants. He drank ginger tea and held each mouthful in his mouth like a man panning for gold, and got nothing, and told himself the river simply hadn't yielded yet.

On the fourth day he cooked a test meal for himself in his flat in London — he lived above a shuttered tailor's in Pimlico, a flat chosen for its kitchen the way other men chose flats for their views — and he approached it as an examination. He wanted rigor. He wanted the problem isolated, variables controlled, because this was how he had survived every difficulty of his adult life: by making it a procedure.

He roasted a chicken. He made a pan sauce with shallots and vermouth. He steamed new potatoes and dressed them while they were hot so they'd drink the butter. He laid it all out with a glass of white Burgundy he'd been saving, a Meursault from a vintage he could have lectured on, and he sat down at his own table like a man sitting down to a séance.

The chicken: nothing. The sauce: warmth, acidity registering somewhere as a faint electric prickle at the back of the jaw, and nothing else. The potatoes: hot, soft, gone. The wine — he swirled it, warmed the bowl in his palm, took a proper mouthful and drew air across it the way he'd taught a thousand readers to do —

Nothing. Not a bad wine. Not a flawed wine. No wine at all. Grape-colored water with opinions about temperature.

He sat in his kitchen for a long time with the food cooling in front of him, and what frightened him was not the loss itself but how quickly his mind moved to protect him from it — how fast it offered explanations, the way a good maître d' offers excuses for a delayed table. Fatigue. A virus. Medication. He wasn't taking medication. Allergies. He'd never had allergies. And then, arriving quietly, wearing respectable clothes, the explanation he would carry into the doctor's office like a shield: stress. Overwork. The review due Friday. The body staging a small strike, easily settled.

He wrote the review that night. This was the part he never told anyone, the part that shamed him more than the loss itself: he wrote it anyway, and it was good. He wrote about a restaurant in Bermondsey where a young chef was doing things with smoked eel and brown butter that deserved a wider audience, and he described the eel's sweetness giving way to smoke the way dusk gives way to streetlight, and he described the butter as nutty and deep, carrying the dish on its back like a patient animal — and every word of it was true, and none of it was present. He was writing from the archive. Forty years of meals were stored in him, indexed and cross-referenced, and he found he could compose fluently out of that vault without once consulting the ruined instrument in his mouth. It was like a pianist playing after going deaf, except that Beethoven had heard the notes first. Felix was discovering that he had spent his whole career hearing them first too — that taste, for him, had long ago become a form of memory performed in real time, and that the real time had apparently been optional for years.

He filed the review. He poured the Meursault down the sink and watched it go and felt nothing, which was the whole problem, everywhere, all the time now: feeling nothing and being unable to locate the border between the symptom and the self.

* * *

His mother's stroke came on the Sunday.

Margot called him at six in the morning, her voice stripped down to wire. "It's Mum. They think it's a bleed. Left side. Felix — I'm already in the car, are you—"

"I'm coming," he said, and stood in his kitchen holding the phone, and noticed distantly that he had drunk his morning coffee without noticing whether it tasted of anything, and understood that the question had stopped mattering, and that this was its own kind of catastrophe.

The week that followed existed afterward in his memory as weather rather than event. Hospital corridors the color of weak tea. Margot's competence, ferocious and brittle, handling forms and consultants while he handled — what? Chairs. He fetched chairs. He learned the geography of the vending machine. He sat beside his mother's bed in the neurology ward and watched her left hand lie on the blanket like something washed ashore, and listened to her speak in a voice slurred at the edges but unmistakably hers, and heard her say, on the second evening, looking at him with an expression he couldn't read:

"You'll write it all down, won't you. One day. Everything."

"Everything about what?" he asked.

But her eyes had gone to the window, where rain was doing its patient work on the glass, and she said, "Nothing, love. Never mind," in the tone she used for closing doors.

The doctors used words like *event* and *insult* and *recovery trajectory*, and Margot translated them into decisions, and Felix moved through it all with the strange buoyancy of shock, aware dimly that he should be feeling more than he was feeling, and filing even that observation away with everything else.

It was eleven days after the soup — he counted, because counting was the last procedure available to him — that he finally saw Dr. Anwar, an ENT consultant recommended by a colleague, a precise woman with reading glasses pushed up into grey hair who examined him with an efficiency he found almost comforting. Nasal endoscopy. Cranial nerve testing. Questions about trauma, toxins, dental work, viral illness. Then she sat back, folded her hands, and looked at him over the glasses.

"Your olfactory system is structurally sound," she said. "Your taste buds are intact. Your cranial nerves are behaving beautifully." A pause, calibrated. "Mr. Underhill, when did your mother have her stroke?"

The question landed in the room like a dropped glass.

"Eleven days ago," he said. "Twelve. Why?"

"And the loss of taste preceded it by — a day? Two?"

"The same week," he said. "I don't see—"

"Pure taste loss is rare," she said. "Almost always it's smell doing the heavy lifting, and yours is fine. What you're describing — complete gustatory silence with intact anatomy, onset coinciding with acute family crisis — is something we see occasionally. We call it psychogenic ageusia." She let the word sit there. "Your brain has switched the sense off, Mr. Underhill. Not because it's broken. Because something has arrived that it cannot process, and taste — for reasons you probably understand better than I do — appears to be where the circuit overloaded."

"That's not—" He heard his own voice go thin. "I'm a restaurant critic. Taste is — it's my profession. My identity. If my mind were going to choose something to break, it wouldn't choose—"

"Wouldn't it?" she said, not unkindly. "Think about it differently. You've spent your life converting feeling into description. Food is your sanctioned channel for emotion — pleasure, nostalgia, grief, all of it routed through the mouth and out onto the page where it's safe. Now your mother is dying, and there is apparently a great deal you haven't said to her." She removed her glasses entirely. "I can't know that. I'm reading your file and your face. But bodies are honest in ways their owners aren't. Something shut the door, Mr. Underhill. My job is to tell you the door is locked from the inside. Finding the key is your department. I'd suggest someone in mine's sister profession."

He walked out of the hospital into a bright cold afternoon and stood on the steps for a long time, not moving, while London flowed around him. Psychogenic. In his head. Locked from the inside. He turned the word over and felt the professional in him reach instinctively for the counterargument — *unfalsifiable, unfalsifiable diagnoses are how medicine launders its ignorance* — and felt, beneath that, the other thing, the true thing, rising slowly like silt in disturbed water:

That she was right.

That he had known since the churchyard-quiet moment in his mother's kitchen, spoon in hand, when he'd said *it's perfect* and meant *I cannot feel this*, that the loss was not in his tongue. That his tongue had merely been the first honest witness to resign.

* * *

He tried, in the weeks after the funeral — no. He corrected himself, lying awake in the Saltmere house in the days after the funeral, listening to the pipes tick and Margot moving below him like weather in the lower floor. He tried, in the weeks after the *stroke*, to follow the doctor's prescription. He saw a therapist twice, a soft-spoken man in Bloomsbury who asked him what his relationship with his mother had been like before the illness, and Felix gave him the official version — warm, close, weekly calls, the soup, the rituals — and watched the therapist listen with the particular patience of a man being handed a menu instead of a meal. On the third appointment Felix didn't go. There was a review. There was always a review.

Because here was the arithmetic he could not escape, the sum that followed him from London to Saltmere and sat on the edge of his bed at night: if the doctor was right, then his taste had not been taken from him. He had put it down. Voluntarily, in the way of all denials — the hand does the setting-down while the mouth insists it was stolen. Somewhere in the days around that last bowl of soup, something had surfaced in him too large to swallow, and his mind had done what minds do: it had closed the kitchen. And the something was not the stroke. The stroke came after. The stroke was almost a relief, in the terrible economy of these things — a medical explanation arriving just in time to cover the psychological one, grief wearing a diagnosis like a borrowed coat.

Except that grief for what? She hadn't died yet, that Tuesday. She'd been well. She'd been laughing. She'd said *eating isn't tasting*, and laughed, and the sentence had passed through him like a bullet through a window, leaving no hole he could find until months later.

Unless — and this was the thought he could not finish, the sentence his own mind now refused to serve him — unless something else had happened that Tuesday. Some look on her face. Some hesitation in the hallway. Some half-opened door in her manner that his unconscious had read instantly and completely, and that his conscious mind, loyal son, faithful archivist of surfaces, had declined to witness.

He lay in his childhood bed in the house on the hill above Saltmere, in the week of the funeral, and stared at the ceiling his father had painted white thirty years ago, and made himself a private list, because lists were what he had instead of prayer:

Sentences I have never said to my mother.

*I know there was someone before Dad.* No — he didn't know that; he suspected nothing; that entry couldn't go on the list yet. He struck it. Started again.

*I noticed you flinched when Aunt Celia mentioned the old summers.*

*I know you keep a room locked in the garden and I have never once asked why, and neither has Margot, and we both learned that silence from you, and I am tired, Mother, I am so tired of being fluent in everything except the one language this family speaks.*

He didn't say any of it. She was ash now, in a rosewood box on the mantel downstairs, and the sentences sat in him undelivered, undeliverable, accruing interest.

Below his window, the garden lay grey under drizzle, and at the far end of it, half-hidden by a holly that had grown lawless in its neglect, stood the garden room with its padlocked door — the room Margot hadn't entered since the stroke, the room nobody spoke of, the room that had been silent his entire life the way his mouth had been silent for five weeks now.

Locked from the inside, the doctor had said.

Felix got up and stood at the window, looking down at it, and felt for the first time since the soup the faintest ghost of something on his tongue — not a taste, nothing so kind, just a stirring, an attention, the palate leaning forward like a dog hearing its name.

Tomorrow, he thought. Tomorrow he would ask Margot about the room.

He did not yet know that tomorrow the question would be answered for him — that a stranger in a grey coat was already in Saltmere, saying their mother's other name to anyone who would stand still long enough to hear it.
