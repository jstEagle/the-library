## 5. The Neighbor Who Kept Copies

Mr. Anselm still lived at number 14, which Zephyrine knew because he had checked the electoral roll at the Meridian the way other men check horoscopes — not for guidance exactly, but for the comfort of seeing a name persist in print. Anselm, H. The H turned out to be Hollis, though for twenty-two years of Zephyrine's childhood he had been nothing more than *the man who swept his step*, a figure in a cardigan the color of weak tea who appeared every morning at seven with a broom and an opinion about the weather.

The 47 dropped him two stops short of the old neighborhood, as it now did everything; the rerouting had already begun eating the route from its edges like a moth working through a coat. He walked the rest. The streets had shrunk, the way childhood streets do, and grown quieter, the way all streets were doing lately, as if the whole city were holding its breath before some announcement.

Number 14 had a new door — blue where it had once been green — but the same stone cat on the sill, one ear worn to a nub by decades of small hands. Zephyrine stood on the step longer than was dignified, rehearsing openings. *Hello, you don't know me, except that you absolutely do.* In the end the door opened on its own, because Mr. Anselm had seen him through the lace curtain, and old men who sweep their steps see everything.

"Well," said Mr. Anselm. "You've got her chin."

Zephyrine had prepared several speeches. All of them left him. "Hello, Mr. Anselm."

"Come in before the cold does. It follows people up the path, cold. Like a dog that's decided you're its person."

* * *

The front room smelled of toast and furniture polish and, underneath both, something older — the particular dust of a house that has been loved by one occupant for fifty years and has stopped pretending to be anything else. There was a gas fire with real coals arranged on it, purely decorative, and above the mantel a clock that ticked with the confidence of a metronome. On the wall hung photographs: children long grown, a woman in a nurse's cape, a dog. No photographs of the Vane family. Zephyrine noticed this the way he noticed everything now — filing it, cross-referencing, unable to stop.

"You'll want tea," said Mr. Anselm, not asking. "Everyone says they don't, and everyone drinks it."

The tea came in a mug with a faded picture of a lighthouse. Mr. Anselm lowered himself into an armchair with the careful choreography of a man whose knees had filed formal complaints years ago, and looked at Zephyrine with eyes that were watery and shrewd at once, like a harbor in weather.

"Odile rang me," he said. "Tuesday. She said you'd found the box."

"She said I shouldn't read them."

"Odile always did think silence was a kind of shelving. Put things away properly and they keep." He sipped his tea. "She gets it from her mother. Your mother could keep a secret the way other women keep orchids. Fed it daily. Turned it toward the light. Never let anyone touch it."

"Did you help her?"

It came out harder than Zephyrine intended, and he watched it land — watched the old man receive the accusation without flinching, the way you'd catch a ball thrown at your chest.

"Yes," said Mr. Anselm.

Just that. Yes. The clock ticked through four entire seconds of it.

"I loved your mother," the old man went on, "in the way you love someone you've shared a fence with for thirty years. Not romantically. Don't make that face. Fences are serious business. You learn a person's whole soul over a fence." He set down the mug. "And when she came to me that winter — you'd have been six, maybe seven — and told me what she meant to do, and asked me if I thought she was doing right—"

"What did you tell her?"

"I told her no." A pause. "And then I helped her do it anyway."

Zephyrine felt the sentence go into him like a splinter, clean and deep. "Why?"

"Because she wasn't asking permission. She was asking for a witness. There's a difference, and a man learns it late or never." Mr. Anselm's hands were folded on his knee, knuckles like a row of small hills. "Your father wrote. Every year, near your birthday. I know because they came to my address first — he didn't have theirs anymore, and I was the only fixed point either of them trusted. I'd walk them round, hand them to her, and she'd take them like they were hot out of the oven, and write on the envelope, and give them back to me to post back. Return to sender. And every year I'd say, Marguerite, this is a cruelty dressed as a kindness. And every year she'd say, Hollis, so is the truth, to a child."

"And you agreed with her."

"No," said Mr. Anselm. "I disagreed with her for twenty-two years and carried her letters to the post office anyway. That's what love looks like at my age. It looks like disagreement you keep showing up for." He almost smiled. "I kept copies, mind."

Zephyrine looked up. "Copies."

"I'm a retired postman. We're sentimental about paper. It's a occupational disease." He levered himself out of the chair, muttering at his knees in a tone one uses with old colleagues, and went to the sideboard. From the bottom drawer, beneath a folded flag and a tin of buttons, he brought out a shoebox — an actual shoebox, the cardboard gone soft at the corners, labeled in pencil: *VANE. NOT MINE TO OPEN BUT KEPT ANYWAY.*

He held it a moment before giving it over, weighing it, or weighing something else.

"He wasn't a monster," Mr. Anselm said quietly. "Whatever story got built around him in that kitchen. He was a frightened man who made a bad bargain and then spent his life paying interest on it. I saw him, near the end. He asked after you. He asked whether you liked books." A pause, and the old man's voice went rough at the edges. "I told him you worked in a library. He laughed. First time I'd heard him laugh since the winter. He said, *of course he does.*"

Zephyrine took the box. It weighed almost nothing and entirely too much.

"There's one more thing," Mr. Anselm said. "Not in the box. This one I kept out." He reached into his cardigan pocket and produced a photograph, handled soft as cloth at its edges. "Because some things you shouldn't find in a box. Some things should be handed to you."

* * *

The photograph was black and white going silver at the margins, the way photographs do when they've been looked at too much by one person. A young man stood on a front step, holding a baby against his shoulder with the terrified tenderness of all new fathers — arms slightly wrong, head bent as if listening for instructions. The baby was asleep, one fist curled under its chin, wearing a knitted hat with ears.

Zephyrine knew the baby. Not by memory — he had no memory of this — but by the family album, where the same hat appeared on page three, captioned in his mother's hand: *Z., autumn.* He had assumed, all his life, that the step beneath them belonged to the family house. That the man was a neighbor, a cousin, a stranger caught in the frame.

But the man was looking at the camera with an expression Zephyrine saw in the mirror every morning when he logged overdue books and tried not to look hopeful about anything. Same jaw. Same way of standing slightly apologetic in his own body.

"That's him," Zephyrine said. Not a question. His voice came out strange, flattened, like sound heard through a wall.

"That's Emile," said Mr. Anselm. "Your father."

And then Zephyrine looked past them, at the step, at the door behind them — a door painted a dark, glossy green, with a brass knocker shaped like an acorn — and the ground performed a slow, quiet revolution beneath him.

He knew that door.

He had walked past it a hundred times. A thousand. Every visit home, every bus ride, every walk to the corner shop with Odile complaining about the price of everything — he had passed the green door with the acorn knocker, and once, he distinctly remembered, he had touched the acorn because it was the sort of thing a child touches, and a woman had opened the door and smiled at him and said, *careful, love, it bites,* and he had laughed and run on.

Two streets. The house was two streets from the house he grew up in. His father had lived — had *died*, the word arrived unbidden and stayed — within shouting distance, within walking distance, within the distance a boy covers in ninety seconds at a run, and nobody had ever said.

"All those years," Zephyrine said. His voice sounded like someone else's. "I walked past."

"Every Sunday," said Mr. Anselm gently. "He used to watch for you. From the upstairs window. He'd know you by the way you read while you walked — book in one hand, dodging lampposts. He called it your *perambulatory library.*" The old man's eyes were wet now and he made no apology for it. "He never once crossed the street. He said he'd promised. But he watched. A man can starve on watching, you know. Most people don't know that. They think watching is free."

Zephyrine sat with the photograph in both hands. Outside, somewhere, a bus changed gears — the 47, probably, making its diminished rounds, carrying strangers past houses full of unsaid things, as it always had, as it would only a few more weeks.

"Why are you telling me this now?" he asked. "Why keep it all these years and hand it over today?"

Mr. Anselm considered the question with the seriousness he presumably gave to weather.

"Because I'm eighty-one," he said, "and I've started thinking about who sweeps whose step after me. Because Odile rang me Tuesday and lied to me, and Odile's never lied to me, which means she's frightened, which means it's time. And because—" he paused, and the harbor-eyes steadied, "—because your mother is forgetting, son. Forgetting is coming for all of it. The pact, the reasons, the arithmetic she did. In a year there'll be no one left alive who knows why the silence was built, and a silence nobody remembers the reason for isn't kindness anymore. It's just a wall with no door in it. I've spent my life around walls. You want to know which ones have doors."

He nodded at the shoebox.

"There's a gap in there," he said. "One year missing. Nineteen years ago. Your mother answered him once — I posted the reply myself, and I never saw it again, and he never mentioned it, and I've wondered about it every day since. If you're going to read these, you'll want to know about the gap. Ask your sister."

The clock ticked. The decorative coals sat in their fireless grate. Zephyrine looked down at the photograph — his father's face, his own face, the green door, the acorn knocker, the street he had walked his whole life without knowing he was walking home.

"Mr. Anselm," he said slowly, "which year? Which letter is missing?"

The old man looked at him for a long moment, and then at the window, where the light was beginning to fail, and Zephyrine understood that he was not deciding whether to answer but whether the answer was his to give.

"Nineteen years ago," he said at last, "your sister Odile was fourteen years old. And she was the one who collected the post that winter."
