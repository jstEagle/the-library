## 1. Expansion Joints

The bridge over the Callum River had been breathing for sixty-two years, and Vesper Oyelaran had learned to listen to it the way other people listened to music.

She knelt at the north abutment at seven in the morning, before the traffic thickened, and opened her calipers. The expansion joint here—a finger joint, steel fingers interleaving like two hands about to clasp—was designed to open forty millimeters in winter and close to nearly nothing in August heat. Today, in the last week of September, with the river running low and brown beneath her and the air smelling of cold coming, the gap measured thirty-one point four millimeters.

She wrote it down. 31.4. Within tolerance. She wrote down the date, the joint number, the ambient temperature, the condition of the neoprene seal—perished at the edges, weeping a little at the third segment, replace within twelve months. Her handwriting was small and exact, the handwriting of someone who had never once wanted to be misunderstood on paper.

Below her, the river moved with the patience of water that had been moving for ten thousand years and intended to keep at it. Above her, a semi truck crossed and the deck shivered, a fine tremor she felt through her knees, through the bones of her hands, a greeting from a structure she knew better than she knew most people.

"Morning, old girl," she said. She always said it. Nobody was around to hear.

She had been inspecting bridges for nineteen years. She could read a weld the way her mother had once read faces—quickly, totally, with a fluency that looked like intuition but was really just ten thousand hours of attention. She knew that steel got tired the way people did, that fatigue cracks began invisibly at points of stress and only announced themselves when they were nearly fatal. She knew that every bridge was a negotiation between things that wanted to move and things that held them, and that the whole art of engineering was designing for the movement, planning the gap in advance, building the failure into the success so the structure wouldn't tear itself apart.

Expansion joints. She had explained the concept to Theo once, years ago, on a drive over this very bridge, back when they still narrated things to each other. *The bridge can't be rigid,* she'd said. *It has to be allowed to change length, or the forces build up until something cracks.* And Theo had said, *Like a marriage,* and they had both laughed, and it had been true in a way that neither of them had followed up on, the way you don't pull a thread you suspect is holding the whole garment together.

She moved along the deck on her hands and knees, checking the second joint, the third. Traffic passed over her in a river of its own. She was invisible down here, a hi-vis orange smudge against the gray steel, and she had long ago made her peace with invisibility. It was, she sometimes thought, her native language. Her mother had spoken it fluently too.

*Her mother.* The thought arrived the way thoughts about her mother always did—sideways, uninvited, carrying weather.

She checked the bearing at pier two. Elastomeric, bulging slightly on the south edge, not yet failed. She photographed it. She measured the bulge: nine millimeters against a design limit of twelve. She wrote it down. Everything got written down. That was the job: you measured the distance between what a thing was and what it was supposed to be, and you reported the distance honestly, because the distance was where the danger lived, and also where the life was. A bridge with no gap in it was a bridge about to break.

Her phone buzzed in her chest pocket. She didn't look. It would be Theo, or it would be the office, and either way the message would be the same size and shape as all their messages: small, functional, load-bearing in its way, and empty of anything you could actually live on.

*Running late. Starting without you?*

That was what it would say. Or: *Milk's out.* Or: *Did you pay the water bill.* Fourteen years of marriage had distilled their correspondence down to logistics, a series of hand signals between people standing on opposite sides of a canyon they had both, at some unmarked moment, agreed to stop looking across.

She finished the bearing and sat back on her heels and let herself look at the water.

The Callum in late September ran shallow over its stones, and the light came down through it in long slanted bars, and there was a heron standing in the shallows on the far side with the stillness of something that had made stillness its profession. Vesper watched it. She was good at watching. It was the other thing—the being watched, the being seen, the standing in the open water of your own life and letting someone know where you actually were—that she had never mastered.

* * *

At the fourth expansion joint, she found the crack.

It was small—a hairline, running transverse across the third finger of the joint, maybe forty millimeters long. She cleaned it with a wire brush, blew the dust out, and examined it with the dye penetrant kit from her bag. The dye bled along the crack's length and stopped, clean-edged, at the finger's root. A fatigue crack, early stage. The kind of thing that, caught now, was a maintenance item. A note in a file. A work order for a welder with a grinder and a free afternoon.

The kind of thing that, missed, became the reason a bridge closed, or worse—the reason a bridge made the news.

She photographed it from three angles. She measured its length and width. She opened her inspection form to the section for joint four and began to write.

And here was the thing she would think about later, in the dark, in the bed she shared with a man she had stopped being able to talk to: she hesitated. Not long. Three seconds, maybe four. Her pen hovered over the box marked *Condition Rating*, where a 5 meant *fair* and a 4 meant *poor* and the difference between them was the difference between *monitor* and *act*. The bridge was scheduled for resurfacing next summer anyway. The county was stretched thin—she knew this, everyone knew this, the maintenance backlog was a canyon of its own. A 4 would trigger an immediate review, a flag, paperwork, a hearing maybe, a whole apparatus of attention that would fall on a bridge that was, overall, fundamentally sound, over one small crack in one steel finger that could be ground and welded in an afternoon.

She wrote 5. *Fair. Monitor at next inspection.*

It was even defensible. It was arguably correct. The crack was early-stage; the rating scale was a matter of judgment; other inspectors would have written 5. She told herself all of this while she packed up the calipers, and she noticed, the way you notice a sound in another room, that something in her had gone quiet and dishonest at the same time, and that she had done it smoothly, without drama, the way you do anything you've practiced.

She had signed off on worse than a crack, she thought, walking the length of the deck back toward her truck with the wind pushing at her back. She had spent fourteen years certifying a structure she knew had a fatigue crack running through it, and she had never once filed the honest report.

* * *

The inspection office was a converted farmhouse outside town, all radiators and sagging bookshelves, and her supervisor, Dennis, took her report at four o'clock without reading it, the way he took everyone's reports, stamping the front page and filing it in the cabinet that was itself, she sometimes thought, a kind of bridge—spanning the gap between what the county knew about its structures and what it wanted to know.

"Callum's holding up?" he asked.

"Holding up," she said.

"Good woman." He meant the bridge. He said it about every bridge. It was the closest thing to tenderness the office permitted.

She drove home through the long amber light of the equinox, past fields going gold and bare, and she thought about the crack. Not the steel one. The other one. She thought about how she and Theo had built their marriage the way engineers built the first bridges—rigid, no expansion joints, no allowance for the fact that two things joined together would grow and shrink and shift with the seasons, would become, over time, slightly different lengths than they started. No gap designed in. And so the forces had built up, invisible, at the points of highest stress, and the structure hadn't cracked all at once but slowly, hairline by hairline, in places no inspection was scheduled to check.

The job Theo didn't take. That was one. Four years ago, a firm in Edinburgh, a role he'd wanted with a wanting she could feel across a dinner table like heat off a stove, and he'd turned it down without discussing it, and she had known why—he'd turned it down because of her, because her work was here, because he'd decided for both of them and called it consideration—and she had said thank you, or something adjacent to thank you, or possibly nothing at all, and the crack had opened a millimeter and then sealed itself over with the oxide of daily life, and they had never spoken of it again. It was still there. She could put her thumb on the exact spot.

The child. That was the other one. The one with no oxide on it at all, still bright metal, still sharp. The year they were thirty-four, when the question had stood between them at the kitchen table like a guest neither would acknowledge, and Theo had said, eventually, carefully, *I'm happy either way, whatever you want*—handing her the whole decision wrapped in the language of generosity—and she had heard underneath it the thing he actually meant, which was *I don't want to be the one who wanted it and didn't get it*, and she had said *me too, either way*, and they had let the window close with the two of them standing on either side of it, each waiting for the other to say the true thing, and the true thing had never been said, and now she was forty-three and the window was not a window anymore, it was a wall with wallpaper over it.

*Either way.* She said it to herself now, in the truck, and heard how it sounded. The two most expensive words she had ever spoken.

She was fluent in structures. She could tell you the modulus of elasticity of weathering steel and the difference between a pin and a hanger and why the Tacoma Narrows fell and why the Firth of Forth still stood. She could read the health of a hundred-year-old bridge from the sound its railing made when you struck it with a knuckle. And she could not—she had discovered this with the slow horror of a person realizing they've been illiterate in their own native country—she could not read herself, could not say the simplest true sentence about her own interior to the one person who had signed on to hear it.

*Theo, I didn't want you to take that job, and I have never forgiven you for not taking it, and I have never forgiven myself for making it a choice between me and it.*

*Theo, I wanted a child. I wanted one and I let the wanting die quietly because you wouldn't say first whether you wanted it, and some mornings I wake up and the silence where that life would have been is the loudest thing in the house.*

She rehearsed neither of these. She had never rehearsed them. They existed in her the way the fatigue crack existed in the steel finger—present, measurable, growing at a rate that depended entirely on the loads placed on it, and reportable at any time by anyone with the courage to open the box of dye and look.

* * *

The house was lit when she got home, gold in the windows, and for a moment—this happened sometimes, less now than before—she felt the old pull of it, the *coming home* feeling, and she let herself have the moment because she was tired and it was warm and the lie was comfortable the way an old joint is comfortable, worn to the shape of its use.

Theo was in the kitchen cooking, which he did on Tuesdays, sleeves rolled, a dish towel over his shoulder, looking like an advertisement for a life they were not actually living. He was a good man. She wanted to establish that, even in the privacy of her own skull, even with no one to hear the ruling. He was a good man who had made the same deal she had made: comfort in exchange for silence, warmth in exchange for depth, a marriage rated *fair* and monitored at intervals and never, never flagged for review.

"You're late," he said, pleasantly.

"Joint four had a crack." She hung her jacket on the hook. "Fatigue, transverse, forty millimeters. I rated it fair."

"Sounds serious."

"It's not. Caught early, it's nothing. It's only serious if you pretend it isn't there."

He smiled at her over the chopping board, that smile, the one that said *there she goes with the bridge metaphors*, the one that had once been affectionate and had become, by degrees so fine she couldn't date the transition, a way of ending conversations before they started. "Lamb or chickpeas?" he said.

"Either way," she said, and heard it again, those two words, and this time something in her chest shifted, a load redistributing, a bearing taking weight it wasn't designed for.

They ate at the table with the radio on low, which was their version of conversation, a third voice in the room so the two of them didn't have to produce one. He told her about his day—planning permission, a client, a colleague's retirement—and she listened the way she listened to traffic over a deck, registering load, weight, rhythm, none of the content. She caught herself doing it and made herself stop and actually listen, and the effort of it was so enormous and so sad that she put down her fork.

"Theo."

"Mm?"

"I have to go through my mother's house. The lawyer sent the letter. Probate's done, and the estate agent wants it on the market by the end of the month. There's a deadline."

He looked up. She watched him decide, in real time, how to respond—watched him choose between the response that would open the subject and the response that would close it, and she knew which one he'd pick, she could have rated his probability the way she rated steel, and he picked it, of course he picked it, because they had built him this way too, the two of them together, joint by joint, year by year.

"You could just sign remotely," he said. "Get a cleaner, get a house clearance company, sign the papers by post. Why put yourself through it? You haven't been back there in fifteen years."

Fifteen years. Since the funeral. Since she had stood in that house with its curtains drawn and its smell of her mother's life—camphor and black tea and the lavender soap she'd bought in bulk from a catalog—and had closed the door on it with a firmness she had told herself was grief and knew, even then, in the honest room of herself she rarely entered, was something closer to fear.

"No," Vesper said. "I should go. I should do it myself."

He looked at her for a moment—a fraction longer than usual, a fraction too long—and then he went back to his plate, and the conversation closed like a joint in cold weather, metal sliding over metal, the gap sealing up as if it had never been open, as if the structure had never needed the gap at all.

* * *

That night she lay awake in the dark beside him, listening to his breathing, and she thought about the crack in joint four. She thought about how the dye had bled into it and stopped, clean-edged, and how the whole art of her profession was knowing that the crack was not the failure—the crack was the *report*. The crack was the structure telling you the truth about itself, if you were willing to get down on your knees and look.

Her mother had died fifteen years ago with a house full of drawers she had never opened. Vesper had signed the inventory at the time—she remembered it, the solicitor's list, *contents of the house: miscellaneous*—and had let it all go into storage, or the skip, or wherever the unexamined contents of a life go when the person who might have asked about them can't bear to ask.

She had never gone back. She had rated the whole structure *fair* and walked away.

In the morning there would be the letter from the solicitor on the hall table, the one that had come three days ago and that she had propped against the fruit bowl like something mildly radioactive, the one with the deadline in it, the one she had not yet told Theo she'd already decided to answer in person. In the morning she would open it. Tonight, in the dark, with her husband's breathing beside her and the fatigue crack running quietly through the steel of everything, she lay still and felt the gap between what her life was and what it was supposed to be, and for the first time in years she did not write the number down.

It was larger than tolerance. She had known that for a long time.

The house, she thought, into the dark. I'll start with the house.
