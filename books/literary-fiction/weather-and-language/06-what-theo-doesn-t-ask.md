## 6. What Theo Doesn't Ask

The letters had been in the house for three days now, sitting in the canvas tote Vesper used for groceries, because she hadn't been able to decide where else they belonged. Not on the bookshelf, among Theo's engineering monographs and her own paperbacks with their cracked spines. Not in the study, where the desk drawer stuck and he would eventually go looking for batteries or a charging cable and find them. Not under the bed, which was where she'd kept the box for the first two nights, on her side, close enough that when she woke at three a.m. she could put her hand flat on the cardboard and feel—nothing, of course, just cardboard, just cool air—but feel it anyway, the way you put your hand on a railing not because the railing needs you but because you do.

She carried them around the house like a diagnosis she hadn't yet told anyone about.

Theo noticed things. That was the strange part, the part that made the silence between them so much stranger. He was an observant man in the way engineers are observant: he registered loads, deflections, anomalies. He had noticed within a day that something was different about her—not what, but that. He watched her across the kitchen island with the particular patience of a man waiting for a reading to stabilize.

"You've got that look," he said Tuesday morning, standing at the coffee machine in his socks. "Like you're carrying something."

"I found some papers at Mum's," Vesper said. "Letters."

"Ah." He nodded, satisfied, and took his mug to the table, where his laptop was already open, already glowing with someone else's priorities. "From the estate stuff? Solicitor will want those itemized, probably. Probate can be funny about documents."

"They're personal letters. To someone in Nigeria."

"Mm." He was already scrolling. His thumb moved down the trackpad with the small repetitive motion of a man petting a dog that wasn't there. "Well, hang on to them. Don't bin anything until the sale's done."

That was the whole conversation. Forty seconds. She stood by the counter holding her tea and understood that she had offered him a door and he had walked past it without seeing it, not out of cruelty—he was not cruel; cruelty would at least have required him to turn his head—but out of a fluency so complete in the language of their marriage that he no longer heard its silences as anything but normal acoustics.

* * *

She spent Wednesday evening with the letters again, reading them in order this time, 1974 forward, building the chronology the way she'd build a load path: trace every force from its origin to the ground. Ngozi at twenty-two, writing to Ada Okonkwo, Flat 4, 11 Akinwunmi Street, Yaba, Lagos. The early letters were all jokes and longing—a girl describing English rain as if it were a personal insult, asking after mutual friends by nicknames Vesper didn't know, sending love to somebody called Auntie Risi, complaining that there was no decent pepper anywhere in Sheffield and that she had walked forty minutes to find a shop that sold plantains and they were black. *Black, Ada. Not ripe-black. Spoiled-black. I nearly cried in the street.*

Vesper laughed out loud, alone in the living room, and then the laugh caught on something and turned into the other thing, and she sat with her hand over her mouth while the radiator ticked.

Her mother had been funny. Her mother, who in Vesper's memory existed mostly as weather—present everywhere, graspable nowhere, warm some days and impenetrable others, a climate rather than a person—had once been a girl who wrote *I nearly cried in the street* about plantains and meant it completely.

By eleven she had reached 1979 and stopped, deliberately, the way you stop drilling before you break through into a void. There was a letter in the stack from March of that year with a heavier weight to it; she could tell from how the pages had been handled, creased and re-creased, read so many times the ink had begun to ghost through. She put it back in the tote. Some loads you have to stage. You don't bring the whole crane to bear at once.

* * *

Thursday she cooked. This was itself an event worth noting in the site diary: Vesper Oyelaran cooked an actual dinner on a weeknight, defrosted the lamb she usually saved for weekends, made the jollof-adjacent rice she'd learned from her mother—the one dish Ngozi had taught her without ever teaching it, absorbed standing on a chair at six years old watching her mother's hands move—and opened the bottle of Rioja Theo liked, the one that cost enough that he always raised his eyebrows slightly, pleased, and said nothing.

He came home at seven-forty instead of seven. She heard the key and felt her body do the thing it always did, the small involuntary straightening, the hostess reflex fifteen years deep.

"Something smells serious," he said, hanging his coat.

"It is, a bit."

He smiled, pleased, oblivious, and went to change out of his work clothes.

They ate at the table, not the sofa, because she'd set the table—cloth napkins, the good ones, another signal flaring over the water that he did not see or chose not to see. He told her about his day first, the way he always did, a recitation: the client call, the contractor who was behind, the young engineer who'd made a units error that could have been catastrophic and wasn't. He was good at telling it. He had a dry humor about work that strangers found charming, that had charmed her once, at a conference in Manchester, fourteen years ago, when he'd described a retaining wall failure as "the building equivalent of a midlife crisis" and she had laughed before she'd even decided to.

She waited until his plate was half empty. Then she put down her fork.

"Theo. I need to tell you about the letters."

"Go on." He was reaching for the wine.

"My mother wrote to someone. For thirty years. A woman named Ada, in Lagos. Dozens of letters—all of them sealed, stamped, addressed. None of them sent. They were behind the wardrobe in her bedroom, in a box, like—" Vesper heard her own voice do something she didn't authorize, wobble slightly on the word *like*, "—like she couldn't throw them away and couldn't send them either. And the early ones are just... her, being young. Being homesick. Being a person I never met. But the later ones—" She stopped. Breathed. Picked up the thread. "The later ones are about me, Theo. About why we left. About my father. And it isn't the story I was told. It isn't the story on my birth certificate. There was a man named Femi, and he was in Lagos, and she was pregnant with me, and—"

His phone rang.

It sat on the table beside his elbow, screen lighting up, buzzing against the wood with that flat insistent vibration, and Vesper watched him glance at it the way you glance at a gauge during a routine inspection—automatic, unweighted—and she saw the name on the screen upside down: *Karlsson*. The client. The Swedish firm. The job.

The job. The one in Rotterdam that had been on the table last spring, the one Theo had turned down—or rather, the one Theo had let die by never discussing it, by letting the deadline pass in a week neither of them had marked, because bringing it to the table would have required the conversation underneath it: What are we? Where is this going? Is this life the one we agreed to, or just the one we accumulated?

"He'll leave it," Theo said, eyes still on the screen.

"He won't. It's nine o'clock in Stockholm. It's his only window."

"Ves—"

"Answer it," she said. "It's fine."

And here was the terrible thing, the thing she would turn over later like a coin worn smooth: he believed her. He looked at her face, saw permission granted, and took it, because taking it was easier than testing whether the permission was real. He picked up the phone and said, "Henrik. Yes—sorry, I'm at dinner, give me one second," and pushed back his chair, and carried his glass and his half-eaten plate toward the study, and closed the door most of the way.

Most of the way. The gap stayed open, four inches of warm kitchen light falling into the dark hallway, and through it she could hear his voice change registers—the lighter, quicker, more alive voice he used with people who excited him. She heard him laugh. She heard him say, "No, I understand the timeline's aggressive. Let me look at the structural scope again and come back to you Monday."

*Let me look at the structural scope again.* As if the job were still live. As if the decision hadn't been made and buried without ceremony, without a marker, without anyone filing the paperwork.

Vesper sat at the table with the cloth napkins and the cooling food and the bottle of Rioja breathing alone in its decanter, and she did the arithmetic she had been refusing to do for years. Not the money arithmetic. The other one. The one where you calculate how long a structure has been carrying loads it was never rated for.

Fifteen years. Longer. Since before the house, maybe since before the wedding—since the first time one of them had said *it's fine* and meant *we'll deal with this never*, and the other had accepted the certification without checking the drawings. Every year since, they had signed off on each other. *Fine. Fine. Fine.* Annual inspections, rubber-stamped, no access required to the interior members. And the interior members—she thought of the word from her own reports, *inaccessible, condition unknown*—the interior members had been corroding quietly the whole time, hidden behind cladding, behind competence, behind two people who were very good at their jobs and illiterate in exactly one language: their own.

Her mother had done the same thing. That was the discovery underneath the discovery, the one that had been pressing on her chest since the wardrobe came away from the wall. Ngozi Oyelaran had built a life on a sealed chamber. Had written thirty years of letters to the truth and mailed none of them, had kept the pressure vessel intact by never opening the valve, and had died with the whole archive intact behind a wardrobe—sound, preserved, and utterly useless, because a truth that is never transmitted carries no load. It just sits there, dead weight, warping the wall around it slowly, invisibly, until one day your daughter pulls the furniture aside to paint and finds the whole hidden architecture of your life.

Vesper looked at the gap in the study door, at the slice of Theo visible in it—his profile lit by the laptop, hand moving as he talked, animated, engaged, more present to a man in Stockholm than he had been to her at this table five minutes ago—and she felt something shift in her chest that was not anger. Anger would have been simpler. Anger has a direction. This was colder and more useful: it was clarity. The specific, professional clarity of an inspector who has finally gotten inside a structure everyone assumed was sound and seen the section loss for herself.

He came back eight minutes later, apologetic, rubbing the back of his neck. "Sorry. Sorry. Henrik, he's—look, it doesn't matter. What were you saying? Your mum's letters?"

And that was the question. That was the whole question, laid out on the table between the cloth napkins like an exhibit: *what were you saying?* Because he genuinely didn't know. He had missed it—not the words, he'd caught the words, Lagos, father, birth certificate—but the weight of them. He had been standing next to a controlled demolition and heard only a phone ring.

"You were saying something about your dad?" he prompted, sitting down, refilling his glass, doing the work of re-entry, and she saw that he was even trying, in his way, and that his way was not going to be enough, and that knowing both of those things at once was its own kind of grief.

"No," Vesper said. "It keeps."

She ate. She was surprised by how steadily she ate, how good the rice tasted, how calm her hands were. Somewhere in the middle of the meal Theo started telling her about the Rotterdam scope again, testing the water, watching her face, and she realized he was doing it gently—that the phone call had reminded him the job existed, that some part of him was now circling the old unfinished conversation the way you circle a crack you've finally noticed in a wall you walk past every day.

"We could talk about it," he said carefully. "Rotterdam. If you wanted. I know I dropped it badly last year. I should have—"

"Monday," she said. "Let's talk Monday."

He relaxed visibly, gratefully, and she hated herself a little for offering him the mercy and knew she'd offer it again, because that was the architecture: they were both load-bearing walls in this house, and you don't pull one out without the ceiling coming down, and she needed the ceiling to stay up for approximately ten more days.

Because here was what she had decided, somewhere between the second glass of wine and the moment she heard herself say *it keeps* and recognized her mother's entire life in the phrase: she was going to Lagos.

Not asked. Not discussed. Not run past him over dinner with cloth napkins, where the sentence would die of exposure the way every important sentence had died in this house for fifteen years. She was going to carry the box onto a plane and put it in the overhead locker and fly eleven hours to a city that smelled, according to her mother's letters, of dust and diesel and frying plantains, and she was going to stand at a door on Akinwunmi Street or wherever Ada Okonkwo had ended up, eighty-something years old or dead, address lapsed or not, and she was going to knock.

Her mother had written the truth for thirty years and sent nothing. Vesper could at least manage delivery.

* * *

After dinner she washed up while Theo took his usual place on the sofa, laptop open, feet on the ottoman, the television murmuring something neither of them watched. She dried the good plates and put them away and stood for a moment with her hands on the counter, looking at the dark window over the sink, at her own reflection superimposed on the garden.

Then she went upstairs, took the tote from the wardrobe floor—her wardrobe, the irony of that not lost on her—and brought it back down, and set it by the front door, in plain sight, where Theo would see it in the morning and ask, and she would say, *I'm going through Mum's things properly, at the house,* which would be true, technically, the way a certified report is true right up until the point where it matters.

In the notebook she'd bought at the petrol station—the inventory notebook, the ledger of unsaid things—she wrote a new line beneath the others:

*Rotterdam. The child. Fifteen years of fine. My father's name is Femi.*

She looked at the list for a long time. Then, underneath, in smaller handwriting:

*Lagos. Friday.*

Outside, the wind had come up, moving through the big sycamore at the end of the garden with a low continuous rushing sound, and Vesper stood at the window listening to it and thinking about structures, about how the ones that fail are almost never the ones groaning loudest—they're the quiet ones, the certified ones, the ones everyone drove over without a second thought, right up until the morning there's a gap where the deck used to be.

Tomorrow she was due on the Kestrel Viaduct. Deck joint inspection, north span. Routine.

She turned off the kitchen light and went up to bed, and lay awake listening to Theo breathe beside her, and did not sleep, and did not wake him, and the wind kept on.
