## 2. Inventory of Silence

The house smelled the way it always had—of lemon polish and cold stone and something older underneath, something like paper left too long in a drawer. Edmund stood in the front hall with her coat still on and her keys still in her fist, listening to the particular silence of a house that had stopped being lived in. It was not an empty silence. It was a held one, the way a breath is held.

She had smiled at forty-one people yesterday. She knew the number because she had counted, the way she counted everything when she needed to keep herself upright: the casseroles (nine), the hymns (four), the times someone said *she's in a better place* (eleven). Forty-one smiles, each one calibrated to end a conversation before it could begin in earnest. The smile was her mother's invention, actually—Margaret Coldharbour had worn it like a brooch at church fêtes and hospital corridors and the funerals of three husbands—and Edmund had inherited it along with the cheekbones and the habit of reading barometers. *You have the Voss eyes,* the woman had said, and Edmund had felt the smile crack down the middle like river ice in March.

She had not answered. She had turned away, found the vicar, asked him about the organ fund. By then her hands were shaking so badly she'd had to hold her teacup in both hands like a child.

Now she set her keys on the hall table, beside the bowl that had held her mother's gloves for as long as Edmund could remember, and began, methodically, to take off her coat.

There was work to do. That was the mercy of it. Grief was unbearable in the abstract but manageable in inventory, and Edmund Coldharbour had been managing things since she was nine years old.

* * *

The study was on the north side of the house, the room that never quite warmed, where her mother had spent every evening of Edmund's childhood behind a door that was never locked but never, somehow, open. The desk sat under the window like a patient animal. On it: a green-shaded lamp, a blotter worn pale in the center, a fountain pen capped and aligned parallel to the desk's edge with a precision that made Edmund's throat tighten.

She started with the desk drawers, because starting there was reasonable, and reasonableness was the rope she meant to hold all day.

The top drawer held stationery, stamps in a tin, a magnifying glass. The second held bank statements going back eleven years, bundled and dated in her mother's small, upright hand. The third drawer stuck. Edmund worked it loose with the patience of long practice—the whole house stuck, swelled, resisted; it was a house that required persuasion—and found the journals.

Not one journal. Dozens. Cloth-bound ledgers, the kind sold to farmers for accounts, spines faded from green to the color of old lichen. She lifted them out two at a time and stacked them on the desk, and the stack kept growing, and she kept reaching back into the drawer, until forty-two of them stood in columns across the blotter like a small city.

Each spine was labeled in the same upright hand. *1961.* *1962.* *1963.*

Edmund opened the earliest one and understood, within a sentence, what she was holding. Not diaries. Weather journals. Her mother had recorded the weather every single day of Edmund's life and decades before it, in a shorthand that was almost liturgical:

*14 Jan 1961 — Overcast, wind SW light. Barometer 29.7 falling. Frost expected.*
*15 Jan 1961 — Hard frost. Clear by noon. Barometer steady.*

Page after page, year after year. Rainfall in millimeters. First snowfall, last snowfall. The date the swifts arrived over the chimney (noted, every year, with what looked suspiciously like satisfaction). The date the roses opened. It was the most complete record of anything Edmund had ever seen, and it told her nothing about her mother at all—or so she thought, at first, turning pages with the flat efficiency of an appraiser.

Then she noticed the gaps.

Between December 1974 and the following entry there was nothing—not a skipped day, which would have been forgivable, but a clean severance. The last line of 1974 read *Barometer 28.9 falling fast, storm coming*, and then the next entry began weeks later in the same ink but under different pressure, the letters pressed deeper into the paper, as though the hand writing them had changed its mind about how much of itself to commit. Edmund ran her thumb over the page. She was not a fanciful woman. But the difference was physical, tactile, undeniable—the way a lie feels different from the truth even when both are written in the same alphabet.

She checked. 1974–75: gap, change of pressure. 1980: gap. 1988: gap, and this time the handwriting itself shifted subtly, the upright letters leaning a degree or two eastward, as if blown. Each time the entries resumed, they resumed with weather only—no reference to the absence, no apology, no explanation. As if the missing weeks had been cut out of the world and the world had agreed not to mention it.

Edmund sat back in her mother's chair, which was cold, and looked at the window, where rain had begun without her noticing. Falling pressure before every rupture. She didn't yet know what ruptures; she only knew the pattern the way you know a scar's shape without knowing the accident. Her mother had written down the sky every day for half a century, and the sky, apparently, had known things coming.

She wrote *journals, 42, condition good* in the catalog notebook she'd bought that morning—a new notebook, deliberately, because using one of her mother's would have felt like eavesdropping—and moved on.

* * *

The postcards were in the wardrobe in the blue bedroom, the room that had been Edmund's until she left for university and had been kept, ever since, exactly as she'd left it: the faded curtains printed with sailing ships, the single bed, the shelf of books she'd outgrown. Her mother had preserved the room the way you preserve a specimen. Edmund had always found it touching. Standing in it now, she found it strange.

The postcards were in a hatbox on the wardrobe's high shelf, wrapped in a silk scarf Edmund didn't recognize. She unwound the scarf and lifted out a brick of postcards bound with string—forty, fifty, perhaps more—all of them sealed in envelopes, none of them addressed, none of them stamped. Unsent. Every one.

She turned them over in her hands. Picture side down, message side hidden. Some were old enough that the card stock had yellowed to ivory; the images on the backs, visible through thin envelopes, showed seaside towns, cathedrals, a harbor in fog. Postcards from places Margaret Coldharbour, as far as Edmund knew, had never gone.

Why keep unsent postcards? People drafted letters they never mailed—Edmund herself had drafts in a drawer somewhere, apologies and accusations composed at midnight and abandoned by morning. But drafts got thrown away or forgotten. These had been kept. Bound. Wrapped in silk. Hidden in a child's empty room, of all places, where no visitor would look and where her mother would pass them every time she came to dust a shelf that needed no dusting.

Edmund weighed the box in her hands. She did not steam them open. Not today. Today she wrote in the catalog: *postcards, approx. 50, sealed, unsent, provenance unknown.* And beneath that, after a moment, in smaller letters she immediately regretted: *to whom?*

* * *

It was nearly dark when she found the photograph, and she almost didn't find it at all. It had been slipped inside the cover of the 1953 journal, flat against the cardboard, as if it had lived there for years—as if her mother had used it as a bookmark and then, at some point, simply stopped opening the book.

Two girls stood on a gravel path in front of this house, when the house was younger and its paint unblistered. Summer light. A border of hollyhocks, taller than the girls themselves. The girl on the left was perhaps ten, dark-haired, squinting into the sun with the uncomplicated confidence of a child who has never once been told to be careful. Her arm was slung around the shoulders of the smaller girl beside her, who was maybe seven, fair, solemn, holding a doll by one arm the way children hold things they've been told to hold for the camera.

The smaller girl's face had been gouged away.

Not scratched—gouged. Something sharp, driven deep into the emulsion, again and again, until the paper itself was torn through in places and the face was a ragged oval of white. The violence of it was shocking in the quiet study. Whoever had done it had done it slowly, thoroughly, with a hatred or a grief that had taken its time. And then had kept the photograph. Had kept it for seventy years, tucked inside a book of weather, face-down against the record of ordinary skies.

Edmund held it under the lamp. The gouged face was roughly where a seven-year-old's face would be. The surviving girl, the confident one, stared out at her across the ruined image, and Edmund felt—irrationally, unpleasantly—that the girl was staring *at* her, that the squint was actually a look of recognition traveling seventy years to arrive at precisely this moment, in precisely this chair.

She turned the photograph over. On the back, in pencil, in a hand younger than the upright hand of the journals—looser, rounder, a child's hand learning cursive—two words:

*M. and E.*

Margaret. And E. Edmund set the photograph down carefully, aligning its edge with the desk's edge, because her hands wanted to shake and she would not permit it. There had been no E in the family. Her mother had been an only child; her grandparents dead before Edmund's birth; the family tree, as presented throughout Edmund's childhood, was a trunk with a single branch. M. and E. Two girls. One erased.

* * *

She should have stopped there. Any sensible person would have stopped, poured a drink, gone to bed, let the archive keep its secrets until morning. But the catalog notebook was nearly full of entries and the dread had begun to feel like momentum, and momentum, Edmund had learned, was the only reliable substitute for courage.

The birth certificate was in the fireproof box under the floorboards of the linen cupboard—the same floorboard her mother had lifted, without explanation, every November, in some private rite Edmund had witnessed twice as a child and never been invited to understand. The box yielded passports, deeds, the deeds' predecessors, a marriage certificate, and, in a stiff brown envelope, Edmund's own birth certificate.

She knew its contents. Or she believed she did. She had seen it once, at eighteen, applying for her first passport: *Edmund Coldharbour, born [date], mother Margaret Coldharbour, née—* and here was the thing, here was the hinge on which the whole evening now swung—née *what?* She had read past it at eighteen the way one reads past any adult's history. She could not remember. She had never thought to wonder.

She slid the certificate out and unfolded it under the lamp.

Her name was there: Edmund. Her birthdate. Her mother's name. All as remembered. But in the space where her mother's maiden name belonged, where the document recorded the lineage the state required, the clerk's faded type spelled out a word Edmund had never heard spoken in her life, a word that meant nothing and therefore, suddenly, might mean anything:

*Voss.*

She read it three times. The rain ticked against the study window. Somewhere in the walls the house settled, the way old houses do at night, like a sleeper turning over.

*Voss.* Not Coldharbour. Not any name attached to any relative Edmund had ever been shown or told of. Her mother had carried another name into this marriage, this house, this life of barometers and silences, and had buried it so completely that even the daughter who inherited everything else had never once heard it aloud.

Except once. Yesterday. In a reception hall smelling of lilies, a stranger with kind, terrible eyes had leaned close and said: *You have the Voss eyes.*

Edmund closed the catalog notebook. Outside, the barometer in the hall—her mother's barometer, brass-cased, polished every Sunday—had begun, she was almost certain, to fall.
