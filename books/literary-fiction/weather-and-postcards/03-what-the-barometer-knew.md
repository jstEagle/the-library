## 3. What the Barometer Knew

The journals lived on the third shelf of the study closet, forty-one of them, bound in oilcloth and stacked spine-out like bricks in a wall someone had built to keep something out—or in. Edmund carried them down four at a time and laid them across the dining table until the mahogany disappeared beneath decades. The earliest was dated 1954, the year her mother married; the last had been abandoned mid-sentence eleven days before she died.

Edmund made coffee she didn't drink and opened the first volume to its first page.

*14 March 1954. Wind WSW, light. Barometer 30.11 and steady. A good day for hanging curtains.*

That was all. No mention of a wedding, though Edmund knew from the parish register that her parents had married on the twelfth. No mention of a husband, a house, a life beginning. Just wind direction, pressure, and curtains—as if the weather were the only event worth recording, or as if it were a language and everything else was translation.

She turned pages. The entries came daily at first, then in clusters, then with gaps that widened like cracks in ice. Her mother's handwriting was small and upright, the letters of a woman who had been taught penmanship by someone strict: *Rain by evening. Barometer falling since noon. 29.62.* Sometimes a single sentence more—*The lilacs are early this year*—and sometimes nothing but numbers, columns of them, marching down the margin like soldiers.

By the fourth cup of untouched coffee, Edmund had developed a system. She flagged the gaps with slips of paper, one color for short absences, another for long ones. By dusk the book lay bristling like a hedgehog, and a pattern had begun to assert itself—not in the weather, which obeyed no pattern she could find, but in the silences between the entries.

The gaps were not random. They came every three to five years, and they lasted weeks. Sometimes months.

And when the writing resumed, it was not quite the same.

* * *

She noticed it first in the 1961 volume. The entries through April were her mother's hand—light, quick, the ascenders of the *t*'s clipped short. Then nothing for fifty-one days. When the record picked up again in June, the same alphabet appeared, but pressed harder into the page, the strokes dug deep enough to emboss the paper's reverse. The *t*'s were crossed with fury. The *y*'s descended like hooks.

Edmund held the page to the window light. She ran her fingertip over the grooves. Whoever had written this had been angry, or afraid, or both—and had chosen, consciously or not, to say so through the pressure of a nib rather than through words.

The words themselves said nothing at all.

*3 June 1961. Overcast. Barometer 29.40, falling. Rain expected within the day.*

But the barometric readings told their own story. Edmund copied them onto graph paper she found in the desk drawer, plotting pressure against date, month after month, year after year, the way she had once plotted sales figures for a company that no longer existed. The line rose and fell in natural rhythms—until it didn't. Before each gap, before every one of those abrupt stoppings, the line dove. Not gently, the way pressure falls before ordinary rain, but steeply, sustainedly, the way it falls before something structural. Before a storm that rearranges the coastline.

She checked the dates against what she knew of family history, thin as that knowledge was. The 1961 gap began in April. In May of 1961, her grandfather Voss—no, Coldharbour, the name had always been Coldharbour—had died. She remembered the stone in the churchyard because as a child she'd traced the worn letters with her finger while her mother stood looking at the middle distance.

The next gap: autumn 1966. Resumption in February 1967, heavy-handed again, then gradually easing back toward her mother's lighter script over the following months. Edmund had no event to match it to. But the pressure line fell through October and November like a stairway going down into the dark.

Another gap, 1971. Another plunge beforehand.

She sat back. The lamp hummed. Outside, the October evening pressed against the windows, and the house made its small settling sounds, the sounds of a body shifting in sleep.

"You recorded storms," Edmund said aloud to the empty room. "You just never said what kind."

* * *

She worked through the night. It was not diligence; it was the particular compulsion of a person who has heard a floorboard creak overhead and cannot go to bed until she knows whether the house is empty. The dead do not knock. But they leave records. Her mother had left forty-one of them, and somewhere inside the columns of pressure readings and wind directions, something was trying to be said.

At two in the morning she found the entry that stopped her cold.

It was in the 1974 volume, dated the ninth of September. The handwriting was her mother's—the true hand, light and quick—but the sentence beneath the weather was not weather at all:

*Barometer 28.90 and falling fast. She will come again Thursday. I will not answer the door.*

Edmund read it six times. *She.* Not a name. Not *Vera*, not *cousin*, not any word that would have meant anything to a stranger flipping through these pages—which was perhaps the point. The journals were written to be read by no one, or by someone who already knew. A diary kept in code is not a diary; it is a letter waiting for its recipient to be born.

Edmund flipped forward. The ninth of September 1974 was followed by entries on the tenth, eleventh, twelfth—all weather, all in the light hand, all meticulously neutral. Then nothing for three weeks. When the record resumed, the hand was the heavy one, the furious one, and it stayed that way for the better part of a year.

*She will come again Thursday. I will not answer the door.*

Edmund thought of the woman at the funeral reception. Vera Anselm, with her uninvited certainty and her unbearable sentence: *You have the Voss eyes.* Edmund had spent thirty years perfecting a smile that ended conversations, and one stranger had dismantled it in four words. Now here was her mother, decades earlier, standing behind a closed door while someone knocked—a woman who would come again, and again, for years, if the postcard box upstairs was any measure. Forty years of returned mail. Forty years of a door that never opened.

And the barometer falling before every rupture, as if the house itself had known.

* * *

Toward dawn, Edmund went down to the hall where the barometer hung beside the coat rack—an aneroid instrument in a brass case, older than she was, its dial yellowed to the color of weak tea. Her mother had tapped it every morning. Edmund could hear the sound still: the small metallic *tick-tick* of a fingernail against glass, as ritualized as prayer. As a child she had asked what it was for, and her mother had said, "To know what's coming," and had said nothing further, because saying nothing further was the family art.

Edmund took the instrument down. It was heavier than it looked. She turned it over, looking for the adjustment screw, and her thumb caught on a seam in the brass casing that shouldn't have been there—a hairline join, almost invisible, running around the rim.

She did not open it. Not yet. Something in her understood that once she started opening things in this house, she would not be able to stop, and there were still thirty-odd volumes on the dining table, and a birth certificate in her handbag bearing a name she did not recognize, and a photograph upstairs with a face gouged out of it, and a woman named Vera Anselm who knocked twice.

Instead she carried the barometer to the table and set it among the journals like a witness called at last to testify.

Then she went back to 1977, the year of Edmund's own birth—or the year of the birth of whoever the certificate belonged to—and read.

The entries that spring were normal. Light hand, steady pressures, the usual liturgy of wind and rain. On the twenty-second of May: *Barometer 30.05, rising. Fair.* On the twenty-third: nothing. On the twenty-fourth: nothing. The gap that followed lasted four months—the longest yet—and when the writing resumed in late September, it was the heavy hand, and it remained the heavy hand, without relief, for two full years. Page after page of gouged letters. *Barometer falling. Rain by morning. Barometer falling.* As if the person holding the pen were pressing hard enough to hold something else down.

Edmund looked up at the gray light gathering in the windows. Her hands were shaking, and she watched them shake with a strange detachment, as though they belonged to the scratched-out girl in the photograph.

Her whole life, she had believed her mother's silences were temperament—coldness, reserve, the natural climate of a certain kind of woman. Now the silences had dates. They had durations. They had barometric precursors, falling pressure like a held breath before every one of them. Her mother had not been silent by nature. She had been silent by decision, repeatedly, at intervals, and each time the silence set in, her handwriting changed as though a different woman had picked up the pen.

Or as though the same woman had picked it up carrying something too heavy to write lightly.

Edmund gathered the flagged volumes into chronological order, squared their edges, and sat before them as the sun came up over the wet garden. Forty-one books. Forty years of weather. And threaded through them, invisible until now, a second record: the record of a door that would not open, a name that would not be spoken, a pressure that fell and fell before every act of erasure.

On the last page of the final volume, dated eleven days before the funeral, her mother had written only this, in the light hand, faint as breath:

*Barometer uncertain. I can no longer predict.*

Edmund closed the book. Somewhere upstairs, in the brass belly of the barometer, whatever her mother had hidden was waiting to be read—and downstairs, on the porch, as Edmund would discover when she finally went to the door, someone had left a postcard face-down against the mat, addressed in handwriting she almost recognized, to a name the house had spent half a century refusing to say: *M. Voss.*
