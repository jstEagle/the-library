## 8. Storm Front

The storm arrived the way her mother used to say storms did—not all at once, but in the manner of a guest who has been standing on the porch deciding whether to knock. Edmund watched it come from the upstairs window, the same window she'd catalogued three days ago as *sash, painted shut, 1948 hardware*, and she felt the pressure drop in her ears before the first leaf lifted off the lawn. The barometer in the hall had been falling since noon. She had checked it without thinking, the way her mother must have checked it forty thousand times, and when the needle slid past *rain* into territory the dial's printed words didn't bother to name, something in Edmund's chest fell with it.

By six the light had gone the color of old pewter. By seven the wind was walking through the maples like a man searching the house room by room. And at 7:41, with a sound like a door slamming somewhere deep inside the walls, the power died and the Coldharbour house went dark for the first time, Edmund suspected, since her mother's death—and possibly for years before that, if her mother had sat here in the evenings refusing to turn on lamps, preferring the dark she'd been raised in.

Edmund found the candles in the third drawer of the kitchen sideboard, beneath a stack of cloth napkins still creased from a wedding that had happened before she was born. She lit one at the stove and carried it through the rooms, and the flame made the house strange—furniture leaning out of shadow, the photographs on the staircase wall flickering like people about to speak. She caught her own reflection in the hall mirror, a smudge of face above a held candle, and thought: *this is what my mother looked like to herself, some nights. This is who lived here.*

She should have gone to the inn in town. Vera Anselm would be there, or somewhere nearby, waiting, patient as weather, for Edmund to come back with questions. Edmund had spent two days not calling her, not answering the note Vera had left under the wiper of her rental car—*Whenever you're ready. I'm not going anywhere. I never did.*—and the storm felt like a judgment on that silence, the sky doing what Edmund refused to do: break open.

Instead she went to the study.

* * *

The barometer hung where it had always hung, on the study's north wall, its walnut case dark as a coffin lid. Her mother had dusted it weekly, wound it, tapped its glass face twice every morning with one fingernail—a ritual so constant that Edmund had assumed as a child it was simply how clocks worked in this house. *Tap it gently,* her mother would say. *Instruments resent rough handling.*

Edmund took it down. It was heavier than it looked. She turned it over in the candlelight and saw, for the first time in thirty-odd years of living beneath it, that the brass hanging bracket was screwed into the case with screws too new for the rest of the hardware—bright, unworn, recently turned. Her mother had opened this case. Recently. Perhaps recently enough to matter.

The screwdriver was in the kitchen drawer where screwdrivers live in every house, and it took Edmund four tries and a skinned knuckle to work the screws, her hands unsteady from more than the cold. The back panel came away with a sigh of old glue.

Inside, wedged into the space where the mechanism should have had room to breathe, wrapped in a dishtowel gone soft as skin, was half a photograph.

And beneath it, folded into eighths, a letter.

* * *

She set the photograph on the desk first, because her hands wanted the letter and she didn't trust them yet.

It was the other half. There was no question, no possibility of doubt—the torn edge fit the gouged photograph from the box the way a key fits the lock it was cut for. The missing girl stood at the left of the frame now restored to whole: two girls on the porch steps of this very house, summer light, white socks, arms linked at the shoulders in the fierce way that sisters link arms when they have been told to stand still and smile and are determined to make an alliance of it. The girl on the left—Vera, it had to be Vera, Vera with the Voss eyes—was mid-laugh, head tipped toward the other girl, caught in the act of saying something wonderful. And the girl on the right, the one whose face had been gouged away in the copy Edmund had found, the one whose face was whole here—

Her mother. Young. Laughing.

Edmund had seen perhaps four photographs of her mother smiling in her entire life, and none of them like this. This was not the smile that ended conversations. This was the smile that began them. This was a girl who had not yet learned that love was a thing you buried in instrument cases, that the people who knew your true name were the people you had to survive.

The gouge in the duplicate suddenly made a different kind of sense. It wasn't vandalism. It wasn't rage—or it wasn't only rage. Her mother had destroyed the copy but kept the original, hidden behind glass and mercury and falling pressure, kept the way you keep a wound: where no one can see it, where you can press on it yourself.

Two girls, arms linked. One erased by hand, one preserved by hiding. Both, in the end, kept.

Edmund sat down slowly in her mother's chair, which still smelled faintly of lavender and camphor, and unfolded the letter.

* * *

There were eleven beginnings.

She counted them twice, then a third time, running her finger down the pages in the candlelight while the storm threw rain against the study windows like handfuls of gravel. Eleven sheets, eleven attempts, each abandoned after a few lines, each folded with terrible neatness—as if even her failures had to be presentable. They were written on different papers across what must have been years; the ink changed, the handwriting changed, the pressure of the pen changed exactly the way the journal entries had changed, and Edmund understood now that the journals and the letters were the same document, the same woman trying and failing to say the same sentence across four decades.

The earliest was dated 1971, on paper yellowed to the color of weak tea:

*Dear Vera,—I have started this letter nine times and burned eight. The trouble is that everything I have to say to you is either too small (it rained) or too large (I was wrong) and I do not know how to build a bridge out of either.*

It stopped there. Nine times, she'd written. So this was attempt ten, abandoned too.

Another, undated, in harder handwriting, the pen pressed nearly through the page:

*Vera. You will have heard by now what I did and you will hate me and you should. I told them what they wanted to hear because they promised me things they had no right to promise. I was nineteen and I believed that a name could keep me safe. I want you to know that I have paid for it every day since in a currency you would not wish to count.*

Stopped. Folded. Kept.

A later one, gentler, the handwriting looser, older:

*I named her Edmund. You'll understand why when you understand nothing else about me. I could not let the family take another daughter's name and use it as a headstone. If I gave the name to a living child, then Edmunde stays alive, doesn't she? Tell me I'm wrong. You were always better at telling me I was wrong.*

Edmund put the page down. Her hands were shaking and she let them.

*A Name Borrowed from the Drowned*—that was what Vera had called it, that afternoon on the porch, and Edmund had flinched from the phrase as from a slap. But here it was in her mother's own hand, the arithmetic of it laid bare: a dead child's name, transferred to a living one, a memorial disguised as a christening. *I could not let the family take another daughter's name and use it as a headstone.* Except that was precisely what she had done. She had simply done it lovingly, which is to say she had done it completely.

And yet—Edmund read the line again—*tell me I'm wrong.* Even in this, even in the founding fiction of Edmund's own identity, her mother had been asking. Had been writing letters to the one person alive who might have said: *you're wrong, Marta, bring her to me, let her be someone new.*

Eleven beginnings. None finished. The last was recent—the paper bright, the ink the same brand Edmund had found in the desk drawer, the handwriting thinned and trembled:

*Vera,—Three weeks, the doctor says, though doctors say three weeks the way weathermen say clearing. I am writing to tell you—*

That was all. That was where she had stopped, three weeks before she died, with the sentence open like a door.

*To tell you—*

To tell her what? Edmund held the page to the candle. To tell her she was sorry. To tell her to come home. To tell her the truth about the will, about the clause, about the cage she'd built around her daughter's inheritance. To tell her *come quickly, I am frightened, I have been frightened for fifty years and you are the only person who ever saw me laugh.*

The storm hit the house full-on then, a long rolling blow that made the windows flex and the candle gutter, and in the stuttering light Edmund looked from the letter to the photograph and back, and something broke in her that was not grief, or was grief finally arriving at its correct address.

Her mother had murdered a friendship—that was true, that was the word Vera used and the word the evidence supported. But she had also kept the body. For fifty years she had kept every returned postcard sealed, every failed letter folded, the photograph whole behind the barometer's face, tapping that glass every morning of her life—*tap it gently, instruments resent rough handling*—tapping on the case that held the only proof she had ever been loved without conditions, by a girl with the Voss eyes, on a porch in summer, arms linked.

You do not hide a thing that carefully unless you mean to keep it forever. You do not keep a thing forever unless you cannot bear to lose it. And you cannot bear to lose a thing unless—

Unless you meant, someday, to give it back.

* * *

Edmund worked until the candle was a thumb's width from drowning in its own wax. She read all eleven beginnings twice. She matched them by ink and paper into their probable decades and laid them in order across the desk like a timeline, like a weather record, like the falling-pressure chart of a life. Then she took a twelfth sheet of her mother's stationery from the drawer, uncapped her mother's pen, and wrote at the top of the page, in handwriting that was hers and not her mother's and might, she thought, belong to both of them:

*Dear Vera,—*

She stared at the dash for a long time. Rain ran down the black windows. Somewhere below the floorboards the house ticked and settled, and the barometer lay open on the desk between the photograph and the letter, its needle pinned hard against the stop, pointing into the unnamed country past *storm*.

She wrote: *My mother never finished her letters to you. I have eleven of her beginnings and I think—I think she was trying to say what I am about to say badly, so I will say it plainly—*

The candle went out.

Not guttered—went out, snuffed by a breath of wind that had found its way through some seam in the old house, and Edmund sat in the absolute dark with the unfinished sentence wet on the page, her heart going hard, listening to the storm shake the bones of the Coldharbour name around her.

When lightning filled the room, white and total, she saw the photograph on the desk—two girls, arms linked, laughing—and in the next darkness she heard, unmistakably, from the front of the house, over the wind and the rain:

Three knocks. Slow. Deliberate. Twice, then once again.

Someone was at the door.
