## 3. Tea with the Last Speaker

The porch of the Vass house smelled of cedar shingles and old paint, and beneath that, faintly, of dill. Jude stood on the second step for a long moment, the notebooks pressed to their chest like something stolen, though nothing had been stolen. Everything in the attic had belonged, technically, to the estate, and the estate was Jude's to sort. Still, they had crossed the street between the houses the way you cross a border at night, quick and hunched, and when Ilonka Vass opened the door before the second knock finished sounding, Jude felt caught at something.

"You came," Ilonka said. Not surprised. She stepped back to make a doorway of herself—small, wide-hipped, cardigan the color of weak tea—and the smell inside rolled out to meet Jude: dill again, and boiling water, and something sweeter under it, burnt sugar maybe, or the ghost of it in a pan long since scrubbed.

"You said Thursday," Jude said.

"I say many things. People rarely come on the day I say." She turned and walked into the house without checking whether Jude followed, which was its own kind of invitation, or its own kind of test.

The kitchen was the oldest room Jude had ever stood inside that wasn't a museum. Not old the way the Sandoval house was old—old the way a body is old, everything worn to the shape of its use. The linoleum had a path worn pale from the stove to the table. The table itself was covered in oilcloth printed with faded lemons, and on it sat two cups already, and a plate of something dense and golden cut into squares.

"I didn't— I can't stay long," Jude said, which was a lie, and Ilonka looked at them with the mild, terrible patience of a woman who had heard every possible sentence a person could produce to avoid sitting down.

"You are standing in my kitchen with your mother's notebooks," Ilonka said. "Sit."

Jude sat.

The tea was dark and bitter, with something resinous in it, and the pastry crumbled at the first bite into a texture like sweet sand. Jude ate because it was easier than speaking. Ilonka did not eat. She folded her hands on the oilcloth and watched Jude with an attention that was not quite warmth and not quite appraisal—more like the attention of someone reading a letter she'd waited a long time for.

"You have the accent of your mother when she first came," Ilonka said.

Jude set down the cup. "You knew her. Before."

"I knew her before she was your mother, if that is what you mean. Before she was anyone's anything. She came to this street as a girl, with her father's car and her father's face, and she knocked on doors." Ilonka's mouth did something complicated. "Nobody answered hers. So she came to mine."

The radiator in the corner of the kitchen ticked and knocked, a sound Jude had heard all their childhood in the Sandoval house and had never once listened to. Here it sounded like a language too.

"She told you about the notebooks?" Jude asked.

"She told me nothing. She never told me anything. That was the arrangement." Ilonka rose, joints cracking audibly, and went to a sideboard, and from beneath a stack of doilies and a tin of buttons she drew out a cassette recorder, the kind with a rubber bumper and a handle, the kind that had been old when Jude was a child. She set it on the table between them with the care of a priest setting down a relic. "She brought this every Thursday. Eleven years. She would sit where you are sitting and she would say, *Ilonka, how do you say*, and I would say, and she would write."

"Eleven years," Jude said. The number came out strange, hollow. Eleven years was most of Jude's adult life. Eleven years was every Thanksgiving Jude had spent trying to make conversation with a mother who answered in single syllables, every Christmas where Ruth Sandoval sat at the end of the table like a guest at her own party, and all that time—every Thursday, eleven years—she had been here, three houses down, filling notebooks with a script Jude couldn't read.

"You didn't know," Ilonka said. It wasn't a question, but Jude answered it anyway.

"No one knew. My brother thinks she played bridge. My sister thinks she volunteered at the library."

"And what did you think?"

Jude looked at the recorder. The plastic had gone amber with age, and the rubber bumper was cracked, and there was a strip of masking tape on the side with a date in Ruth's small, upright hand: *14 Oct.* No year. As if the years went without saying.

"I thought she was lonely," Jude said. "I thought she was just—quiet. I built her out of quiet. I thought that was the whole house."

Ilonka nodded slowly, as if Jude had said something correct on an exam. She poured more tea without asking, and the steam rose between them, and outside the window the light was doing the late-afternoon thing it did in mill towns in October, going gold and then going away.

"Your mother came to me because I am the last one," Ilonka said. "The last one who speaks it. You understand? Not the last one who remembers it—there are old men in the cemetery who remember a song, a curse, the word for a kind of bread nobody bakes. But speak. Speak and be answered. There is no one else. There has been no one else for six years, since Marta Ferencz died in the spring, and the priest said the mass in Hungarian because he did not know what else to say over her."

She said *it* the way people say the name of a dead child. Jude understood, without being told, that *it* was Kavreni, that the word itself was a kind of grave Ilonka carried.

"How many people used to speak it?" Jude asked.

"On this street? When I was a girl, this whole block. You could stand on the porch and hear it going house to house like weather. The boarding houses, the kitchens, the men coming off the night shift at the mill—the mill was loud all day so at night everyone talked twice as much." Ilonka's eyes went to the window, to the street, where the houses stood with their porches and their dead gardens and their satellite dishes bolted over old carved lintels. "Two hundred families, maybe. My mother counted once. She liked counting things that were going away. She said you should always know the number of a thing before it's gone, so that afterward you can say the number and make people feel it."

"And now it's you."

"And now it is me." She said it without drama, the way you report the weather. "A language is not a treasure, whatever people say. A treasure can be buried and dug up and it is still a treasure. A language is a fire. It needs a mouth every day or it goes out. I have been keeping this fire alone in this kitchen for six years, talking to a tape recorder, and I will tell you what that is like. It is like being the last lit window on a street where everyone else has moved away. You keep the light on, but for whom?"

Jude's throat was tight. They looked down at the notebooks, at the topmost page, where Ruth's handwriting marched in English down one margin and the other script flowed across the page in tall, strange letters, letters with little hats and tails, letters that looked like a fence of winter trees.

"Why didn't she tell us?" The question came out of Jude before they could decide whether to ask it, and it came out with an edge, an accusation aimed at a dead woman, and Jude hated the sound of it and couldn't take it back.

Ilonka didn't flinch. She refilled her own cup, and her hand was steady, and when she spoke her voice had gone quieter, the way voices go when they approach something with a floor that might not hold.

"You are asking the wrong woman. I asked her once, near the end—year nine, maybe. I said, *Ruth, why do you hide this from your children?* And she sat for a long time and then she said—" Ilonka stopped. She looked at the recorder. She pressed a button on it with one finger, and the spools inside turned with a soft hiss, though there was no tape in it, or if there was, she did not play it. "She said, *Because they would ask me why I started.*"

The radiator knocked. Somewhere in the walls a pipe shuddered and went still.

"And why did she start?" Jude asked.

Ilonka's gaze came up and settled on Jude, and it was not unkind, but it was not soft either. It was the gaze of a woman who had spent eleven years being asked questions by a Sandoval and understood, perhaps better than Jude did, what it cost to answer one.

"That," she said, "is not a story for tea. That is a story for a long winter." She folded her hands again. "But I will tell you a smaller thing, because you came when I said Thursday, and you ate my food, and you are holding her notebooks like they might burn you, which means you understand they are not only paper."

She leaned forward.

"Your mother did not come here to take a language. People come to take languages—professors, students, people with grants. They come, they fill their notebooks, they go away, and the language goes into a drawer in a university and dies there in a drawer, nicely alphabetized. Your mother did not do that. Your mother did something I have never seen anyone do. She learned it the way you learn a person. She learned the words for things I was ashamed of. She learned the words for the parts of the boarding houses they tore down. She learned what my mother said to me the last morning in the old house on Delano Street, before the trucks came. She learned that, and she wrote it down, and she read it back to me until I could say it without crying. Do you know what that is worth? To have someone carry the heaviest sentence of your life for you, in your own language, so that you are not the only one in the world who holds it?"

Jude could not speak. The pastry had gone to lead in their stomach. Outside, a car went past, slow, and its headlights swept the ceiling, and Ilonka waited out the light like a woman who had waited out many things.

"So," she said. "Here is what I want to say to you, and then you can go home to your lists and your boxes, because I know about the lists and the boxes, everyone on this street knows about the lists and the boxes, the Sandoval house is being inventoried like a warehouse."

Jude flinched. "The estate—"

"Is an estate. Yes. A word for a house with the people taken out of it." Ilonka waved this away. "Here is what I want. I am eighty-one years old. My heart is—well. The doctors use words like *monitors* and *appointments*, and I do not argue with them, because I have seen what happens to people who argue with that kind of word. I have a fire, and I am the last window with the light on, and I am tired." Her voice did not waver, but something under it did, a vibration, like a glass ringing at a pitch only dogs could hear. "Your mother was going to be the one who carried it forward. She was learning to speak it, not just write it. She was slow—she had a farmer's ear, all consonants—but she was faithful. And then she died, and she died in the middle, and now there is a dictionary with no ending and a fire with one log left."

She put her hand flat on the notebooks, on Jude's hands, on the table, so that for a moment everything was connected—dead woman, living stranger, oilcloth lemons, the amber recorder with its blank spools.

"Be my student," Ilonka said. "My last student. I will teach you what she didn't get to. You will finish her book. Not for her—for the language. For the two hundred families my mother counted. For the bread nobody bakes. I am not asking you to love it. Love comes later or it doesn't. I am asking you to come on Thursdays and put your mouth around the words and not let them die in a drawer."

The kitchen was very quiet. The tea had gone cold in Jude's cup, and they could see their own face dimly in its surface, distorted, doubled.

"I don't know anything about it," Jude said. "I don't even know why she— I don't know what my family did. I don't know what I'm inheriting here. I came to put stickers on furniture and sell a house."

"Yes," Ilonka said. "I know. I was there in 1971. I know exactly what you are inheriting." She said it without heat, and the absence of heat was worse than any accusation would have been. "So you can go home and put your stickers on your furniture and sell the house and go back to wherever you live, and in forty years someone will find a notebook in an attic, the way you did, and they will not even know what language it is, because there will be no one left to ask. Or you can come back on Thursday and I will teach you the first word."

Jude sat with it. The word *obligation* was in the room somewhere, and the word *duty*, and Jude reached for both and found them wrong, too heavy and too legal, words for contracts and not for this—the strange gravitational pull of a small kitchen, an old woman's steady hands, a dead mother's handwriting, the sense of standing at the edge of something enormous that had been going on for years beneath the floorboards of Jude's own life without making a sound.

"What's the first word?" Jude asked.

Ilonka smiled. It changed her whole face, the way weather changes a landscape; for a moment she looked like a girl on a porch on a street where two hundred families talked to each other in the evenings.

"You come back Thursday," she said, "and I will teach you the word for *stay*."

* * *

Jude walked home in the blue dark. The streetlamps came on one at a time as if deciding, each in its turn, to bother. The Sandoval house rose at the end of the block, big and dark and full of boxes, and Jude stood on the sidewalk looking at it for a long time—the porch where they'd smoked their first cigarette, the window of the room that had been theirs, the chimney standing up against the last of the light like a sentence with no verb.

Inside, the answering machine was blinking. Jude pressed play without turning on the lights, and Marcus's voice filled the dark kitchen, brisk and cheerful, the voice of a man who had already made a plan and was simply informing the world of it.

"Jude. It's me. Listen—I'm coming up Saturday, I've got the appraiser lined up and the realtor wants photos by the first. Dana's on board. Let's get this done fast and clean, okay? Mom would've wanted it handled. Call me back."

The machine clicked off. In the silence after, the house ticked and settled, and Jude stood in the dark with Ilonka's pastry crumbs still on their fingers and the notebooks under their arm, and thought: *Saturday.* Three days. Marcus with his clipboard, Dana with her timelines, the whole machinery of the estate grinding forward toward an empty, sold, clean house—and somewhere inside it, in the attic, in the margins of everything, a language with one speaker left, waiting on a Thursday.

Jude turned on the kitchen light, set the notebooks on the counter, and opened the first one to the page where Ruth's handwriting stopped mid-word, mid-sentence, the pen pressed hard into the paper as if she'd been interrupted.

Then they found a pen, and in the margin, in their own hand, wrote a single word they didn't yet know how to say aloud, only how to spell it, the way Ilonka had spelled it slowly for them at the door, letter by letter, like a handhold on a dark stair: the word for Thursday. The word for the day you come back.
