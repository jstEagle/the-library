## 8. Ruth's Margins

The notebooks did not want to be read at night. Jude understood this the way one understands weather—not as fact but as pressure, a low system moving in behind the eyes. Still, after three days of cataloging by daylight and sleeping badly in the twin bed of their adolescence, they carried the box down to the kitchen table after dinner and set it under the hanging lamp like a patient on an examining table.

The house had its own acoustics after dark. The radiators ticked and knocked in the walls, a language of their own—metal contracting, water settling, the old boiler in the basement muttering to itself about the cold. Jude had grown up inside that sound and had never once listened to it. Now it seemed to be the only thing in the house still speaking honestly.

They opened the first notebook.

It was a school composition book, the kind sold in drugstores for ninety-nine cents, its cover gone soft and gray at the edges. RUTH was printed on the tape label in her mother's upright hand—the same hand that had signed permission slips, that had written *Buy milk* on the grocery list magnet, that had filled out the funeral home's forms with a steadiness Jude still couldn't fathom. Inside, the pages were ruled into columns. Down the left side ran words in the unfamiliar script Ilonka had read aloud on the porch—that looping, feathered alphabet that looked like handwriting pretending to be print. Down the right, in smaller English: transliterations, then definitions, then example sentences, some translated, some left standing alone in Kavreni like birds on a wire.

Jude turned pages slowly. The lamp hummed. Somewhere upstairs a pipe groaned and went quiet.

The work itself was meticulous—Ruth had been meticulous her whole life, the woman who balanced the checkbook to the penny, who ironed pillowcases—but it was the margins where she came alive. In the margins she argued. With whom, at first, Jude couldn't tell.

*Not boarding house — HOME,* one note said, underlined twice, beside a definition of the word *doma*, which the column translated as *household, hearth, the place one is obligated to.* The underline dug so hard into the paper it had nearly torn through.

Another margin, several pages later, in smaller script, almost apologetic: *Grandfather would say progress. He'd say those buildings were falling down anyway. He'd say someone had to develop the riverfront or it would have rotted.* And beneath that, in a different pen—blue instead of black, the ink bled differently, dated maybe years later—*He'd say that because he never once asked what the word for neighbor was.*

Jude sat back. The radiator under the window gave a long metallic shudder, like something turning over in its sleep.

They had known, of course, in the way children know things—absorbed without being told, the way you absorb that the attic is off-limits or that certain names aren't mentioned at dinner. Sandoval Development. The firm. Grandfather Emil's name was on a plaque downtown, or had been; Jude hadn't checked in years. The family story was a good story, well-built, load-bearing: Emil Sandoval came off the boat with nothing, worked the mills, bought land cheap when nobody wanted it, built the town back up after the mills died. A self-made man. The kind of man who got his name on plaques.

Nobody in the family had ever used the word *bought out*. Nobody had ever said what happened to the people who were already living in the buildings Emil bought cheap.

Jude poured a finger of whiskey from the bottle in the cabinet above the stove—Ruth's bottle, barely touched, dust on the shoulder—and kept reading.

* * *

By midnight they had spread six notebooks across the table in chronological order, or what looked like chronological order; Ruth had numbered them in the upper corners, 1 through 14, but numbers 3 and 9 were missing from the box, and Jude made a note to check the attic rafters, the crawlspace, the false bottom of the cedar chest. The earliest notebook was dated twenty-two years ago. Jude did the arithmetic twice. Twenty-two years ago, Jude had been nine years old. That was the year of the blue winter coat, the year of the science fair, the year Mother drove them to swim lessons every Saturday morning and waited in the car reading. Reading what? Jude tried to remember and found only the image: her mother in the driver's seat, a paperback or a notebook open on the steering wheel, absorbed, faintly smiling. All those Saturdays. All those hours. She had been here, four blocks away, in this kitchen or in Ilonka Vass's kitchen, writing down how a dying language said the word for bread.

The margins thickened over the years. In the early notebooks they were sparse, technical—*check stress pattern with I.*, *ask about plural of rivers*—the notes of a careful amateur linguist. But somewhere around notebook five the technical notes began to share space with something else, something rawer, written fast, sometimes sideways, sometimes upside down in the corner of the page as if Ruth had needed to hide the sentence even from herself.

*I. says the families got thirty days. Thirty days in February. Where does a family go in thirty days?*

Then, two pages later, in the black pen again, tighter: *Father says Emil paid fair prices. Above market. He has the ledgers to prove it.* And below that, in the blue: *A ledger proves what you paid. It doesn't prove what you took.*

Jude read that line four times. The whiskey had gone warm and useless in the glass. Outside, a truck ground past on the county road, headlights sweeping the ceiling, and in the sweep of light the kitchen looked briefly like a room in someone else's house—a stranger's mother's kitchen, full of a stranger's secrets.

That was the thing Jude kept stumbling over, the thing that made reading feel less like research and more like eavesdropping: none of this matched the woman they'd known. Or rather—it matched her exactly, and that was worse. The precision. The refusal to waste words. The way Ruth could hold two opposing positions in a single silence at the dinner table while Marcus talked about his promotion and Dana about her divorce and Jude about nothing at all. Jude had always read that silence as disapproval, or fatigue, or the simple emptiness of a woman past caring. Now it looked like a woman running an argument in the background of her life, twenty-two years long, and never once letting it surface.

*She defended him,* Jude thought, touching the faded black ink. *And then she convicted him. And then she defended him again.* The margins swung like a pendulum, decade to decade. Notebook seven, mid-2000s: *Emil was a product of his time. You can't judge—* and the sentence simply stops, abandoned mid-line, the pen pressed hard enough to emboss the next page. Notebook eight, a few years later: *Stop saying "his time." I. lived in his time too. She's still living in it.*

In the margin of the page defining *kavreni* itself—the word for the language, which the column traced to an older root meaning *of the same river*—Ruth had written, small and level, the most frightening sentence in all fourteen books:

*This is not a hobby. Decide what it is.*

Below it, much later, different ink: *It's evidence. No. It's penance. No. It's a debt ledger of a different kind. Write faster.*

* * *

Jude woke at dawn in the kitchen chair, neck stiff, one notebook open under their cheek, a page ghost-printed backward on their forearm. The radiator had gone quiet in the night, and the silence felt enormous, institutional, like the silence of a church or a courtroom before proceedings begin.

They made coffee and kept reading, and this time they read the way Ilonka had touched the notebook on the porch—with both hands, carefully, as if the paper might remember.

Because the paper did seem to remember. Water damage warped the bottom third of notebook eleven; tea, maybe, or a burst pipe, and along the tide line of the stain Ruth's handwriting grew cramped and hurried, as if she'd been racing the damp. There were dates now, real dates, cross-referenced: *I.V., rec. 4/12, 47 min., tape #31.* Tapes. There had been tapes—Jude remembered a shoebox of cassettes in the hall closet, labeled in that same upright hand, which they had cataloged yesterday as *audio, misc.* and moved toward the discard pile. They put down the coffee and went and retrieved them. Thirty-one tapes became forty-six. Forty-six hours, minimum, of Ilonka Vass speaking Kavreni into Ruth's recorder. Eleven years of Thursdays, compressed onto magnetic ribbon and hidden in a hall closet between the extension cords and the Christmas lights.

Jude stood in the hallway holding the shoebox and felt something shift in their chest—not grief exactly, grief's cousin, the one that lives next door and comes over unannounced. Their mother had been conducting a second life in plain sight, and the instrument of concealment had been the family's own incuriosity. None of them had ever asked what was in the closet. None of them had ever asked why Thursday evenings belonged to no one. You can hide anything, Jude thought, from people who've agreed not to look. We didn't just permit her secrecy. We subsidized it.

Back at the table, the last readable notebook—number fourteen, its spine broken, its final pages blank except for a shopping list (*lightbulbs, salt, call D. re: furnace*)—held the margins that undid Jude completely.

Beside the entry for *sava*, *to owe*, Ruth had written: *Emil never learned this word. Neither did I, really. I'm learning it now, at sixty-one, from a woman I should have met forty years ago at a block party instead of across a deposition table that my father's money built.*

Deposition table. Jude read it again. *Deposition table.* So there had been testimony once—Ilonka had said as much on the porch, obliquely, her hands flat on her knees. And Ruth had been there, or near it, or downstream of it, close enough to know the shape of the room.

And then, on the very last written page, beside a half-finished entry for a word whose translation Ruth had left blank—just the Kavreni letters, patient, waiting—she had written:

*If anyone finds these: I wasn't ashamed of him. I was ashamed of how easy it was to love him anyway. Those are different things. Don't confuse them. I confused them for thirty years.*

The kettle ticked on the stove, forgotten. The light through the window had gone from gray to a thin, rinsed gold. Jude sat with the notebook open and understood that they were angry—at Ruth, at Emil, at Marcus's smoothness and Dana's efficiency and their own nine-year-old self, happily swimming while all this was being written four blocks away—and that the anger had nowhere solid to stand. Every accusation they raised against their mother, the margins had already raised first, decades earlier, in her own hand, more precisely than Jude ever could. You couldn't indict a woman who had spent twenty-two years indicting herself. You could only sit with her, in the cold kitchen, and finish the argument she'd left open.

*Decide what it is,* she had written. Evidence or penance.

Jude looked at the columns of Kavreni, the blank space after the last unfinished word, and thought: it's both, obviously. It was always both. A confession entered into record is called testimony. A debt you write down is called a promise. Their mother had been building a document that could serve either court—the court of history or the court of her own conscience—and had died, apparently, still unable to choose which filing cabinet it belonged in.

Which meant the choice had been left, like everything else in this house, to whoever opened the boxes.

Jude gathered the notebooks into order, squared their edges, and was reaching for the rubber band when they saw it: tucked inside the back cover of notebook fourteen, a folded sheet of legal paper, yellowed, typed on an actual typewriter. A letterhead. Jude smoothed it open on the table.

It was a copy of a notice of eviction—dated March 1981, addressed to I. Vass and other tenants of the rooming house on Delmore Street—and the name at the bottom of the letterhead, under the words *Sandoval Development Corporation*, was Emil Sandoval's own.

And in the margin of the copy, in Ruth's hand, fresh enough that the ink still looked wet, a single line:

*They're doing it again. Ask I. what the word for witness is.*

Jude sat very still. Under the floorboards, the boiler kicked on, and the radiators began, one by one, up through the house, to knock.
