## 1. The Inventory

Three days after the funeral, I woke in my father's house to the sound of gulls arguing over something dead on the beach, and I thought: *I should make a list.*

This is what grief does to a certain kind of person. It doesn't make you weep or wander the shore clutching your own elbows like a widow in a painting. It makes you administrative. My father was an accountant for forty-one years before he retired to this house on the coast, and it appears he passed the condition down like a gene — along with his eyebrows and his habit of reading the last page of a book first, just to know where he stood.

So: a list. An inventory. Room by room, object by object, because the estate lawyer had used the word *probate* twice and the word *timeline* four times, and because my hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking.

I started with the kitchen, which seemed right. Kitchens are where houses tell the truth about themselves.

*One (1) electric kettle, limescale interior, cord frayed at the plug.* He'd been meaning to replace it. There was probably a note somewhere saying so. My father's notes were legendary in our family — small yellow slips of paper with instructions like *Mara: call the plumber, Tuesday, not Monday, he doesn't come Mondays,* pinned to things at eye level so you couldn't pretend you hadn't seen them. The house was still full of them, actually, though now they read like messages from the beyond. *Don't forget the gutters.* *Bread in freezer.* *Lock the back door — wind.*

He'd known, I think, that the end was coming. Not in a dramatic way. In an accountant's way. He'd balanced his books, filed his affairs, left everything tidy except the one thing he never left tidy, which was himself — the man inside all those notes, who remained, after fifty-three years of being his daughter, essentially untranslated.

*Two (2) mugs, mismatched. One reads WORLD'S OKAYEST FISHERMAN.*

That one stopped me. Not because it was funny — it wasn't, particularly; someone at some office party had given it to him decades ago — but because I couldn't remember him ever fishing. I had no memory of my father holding a rod. And yet there was the mug, chipped at the handle, stained brown inside from forty years of tea, which meant it had been *used*, daily, by a man who apparently identified as a fisherman of merely adequate quality.

I wrote it down anyway. *Mug, novelty, condition fair.* The pen moved. That was the point of the pen.

Here is what nobody tells you about cataloging a dead parent's belongings: every object is a small interrogation. You pick up a thing and it asks you a question you can't answer. *Do you know why I'm here? Do you know who gave me to him? Do you know anything at all about the man whose hand held me?*

And the honest answer, three days after the funeral, standing in his kitchen in my funeral dress because I hadn't unpacked anything else, was: no. No, I did not.

* * *

My father Aldous Dunmore was, by every external measure, a simple man. Retired accountant. Widower. Lived alone in a weatherboard house two streets up from the harbor in a town whose name most people mispronounced. He took his tea black. He walked the seawall every morning at seven regardless of weather, which the neighbors mentioned at the funeral with the fond exasperation people reserve for the habits of the dead. He voted, paid his taxes early, and owned exactly two opinions he was willing to share aloud: that modern music was "mostly rhythm," and that the harbor master was a fool, though he never said why.

He was also, I realized as I worked through the kitchen drawers, a stranger.

Drawer three contained: rubber bands (a fistful), batteries (dead, kept anyway — optimism of a very specific accountant variety), a corkscrew, and a photograph I had never seen. It showed a boat. Just a boat, moored, gray water behind it, no people. On the back, in his handwriting: nothing. No date, no caption. A photograph of a boat kept in a junk drawer for God knew how long, for reasons known only to a man who was now past explaining himself.

I put it in the box I'd started labeling MISC., which was becoming less a category than a philosophy.

The living room went faster, because living rooms are mostly furniture, and furniture doesn't ask questions. Sofa, two armchairs, bookshelf. The books were the surprise: not the accounting texts and naval histories I expected, but poetry. Three shelves of it. Neruda, Rilke, a water-stained collection of sea poems with the spine cracked white from rereading. Marginalia in pencil — my father, who annotated nothing, had annotated these. Little checkmarks beside lines. Once, beside a poem about the sea taking something back, a single word: *yes.*

I sat down on the sofa — his sofa, which I now technically owned but could not bring myself to think of as mine — and looked at that word for a long time.

*Yes* to what? Yes, the sea does take things back? Yes, he agreed? Yes, it had happened to him?

Grief has no other grammar, I've decided. You can't conjugate it. So you inventory instead. You count the spoons because you cannot count the years you didn't call. You write *bookshelf, oak, one shelf bowed* because you cannot write *I don't know who you were and now I never will.* The ledger is a lie, but it's a lie with columns, and columns hold you up.

* * *

By afternoon I'd reached the hallway, which contained a coat rack, an umbrella stand, and a framed photograph of my mother that he'd never once taken down in the nineteen years since she died. Her smile in it was the smile she saved for cameras, slightly wider than her real one. Beside it, smaller frames: me at graduation, me at some wedding, me at thirty holding a glass of wine at a restaurant I didn't remember. All photographs of me. None of him. My father had curated his hallway into a museum of other people, and I understood suddenly that this was not modesty. It was strategy. A man who keeps no pictures of himself is a man managing what can be known about him.

I noted the coat rack. I did not note the feeling.

Upstairs: his bedroom, the spare room, the bathroom, and a fourth room at the end of the hall whose door was shut. It had always been shut, even when I was a child visiting in summers. *Storage,* he'd said. *Full of boxes.* I'd believed him the way children believe flat statements from tall men.

The door wasn't locked. That surprised me more than a lock would have. A lock would have said *secret.* An unlocked door just said *private,* which is worse, somehow — it means the person trusted the world not to look.

I looked.

It was a study. A real one — desk, lamp, chair worn shiny at the arms, a window facing the harbor. Not storage. A room he used, clearly, often, and never mentioned to anyone, including the daughter who'd spent a week here every summer of her childhood and had assumed this whole wing was full of Christmas decorations and mouse poison.

On the desk: a blotter, a brass lamp, a jar of pencils sharpened to uniform points, and a ledger. Of course a ledger. This was Aldous Dunmore; the man probably inventoried his dreams.

But it wasn't accounts. I opened it — I'll admit that without excuse; the dead forfeit their privacy by dying, or at least that's what I told myself at the time — and found lists. Lists of words.

Not numbers. Words. Handwritten, dated, in columns going back years. Foreign words, or words from some language I didn't recognize, each with a phonetic spelling beside it and, sometimes, a note in English. *Say it soft — the h is breath only.* *E. says I still flatten the vowels.* *Practice.*

*E.*, whoever that was, had apparently been teaching my father a language. For years. Decades, judging by the dates. The earliest entries were in handwriting I recognized from old tax documents — younger, sharper, the letters leaning forward like they were going somewhere.

I turned pages until the light through the window went the color of weak tea. Words for water. Words for wind — six different ones, distinguished by direction and mood. A word that the margin glossed, in my father's careful hand: *weather = also temper, also fate. One word for all three.*

At the bottom of the last filled page, undated, in the shakier script of his final year:

*Ask him. Before it's too late. Ask him properly.*

Him. Ask whom? Ask what?

I closed the ledger and sat in his chair, which was still warm in the way chairs get warm when they've held one body for twenty years — permanently, structurally warm, warm the way a stone is warm. Through the window I could see the harbor, the masts, the gray-green water doing its eternal indifferent business.

Somewhere out there was a *him*. Someone my father had met regularly enough to fill a ledger with homework, someone whose language he'd tried to learn badly for decades, someone he'd meant to *ask properly* — and none of it, not one word of it, had ever made it into any conversation he'd had with me. Not over Sunday dinners. Not during the nineteen years of phone calls that followed a script so fixed we could have recorded one and played it forever: *How are you. Fine. Weather's turning. Yes. Love you. You too.*

Weather. We talked about weather constantly, my father and I, and it turns out weather was a word that meant fate, and neither of us ever said so.

I went back downstairs and made tea in the limescaled kettle, in the OKAYEST FISHERMAN mug, because using his things felt closer to mourning than crying did. Outside, the gulls had settled whatever dispute they'd been having. The light was going. Tomorrow I would do the garage and the shed, and the day after that the lawyer would call, and life would proceed in its orderly columns.

I added a final entry to the day's inventory, in the notebook I was keeping:

*House, two-story, weatherboard. Contents: largely unknown. Occupant: deceased. Tenant: temporary.*

Because that's what I was, I realized. Not the heir. Not the daughter. A tenant — someone living briefly in a property whose true owner had departed without leaving forwarding instructions. Grief, it turns out, is a lease with no terms you agreed to.

I was rinsing the mug when I heard the footsteps on the porch.

Slow ones. Deliberate. The kind that belong to someone who has climbed these stairs many times before, or who has steeled himself to climb them once. Through the frosted pane of the front door I could see a shape — a man, elderly, hat in his hands.

We weren't expecting anyone. Nobody was expecting anyone. That was rather the whole condition of the house.

I dried my hands, crossed the hall, and opened the door onto the evening and a stranger who looked at me with an expression I couldn't file under anything — and said, in a voice like gravel poured slowly:

"I'm looking for the son of Aldous."
