## 2. Behind the Wardrobe

My mother's bedroom smelled the way it had always smelled, which was a small miracle in a house that had otherwise surrendered to salt and rot: lavender, faintly, and beneath it something like clean wool, like a sweater pulled from a drawer in October. Twenty years of damp had eaten the wallpaper and buckled the floorboards, but the room itself had held its breath and refused to exhale.

I stood in the doorway for a long time with my clipboard against my chest like a shield, doing what I do best, which is nothing, gracefully.

The estate agent — a brisk woman named Colleen who called me "love" as though we'd shared a bunk at summer camp — had given me a system. Three boxes per room: Keep, Sell, Skip. It was a good system. It assumed a person could look at an object and know, instantly, which box it belonged to. Colleen had clearly never met a family like mine, where every object came pre-loaded with twenty years of litigation waiting to happen.

I started with the nightstands because they were small and cowardly of me. A hairbrush with silver hair still in it — I put it down, picked it up, put it down again, and finally wrapped it in a tea towel and set it in the Keep box without letting myself think about why. Reading glasses. A paperback romance with a cracked spine, the heroine swooning on the cover in a way my mother would have called "nonsense" and then read twice. A tin of peppermints gone to sugar-ghosts.

*Keep, keep, skip.* The clipboard filled with tidy columns. This was the work I understood: inventory. Naming things so they couldn't surprise you.

The bed was the hard part. Not making it — though I did that too, out of some reflex older than grief, tucking the hospital corners the way she'd taught me when I was nine and she'd said, *a made bed is a promise you make to tomorrow, Bram, even if tomorrow's a liar*. No, the hard part was that the indent of her head was still in the pillow, or I imagined it was, and either way I stood there like an idiot holding a corner of sheet while the sea did its slow percussion against the seawall below.

I made the bed. I didn't cry. I noted, clinically, that I didn't cry, and filed that under evidence that I was handling things.

* * *

The wardrobe was Dev's problem, technically. He'd claimed the big furniture on the phone — "I'll handle the heavy stuff, Brambo, you do your little lists" — but Dev was in the city doing whatever Dev did, which lately meant not returning calls, and the demolition date wasn't moving for anyone's convenience. So on the second morning I dragged the wardrobe away from the wall myself, expecting dust and maybe a dead mouse, and got neither.

What I got was a panel that didn't sit right.

It was the back panel, lower left, behind where the hanging rail ended. Warped — no, not warped. *Bowed.* Pushed from behind by something flat and patient. I ran my thumb along the edge and felt the give, that soft wrongness wood gets when it's been asked to hold a shape it doesn't want. Any sensible person would have written it off as water damage. The whole house was water damage wearing a roof.

But I'd grown up in this house, and houses like this one teach you their grammar early. I knew the difference between a board that had swelled and a board that had been *fitted*, badly, by someone in a hurry who wanted it to look like swelling.

I got the flathead screwdriver from the kitchen drawer where it had lived since before I was born, next to the corkscrew and the mysterious third tool nobody could identify. I worked the blade into the seam. The panel resisted, then complained, then gave with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

Behind it: a bundle, tied in butcher's twine, wrapped in a flour-sack dish towel gone soft as skin.

Forty-one envelopes.

I sat down on the floor of my mother's bedroom with the bundle in my lap and counted them twice, because counting is another thing I do instead of feeling. Forty-one. Each addressed in her handwriting — that upright, unhurried script she used for grocery lists and sympathy cards alike — and each addressed to the same name.

*Bram Hartwell.*

Not care-of anyone. Not forwarded through Dev or the lawyers or the estate. Just my name, over and over, forty-one times, like a prayer repeated past the point where prayer stops meaning anything.

The postmarks were absent because the stamps were absent because — and this took a moment to land, a cold drop finding its way down my spine — these letters had never been posted. She'd written them and kept them. Written them across — I fanned them out, checked dates — nineteen years. The earliest was dated three months after I left. The latest was dated eleven days before she died.

Eleven days. She'd been writing to me from her deathbed, or near enough, and had still chosen the twine and the towel and the gap behind the wardrobe over the mailbox.

Here is what I had believed, for twenty years, without ever saying it aloud even to myself: that my mother's silence was a kind of weather. Something that happened to her. Something atmospheric, impersonal, regrettable. Silence as absence — a room where a voice should have been, empty.

Forty-one envelopes say otherwise. Forty-one envelopes say the room was never empty. It was full, deliberately full, stuffed to the paneling with everything she chose not to send. Silence, it turns out, isn't the absence of speech. It's speech, performed daily, with effort, like a job. Like a vocation. My mother had been fluent in it her whole life and I had mistaken fluency for muteness.

I don't know how long I sat there. Long enough for the light through the east window to move off the bed and onto the floor. Long enough for my legs to go numb under me, which felt appropriate, thematic even.

Then I opened the first one, because I am weak, and because the alternative was putting it back behind the panel and pretending the wardrobe had won.

* * *

The paper was cheap, drugstore stationery, the kind with a faint blue rule that always bled. Her handwriting was younger here — looser, less guarded, the loops of her g's still showing off.

*Bram,*

*I started this letter four times and burned three of them in the sink, so consider this one the survivor. Your father always said I could out-stubborn a mule and he meant it as a compliment, mostly.*

*You're gone now. Dev says I should let you go, that chasing you with letters is just another way of holding on, and maybe he's right, he's usually right in the way that wins arguments. But there are things I need to say to you that I can't say yet, and if I don't write them down somewhere they'll curdle in me. So this is where I'll put them. Not to send. Just to keep them from going bad inside me like fruit in a closed drawer.*

*Someday you'll come back to this house. You'll have to, eventually — everyone comes back to a house eventually, if only to bury someone. And when you do, I want you to find these. I want you to know that I was not silent because I didn't love you. I was silent because—*

And there the first page ended, mid-sentence, the ink pressed hard into the paper on that last word — *because* — as though she'd borne down on it, as though the pen had needed all her weight to carry it that far.

I turned the page over. Blank. I checked the envelope. One page only. She had stopped there, on the cliff-edge of her own sentence, and folded it up, and tied it with the rest.

Because *what*, Mother. Because of what.

I set the letter down on the floorboards with more care than I've ever shown anything, the way you set down something that might go off. Then I looked at the other forty envelopes, fanned across the dusty floor like a losing hand, each one a sentence she'd started and a sentence she'd stopped, two decades of almost.

The tide turned outside. I heard it — that subtle shift in the sound of the water, the inhale after the long exhale — and thought, absurdly, that the house had been keeping these letters the way the sea keeps things: not hiding them, exactly. Holding them. Waiting for the water to drop far enough.

My phone buzzed in my pocket. Dev. Third time today. I watched his name glow on the screen and thought about what he'd said on the drive from the funeral, his hand warm on my shoulder: *Clean it out fast, Brambo. Nothing in that house worth digging through.*

Nothing worth digging through.

I declined the call. Then I gathered up all forty-one letters, stacked them square, retied the twine in a bow the way she would have, and carried them downstairs to the kitchen table, where I sat under the one working bulb and opened the second envelope.

The first line of that one began: *You were sixteen, and I know what you saw.*

I stopped reading.
