## 2. Wardrobe 12

The east wing smelled the way old buildings smell when they know they're dying: dust and rosin and something faintly sweet underneath, like fruit going over in a bowl nobody remembers buying. Zephyrine had been mopping his way through the conservatory for three weeks now, and he had learned its smells the way he had once learned its acoustics — the lemon of the ground-floor corridors, the chalk-and-sweat of the practice rooms, the particular cold iron breath of the stairwell to the basement where Doro kept the boilers grumbling like an old man asked to share his opinions.

But the east wing was different. The east wing smelled like his seventeenth year.

He tried not to think about that. He had gotten quite good at not thinking about things. It was, in fact, the only skill from his conservatory training he still practiced daily.

The demolition notice had gone up on Monday — a laminated sheet on the staff board, cheerful as a birthday invitation: *EAST WING CLOSING FOR RENOVATION. ALL ROOMS TO BE CLEARED BY MONTH'S END.* Ten weeks, Marisol had said, until the wreckers came with their machines and their hard hats and their total indifference to what a room had held. Until then, it fell to whoever was cheapest and least able to refuse.

Which was, apparently, him.

"Prop room," Doro had said that morning, pressing a clipboard into his hands with the tenderness of a man handing over a live grenade. "Everything out. Everything catalogued if you can be bothered, binned if you can't. Mind the wardrobes. They bite."

"They *bite*?"

"Old wood," Doro said, shrugging. "It gets ideas."

So here he was, at nine in the morning, standing in a room the size of a small chapel, surrounded by the accumulated debris of forty years of student productions. A plaster bust of Beethoven with a chipped nose regarded him from atop a stack of folding chairs. A cardboard moon hung from the ceiling by a thread, still painted with someone's careful craters. There were three fake swords, a birch-bark canoe that had never touched water, a taxidermy heron whose glass eye had gone cloudy with age so that it seemed to be looking slightly past you, into some middle distance of heron concerns.

And there were the wardrobes. Twelve of them, lined along the back wall like a jury. Tall, dark, mahogany or something pretending to be, each one carved with the same fussy scrollwork along the top. Someone had numbered them in white paint long ago — the props master's system, presumably — and the numbers had faded unevenly, so that Wardrobe 4 looked like it said *Wardrobe 9* if you squinted, which struck Zephyrine as a metaphor for something but he couldn't say what.

He started with the easy stuff. He carried chairs. He wrapped the Beethoven bust in newspaper and set it aside for Marisol, who would either archive it or stage an intervention on its behalf. He found, behind a rack of costumes, a violin case containing no violin but rather eleven years of lost mittens, which he chose not to think about too closely because grief arrives in strange containers.

By eleven he had worked his way down the line of wardrobes. They were heavier than they looked, full of costumes mostly — cloaks, graduation gowns, one truly alarming dragon costume made of green felt and ambition. He emptied them one by one, sweeping out the mothballs and the occasional desiccated spider, and told himself the tightness in his chest was just dust.

Wardrobe 11 was empty except for a single opera glove, left hand, size small, which he pocketed without deciding to. Some things you keep before you know why.

Then Wardrobe 12.

It stood slightly apart from the others, pushed back against the wall at an angle, as though it had been moved in a hurry once and never straightened. The door didn't open so much as surrender, hinges shrieking, and inside there were no costumes at all — just an odd, hollow emptiness, and a smell of cedar gone tired.

Zephyrine frowned. He put his head partway inside — a foolish thing to do, he'd later reflect; this was exactly how people ended up in local news stories — and reached along the back panel. His fingers found a gap. Not a gap in the wardrobe. A gap *behind* it, between the wardrobe's back and the wall itself, a space maybe eight inches wide, running floor to somewhere above his reach.

Something was wedged in it. Something flat and heavy and soft-cornered.

He hauled the wardrobe away from the wall — it fought him, groaning across the boards like a ship changing its mind about a voyage — and there it was, in the shadow it had been keeping all these years:

A box. Cardboard, gone soft at the edges, furred with grey dust. Tied shut with string in a bow that somebody had tied carefully, the kind of bow you tie when you intend the thing to stay tied.

Zephyrine knelt.

He should have fetched Doro. He knew that even then, in the way you know you shouldn't touch a hot stove — some knowledge lives below thought. A box hidden behind a wardrobe in a condemned wing of a building with more secrets than radiators was not his business. His business was mops. His business was being invisible, and invisibility had rules, and rule one was *don't find things.*

He untied the bow anyway.

Inside: letters. Dozens of them. Envelopes, cream-colored, stacked in loose bundles, some rubber-banded, some simply leaning against each other the way papers lean after years of settling. He lifted the top bundle out and the dust came off it in a soft sigh, and the light from the high window fell across the front of the first envelope, and Zephyrine Oyelaran stopped breathing.

He knew the handwriting.

Of course he knew it. You spend your whole childhood being told your handwriting is a disgrace, your slant is inconsistent, your capital letters are showing off — and then you grow up and see it again, decades later, on the face of an envelope, and every criticism turns out to have been a portrait. The slanted, ambitious capitals. The *y*s that looped too far, like they were trying to escape the line. The ink gone brown at the edges with age.

*Mr. Zephyrine Oyelaran,* the envelope said, in his own seventeen-year-old hand.

Not care-of anyone. Not through any office or department. Just his name, spelled out with the earnest formality of a boy who had been taught that names were important things and must be written properly, especially your own.

His address — his mother's house, the flat above the bakery on Corvus Street, the one with the window that whistled in easterly wind — was written beneath it in smaller letters. He could remember writing it. He could almost feel the pen. He could remember the kitchen table, the good lamp, the way his mother had hovered and then deliberately not hovered, pretending to read her magazine while listening to him breathe.

He did not open it. His hands wouldn't. They held the envelope the way you hold a bird that has flown into your window — gently, uselessly, aware that something is stunned and something is broken and neither of those things is your fault, probably.

There were more beneath it. He counted without wanting to. Fourteen in the top bundle. Two more bundles under that. Thirty-some envelopes, all sealed, all undelivered — no postmarks, no stamps, some without addresses at all, just his name and nothing else, as though the boy who wrote them had run out of certainty halfway through and kept writing anyway.

All in his handwriting.

All addressed to him.

The room did something strange then. It tilted — not physically, the room was fine, the room was a room — but the light went thin and far away, and the smell of rosin sharpened until it was almost a sound, and Zephyrine had the distinct vertiginous sensation of being two ages at once: a man of thirty-one kneeling in dust, and a boy of seventeen sitting at a kitchen table licking an envelope, and the two of them looking at each other across a gap of years with no idea which one of them was the ghost.

*You stopped writing back,* said the boy, from inside the box. That was the accusation in it, though the envelopes said nothing yet. *I wrote and wrote and you stopped writing back.*

"I didn't know," Zephyrine said aloud, and his voice cracked in the empty room, embarrassing him. "I didn't know you'd — I thought you gave up."

The heron stared past him. The cardboard moon swayed very slightly on its thread, though there was no wind.

He sat down properly on the floor, in the dust, in his uniform, and pulled the box into his lap like something rescued from a fire. He turned the envelopes over. On the back of the last one in the first bundle, in smaller writing, squeezed near the flap as if the boy had been afraid to commit it to the front:

*If no one will tell me the truth, I will write it myself until someone has to listen.*

Zephyrine's mouth went dry.

He knew what year those words came from. He knew it the way you know the taste of a food that once made you ill — instantly, bodily, before any part of him could arrange the facts into their safer order. There had been a winter. There had been a sonata. There had been a name on a program that wasn't his, and applause that rolled over him like weather, and Maestra Voss's hand on his shoulder saying *this is how it must be, little Zeph, this is how we protect what matters* — and then there had been silence, the long engineered silence, the silence with a career built on top of it.

He had spent twelve years telling himself he remembered it wrong. Memory was unreliable. Grief edited. He had become very sophisticated about it, actually; he could give a small lecture on the unreliability of memory, with examples, and he had given it to himself many times, usually at three in the morning.

But the boy in the handwriting hadn't been unreliable. The boy had known exactly what happened, and had written it down, and had addressed it forward through time like a message in a bottle thrown at his own future — *you, the man I become, you are my addressee, you are the only one left who might answer.*

Thirty-odd letters. Sealed. Undelivered. Hidden behind a wardrobe by hands he could guess at and refused to.

Somewhere down the corridor, a door banged. Footsteps — Doro's uneven tread, or Teodor's careful one. Zephyrine moved without thinking, fast, animal: he slid the bundles back into the box, retied the bow — his fingers shook and the bow came out crooked, nothing like the careful original — and pushed the box back into the gap behind Wardrobe 12, and swung the wardrobe back against the wall, and stood up, and brushed off his knees, and picked up his mop.

By the time the footsteps arrived he was cleaning the window ledge, humming, invisible, a man with no interior life whatsoever.

"You alive in here?" Doro's voice, from the doorway. "Marisol wants the inventory by Friday. Also there's soup."

"Soup," Zephyrine said. It came out steady. He was almost proud of himself.

"You look pale."

"It's the dust." He smiled the smile — the practiced one, the armor, the smile that ends conversations — and Doro, who missed nothing but respected everything, let it end the conversation.

They ate soup in the basement kitchen an hour later, and Zephyrine laughed at the right moments, and washed his bowl, and was pleasant, and was nowhere at all.

But all evening, as he mopped corridors that had nothing to do with any of it, one sentence followed him, patient as a drip of water behind a wall:

*If no one will tell me the truth, I will write it myself until someone has to listen.*

At midnight, alone in the boiler room's orange light while Doro dozed over his crossword, Zephyrine took off his gloves, flexed his hands — hands that hadn't played anything but scales of silence in twelve years — and understood, with a dread that felt almost like relief, that tomorrow he would go back to the east wing.

Tomorrow he would move the wardrobe again.

Tomorrow he would start reading mail that had been waiting for him since he was seventeen years old — and the first envelope, he already knew, already dreaded, was addressed in a hand he recognized because it was the Maestra's: *To Z., when he comes looking.*

She had known he would come looking. She had left it where only someone clearing the room for demolition would ever find it.

Which meant she had known about the demolition, too.
