## 9. Voss's Version

Ilsa Voss had not descended into the basement of the Meridian Conservatory in eleven years, and the stairs reminded her why. They creaked in the same three places they had creaked when the building was new, as if the staircase itself had never learned to lie gracefully, which was more than could be said for the people who climbed it.

She had dressed for the occasion with the care of a general choosing a uniform. Not the emerald velvet she wore for galas—that was armor for crowds, and this would not be a crowd. Not the gray wool of the lecture hall, which said *I am authority, take notes*. She had chosen instead the soft brown cardigan, the one with the frayed left cuff, the one students called "the grandmother sweater" behind her back and never to her face, because it said *I am harmless, I am old, I am here to listen*. She had learned long ago that the most dangerous thing a powerful woman could wear was the suggestion that she had no power at all.

In her canvas bag she carried a thermos of good coffee—real coffee, not the burnt sludge Doro produced in that ancient percolator, which Ilsa suspected had once been used to strip varnish—and a folder that did not exist, officially, in any archive.

The night kitchen was exactly as she remembered it, which was to say it had gotten worse in ways only love could explain. The kettle was older. The table had acquired more rings, more dents, more of the gentle archaeology of people who ate where they worked. Marisol's archive boxes had colonized one corner like a polite invasion. A radio murmured something with accordions in it.

And there they all were. Her building's secret heart, beating in the dark under her feet for decades while she taught upstairs in the light.

Doro stood at the stove, enormous and unhurried, stirring a pot with the serenity of a man who had made peace with every pot he would ever stir. Marisol sat cross-legged on a chair with a folder open on her knees, reading the way other people breathed. Teodor was at the far end of the table with a tuning fork pressed to his sternum, eyes closed, listening to the building through his own ribs.

And Zephyrine Oyelaran sat with his back to the door, eating soup.

Ilsa stood in the doorway for a moment longer than was strictly necessary, because she had learned that a moment of being watched unawares told you more about a person than an hour of conversation. He sat the way he had always sat, even at seventeen—curved slightly inward, as though his body had never quite agreed to the dimensions of the world. His spoon paused. His shoulders, before he even turned, already knew.

He turned. And there it was—the face she had prepared for and still was not prepared for. Twelve years had planed him down, worn the shine off, left the grain showing. He looked, she thought with a pang she filed away for later inspection, like a piece of furniture that had finally stopped being polished and started being *used*, which was its own kind of beauty.

"Maestra," he said. His voice was careful. His soup steamed between them like a neutral party.

"Mr. Oyelaran," she said, and enjoyed, in a small and shameful way, the flicker that crossed his face at the formality. "I'm told the coffee down here is a war crime. I've come to negotiate a peace treaty."

* * *

Doro, to his credit, did not drop the ladle. He simply looked at her the way he had looked at her for forty years—the look of a man who had watched her arrive as a young firebrand, watched her become an institution, and reserved judgment on both. "There's cups," he said, which from Doro was a state visit.

She poured her own coffee, offered it around, was refused by everyone with varying degrees of grace—Marisol with suspicion, Teodor with a smile that said he could smell the performance, Doro with the flat courtesy of a man guarding a border. Zephyrine said nothing at all.

She sat down across from him. She had chosen the chair deliberately: not the head of the table, never the head of the table down here, but the seat directly opposite him, where she could not be accused of looming and could not be accused of avoiding.

"You've been finding things," she said. "In the east wing."

The kitchen went very quiet. Even the radio seemed to lower its accordions.

"Props," Zephyrine said. "Wardrobes. A great deal of dust."

"Marisol tells me the demolition survey turned up more than dust."

"Marisol tells you a lot of things," Marisol said, not looking up from her folder, in the tone of a woman establishing that her loyalty had limits and the limits had been recently surveyed.

Ilsa smiled at her. "Marisol is the finest archivist this institution has ever employed and I include the ones with doctorates. She also cannot resist a well-placed provocation, which is why we get along."

Something almost like a laugh escaped Marisol and was immediately imprisoned.

Ilsa turned back to Zephyrine. She had rehearsed this. She had rehearsed it in the bath, on the train, in the long gray hour before sleep. She had rehearsed it the way she rehearsed students for competitions—not to make them perfect, but to make them *believable*, because juries did not reward perfection, juries rewarded conviction.

"I'm going to tell you a story," she said. "You're going to want to interrupt it. I'd ask you not to, but you've never once done anything I asked, so I'll simply say: the interruptions will still be available afterward."

His spoon came to rest in the bowl. He folded his hands. It was, she noted with something between pride and grief, exactly the posture of a seventeen-year-old waiting for a verdict.

* * *

"In the spring of your final year," she began, "you wrote to me. More than once. I know because I read them."

She let that land. His face did not move, which was itself an answer.

"You wrote to me because there was a question in the air—the sonata, the prize, the name on the program—and you wanted me to say the true thing out loud. You wanted *me* to say it. Not the board, not the newspapers. Me." She wrapped her hands around the thermos cup. "And here is what you were too young to understand, and what I was too proud to explain, and what I have had twelve years and a great many sleepless nights to get clear in my own mind."

She told him about Cassian's family.

She told it well, because it was largely true. The Veys were not merely wealthy; they were *invested*—patrons of the conservatory for three generations, their name on the recital hall, their money in the scholarship fund that had paid for Zephyrine's own education, a detail she deployed now with surgical gentleness. Cassian's father had built his entire public identity on the boy's genius. And Cassian, poor Cassian, had been a boy who could play anything put in front of him and compose nothing, a performer so gifted at interpretation that he had, in the end, interpreted a sonata into existence that was not his—because the alternative was to walk on stage at the prize concert and admit, in front of everyone who mattered, that the great Cassian Vey was an empty, beautiful instrument.

"You wrote the sonata," Ilsa said. "I have never once, in twelve years, in any room, told anyone otherwise. Go through every interview, every lecture, every obituary I have mentally drafted. I never said Cassian wrote it. I said the piece was 'submitted under his name.' I said the authorship was 'a private matter between students.' I lied the way a doctor lies with a comfortable tone of voice. But I never once told the lie itself."

"Because you didn't have to," Zephyrine said quietly. "Everyone else told it for you."

"Yes," she said. "That is how the best lies work. You don't build them. You just decline to knock them down, and then you stand back and admire the architecture."

She saw Marisol's pen stop moving. She had known Marisol was writing. She let her.

"The letters you wrote me—there were eleven of them, over five months. I kept every one. I read every one. And on the nights I couldn't sleep, which were most nights that year, I answered them. In my head. Whole letters. Pages." Her voice did something she did not authorize it to do, a small crack in the varnish. "I answered you the way I should have answered you, which was to say: *you are right, and I am a coward, and here is why the cowardice is also the only thing standing between you and the Veys' lawyers, who would have eaten you alive, who would have called it plagiarism, who would have produced—*"

She stopped. Breathed. The kitchen waited.

"—who would have produced witnesses," she finished, more quietly. "Cassian, for one. Cassian, who by then had so much to lose he would have sworn anything on any altar. And I could not protect you from that. Do you understand? I was not protecting my reputation, Zephyrine. I was protecting a seventeen-year-old scholarship boy with no family name and no lawyer, from a family that had ruined a rector in Vienna over a smaller slight. I chose the only shield I had. It was me. I made myself the wall, and I let you hate the wall, because a wall that is loved stops being a wall."

Silence. The radiator ticked. Somewhere above them, the building settled its old bones.

It was, she thought, the best performance of her life, and the terrible thing—the thing she would examine at three in the morning for the rest of her natural life—was how much of it was true.

* * *

"And the letters themselves," Zephyrine said. His voice was steady in the way that ice is steady. "The ones in the box behind Wardrobe Twelve. Those are mine. Addressed to me. In my handwriting."

The kitchen temperature dropped by a degree Ilsa could not measure but everyone felt.

"No," she said.

"No?"

"No, Zephyrine. Those are not your letters. I told you—I kept yours. Every one." She reached into the canvas bag and set the folder on the table, but did not open it. "The box behind the wardrobe contained my letters. The ones I wrote to you and never sent. Eleven of them, one for each of yours, because I am a vain, sentimental old woman who could not destroy them and could not send them, and so I hid them in the east wing, in the prop room, behind the wardrobe where we—"

She stopped. She had been about to say *where we rehearsed*, which was true, and where *you used to hide from Cassian during theory week*, which was also true, and both truths together made a door she was not ready to open in front of an audience.

"Where we rehearsed," she finished.

Zephyrine was staring at the folder. "You're telling me the letters I found—"

"Are mine. My handwriting. My confessions. My cowardice, in installments." She laughed, short and dry. "You've spent a week thinking a boy's ghost was writing to you from 1998, and it turns out it was only me. I'm sorry. I've been told I haunt."

It was a joke. It was, in its way, a gift—the gift of absurdity, of the mundane, of a truth small enough to hold. She watched it work on him, watched the vertigo in his eyes begin to settle into something more navigable, and she hated herself with a professionalism that would have impressed her students.

* * *

"Now," she said. "The offer."

Doro made a sound that in a smaller man would have been a scoff.

"The conservatory is creating a position," Ilsa said. "Composition instructor, non-tenure-track, small studio, your own hours. The east wing is coming down, but the west wing is not, and a glass pavilion is not the only thing a donor can fund. I have already spoken to the board. The position exists as of this morning. It exists because I made it exist. And it is yours."

She laid it out with the economy of long practice. The salary, respectable. The students, handpicked—she had three in mind already, one of whom reminded her so violently of Zephyrine at seventeen that she had to sit down after the audition. The dignity of it. The return. *Come back through the front door*, was the shape of what she was saying. *Come back with a title, a studio, a name on a door. Come back the way it should have been all along, and let the past be a detour instead of a verdict.*

"And in exchange," Zephyrine said.

"There is no exchange."

"Maestra."

"There is no *exchange*," she said. "There is a hope. My hope is that a man with a future does not spend his remaining weeks tearing through a past that I have already told you the truth about. My hope is that you will be too busy teaching to be digging. That is a hope, not a term. I am seventy-one years old, I have outlasted three deans and two scandals that never touched me, and I am too old for terms."

Teodor set his tuning fork down on the table with a small clear note, and Ilsa, who did not need to hear Teodor speak to hear him speak, understood that the note meant: *she is lying, or she is not, and only you can tune it.*

* * *

Zephyrine was quiet for a long time. Long enough that Doro, without being asked, refilled his soup. Long enough that the radio moved from accordions to rain sounds, as if the universe had opinions about the mood.

She watched him and she knew—she had always known, this was her curse, she could read a phrase before it was played—what was happening inside him. She was watching a drowning man being offered a rope, and she could see him testing it, and she could see the terrible arithmetic running behind his eyes: *if her story is true, then I was wrong about everything, and I can put it all down, and I can be warm again, and I can stop being the janitor who was once a prodigy and just be a teacher, just be a man, just be—*

And underneath it, fainter, the counter-voice: *if her story is true, why did it take a demolition crew to get it out of her?*

"Say it's all true," he said at last. "Say you were the wall. Say the Veys would have destroyed me." He looked up. "Then why is Cassian the one who disappeared? You protected me from ruin, and the boy you were protecting me *from*—he's the one who's gone. Nobody's heard him play in twelve years. Nobody's heard him do anything. If I was the one being saved, Maestra, who was being buried?"

The question went through her like the tuning fork through the table.

She had prepared for anger. She had prepared for grief, for the courtroom, for the long slow thaw of a man deciding to forgive her. She had not prepared for him to be *precise*, because precision had always been her weapon, and there is a special horror in watching your own gun pointed back at you by someone you taught to shoot.

"Cassian made his own choices," she said, and heard, even as she said it, how thin it was, how it sounded exactly like what it was: the sound of a story with a hole in it, papered over by a woman who had papered over that hole for twelve years and was running out of paper.

"Did he," Zephyrine said. Not a question. A door, opening.

Marisol had stopped pretending to read. Doro had turned off the stove. Even the rain on the radio seemed to be listening.

Ilsa stood, gathered her thermos, her folder, her eleven unsent confessions, her seventy-one years. At the doorway she paused, because she was a professional, and professionals always knew where the exit was and when to use it.

"Think about the position," she said. "The offer doesn't expire. Neither do I, despite the board's hopes." And then, because the sweater had done its work and some part of her meant it, she added, softly: "The coffee down here really is a war crime, Doro. It was a war crime in 1989. Some things in this building are eternal."

She climbed the stairs, past the three creaking steps, up into the light.

Behind her, in the warm dark of the kitchen, she heard Zephyrine say, very quietly, to no one and to everyone:

"I need to find Cassian."

And Ilsa Voss, standing alone on the landing with her folder of lies that were half-true and truths that were half-lies, understood that she had perhaps four days—five, if the trains were bad—before two men she had spent twelve years keeping apart stood in the same room and compared notes.

She had taught composition for fifty years. She knew exactly how a theme sounds when it comes back in the wrong key.
