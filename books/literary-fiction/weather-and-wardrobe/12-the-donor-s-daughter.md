## 12. The Donor's Daughter

She came back on a Thursday, which felt right. Thursdays were the days the conservatory pretended hardest to be impressive—open rehearsals, donor tours, the smell of floor polish laid down thick enough to hide the mildew under it. I had learned to read the building's moods by then, the way farmers read weather, and Thursday was always a day of performance.

I was waxing the banister in the west foyer when her car pulled up—a low silver thing that purred rather than drove—and Odile Ferrand stepped out of it wearing a coat that probably cost more than my annual salary and looked like she'd chosen it specifically so people would think otherwise.

I recognized her before I recognized myself in her.

That was the strange part. Twelve years ago she'd been a girl in the second row of Voss's harmony seminar, always three minutes early, always with a pencil sharpened to a point that could have committed crimes. Now she was forty-something and expensive, but she still walked like someone arriving at an exam she had already bribed her way through.

"Excuse me," she said, not looking at me, looking *through* me at the foyer's marble, assessing its resale value. "Is there someone who can tell me whether Maestra Voss is receiving visitors?"

"She's teaching until four," I said. "But you can wait in the salon."

Her eyes came down to me then, and I watched the arithmetic happen—the uniform, the mop bucket, the face. I watched her decide I was furniture. And then, halfway through deciding it, she stopped.

"Zephyrine Oyelaran," she said.

"Madam Ferrand."

"You're—" She gestured at the bucket with one manicured hand, a gesture that managed to be both delicate and devastating. "You're the janitor."

"I'm the man who waxes the banister," I said. "The mopping is a side project."

Something flickered across her face—not pity, worse than pity: recognition. The look of a person seeing their own reflection in a funhouse mirror and finding it less distorted than expected.

* * *

She did not wait in the salon. She waited near me, which was more alarming.

I retreated to the east corridor, because the east corridor was where I went when I wanted to be left alone—it smelled of dust and old glue and, these last weeks, of endings—and she followed at a respectful distance, the way a cat follows you into a room it has no business in.

"The east wing," she said, stopping at the barricade tape strung across the stairwell. "They're really going to do it."

"In ten weeks."

"A glass pavilion." She said the words as if tasting something imported. "My money."

"Your money?" I kept my voice level. In the weeks since the demolition notice had gone up, I had gotten good at keeping my voice level; it was a skill adjacent to invisibility, part of the same curriculum.

"My foundation's money. I signed the pledge two years ago. A pavilion, they told me. Light, transparency, a new era." She touched the tape with one finger, lightly, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you intended to have put down. "Nobody mentioned that the new era required demolishing the old one."

"There was a wardrobe behind which a box lived for twelve years," I said, before I could stop myself. "The old era keeps its secrets in odd places."

I heard the words leave my mouth and thought: *fool*. But Odile Ferrand did not laugh or look confused. She turned to me slowly, and her face did something I recognized from my own mirror—the careful rearrangement of expression that happens when a person has spent decades making sure no one ever sees them flinch.

"What box," she said.

* * *

We ended up in the basement kitchen, because it was the only room in the building without ears. Doro was down there, feeding the boiler and simultaneously feeding himself—he could do both with one hand each, a domestic virtuosity I never tired of watching—and he took one look at Odile Ferrand's coat, grunted the grunt of a man who has heated water for duchesses and found them no different from plumbers, and poured three coffees instead of two.

Odile sat on the bench by the radiator like she wasn't sure the bench would hold her dignity, and told me about herself in the way certain people do—as though confessing were a form of invoice.

She had studied with Voss from fourteen to twenty-two. She had been, she said, "the other one"—the talented student who was never the *chosen* one. She'd watched Voss build Cassian Vey up and set him out like a lantern, and she'd watched what happened after the sonata scandal, and she had understood, even at nineteen, that the story everyone swallowed had been written by someone with a great deal to lose.

"And you?" she asked me. "What did you understand?"

"That asking questions was how you ended up sweeping floors in another city."

She smiled at that, and it was the smile from chapter one of my acquaintance with her—the smile that ends conversations, that closes doors politely from the inside. I knew that smile intimately. I had invented it, or it had invented me, somewhere around the age of seventeen, and I had worn it for years like a coat against weather.

"Voss wrote to me, you know," Odile said. "After I made my first real money. Twice a year, every year. Warm letters. *Odile, dear, your Chopin was always the truest in the class.* Twenty years of warmth, and every letter asked, in its way, for a donation." She turned her cup on its saucer. "Then this spring the letters changed. Shorter. Colder. And in the last one—" she paused, and I saw her choose her words with the care of a person laying mines—"she wrote that the conservatory was entering a period in which 'certain papers' might surface, and that if they did, I should remember who had kept my name clean all these years."

Doro, by the stove, went very still. He was a large man who moved like a small one when he needed to.

"Certain papers," I repeated.

"You've found something." It wasn't a question. "Behind a wardrobe, I'd guess. That east wing prop room is a mausoleum. Everything Voss ever wanted forgotten went in there eventually—including students." Her eyes came up to mine, and here was the thing about Odile Ferrand: she did not blink when she lied, but she also didn't blink when she told the truth, which made her either very honest or very dangerous, and possibly those were the same profession. "How much do you want?"

"For the letters?"

"For whatever you found. All of it. Name a figure."

The kitchen was quiet except for the boiler's patient respiration. Somewhere above us, faintly, someone was practicing a Chopin étude badly enough that the pianist clearly loved it.

"Why would you want them?" I asked.

"Because I'm going to be on the board within two years, and Voss is going to oppose me, and a woman in my position cannot afford to be the only person in the building whose secrets are already public." She said it flatly, without shame, and I understood that the absence of shame was itself the confession. "You want justice, presumably—you have the face of a man who wants justice, it's very becoming—but justice is slow and public and it burns everything it touches, including you. Leverage is quiet. Leverage protects people. I'd protect you, Zephyrine. Better than she ever did."

And God help me, for about four seconds, I wanted it.

Not the money—I had spent twelve years learning to need almost nothing, and needing nothing is a kind of wealth, though a poor country to live in. What I wanted was the offer underneath the offer: someone else to carry it. Someone else to decide, someone else to blame, someone else's hand on the wardrobe door so mine never had to open it. For four seconds I stood inside the fantasy of being protected again, the way I had once stood inside Voss's protection, and we know how that ended—with me in another city, learning to push a mop, perfecting a smile that ended conversations.

I looked at Odile Ferrand and I saw the finished version of myself. The one who took the deal at seventeen instead of taking the silence. The one who learned that safety purchased with other people's truths compounds interest forever. She was what I might have become if I'd been richer, colder, or simply better at arithmetic—and the terrible thing, the thing I carried out of that kitchen like a stone in my shoe, was that she wasn't monstrous. She was reasonable. She was the reasonable end of the road I had started down.

"No," I said.

"Everyone has a figure."

"Not for this." I wrapped both hands around my coffee cup, because my hands wanted to do something and I wouldn't let them shake. "These letters aren't mine to sell. Half of them are addressed to a boy who doesn't exist anymore, and the other half belong to people who haven't been asked yet. When they're ready to be read, they'll be read. Out loud. By someone with nothing to gain."

Odile studied me for a long moment, and then she laughed—a short, genuine, startled laugh, the first honest sound she'd made all afternoon.

"There it is," she said. "Do you know what you just sounded like? You sounded like her. Forty years ago. Before the plaques." She stood, smoothed a coat that didn't need smoothing, and left a bill on the table that would have covered a month of Doro's groceries. "For the coffee," she said, and at the door she paused. "When they read them aloud—because they will, men like you always do, it's why you're poor and why I'll sleep tonight—remember that I offered. Remember that the door was open and you chose the wardrobe."

She left. Doro collected the bill, looked at it, and put it in the tin marked *TEODOR'S PIANO FUND*, because that was the kind of man he was.

* * *

I didn't sleep that night. I sat in my narrow room above the maintenance office with the box on my knees and read the letters again, all of them, the boy's handwriting getting smaller and angrier as the pages turned, and I tried to find the moment in my own history where I had almost become Odile Ferrand, and couldn't, because there wasn't a moment. There was only a direction. Every day you lean a little toward the door or a little toward the wardrobe, and neither choice announces itself, and one morning you wake up and you are simply the sort of person who buys things or the sort who reads things aloud.

Near dawn I did something I hadn't done since I found the box. I took out the earliest letter—the one dated March, the boy's handwriting still round with hope—and I read it not as evidence but as a letter. *Dear Maestra Voss. I know you are protecting someone. I am asking you to protect me instead. Tell them it was mine. I don't need the prize. I need to exist.*

*I need to exist.*

A seventeen-year-old had written that, sealed it, handed it to the woman he trusted more than gravity, and she had put it behind a wardrobe, and the wardrobe had held it for twelve years, and now a woman with a silver car wanted to buy it for leverage while the wrecking ball idled ten weeks away like a beast outside the gates.

There was one person I hadn't gone to yet. One person whose own correspondence from that year might still be sitting in the archive, filed and boxed and waiting, because Marisol archived everything—Marisol archived *receipts*, Marisol archived lunch wrappers if they bore historical significance.

At six in the morning I went down to the basement and found her already there, as always, surrounded by paper, a headlamp pushing a cone of light through the dust like a lighthouse in a bottle.

"Marisol," I said. "The year of the sonata. Do we have Voss's outgoing correspondence?"

She looked up at me over her glasses, and I watched her understand, in the way archivists understand things—completely, instantly, and with terrible calm—exactly what I was asking and exactly why.

"Zephyrine," she said, reaching for her keys, "I've been waiting for you to ask me that for three weeks."
