## 13. Marisol's Boxes

There were four hundred and twelve boxes in the Meridian Conservatory archive, and Marisol Reyes had catalogued every one of them. She knew this the way other people knew their children's birthdays, or their own blood type, or the number of stairs to the basement (nineteen, and the seventh one creaked, and she had petitioned three successive directors to fix it and would petition a fourth if she lived long enough).

Box 317 was labeled, in her own handwriting from 1998, VOSS, I. — CORRESPONDENCE, INCOMING. It sat on the third shelf of aisle C, between a box of mid-century concert programs and one that contained nothing but forty years of radiator complaints, which she kept because history was history, even the whiny parts.

The problem was that Box 317 was heavier than it should have been.

She noticed it on a Tuesday, the way you notice a picture frame hanging crooked — not at first, but then relentlessly. Incoming correspondence meant letters *to* Voss. Programs, reviews, fan mail from provincial music societies, the usual sediment of a celebrated career. But when she lifted the box, something shifted inside it with a denser sound than paper. A sound like intention.

Marisol was sixty-one years old and had worked in archives long enough to know that boxes lied. Labels were written by people in a hurry, by people with something to hide, by people who genuinely believed they were being helpful. The box itself did not care what the label said. The box only knew what it held.

She carried it to her worktable under the window where the light came in slantwise and gold in the late afternoon, untied the string — actual string, cotton twine, someone had cared once — and opened the flaps.

On top: incoming letters, exactly as advertised. A thank-you note from the Riga Philharmonic, 1987. A Christmas card with a printed signature. A furious telegram from a conductor whose tempo she had publicly questioned, which Marisol read twice because it was magnificent, a small symphony of grievance in thirty words.

Beneath them, wrapped in a sheet of acid-free tissue that had no business being there — tissue was *her* material, her ritual, and Voss had never set foot in this archive that Marisol knew of — was a second bundle. Smaller. Tied with the same cotton twine. And addressed, in a hand she recognized instantly because she had spent the last several weeks staring at it across a kitchen table while Zephyrine Oyelaran turned it over and over like a coin he couldn't spend:

*To Maestra Ilsa Voss.*

Outgoing. Unsent. Or rather — sent nowhere. Kept. Which was worse than unsent, in Marisol's professional opinion. An unsent letter is an accident. A kept letter is a decision, renewed daily for decades.

She should have called someone. That was procedure. Anything touching the Oyelaran matter — and the whole building now spoke of it as *the Oyelaran matter*, in the hushed tone usually reserved for deaths and faculty salary negotiations — anything touching it belonged to all of them now, the night crew's strange shared custody of a scandal.

But her hands were already untying the string.

She told herself it was archival instinct. She told herself a great many things, standing there in the gold light, and none of them were true, and she suspected the letters could tell.

* * *

The earliest one was dated March 14th — no year given, but the paper stock dated it, because paper stock always dated things, that was the quiet miracle of her profession, everything confessed eventually through its materials. The stationery was conservatory issue, the good cream bond they gave to senior faculty, and the handwriting was Voss's: upright, disciplined, each letter a small soldier standing at attention.

*My dear boy [it began],*

*You ask me to tell the truth about the sonata as though truth were a door one simply opens. I am sixty-three years old and I will tell you what I have learned: most doors in this profession open onto other doors, and behind the last one there is usually a committee.*

*You want to know if I believe Cassian wrote it. You deserve a plainer answer than I have given you. Here is plainness, then: I heard you play the third movement in my studio eleven months before the premiere, in a version with different fingering and a bridge that did not yet exist, and I have not been able to unhear it since. There. Is that what you wanted?*

*What you do not understand — what you cannot understand at seventeen, God willing you never have cause to — is what happens to a woman who tells that truth. They will not say "Cassian did not write it." They will say "Voss says Cassian did not write it," and then they will ask why she permitted it, and then they will ask what else she has permitted, and I have spent forty years building a name that is the only wall between this conservatory and the men who would love to see it close. My name is not vanity, Zephyrine. It is load-bearing.*

*I need time. I am asking you for time, which is the one thing we never give the young and always squander ourselves.*

*— I.V.*

Marisol set it down. Her hands were not entirely steady, which annoyed her; she prided herself on steady hands, on being the person in any crisis who said *let's look at the record* in a calming voice.

*Load-bearing.* She almost laughed. Forty years of dust and she had never heard a lie described so honestly — not as a lie at all, but as architecture.

* * *

There were nine letters in the bundle. She made herself tea from the electric kettle she wasn't supposed to keep in the archive (fire code; she kept it anyway; the fire marshal and she had an understanding built on her excellent biscuit supply), and she read them in order, because order mattered, because the arc of a soul was visible only in sequence.

The second letter, two months later, was harder. Doubt had begun to curdle into self-argument.

*You have stopped writing to me, and I confess I prefer it, which is its own confession, isn't it?*

*Odile Ferrand's father telephoned today. He wants Ilsa Voss: A Life commissioned for the anniversary volume. A life! As though I have one, as though it is finished, as though it can be bound and shelved. I said yes. Of course I said yes. Do you know what I thought, in the half-second before yes? I thought: if the book exists, the story is fixed. Set in type. No one reopens a book with a foreword by the president of the board.*

*You see what I have become. I used to be a musician. Now I am a fortification.*

*If you are reading this — and you are not, no one is reading this, that is the entire point of writing it — hate me efficiently, boy. Hate me and go write your second sonata and let the first one be Cassian's burden. He carries it badly, but he carries it. I have seen him at the piano. His hands have forgotten how to be innocent. Yours still play like weather.*

*Like weather. Where did I find that? I think it was yours originally. I think you said it about your own music once, at seventeen, and I took it the way I take everything now.*

*— I.V.*

Marisol wiped her eyes with the back of her wrist and was irritated to discover she'd been crying. It wasn't grief exactly. It was worse: it was recognition. She had spent six weeks angry at Ilsa Voss — righteously, communally angry, the kind the night crew passed around the kitchen table like bread — and here was the woman herself, alone at some desk, calling her own name a fortification, knowing precisely what she was doing and doing it anyway.

Anger, Marisol was beginning to understand, required a monster. This was not a monster. This was a person, which was infinitely more disturbing, because persons were possible.

* * *

The middle letters shortened. That was the pattern she'd catalogued in a thousand correspondences: despair expressed itself in length. The fourth letter was a page. The fifth was half. The sixth was four lines and Marisol read them five times:

*Cassian's family has made inquiries. Not threats — inquiries, which are threats wearing coats. If I speak, they will speak, and what they know about certain years of my life would end more than my career. I did not build this wall to protect myself alone. I tell myself that nightly. Some nights I even arrange to believe it.*

*Forgive me. Or don't. Either way, stop writing. Your letters arrive like weather too, and I can no longer afford to be rained on.*

That was the pivot. Marisol could feel it, the exact hinge of the thing — the moment pity became resolve became silence, the corrupting arc of power rendered in shrinking stationery. After that, the letters changed direction entirely. They stopped being addressed to Zephyrine. The seventh was addressed to *Cassian*, and it was brief and terrible in its efficiency: instructions, essentially. What to say if asked. What never to say. An alibi composed with the same rigor she brought to fingering.

The eighth was addressed to the board chairman, and it was a masterpiece — Marisol hated how much she admired it — a preemptive defense so elegant that no one ever asked the question it answered. She had read enough institutional correspondence to know that the most dangerous documents in any archive were the answers to questions nobody remembered asking.

And the ninth —

The ninth was undated. The paper was older, softer at the edges, handled many times. The handwriting had loosened, the little soldiers deserting their formation. It was short.

*I dreamed about the east wing last night. In the dream the wardrobe was open and all the letters had been delivered and everyone was standing in the hall looking at me, and do you know what I felt? Relief. Enormous, disgraceful relief. Like setting down something I had convinced myself was a gift I was carrying for others.*

*I am seventy years old. I have won everything. I wake up tired in a way sleep does not touch.*

*Someday someone will find these. I keep them where I keep them because I cannot destroy them and cannot send them, and because some part of me — the part that was a musician before it was a wall — is waiting to be caught.*

*— I.V.*

Marisol sat for a long time after that, while the gold light went grey and the radiator ticked its evening complaint somewhere below.

*Some part of me is waiting to be caught.*

Well. Marisol thought. Caught, then.

* * *

She photocopied everything — no, she photographed everything, twice, on two devices, and uploaded the files to three places including one cloud account under the name of her deceased cat, Baron von Biscuit, because paranoia was just professionalism with anxiety. Then she rewrapped the originals in fresh tissue, retied the twine, and wrote a new label for the box in her careful archivist's hand:

VOSS, I. — CORRESPONDENCE, INCOMING *(and one thing that isn't)*

Let the next archivist earn that one.

It was nearly eight when she climbed the nineteen stairs — the seventh creaking its familiar reproach — and followed the smell of Doro's stew down to the basement kitchen, where the night crew gathered the way iron filings gather around a magnet nobody can see.

They were all there. Doro stirring something with the wooden spoon he'd owned since the Nixon administration. Teodor at the corner table feeling the radio's bass through his forearm, his preferred method of listening. And Zephyrine, mopping-adjacent even off duty, sitting with his hands around a mug like it was the last warm thing in the world.

"Sit," said Doro, who considered the word a complete sentence.

Marisol sat. She accepted a bowl. She looked around the table at these people — the boiler-keeper, the tuner, the janitor who had been a prodigy, herself, the keeper of boxes — and understood that whatever happened next, it could not happen as a solo performance. The letters had taught her that much. Even Voss, fortress that she'd become, had spent her whole corruption writing to someone.

"I went back to the archive today," Marisol said. "To Box 317."

Zephyrine looked up. He had that expression he got, the one like a man hearing a floorboard give way one room away.

"There were more letters," she said. "Hers. Nine of them. Written to you, mostly, and never sent. And Zeph—" She paused, choosing among all the true things the one that would land softest, and failed, because sometimes the softest landing was simply the truth. "She wanted to be caught. She wrote it down. She's been waiting twenty years for somebody to open the box."

The kitchen went very quiet. Somewhere above them, faintly, the building settled and sighed, the way old buildings do when they've been holding something all day.

Then Doro put down his spoon, and Marisol saw that whatever came next was going to be argued out at this table, tonight, by all of them — and that Zephyrine Oyelaran had gone white as the steam rising off his stew.
