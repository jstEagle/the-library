## 14. Intermission

The basement kitchen of the Meridian Conservatory had never appeared on any floor plan, which was precisely why it had survived every renovation since 1961. It lived behind the boiler room, down a corridor that officially did not exist, and it smelled permanently of Doro's coffee—which was less a beverage than a geological event—and of the onions Marisol caramelized on Thursdays whether anyone asked her to or not.

On this particular Thursday, the onions were burning, because Marisol kept abandoning them to pace.

"Ten weeks," she said, for the fourth time since Zephyrine had arrived. She held a wooden spoon like a conductor's baton, as if she might conduct the onions into better behavior. "Nine and a half, really, if you account for the contractors who always show up early when they're being paid by the day. The archive is forty years of my life. Forty years. And they want to put a *glass pavilion* on top of it."

"A pretty one," Teodor offered from his chair by the radiator. He signed as he spoke, out of habit, though everyone in the room could hear him fine; it was the rest of the world that couldn't hear him. His hands moved with the economy of a man who had spent fifty years saying exactly what he meant. "Glass pavilions photograph well. Archives don't."

"Nothing about this is funny."

"I wasn't laughing." He touched his hearing aids, then let his hands fall. "I was agreeing with you. It's disorienting, I know."

Doro sat at the head of the table, which was not a position anyone had assigned him but rather a fact of furniture, the way rivers have banks. He was seventy-three years old and built like the boiler he tended, and he had been feeding the night crew of the Meridian since before Zephyrine was born. He poured coffee into four mismatched mugs without looking, the way other men breathe.

"Sit down, Marisol," he said. "You'll wear a groove in the floor, and the floor's got enough problems."

She sat. She pushed the onions off the heat without checking them, which for Marisol constituted a nervous breakdown.

Zephyrine took the corner seat—the seat that had somehow become his over six weeks of Thursdays, though no one had said so—and set the box on the table between them.

The room went quiet the way a piano goes quiet when you lift your hands: not silent, exactly, but holding everything.

The box was shoebox-sized, cardboard gone soft at the corners, dust ground into its seams like grout. Inside were sixty-one letters. Zephyrine knew the number because he had counted them three times, alone, in the dark of Wardrobe 12, as if the count might change and spare him. They were addressed in his own handwriting—a seventeen-year-old's handwriting, all ambitious loops and unfinished tails—to *Z. Oyelaran*, care of the Meridian Conservatory. Every one of them sealed. Every one of them undelivered. Intercepted, somewhere between the boy who wrote them and the man he'd become, by a woman whose name was carved in brass above the west wing entrance.

"So," Doro said. "That's them."

"That's them."

"You've read them?"

"Some." Zephyrine turned his mug a quarter-turn on the table. A habit. "Enough."

"And?" Marisol leaned forward. "What do we do? That's why we're here. We're deciding. Tonight. Because if the east wing comes down in ten weeks, that box comes down with it, and whatever's in those letters—" she gestured at it the way you'd gesture at a sleeping animal you hoped would stay asleep, "—goes into the landfill with my archive and Teodor's tuning forks and forty years of everybody's lives."

"We could burn them," Doro said.

Marisol made a sound like a kettle taken off heat too late.

"I'm not saying burn them," Doro said. "I'm saying say it out loud first, so we can all agree how bad an idea it is. Some things should be burned. Some things are only wounds with postage on them. I've seen men carry letters around for decades like stones in their pockets, and the letter doesn't get lighter, and neither does the man."

"They're not his wounds," Marisol said. "They're evidence."

"Evidence of what? You've read them?"

"Some," Marisol admitted. "The early ones. He was seventeen, Doro. Seventeen, writing to Voss every week, begging her to tell the truth about the sonata, and she kept them behind a wardrobe like contraband. That's not a wound. That's a crime with a bow on it."

"It might be kindness," Doro said. "It might be cowardice. Might be both. You can't know until you know what she was protecting him *from*."

"Or protecting herself," Teodor said.

The radiator ticked. Somewhere above them, faintly, someone was practicing Chopin badly—the third-floor practice rooms never fully emptied, even at eleven at night; there was always one stubborn soul sawing through the same eight bars. Zephyrine found, lately, that he could identify the practice rooms by their acoustics the way sailors identify water by its smell. He hated how much he still loved this building.

"Sell them," Marisol said suddenly. "No—wait. Hear me out. Odile Ferrand wants them. She offered money, Zeph told us. Serious money. We take the money, we fund the archive ourselves, we buy the east wing's survival with the letters and nobody has to be burned at any stake."

"And Ferrand gets leverage over Voss forever," Teodor said. "And the truth becomes a bargaining chip in a negotiation between two rich women, and none of it ever belongs to the people it happened to." He looked at Zephyrine. "No offense."

"None taken. It's accurate."

"Publish them, then," Marisol said. "All of them. Journalist, front page, the whole thing. Let the board choke on it. Let the donors choke. Let the *pavilion* choke."

"And Voss?" Doro asked quietly.

"What about Voss?"

"She's eighty-one years old. Whatever she did, she did it thirty years ago, and she has taught four hundred children since, and half of them worship her, and some of them are right to." Doro wrapped both hands around his mug. "I've known Ilsa Voss for forty years. She is vain, and she is brilliant, and she is capable of a cruelty so refined you don't feel the cut until you see the blood. And she stayed here when every other name on those plaques left for Berlin and New York. When funding collapsed in '09, she played a benefit concert in a coat because the heating was broken, and raised enough to keep this place open two more years. Both of those women exist. The one who hid the letters and the one in the coat. If we publish, we don't get to choose which one the world meets."

The bad Chopin upstairs stopped. Started again. Stopped.

"Let it fall, then," Teodor said. Everyone looked at him. He shrugged. "I'm serious. Option four. The building comes down, the box comes down, the past stays buried under glass, and everybody gets to go on being whoever they decided to be. Voss keeps her myth. Cassian keeps his quiet. Zeph keeps—" he paused, and his hands, for once, hesitated before finishing, "—his armor. It's not nothing. Sometimes a buried truth is load-bearing. Dig it out and see what the ceiling does."

"That's cowardice with architecture," Marisol said.

"Yes," Teodor agreed pleasantly. "Most cowardice is. Mine included. I've known the truth about that sonata for twenty-two years and I've said nothing, so I'll thank everyone at this table not to grade me on courage."

The kitchen went still again. Zephyrine realized he had heard this before—Teodor's flat confession, delivered twice now, each time like a coin placed quietly on a table—but Marisol and Doro had not.

"You *what*?" Marisol said.

* * *

Teodor told it simply, the way he tuned: note by note, no ornamentation.

Twenty-two years ago, he had tuned the hall's Bösendorfer for the premiere of the sonata that won the Voss Prize and made Cassian Vey's name. He had felt the piece come together over three weeks—not just the strings and hammers but the *hands*, because a tuner learns a pianist's hands the way a doctor learns a heartbeat. He had felt Voss coaching. He had felt Cassian playing. And underneath both, in the practice rooms at odd hours, he had felt a third presence—someone else's hands entirely, hands that argued with the music, that pushed it where it didn't want to go, that made the piano sound, briefly, like weather.

"The sonata's second movement," Teodor said. "Everyone praised Cassian's originality. But I'd felt that storm before. In a practice room at two in the morning, the year before. A boy who was supposed to be doing his theory homework." He nodded at Zephyrine, gently. "I never said anything. Who would I have said it to? A deaf tuner's feeling isn't evidence. And the boy seemed—" his hands searched, "—relieved, almost. Like he wanted it buried deeper than anyone."

"I did," Zephyrine said.

It came out before he decided to say it. Small. Almost conversational. The way a floorboard gives.

Three faces turned to him. He looked at his coffee.

"Zeph," Marisol said carefully. "You don't have to—"

"No," he said. "I think maybe I do. I think that's actually what tonight is." He laughed, and it surprised him—it came out cracked, unpracticed, nothing like the donor-smile. "I've been sitting here for six weeks letting you argue about me like I'm a filing question. Burn, sell, publish, bury. And you've been very kind about it, and I've been very good at the smile, and I'm—God, I'm so tired of the smile."

He turned his mug another quarter-turn. Then stopped himself. Put his hands flat on the table instead, palms down, like a man steadying a surface in rough water.

"The sonata was mine. All of it. Every note. I wrote it when I was sixteen and seventeen, in this building, mostly at night, mostly in the east wing because the radiators up there worked and the practice rooms faced the courtyard and nobody checked after midnight. Voss knew. She was my teacher—my real teacher, the only person who ever—" His voice snagged. He waited it out. "She heard it before anyone. She said it was the best thing to come out of the conservatory in a generation. Her words. I've had thirty years to memorize them.

"Cassian was her other protégé. Older than me by two years, polished, connected—his family sat on the board. The spring of the prize competition, Voss came to me and said the sonata should go in under Cassian's name. She had reasons. She always had reasons; that woman could build a cathedral out of reasons. My family situation was—complicated. There were people I needed to stay invisible to, and a prize win meant photographs, programs, names in papers. She said she was protecting me. She said Cassian's family would fund my scholarship either way. She said the music mattered more than the name on it, and that a true composer shouldn't need—" his mouth twisted, "—shouldn't need *credit*. Shouldn't need applause. As if she was handing me a philosophy instead of stealing from me.

"And I said yes. That's the part nobody believes. Nobody made me. I was seventeen and flattered that anyone thought my music was worth stealing, and terrified of the photographs, and she was—Ilsa Voss. You don't say no to weather. So Cassian premiered it. It won everything. His family endowed a chair. And I went home and I wrote her letters asking her to fix it, and she kept them behind a wardrobe, and eventually I stopped writing, and then I stopped coming, and then I stopped playing, and then twelve years passed and I came back with a mop."

He looked up. He had expected—what? Pity, probably. Or worse, the careful blankness people wear around a wound.

Instead Doro reached across the table and put one enormous hand over both of Zephyrine's, and left it there, heavy as a boiler plate, saying nothing, because Doro had never in forty years mistaken silence for absence.

Marisol was crying, furiously, silently, wiping her eyes with the heel of her hand like the tears were an administrative error. "You *idiot*," she said, which in Marisol's language was a term of profound endearment. "You absolute—you were a *child*. You were a child and she was the most powerful woman in the building, and you've spent twelve years calling it your choice."

"It was my choice."

"It was a choice the way drowning is a choice."

Teodor had not moved, but something in his face had opened, the way a hall opens when the doors unlock. "The two-in-the-morning hands," he said. "I used to sit on the stairs sometimes and listen. Best thing in the building, and it was free, and nobody knew. I thought: whoever that is will be famous someday, and I'll be able to say I heard them first." He smiled, small and crooked. "Turns out I was right. Just late."

Zephyrine discovered he was shaking—not dramatically, just a fine tremor in the hands pressed under Doro's, like a string still ringing after the note ends. Something was leaving his body. He could feel it going: twelve years of a story carried alone, exiting through his mouth in a basement that didn't officially exist, and the strange thing was that he did not feel lighter, exactly. He felt *held*. As if the story, having left him, had been caught by four hands and a radiator and the smell of burnt onions, and now belonged to a place instead of a wound.

"The scandal," Doro said quietly. "Twelve years ago. The reason you left. Was that—"

"Was the same thing wearing different clothes." Zephyrine exhaled. "Cassian's father found out—or claimed to find out—that I was telling people the sonata was mine. Drunk, at a gala, age twenty-three, after too much champagne and one too many reviews praising Cassian's 'startling maturity.' I made noise. The family made noise back. Voss arranged it so that I resigned before I could be expelled—her last gift, she called it. Quiet exit, clean references, no photographs." He laughed again, that cracked sound. "She protected me right out the door. She's been protecting me my entire life, straight into the ground."

Nobody spoke for a while. Upstairs, the bad Chopin had given up entirely, and the building settled into its nighttime creaks, the sounds of a structure holding itself up out of habit and love.

"So," Marisol said at last, scrubbing her face dry. "Burn, sell, publish, bury. Now that we know what's actually in the box. Vote?"

"No," Zephyrine said.

Four heads turned.

"Not a vote. Not tonight. Maybe not ever, the way you mean it." He drew the box toward himself and rested his hand on its soft cardboard lid. "I've been thinking it's a decision about the letters. It's not. It's a decision about whether I spend the rest of my life being a man things were done to. Voss did what she did. Cassian did what he did. Fine. Filed. But I stood in that hall twelve years ago and said nothing, and I've stood in every hallway since saying nothing, and the letters aren't evidence of her crime. They're evidence of mine. Sixty-one weeks of a boy telling the truth and mailing it to the one person who could act on it, instead of walking downstairs and saying it out loud." He looked around the table—at Doro's steady hands, at Marisol's red-rimmed defiance, at Teodor's patient, listening face. "So here's what I want. Not a vote. A favor. When I decide—and I will, soon, before the wrecking ball makes the decision for me—I want to not be alone in the room. That's all. That's the favor."

"You were never going to be alone in the room," Doro said. "That's what the kitchen is *for*."

Marisol sniffed, stood, rescued the onions—hopelessly burnt, blackened at the edges, salvaged anyway, because Marisol did not accept losses she could nominally prevent—and began scraping them onto rice with the violence of a woman processing emotion through cookware.

"Fine," she said. "Not a vote. But Zeph—for the record, for when you decide: I vote publish. Loudly. With footnotes."

"For the record," Teodor said, "I vote whatever lets that boy at two in the morning finally sleep."

"Doro?" Marisol asked.

Doro drank his coffee. Set the mug down with a click like a gavel adjourning nothing.

"I vote," he said, "that nobody in this kitchen says a word of what was said here tonight to anybody. Including especially to each other, until Zeph says otherwise. What leaves this room leaves when he carries it out. Agreed?"

"Agreed," said Marisol.

"Agreed," said Teodor.

They all looked at Zephyrine. He nodded, and the nod cost him something and gave him something back, and he couldn't have said what either thing was.

* * *

It was nearly two in the morning when they climbed the service stairs to leave—Doro to his boiler rounds, Marisol to her cot in the archive, Teodor to the night bus. Zephyrine lingered, as he always did, to lock the kitchen's improbable door, and was halfway back up the corridor when he saw it.

An envelope, white and heavy-stock, lying on the basement steps where someone had slid it under the door at the top.

His name was written on it in a hand he would have known blindfolded, in the dark, at the bottom of the sea: small, precise, elegant, each letter a small act of authority.

*Ilsa Voss.*

He stood on the steps a long time, the envelope in his hand, the box of letters heavy under his arm, while somewhere far above him the old building ticked and settled, counting down its ten weeks like a clock with opinions.

He broke the seal.
