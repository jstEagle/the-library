## 19. Publication

Mara woke on Thursday morning to the sound of Kilbride talking.

Not metaphorically — actually talking, a low collective hum that rose from the street below her aunt's front window like bees in a wall. She lay still for a moment, cataloguing it. Nine years of living in towns had taught her the difference between ordinary morning noise and something else. Ordinary noise was scattered, private, each household muttering its own grievances. This was unified. This was one conversation with many mouths.

She got up and looked out.

Bridie Flanagan was standing outside the post office with a fistful of photocopied pages, taping them to the window beside the notice for the charity ceili. Bridie was seventy-three years old and had once told Mara, at Desmond Faye's funeral reception, that grief was like a wet coat — you didn't take it off, you just learned which shoulders to hang it on. Now she smoothed the pages flat with the tenderness of someone hanging a child's drawing, stepped back, and read them again as if she hadn't been the one to put them up.

Mara went downstairs without bothering with tea first.

The dossier had gone out the night before. Wendell had handled the copying — two hundred sheets run off at the all-night place in Carrickmore, forty minutes away, because he'd said, with his dry precision, that he did not trust any machine within twenty miles of Thomas Rourke. Nuala had driven the second circuit of shopfronts after midnight while Mara slept badly in a chair. The retired guard, Patsy Hennessy, had insisted on delivering three copies personally: one to the Garda station, one to the county paper's office in Tullamore, and one — this had required some negotiation — to the parish priest, slid under the door of the presbytery with a note reading *Father, you'll want to see this before Sunday.*

By eight o'clock, the pages were everywhere.

They were in the window of Doyle's hardware, taped over the display of lawnmowers. They were in the pub, where Tom Doran had pinned them behind the bar next to the GAA fixtures, and by half seven there were men standing at the counter not drinking, which in Kilbride was its own kind of miracle. They were on the noticeboard of the church — Father Brennan had apparently decided that since he couldn't unsee them, he might as well let God have a look too. Someone had even put one inside the plastic sleeve of the bus shelter timetable, where it would be read by every schoolchild waiting for the 8:40 into Tullamore, which Wendell later called *the most effective distribution strategy we could have chosen.*

Mara walked down the main street with her hands in her pockets and watched her mother come back to life in public.

That was how it felt. For nine years, Eilish Delacroix had existed only in Mara's head — a voice on old answering machines she'd kept on a cassette, a handwriting on nine years' worth of undeliverable postcards, a woman reconstructed nightly out of guilt and guesswork. Now here she was in twelve-point font, taped to glass: *Eilish Delacroix, 14 March 1997: "Thomas Rourke came to the house himself. He sat where you're sitting now and told us what would happen if Eamon talked. He didn't raise his voice. That was the worst part."* Here she was again: *9 June 1997: "€4,000 left in an envelope in the church porch. No note. We know whose hand delivered it."* And here, dated eleven days before the fire, the line Mara had read so many times she could recite it against her will: *"We are not afraid of him anymore. Fear is a debt and we have paid it long enough."*

People came out of shops to read. People stood in the cold with their collars up and read all four pages, top to bottom, and then went into the next shop and read them again there, as if checking for discrepancies. Mrs. Kelleher from the bakery set out a tray of scones on the pavement like it was a wake, and maybe it was.

Old Patsy Hennessy found Mara outside the butcher's. He looked terrible — he'd clearly been drinking since the dossier went out, or possibly since 1998 — but his eyes were steady, and he gripped her arm with a hand like a fence post.

"They're saying my name's in it," he said.

"It is."

"Aye." He nodded slowly, working through something. "I spent twenty years thinking I'd lost my nerve that night. Turns out I lost it earlier than that, is all." He patted her arm twice, an awkward, ancient gesture, and shuffled off toward the pub, where no doubt the day's first pint was being poured in his honor.

*

Rourke's world began shedding him before lunchtime.

It started small. Councillor Gerry Madden, who had played golf with Rourke every Saturday for fifteen years, issued a statement at 10:15 saying he would be "recusing himself from all matters connected to the Kilbride redevelopment pending clarification." By 11:30, the word *clarification* had done its work, and two more councillors had used it. By noon, the hotel's head chef — a big Cork man named Fintan who had once thrown a man out of the kitchen for insulting the seafood — announced he was taking leave. The hotel's booking page, Mara noticed with a small cold satisfaction, had begun showing dates as unavailable. Nobody had cancelled anything officially. The dates were simply, quietly, closing themselves, the way a flower closes at dusk.

At one o'clock, the county paper's website posted a single sentence: *The Tullamore Herald has received documents concerning events in Kilbride in 1998 and is reviewing them with legal counsel.* At 1:20, RTÉ's regional desk called the same legal counsel. By two, a reporter was standing outside the hotel holding a microphone as though it were a candle at a vigil.

And at 2:45, the county council announced — with the speed of an institution that has suddenly remembered it has principles — a formal inquiry into "historical matters arising from the 1998 fire investigation," to be chaired by a retired judge from Galway who owed nobody in Kilbride anything.

Nuala heard about the inquiry in the bakery and came home laughing, a strange cracked sound Mara had never heard from her before, halfway between joy and the other thing.

"An inquiry," Nuala said, sitting down heavily at the kitchen table. "Twenty-six years. I made your school lunches out of that man's money, Mara. I bought your uniform with it. And now they're going to have an inquiry." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "It's not enough. You understand that? It's not enough and it's everything, both at once, and I don't know what to do with that."

"You don't have to do anything with it," Mara said. "That's rather the point."

Wendell, who had been sitting by the window with his tea untouched — he drank tea the way other men conducted surveillance — spoke without turning around. "He'll want to see you today."

Mara looked at him. "How do you know?"

"Because powerful men don't shed gracefully. They shed like animals — fast, and looking for something to blame." He turned then, and his face was calm in the way she had learned meant the opposite. "And because you're the only thing left in this town he hasn't tried."

*

He came at dusk, but not to the house. He was waiting outside the church, of all places, standing at the gate in a good coat, hatless, his silver hair doing what it had always done in photographs — making him look distinguished rather than merely old. Behind him the memorial garden he'd sponsored was putting on its autumn show of bare roses, and Mara thought there was probably a joke in that somewhere, but she was too tired to find it.

"Miss Kavanagh." He used the wrong name deliberately, and they both knew it. "You'll forgive the ambush. I've been told my calls aren't being returned."

"I can't imagine why."

He smiled. It was, she had to admit, still a fine smile — the smile that had opened hotels and closed investigations and made grown councillors laugh at jokes that weren't funny. But she had spent nine years perfecting a smile herself, the one that ended conversations, and she recognized craftsmanship when she saw it.

"You should know," he said, "that none of this touches me. Documents can be forged. Dead women can't be cross-examined. A judge from Galway will take two years to decide what year it is." He said it lightly, almost fondly, the way you'd explain the rules of a game to a newcomer. "But you — you've had a hard life, Mara. Everyone knows it. Nobody would blame you for wanting to put distance between yourself and all this. Australia, perhaps. Canada. Somewhere the name Delacroix never followed anyone." He reached into his coat, and Wendell, who had materialized at Mara's shoulder with the silent inevitability of weather, shifted his weight — but Rourke only produced an envelope, thick and soft with banknotes. "A gesture. Nothing more. For your trouble."

Mara looked at the envelope for a long moment.

Then she laughed.

It surprised her as much as it surprised him — a real laugh, unpracticed, rising out of her like a bird off water. Because that was the thing he hadn't understood, the thing none of them had understood for twenty-six years: her mother had already written the answer. *Fear is a debt and we have paid it long enough.*

"No," Mara said.

"Mara—"

"My mother sat where you told her to sit. My father took your money and died in England without ever coming home. My brother watched you burn a man's livelihood and planned to say so, and he was fourteen." Her voice stayed level; that was the strange part, the terrifying part, how level it was. "You bought everyone, Mr. Rourke. You bought the guards and the priests and my own family, and mostly it worked, and I spent nine years writing postcards to a dead house because I thought *I* was the one who lit the match. So no. There's nothing left to buy. You're standing in the last shop in Ireland that won't take your money, and I'd stand here all night if it helped, but it won't, because it's already done."

She turned toward the gate. Then stopped, and gave him the smile — the practiced one, the good one, the one Bridie Flanagan had once called *the smile that ends conversations* — and deployed it now in the service of something better than numbness.

"The inquiry judge is from Galway," she said pleasantly. "My mother's people were from Galway. Small world."

She walked away. Behind her she heard the envelope drop — whether dropped in defeat or thrust back at her she didn't turn to see — and heard Rourke's shoes on the gravel, unhurried still, because men like him stayed unhurried longest. But underneath the shoes, underneath everything, she could hear the town talking, the great low hum of it, and it was not going quiet.

Wendell fell into step beside her. For a while neither of them spoke.

"He offered you enough to disappear twice over," Wendell said at last.

"He did."

"And you laughed at him."

"I did." She glanced sideways at him. In the dusk his face was unreadable, but there was something at the corner of his mouth that on another man she'd have called pride. "Why? Does that worry you?"

"No," Wendell said. "But you should know that men like him are most dangerous when the money stops working." He paused at the corner where their ways divided, and looked back toward the hotel, where the lights were coming on one by one in windows that had begun, quietly, to empty. "Tomorrow the investigators arrive. Tonight is his."

She felt it then — not fear exactly, but its cousin, the animal awareness of it — a cold thread down the spine, the same feeling she'd had at the burned lot when the memory finally surfaced. Quiet triumph, she thought, was a strange creature: it sang in one ear and counted footsteps in the other.

They walked home together through the talking town, past shop windows full of her mother's voice, and above the hills the first stars were out, indifferent and exact.

At Nuala's gate, Wendell stopped and handed her something folded — a page photocopied from the dossier, the final letter, the one addressed to her. On the back, in his small precise hand, he had written a single line:

*He checks. Be indoors before dark.*

Mara stood a moment in the cold, the paper trembling very slightly in her hand, listening to the hum of Kilbride — and beneath it, far off down the darkening street, the slow deliberate click of a car door closing.
