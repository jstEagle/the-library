## 22. The Road Out of Kilbride

The solicitor's office smelled of lemon polish and old paper, the way offices do when they have been cleaned for an occasion. Mara sat at the long table with her hands folded, watching the pen as though it might bite. It was a good pen — heavy, gold-capped, the kind Desmond Faye would have approved of — and it lay on the closing papers like something waiting to be picked up.

"You don't have to read them all," said the young solicitor from Cork who had inherited Faye's practice along with his filing cabinets and his habit of speaking too quickly. "It's all standard. The buyer's people drew it up."

"I know," Mara said. "I'd like to read them all anyway."

So she did. She read every clause about boundaries and easements and the retention of existing structures, which was a lawyer's way of saying the burned shell of the house would stay standing until someone decided what to do with it. She read the part where she released all claim to the land her grandfather had bought in 1961 with money he'd saved driving lorries to Dublin and back. She read the part where her signature, once applied, ended nine years of owning a house that was mostly absence.

Wendell sat beside her, still as furniture, wearing the grey suit he seemed to keep for occasions of gravity. Nuala sat across the table with a handkerchief rolled into a tight cylinder in her fist, as if she were holding a small, obedient animal.

"Any questions?" the solicitor asked, when she reached the last page.

Mara thought about it. There were so many questions — there had been questions all year, questions that had cracked a town open like a dropped plate — but none of them belonged on this form.

"Just one," she said. "Who's buying?"

"A developer from Galway," he said. "They're talking about four houses. Detached. Good views of the water."

Of course. That was how places ended now — not with fire but with floor plans. She found, examining herself carefully, that she didn't mind. Ash made poor foundations anyway. Let someone else build something warm on the spot where hers had stood; let children run through rooms where Eamon had never got the chance to grow tall. The land wouldn't remember. Only she would, and she was taking the memory with her.

She signed. Her name came out steady, and she noticed that she wrote *Kavanagh* without hesitating, and then, in the smaller box underneath, where the form asked for any former names, she wrote *Delacroix* in careful letters, giving both names their due, like introducing two old friends who had finally agreed to be in the same room.

* * *

The post office was quiet at half ten on a Thursday, the way it always was between the morning rush and the lunchtime queue. Mrs. Brennan was behind the counter, and she looked up when Mara came in with the expression of a woman who has watched a story unfold for months and is trying very hard not to appear interested in its final page.

"One postcard?" Mrs. Brennan said.

"One postcard."

Mara set it on the counter. She had bought it that morning from the newsagent's rack — the same rack, the same rotating stand of Irish scenes, that had supplied her for nine years. This one showed the harbour at Kilbride in low golden light, the boats at rest, the water doing its patient glittering work. It was, she had to admit, a beautiful picture of a place that had been very cruel to her family, and she supposed that was true of most beautiful pictures.

Mrs. Brennan turned it over to stamp it, then paused. Her eyes moved across the address, and Mara watched her read it twice.

"It's addressed to you," Mrs. Brennan said.

"That's right."

"To yourself." Mrs. Brennan looked up over her glasses. "Care of..." She squinted. "A flat in Galway. Number 4, The Crescent."

"That's the one."

Mrs. Brennan was a woman of perhaps sixty who had sold Mara stamps every week for nine years and had never once asked why the postcards went to a house with no door. Mara had always assumed it was tact. Now she suspected it was something more like respect — the particular respect of small towns for griefs they cannot fix.

"Well," Mrs. Brennan said. She stamped the card with a firm thump, as though sealing a pact. "That's the first sensible address you've used yet."

Mara laughed. It surprised her, the laugh — it came up from somewhere unguarded, and it sounded like her mother's laugh, or maybe just like a laugh, plain and ordinary, the kind people did every day without thinking anything of it.

On the back of the card, in blue ink, she had written:

*Dear Mara,*

*You don't know me well yet, but I'm told we're going to be living together for some time. I should warn you: I talk to myself, I keep odd hours, and I have spent nine years writing to dead people, so my correspondence skills may need work.*

*I want you to know that I forgive you. All of it. The night you hid under the stairs and heard things no child should hear, and built a story out of the wreckage because a story was all you could carry. The nine years of postcards. The smile you practiced in mirrors until it worked on everyone except you. None of it was a crime. It was survival, and you survived, and here you are.*

*Mam wrote that the truth was mine to spend however I needed. I spent it. It bought more than I expected — it bought Nuala's tears and Wendell's stubbornness and a whole town reading her handwriting in shop windows. It cost Rourke everything, which I mention only because I promised myself I'd allow myself one petty sentence per decade.*

*Eamon Delacroix. Brigid Delacroix. Séamus Delacroix. I am writing your names down so the card carries them out of this town and into whatever comes next. You are not ash. You are carried.*

*See you soon,*
*Mara*

She watched Mrs. Brennan drop it into the slot, heard the small final sound of it landing among the other mail — bills, birthday cards, catalogues, all the ordinary traffic of ordinary lives — and felt something settle in her chest that she recognized, after a moment's searching, as peace. Not the numb kind she had worn like a coat for nine years. The other kind. The kind that coexists quite comfortably with sorrow, the way a harbour coexists with weather.

* * *

Outside, Wendell was leaning against his car — a sensible grey hatchback that he kept immaculately clean, which told you most of what you needed to know about him before he said a word. He had the driver's door open and was looking down the street toward the harbour with the expression of a man memorizing a place he intends never to return to, or possibly one he intends to return to when it has improved. With Wendell it was often hard to tell.

Nuala came out of the shop behind Mara carrying a paper bag of sandwiches she had insisted on buying, because in Nuala's world no journey longer than twenty minutes could be undertaken without provisions.

"There's ham," Nuala said, pressing the bag into Mara's hands. "And there's cheese. And there's a flask of tea, because God knows what they drink in Galway."

"Nuala, it's an hour away."

"An hour is a long time to be hungry." Nuala's eyes were red-rimmed but dry, and she held her chin at the angle of a woman who has cried her full allotment and means to hold the line. She took Mara's face in both hands, the way she had when Mara was small enough to be lifted onto a kitchen stool. "You'll ring me Sunday."

"I'll ring you Sunday."

"And you'll eat actual meals. Not toast. Toast isn't a meal, it's a cry for help."

"Yes, Aunt Nuala."

"And if that flat of yours has damp, you tell me, and I'll come down with the dehumidifier, and I don't care what the bus schedule says."

Mara hugged her then, properly, breathing in the smell of her — wool and hand cream and, faintly, the sea — and felt the strange double weight of it: this woman had taken Rourke's money and raised her on it, and had confessed it all with her heart in her open hands, and had been forgiven, and forgiveness, it turned out, was not a door closing but a room opening. They were going to be awkward with each other for years. Mara found she was looking forward to it.

"Go on," Nuala said thickly, releasing her and stepping back and immediately becoming brisk again, because emotion in Nuala's company was permitted only in measured doses. "Before I make a show of us both."

Wendell opened the passenger door for her, an old-fashioned courtesy he performed without irony, and Mara got in and arranged the sandwich bag on her lap like a ceremonial object.

He drove slowly through the town. Past the hotel, where the windows bore the faded ghost-tape of removed notices and a receiver was managing the place pending sale — Rourke's empire dismantled by photocopier, which was somehow fitting; past the memorial garden, where the flowers Faye's funeral had sent had long since gone over and new ones had been planted by volunteers, ordinary people, the town tending its own memory now; past the pub, where through the window Mara could see the corner table where the dossier had first been read aloud, a man still sitting near it with a pint, keeping it company.

At the bottom of the hill, without deciding to, Wendell turned left instead of right, and they went the long way — past the turning for the lot. The fence was still up. The house stood inside it, black-windowed, patient, already half a memory itself. Mara looked at it the way you look at a photograph of someone you loved: fully, briefly, without flinching.

"Goodbye," she said quietly. Not to the house, exactly. To all of it — the stairs she'd hidden under, the kitchen that smelled of bread, the boy who had been brave and never got thanked for it. "Goodbye, all of you."

Wendell said nothing, which was his way of agreeing, and turned the car back toward the coast road.

* * *

The road out of Kilbride ran along the water, and the morning had turned generous — high bright clouds, the sea throwing light up at the sky like a child showing off. Wendell drove the way he did everything: carefully, without hurry, both hands on the wheel, as though the road were a person deserving of respect.

They passed the turnoff for the church. They passed the field where the fair came every August. They passed the little strand where Eamon had once, in another life, taught her to skim stones, counting her successes aloud with the solemnity of a judge.

"You're smiling," Wendell said.

"I'm not."

"You are. I can see it in the wing mirror."

Mara checked. And there it was — her own face, small and slightly distorted in the mirror's curve, wearing an expression she had practiced for nine years in bathroom mirrors and shop windows and the darkened glass of the post office door. The smile that ended conversations. The smile that deflected sympathy. The professional smile, the armor smile, the one that had gotten her through funerals and queues and nine years of Thursdays.

But this one was different, and she could see the difference even in the warped little glass. This one had arrived on its own. Nobody had asked her for it. Nothing needed deflecting. It was just there, sitting on her face like sunlight, crooked and real and slightly astonished at itself.

"Huh," she said.

"Huh," Wendell agreed.

They drove on. Behind them Kilbride shrank in the mirror — the harbour, the hill, the church spire, the whole complicated town with its silences and its kindnesses and its long-delayed reckoning, getting smaller and softer in the haze until it was just a shape on the edge of the water, a place she had been, a place she had, against all odds, survived.

In her lap, the sandwich bag. In her handbag, the keys to number 4, The Crescent. In the post, somewhere ahead of her, travelling at the speed of ordinary mail, a postcard was making its way to a woman named Mara Kavanagh, formerly Delacroix, who was going to read her own handwriting in a few days' time and remember exactly who she was.

"Galway, then," Wendell said.

"Galway," Mara said. She settled back into the seat and watched the road unspool ahead of them, silver and certain, going somewhere. "Drive on, Mr. Osei. We've kept ourselves waiting long enough."

And the car went, and the town let go, and the sea kept its patient glittering beside them all the way to the edge of the map.
