## 1. The Tenant

The conservatory at ten o'clock at night smells of rosin and floor wax and, faintly, of the lemon disinfectant that Jude Voss has been buying himself for six years now, because the school's supplier switched brands and nobody consulted him. Nobody consults him about anything. That is the arrangement he has spent two decades constructing, brick by careful brick, and it is the only structure in his life that has never once threatened to collapse.

He lets himself in through the loading dock on Delancey Street, where the alarm keypad's code is the same one it was when he was seventeen: 4-1-9-6, the year the school was founded. He has suggested to no one that this is a security flaw. He keys it in without looking, the way a man might cross himself, and steps into the dark.

The building knows him before he knows it, if buildings can be said to know anything. His hands know it. They know which door sticks in winter—the second practice room on the third floor, the one with the warped frame—and they know to lift the mop bucket over the lip of the service elevator rather than roll it, because the wheels catch and announce you. He has learned to move through this place the way water moves through it, filling the low places, asking nothing, leaving nothing behind but cleanliness.

There are eleven students still in the building when he arrives. He counts them the way other men count sheep. The girl in Studio C—cello, always cello until midnight, always the same Dvořák passage, always failing it in the same place at bar forty-one—is here tonight, her lamp burning yellow under the door. Two boys are sharing a piano in the large ensemble room, playing something four-handed and foolish, laughing between attempts. A violinist stands in the corridor outside the recital hall itself, running scales so quietly they are barely more than breath.

Jude passes them all without a word. This is not coldness; it is craft. In twenty years he has perfected the art of being furniture—not ignored, because ignoring requires noticing first, but simply unremarked upon, a fact of the building like the radiators or the draft under the east door. Students see a heavyset man in a gray uniform shirt pushing a mop, and their eyes slide off him the way eyes slide off a wall. Once, three years ago, a girl had looked directly at him and said, "Excuse me, do you know if the practice rooms stay open late?" and he had shaken his head and kept walking, and she had accepted this as a complete answer from a complete stranger, and he had thought about it for a week.

The truth is that he could have told her the practice rooms never close. He could have told her which ones have the best pianos (Studio B, the Yamaha, tuned monthly) and which have the worst (the basement, where the upright has a dead key, F above middle C, and has had one since before she was born). He could have told her things about this building that no one alive knows better. But telling her would have required speaking, and speaking requires a name, and his name is a door he sealed shut twenty years ago with his own hands.

* * *

He starts on the ground floor, as always. The main corridor first, then the lobby, then the long gallery where the portraits hang.

He does not look at the portraits. This too is discipline.

The gallery runs sixty feet, lit by sconces that someone years ago put on timers so they dim at nine and go dark at eleven, and in those two hours the oil paintings become a procession of shadows—founders and benefactors and dead professors, all of them gazing down the corridor toward the entrance as if waiting for someone to arrive. At the far end hangs Aldous Renner, the founder, painted young and severe, his hands folded on a stack of scores. And beside him, added later, smaller, Ilse Voss, his most celebrated student, painted at perhaps thirty-five, dark hair pinned up, an expression on her face that visitors always read as serenity and that Jude has always read as something else entirely—patience, maybe, or the particular exhaustion of a woman holding very still so that a secret doesn't spill out of her.

His mother. Who died eleven days ago.

He mops around the portrait's stretch of floor with the same even strokes he gives every other stretch. He does not look up. Looking up would be a kind of prayer, and he gave up prayer along with everything else that requires an audience.

But the body keeps its own devotions. Somewhere around the midpoint of the gallery, every night, his chest tightens in a way that has nothing to do with exertion, and he has learned not to fight it. He lets it pass through him like weather. Grief, he has discovered, is mostly a matter of scheduling.

* * *

At midnight he takes his break, fifteen minutes in the janitor's room off the loading dock, a space the size of a generous closet containing a cot he has never used, a hot plate, a small refrigerator, and a chair positioned precisely so that if the door opens a crack, whoever enters sees only the back of it. He eats what he brought—a sandwich, an apple—and he reads.

Tonight he is rereading a newspaper clipping he should have thrown away weeks ago: the obituary, half a column, with a photograph of Ilse Voss at sixty-eight, taken at some gala a decade back. *Composer-in-residence emerita. Teacher to three generations. Survived by a son.* No name given for the son. Even in death, the anonymity held; even the paper had conspired in it, or perhaps Marguerite Hale had called them, or perhaps there had simply never been anything to say. *Survived by a son.* As if he were a piece of furniture too. As if he were the piano bench, present, necessary, unnamed.

He folds the clipping along its old creases and puts it in his shirt pocket, against his heart, which is a sentimental gesture and he knows it, and he permits himself exactly one sentimental gesture per night, and this is tonight's.

The funeral is Saturday. He knows because Marguerite told him, standing in this same doorway three nights ago, having come down herself—which she never does—to say, "Jude. Saturday, eleven o'clock, St. Anselm's. You'll be there, of course." Not a question. She had looked tired in a way he'd never seen, her famous composure showing seams, and she had touched the doorframe with one hand as if steadying herself against the building itself.

"Of course," he had said. First words spoken aloud in the building in—how long? He'd lost count. His voice had come out rusty, a hinge unused.

She had studied him then, a moment too long, the way she used to study his mother's students when she suspected they were bluffing about having practiced. "You'll sit with us," she'd said. "The family section."

And he had wanted to say: I am the family section. I am the entire family section. There is no one else.

He had said, "Thank you," instead, and she had nodded and gone back up the stairs, and he had stood in the doorway listening to her footsteps diminish and thought: *she called me Jude.* Not Mr. Voss, not *the boy*, not any of the careful circumlocutions the faculty use among themselves when they think he can't hear—though he hears everything, that is his profession, hearing. Just *Jude*, plain, the way you'd say the name of someone who belongs to you.

It had frightened him more than any stranger could have.

* * *

At one a.m. he works the second floor, and this is the hour he dreads and craves in equal measure, because the second floor holds the practice rooms, and the practice rooms hold the sound.

Even empty, they hold it. Sound soaks into these walls the way rain soaks into brick, and at night, when the heating system ticks and settles, the rooms give it back in fragments—a phrase of Bach here, a scale there, ghost-notes rising out of the plaster. He has asked himself more than once whether he imagines it, and has decided the question doesn't matter. Either way, the music is in the walls, and either way, he is the one who cleans around it.

Room 214 is the one. Of course it is. It faces the quad, it has the good light in the afternoons, and on a February afternoon twenty years ago it was where he sat warming up before the competition finals, running the Liszt, when the thing happened in his hands.

He has never found language for the thing that happened in his hands. The doctors had words—stress response, performance anxiety, a cascade of terms that described the weather without explaining the sky—but what it felt like was simpler and worse: his fingers simply left him. Mid-phrase, mid-hall, mid-life, four hundred people watching, his hands announced that they belonged to someone else now, and the music stopped, and the silence that followed lasted, as far as he can tell, the rest of his life.

He mops Room 214 last, always last, and always standing just inside the door, never crossing the threshold to the corner where the piano sits. The piano is new—replaced years ago, of course, nothing in this school survives except the walls and the grief—but the corner is the same corner, and the light fixture is the same fixture, and some nights he swears the silence in that corner is still the exact silence from that afternoon, preserved like a specimen, waiting for him to come finish the phrase.

He never finishes the phrase. That is the covenant. He cleans the room; the room keeps the silence; neither of them mentions the afternoon.

* * *

Two a.m., and the building begins to empty of even its ghosts. The cellist packs up—he hears the case click shut through two doors—and the boys in the ensemble room leave arguing happily about tempo. By three the corridors are silent in the way that only this building achieves, a silence with texture, a silence made of a hundred years of practicing.

This is the hour he loves. He will admit it to no one, and there is no one to admit it to, but this hour belongs to him completely. The conservatory at three a.m. is the only version of the conservatory that has never hurt him. No stage, no audience, no name in a program. Just floors, and the work of floors, and the small honest satisfaction of a surface coming clean under his hands.

He is finishing the third-floor corridor when he hears the piano.

Not a ghost-note. Not the heating system. Someone is in the large ensemble room, playing softly with the door open, and the playing is good—not student-good, not competent-good, but the specific, unhurried goodness of someone playing for themselves at an hour when no one could possibly be listening. Chopin, the E minor prelude, taken slowly, the way you'd take it if you had all the time in the world and no one to impress.

Jude stops with his mop in the middle of the corridor and listens the way a starving man looks through a window.

He should keep working. The schedule is the schedule; the schedule is the wall he built his life inside. Instead he stands there while the prelude winds down to its last bars, and when the final chord comes—played soft, played true, released rather than struck—something in his chest turns over with an ache so familiar he could greet it by name.

Then the playing stops, and footsteps approach the door, and Jude does the thing he has trained himself to do for twenty years: he becomes the corridor. He lifts his mop, angles his face toward the wall, and pushes onward with the perfect blank industry of a man who has heard nothing, seen nothing, been nothing.

A young woman emerges—sleep-deprived, cardigan over pajamas, earbuds around her neck. She glances at him. Her eyes do the sliding-off thing. She walks past him toward the stairwell, yawning, and he continues mopping, and the whole exchange occupies less than two seconds and costs him nothing and costs him everything, both at once, the way it always does.

Because here is the arithmetic he performs nightly and never solves: the anonymity is the comfort. It is also the cost. He chose it—chose it deliberately, methodically, the year after the collapse, when he realized that the alternative to being nobody was being *that* somebody, the prodigy who broke, the cautionary tale, the name that made teachers lower their voices. He had scrubbed the name off himself the way he scrubs these floors, patiently, thoroughly, and what remained was a man who could walk the halls of his own childhood unseen. A tenant in the house he grew up in. Paying his rent in labor, keeping to his hours, disturbing nothing.

It has worked. It has worked for twenty years. He wants, more than almost anything, for it to keep working.

And yet.

He finishes the corridor. He rinses the mop. He descends to the ground floor for the last pass, and that is when he sees the light.

Under the door of the recital hall itself—the big hall, the old hall, the one the board has been muttering about demolishing for years—there is a strip of gold falling across the corridor floor. Stage lights. Someone has turned on the stage lights at three-forty in the morning, in a hall that should be locked, in a building where everyone has gone home.

Jude stands in the dark with his mop dripping quietly onto the clean floor, and he feels the building shift beneath him, subtle as a change in pressure, the way it did the day his hands left him.

Through the gap at the door's edge, faintly, he can hear a voice. Low, uncertain, talking to no one—or talking to the hall, or talking to the stage—an older voice, a stranger's voice, murmuring in the dark of the empty house.

He should get the night supervisor. He should finish his rounds and be gone before dawn, as he has done ten thousand times, invisible, immaculate, safe.

Instead, Jude Voss sets the mop against the wall and stands in the corridor of his own history, listening to a stranger speak to the dark.
