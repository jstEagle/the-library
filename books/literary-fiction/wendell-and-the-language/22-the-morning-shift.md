## 22. The Morning Shift

The conservatory at six in the morning is a different animal than the conservatory at midnight.

At midnight it is all held breath and closed doors, the corridors humming with everything the students couldn't finish saying before they went to bed. At dawn it exhales. The radiators tick themselves awake. Light comes through the east windows in long amber bars that lie across the parquet like measures of music nobody has written down yet. I have come to prefer this hour. I have come to prefer most hours, if I'm honest, which is itself a kind of miracle I don't examine too closely, the way you don't tap on glass you're afraid might crack.

I still mop. I want to be clear about that, because there are people in this building who seem embarrassed for me—the administrators who now nod to me in the stairwell with a deference that sits wrong on their faces, the faculty who say *Mr. Ravensworth* as though the name were a title they'd only recently learned to pronounce. They think the mop is a costume I haven't yet been persuaded to take off. They are wrong. The mop is the truest instrument I own. Ten years of pushing one across these floors taught me more about listening than any masterclass ever did: how sound lives in rooms after the players leave, how a building keeps score, how the things people can't say collect along the baseboards like dust.

Besides, the morning shift pays the same as the night shift did, and I have discovered that I like being awake when the students arrive. I like watching them come in out of the cold with their cases clutched to their chests like sleeping children. I like being the man with the keys who nods at them from the end of the hall. There is a version of me that would have found this humiliating once. That version is gone. I don't miss him exactly. But some mornings I notice the space where he used to stand.

* * *

The seminar meets Thursdays at four, in Room 214—my mother's old classroom, though I didn't choose it and I've stopped flinching when I unlock it. The registrar assigned it by accident, or maybe not by accident; Aldermoor has always had a taste for small ironies, and I've learned to let the building have its jokes.

Six students. That's all they gave me, and all I asked for. Composition for the Reluctant, Maren called it once, laughing, and she wasn't entirely wrong. These are not the prodigies. The prodigies have teachers with names on concert posters. Mine are the ones who came to music sideways, or late, or against someone's advice—a cellist who writes songs she's afraid to sing, a percussionist whose pieces are mostly silence, a boy named Dev who composes only at night because his father sleeps with the door open and he cannot bear to be heard practicing anything so private as hope.

I teach them the way I was never taught, which is to say I teach them almost nothing. I make them play what they've written and then I ask questions. What were you afraid of here? Why does this measure apologize? Where did you stop telling the truth? They hate the questions for the first month. Then they start bringing answers, and then they start bringing better music, and somewhere in there the room stops feeling like a classroom and starts feeling like what my mother's kitchen felt like on Sunday afternoons when she hummed over the cutting board and let me sit inside her attention like a warm coat.

Her name is on the archive shelf now. Third floor, restricted collection, drawer four—the same drawer where I once found a folder labeled only *R.* and understood that theft could be conducted through the mail. The folder is gone. In its place stands a bound score, cream-colored boards, gold lettering pressed into the spine:

**VESPRS FOR A SMALL ROOM**
*Ilse Ravensworth (1948–1994)*
*Completed by Lior Ravensworth*

Ilse. It took me three weeks after the premiere to be able to write her full name without my hand shaking. Now the letters sit there in gold, patient as stones, and every time I walk past the case I touch the glass—not because I need to, but because ten years ago I would have given anything to know she existed anywhere outside my memory, and I have decided not to take the knowing for granted.

Veyra's arrangement credits were struck from the foundation's catalog in the spring. The royalties rerouted. He signed everything without a lawyer in the room, which surprised me until I remembered that a man who spends forty years curating other people's ghosts knows precisely what it costs to become one himself. He retired from teaching in June. The announcement called it *to pursue independent scholarship.* Nobody asked him what that meant. Some departures are designed to be unexamined.

I saw him once more, in September, at the train station. He was buying a ticket to somewhere flat and northern—I didn't look closely—and he nodded at me across the concourse the way you nod at a man you owe everything to and will never finish paying. I nodded back. That was the whole conversation. It was enough. It will keep being enough, I suspect, for both our lifetimes, and I have made a fragile peace with the fact that peace doesn't have to be complete to hold weight.

* * *

Maren graduates in May. She plays the second piano part in the seminar sometimes, when we work through the Vespers as a teaching score—her idea, not mine, and I resisted it longer than I should admit. Hearing those movements in that room, with strangers' hands on her mother's—on *my mother's*—notes, was like watching someone else brush my hair. But Maren argued me down, the way she always does, standing in the practice-room doorway with her arms crossed and her chin up, twenty-two years old and utterly certain that hiding things from students is how institutions rot.

"They need to hear it's survivable," she said. "You wrote program notes about your decade of silence. You don't get to be quiet in person too."

So we play it. And last Thursday, Dev—who has never said more than nine words in a row in my presence—raised his hand afterward and asked who the woman was, and I said, "My mother," and the room went very still, and then he said, "She must have been really something," and I said, "Yes," and that was the entire exchange, and I drove home with the windows down even though it was November, because some things need air moving through them or they close up again.

Maren is applying to graduate programs. She showed me the list yesterday, fanned out on the top of the recycling bin like a card trick, and watched my face while I read it. Juilliard. Eastman. Two places in Europe. "You'll get in everywhere," I told her, and she said, "I know," and then, quieter, "That's not what I'm asking." What she was asking was whether talent still frightened me. Whether I thought it would frighten her. I told her the truth: that it would, forever, and that fear was not a stop sign, it was just weather. She considered this. Then she took back her list, folded it into her coat pocket, and said, "You're coming to my recital," in a tone that closed the subject, and I said yes, and meant it, and noticed—with a small internal shock—that meaning things had started to feel normal again.

* * *

It happens on a Tuesday in February, which is to say it happens on an ordinary day, which is how the important things always happen.

I'm working the third-floor corridor, the archive wing, pushing the mop through the long light. The building is empty except for the radiators and me. And when I pass the practice room at the end of the hall—Room 311, the one with the bad radiator and the good piano—I see it in the trash bin by the door: a single sheet of manuscript paper, crumpled into a loose ball, sitting on top of the liner like something set down rather than thrown away.

I know the difference. Thrown-away paper lands hard, crushed tight, angry. Set-down paper rests. This one rests.

I stand there for a moment with the mop dripping quietly onto the floor, and I am eighteen again, and thirty again, and forty-something again, all at once, all of us looking at the same wastebasket. Because I know what's in that ball of paper before I open it. I know it the way you know your own handwriting. It's a sketch—fourteen bars, treble clef only, the ink going thin where the pen ran dry and then stopping altogether mid-phrase. It's a student who got to the edge of what she could hear and couldn't follow herself past it. It's a question asked into an empty room at eleven o'clock at night by someone who is almost certainly not supposed to be in the building, and who left the question behind because leaving it felt safer than carrying it home.

I smooth the page open on the piano lid.

The writing is careful, over-careful, the noteheads drawn rather than written. First-year theory student, probably. The melody is nothing yet—it circles two pitches, hesitates, reaches for a third and pulls back—but underneath the hesitation there's a shape, an actual shape, the ghost of something the writer heard clearly once and is trying to find again. I have read ten thousand pages like this. I have *been* ten thousand pages like this. And I know exactly what the fourteen bars are waiting for, because it's the same thing every unfinished piece is waiting for, the same thing mine waited for, the same thing hers waited for in a notebook in a locked drawer while a dying woman paid for her own hospice with the only currency she had left:

Permission.

I take out my pen. I don't correct anything—that was the old game, the anonymous corrections I used to leave for Maren, red ink in the margins like a ghost grading homework. I learned better. Instead, under the final bar line, where the phrase dies mid-breath, I write a single word:

*Continue.*

Then I fold the page—not crumple it, fold it, the way you fold a letter you intend to have answered—and I set it back in the bin, on top, where she'll find it tomorrow and understand that the building itself read her music and did not laugh.

I pick up the mop. The corridor stretches out ahead of me, all that amber light, all those doors, all the unsaid things gathering along the baseboards of another ordinary morning. Somewhere downstairs, the front lock clicks and the first students come in out of the cold, clutching their cases, carrying their questions. Somewhere upstairs, a score sits in a drawer under a name that finally gets to be spoken. Somewhere in a wastebasket, a stranger's fourteen bars are waiting for their fifteenth.

I push the mop toward the light. My knees ache. The floor takes the shine.

Survival, it turns out, looks exactly like this: an ordinary Tuesday, a wet rag, a folded page, a word left behind for someone I will probably never meet. Worth its price. Worth every year of silence it cost to learn that the music was never mine to keep—

only mine to pass on.
