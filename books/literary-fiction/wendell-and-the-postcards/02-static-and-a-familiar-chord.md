## 2. Static and a Familiar Chord

There is a science to the break room, and Oscar Eastwick had mastered it the way she had once mastered the Goldberg Variations: with obsession, precision, and an unreasonable amount of private suffering.

Rule one: never sit in the chair closest to the microwave, because that chair belonged to Doreen from Admissions, who had opinions about other people's soup that she shared freely and at volume. Rule two: never make eye contact with anyone before 9 a.m., because eye contact invited conversation, and conversation invited questions, and questions were the small sharp knives of the world. Rule three — the golden rule, the one that had kept her employed and anonymous for three years — was this: always look busy, even on break. A woman reading a paperback with her finger wedged in the spine looked like a woman with a life. A woman staring at the wall looked like a woman with a past.

So on the morning it happened, Oscar was sitting on an overturned milk crate in the loading dock, holding a paperback of *Middlemarch* she had been pretending to read for eleven days, drinking vending-machine coffee that tasted like a punishment she couldn't remember earning, and listening to the rain come down on the loading bay roof in a rhythm that was almost, but not quite, a waltz.

The radio sat on a shelf above the recycling bins. It belonged to no one and everyone, the way loading dock radios do. It had a coat hanger jammed into the antenna socket, which was a solution to a problem nobody had ever actually solved, and it spent most of its life playing either static or the morning traffic report with the enthusiasm of a man reading his own eulogy.

Oscar liked it. It was the one thing in the building that never asked her anything.

She was on page 214 of *Middlemarch* — the same page she had been on for eleven days, because Dorothea kept making decisions Oscar found painfully familiar — when the traffic report ended, the announcer said something about a morning of "forgotten music, the pieces history misplaced," and the radio, with the coat hanger trembling faintly in the draft from the bay doors, found a chord.

Not just any chord.

A chord that opened like a hand.

Oscar's body knew it before her mind did. Her spine went straight. Her thumb stopped moving against the paperback's spine. Somewhere in her chest, an organ she had spent twelve years carefully wrapping in bubble wrap and labeling DO NOT USE shifted, creaked, and woke up.

It was the opening of the *Lullaby for the Unsent*.

She would have known it anywhere. She would have known it underwater, in a crowd, in her sleep, at her own funeral. She had known it at seventeen, sitting cross-legged on the floor of a studio that smelled like rosin and strong tea, watching Ilse Marchetti's hands move over the keys the way other people's hands moved when they were praying. She had known it at nineteen, when the world caught fire. She had known it every night since, in the dark behind her eyes, where she kept the things she could not afford to keep anywhere else.

It had never been recorded.

That was the thought that arrived first, absurdly practical, like a fire marshal walking through a burning building taking notes. *It was never recorded. Ilse refused. Ilse said — Ilse said the piece wasn't finished being itself until someone played it for one specific person, and since she couldn't say who that person was out loud, no machine would ever be allowed to listen in.*

*Ilse said: "Some songs are letters, Oscarchen. You don't photocopy a letter. That's how you get a lawsuit and a broken heart."*

And here it was. On a radio. In a loading dock. Between the recycling bins and a mop sink, on a Tuesday, in the rain.

The melody unspooled the way it always did — that gentle, limping 6/8, the left hand walking down like someone descending stairs they'd rather not descend, the right hand singing a line that kept almost resolving and then, at the last possible moment, deciding not to, the way a person at a door says "well" and "anyway" and "I suppose I should" and doesn't leave. Ilse had written it — Oscar had always believed — for someone she could never send anything to. Hence the title. Hence the way the final cadence landed not on the tonic but a half-step above it, wrong and right at once, like a letter that arrives with the address smudged but the heart of it legible.

Oscar had spent twelve years not thinking about that half-step.

She was thinking about it now with the full, undivided, merciless attention of a woman whose entire defense system had just been picked like a cheap lock.

*It's a transcription,* she told herself, gripping the milk crate with her calves. *Some student transcribed it from memory. Students transcribe things. It won't be right. It'll be wrong in a dozen small ways, and when it's wrong, I'll laugh, and I'll finish my terrible coffee, and I'll go back to scrubbing the practice rooms where I used to be somebody, and everything will be fine.*

The pianist on the radio — someone with soft hands and a respectful, museum-quiet touch, the kind of playing that was all reverence and no blood — arrived at the fourth bar.

And played it *right*.

Not approximately right. Not close enough. Right — with the little hesitation before the third beat that Ilse always took, that Ilse had taught her to take, that Ilse had called "the breath before you say the true thing." No transcription made from a single memory would have that hesitation. You'd only know to play it if you'd sat in that studio. If you'd been in the room.

Oscar stood up.

She didn't decide to stand up. Her body made the decision on her behalf, the way bodies do when they've been asked to sit still through one catastrophe too many. The milk crate tipped over behind her with a hollow plastic clatter that she didn't hear. The paperback hit the floor, spine up, surrendering at page 214. She took one step toward the radio, then another, her wet-soled shoes squeaking on the concrete, her hand rising toward the volume dial with the strange tenderness of someone reaching toward a face in an old photograph.

*Say the fourth bar wrong,* she begged the radio, the way you beg a doctor to say the word *benign*. *Please. Please be a stranger. Please be a stranger who almost knew her.*

The pianist took the breath before the true thing and played the true thing.

The melody climbed, hesitated at the top like a bird deciding whether the window was open, and came down again — and there it was, the descending figure, the one Ilse had hummed while stirring tea, while walking ahead of Oscar down the corridor, while standing at the window of the studio on the last afternoon, watching the parking lot, not turning around, saying, in that low smoke-and-gravel voice, *"Play it for me one more time, Oscarchen. Slower. I want to remember it slower."*

Oscar's hand closed on the mop bucket handle.

She hadn't gone to the mop bucket. She had no memory of walking to the mop bucket. But there her hand was, wrapped around the handle of the gray industrial bucket with the yellow wringer, her faithful stupid companion of three years, and the bucket — full, because she had filled it at the start of her break out of pure habit, because a bucket of gray water is a very convincing prop for a woman who wants the world to believe she is exactly what she appears to be — the bucket came up off the floor with her, and then it did not stay up.

It went down.

It went down with the full, committed, magnificent crash of forty liters of dirty mop water meeting a concrete loading dock floor, and the sound it made was so enormous, so utterly disproportionate, so apocalyptic, that Oscar would later swear the coat hanger fell out of the radio.

The quiet afterward was total.

The rain kept falling on the roof, but the rain didn't count. The radio kept playing — the lullaby had reached the middle section now, the part where the left hand stops walking and just holds one low note, a pedal point, a held breath — but the radio didn't count either. What counted was the silence Oscar stood in, ankle-deep in her own spilled water, staring at the bucket lying on its side like a small gray animal that had fainted, and understood, with terrible clarity, that she had just made more noise in four seconds than she had made in three years of deliberate, practiced, professional quiet.

*Well,* said a voice in her head, in a low smoke-and-gravel voice that was not hers. *That was honest, at least.*

"Everything all right out here?"

Oscar turned so fast her wet shoes nearly went out from under her.

A man was standing in the doorway from the mail room — a postman, she registered, one of the ones who came through the conservatory every morning with his little wheeled cart, the one with the mustache and the gentle stoop, the one she had seen a hundred times and never once looked at, because looking at people was against rule two. He was holding a rubber-banded bundle of letters against his chest the way you'd hold a small dog in the rain, and he was looking — not at her, not at the bucket, but at the radio.

"You dropped your bucket," he said.

"I dropped my bucket," Oscar agreed. Her voice came out strange. Steady, but strange, like a recording of her voice played back to her. "I'm — it slipped. The floor's wet. Because of the water. That I dropped."

*Brilliant,* said the smoke-and-gravel voice. *Twelve years of silence and that's what you spend it on.*

The postman nodded slowly, as if this were a perfectly coherent explanation. Then he nodded again, at the radio this time. "That's a nice piece," he said. "I don't know it. It's not one of the famous ones."

"No," Oscar said. "It isn't."

"They said on the news it's never been played before. The announcer. He said it was from an archive, some private recording, and nobody knows who made it." He said it simply, the way postmen say things — as information, passed along, weightless. "Funny, isn't it? A song nobody ever heard, and it turns out somebody heard it the whole time."

The lullaby was coming to its end. The wrong-and-right chord. The half-step above the tonic, the smudged address, the legible heart. On the radio, the soft-handed pianist played it and let it ring, and the announcer's voice came back in over the decay, bright and oblivious, saying something about the archive, something about a retrospective, something about a name—

Ilse Marchetti's name. Spoken aloud, in a loading dock, on a Tuesday, by a stranger on a radio, for the first time in twelve years.

Oscar's knees did something unprofessional.

She put her hand flat against the wall — the wall by the mop sink, the wall she had washed a hundred times, her wall, the only territory in the conservatory that was safely, irreversibly hers — and breathed. In through the nose. Out through the mouth. The way Ilse had taught her to breathe before auditions. *The body is a liar, Oscarchen. It says panic. You answer: not yet. Not today.*

"Ma'am?" The postman had come a step closer. He had a kind face, she noticed now, an absurdly kind face, the kind of face that made you want to confess to things — parking violations, childhood cruelties, perjury. "You've gone quite pale. Should I fetch someone?"

"No," Oscar said, and then, because rule three was the golden rule and rule three had just saved her life, she did the smile. The smile that ends conversations. The practiced, professional, dawn-bright smile that said *I am fine, I am content, I am exactly who I appear to be, please move along, nothing here but a janitor and a mop.* She could feel it settle onto her face like a mask settling onto wet clay. "Just clumsy. It happens. I'll clean it up."

The smile, for the record, worked. It always worked. It had worked on students, on directors, on the woman at the licensing office, on every human being who had ever looked at her and started to form a question.

The postman looked at the smile.

And then — this was the thing, this was the thing she would think about later, on page 214 of *Middlemarch* and every page after — the postman looked at the smile and did not end the conversation. He looked at the smile the way you look at a locked door when you happen to know there's a cat inside, and he said, quietly, not unkindly:

"That song meant something to you."

It was not a question. Questions she had defenses for. Questions she could deflect, parry, smile at until they went away. This was worse. This was a man simply stating the weather, and the weather happened to be her entire ruined past.

Oscar opened her mouth to deploy the smile's second line — *I just don't like messes, that's all* — and found, to her genuine astonishment, that the line wouldn't come. It was the first time in twelve years the machinery had failed. She stood there with her mouth slightly open, ankle-deep in gray water, a woman with no script, while the radio hissed gently into the next segment and the rain kept its almost-waltz on the roof.

The postman didn't press. He shifted his bundle of letters, and — she saw it clearly, she would always see it clearly — his eyes went to the radio, and then to her, and then to the radio again, and something passed over his face. Not suspicion. Recognition. The particular, terrible, gentle recognition of one person who is carrying something seeing another person who is carrying something, the way two soldiers in civilian clothes can find each other across a crowded train platform without a word.

"I'll leave you to your floor," he said.

He wheeled his cart away down the corridor, and the sound of the wheels went on for a long time, longer than it should have, and Oscar stood alone in the loading dock with the water spreading slowly around her shoes, listening to the announcer say the words *Marchetti retrospective* one more time, and *donations from an anonymous private archive* one more time, and she thought:

*Recordings don't exist. She never allowed recordings. So where did they get one?*

And underneath that thought, older and colder, rising from twelve years underwater like something pale and blind finally surfacing:

*Who else was in the room?*

She picked up the bucket. She wrung out the mop. She cleaned up her own catastrophe, thoroughly, professionally, the way she cleaned up everything — and all the while, in the back of her skull, the lullaby played on, the breath before the true thing, the true thing, the smudged address, the legible heart, over and over, and it did not sound like a memory anymore.

It sounded like a summons.

* * *

At 9:40 that morning, a postcard arrived at the front desk of the conservatory, addressed in careful block letters to no one in particular — to *The Staff, Loading Dock* — which was odd, because postcards are not usually addressed to loading docks, and odder still because the handwriting on it was round and patient and faintly familiar in a way Oscar couldn't place.

The desk clerk, bored, read it aloud to the break room: *"Whoever was listening to the radio this morning — the song knows you too. Ask Wendell. He delivers on Thursdays."*

Oscar, mopping the corridor outside, stood very still.

She had not told anyone her name in three years.
