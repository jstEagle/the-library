## 6. Who Owns a Song Never Recorded

The mop bucket stayed broken for three days before anyone noticed, which told Oscar everything she needed to know about how much the conservatory valued its loading dock. She'd wedged the wringer back together with a bent paperclip and a prayer, and now it squeaked on every pass like a small animal being murdered. She had come to think of the sound as company.

She could not stop thinking about the song.

That was the trouble with a melody like that one — it didn't leave. Most music passed through her the way weather passes through a town: noticed, appreciated, forgotten by Tuesday. But Ilse Marchetti's *Lullaby for the Unsent* had been written for one room and one evening and maybe four people alive, and it had never been recorded. Ilse had said so herself, more than once, in that dry voice that made everything sound like either a joke or a verdict. "No recordings," she'd said, tapping her temple. "It lives here or it doesn't live."

And yet there it had been, leaking out of a tinny radio between a traffic report and an advertisement for a mattress store, played by hands that were not Ilse's hands, in a tempo that was almost right but not quite — a little too careful, the way people play when they're reading from a page instead of remembering from their bodies.

Oscar had spent two nights telling herself she'd imagined it. Grief did that. Memory did that. Twelve years of mopping other people's music off the floors could do that to a person.

But she didn't believe it. And not believing it was worse.

* * *

The station was WQXR-adjacent, a small classical outlet that broadcast out of a converted bank building across town, the kind of place where the lobby still had a marble floor and the studios still had 1970s carpeting. Oscar knew this because she'd looked it up at the public library, on a computer she signed into as GUEST, feeling like a burglar the entire time. She had considered calling. She had even dialed once, from the pay phone outside the laundromat — the conservatory monitored its staff phones, a fact she'd learned the hard way during her first month — and then hung up when a man answered with the breezy authority of someone who had never once been accused of anything.

So she went in person. On a Saturday. In her least memorable coat.

The receptionist was a young man with headphones around his neck and the serene expression of someone paid to be unbothered. "Can I help you?"

"I'm doing research," Oscar said, and deployed the smile. The smile was a well-engineered thing by now: warm enough to open doors, bland enough to close conversations. "On archival recordings. I understand you aired a piece recently — a lullaby? I'm trying to trace its provenance."

"Provenance." He said it like a word he'd seen on a menu. "Hang on." He typed with two fingers, each keystroke a small declaration of independence. "What was the composer?"

"Marchetti. Ilse Marchetti."

More typing. His eyebrows went up slightly, which Oscar noted with the particular attention of a woman who had spent years learning to read faces over piano benches. "Huh. Yeah, we ran that last week. 'Lost Works of the Twentieth Century' series. It came from the Halloran Archive."

"The Halloran Archive," Oscar repeated, keeping her voice level while something behind her sternum began to pace.

"Small outfit, upstate. They take donations of papers and recordings, private collections, estates, that kind of thing. We license from them sometimes." He turned the monitor toward her helpfully, as if showing a child a picture book. There it was: *Lullaby for the Unsent (rec. 1998), Ilse Marchetti, courtesy of the Halloran Archive, donor anonymous.*

Recorded 1998. Two years before Ilse died. Someone had recorded it. Someone had sat in a room with Ilse Marchetti and a microphone and captured the song Ilse swore would never leave her head — and then, eleven years later, had mailed it anonymously to a small archive, where it had slept until some programmer needed forty seconds of atmosphere for a series about lost things.

"Is there any way to find out who donated it?" Oscar asked.

"Donor anonymous means anonymous." He shrugged with real sympathy. "Sometimes people don't want their names attached. Estate stuff, family drama. You would not believe the family drama." He leaned in, lowering his voice as though delivering state secrets. "One guy donated his whole brother's collection just to spite the widow. We're talking four hundred reel-to-reels. Spite is a powerful motivator in this business."

Oscar laughed — the practiced laugh, light and disposable — and thanked him, and walked back out into the cold with the word *anonymous* ringing in her ears like a struck string that refused to dampen.

People didn't donate things anonymously out of spite. Spite wanted credit. Anonymous meant careful. Anonymous meant someone who understood exactly what the recording was worth and exactly what trouble it could make, and who wanted it preserved without wanting to be found holding it.

That was not grief. That was strategy.

* * *

The Halloran Archive turned out to be three rooms above a feed store in a town whose main street consisted of the feed store, a diner, and a church engaged in a long territorial dispute with the diner's parking lot. Oscar took two buses and walked a mile in wind that smelled like snow deciding whether to commit. She told herself it was curiosity. She was aware, in the honest basement of herself, that it was not curiosity. Curiosity strolls. This was a hunt, and she had known it since the moment the receptionist said *donor anonymous* and her heart had done something complicated and hopeful and terrified.

The archivist was a woman in her sixties named Dot, per the nameplate, though the nameplate also featured a cartoon cat wearing glasses, which undermined its authority considerably. Dot wore a cardigan the color of oatmeal and had the unhurried air of someone who had catalogued ten thousand dead people's letters and found most of them boring.

"A lullaby," Dot repeated, when Oscar explained. "Marchetti. Sure. Came in — oh, must be eight months ago now. Boxed donation, no letter, no return address. Postmarked from the city." She squinted at Oscar. "You a scholar?"

"Something like that."

"Students always say that. Scholars say it too, actually. Nobody ever says 'I'm a busybody,' and honestly the busybodies are my favorite patrons." Dot heaved herself up. "Come on back. You can look at the intake sheet if you promise not to breathe on anything."

The archive's second room smelled of acid-free paper and dust that had been respectfully processed. Dot produced a ledger, flipped to a tabbed page, and ran a finger down it. "Here. Donation received, March. Contents: one compact disc, unlabeled; one cassette, labeled in hand — 'Lullaby, take 3'; assorted correspondence, fourteen items; one photograph." She glanced up. "You want to see the photograph?"

Oscar's mouth had gone dry. "Yes."

Dot led her to a reading table and brought out a gray archival box with the tenderness of a nurse. Inside, under tissue paper: the cassette, its label in handwriting Oscar knew better than her own — the sharp ascenders, the way Ilse crossed her t's like she was fencing. Fourteen letters. And a photograph of a young woman at a piano, caught mid-phrase, head tilted, entirely unaware of the camera.

It was Marta. Marta at perhaps thirty-five, at the piano, playing, luminous with concentration. On the back, in Ilse's hand: *Marta, who makes my left hand braver than it is.*

Oscar sat down slowly, because her knees had opinions.

"You okay, hon?"

"Who sent this?" Oscar asked. "There has to be something. A courier log. A shipping label. Anything."

Dot studied her for a moment — a longer look than the question strictly required — and then, apparently deciding that whatever this woman was, she wasn't a thief, pulled the intake folder. "Hand-delivered, actually. Not mailed. My mistake — the postmark notation is for the follow-up letter." She tapped a line. "Clerk on duty logged it as 'female, approx. thirty, green coat, wouldn't leave a name.' Paid the intake fee in cash." Dot closed the folder. "Green coat's not much to go on. Half the women in the world own a green coat."

"Fourteen letters," Oscar said. "From whom?"

"That's the strange part." Dot drew out a folder of photocopied facsimiles — originals kept in the vault — and laid them out with both hands, flat, careful. They were all addressed to the same person. All in Ilse's hand. All unsent.

All addressed to *M.*

Oscar read the first line of the topmost letter and had to put her fingers flat on the table to keep the room steady.

*Marta — If I go before I finish telling you what happened, know that the girl didn't mean to. Whatever they told you she said, remember that nineteen-year-olds are given scripts and frightened children read them aloud. I never blamed her. I blamed the men who wrote her lines...*

"She wrote these before she died," Oscar managed.

"Oh, sure. Dated, see?" Dot pointed. "Last one's three weeks before." The archivist's voice softened into something professional and kind. "We get donations like this sometimes. Somebody dies, and the person who loved them can't bear to throw the papers out, and can't bear to keep them either. So they give them to us. It's a funny thing, this job. People trust strangers with their grief because strangers don't have any stake in it." She paused. "Whoever brought these in — she cried in my bathroom for twenty minutes, if you want the full record. Then she washed her face, thanked me, and left. Never gave a name. Some donations are like funerals. People just need somewhere to put the body down."

Oscar stared at the letters. *I never blamed her.* The words swam. She thought of the radio, of the melody escaping into the world like a bird from a house whose door someone had left open on purpose.

Someone was letting Ilse out. Carefully, quietly, piece by piece — a recording here, a letter there — someone was releasing the past into circulation, and doing it anonymously, and crying in strangers' bathrooms while they did it.

"Could I get copies?" Oscar asked. "Of the intake information. Dates, times."

Dot looked at her over the cartoon-cat nameplate. "You going to tell me who you are now?"

Oscar weighed it. The smile rose automatically, ready — and for the first time in twelve years, she let it fall halfway and told the truth instead.

"My name is Oscar Eastwick. Ilse Marchetti was my teacher. And I think those letters might be about me."

Dot was quiet for a moment. Then she nodded, unsurprised, the way people who work with the dead's paperwork often are. "Figured it was somebody like that. Letters like these always belong to somebody like that." She stood. "Copies are fifty cents a page. Cash box is by the door. And Miss Eastwick —" she added, as Oscar gathered her coat — "whoever's releasing all this, they didn't send it to a big institution. They sent it to me. Three rooms over a feed store. You want to know why? Because nobody important looks here. Whoever it is doesn't want the music found by the wrong people first."

* * *

Oscar rode the bus back with the photocopies against her chest like a hot water bottle, reading and re-reading, and it was somewhere around the third rereading of *I blamed the men who wrote her lines* that the shape of it finally assembled itself in her mind — not the emotion of it, she'd had the emotion for days, but the logistics.

Because a retrospective required an institution. Required funding, a hall, a program, a director willing to stake a season on a dead woman's name. And there was only one institution in this city with a claim on Ilse Marchetti, and only one man currently running it who had made his career out of proximity to her.

Dr. Voss.

She'd seen the posters going up all week, actually — she'd mopped beneath them, which was its own private joke. MARCHETTI: THE REDISCOVERED YEARS. A season of lectures, masterclasses, premieres of "newly surfaced materials." Curated by Dr. Edwin Voss, Director. Voss, who had been Ilse's last teaching assistant. Voss, whose office occupied the corner room with the good light that used to be hers. Voss, who shook hands like he was handing you a gift you hadn't asked for.

Voss, who had sat beside her at a conference table when she was nineteen, sliding a statement across the wood with two fingers, saying *it's better for everyone this way, Oscar, especially for you,* in the gentle voice of a man opening a door he intended to push her through.

The bus jolted over a pothole. Oscar looked down at the photocopies, at Ilse's fencing-t crossed t's, at the sentence she couldn't yet read — the one written in that private notation, half music, half cipher, tucked into the fourth letter like a stone at the bottom of a river.

*I never blamed her.*

Then why did the sentence feel like an accusation? Why had Ilse taught her the cipher only once, years ago, laughing, saying *in case you ever need to hide something from the men, Oscar, and you will*?

The bus groaned toward the city. Outside the window, the conservatory's towers appeared over the rooftops, lit gold against the early dark, and beneath them, somewhere in that beautiful building, Dr. Voss was presumably at this very moment arranging Ilse's resurrection — choosing which pieces to play, which facts to smooth, which inconvenient witnesses to leave in the basement with the mop buckets.

He was curating her. He was curating *them*. And somewhere out there, a woman in a green coat was feeding the truth to the world one anonymous scrap at a time, and the truth and the curation were on a collision course, and the collision was scheduled for a gala in the spring.

Oscar folded the photocopies, squared their edges precisely, and slipped them inside her coat.

For twelve years she had believed the story was finished — that she had signed the ending herself, in ink, at nineteen, and that her whole life since had been the epilogue nobody reads. But stories, Ilse used to say, don't end just because the coward leaves the room. Somewhere a door had been left open. Somewhere a lullaby was loose.

She got off the bus two stops early and walked the rest of the way, and by the time she reached the conservatory steps she was no longer asking *who owns a song never recorded.*

She was asking, with a clarity that felt almost like danger: *what does Dr. Voss know, and when is he planning to use it?*

Inside, past the front desk, the elevator doors opened and Dr. Voss himself stepped out, flanked by two board members, mid-anecdote, radiating charm like a space heater. His eyes swept the lobby, snagged briefly on the janitor with the bucket — registered nothing, dismissed everything — and moved on.

Oscar smiled her smile and pushed her mop toward the practice halls.

But her hands, under the water, were shaking.
