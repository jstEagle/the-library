## 8. The Testimony File

Wendell had a theory about packages, which he shared with no one, because sharing theories about packages was how you got moved off the good routes and onto the industrial park loop, where the only conversation for six hours was with a loading dock gate that opened for trucks and not for people.

The theory was this: a package remembers where it has been.

Not in any mystical way. Wendell did not believe in mystical things, or rather he believed in them the way he believed in weather — they existed, they had moods, but you couldn't ask them to do favors. No, a package remembered in the practical sense. It remembered in its tape and its creases and the faint ghost of a label peeled off and re-stuck. It remembered in the handwriting of the person who sent it, which was as good as a fingerprint, better really, because a fingerprint only told you *who* and handwriting told you *who, and how they were feeling that morning, and whether they'd had coffee, and whether they were the sort of person who crossed their sevens.*

Ilse Marchetti's papers had been mailed somewhere. Wendell was almost certain of it. Papers always were. A dead woman's estate did not walk itself into an archive; someone had boxed it up, taped it shut, and carried it to a counter or a curb, and that someone had written — or at least printed — a return address, because the postal service, whatever its flaws, ran on the stubborn assumption that everything could be sent back.

And Wendell wanted to know where it had started. Because Oscar Eastwick had gone white as fresh paper when the radio played that lullaby, and white as fresh paper was not a color he associated with innocent people. Innocent people went pink. Innocent people got *angry* — *how dare they play that, who gave them the right* — and demanded the station be called. Oscar had gone quiet in a way Wendell recognized, because he had spent ten years watching a woman go quiet in that same way, a little at a time, as the tide of her memory went out.

He did not think Oscar was a bad person. Wendell had a reliable sense about bad people, the way some people had a sense about storms. Bad people, in his experience, did not mop floors at dawn with that particular tenderness, as though the floor had feelings. Bad people did not look at a postcard addressed to a stranger and ask, in a voice like they were handling something hot, *who writes these?* Bad people, above all, did not flinch at a dead woman's music. They *used* it.

But flinching was not innocence either. Flinching was guilt with the serial numbers filed off, and Wendell had learned — from Marta, from the postcards, from a decade of delivering small paper rectangles to a woman who no longer knew his name but still lit up when he came through the door — that you could love someone and still need to know what they were hiding. The two things were not opposites. Sometimes the knowing was the love, done properly.

So on his day off, which was a Wednesday, which was the day the postal archive was open to people like him, Wendell put on his good coat — the one without the reflective stripes — and went downtown to ask a favor.

* * *

The postal archive lived in the basement of the main sorting facility, behind a door with a sign that said RECORDS in letters so official they had begun to flake, and inside that door lived a man named Ambrose Pell.

Ambrose had retired eleven years ago. This had not taken, in the way that some retirements don't take, like a transplant rejected by the body. He still came in three days a week, sat in the same chair, drank the same terrible coffee from the same chipped mug that said WORLD'S OKAYEST CLERK, and guarded the records with the devotion of a man who had found the one job on earth shaped exactly like himself. Wendell had brought him a postcard from every route he'd ever worked. Ambrose kept them in a shoebox labeled, in pencil, *Wendell's Nonsense*, and he would deny to his dying day that the shoebox mattered to him.

"You look like a man about to ask for something," Ambrose said, before Wendell had even sat down.

"I haven't said anything."

"You did the thing with your hands. You do the thing with your hands when you want something. You're doing it now."

Wendell looked down. His hands were indeed doing the thing, which was that his left thumb was rubbing the knuckles of his right in slow circles, like a man warming up to play piano he did not know how to play. He put them flat on the table.

"I need a delivery record," he said. "From about eleven years ago. A parcel, probably large, probably heavy. Books and papers. It would have been sent to an archive — I don't know which one. The sender matters more than the recipient."

Ambrose took a long, theatrical sip of coffee, over the rim of which his eyes watched Wendell with the flat patience of a heron watching water.

"Eleven years is a long time," he said. "Records get culled. Systems get migrated. Things get lost, Wendell, in ways that would make you weep."

"I know."

"Eleven years ago I was still behind the counter at the Halloran Street branch. If your parcel went through Halloran Street, I might remember it myself. If it went through any of the other eleven branches, I'd have to go digging in the microfilm, and my eyes are seventy-three years old, and the microfilm machine is older, and it has a temper."

"I'll bring you coffee that isn't a war crime," Wendell said.

"I'll bring the microfilm," said Ambrose, already getting up. "The coffee is a bribe and I am a professional. Sit there and don't touch anything."

* * *

What Wendell knew, he laid out for himself while Ambrose clanked around in the back like a ghost with a grudge. He knew Ilse Marchetti had died eleven years and four months ago, because Marta had told him the date once, twice, forty times, the date being one of the things that stayed longest when everything else went — the way a lighthouse stays lit after the town behind it goes dark. He knew that Marta had received nothing after the funeral. Not a card. Not a box. Not a single sheet of the music she had spent thirty years turning pages for. Wendell knew this because he had asked her, early on, back when her answers were still mostly moored to the right century, and she had said, *Ilse would have sent me the Lullaby. She promised. She said when it was finished I'd be the first to hold it.* And then Marta had looked at her own empty hands with an expression Wendell had never managed to forget and never managed to describe.

So the papers had gone somewhere that wasn't Marta. And someone had chosen that.

"Found it," Ambrose announced, wheeling a cart out of the back with a box of microfilm reels and the expression of a man who had personally defeated the machine in single combat. "Halloran Street, March of that year. Large outbound parcels, logged by sender. There weren't many. People don't mail archives, Wendell. They mail birthday cards and bills and, in my experience, an alarming quantity of live insects, which is against regulations and yet."

He threaded the reel with the tenderness of a man handling a sleeping animal, and the reader flickered to life, and there they were: the outbound log, March, row after row of entries in the small patient handwriting of whichever clerk had worked the desk that month. Wendell read down the column. A parcel of books to a university library. A crate of dishes — someone moving house, someone starting over. A package of baby clothes, which made something in his chest do a small warm somersault, because someone had been mailing a baby its clothes, which meant someone had been loved enough to be sent them.

And then, third from the bottom, dated the seventeenth:

*One (1) parcel, 14.2 kg. Contents declared: papers, musical scores, correspondence. Recipient: Marchetti Estate Archive, c/o Halloran Street Annex. Sender:*

Wendell read the return address twice. Then a third time. Then he made himself stop, because he could feel his hands wanting to do the thing again, and he did not want Ambrose to see.

The sender's name was a law firm. *Harrow & Voss, Legal Counsel.* But the return address — the physical address, the place the parcel had been handed over, the place a courier or a frightened young woman had carried fourteen kilos of a dead woman's life —

The return address was a house on Corliss Street. Number 9. A narrow brick house with a lilac by the door that he happened to know about, because he had delivered to that street for six years, and because he had a habit, on slow afternoons, of noticing which houses looked like they were waiting for someone to come home.

Corliss Street was where Oscar Eastwick had grown up. He knew this the way he knew most things — sideways, from Marta, who had known Ilse's students the way a harbor knows its boats. *The little one with the hands,* Marta used to call her. *The one who played like she was apologizing.* Oscar had grown up at number 9 Corliss Street, in a narrow brick house with a lilac by the door, and she had left at nineteen and never come back, and eleven years and four months ago, someone at that address had mailed Ilse Marchetti's papers to an archive, in a parcel logged by a firm of lawyers.

"Interesting?" Ambrose asked.

Wendell realized he had been silent for a long time. The reader hummed. Somewhere above them, the sorting machines did their enormous patient breathing.

"There was a girl," he said carefully. "A student of Ilse Marchetti's. She grew up on Corliss Street."

"Mm." Ambrose peered at the screen. "Harrow & Voss. I remember that name. Not the firm — the name. Voss." He tapped the entry. "There was a young man used to come into the branch that year. Sharp coat, sharp smile, the kind of fellow who says your name too often. Voss. He mailed things from our counter maybe half a dozen times, always parcels, always in a hurry, always friendly in the way that costs nothing." Ambrose shrugged. "Then he stopped coming. People do that. They're weather, most of them. They pass through."

Wendell looked at the entry until the letters blurred. *Voss.* The same name as the new director's name. The conservatory's charming, ambitious, hand-shaking new director, who was curating an Ilse Marchetti retrospective, whose name was Dr. Voss, and who — Wendell was fairly sure, in the way you are fairly sure of a shape seen through frosted glass — had been Ilse's last teaching assistant, the one who had risen while everyone around him fell.

And the parcel had gone out from Oscar's childhood home.

* * *

He walked home the long way, which was to say he walked past Corliss Street, which was not on his route and never had been, and stood across from number 9 for longer than a man should stand across from a house he had no business with.

The lilac was still there, overgrown, leggy, blooming anyway the way lilacs do — generously, stubbornly, without asking whether anyone still deserved it. The house had new curtains. Someone else lived there now, probably, someone who had no idea that the papers of a great musician had once been carried out of its front door in a box, weighed to the decimal, and sent away.

Here was the thing Wendell kept circling, the way his thumb circled his knuckles: the parcel had been mailed *after* Ilse died. After. Which meant that in the weeks between the funeral and the seventeenth of March, someone had gone to Corliss Street — or someone had already been there — and had boxed up Ilse's life and sent it to an archive, through lawyers, and had not sent so much as a postcard to the woman Ilse had promised the Lullaby to.

And Oscar — who flinched at the Lullaby, who went white as fresh paper, who mopped floors like she was doing penance, who had fled the conservatory at nineteen and never once, in twelve years, come back until she came back as a janitor with a smile like a closed door —

Oscar had been nineteen when Ilse died.

Nineteen, and gone from the conservatory, and living — or once living — at number 9 Corliss Street.

Wendell stood under the lilac a while longer. A bus went by. A man walked a small dog that was mostly enthusiasm. The world continued being ordinary, which Wendell had always found to be one of its more comforting habits.

He was not angry. That surprised him a little. He had expected, walking over, to feel the specific heat of betrayal — the *you let me believe* heat. Instead he felt something more like the ache you get when you find out a friend has been carrying something heavy for a long time and never once set it down in front of you. It was not a question of whether he still trusted her. It was a question of what she had been trusted with, and by whom, and what it had cost her to hold it.

Marta had told him once — years ago, on a good day, one of the last good days — *that girl didn't do anything wrong, whatever they say. Whatever they say, Wendell. Remember that.* And he had nodded, the way you nod at the things people you love say when they are surfacing briefly from deep water, and he had not thought about it again for years, because that was the thing about Marta's good days: you filed them away like postcards, and you didn't know until much later which ones were the important ones.

*Whatever they say.*

He turned and walked home. On the way, he stopped at the shop and bought two things: a tin of the good tea that Ambrose pretended not to like, and a blank postcard, because he had an idea forming, the way ideas formed in him — slowly, in the shape of a route, a thing to be delivered.

He would not ask Oscar what happened. Asking was what lawyers did, and what Vosses did, and it got you a parcel weighed to the decimal and a life sent away in a box. Instead he would do what he did best. He would wait. He would keep showing up. He would deliver, every week, the small paper rectangles of a dead woman's love to a woman whose memory was going out like a tide — and he would keep a door open, quietly, without announcing it, for the day Oscar Eastwick decided to walk through it.

But that night, before he slept, he took out the blank postcard and wrote on the back, in his small careful hand, the only sentence he trusted himself to say honestly:

*Whatever they say, remember that. — M.*

He didn't send it. He put it in the shoebox with the others, in the drawer, under the socks. Some things you keep in the mail system of the heart, pending delivery.

And pending delivery, he thought, turning off the lamp, was exactly where Oscar's secret had been sitting for twelve years. He only hoped — with the stubborn, unreasonable hope that had carried ten years of postcards to a woman who forgot him every week — that when it finally arrived, it would find her ready.

The next morning, he came to the conservatory early, postcards under his arm, and found the practice hall dark and Oscar's mop bucket standing in the corridor, abandoned, still full of gray water — and Oscar nowhere in it.

She had left in the middle of a shift. And the door to Dr. Voss's office, at the end of the hall, was standing open, and the light inside was on.
