## 10. Marta Remembers a Tuesday

The care home was called Rosewood Manor, which Oscar thought was a lie of the gentlest kind. It was a converted brick schoolhouse with a ramp where the front steps used to be, a garden gone slightly wild at the edges, and a front desk staffed by a nurse named Beatrice who had the unflappable calm of a woman who had been lied to by professionals.

"Marta's having a good morning," Beatrice said, leading them down a corridor that smelled of coffee and lavender polish. "But I'll warn you — good mornings with Marta aren't always what you'd hope. Sometimes she's clear as a bell. Sometimes the bell is ringing in 1974."

"And sometimes?" Wendell asked, adjusting the small canvas bag on his shoulder. He had brought postcards, of course. He brought postcards the way other people brought flowers.

"Sometimes she's clear about things that happened in 1974, which is its own category of trouble." Beatrice smiled at Oscar, then at Wendell, with the particular warmth of someone who had watched him visit every week for a decade and had long ago stopped finding it strange. "You're the postman. She asks for you on Thursdays even when it's not Thursday."

"I know," Wendell said softly. "It's not Thursday. I'm sorry."

"Don't apologize. She'll be delighted to be wrong about the day. It's one of the few things she can still be wrong about that makes her happy."

Oscar hung back a step. Her hands, she noticed, had found each other and were wringing themselves like laundry. She made them stop. Made them hang at her sides like a normal person's hands. Made them start wringing again almost immediately.

The room they entered was small and sunlit, with a window box of geraniums that someone — Marta herself, perhaps, or a kind nurse — kept stubbornly alive. Marta Voss sat in an armchair by the window, a small woman folded into wool, her white hair pinned with the kind of clip that hadn't been sold in forty years. On her lap lay a photograph she was studying with the intense concentration of a customs officer.

"Marta," Wendell said, crouching to her eye level the way he must have done a thousand times, "I've brought you Tuesday's post."

"It's not Tuesday," Marta said, without looking up.

"It's not," Wendell agreed happily. "But the post is late. Ten years late, some of it."

That made her look up. Her eyes were pale gray, clouded at the edges like frosted glass, but the centers were still sharp, still searching. They found Wendell's face and something in her rearranged itself — not recognition exactly, but the shape recognition leaves behind when it goes.

"The postman," she said. "You always come. Even when I'm not here."

"I know," Wendell said. "I keep hoping I'll catch you."

Oscar stood by the door. She had planned, on the drive over, exactly what she would say — something casual, something warm, something with her practiced smile attached. But standing in the actual doorway of the actual room, every prepared sentence dissolved, and she was nineteen again, wet-haired and terrified, standing in a different doorway in a different decade.

Marta's gaze slid past Wendell and landed on her.

The room went very quiet. Somewhere down the hall, a television murmured about weather. Marta looked at Oscar for a long, long moment, and Oscar felt the photograph of herself that lived in other people's memories — the prodigy, the traitor, the girl from the program notes — being held up against the actual woman in the doorway, and found, somehow, not to match either.

"You," Marta said.

"Hello, Marta," Oscar managed. "I'm—"

"I know who you are." Marta set the photograph aside with deliberate care, the way one sets down something breakable. "You're the one who plays too loud when you think no one is listening."

Oscar blinked. "I — that's—"

"Every night, eleven o'clock, Practice Hall C. You think the walls are thicker than they are." Marta's mouth twitched. "Ilse said the same thing about you. She said, *that girl plays like she's apologizing and furious about it.*"

* * *

They stayed for two hours.

It should not have worked. Oscar had steeled herself for the other version of the visit — the one Wendell had described gently on the drive, where Marta asked the same question four times and grieved, fresh each time, for people who had died decades ago, and Wendell answered each question as if it were the first, because for Marta it was. He had a way of doing it that broke Oscar's heart in a new place every time. *No, your sister hasn't called yet. Yes, the recital went beautifully. No, Ilse hasn't come by today.*

But this morning — this strange, sunlit, geranium-scented morning — Marta was *surfaced*, as Wendell put it. Riding high on whatever tide moved through her mind. She drank her tea, complained that it was too weak, accepted more sugar than Beatrice would have approved of, and talked.

She talked about Ilse.

"You have to understand," Marta said, leaning forward with the urgency of a woman handing over evidence, "that Ilse Marchetti was the most infuriating person I have ever loved. She would compose at four in the morning. On purpose. She said the dark made the notes honest. I said the dark made the *neighbors* honest, and what they were honest about was calling the police."

Wendell laughed — a real laugh, surprised out of him. "She never told me that."

"She never told you anything. She never told anyone anything. That was the whole problem, in the end." Marta's eyes drifted to the window, to the geraniums, to some middle distance where the past kept its office hours. "She carried everything alone. The music, the fights with the conservatory, the — well." She stopped. Her hand, thin and spotted, flattened on the armrest.

"The Lullaby," Wendell said quietly. "Marta, we came because we heard it. On the radio. The *Lullaby for the Unsent.*"

Marta turned back to them, and something in her face shifted — the humor draining out, replaced by an attention so total it was almost frightening. Dementia had not touched this. Whatever room in her mind held that night, it was kept locked and dusted.

"You heard it," she repeated.

"Someone's playing it," Oscar said. Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "It was never recorded. I know it was never recorded. I was there when—"

"You were there," Marta said.

The words were not an accusation. That was what undid Oscar. If Marta had spat them, Oscar could have armored up, could have smiled, could have done the whole performance. But Marta said it the way you confirm an address. *You were there. Yes, that's the house.*

"I was there," Oscar whispered.

"Tell me what you remember," Marta said, "and I'll tell you if you're right."

* * *

What Oscar remembered — what she had spent twelve years not remembering — was a Tuesday in late October. Rain. The conservatory after hours, the corridors smelling of floor wax and radiator dust. Ilse Marchetti in Practice Hall C, which she had commandeered for the evening with a note on the door that read *REHEARSAL, PRIVATE, GO AWAY (AFFECTIONATELY, I.M.)*.

Oscar had been nineteen, and Ilse had been the only person in the world whose opinion of her mattered more than her own fear. She had come to the hall that night to return a score. That was what she told herself, then and for twelve years after: *I was returning a score.* The truth, which she could only now admit in the quiet of a care home in front of a woman with frosted eyes, was that she had heard Ilse playing something through the door — something she didn't recognize — and she could not make her feet carry her away from it.

So she had stood in the doorway. Half in, half out. The girl in the doorway.

And Ilse had played the Lullaby for the Unsent, all the way through, once, with the windows rattling and the rain coming down, and when she finished she had turned around on the bench — she had *known*, of course she had known, she always knew — and said:

"Well. Now it's been heard. That's the dangerous part done."

"I remember the rain," Oscar said now, slowly, testing each plank before putting weight on it. "I remember the note on the door. I remember — she was wearing that gray cardigan with the elbow patches, the one she'd sewn a pocket into the wrong place. She said she liked having somewhere to put her hands that wasn't her pockets."

Marta made a sound that was half laugh, half sob. "She sewed that pocket in 1989. Swore it was intentional for the rest of her life."

"I remember she said—" Oscar's throat closed. She pressed on. "She said the piece wasn't finished. She said it couldn't be finished until—"

"Until it was *sent*," Marta finished. "Yes. She said a lullaby you never give anyone is just a wish with a melody attached. She wanted to send it somewhere. She never told me where." Marta's eyes, sharp as awls, fixed on Oscar. "Did she tell you?"

"No." The word came out cracked. "She never got the chance to tell me anything again."

The room held still. Wendell's hand moved — Oscar felt it before he touched her, the warmth arriving a half-second before his fingers closed over hers on the arm of her chair. He didn't say anything. He had the postman's gift for knowing that some deliveries are just presence.

Marta looked at their hands, then at Oscar's face, and something passed over her expression like weather — a shadow, a gathering, a decision.

"I remember that night," Marta said. "Better than I remember this morning's breakfast, which was, I'm told, eggs. I remember that Tuesday like it's happening. Do you know what that's like? To have one evening preserved in aspic while everything around it turns to soup?" She laughed, a small dry sound. "The doctors say it's because of the emotion. Strong feeling preserves memory. So my mind has decided that the most important night of my life was a rainy Tuesday in October, and honestly, it wasn't wrong."

"Tell us," Wendell said gently.

Marta folded her hands in her lap. For a moment she looked, Oscar thought, exactly like an accompanist waiting for a downbeat.

"Ilse called me at six. She said, *come tonight, I've finished something and I need one person in the room who will tell me the truth.* I said, *you know I will.* She said, *that's what worries me.*" Marta's mouth curved. "I got there at seven. The hall was dark except for one lamp. She was at the piano with the score in front of her, and she was — I've never seen her like it, before or after. She was frightened of her own music. She'd written something she couldn't take back, and she knew it.

"She played it through. Once. And when she finished, the silence was so complete I could hear the rain individually. Each drop. Like the building was counting them." Marta's voice dropped. "And then she said, *Marta, I need you to witness this. Not the performance. The date. If anything happens to me, I want one person who knows this piece was mine, and when, and that it was whole.*"

Oscar's hands had gone cold. "Witness."

"Witness," Marta confirmed. "She wrote the date on the manuscript. She made me initial it. She said — and I have carried this sentence for years, it's the one thing the soup hasn't touched — she said, *'There are people in this building who would prefer this music never existed, and one of them is sitting on the committee that decides what exists.'*"

"Voss," Oscar breathed.

"Not our Voss," Wendell said quickly. "The old one. The director then."

"No," Marta said. "Not the director." Her pale eyes came up, and for a moment they were terrible in their clarity. "The *student*. The one who smiled too easily. The one who was always in the hallway when he shouldn't be. Ilse called him the *archivist* — she meant it as an insult. He collected things. Favors. Mistakes. He'd already asked Ilse twice if he could transcribe her unpublished work for his thesis, and she'd said no both times, and the second time he smiled at her in a way that made her lock her office for a week."

The floor seemed to tilt. Oscar heard herself say, "He was her teaching assistant."

"He was her *shadow*," Marta said. "And that night, when Ilse finished playing — when the rain was counting itself — I looked at the door, and the archivist was standing in it. Listening. He thought we couldn't see him in the dark." Marta's hand found Wendell's and gripped it with surprising strength. "And there was a girl in the doorway too. On the other side. Half in, half out. Ilse saw her — Ilse saw everything — and she didn't stop playing. She played the last bars *to her*. I watched her do it. She turned the ending toward the door like she was handing it through."

Oscar could not speak. Twelve years of not-remembering collapsed all at once, and the memory came back whole and merciless: the last bars, the melody turning, the sense — which she had buried so deep she'd nearly killed it — that the music had been *given* to her. That she had been, for the length of a lullaby, the place the unsent letter was addressed.

"And then what?" Wendell asked softly. "Marta, what happened after?"

Marta's clarity flickered — Oscar watched it go, like a candle in a draft. The sharp gray eyes softened, drifted toward the window, toward the geraniums.

"After," Marta repeated, pleasantly, vaguely. "After the recital, everyone had punch. It was a lovely recital. Wasn't it lovely?"

"Yes," Wendell said, steady as a metronome. "It was lovely."

But then, just as Oscar began to exhale, Marta looked back one more time. The clarity returned — briefly, like lightning inside a cloud — and she said:

"The girl in the doorway came to me the next day. She was crying. She said, *Marta, they're going to ask me questions, and I don't know what to say.* And I said, *say what's true.* And she said—" Marta's voice wavered, and for a moment she was not in the past at all but in some terrible present where all her griefs happened at once — "she said, *I'm scared of him.*"

The clock on the mantel ticked. Somewhere down the hall, the television changed to a jingle.

"I never learned what they asked her," Marta said, and now she was fading, the tide going out, her words growing softer and more distant. "I asked her, afterward, and she looked through me. They all looked through me, after. Ilse most of all. Ilse stopped speaking to everyone. She carried it alone, right to the end, and then she—" The sentence dissolved. Marta's hands patted the armrest, searching for the photograph. "Did I tell you about the recital? It was lovely. Everyone had punch."

Wendell placed the photograph back in her hands — Ilse and Marta, young, squinting into sun outside a concert hall, Ilse mid-laugh with the gray cardigan's misplaced pocket just visible. Marta looked down at it and her whole face changed, became simple and glad.

"There she is," Marta said. "There's my girl."

Oscar stood up because if she didn't stand up she was going to come apart in front of a woman who would then, mercifully, forget it. She crossed to the window and looked at the geraniums and breathed.

*I'm scared of him.*

Nineteen years old. Crying in a corridor. Telling the truth to the one person who'd have stood by her — and then, a week later, sitting in a conference room with lawyers and Dr. Voss's hand on her shoulder, saying the other thing. The thing that was signed and filed and had killed Ilse Marchetti by inches over six years.

*Say what's true,* Marta had told her.

*I'm scared of him,* she had answered.

And Marta — Marta had remembered it all along, held it in aspic, the one evening her mind refused to surrender. Which meant that every Thursday, every postcard, every visit Wendell had made for ten years, Marta had known exactly who the postman's quiet friend was, on the good days. And had said nothing. Had waited.

*Marta,* Oscar thought. *You've been waiting for me too.*

She turned around. Wendell was gathering the postcards, sliding them back into his canvas bag with the unhurried care of a man who had all the time in the world. Marta was asleep in the chair, the photograph rising and falling on her chest, her face in sleep unguarded and young.

In the corridor, Beatrice looked up from the desk. "A good morning, then?"

"The best kind," Wendell said. "She was clear about everything."

"That's the dangerous kind," Beatrice said, and smiled.

They walked out into the cold bright afternoon. Oscar made it exactly four steps down the ramp before she stopped, and Wendell stopped with her, and she said, not looking at him, her voice scraped raw:

"She said the archivist was in the doorway that night. Wendell — he heard it. Voss heard the Lullaby before anyone. He's had the melody in his head for twenty years, and now he's presenting a 'reconstruction' of it as scholarship, and the only person alive who can say what she heard that night is—" She gestured helplessly back at the brick building, at the frosted window, at the woman sleeping with a photograph on her chest. "She surfaces like that once in a blue moon. What happens when Voss figures out she's a witness? What happens if he decides a witness with dementia is a problem to be managed?"

Wendell was quiet for a moment. Then he reached into his canvas bag and drew out a postcard — one Oscar hadn't seen him set out during the visit. It was old, the edges soft as cloth. Ilse's handwriting curved across the front in that private notation of hers, music and cipher braided together, and beneath it, in plain ink, a single line:

*For Marta, who witnessed. Some testimony doesn't need a courtroom. It needs a keeper.*

"I've had this one for years," Wendell said. "I never delivered it. I couldn't read it, and it felt like the kind of thing you deliver only when the recipient asks." He held it out to Oscar, and his mild, patient eyes were anything but mild. "Oscar. I think Marta just asked."
