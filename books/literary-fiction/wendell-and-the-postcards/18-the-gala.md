## 18. The Gala

I had mopped the floor of Hollis Auditorium four hundred times, and I had never once seen it from this side of the velvet rope.

That was the thing about floors. You learned them on your knees. You knew which boards near the stage left splinters in the mop head, which corner by the fire exit pooled water no matter how many times you told the facilities office about it, which stretch of parquet sang a faint B flat when the radiators came on in January. I knew Hollis Auditorium the way you know a face you've washed every morning for years — intimately, unglamorously, without ever being introduced.

Tonight it wore its good dress. Two hundred chairs in rows so straight they looked embarrassed. The chandeliers polished by someone else's hands — mine, actually, last Tuesday, though nobody would ever know that and I had long ago stopped needing them to. Programs printed on heavy cream stock: *The Marchetti Retrospective: Recovery and Reconstruction*, with Dr. Edwin Voss's name set in type so confident it practically shook your hand at the door.

Wendell found me by the coat check, where I was pretending to study the program with the intensity of a woman who could read.

"You're doing the smile," he said.

"I'm always doing the smile."

"Not that one. That's the one that ends conversations." He tilted his head at me. He was wearing his postal jacket over a collared shirt, because Wendell owned exactly one collared shirt and believed in it the way some men believe in lucky socks. "This is the one you do when you're about to do something terrible to your own life."

"I'm not going to do anything to my life," I said. "My life is fine. My life is a bucket and a mop and a very reliable alarm clock."

"Oscar."

"Wendell."

He looked at me for a long moment, and then he did the thing he does, which is reach into his bag — he still carries his mail satchel everywhere, even places where no mail has any business being — and hand me something. A postcard. Ilse's handwriting, slanted and impatient even in ink: *Courage is just fear that has learned to keep time.*

"When did you—" I started.

"I've been handing you these for ten years," Wendell said mildly. "You just never noticed until recently. It's a delivery service. We're very discreet."

I put the card in my uniform pocket, over my heart, which was hammering out a rhythm Ilse would have called *enthusiastic* and my old conservatory teachers would have called *rushed, Ms. Eastwick, again.*

Across the lobby, Dr. Voss held court in a charcoal suit, laughing at something a donor said, his hand resting on the shoulder of a young woman — Priya, who caught my eye for exactly one second and then looked away with the flawless blankness of someone who had been rehearsing blankness all week. She wore green. Of course she did. Somewhere in her handbag was a USB drive containing the only true recording of the Lullaby in existence, and somewhere in her expression was nothing at all.

"She'll be fine," Wendell said, following my gaze. "She's braver than both of us. She chose this."

"People who choose things are always braver than people who get chosen by them," I said.

"That doesn't make sense."

"No," I admitted. "But it's true anyway."

* * *

The house filled the way houses do before galas — in murmurs and perfume and the small percussion of programs being unfolded. I took my place at the back, standing, because my plan did not include a seat. Marta sat in the third row between Wendell's retired clerk friend, Mr. Okafor, and one of Ilse's former students, a cellist named Ruth whose hands trembled now but whose eyes were steady as anchors. Marta wore the lavender cardigan Wendell had picked out for her, and when the lights dimmed she patted the armrest twice, the way she used to pat the piano bench before a performance began. Some habits live deeper than memory.

Voss took the stage to applause that was warm if not adoring, and I watched him become, in real time, the version of himself the room wanted. He was good at this. That was always the horror of him — not cruelty, exactly, but competence. He had been a mediocre pianist and an excellent survivor, and he had understood at twenty-six what I hadn't understood at nineteen: that institutions don't reward truth, they reward tidiness. He had offered the conservatory a tidy story twelve years ago, and it had swallowed it whole, and Ilse Marchetti had spent her last years being politely, devastatingly untidy in a world that preferred her quiet.

"Friends, colleagues," Voss began, "tonight we recover a treasure."

He spoke about Ilse the way curators speak about dead artists — lovingly, possessively, in the past tense. He spoke of her late period, her private notation, her "untranslatable sentences." And then he said the sentence I had known was coming and still felt like a slap:

"What you will hear tonight is a reconstruction. The Lullaby for the Unsent survives only in fragments — sketches, marginalia, a few bars in Ilse's cipher. From these, over two years of painstaking scholarship, I have assembled a performing edition. It is, of course, an act of humility. No reconstruction can be definitive. But I believe Ilse would forgive our imperfections, because she forgave so much else."

Beside me in the dark, Wendell made a sound low in his throat, like a kettle deciding whether to boil.

"He's good," I whispered.

"He's lying," Wendell whispered back.

"Yes. But he's *good* at it. That's the problem with liars who practice."

The lights shifted. On the screen behind him, slides appeared: manuscript pages, close-ups of Ilse's cramped notation, arrows and annotations in red — Voss's red, editorial, proprietary. And then the lights went down further, and a pianist I didn't recognize walked out — young, serious, probably told this was an honor — and sat at the grand piano, and Voss raised his hand like a conductor, and the room hushed itself into reverence.

And the Lullaby began.

Not the Lullaby. A polite ghost of it. The opening phrase was right — Ilse's falling third, that little sigh she built into everything — but the second bar had been smoothed, regularized, corrected into something with manners. Ilse's music had never had manners. Her music leaned against doorframes. It arrived early and stayed late. This version sat up straight with its hands folded, and two hundred people listened to it the way you listen to a eulogy delivered by someone who met the deceased once, at a conference.

In the third row, Marta's hand rose from the armrest.

She didn't stand. She just lifted it, palm open, fingers curved slightly, the way an accompanist's hand rises when the soloist has wandered off the page — a gesture half a century old, involuntary, saying *no, dear, there* — and my chest cracked open along a seam I thought had scarred over years ago.

Because Marta remembered. In whatever room of her mind was still lit tonight, Marta heard the wrong notes and reached to correct them, and there was no one at the piano beside her to feel it, and her hand hung in the dark air of Hollis Auditorium like a question nobody would answer.

Except that somebody was going to answer it.

I had planned to wait for the end of the movement. I had a whole choreography worked out — walk during the applause, take the stage during the confusion, speak before security got organized. Priya had timed the lights. Wendell had scouted the wings. There was a plan, and plans are wonderful things, and I abandoned mine entirely in the space between one bar and the next.

Because the pianist played the bridge — the section Ilse wrote the winter she was sick, the section she taught me once, privately, saying *this part is not for competitions, this part is for 3 a.m.* — and Voss had reconstructed it wrong. Not smoothed. Wrong. He had taken the one passage of the Lullaby that was pure grief and given it a happy ending, resolved the dissonance that Ilse had left hanging on purpose, because the hanging was the point. She used to say the unresolved chord was the sound of a letter you write and never send. Voss had mailed it. He had stamped it and delivered it and called it scholarship.

Marta's hand was still in the air.

And I found that my feet had already decided.

* * *

Here is what terror feels like when it changes its mind: it doesn't leave. It converts. Every symptom stays exactly where it is — the hammering heart, the cold hands, the sensation of walking underwater toward something enormous — but the meaning flips over, the way a word repeated too long suddenly sounds foreign and then, miraculously, means something new. Terror says *run*. Resolve says *run* too. They just run in opposite directions.

I walked up the aisle in my janitor's uniform.

Navy coveralls, name patch, the rubber-soled shoes I'd bought three pairs of because they don't squeak on waxed floors. I had considered changing clothes tonight. Wendell had even brought a blazer, God help him, hanging protectively over his arm like a rescued animal. But somewhere around midnight last night I had understood that the blazer was another kind of lie — the respectable costume, the redeemed student, the presentable witness. If I was going to say what I came to say, I was going to say it dressed as what I actually was: the woman who cleaned this building while the truth gathered dust onstage.

The first few rows didn't notice me. Then a woman in the fourth row turned, frowning at the movement, and her frown traveled backward through the audience like weather. Murmur, murmur. Someone whispered, "*Is that—*" and someone else whispered, "*the janitor?*" and I kept walking, one foot, the other foot, down the center aisle of the hall I had mopped at dawn for six years, while behind me the false Lullaby played on, serene and wrong.

Voss saw me at the same moment the front row did. I watched it happen to his face — the flicker, the calculation, the swift assembly of the charming expression he'd wear while deciding what to do with me. He raised one hand slightly, a gesture meant to look gracious and function as a stop sign. *Ms. Eastwick. How lovely. Let's speak after.*

I didn't stop. I walked to the edge of the stage, and the pianist faltered, and the music wobbled and died, and for one suspended second the entire auditorium was silent except for the radiator humming its faithful B flat.

Then I stepped up onto the stage.

It was smaller than I remembered. Everything about performance is smaller from inside it — the stage, the audience, the fear. At nineteen, stages had seemed like oceans. This one was a raft. I stood in the middle of it in my coveralls with Ilse's postcard pressing against my chest through the fabric, and I looked out at two hundred faces, and I found the ones I needed: Wendell, standing at the back with his ridiculous blazer over his arm, nodding once, slow, like a man watching a train finally arrive. Ruth the cellist, leaning forward. Mr. Okafor, hand already in his jacket pocket where the envelope waited. Priya, rising quietly from her seat near the sound board, her thumb on the play button of a machine Voss did not know existed.

And Marta, third row, lavender cardigan, her hand still lifted in the air — and as I watched, she lowered it slowly onto her knee, and she smiled at me. Not the vague, drifting smile she gave most of the world. The other one. The Tuesday smile. The one that said *I know exactly who you are, girl, and I have been waiting for you to come correct the page.*

"Dr. Voss," I said, and my voice carried better than I expected; twelve years of silence will do that, it banks itself, it gathers interest. "Before we applaud this reconstruction, I think the room should hear what was reconstructed *from*."

Voss stepped forward, hands spread, warmth deployed like a tarp over a spill. "Oscar Eastwick — ladies and gentlemen, one of Ilse's own students, joining us tonight. What a—"

"The recording is real," I said. "There is a recording. Ilse played the Lullaby complete, once, in this building, on a Tuesday night in March, and Marta Vasquez accompanied her, and it was captured on a cassette that Dr. Voss has spent two years telling everyone doesn't exist. Because if it exists, then the piece isn't fragments. And if it isn't fragments, then it isn't his."

The murmur turned into something with edges.

"And there's one more thing," I said, and here the terror tried to come back, tried to repossess me, and I let it — I let it flood in and change sides. My hands were shaking. Good. Let them shake. Ilse's music never had manners either. "Twelve years ago, I signed a statement saying Ilse Marchetti stole this piece from a student. I said it under pressure. I said it because a charming man with clean hands told me it was the tidy thing to do. It was a lie. It destroyed her. And the sentence in her cipher — the one Dr. Voss calls untranslatable — I've translated it. It says: *The one who lied knows the melody was mine, and silence is the theft's second verse.*"

I turned to the wings, where Priya stood with her finger hovering, and I nodded.

"So let's stop being silent," I said. "Let's hear her play it."

Somewhere behind me, Voss said my name in a voice stripped suddenly of charm — and the lights dipped, and the speakers woke, and out of them, thin and warm and imperfect, came the sound of a tape hiss and then a piano, and then Ilse Marchetti's voice, faintly, saying *ready, Marta?* and Marta's laugh, younger than the one I knew, saying *whenever you are, dear.*

The true Lullaby began to play.

And in the third row, Marta closed her eyes, and lifted her hand again — not to correct this time, but to follow, conducting softly, keeping time with a friend the whole world had told her she'd forgotten.

I stood on the stage in my janitor's uniform, shaking, and did not run.
