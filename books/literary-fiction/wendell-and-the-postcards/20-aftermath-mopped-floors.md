## 20. Aftermath, Mopped Floors

The morning after the gala, the conservatory smelled of lilies and panic.

I noticed the panic first, because I have a professional relationship with smells. Ten years of walking routes through other people's buildings teaches you that a hallway tells you everything before anyone does. Lilies meant someone had ordered flowers in bulk — apology flowers, or celebration flowers, and given what had happened on that stage the night before, I was betting my pension on apology.

Wendell, I should explain, is not my real name. It's just the name Oscar gave me once, early on, when she asked why a grown man would deliver postcards to a woman who couldn't remember receiving them. "Because somebody has to be Wendell about it," I'd said, which explained nothing, and she'd laughed for the first time since I'd known her, so it did its job. But that's not this story. This story starts with me standing in the loading dock at six-forty in the morning, holding a canvas sack of mail, watching Dr. Voss's assistant tape a notice to the staff bulletin board.

The notice was cream-colored. Expensive paper. The kind of paper you print resignations on.

*The Marchetti Retrospective is suspended pending internal review,* it said, in a font chosen to look humble. *We thank the community for its patience.*

"Patience," said Priya, appearing beside me with two coffees and the expression of a woman who had slept four hours and regretted none of them. "That's rich. He used our patience like a mop."

"That's the thing about mops," I said. "People only notice them when something's spilled."

Priya looked at me sideways. "Did you practice that?"

"I've been saving it since Tuesday."

She handed me one of the coffees. It was terrible, vending-machine terrible, the kind of coffee that tastes like it was brewed as a punishment, but I drank it anyway because refusing coffee from an ally seemed ungrateful. Together we read the notice again. Below the announcement, someone — a student, judging by the handwriting, round and furious — had written in ballpoint: *WE HEARD THE RECORDING. WE KNOW WHOSE SONG IT WAS.* Someone else had underlined it twice. A third person had drawn a small heart next to Ilse's name, and then, apparently thinking better of sentimentality, had crossed out the heart and then uncrossed it.

"You know what happens now," Priya said. "Inquiry committee. Subcommittees. A report nobody reads until it leaks. Voss will be 'on leave' by spring, 'pursuing other opportunities' by summer."

"And Oscar?"

Priya was quiet for a moment. Down the corridor, somewhere, a piano was being played badly by someone young enough not to care. "She got a letter this morning," Priya said finally. "Hand-delivered. Voss's stationery." She turned the second coffee cup in her hands. "They're offering her a position. Curriculum consultant. Honorary title. They want to fold her back in, Wendell. Quietly. Like tucking in a shirttail."

"What did she say?"

"She hasn't opened it yet. It's sitting on the table in the janitor's closet like a small bomb." Priya smiled, but it was a tired smile. "She's sorting papers instead. She asked if you were coming."

I checked my watch, out of habit more than necessity. My route didn't start for another hour, and Marta's postcard was already written — I'd written it the night before, after the gala, sitting in my kitchen at midnight with my shoes still on: *Dear Marta, You were right about the girl. She came back. More soon. — W.*

Some letters you write before you know they're true. That's the whole trick of the postal service, if you ask me. Nobody admits it, but half of what we carry is people mailing things to futures they can't quite believe in yet.

"I'm coming," I said.

* * *

Ilse Marchetti's remaining papers lived in eleven boxes in the back room of the practice hall, where they had been stored — officially "temporarily," unofficially forgotten — since her death. The conservatory had never known what to do with her. She was too famous to discard and too inconvenient to celebrate, and so the boxes had sat there through three directors like a relative nobody wants to seat at dinner.

Oscar was already there when I arrived, sleeves pushed up, hair tied back with what appeared to be a rubber band liberated from a bundle of newspapers. She had made three piles: KEEP, MAYBE, and a third pile that had no label but which I understood, immediately, to be BURN.

"Morning," she said, without looking up.

"Morning."

"There's coffee. It's bad."

"Priya warned me."

"She always did have your interests at heart." She held up a sheet of manuscript paper, yellowed at the edges, covered in Ilse's handwriting — that fierce leftward slant, like the notes were trying to escape the page. "Do you know what this is?"

I looked. To me it might as well have been weather patterns. "A song?"

"A fingering exercise. For the left hand alone." Oscar's voice did something complicated on the word *alone*. "She wrote these for students whose hands had been injured. There are dozens of them. None published. None performed. Just—" she gestured at the boxes, "—this. Decades of music written for people in pain, and nobody thought it was worth keeping."

"The conservatory kept it."

"The conservatory stored it. Those aren't the same thing." She set the page down gently, the way you set down something sleeping. "Voss's letter is over there. Have you seen it?"

"I heard about it."

"Then you know they want to make me a consultant." She said it lightly, but I've spent ten years reading postcards, and I know what lightness sounds like when it's carrying weight. "Honorary. Redeemed. The prodigal janitor returns to the fold. All they ask is that I sign a confidentiality agreement and let the inquiry run its course without— " she made air quotes with fingers still gray at the knuckles from floor wax, "—'unilateral public statements.'"

"And you told them—"

"I haven't told them anything. I told you I haven't opened it." She picked up the envelope, weighed it in her hand like a fishmonger assessing a suspect catch, and set it down again. "You know what the strange part is? Twelve years ago I would have signed it. Twelve years ago I signed worse things than that. I signed things that ruined a woman's health." She looked at the boxes. "It turns out the hard part isn't saying no. The hard part is trusting that no is enough. That I don't have to burn anything down to prove I've changed."

"No applause needed," I said.

She looked at me. "What?"

"Marta used to say that. About Ilse. That Ilse never wanted applause, she wanted witnesses. Applause ends. Witnesses go home and tell people what they saw." I shrugged, feeling suddenly self-conscious, the way I always do when I quote Marta, as though borrowing someone else's coat. "You walked onstage last night in a janitor's uniform and told four hundred people the truth. That's not a performance, Oscar. That's testimony. You don't need them to clap. You need them to remember."

For a long moment she didn't say anything. Then she picked up the envelope again, and this time she opened it, read it straight through, and folded it into quarters with the crisp efficiency of someone making a bed she'll never sleep in.

"They want an answer by Friday," she said.

"It's Wednesday."

"I know." She tore the letter in half. Then in quarters. Then she dropped the pieces into the BURN pile, reconsidered, retrieved them, and put them in the recycling bin instead, because Ilse, she informed me, had been a fanatic about recycling and would have haunted her otherwise. "There. Answer delivered. I'm not going to be absorbed back in, Wendell. I'm not a stain they get to launder. I quit. Formally. In writing. With a copy to the inquiry committee, so it's on record that I resigned rather than accepted their generous offer to help them forget."

I won't pretend I wasn't relieved. I was. But I also understood, watching her do it, that resignation is its own kind of grief. She had spent twelve years outside those walls wanting in, and now that the walls had cracked open, she was choosing the door. Choosing is harder than escaping. Escaping lets you blame the cage.

* * *

We worked through the morning, and the morning became afternoon, and the afternoon had that particular golden quality that October saves for days when nothing dramatic happens, which is to say the most important days.

We sorted. Manuscript pages went to KEEP. Old programs went to MAYBE. Letters — and there were hundreds, Ilse had been a relentless correspondent — we read aloud to each other in turn, because some of them were funny and it seemed wrong for funny things to be read silently.

"*Dear Heinrich,*" Oscar read, holding a page at arm's length, "*Your student's tempo is criminal and your excuses are worse. Tell him the metronome is not a suggestion, it is a contract. Also, thank you for the pears.*"

"She sounds terrifying," I said.

"She was terrifying. She was also the person who smuggled soup to sick students and pretended it was from the cafeteria." Oscar filed the letter in KEEP. "Terrifying and kind aren't opposites. Nobody ever tells you that."

Around three o'clock, Priya came by with sandwiches and news: the inquiry committee had requested the original recording, the radio station had confirmed its provenance, and two more of Ilse's former students had come forward, independently, with stories that matched Oscar's testimony down to the details. One of them, a cellist named Ruth who now taught in Vienna, had written a letter that made Priya cry in the loading dock, which she claimed was allergies, which nobody believed.

"And Marta?" I asked.

"Wendell." Priya put her hand on my arm. "Marta had a good day yesterday. After the concert. A whole evening, the nurses said. She listened to the recording and she talked about Ilse like she was in the room." She hesitated. "They don't know how many of those evenings she has left. Her doctor doesn't, anyway."

I nodded, because nodding was what my body knew how to do, and went back to sorting, and sorted very badly for about ten minutes.

Here is what nobody tells you about loving someone whose memory is leaving: you become the keeper of the version of them that stays. Marta at seventy, correcting a student's pedaling with one raised eyebrow. Marta teaching me to address envelopes properly, licking the stamp with tremendous dignity. Marta saying *no applause needed* in her kitchen while rain came down outside. Every year she lost more, and every year I wrote her a postcard she wouldn't remember receiving, and I kept doing it because the cards weren't really for her. They were for the version of her that stayed. And now, for one evening, the whole version had come back — all of her, the accompanist, the witness, the woman who had watched Ilse play the Lullaby for the last time — and then folded herself away again, and none of us knew how many more unfoldings there would be.

Oscar found me in the corner, holding a stack of unsorted letters I had been pretending to sort.

"She'll hear it again," Oscar said quietly. "Saturday. Priya's arranging it. The students, the live performance, the whole room. Marta's care home has a little hall. We checked."

"You checked."

"We checked." She took half the stack from my hands. "You've been delivering to her for ten years, Wendell. Let somebody deliver something to you for once."

I am not, as a rule, a man who cries in front of manuscript boxes. I held the line that afternoon. Barely.

* * *

By five o'clock the KEEP pile had grown into a country of its own, and Oscar stood looking at it with her hands on her hips like a surveyor.

"This needs a plan," she said. "Cataloguing. An archive that isn't a closet. Performances — real ones, programmed honestly, with her name spelled correctly on the posters." She paused. "And a class."

"A class?"

"For kids. Saturdays. Kids whose parents can't pay conservatory tuition, kids the audition committees wave past." She said it fast, the way people say things they've been carrying a long time. "Ilse taught them for free, near the end. Nobody knows that either. It's in the letters — dozens of them. *My Saturday girl improved. The boy with the burned hand played the exercise perfectly.* She never charged them. She never told anyone." Oscar looked at me. "I keep finding out she was better than I knew. Is that strange? That knowing her better makes missing her worse?"

"No," I said. "That's just love doing its paperwork."

She laughed — a short, surprised laugh, the kind that escapes before permission. Then she grew serious in the way that was becoming, week by week, less like armor failing and more like a door opening.

"I'll need help," she said. "Furniture for the classroom. Chairs that don't wobble. A piano that isn't a crime scene. I have almost no money and no institutional support and, as of today, no job."

"Mrs. Okafor's nephew runs a furniture warehouse," I said. "He owes me for eleven years of Christmas cards delivered on time. And the postal social club has a storage room full of chairs from the union hall. And—" I counted on my fingers, "—there's a piano in the basement of St. Brigid's that the choir calls 'the boat anchor.' It's out of tune, but it's honest."

"Honest piano, dishonest tuning. We can work with that." She stuck out her hand, formal as a treaty. "Partners?"

I shook it. Her hand was small and calloused and steady. "Partners. Though I should warn you, I'm keeping the postcards going. Marta gets hers every week, whatever happens."

"I know," Oscar said. "I read one, that night in your kitchen. The one that said *you were right about the girl.*" She looked away, toward the window, where the October light was going long and amber across the practice hall. "Wendell, can I ask you something? Why did you keep writing them? Ten years. She didn't remember. Some weeks she threw them away."

I thought about it honestly, because she deserved honesty and because I'd had ten years to practice the answer.

"Because memory isn't only what a person holds," I said. "It's what gets carried. Somebody has to walk the route. Somebody has to show up every Tuesday with proof that she mattered, even if the proof gets thrown away. That's the whole job. That's any job worth doing." I shrugged. "Also, Marta once told me that Ilse said the unsent letters were the truest ones, because nobody wrote them for effect. So I figured — write for the truth, send it anyway, and let the universe handle delivery."

Oscar was quiet. Then she reached into the KEEP pile, pulled out a single sheet of manuscript paper — the left-hand exercise, the one for injured hands — and held it out to me.

"Take this," she said. "Put it somewhere safe. Not a box. Somewhere it gets seen."

"Where?"

"I don't know yet." She smiled, and it was her real smile, the one that had taken me years to see and cost her years to find. "But Saturday, when we play the Lullaby for Marta — bring it. Maybe it's time one of Ilse's exercises came off the shelf. Maybe there's a kid somewhere with a burned hand waiting for it."

I folded the page carefully and put it inside my jacket, over the pocket where I keep the stamps, which is where I keep everything that matters.

Outside, the light was fading, and somewhere down the corridor a piano was being played — well, this time, played the way Ilse must have taught it, with the metronome as a contract and the heart as the signature. Neither of us moved to leave. There were still boxes to sort, and letters to read aloud, and a Friday deadline for a resignation letter, and a Saturday to prepare for, and a woman in a green coat downstairs who had promised to bring the good recording.

No applause. No committee. Just two people and a dead composer's papers and the slow, patient work of putting things where they belong.

"Tomorrow," Oscar said, rolling up her sleeves again, "we start on the letters from 1998. I want to know exactly what she wrote the week after I testified. I want to know what she knew, and when, and whether she—" Her voice caught, steadied. "Whether she was already forgiving me before I ever found the postcard."

I picked up the next box. Under the flap, on top, was an envelope in that fierce leftward slant, addressed in full:

*To the girl in the doorway — second letter.*

Oscar went very still.

"Ilse wrote more than one," she whispered.

I lifted the envelope out, and it was heavier than paper should be.
