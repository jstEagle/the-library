## 5. Almost Saying It

### Rosalind

The inventory began in the dining room, because Wendell believed in beginning where the money was.

He moved through the house with a clipboard and a pencil he sharpened himself, as though the auctioneer's people could not be trusted to hold an edge. *Twelve Chippendale chairs. Boulle clock, working. Sèvres service for eighteen.* His voice had the flat music of a man reading scripture he no longer believed in but would not put down.

"Mother used this room," Rosalind said, "for exactly two occasions a year."

"Three." Wendell did not look up. "Christmas Eve, your birthday, and the night she decided something."

Rosalind almost smiled. "You noticed that too."

"I notice everything." He said it without pride, the way you'd report weather. Then his pencil stopped above the sideboard, and for one unguarded second he looked at her — really looked, the way he hadn't since she'd come back, since before that, maybe since the summer they were nineteen and fifteen — and Rosalind felt the old machinery of the family begin to turn inside her chest: *say nothing, say nothing beautifully.*

"The reception," Wendell said. "After the funeral. You stood right there." He nodded at the window, at the light coming through the lace like water through gauze. "I kept expecting you to—"

"To what?"

The pencil resumed. "Nothing. The appraiser comes Thursday. Don't touch the study."

*Don't touch the study.* As if she needed telling. As if she hadn't already been in it, the journals hidden under her sweater like contraband, their contents burning a hole through her all night in the rented room above the pharmacy. She thought of the entry from August, the year she was seventeen — *D came to dinner again. R. quiet at table. I have spoken to him.* Spoken to him. That was all. Four words where a war should have been.

"I won't touch anything," Rosalind said, and heard how much of her mother's voice had gotten into hers.

* * *

### Wendell

The funeral had been in June, eleven years ago now, and Wendell remembered it the way he remembered most things: as logistics gone slightly wrong.

There had not been enough chairs. He had counted — forty-one guests, thirty-six chairs — and spent the first hour of his mother's reception solving this problem while everyone else wept and ate the ham salad Mrs. Pell from the church had brought in Tupperware. His father had been dead nine years by then; the grief was his alone to administer, and he administered it with folding chairs borrowed from the Elks lodge, arranged in rows on the lawn because the rooms were too small, because the house had always been too small for what it held.

Rosalind had arrived late, in black, looking like a woman who had dressed for someone else's funeral entirely. She'd stood by the dining room window — that window — holding a glass of sweet tea she never drank, and when Wendell steered her toward the relatives she performed the introductions flawlessly. *Aunt Corinne, you look wonderful. No, I'm still in New York. Yes, still writing about food.* Her hands were steady. Her eyes were somewhere else, some country with no extradition.

"You should stay longer than the weekend," he'd told her, low, near the sideboard. "There are papers. The estate—"

"I know what there is."

"There are things we should discuss." He had meant the will, the taxes, the disposition of the property. He had meant only that. He had told himself only that, so many times over eleven years that the sentence had worn smooth as river stone.

Rosalind had looked at him then, and he had seen it — the thing rising in her face like a fish rising toward light, mouth opening on air —

And Daniel had come through the screen door with a casserole and his easy laugh, calling *Wendell Joubert, you old stick,* and the fish had gone down again, down into the deep water where it lived, and Rosalind had said, "Later. We'll discuss it later."

They had never discussed it later. They had discussed everything else instead: shingles, gutters, the trust, the insurance, whether the piano should go to the church sale. Eleven years of everything else, delivered quarterly by letter, and Wendell had begun to suspect that "everything else" was not a substitute for the thing unsaid but its bodyguard.

Now, in the emptied echo of the dining room, he wrote *sideboard, mahogany, minor water damage* and thought: *I am my mother's son after all.*

* * *

### Rosalind

In the afternoon they worked their way upstairs, and the house gave up its rooms one by one like a witness surrendering documents.

Her old bedroom first. Wendell stood in the doorway and would not enter it, which told her more than anything he'd said all week. The wallpaper was still the pale yellow roses; the nail was still in the wall where she'd hung a poster of the sea. On the desk, impossibly, sat her high school dictionary, its spine broken at *silence*, or so she imagined — she didn't check, couldn't bear to be wrong.

"They wanted to give this room to Daniel," Wendell said suddenly, from the hall. "That summer. Mother said no. Said you'd want your own room when you came home from college."

Rosalind turned. Wendell was studying the wallpaper with the intensity of a man reading fine print.

"She said that?"

"Word for word. I remember because it was strange. You'd already left by then. There was nothing to keep the room for." He paused. "Unless she knew something."

The word *knew* dropped between them and neither picked it up. This was the family art, Rosalind thought — the near-speech, the sentence abandoned at the lip of the cliff, both of them standing at the edge agreeing silently not to jump. They had been performing it their whole lives. Their mother had been its virtuoso. *I have spoken to him.*

"Wendell." She made herself say his name plainly, without subtext, and saw him flinch at the nakedness of it. "That summer. Do you ever think about—"

"Don't." Not sharp. Worse: tired. "Not today. There's an inventory."

"There will always be an inventory."

"Yes," he said quietly. "That's rather the point of me."

He went downstairs. She heard the clipboard being set on the hall table, precisely, like a gavel.

* * *

### Wendell

He fled to the kitchen because the kitchen was honest. Kitchens were where the real conversations of that house had happened — his mother's cigarettes stubbed in the sink, his father's coffee going cold, the radio murmuring the grain report to no one. And once, the summer he was fifteen, a conversation he had overheard through that door and had spent his entire adult life pretending he hadn't.

*Rosalind's voice, low and cracked: "Mother, please."*

*His mother's voice, level as a shelf: "Sit down, Rosalind. Think about what you're asking. Think about what you'd be doing to this family."*

*"To this family? What about—"*

*"Daniel is blood. Daniel has a future. You will go upstairs, and tomorrow you will apologize for the accusation, and we will never speak of it again."*

*A silence so long the refrigerator hum seemed orchestral.*

*"Yes, Mother," Rosalind had said. And then, barely audible, the sentence Wendell had carried like a stone in his shoe for twenty years: "You're choosing him."*

*"I'm choosing us," his mother said. "Someday you'll understand that I saved this family."*

Wendell stood in the kitchen now, in the present, with the appraiser's dust sheets ghosting over the counters, and admitted it to himself the way you admit a debt: he had heard. He had been fifteen and cowardly and hungry for dinner, and he had heard, and he had gone downstairs and eaten the pot roast and asked Daniel to pass the salt.

Every inheritance is a debt, he thought. He had simply preferred not to read the terms.

When Rosalind came down an hour later, her arms full of journals she pretended she'd found that morning — he saw the dust on her sleeve, saw the lie assembling itself behind her face and let it assemble — he said only:

"The study can wait until after the sale. Some things shouldn't be unpacked twice."

She looked at him for a long moment. Something moved behind her eyes — gratitude, or contempt, or the place where those two had long ago merged.

"No," she said. "Some things shouldn't."

* * *

### Rosalind

Evening came down over the town like a lid. Wendell left at six, promising the lawyer's office in the morning, and Rosalind stayed to lock up, walking the rooms and turning off lights the way you'd close a dead relative's eyes.

At the front door she paused. From here she could see the whole street, the maples going bronze, the lawns of her childhood keeping their perfect green alibis. And three houses down, under a porch light just flickering on, a tall man was helping a woman from a car — silver-haired now, heavier, but carrying himself with the same unhurried entitlement, the same smile that arrived a half-second before the rest of his face.

Daniel. Back in town, apparently. Back for the open house, Wendell had mentioned — *half the county will come, they always do* — and here he was already, surveying the neighborhood like a man checking on property.

Rosalind stood in the doorway of her mother's house with the journals heavy in her bag against her hip, four words of her mother's handwriting burning through the leather: *I have spoken to him.*

Daniel looked up the street, toward the lit doorway, toward her.

He raised one hand. A neighborly wave.
