## 2. A Song With No Name

The last hour of the night shift had a texture all its own, and Bram Blackwood knew it the way other people knew the taste of their own mouths—intimately, without ever having to think about it. By five in the morning the Halloway Branch went quiet in a way that was different from the quiet of midnight. Midnight still held the residue of people: the ghost-warmth of the man who slept two seats back from periodicals until security nudged him, the faint chemical sweetness of the teenager who did her homework in the biography stacks because her house was worse than the library, the papery rustle of the retired teacher who came every Tuesday to read obituaries from towns she'd never lived in. But by five, even those ghosts had gone home or gone under, and the building belonged entirely to Bram and to the heating system's slow arterial knocking, and to the books themselves, which at that hour seemed almost to breathe.

She liked this hour best, which was a thing she never said aloud, because liking anything felt like leaving a door unlocked.

Her routine was liturgical. She straightened the returns cart first, squaring its edges against the desk as if the cart itself might otherwise drift. Then she walked the floor with her book truck, reshelving what the day's patrons had pulled out and abandoned—the great graveyard of good intentions, biographies of Churchill abandoned at chapter three, a self-help book about boundaries left face-down and splayed like something wounded. She shelved by touch mostly, the Dewey numbers worn into her fingertips after eleven years, and she did not think about how long it had been, because thinking about durations was another unlocked door.

Outside the tall east windows, the sky was doing its slow dishonest work, black giving way to a gray that pretended to be neutral but wasn't, that leaned already toward blue, toward morning, toward the part of the day Bram had spent a decade learning to sleep through. She watched it without watching it. That was her real skill—not the cataloging, not the reference interviews, not the smile she'd perfected in the mirror of the staff bathroom during her first month here, the smile that said *I am happy to help you* while meaning, with perfect clarity, *please do not ask me anything else.* The smile worked. Patrons thanked her for it. Odile, her supervisor, had once observed that Bram could end a conversation with a single expression, "like a drawbridge going up," and Bram had taken it as the compliment it was.

At 5:40 she began closing procedures. Computers to sleep. Chairs tucked in. The lights in the children's room dimmed to their overnight amber. She logged the donation box at the front desk—a cardboard carton of cassettes someone had left sometime before midnight, forty-odd tapes in cracked plastic cases, no note, no name, just a rubber-banded brick of somebody's past that they hadn't wanted anymore. She wrote *DONATION — MEDIA — UNPROCESSED* on a slip in her small square handwriting and set the box on the cart to deal with tomorrow, or the tomorrow after that. She didn't look closely at them. Looking closely at things was how you ended up keeping them.

By six she had her coat on, her bag over her shoulder, the alarm code half-entered in her head. The parking lot was empty except for her car and the wet shine of the streetlights giving up one by one as the sky brightened behind them. November cold had come early this year. Her breath made small clouds that dissolved before they finished forming, which struck her, in the flat way such things sometimes struck her, as an apt metaphor for nearly everything she'd said in the last eleven years.

She started the car. The heater groaned and produced nothing yet. And because the silence of the lot felt too large, too much like the inside of her own skull, she did the thing she always did: reached for the radio, found the far left end of the dial where the college station lived, and let some stranger's voice fill the car so she wouldn't have to.

It was a late-night program she only half knew—*The Hours That Belong to No One*, some DJ named Cass Merrow who broadcast out of wherever, playing whatever he wanted between midnight and dawn: field recordings, demo tapes, answering machine messages, songs with the labels peeled off. Tonight his voice came through low and roughened, a man talking quietly at the end of a very long shift of his own.

"—and that brings us to the part of the show I save for things I can't explain. This next piece came to me a few weeks back. No label, no note, just a tape in an envelope with a postmark I'm not going to share, because some of you would go looking, and I'm asking you not to. It sounds like a home recording. One voice, one guitar, recorded close enough that you can hear the room. I've played it four times now and I still don't know who she is. Anonymous, found tape. That's all I've got."

Bram's hand was still on the dial when the guitar began.

Three notes. Just three notes, picked slow, the third one bent slightly flat the way a certain person had always bent it flat because, she used to say, *perfect is a place nobody lives.*

The world did not go white. That was the strange part, later—the part Bram could never make anyone understand. The world stayed exactly as it was. The streetlight stayed on. The heater kept its broken groan. A crow crossed the gray sky above the library roof with the same unhurried indifference it would have shown any other morning. Nothing announced what was happening. There was no thunderclap, no cinematic swell. There was only a melody coming out of a car radio speaker the size of a coaster, and it was a melody that had existed, as far as Bram knew, in exactly one place in the universe: in a bedroom above a farmhouse outside Marrow Creek, played at two in the morning through a thin floorboard floor, written for one listener only, and never—not once, not ever—recorded.

Juniper had called it *the B song*, because she refused to give it a name until it earned one, and because Bram's name began with B, and because Juniper Vale had never once in her life done anything the straightforward way.

"You're the only audience it gets," she'd said, cross-legged on her bed with the guitar across her knees, seventeen years old and luminous with certainty. "If I record it, it belongs to everybody. This way it's yours. You have to keep it alive by remembering it. That's better than a tape. Tapes degrade." She'd grinned. "Memory doesn't, if you feed it."

Eleven years, and Bram had fed it every single night whether she meant to or not. It rose unbidden in the shower, in the shelving aisles, in the gray hour before sleep finally took her. She had never once played it on purpose. Playing it on purpose would have been a door opened, and Bram Blackwood did not open doors.

Now it was coming out of a radio.

The voice joined the guitar on the second verse, and Bram's whole body understood before her mind did—that particular timbre, smoke and honey and a slight rasp at the top of the register that Juniper had hated and everyone else had known was the best thing about her. The recording was rough. A chair creaked somewhere in it. Someone laughed, very softly, off-mic, at the bend in the third note—and Bram made a sound she didn't recognize as coming from herself, a small punched-out breath, because she knew that laugh. She had heard it ten thousand times. She had heard it last, she realized with a vertigo so complete it felt physical, eleven years ago, and had spent every day since constructing a life whose entire architecture was designed to ensure she would never hear it again.

*Anonymous, found tape.*

Found. As though it had been lost. As though it were a thing that could be lost, like keys, like an umbrella, and not a thing that had been sealed inside a burning house along with everything else—

But it hadn't burned. That was the arithmetic her mind was refusing to finish. If the tape existed, then it had been made, which meant it had been recorded somewhere other than that bedroom, or it had been carried out, or—her thoughts skidded sideways, refusing the obvious corridor—someone had carried it out. Someone had stood in that fire's path and chosen this cassette over—

She gripped the wheel. Her hands were shaking. Not trembling—shaking, a fine violent oscillation that traveled up her forearms and into her shoulders, and she watched them do it as though they belonged to a stranger, a woman sitting in a cold car in a library parking lot at six in the morning being dismantled by three notes of a guitar.

The song moved into the bridge, and there it was—the change Juniper had written the week after Bram told her she was thinking of leaving, the minor turn that hadn't existed in the earlier version, the musical equivalent of a hand reaching across a table. *Stay,* the bridge said, in the language Juniper spoke fluently and Bram had never learned. *Or if you go, take this with you.* Bram had heard it exactly twice, both times live, both times through floorboards, and had never told Juniper she'd figured out what it meant, because telling her would have meant answering it, and answering it would have meant the conversation that ended everything, and Bram had chosen instead to leave the band, leave the farmhouse, leave Marrow Creek, and let the silence do the answering for her.

Three days later, the farmhouse burned. Juniper Vale died in it. And the song had gone wherever dead songs go, which Bram had always assumed was nowhere, which she had always assumed was fitting, since she was the one who had failed to keep it alive by remembering it—except she had remembered it, every night, involuntarily, the way you remember a wound.

And now it was on the radio.

"—no idea who she is," the DJ was saying as the last chord decayed into tape hiss, and his voice had changed, gone quieter, more careful, the voice of a man standing near something fragile. "I've listened to this maybe fifty times. Whoever she was, she was better than almost anybody. And whoever she sang it for—" a pause, long enough that Bram stopped breathing, "—I hope they're listening. I get the feeling they might be. That's all for tonight. This is Cass Merrow, and these are the hours that belong to no one. Go home. Get some sleep. Or don't. Sometimes the dark knows things the day doesn't."

Static. Then the automated bird sounds the station ran at sign-off, absurd and cheerful.

Bram sat in the parking lot for a long time. The sun came up properly, indifferent, gilding the wet asphalt, lighting the library's brick face the color of old blood. Cars passed on Halloway Road carrying people to breakfast shifts and school drop-offs, people whose hands were steady on their steering wheels, whose mornings contained no impossible melodies. She watched them and felt the distance between herself and the world open up like a fault line.

Because there was a question underneath the shock, and the question was not *how does the tape exist*, though she would get to that, she would have to get to that. The question underneath was worse. The question was: *who sent it?*

A tape doesn't mail itself. An envelope needs a postmark. A DJ needs a sender. Somebody, somewhere, had possessed a recording of a song that had never been recorded, and had waited—how long?—and had then put it in an envelope and addressed it to a late-night radio show, knowing—or hoping, or praying—that somewhere in the dark, one specific insomniac would be listening.

*I get the feeling they might be.*

Her hands had gone still. That was somehow worse than the shaking. She looked at them lying on the wheel, pale and ordinary, the hands of a woman who shelved books and smiled the ending smile, and she thought: *you built this life out of silence, and now the silence has a voice, and it's singing her song, and it knows your name begins with B.*

She should drive home. She should sleep, the way she always slept, badly and briefly, in the apartment with the blinds that never quite closed. She should come back tonight and shelve books and process the donation box and continue being no one, because being no one had kept her safe for eleven years, had kept the grief packed down where it couldn't reach her, had kept the memory of that night locked away behind the same kind of door she'd locked at the farmhouse—

The thought arrived whole and terrible, the way the melody had arrived, without announcement: *the side door. I locked the side door.*

She hadn't let herself think that sentence in eleven years. It surfaced now like a body rising in water, and she pushed it down again with both hands, hard, the practiced motion, the old reflex—but it didn't go down cleanly this time. It caught on something. It left a wake.

Bram put the car in gear. Drove home on autopilot, through streets going gold and loud with morning. Sat in her spot behind the building until the engine ticked cool. Went up the stairs. Did not sleep.

She lay on her bed with the radio beside her, tuned to the dead air of the college station, waiting for nightfall the way the devout wait for a service. Because the DJ had said *I've played it four times now,* which meant there were others—other tapes, other fragments, other pieces of a past that was supposed to be ash. Which meant the envelope had a postmark, and postmarks could be read, and DJs could be found.

Which meant that somewhere out there, someone was sending her a message one song at a time, and had chosen the one frequency on earth guaranteed to find her: the hours that belong to no one.

Tomorrow night, she thought, staring at the ceiling as the light changed and changed. Tomorrow night I'll listen again. And the night after that.

And beneath the thought, quieter, older, wearing the voice of a girl with a guitar: *You have to keep it alive by remembering it, B. Memory doesn't degrade, if you feed it.*

Bram closed her eyes. For the first time in eleven years, she let the melody play in her head on purpose, all the way through, every verse, every bent note, every word of the bridge that meant *stay.*

When she opened them again, she knew she was already halfway back to Marrow Creek, and had been since the first three notes.
