## 11. Dell Harrow's Ledger

The county records office in Marrow Creek occupied the ground floor of a building that had once been a bank and still carried the posture of one—tall windows, marble wainscoting gone the color of old teeth, a ceiling high enough to make every conversation feel like it was being overheard by someone important. The vault where the money used to live was now lined with filing cabinets. Bram found this fitting. Towns kept their dead in different places than they admitted.

She had told herself she would only be an hour. She had told herself a great many things since stepping off the bus two days ago—the motel on Route 9 was temporary, she wasn't really *back*, she was just following a thread—but the thread had already begun to behave like a rope, and the rope like something thrown to her from a window above, and she was climbing whether she'd agreed to or not.

The clerk was a young man with a beard he hadn't fully earned and a nameplate that read D. PYLE. He looked up at her with the flat courtesy of someone who processed grief, grievance, and property disputes in equal measure all day long.

"Help you?"

"I'm looking for records on someone who died here a couple of years ago," Bram said. "Dell Harrow."

Something moved behind the clerk's eyes—not recognition exactly, but the flicker of a name that had passed through this office before, carrying weight. "Dell Harrow," he repeated. "You family?"

"A friend of the family." The lie came out smooth, and Bram hated how smooth it came out. Eleven years of practice. "I'm trying to settle some things about his estate."

"Dell didn't have much of an estate." The clerk was already turning to his terminal. "Heart attack, right? Out on Kestrel Road. His sister handled everything. Ruthie." He typed. "Death certificate's filed, probate closed out in—" he squinted at the screen, "—March, two years back. What kind of records are you after? Property? He owned that house on Kestrel Road outright, if that helps. No liens. Paid cash for it years ago, which around here always raised an eyebrow."

"Business records," Bram said. "Anything related to his work. He was a band manager, back in the day."

The clerk looked at her then, properly, for the first time. In Marrow Creek, *back in the day* had only one meaning, and everyone knew which night it ended on. "There wouldn't be anything like that here," he said slowly. "We do deeds and deaths and marriages. Not... music."

"Then I'll take what you have. Death certificate. Probate file. Anything that names next of kin or an address."

He printed what he printed. She signed what she signed. And when she left the marble-hushed room with her folder of photocopies, she stood on the courthouse steps in the gray morning light and understood that the county had given her almost nothing except confirmation of the shape of the hole: Dell Harrow, dead two years, survived by one sister, buried so quietly the ground had already forgotten him.

*Two years.* Juniper had been gone eleven. Whatever Dell had known—and Bram was no longer pretending he might not have known something—he had carried it for nine years after the fire and then taken it into the earth without ceremony. Unless he hadn't taken it alone.

* * *

Ruthie Harrow lived on Kestrel Road, which turned out to be less a road than a suggestion—a gravel track that left the paved part of town behind the feed store and climbed into scrubby hills where the houses got smaller and prouder the further out they went. Bram drove the rental car slowly, past rusted mailboxes and a dog that declined to chase her, until she found the place: a cinderblock ranch house with a metal roof, a woodpile stacked with geometric precision, and a garden that was either dying beautifully or being deliberately killed. Tomato cages stood empty. Bean poles leaned bare. A woman in her sixties was pulling the last of the season's stalks and laying them in a wheelbarrow with the tenderness people usually reserved for children.

Bram sat in the car for four full minutes. She catalogued her own hesitation the way she catalogued books—by subject, by condition, by likelihood of damage. Then she got out.

The woman straightened before Bram reached the fence. She was broad-shouldered, silver-haired, wearing men's work gloves cut down at the fingers, and she looked at Bram the way farmers look at weather: assessing, unimpressed, prepared.

"You've been sitting out there a while," Ruthie Harrow said. "Either you're casing the place or you're scared. You don't look like a thief. Thieves don't drive rentals."

"I'm looking for Dell Harrow's sister."

"You found her. Now say what you want, because I'm losing light."

"My name is Bram Blackwood." She watched for the reaction and got it—a stillness, a slight narrowing, the gloves going quiet against each other. So Ruthie knew the name. Everyone in Marrow Creek knew the name; it was just that nobody said it. "Juniper Vale was my best friend. I was there the night of the fire."

For a long moment the only sound was wind moving through the dead beanstalks, a dry rattle like paper turning pages.

"I know who you are," Ruthie said finally. "Dell talked about you. Not often. But when he drank—which was every night, near the end—he talked about the band. About Juniper. About you." She pulled off one glove, finger by finger. "Mostly he talked about the fire. You want to know what he said?"

"Yes."

"No, you don't. Not yet." Ruthie pointed the bare hand toward the house. "But you're going to stand in my kitchen anyway, because you drove all the way out here and because you have his eyes when you're scared—I mean Juniper's, don't look at me like that, I saw her play eleven times and I never forgot a thing about her—and because I'm too old to pretend I don't want to talk about my brother. Come on. Take your shoes off. Dell tracked mud everywhere for forty years and I'm done with it."

* * *

The kitchen was small and immaculate and smelled of coffee and woodsmoke. A cast-iron skillet hung above the stove. On the refrigerator, held by a magnet shaped like a strawberry, was a photograph of two children—a boy and a girl, gap-toothed, squinting into sun—standing in front of a farmhouse Bram recognized with a jolt that went through her like current. The Marrow Creek house. Before. Painted white, porch swing hanging, someone's bicycle flung in the yard.

"That's Dell and me," Ruthie said, following her gaze as she poured coffee she hadn't asked if Bram wanted. "Summer of '89. Our daddy leased us the place cheap because the roof leaked and he felt guilty about something I never found out. Dell was sixteen. He already had a cassette deck and a plan." She set the cup down in front of Bram with a small decisive click. "He was going to find the next big thing before anybody else did. And for about three years, he thought he had."

"He did," Bram said quietly. "He had Juniper."

Ruthie sat down across from her. Up close her face was a map of weather—deep lines, sharp eyes, a mouth that had clearly done most of the talking in her marriage to silence. "Tell me why you're here. All of it. And don't give me the version you gave the county clerk, because Pyle called me twenty minutes after you left. Small towns, Miss Blackwood. We're a nervous system. Touch one nerve, everybody feels it."

So Bram told her. Not all of it—she kept Cass's broadcasts folded away for now, kept the diary locked in the motel room safe, kept her own night at the farmhouse sealed where it had lived for eleven years—but she told her about the tapes arriving at the library, about the royalty-shaped questions they raised, about finding Dell's name missing from every account of the fire, about the county records and the dead end and the sister who kept his files.

"How do you know I keep his files?" Ruthie asked.

"Because you kept the photograph. Because you kept the garden going even though it's November. Because you took your brother's mud off your floors for forty years and you're still taking care of things that are his." Bram wrapped her hands around the coffee cup. "People like you don't throw away paper."

Ruthie laughed—one short, hard note, surprised out of her. "No," she said. "We don't." She looked at Bram for a while, and whatever she was weighing, she weighed it thoroughly. Then she stood, went to the hallway, and came back with a cardboard banker's box, its lid soft with age, its corners reinforced with yellowed tape.

She set it on the table between them like a card played face-up.

"Dell's whole business life," she said. "Contracts, receipts, correspondence. I was going to burn it after he died. Sat in the yard with it and a can of lighter fluid and couldn't strike the match. My husband came out and said, *Ruthie, you'll want it someday, when you're ready to hate him properly.*" Her jaw tightened. "Harold was right. I hated Dell properly about a year later. Haven't touched the box since."

"Why did you hate him?"

Ruthie pulled out a chair but didn't sit. She rested both hands on the box lid, as if holding something down.

"Because two weeks before he died, he got drunk enough to tell me the truth, and sober enough the next morning to remember he'd told me. And he made me promise—swear on Daddy's grave—that I'd never repeat it unless somebody came here who could prove they deserved to hear it." Her eyes were dry and very bright. "I've been waiting two years for somebody to walk up that driveway. Most days I hoped nobody ever would."

Bram's heart was doing something arrhythmic. "And now?"

"Now open the box," Ruthie said. "Start with the green folder. The one marked V.V. And when you get to the letter—and you'll know it when you see it—you tell me if I swore that oath for nothing."

* * *

The green folder was thin, which made what was inside worse. Thinness meant selection. Someone had curated this.

The first items were ordinary: a management contract between Dell Harrow and the band—no name for the band, just a blank line where the name should have been, as if they'd never settled on one, as if naming it would have made it real enough to lose. A rider from a bar gig in Ashford. A receipt for a PA head they'd bought secondhand. Bram touched these papers the way she touched rare books: aware that she was handling time.

Then the royalty statements.

They began three years into the band's life, after a small label in Nashville picked up two of Juniper's songs for a compilation and then, unexpectedly, licensed one to a film. Bram remembered that year. She remembered Juniper calling her at two in the morning, breathless, saying *Bram, Bram, they want the song, they want* — and she remembered the check that never quite arrived, and Dell's explanations, and the way Juniper's joy had curdled by degrees into something quieter and harder that Bram, nineteen and self-absorbed, had misread as artistic seriousness.

The statements told a different story than the ones Dell had shown the band. These were copies of originals—Dell had kept the label's actual accounting alongside his own doctored versions, and the discrepancy was laid out in his own handwriting in the margins, tallied and retallied, month after month, year after year. *Owed: $4,120. Owed: $6,875. Owed: $11,300.* The numbers climbed like water rising in a locked room. By the final statement, dated five weeks before the fire, the total owed to the band's writers—owed, in truth, to Juniper Vale, whose name appeared on both songs—was fourteen thousand, two hundred and sixty dollars. In 1990s money. In Marrow Creek money. Enough to fix the farmhouse roof. Enough to leave.

Bram realized her hands were shaking and pressed them flat against the table.

"He skimmed," Ruthie said from the window, watching the light fail in her garden. "At first it was ten percent off the top, which is sleazy but common. Then it was more. Then it was all of it, and he started cooking the statements, and by the end he owed her more than he'd ever earned in his life and he couldn't pay it back and he couldn't let her find out." She turned. "Except she found out."

Bram reached past the statements to the last item in the folder. It was a single sheet, creased into thirds, the paper gone soft and tea-colored with age. Juniper's handwriting hit Bram before the words did—that slanted, urgent script, letters leaning forward like runners—and for a moment she couldn't read it at all, could only hold it, the way you hold a hand.

*Mr. Harrow —*

*I know what you've been doing. I've had the numbers since August and I've given you every chance to make it right. As of today you owe me $14,260 and an apology, and I'll settle for the money.*

*If I don't have a certified check by Friday, I go to the label, then to a lawyer, then to anyone who will listen. I have copies. Copies travel, Dell. Ask yourself what happens to a man in a small town when the whole story comes out.*

*This isn't a threat. It's arithmetic.*

*— J.V.*

Bram read it twice. Three times. The date at the top swam and steadied.

It was dated the day before the fire.

"She mailed it Thursday," Ruthie said quietly. "Dell got it Friday morning. I know because I was there—he stayed at my place sometimes when things were bad with his wife, and that Friday he came in holding this like it was a live wire. He wouldn't eat. He paced. He said, *Ruthie, I'm in a corner,* and I said, *then pay her, you fool, pay the girl her money,* and he said—" Ruthie's voice caught, not with tears but with the effort of holding something heavy at arm's length for two years. "He said, *I can't. If I pay her, it's a confession. Everything unravels.*"

The kitchen was very still. Somewhere outside, the dog on the neighboring property barked twice and thought better of it.

"What happened Friday night?" Bram asked. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears.

Ruthie came back to the table and sat down heavily, and for the first time she looked old—not weathered, but tired in the marrow.

"That's the part I swore on Daddy's grave not to tell," she said. "And I'll tell you why I swore it, before you decide whether to hate me. Because what Dell told me puts a man in prison—or it would have, if anyone had ever looked. And because Dell wasn't the worst man in that story, only the cheapest one. And because I was afraid." She met Bram's eyes without flinching. "I've been afraid for two years, Miss Blackwood, and afraid for nine before that without knowing it, and I am sixty-seven years old and I am done letting other people's cowardice rent space in my chest. But I need to know something first."

"Ask it."

"The fire was ruled accidental. Electrical, they said, in an old house full of bad wiring. Everybody wanted it to be accidental. The town wanted it, the families wanted it, the sheriff wanted it." Ruthie folded her bare hands on the table. "You were there that night. You lost her. And you walked away and never once, in eleven years, asked the question out loud. Why?"

Because asking meant knowing. Because knowing meant the world would split into *before* and *after* again, and Bram had barely survived the first split. Because she had her own guilt to carry and no hands free for more. Because—

"Because I was afraid of the answer," Bram said. "And because some part of me thought if I didn't ask, Juniper's death could stay a tragedy instead of becoming a crime. Tragedies you can mourn. Crimes you have to do something about."

Ruthie nodded slowly, as if Bram had passed a test whose questions she'd only just learned.

"All right," she said. "That's honest. Here's mine." She drew a breath that seemed to come up from her boots. "The night of the fire, Dell was at the farmhouse. He told me he'd driven out to try one last time to talk Juniper out of going to the lawyer—to buy time, offer her a little money, anything. He parked down the road because he didn't want the others seeing his car. And while he was sitting there, he saw someone come out the back door of that house, quick, in the dark, and get in a vehicle and leave. And ten minutes later the windows lit up orange."

Bram's blood went cold and stayed there. "Who? Who did he see?"

"And fifteen minutes after that," Ruthie went on, relentless, "when the fire trucks were coming up the valley road, a car pulled up alongside Dell's, window down, and a voice he knew offered him money to forget what he'd seen. Fifty thousand dollars, over two years, in cash. Dell took it. Bought the house on Kestrel Road with the first payment and hated himself with every board of it until the day his heart quit." She leaned forward. "Now. Before I say the name—and understand, girl, I will say it, I've just been waiting for someone worth saying it to—I need to know what you'll do with it. Because the law had its chance. The law looked at that fire for three weeks and wrote *accidental* and went home to supper. Whatever happens next won't happen because of the law. It'll happen because of you. So I'm asking you plain: what are you willing to lose?"

Bram thought of the library at night, the shelves, the smile that ended conversations, the eleven-year apartment of her own erasure. She thought of Juniper's handwriting leaning forward like a runner. She thought of a song with no name playing to strangers in the dark.

"Everything," she heard herself say. "I already lost everything once. Turns out it's survivable. Nobody tells you that."

Ruthie Harrow studied her for a long moment, and then something in her face shifted—grief and relief arriving together, like weather from opposite directions meeting overhead.

"Get your coat," she said, standing. "Not here. Not in my kitchen. Some names you say outdoors, where they can blow away if they need to." She was already reaching for her keys. "Besides—if I'm going to burn my brother's oath, I'm going to need a cigarette, and Harold's grave is the only place I ever smoke."

* * *

They drove up through the failing light to a small churchyard on the hill above Kestrel Road, where the stones leaned like tired congregation members and the valley below was beginning to show its lights—Marrow Creek, half-gentrified, half-rotted, keeping its secrets the way it kept its streetlamps: unevenly, and mostly out of habit.

Ruthie stood at a modest granite marker, lit a cigarette with hands that did not shake, and exhaled toward the darkening sky.

"Daddy," she said conversationally, "you remember what I promised. I'm breaking it. Dell would understand. He was too much of a coward to do it himself, and somebody has to." She took another drag and turned to Bram, and in the last of the daylight her eyes were absolutely clear.

"The person Dell saw leaving the back of that house," she said, "the one who paid him fifty thousand dollars to bury it—the voice in the car on the fire road—"

She said the name.

And the hillside tilted, and eleven years of careful architecture came down inside Bram Blackwood all at once, silently, the way buildings fall in dreams.
