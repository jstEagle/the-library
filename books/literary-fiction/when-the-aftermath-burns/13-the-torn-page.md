## 13. The Torn Page

Cass Merrow had learned to read the weather in his own chest the way farmers once read the sky. A pressure in the west. A heaviness gathering behind the sternum. Some mornings the storm passed without breaking; some mornings he woke already soaked in the cold sweat of a heart that had stumbled in the night and caught itself, barely, like a man on ice. The cardiologist in Halloway—twenty miles and a whole former life away—had used words like *manageable* and *lifestyle adjustments*, words designed to be forgotten. What Cass remembered was the other thing the doctor had said, offhand, while writing the prescription: *Most men in your condition get a warning. Not all of them recognize it.*

He recognized it now. It came every time he picked up the diary.

He kept it in a ammunition box he'd bought at a surplus store in 1998, back when the box held nothing more dangerous than guitar strings and a roach clip. The tin sat on a shelf in the barn studio, between a dead hard drive and a mason jar of screws that fit nothing he owned anymore. Water had gotten to the diary long ago—the night of the fire, he suspected, or the rains that came after, when the farmhouse sat open to the sky like a skull—and the pages had swollen and dried and swollen again, so that the paper felt less like paper now than like bark, like something that had grown rather than been written. Juniper's handwriting survived inside it anyway, blue ink gone gray at the edges but stubborn, slanting uphill the way it always had, as if every sentence she wrote was trying to get somewhere faster than the page would allow.

He had read it so many times he could have recited it. He had read it so many times that the reading had stopped working, the way a word repeated stops meaning anything, and he had begun, in the last year, to hear it again—really hear it—only when Bram Blackwood was somewhere on the other side of his fence, or in the parking lot of the gas station, or standing in the archive of the historical society with her careful librarian's face on, pretending to research drainage easements.

He knew she was coming today. She'd called the station line the night before, and her voice had done the thing voices do when they're holding something at arm's length and their arms are tired. *I need to ask you about the diary, Cass. All of it. The parts you've played and the parts you haven't.* And then, before he could perform surprise: *Don't bother rehearsing. I catalog things for a living. I know what a gap looks like.*

So he spent the morning doing what he always did before a broadcast, which was to say he spent the morning dying in small installments. He cleaned the mixing board with a cloth that was filthier than the board. He straightened the tapes he'd already straightened. He took his pills with the last of the coffee and sat very still and counted his pulse the way his doctor had taught him—fifteen seconds, times four—and got a number that meant *proceed with caution*, which was, these days, the only weather report he trusted.

The barn smelled of dust and old solder and, faintly, of hay, though no animal had lived here in decades. Through the open door he could see the fence line, the gravel drive, the road beyond it where the town of Marrow Creek went about its business of forgetting. Three weeks until the demolition crews came for the farmhouse. Three weeks until the last physical evidence that any of them had ever been young was scraped into a dumpster and hauled off to the county landfill. He had been telling himself for a year that the broadcasts were a countdown to courage. He was beginning to suspect they were a countdown to something else entirely.

At 11:58 he opened the microphone out of habit, though he wasn't scheduled until seven that night, and the red ON AIR light bloomed in the empty barn like a small, patient wound. He spoke to nobody. It was a thing he did. A rehearsal for the confession he'd been circling for eleven years.

"This is Cass Merrow," he said to the empty room, "and I have something to tell you about a page."

The light stayed red. Nobody heard. That was the trick of it, he thought. That was the whole trick of cowardice: it looked exactly like preparation, right up until the moment it looked like nothing at all.

* * *

Bram's truck came up the drive at two in the afternoon, in the flat white light that October gave this valley when it couldn't be bothered to be beautiful. She parked at the fence the way she always did, as if the gravel itself might be wired, and got out, and stood there a moment with her hands in the pockets of a coat too thin for the season. Eleven years in the city and she still dressed for a town she'd left. He noticed things like that now. Dying concentrated the attention wonderfully.

He met her at the gate. Up close she had the look of a woman who had been awake for a long time in the way that has nothing to do with hours—awake the way the dead in old stories are awake, aware and unable to act. Her eyes went past him to the barn, cataloging. He wondered what call numbers she'd assigned him.

"You look terrible," she said. It wasn't cruelty. It was inventory.

"Heart's going," Cass said. "Has been for a while. I figure I'll outlive the farmhouse by a week or two, and we'll call it even."

Something moved through her face—pity, or its cousin—and he watched her decide not to offer it, because he could see her doing the arithmetic of what pity would cost them both. Good. He'd have hated to be forgiven in advance. It made the confession too easy, and God knew he'd earned the hard version.

Inside the barn he made coffee he didn't want and she didn't drink, because that was the liturgy of these things, and then he sat on the amp case and she sat on the folding chair across from him, and between them, on the mixing board where the faders made a little skyline, he set the ammunition tin.

"I found it in the spring of '15," he said. "Fourteen years after. The county had finally opened the site up—some easement dispute, nobody cared—and I went out there with a metal detector like an idiot, like a tourist of my own disaster. And I didn't find anything with the detector. I found it because the piano had been dragged halfway out the back door, and the seat was under it, and the seat had a false bottom Juniper built herself, for—God, I don't even know. Smokes, maybe. Song fragments she didn't want us hearing early. The diary was inside."

He opened the tin. The smell came up out of it, mildew and old smoke, a smell that had no name in English and therefore had to be described by everything it wasn't. Bram didn't reach for the book. She looked at it the way you look at a dog you've been told will bite.

"You've been playing from it," she said. "On air. Her words."

"Her words, my voice. Yeah." He rubbed his face. "I told myself it was tribute. A séance with a transmitter. And maybe it started that way. But you called me last night and used the word *gap*, and I've been sitting here since four in the morning knowing you were right, because there is a gap, Bram. There's a gap in this book the size of a page, and I put it there."

The barn was very quiet. Out in the yard, a crow said something dismissive and left.

"The last entry," Cass said. "Dated the day of the fire. I tore it out in 2015, in my truck, in the parking lot of the Feed & Seed, and I burned it in the ashtray, and I have thought about it every single day since, which I understand is not a defense, it's just a sentence I've been serving."

Bram's hands had gone still in her lap. Still the way water goes still. "Read it to me," she said. "You kept it."

And there it was—the thing he had both dreaded and, in some shameful cellar of himself, hoped for. Because of course he'd kept it. He had folded it into a foil gum wrapper and it had lived in his wallet for nine years, through the trailer in Knoxville and the rehab in Chattanooga and the long gray years of driving a delivery route for a medical supply company, delivering oxygen concentrators to dying men and thinking, *at least I'm not the worst coward on this route.* He took the wallet out now. His hands were shaking, and he let her see them shake, because hiding them would have been one more page torn out.

"I didn't read it to you," he said. "You're going to read it yourself. And then you're going to hate me, and you should know that I've already agreed with you, in advance, at length."

* * *

The foil wrapper opened like a small, terrible flower. The page inside had been folded and refolded so many times the creases had gone to felt, and the ink had transferred in ghost-letters to its own reverse, so that Juniper's words existed twice, once in the living hand and once as a shadow of itself. Bram held it under the barn's one working lamp and read.

Cass watched her read. He had a long time to watch, because she read it three times, her lips moving slightly on the third pass, and what he saw on her face was not the grief he'd expected. It was recognition. That was worse. That was so much worse.

*Theo knows,* the page said, in that uphill-slanting hand. *I told him everything tonight—the ledger, Dell, the numbers, all of it. He didn't try to talk me out of it. That's the strange part. I thought he'd tell me to let it go, take the money, keep the peace. He just listened and then he said, "Then we do it right." We. Like it's ours now. Like he's finally on the field instead of in the stands. Meeting tomorrow night at the house. I'm bringing everything. If I'm wrong about him I'll never forgive myself, but I'm not wrong. I've been wrong about a lot of people but not him. He's the only one who never wanted anything from the music except for it to be true.*

Bram set the page down on the mixing board, very carefully, the way you set down something that might still go off.

"She trusted him," Bram said. Her voice came out level, which he understood had cost her something enormous. "The night she died, she was walking into that farmhouse believing she had an ally. Believing *we*."

"Yes."

"And you tore this out. Why?" And then, before he could assemble the lie, because he could see the lie assembling itself in her face too—she was a librarian, she could smell a misfiled thing at forty paces—she said, "No. Don't give me the version where you were protecting her memory. Nobody burns a page to protect a memory. You burn a page to protect a man."

The crow was back on the fence post. Cass wished it would shut up.

"Because it named Theo," he said. "Not as the one who struck the match. I want to be clear about that, Bram, because I have gone over it ten thousand times and I will go over it ten thousand more: there is nothing in that page, nothing in the diary, nothing I ever saw, that says Theo Vance set that fire. What it says is that Juniper trusted him with the one thing she should have trusted no one with—her plan, her evidence, her timing. She told him where she'd be and when and what she'd be carrying. And if somebody used that—" His voice went, and he had to stop and look at the dead hard drive on the shelf until it came back. "If somebody used that, then the page isn't a memorial. It's a map. And I knew who it pointed to, and I decided—I *decided*, in a truck, over an ashtray—that Marrow Creek had suffered enough, that Theo had suffered enough, that *I* had suffered enough, and that the truth was a luxury the town couldn't afford."

"That's not why," Bram said quietly.

"No," Cass said. "That's not why."

He got up because sitting was unbearable, and walked to the open door, and stood looking out at the road, at the fence, at the far ridge where the farmhouse's chimney was still visible on clear days, a gray finger pointing at nothing.

"The real why is that I was already bought," he said. "That's the thing. You want the whole ugly architecture, so here it is. After the fire, when the insurance people and the sheriff's people came around, I told them I'd left the rehearsal early. That part was true. What I didn't tell them was why I left, or what I saw in the driveway on my way out. Dell Harrow's car. Sitting there with the lights off. Dell, who was supposed to be in Nashville. I saw it, Bram. I saw it and I said nothing, because Dell had been paying me—small money, insulting money, *hush* money in every way but the word—for two years to look the other way on the royalty skim. I was on his payroll the night his car was in that driveway, and if I said his name, I'd have to explain why I knew what his car looked like parked in the dark, and I'd have had to explain what I'd taken, and I was twenty-four years old and a coward with a drug habit and I chose the version of the story where I was just a sad boy who left early."

He turned around. Bram hadn't moved. The page lay between them on the board, its shadow-self bleeding through from the back.

"So when I read that last entry in 2015," Cass said, "and I saw her writing *we* about Theo Vance, I understood that the truth had a shape, and the shape was a chain, and I was a link in it. Tear out the page, break the chain. If the page named Theo, then someone would ask how Theo knew, and someone would ask what Theo did with what he knew, and somewhere down that road a sheriff's deputy with a fresh cup of coffee would ask Cass Merrow what *he* knew. I didn't protect Theo. I protected the door I'd hidden behind. I tore out her last words to buy myself one more year of being a man who'd merely lost everything instead of a man who'd helped bury it."

The words came out of him and kept coming, and he let them, because he had learned in the last year that a confession is like a hemorrhage—you don't get to do it in installments.

"And the worst of it," he said, "the part I can't dress up, is that some nights it *worked*. Some nights the page was out of the book and the book was in the tin and the tin was on the shelf and I was just a DJ playing dead girls' songs to insomniacs, and I'd feel—God help me—*lighter*. That's what I need you to understand about me, Bram. It wasn't agony. The agony was the tax. The relief was the wage. Eleven years of relief, paid out nightly, and every night a little less, until you started showing up at my fence and the whole account came due."

Bram stood. She picked up the page. He flinched—he couldn't help it—and she saw him flinch, and something in her face shifted, not toward mercy exactly, but toward the country adjacent to it.

"I'm not going to hate you," she said. "I want to. I've been practicing since I got out of the truck. But I sat in a library for eleven years not listening to tapes because listening was a thing I'd decided I didn't deserve, so I know exactly what the relief costs and I know exactly how it feels when the bill comes." She folded the page along its old creases, gently, like closing a hand. "I'm going to ask you one question, Cass, and I need you to not protect me from the answer the way you protected him."

"Ask it."

"If Theo knew she was coming that night—if he knew what she was carrying and when—" Bram's voice held, barely. "Then she didn't die because the fire was fast. She died because someone knew exactly which room she'd be standing in."

The barn's red ON AIR light was still glowing over the dead console, patient as a pilot flame, and Cass looked at it and thought: *that's it, that's the warning the doctor told me about,* and it wasn't in his chest at all. It was in her question, hanging in the dust.

"I don't know what Theo did that night," he said. "But I know who else does. Because I wasn't the only one in that driveway with something to hide—and Ruthie Harrow has been sitting on her brother's files for two years, waiting for somebody brave enough to knock."

Bram was already reaching for her keys.
