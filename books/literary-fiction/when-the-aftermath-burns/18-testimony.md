## 18. Testimony

The county courthouse in Marrow Creek had been built in the same optimistic decade as the library where Bram had spent eleven years hiding, and it shared the same architecture of pretense—tall windows meant to suggest transparency, stone eagles meant to suggest vigilance, a rotunda meant to suggest that justice was a kind of light. Bram stood beneath the dome on a Tuesday morning in October and understood, with a clarity that felt almost physical, that none of it was true. Light did not fall evenly in this building. It pooled where it was wanted and left the rest in shadow, and everyone who walked through these doors learned quickly which pools to stand in.

She had dressed carefully, the way Odile had instructed her over soup two nights earlier. "Not your funeral clothes," Odile had said, ladling broth with the authority of a woman who had buried a husband and outlived three town councils. "And not your librarian clothes either. You're not going there to disappear. Wear something that looks like you decided to show up."

So Bram wore the gray blazer she'd bought for Juniper's memorial service eleven years ago and never worn since—the tags still pinned to the lapel until this morning—and beneath it a shirt the color of juniper berries, which she had chosen at seven a.m. in a fit of something she refused to call sentiment and which Ruthie Harrow, waiting beside her on the courthouse steps, identified immediately.

"Good," Ruthie said. "Wear her into the room."

* * *

The reopened inquiry occupied a hearing room on the second floor, presided over by an investigator named Althea Grant, who had come up from the state fire marshal's cold case division with a reputation for exhuming things other people preferred buried. She was fifty-ish, square-built, with reading glasses pushed up into gray-streaked hair and a manner that suggested she had heard every lie the human throat could produce and rated most of them as mediocre.

Bram had met her once before, briefly, in a phone call that lasted forty minutes and during which Bram had said almost nothing. Now Grant looked up from her files as Bram entered, and there was no warmth in the look and no hostility either—only the flat attention of someone preparing to weigh something.

"Ms. Blackwood." She gestured to the chair across the table. "You understand you're here voluntarily. You're not under subpoena. You can have counsel present if you want it."

"I don't want counsel."

Grant studied her for a moment. "Most people do."

"Most people are trying to protect something." Bram sat down. The chair was hard, institutional, angled so that its occupant could never quite relax. She thought: they design these rooms so that comfort is impossible. Good. Comfort would be an insult today.

"And what are you protecting?"

"Nothing anymore." Her voice came out steadier than she expected. "That's why I'm here."

Grant opened a legal pad, uncapped a pen, and then—Bram noticed—did something small and deliberate: she turned off the recorder sitting on the table between them.

"Before we start the record," Grant said, "I'm going to tell you what I already know, so you understand the shape of the room you're walking into. Dell Harrow's sister has given us a statement regarding his deathbed confession—that he was present at the Vale farm on the night of March ninth, eleven years ago; that he observed an unidentified individual exiting through the rear of the structure shortly before the fire began; and that he accepted payment in exchange for silence. We have the royalty ledger. We have the letter Ms. Vale wrote Mr. Harrow dated March eighth. We have a diary fragment recovered from the scene, and we have testimony from Cassius Merrow regarding the circumstances of its recovery—including the removal of a final page, which he has now surrendered to us intact." She paused. "We also have a statement from Theodore Vance's attorney indicating that Mr. Vance intends to cooperate fully with the inquiry, provided certain conditions regarding timing and publicity are met."

"He wants to testify first," Bram said. "Control the narrative before I can contradict it."

"That is generally how cooperation works when it's purchased rather than offered." Grant clicked the pen once. "Which brings me to you. Your name appears nowhere in the original investigation. Not as a witness, not as a person of interest, not even in the list of known associates. Eleven years ago, a young woman died in a fire along with two other members of her band, and her closest friend—a woman who lived in that house, who was seen in town that afternoon buying gasoline for a generator—simply evaporated from the record. You want to explain that to me before we go on tape? Because I've read enough statements to know the difference between someone coming forward and someone curating their arrival."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, a door slammed; the building's bones carried sound like a ship's hull.

Bram had rehearsed this moment for weeks—not the words, which changed daily, but the act itself, the mechanics of opening her mouth and letting the truth come out in order. She had imagined it as a kind of surgery, and she had been right about that. What she hadn't anticipated was how much it would feel like drowning in reverse: instead of water rushing in, everything she'd held down for eleven years rising at once toward the light.

"The generator gas was mine," she said. "I bought it that afternoon because the power kept cutting out and Juniper wanted to record that night. She always wanted to record. She said songs were birds—you could keep one in a cage but it would die singing, or you could open the window and hope it came back." Her hands were flat on the table. She watched them as if they belonged to someone calmer. "I left the farmhouse around ten-thirty. Juniper and I had fought. She wanted me to stay in the band and I wanted—I told her I wanted to leave. That wasn't entirely true either. What I wanted was for her to choose me over the band, and I was too much of a coward to say so, so I made it about the music."

"And the door."

Bram looked up. Grant's face gave nothing away, but the pen had stopped moving.

"When I left," Bram said, "I locked the side door behind me. It was a habit—from the city, from years of living alone in places where locking the door was the difference between sleeping and listening all night. The bolt stuck sometimes; you had to really throw it. I threw it." She heard her own voice go strange, distant, as though the woman speaking were standing slightly outside herself, reporting. "It was the only key. Juniper kept hers on a nail by the piano and she was forever losing it, so she'd taken to leaving the side door unlocked when the whole band was there. But I didn't know if everyone was staying. I didn't think. I locked it because locking doors was what I did instead of feeling things, and I got in my car, and I drove back to town, and at some point after midnight the farmhouse caught fire, and three people were inside, and the side door was bolted."

"You don't know that anyone tried to use it."

"No. I don't. The official report says smoke inhalation. It says they may never have woken." Bram's throat closed and she worked past it, jaw tight. "But I don't know. That's the thing I need you to write down. Not that the door killed them—I can't prove that, and maybe it didn't, and God knows I've spent eleven years trying every version of that sentence hoping one of them would let me sleep. But I locked it, and I never told anyone, and I disappeared from the record on purpose because I couldn't bear to be asked the questions whose answers I didn't have. So when you ask me whether I'm curating my arrival—" she exhaled— "yes. For eleven years. This is the first day I haven't."

Grant was silent for a long moment. Then she reached forward and turned the recorder back on.

"For the record," she said, "state your full name."

"Bramwell June Blackwood."

"June," Grant repeated, glancing at her file. "You don't use it."

"It belonged to both of us," Bram said. "Juniper and me. We were born six weeks apart in the same hospital. Our mothers were friends. When she died, I put the name away like a coat that wouldn't fit anymore." She folded her hands. "Ask me anything. Start anywhere. I'm done running the other direction."

* * *

They went for four hours.

Grant took her through the night in forensic increments—the fight, word for word as Bram could reconstruct it; the drive back to Halloway; the weeks after, when the sheriff's office interviewed her twice and she told them nothing false but nothing complete, a distinction she now understood was its own species of lie. Grant asked about Dell Harrow, and Bram described the man only in glimpses: at the edge of rehearsals, counting something always, his smile arriving a half-second late like thunder after lightning. She asked about Theo Vance, and here Bram made herself slow down, made herself be exact, because exactness was the only currency she had left to spend.

"He loved her," Bram said. "Or he loved what she made him—she wrote him into being somebody larger than he'd have been otherwise. And when she started pulling at the money thread, when she figured out Dell was skimming and started talking about going public, he panicked. Not because he stole anything. Because the band was the only version of himself he could afford to lose everything else to protect." She paused. "That's my theory. I want to be clear about what's theory and what I know. I know what the ledger shows. I know what Ruthie heard from her brother. I know what page was torn out of that diary and what it said. The rest—the lamp, the fight, the choice not to call it in—that's his to give you. I just want you to know I'll be here to answer against it if it doesn't match."

"You're offering yourself as a check on his testimony."

"I'm offering myself as a witness," Bram said. "The first honest one this county's had in eleven years. Someone should set the standard."

When it ended, near dusk, Grant capped her pen and regarded Bram across the table with an expression that had thawed perhaps two degrees.

"For what it's worth," Grant said, "in twenty-two years I've taken maybe five statements like that. Where somebody walks in carrying more weight than the law requires and puts it all down anyway." She gathered her files. "It won't go easy for you. The prosecutor will look at that door. There may be charges—reckless endangerment at minimum, depending on what the fire reconstruction shows about egress. You understand that."

"I understand that better than anyone alive."

"Then why?" It wasn't hostile. It was genuine curiosity, the question of a woman who had watched too many people choose the comfortable lie and wanted, once, to see the machinery of the other choice up close.

Bram stood, and her knees ached, and she realized she'd been clenching every muscle in her body for four hours.

"Because Juniper Vale wrote a song for me," she said, "and I am not going to be the reason it stays anonymous."

* * *

They arrested Theo Vance on a Thursday.

Bram learned about it the way the town learned about everything now—through Cass Merrow's low-wattage signal bleeding out of a barn on the ridge, through Ruthie's phone ringing before dawn, through the peculiar electricity that moves through a small place when its agreed-upon story finally collapses. She was at Odile's kitchen table, drinking coffee she didn't taste, when the scanner on Odile's counter crackled with the dispatch: units requested at the development office on Chestnut Street, no lights, no sirens.

"No lights, no sirens," Odile repeated, turning the dial down. "Somebody in that department has manners. Or shame."

"They arrested him at work," Ruthie said. She'd driven over from her place on the far side of the creek, arriving with the particular grim satisfaction of a woman who had waited two years to watch a debt come due. "In front of the interns. In front of the plaque with his Citizen of the Year picture on it."

Obstruction of justice. Involuntary manslaughter. The words had come through Grant's office the evening before, relayed to Bram in a phone call that lasted ninety seconds: the fire reconstruction had shown what Bram had always feared and never proven—that the rear stairwell filled faster than the report allowed, that the side door's bolt showed stress marks consistent with force applied from within, that someone had tried, in the last minutes, to get out the way Bram had sealed it. The state's theory was not that Theo struck the match intending death. It was that a confrontation with Dell Harrow had gone wrong, that a lamp or a stove or a carelessly dropped cigarette had become flame, and that Theo Vance had stood in the yard and watched the windows fill with orange and made a calculation: that a fire explained away as accidental was survivable, but a fire reported by the man who'd been fighting with the deceased about stolen royalties was not.

He let it burn. He let them burn. He drove home and slept, and in the morning he became the grieving survivor, the wounded keeper of the flame, and the town—needing so badly for the story to be nobody's fault—had handed him the keys to itself.

Bram had listened to Grant lay it out and had felt, strangely, very little. Not vindication. Vindication implied she'd wanted him destroyed, and what she wanted was smaller and stranger than that: she wanted the record to be true. She wanted the archive of that night to stop lying. Everything else—the cuffs, the courtroom, the sentence—was the world's business, conducted in the world's language, and she had learned over four hours in a hearing room that she could only speak her own part fluently.

Now, at Odile's table, she listened to the scanner and drank her coffee and waited for the feeling to arrive.

It arrived as grief. Of course it did. It arrived as the memory of Theo at nineteen, teaching himself bass lines in the barn with his tongue between his teeth; Theo carrying Juniper on his shoulders through the Marrow Creek fair with her arms spread like wings; Theo the night before the fire, sitting on the porch steps saying, *whatever happens, we're a family, right? Whatever happens.* He had meant it. That was the horror of it, the thing Bram suspected she would carry longer than any verdict: that he had meant it, and meant it meant nothing, that love and cowardice could occupy the same chest without either one ever being asked to leave.

"There he goes," Ruthie said quietly.

Through Odile's kitchen window, across the square, Bram saw the car pull away from the development office—unmarked, unhurried, a gray sedan carrying a gray man toward the county seat. A handful of people stood on the sidewalk watching it go. Nobody cheered. Nobody wept. They simply watched, the way a body watches a splinter leave the skin: with relief, and with the knowledge that the wound is still there, still needs tending, was never just about the splinter at all.

Odile turned off the scanner. She refilled Bram's cup without asking, which was her way of speaking.

"They'll offer you a deal," Ruthie said. "Theo's lawyer. They'll want your statement softened before trial. They'll come at you with something sweet."

"They already did," Bram said. "Last week. The memorial land, the scholarship fund, the tribute concert." She wrapped her hands around the warm mug. "I said no."

Ruthie studied her. "You sure? A garden with her name on it. A bench. That's not nothing, for a girl who got a paragraph in the county paper and a shoebox of ashes."

"It's not nothing," Bram agreed. "That's exactly why it's poison. Anything beautiful enough to make me quiet isn't a memorial. It's a bribe wearing her face." She looked out the window at the square, at the ordinary morning assembling itself around the absence of a man who had run this town's memory like a private estate. "She gets her song back. Under her name. That's the only monument I trust myself to build."

* * *

The charges against Bram came a week later, and they were, as Grant had predicted, modest: reckless endangerment, referred to the county attorney with a recommendation for summary disposition. The prosecutor—a tired, decent man named Fitch who had inherited the original file like a cursed heirloom—laid it out for her in an office that smelled of old carpet: plead to a misdemeanor, a fine, possible community service, no jail time given the circumstances and her cooperation. Or contest it, and let a jury decide what a locked door meant in the dark.

"I'll take responsibility," Bram said. "Whatever form that takes. I'm not going to argue that the door was innocent. I don't believe it was."

Fitch looked at her over his glasses with something like wonder. "Do you know how many people sit in that chair and tell me the door locked itself?"

"I spent eleven years telling myself that," Bram said. "I'm retired."

* * *

On the last Friday of October, Grant called her at the library—her shift, her desk, her nameplate newly legible to anyone who cared to read it.

"Two things," Grant said. "First: the demolition permit on the Vale property. The developer's fast-tracking it—three weeks, maybe less. Once the site's cleared, anything still in that ruin becomes gravel. If there's evidence, if there's anything of hers you want recovered, now is the time to petition for access. As a witness of record, you'd have standing. I'd support it."

Bram closed her eyes. The farmhouse. Eleven years of refusing to imagine it, and now an invitation written in the language of bulldozers.

"Second," Grant continued, "the defense filed a motion this morning. They're arguing your statement should be excluded—that you're compromised, that your account of the door is self-serving, designed to dilute culpability by distributing it." A pause, and when Grant spoke again her voice had lost its procedural flatness. "For what it's worth, I've read four thousand statements in my career. Yours is the only one I've ever read that made itself smaller so the truth could be bigger. The judge will see that. And even if he doesn't—" she let the sentence finish itself— "you said what you said for the record. Records outlast motions."

After Bram hung up, she sat for a while in the blue hour of the closing library, surrounded by the soft industrial sounds of a building settling into night. Then she took out a sheet of the branch's good letterhead and wrote, in her careful cataloguer's hand:

*Request for access: Vale farm property, prior to demolition. Purpose: recovery of personal effects and materials belonging to Juniper Vale, deceased.*

She signed it. Full name. Bramwell June Blackwood.

Outside, the first cold rain of the season was beginning to fall on Marrow Creek, on the fenced ruin at the edge of town where a piano had burned and a wall had kept its secret, and somewhere above the ridge, faint through static, a barn station played a melody the whole valley was learning to hear.
