## 19. The Farmhouse, Before It Falls

The fence had come down overnight.

Bram noticed it from the road before she was ready to notice anything else—the orange plastic mesh that had ringed the property since her return, sagging and sun-bleached, now rolled up against a sawhorse like something shed. In its place stood a single strip of caution tape, already drooping in the morning damp, more suggestion than barrier. A man in a hard hat sat on a folding chair by the gate with a clipboard and a thermos, and when Bram pulled over he looked at her with the flat curiosity of someone who had been told to expect her.

"Blackwood?" he said, though she hadn't given a name.

"I called last week."

"Yeah." He consulted the clipboard as if it might contradict him. "You've got until four. Crew comes back Monday to start on the chimney. Sign here. Hard hat's yours if you want it—honestly, I'd stay out of the house proper. Roof's mostly gone anyway, but what's left of it likes to let go."

She signed. The pen was cold and the clipboard was slick with dew and she wrote her name carefully, fully, *Bramble June Blackwood*, because it seemed like the kind of morning that required full names. The man watched her do it and said nothing, which she was beginning to understand was Marrow Creek's native language.

"You want me to walk you out there?"

"No," she said. "Thank you."

He nodded and went back to his thermos, and Bram stepped through the gap where the gate used to be, onto ground she had not touched in eleven years.

* * *

The field had won, mostly. Goldenrod stood waist-high where the garden had been, and the path to the house—the one Juniper had refused to gravel because she liked the way mud held footprints, liked proof that people came and went—had gone soft and green and anonymous. Bram walked it slowly, letting the wet stalks slap her jeans, letting the smell of it rise: damp earth, wild mint gone feral along the ditch, the faint sweet rot of apples from the tree nobody had picked since. Her body remembered the distance before her mind did. Forty paces from the road to the porch. She counted without meaning to, the way you count stairs in the dark of your own house.

The farmhouse rose out of the goldenrod like a rib cage.

That was the word her mind offered and would not take back: ribs. The fire had taken the roof and the floors and most of the walls, but the chimneys still stood at either end, black stone rising into a gray sky, and between them the charred skeleton of joists made a pattern like something breathing once and stopping. Tarpaper flapped somewhere with a sound like slow applause. Weeds grew through the foundation stones. On the west wall, half the old paint survived—a band of Juniper's yellow, the yellow she'd mixed herself and called *yolk*, the yellow she'd painted the whole kitchen in defiance of every landlord instinct—and above it, ghosted in smoke, the darker shape of a window frame, a window frame, nothing inside it anymore but sky.

Bram stood at the edge of the porch for a long time.

Eleven years ago she had run down this same path in the opposite direction, barefoot, coatless, with the heat at her back and her arms full of nothing, because there had been nothing to carry. She had stood where she was standing now and screamed until her voice tore, and then she had stopped screaming all at once, the way a machine stops, and some part of her had never started again. She had built a life on the far side of that silence. Shelving books in the hours when no one came. Smiling the smile that ended conversations. Cataloguing other people's memories in plastic cases and never, never pressing play.

And now here was the house, smaller than memory, exactly as Cass had promised the past always was. You could see across it in ten steps. The whole enormous architecture of her grief—the thing she had carried like a second skeleton for a decade—had lived inside this modest rectangle of scorched timber and standing stone, and it looked, from the outside, like almost nothing at all.

"I'm here," she said aloud, and her voice cracked on the second word, and the tarpaper applauded.

She went in.

* * *

The floor was gone. She walked on the earth itself, on the old cellar dirt that had been the crawlspace, on a century of dropped coins and button fragments and now ash turned to soil. Grass grew in the parlor. A sapling—birch, impatient, three years old at most—had shouldered up through the spot where the kitchen table used to be, and its pale trunk was scoured black on one side from some later, smaller fire, kids probably, or lightning, or just the house trying once more to finish the job.

Bram moved through the rooms the way you move through a sentence you're afraid to finish reading.

Here: the living room, where the piano had stood against the north wall. Here: the hallway where Dell Harrow's truck headlights had swept the ceiling at two in the morning, three days before the end, and Juniper had said *that man has hands like a till*. Here: the side door.

She stopped in front of it.

There was no door anymore, of course. There was a doorway, a rectangle of night-colored wood, opening onto the weeds where the side yard used to be. But Bram could see it as it was—she couldn't stop seeing it as it was—the door with its brass bolt, the bolt she had thrown at eleven-forty on the last night because she was angry, because she was leaving, because locking things was what she did instead of saying what she meant. *Habit from the city,* she'd told the investigators, and they'd written it down in their tidy hand and filed it under *nothing.* Eleven years she had carried that bolt in her chest like a swallowed key. Whoever set the fire, whoever fled through this door or failed to flee through it, had met Bram Blackwood's fear first, dressed as a lock.

She put her palm flat against the charred jamb. It was warm from the sun. That was all. Just wood, just weather, just October doing its ordinary work.

"I know," she said, to the doorway, to the girl who had locked it, to whoever might be listening in whatever country the dead kept. "I know what I did."

The wind moved through the birch sapling and the leaves said something back that wasn't words, and Bram found that she was crying—not the cracked-open sobbing of Odile's kitchen, but something quieter and stranger, tears arriving like rain on a day that had already accepted rain, and she let them come while she stood in the ruin of the room where her best friend had written songs at a piano with a missing middle C, and she did not wipe her face, and she did not leave.

Then she went to find the piano.

* * *

It was where she knew it would be, because pianos are heavy and fire is fast and the crew had no reason to haul two tons of burned walnut into a landfill when the county had told them to clear the site by November. They had pushed it against the surviving section of the north wall, out of the way, and there it lay on its side like a drowned animal: the upright, the old Hollis & Vane, the one Juniper had bought at auction for forty dollars and tuned herself with a tuning hammer and a YouTube video and an act of will.

Bram circled it twice before she could touch it.

The case was scorched to a deep uneven brown, black at the edges, the varnish bubbled into landscapes like the surface of some burned planet. The keyboard had collapsed inward; keys hung at angles, ivory gone the color of tea, and where the strings had been exposed to the rain of eleven autumns they were rusted into soft red furrows, a harp that had forgotten its own name. But the iron plate—the great gold harp-shaped casting that held the tension of every string—was intact. Fire doesn't much care for iron. It had survived the way the truest things survive: by being too dense and too stubborn to burn.

Cass had told her, on the phone, in his dry dying voice: *If you go in there, look at the piano. I never had the nerve. I'm telling you because I don't think I'll get another chance to tell anyone anything.*

She knelt in the dirt beside it.

The back of an upright piano is a lattice—a wooden framework of posts and braces, and between them, the soundboard, the great wooden belly where all the voice lives. Bram worked her fingers into the gap between the fallen instrument and the wall, into the smell of wet char and mouse nests and time, and began, with the patience of eleven years' practice at small invisible tasks, to clear away the debris.

She found the nails first.

Four of them, square-headed, old-fashioned, driven straight through paper into the soundboard's rim—one at each corner of a rectangle, the way you hang a poster in a room you intend to live in for a long time. The paper itself was fused to the wood in places, water-stained, foxed with mold, its ink faded to the brown of old blood. But the fire had reached this side of the piano only glancingly, starved of air behind the closed lid, and the rain had pooled elsewhere, and so the page—folded once, nailed flat—had spent eleven years in the dark doing the one thing paper does best, which is wait.

Bram eased it free, nail by nail, using the claw of a borrowed multi-tool, working so slowly she could hear her own pulse in her ears. When the last nail came out she lifted the page into the light with both hands, the way you lift a bird, the way you lift a coal you mean to carry somewhere it can become fire again.

Juniper's handwriting. Unmistakable. The big looping capitals, the letters that leaned forward like runners at a starting line, the little stars she drew over her i's when a line felt holy.

At the top, underlined twice: **AFTERMATH — for B.**

And below, the verses. Not the ones Cass had broadcast to the whole county on demolition eve—those had been the finished song, the public song, the song with its chorus worn smooth by rehearsal. These were earlier. Rougher. Written in the handwriting of a woman who knew she was writing a letter and disguising it as a lyric, and Bram read them kneeling in the ruins with ash on her knees and the tears coming freely now, and the verses said:

*When the house goes up, don't count the cost of what you carried out.*
*I know the door you locked behind you—I'd have locked it too, no doubt.*
*Whatever you'll decide you did, whatever guilt you build your house in—*
*listen, B: I wrote this down before, so you can't live without.*

And the last verse, the one that undid her entirely, the one she read four times because the first three times her eyes flooded and slid off the ink:

*You'll make yourself a ghost, my love, a librarian of night,*
*shelving everyone's sad stories, hiding from your own good light.*
*But I forgive you in advance—see, here it is, in writing—*
*come back and play this loud someday. That's all I'm asking. That's the rite.*

Dated three days before the fire. Nailed inside the piano, hidden where only someone who knew the piano, who knew the room, who knew to kneel in the dirt and reach into the dark—where only Bram would ever find it.

Juniper had known. Not the fire, maybe. Not the how or the when. But she had known Bram—known her the way weather knows a coastline—and she had understood, three days before the end, that the person she loved best in the world was building a guilt in advance, was already drafting the prison she would move into, and so she had done the only thing a songwriter knows how to do about the future: she had written it down, and nailed it shut, and trusted the wood to keep it.

*Forgiving her in advance for whatever she'd blame herself for.* Cass had guessed at it. Ruthie had suspected. But here it was in Juniper's own leaning, running, star-crowned hand, and it was not absolution delivered from a distance—it was absolution planted like a seed, timed to bloom eleven years late, addressed to the one person who would dig for it.

Bram sat down in the dirt of the ruined parlor, in the footprint of the room where they had sung, and she held the page against her chest, and she laughed and wept in the same breath, one sound, the sound the song had been waiting all these years to hear.

"You absolute—" she managed, and couldn't finish, and didn't need to. *You absolute Juniper.* The phrase completed itself somewhere below language, in the place where friendship lives after death, which is not memory exactly, and not haunting, but inheritance.

*Come back and play this loud someday. That's the rite.*

She looked at the broken piano, at its rusted strings and its iron heart, and she understood what she was going to do, and the understanding arrived not as a decision but as a fact, the way dawn arrives, the way the fire had arrived: unavoidable, total, hers.

* * *

She was still sitting there when the shadow fell across the doorway.

Not the hard-hat man—this shadow paused, and hesitated, and belonged to a coat she recognized, wool and gray, cut for a man who wanted to look like the kind of person who wore wool. Bram turned, the lyric sheet still pressed to her sternum, and Theo Vance stood at the edge of the ruined parlor with his hands in his pockets and his face doing what his face had always done best, which was look wounded in a way that asked for permission.

"They said someone was out here," he said. "I told them I'd check."

"The crew comes Monday," Bram said. "I have until four."

"I know. I signed the permit." He came a step closer, boots crunching on char, and his eyes went to the page in her hands, and she watched him understand what it was, and she watched something move behind his face—fear, or envy, or the particular loneliness of a man looking at a forgiveness he would never be handed. "Is that—"

"It's mine," Bram said, and heard her own voice come out level and new. "Juniper left it to me. Eleven years in advance."

Theo stood in the doorway of the burned house, Citizen of the Year, awaiting sentencing in nine days, and for a long moment neither of them spoke, and the tarpaper applauded, and the birch sapling turned its scorched face toward the light.

"Bram," he said finally, and there was something in his voice she had never heard there before—not charm, not plea, but something rawer, almost young. "Before they tear it down. There's something I need to show you too."
