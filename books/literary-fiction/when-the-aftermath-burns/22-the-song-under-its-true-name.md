## 22. The Song Under Its True Name

The garden came up green in the spring, which surprised everyone, including the soil.

Bram had expected the lot to resist—expected the char to rise through whatever they planted, the way grief rose through her, unbidden, at odd hours. But the demolition crew had hauled away the farmhouse bones in October, and by November the town had tilled in a hundred yards of clean topsoil, and by April the first crocuses had pushed up through ground that had every reason to stay bitter. Marigolds along the fence line. Lavender where the porch used to be. A row of sunflowers along the north edge because Ruthie Harrow had planted them and dared anyone to comment.

In the center, where the kitchen had been—the room where Juniper had written half of everything she ever wrote—stood a bench. Simple cedar, built by the high school shop class, with a small brass plate that read only: *Listen.*

No dates. No names. No *in memoriam*, no *gone but not forgotten*. Bram had argued for it at the town meeting, standing at the microphone with her hands steady for the first time in eleven years. "She doesn't need us to remember her," she'd said. "She needs us to listen. There's a difference. Remembering is something you do once a year and feel good about. Listening is work."

Old Man Petrie in the third row had snorted. Half the town had looked at their shoes. But the vote carried, and the bench went in, and now on any given evening you could find someone sitting there—not mourning, exactly. Just listening. To the wind in the sunflowers. To the low hum of Cass's transmitter two miles out, carrying on the evening air like weather.

* * *

The station had a name now: WXRV-LP, ninety-one point three, though nobody called it that. They called it *the barn*. Cass had lasted eight months after the broadcasts before his heart finally made good on its threats, and he'd died the way he'd lived those last years—on air, mid-sentence, between one song and the next. Ruthie found him at dawn, headphones still on, and according to her he looked "annoyed, not scared, which was about right for him."

Ruthie ran the station now from a folding chair behind the same scarred mixing board, playing the same strange, beautiful programming: found tapes, local songwriters, hour-long sets of nothing but rain recordings when she felt like it. She'd taken over the way she did everything—bluntly, without ceremony, daring the FCC or God or anyone else to object.

"Your girl's on tonight," she told Bram over the phone that morning. "Nine o'clock. Full version this time, the long take with the false start. I figured a year was long enough for people to hear her clear her throat."

"A year," Bram said, and heard how strange it sounded. A year since the inquiry closed. A year since Theo Vance stood in a courtroom in a gray suit and received five years for involuntary manslaughter and obstruction, his face never quite resolving into anything Bram could hate. A year since she'd stood on the courthouse steps between Ruthie and Cass and felt the sun on her face like something she'd been keeping in escrow.

A year since she'd signed her own name to anything.

* * *

The Halloway Branch Library still smelled the same—old paper, floor wax, the ghost of forty years of coffee smuggled past Odile—but Bram moved through it differently now. The night hours were hers, officially. Three evenings a week, seven to midnight, the community room filled with insomniacs.

That was Odile's doing, naturally. It had started as a joke—"You can't be the only person in this town who's afraid of her own bed"—and become a class: *Writing Through the Night*, Tuesdays and Thursdays, free, drop-in, no grades, no reading aloud unless you wanted to. The first night, four people showed up: a retired mail carrier who couldn't stop dreaming about routes, a young mother whose baby slept fine but who didn't, a teenager who wrote nothing but lists, and a man Bram recognized from the courthouse gallery during the sentencing—he'd sat in the back every day, and she'd never learned why.

Now there were twelve of them, sometimes fourteen, folding chairs arranged in a lopsided circle around the radiator. They wrote about everything and nothing. The mail carrier had started a memoir. The list-teenager had written a poem last month that made three people cry, including Odile, who claimed she had dust in her eye.

"You signing the register tonight?" Odile asked, appearing beside the circulation desk with the particular silence she'd perfected over decades of catching patrons in the act of living.

Bram looked down at the attendance sheet. Twelve names in twelve hands. At the top, the line marked *Instructor*.

For eleven years she had been no one. She had curated her own absence the way she curated the shelves—alphabetized, dusted, invisible. She had practiced the smile that ended conversations. She had catalogued a dead woman's voice without listening to it, because listening meant feeling, and feeling meant the fire, and the fire meant the door, and the door meant—

But that was the old arithmetic. The new one was simpler and cost more: you could not be forgiven by a ghost, but you could be forgiven by the living, if you gave them something true to forgive.

"Instructor," she said, and picked up the pen.

*Bram Blackwood.* Full name. Both names, the whole weight of them, pressed into the paper. Her hand didn't shake. That was the miracle no one would ever put on a plaque—that after everything, the shaking stopped, and no one witnessed it but her and a sheet of lined paper.

Odile glanced at it, nodded once, and said, "About time," which was, from Odile, a eulogy and a christening both.

* * *

The class wrote until ten. Tonight's prompt, scrawled on the whiteboard in Bram's careful hand: *Write about a door.* She'd chosen it without ceremony, the way she chose all the prompts now—letting the old wounds surface as curriculum, transmuted, useful. The mail carrier wrote about the door of the last house on his route, where an old woman waited for him every day just to say his name. The teenager wrote a list titled *Doors I Have Closed On Purpose*, which was funnier than it sounds and ended, devastatingly, with *my grandmother's hospital room, twice*.

At ten they drifted out into the night, nodding at Bram, some of them saying her name now—her actual name, spoken aloud in her direction, a small daily resurrection she still hadn't gotten used to. The young mother lingered.

"The radio thing," she said. "Ruthie's station. Is it true what's playing at nine?"

"It's true."

"I heard it last year. The night Cass played it first." The woman looked down at her notebook, embarrassed. "I don't know why I'm telling you this. I'd just had the baby and I wasn't sleeping and I turned on the radio and there was this song with no name, and I sat in the dark and cried and didn't know why. And then later I found out—" She stopped. "Anyway. I'll be listening tonight. With the window open."

After she left, Bram stood alone in the community room a long moment, the radiator ticking, the whiteboard holding its single word. *Door.*

Eleven years ago she had locked one. She had locked it against intruders, against the dark, against the world's ordinary menace, and the lock had held, and the fire had come anyway, from inside, from a lamp and a fight and a man's cowardice—and for eleven years she had believed the lock was hers to carry forever, a stone in the chest, a sentence without end.

Then she had testified. Had said it aloud, in a room full of strangers, in front of Theo Vance's gray face: *I locked the side door. It was my habit. I don't know if it mattered. I need it to be part of the record anyway.* And the prosecutor had nodded gravely, and the judge had given her a suspended sentence and a fine and a look that was almost kind, and none of it—not the courtroom, not the penalty, not the truth itself—had lifted the stone.

What lifted it was smaller. A lyric nailed inside a piano. *Whatever you're blaming yourself for, B, put it down. I already carried it for you. That's what friends are for.* Charred at the edges, legible at the center, waiting eleven years in the ruin for Bram to walk in and read it.

Juniper had forgiven her in advance. All that remained was to accept—which turned out to be the hardest thing she'd ever done, harder than testimony, harder than the confrontation, harder even than leaving the farmhouse early that night with the argument still burning in her throat. Accepting forgiveness required believing you deserved a future. Bram was still working on it. Some nights she got there. Most nights she got close enough.

* * *

At nine o'clock she drove out to the garden.

It was June again—one year, almost to the week, since she'd come back to Marrow Creek with a bus ticket and a dread she couldn't name. The sunflowers were taller than she remembered planting being possible; Ruthie must have staked them. Fireflies had risen from the lavender in loose constellations. And on the bench, as always, someone was listening—a kid tonight, maybe sixteen, earbuds in but radio playing soft from a portable speaker, head tipped back toward the stars.

Bram didn't sit. Not yet. She stood at the fence line, in the shadow of the sunflowers, and let the broadcast find her.

Static first. Then Ruthie's voice, rougher than Cass's ever was, but carrying the same freight: *"Evening, Marrow Creek. One year ago tonight, a man named Cass Merrow played a tape on this station and changed what this town knows about itself. He'd want me to shut up about that, so I'll keep it short. What follows is a song by Juniper Vale. She wrote it in the kitchen that used to stand right about where you're sitting, if you're sitting where I think you're sitting. She never recorded it proper. This is the rehearsal take, false start and all, and it was written for one person, who has given permission for the world to hear it. Juniper, wherever you are—it goes out under its true name. 'Aftermath.' For B."*

And then the guitar. Then the breath, the little laugh, the clearing of Juniper's throat—alive, so alive, three days before the fire, immortal in the way only tape can make a person immortal—and then the voice.

Bram closed her eyes.

She knew every note. She knew the way Juniper bent the second verse flat on purpose, the way she hummed instead of singing the bridge because the words weren't finished, the way the melody circled back at the end to the place where it began, but lower, quieter, resolved. She had heard it alone in her apartment in the city, once, eleven years ago, just the two of them and a borrowed recorder, and Juniper had said, *Only for you, B. Nobody else gets this one,* and Bram had kept that promise through fire and silence and erasure, until the promise itself became a kind of grave.

Not anymore. The song poured out over the garden, over the sunflowers and the lavender and the listening bench, over the town that had agreed to forget and was learning, slowly, imperfectly, to remember. Windows were open all over Marrow Creek tonight—she knew it the way you know things without evidence. Strangers hearing Juniper Vale for the first time. Old friends hearing her and having to pull over. Somewhere, probably, Theo Vance in whatever facility held him, hearing it too, and Bram found she could wish him nothing in particular—not forgiveness, not hatred, just the song, arriving whether he wanted it or not, the way truth arrives.

The false start came—Juniper laughing at her own fingers, saying *okay, okay, from the top*—and the kid on the bench laughed too, startled, delighted, as if the ghost had winked at him personally. And she had, Bram thought. That was the whole trick of it. That was what Cass had understood and Ruthie kept understanding and Bram was only now learning: the dead aren't gone if you keep playing them. Grief you don't examine becomes a tenant. But grief you play aloud, grief you credit properly, grief you give back to the world under its true name—*that* grief becomes music. It stops burning and starts blooming.

The song ended. Static breathed. Ruthie came back on, quiet: *"That's the record. Goodnight, Marrow Creek. Bench is open till dawn."*

* * *

Bram walked to the bench and sat down.

The kid glanced over, recognized her—everyone recognized her now, which had taken adjustment; invisibility, it turned out, had been easier to wear than presence, lighter, though it fit worse—and nodded, and gathered his speaker, and left her the garden.

She sat in the dark among the flowers that grew where the kitchen had burned, and she took out the notebook she carried everywhere now, the one with the register signature on its first page, and beneath the moon and the fireflies and the fading hum of the transmitter she wrote:

*A year ago I came back here to find out who killed my best friend. I found out. It was cowardice, compounded. It was also me, a little, and a locked door, and a habit of leaving early. None of those things get to have the last word anymore.*

*Her name was Juniper Vale. She wrote songs the way other people breathe, and the last one was for me, and tonight the whole town heard it, and tomorrow strangers will hear it, and someday someone will sit on this bench who never knew either of us and the song will find them anyway, because that's what songs do when you finally set them free.*

*I used to think the aftermath was the burning. It isn't. The aftermath is this: marigolds through old char. A bench that says Listen. A class of insomniacs writing about doors. My name, in my own handwriting, at the top of a page.*

*The aftermath is blooming.*

She capped the pen. Down the road, the barn's single light burned steady over the fields, Ruthie inside it probably, already cueing up the next hour—rain recordings, maybe, or another found tape, the dead and the living sharing the same frequency. The sunflowers stood black and tall against the stars. Somewhere in the lavender, a cricket began, tentatively, to keep time.

Bram Blackwood stayed on the bench a while longer, listening, her face turned up to the enormous ordinary night, and for the first time in eleven years—or maybe for the first time ever—she was not waiting for anything. Not dawn, not forgiveness, not the past to release its grip. Everything that needed finding had been found. Everything that needed saying had been said, and credited, and played twice.

The garden grew. The song went out. And under its true name, in the dark that was no longer empty, the aftermath bloomed.
