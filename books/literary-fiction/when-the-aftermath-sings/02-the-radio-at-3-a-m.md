## 2. The Radio at 3 A.M.

The conservatory at three in the morning had a sound all its own, and Bram knew it the way other people knew their mothers' voices.

It was not silence. Silence was a myth sold to people who had never worked nights. The building breathed: pipes ticking as they cooled, the boiler's low asthmatic hum in the sub-basement, the elevator cable sighing against its sheave every time a draft moved through the stairwell. In the east wing, where the roof had begun to fail, rain found its way in through a dozen small wounds and dripped into buckets Bram had positioned with the precision of an orchestral arranger—*this one for the soprano drip near the window, that one for the baritone plunk by the door*. On good nights the whole thing almost held together, almost made music, and Bram would stand in the dark corridor with a mop dripping between their hands and let it wash over them like weather.

They did not call it listening. Listening was a country they had renounced citizenship in eleven years ago. They called it *inventory*.

Tonight's inventory, Hall C, 2:47 a.m.: seventeen floorboards that creaked underfoot if you stepped wrong, which Bram never did anymore; the smell of dust and cold varnish and, beneath it, the ghost of rosin that no amount of cleaning would ever fully lift; the stage curtain drawn shut like a face turned away. A moth had gotten in through the loading dock and was throwing itself against the exit sign with a soft, idiotic persistence. Bram left it alone. Some things you could not mop up. You just noted them and moved on.

The mop bucket rolled behind them on its squeaking wheel—Bram had oiled it twice and it still squeaked, and eventually they had decided the squeak was part of the building's voice too, and stopped fighting it. They worked the halls in a fixed circuit, counterclockwise, always starting at the entrance where the demolition notice hung.

They did not look at the notice tonight. They had looked at it enough for one lifetime, or at least for one week. SIX WEEKS, they'd calculated on the first night, standing there with their keys in hand. Then five weeks and six days. The arithmetic of erasure, subtracting itself nightly while the city slept. Bram had decided that the notice and they were in the same business: cataloguing what remained before it was gone. The difference was that the notice wanted the building gone, and Bram only wanted to be present for it, the way you are present at a deathbed—not because you can stop anything, but because leaving would be one more departure, and Bram had made their last one.

In the staff room, the old radio sat on the shelf above the sink where it had sat since before Bram's time, a squat silver-faced thing with a broken antenna someone had replaced with a straightened coat hanger. It belonged to the day crew, ostensibly, but the day crew went home at four, and the night belonged to whoever stayed. Bram turned it on most nights—not for the music, God no, never for the music, but for the voices. Talk radio, mostly. The low murmur of strangers arguing about zoning and weather filled a room the way furniture does, without asking anything of you. Voices were safe. Voices didn't have keys or time signatures or the terrible architecture of feeling built into them.

Tonight the voice was a woman's, low and unhurried, the kind of voice that made you think of someone reading aloud in another room while you fell asleep.

"—and if you're just joining us, this is *The Hour of Owls*, I'm Junie Alvarez, and tonight we're doing something a little different. No theme, no requests. Just a single piece of music I've been sitting with for a while now, trying to decide whether the world deserved to hear it. I've decided it does. I don't know who wrote it. That's part of why I'm playing it. Maybe somebody out there knows."

Bram stood in the doorway with the mop handle wet under their grip and felt nothing yet, absolutely nothing, the particular nothing they had spent eleven years constructing plank by plank, and then the piano came in.

It came in the way it always had: alone, unaccompanied, a single line rising out of nothing like a hand reaching up out of dark water. Three notes ascending. A pause exactly long enough to doubt. Then the fourth note falling back down through the first three like something remembered too late.

Bram's body knew it before their mind consented to. Their spine straightened. Their breath went shallow and high in their chest. The mop handle slipped an inch in their loosening fingers and the wet string head slapped the linoleum with a sound like a small rebuke, and Bram did not hear it, because Bram was hearing the rest: the second phrase answering the first, a fifth lower, darker, the left hand entering underneath with those rolling open fifths that Odile had once said sounded like "the ground deciding whether to hold you up." The modulation in the eighth bar that every theory professor would have flagged as excessive and that Bram had refused, at twenty-two, to soften, because grief is excessive, grief does not resolve when your textbook says it should—

No one had ever heard this piece.

That was the fact Bram's mind kept returning to, circling it, pressing against it like a tongue against a broken tooth. No one had heard it. It had never been written down. It had never been recorded. It existed in exactly two places in the universe: in the air of Odile Voss's office on a Tuesday afternoon in March eleven years ago, when Bram had played it for her once, hands shaking, and said *it's for you, it doesn't have a name yet*, and in the sealed vault of Bram's own memory, where they had locked it away with everything else and thrown away the key and welded the door and told no one, no one, not Teo, not Marcus, not the therapists the family had insisted on, no one.

And yet here it was, coming out of a coat-hanger antenna in a janitor's closet of a break room, rendered by some pianist whose touch was lighter than Bram's, more forgiving, holding the tempo back in the seventh bar where Bram had always pushed—and it was beautiful, that was the unbearable part, it was still beautiful, it had survived eleven years in the dark and come out the other side still beautiful, still *theirs*, still carrying the shape of Odile's office in it, the green lamp, the tea going cold, her hands folded in her lap because she had learned early with Bram never to conduct, never to interrupt, only to listen with her whole face until you finished and then say the one sentence that rearranged you.

*"You've been carrying this a long time,"* she had said. *"I can hear it. It's heavy, and you've made it sing anyway."*

The broadcast crackled. The woman's voice returned over the music's tail, gentle, almost apologetic: "That's all we have of it. About four minutes. The tape degrades toward the end—I won't play you the hiss. If anyone recognizes this piece, if anyone knows where it comes from or who wrote it, I'd honestly love to know. It sounds like a goodbye to me. But maybe that's just the hour I'm playing it at. Everything sounds like a goodbye at three in the morning."

A goodbye. Yes. Bram could have told her. Bram had written it as a goodbye and then walked out before delivering it, which meant it had been a goodbye addressed to no one, a letter with no address, and now it was loose in the world, on the air, in the ears of insomniacs and truck drivers and strangers drifting through the small hours, being heard by everyone except the person it was for.

The radio murmured on—a station ID, a weather report for a morning Bram would sleep through—but Bram no longer heard it. They were standing very still in the doorway of the staff room, and the building was breathing around them, and somewhere above, in the failing east wing, a drop of water fell into a bucket with a clear, round tone, and another answered it, and for one vertiginous moment the whole conservatory seemed to be keeping time with a piece that did not exist.

The vertigo came in waves. That was how Bram would think of it later, lying awake in their narrow bed watching the ceiling go grey: not a single shock but a series of them, each one tilting the floor a few degrees further off true. First wave: *impossible*. The piece could not exist outside their head. Therefore they had misheard. People misheard things constantly; the brain was a pattern-hungry organ, it found melodies in traffic and voices in wind. Second wave: *not impossible*. They had caught the seventh bar. Mishearing did not produce a seventh bar. Mishearing produced mush, approximation, the general shape of sadness. This had been specific. This had been *exact*. Third wave, the worst one, arriving around dawn with the birds: *someone played it*. Tapes did not play themselves. Somewhere in the world there was a recording of a piece that had lived and died in one room, and someone had carried it to a radio station, and someone had put it on the air, and none of these someones was Bram.

Which raised the question the vertigo had been circling all along, the question that finally stilled everything else the way a struck bell stills the air around it:

*Where did the tape come from?*

There had been no tape. There had been no paper, no pencil, no MIDI file, no phone recording propped against the piano lid. Bram had been fanatical about it afterward—fanatical the way only the guilty are fanatical—searching Odile's effects after the funeral for any trace, terrified and longing in equal measure to find the piece written down in her hand, and finding nothing. Her shelves held scores by dead men and marginalia in three languages and not one note from Bram Quintana. Bram had taken that absence as punishment. As verdict. *Not worth keeping.* They had built eleven years on top of that reading the way a city builds on a fault line.

But a tape existed. Which meant either the fault line had shifted, or Bram had read the ruins wrong.

The moth in Hall C had given up on the exit sign by now, presumably. The buckets would need emptying before the day crew arrived at seven, complaining about the smell in the east wing, complaining about the heat, complaining about the demolition they claimed to mourn and secretly celebrated—no more night shifts to schedule, no more ancient boilers. Bram would finish the circuit. Bram would clock out at six and walk home past the bakery before it opened and lie down and not sleep, and tomorrow night they would come back and do it all again, and the night after that, and the arithmetic on the door would keep subtracting itself, five weeks and five days, five weeks and four days, until the number reached zero and the excavators came and the buckets and the creaking boards and the ghost of rosin were all one memory.

Unless.

Bram set the mop against the wall with exaggerated care, the way you set down something when your hands are not steady enough to trust. They crossed to the radio. The woman—Junie, she'd said, Junie Alvarez—had moved on to a song about rivers by someone with a guitar, oblivious, benign. Bram stared at the dial, at the little red needle trembling on its frequency, and thought: *she said weekly. She said she'd been sitting with it. Sitting with it implies returning to it.*

Four minutes of music. A pianist they couldn't name. A voice on the radio inviting the world to identify a piece that had no name, no score, no owner—except one, except the one person who had made it and buried it and never once, in eleven years, allowed themselves to hum it in the shower, in the street, in the dark of their own skull, because humming it would mean admitting it was still alive in there, and admitting that would mean admitting everything else was too.

The sky outside the staff room's high window had gone the color of dishwater. Somewhere across town, a demolition crew was fueling machines. Somewhere closer, a young pianist's hands rested on keys, having played a piece they had no right to know, and did not know they had no right to know, and would perhaps play again.

Bram picked up the clipboard hanging by the door—the nightly log, the inventory, columns for rooms completed and hazards noted—and uncapped the pen, and wrote, in the neat hand of a person who has practiced being unreadable:

*Staff room, 3:12 a.m. Radio functioning. Nothing to report.*

Then, below it, smaller, in letters they told themselves were only a draft, only practice, only a rehearsal for a sentence they might never say aloud:

*I know that song. I wrote it. Find out who is playing it before they take the walls down.*

* * *

The next night, Bram arrived an hour early and went straight to the staff room, and the radio was already on, and the woman's voice was saying: "Same time next week, owls. And to the person who called the station yesterday asking me to never play that piece again—" a pause, warm and unhurried, a smile you could hear— "I'm sorry. I can't do that. Some things want to be heard. See you in seven days."

Bram stood in the doorway, keys biting into their palm, and began, helplessly, to calculate.
