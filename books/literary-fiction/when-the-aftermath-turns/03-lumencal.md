## 3. Lumençal

The second lesson began with bread.

Not metaphorically — Odile Vasseur arrived at the archive room at two in the morning carrying a paper bag that smelled of a bakery three neighborhoods away, the kind that kept hours no sane baker would choose, and she set it on the table between them like a text.

"Eat first," she said. "You cannot learn a language on an empty mouth."

Cyrus had spent the week telling themselves this meant nothing. One lesson, they'd agreed to. One lesson, and then Odile would find a proper student, someone credentialed, someone from the linguistics department who could spell phoneme, and Cyrus could go back to being the man behind the desk, the one who belonged to the building but not to anything in it. They had rehearsed the refusal all week. Then she'd walked in with bread, and the refusal had died quietly somewhere behind their sternum.

"It's called *panneu*," she said, watching them tear off a piece. "In Lumençal. But that's only the word for bread as an object. The thing in your hand." She took a piece herself, held it up to the lamplight. "There is another word for bread as it is shared — *pannave* — which contains inside it the idea that food given freely becomes part of the giver. And a third word, *pannelh*, for bread that has gone stale but is kept anyway, out of love or stubbornness, which are considered in Lumençal to be nearly the same emotion."

Cyrus stopped chewing. "There's a word for keeping stale bread?"

"There are eleven words for light," Odile said. "Bread gets three. We were practical people about bread and extravagant about light. Every language tells you what a people feared and what they loved. Ours loved light and feared silence." She leaned forward. "Say *pannave*."

"*Pannave.*"

"Your tongue is lazy. It wants English vowels, wide and easy. *Pan-nah-veh*. Let the middle open like a door."

"*Pannave.*"

"Better. Again."

They went again. And again. And somewhere around the tenth repetition Cyrus noticed that the tightness they carried in their chest during every waking hour — the low-grade vigilance of a person perpetually ready to be asked to leave — had loosened without their permission. Their mouth was doing something it had never done. A sound existed that only they and this old woman and perhaps forty people left alive on earth could make, and they were making it, badly, and it felt like being let into a room everyone else had assumed was locked.

"Tell me what you're feeling," Odile said, "but say it in my language's terms. You may borrow."

Cyrus thought. "*Pannave,*" they said finally. "This feels like *pannave*."

Odile laughed — a short, delighted bark — and clapped her hands once. "First sentence. You've been speaking Lumençal for twenty minutes and already you've said something no dictionary can translate properly. Congratulations. You're ruined for English now, at least a little."

The lesson ran until four. She taught them the eleven lights: the light before dawn (*albessa*), the light through leaves (*follumière*, though she warned the spelling was her own invention since the language had never been fully written down), the light of a lamp kept burning for someone expected home (*lume-attente*), the light that comes off snow at night so bright you can read by it (*blanchard*). When Cyrus asked why a language needed a word for lamplight kept for the expected, Odile's face did something complicated and closed, like a book snapped shut mid-page.

"Because we were a seafaring people once," she said. "On the coast. Everyone was always either waiting or expected." She gathered her papers. "Next week. Bring nothing. I'll bring everything."

At the door she paused. "You have a good ear, Cyrus Marchetti. That is rarer than intelligence. Intelligence argues with a language. An ear surrenders to it." Then she was gone down the stairwell, and Cyrus stood alone in the archive room among the boxed silences of other people's lives, holding a crumb of *pannelh* they had secretly pocketed because throwing it away suddenly seemed unthinkable.

They told themselves, again, that this meant nothing.

They were beginning to suspect they were a bad witness to their own life.

* * *

Noor Vasseur had been sitting in her car outside the library for forty minutes, telling herself she was there by coincidence.

The coincidence theory was not holding up well. Neither was the engine, which she'd left running out of some superstitious belief that idling meant she could still leave, that leaving remained a live option, when in truth she had known since her grandmother's phone call last Tuesday — the first phone call in fourteen months, brief, imperious, *I have taken a student, do not interfere* — exactly where she'd be tonight at two a.m.: here, outside the building where her grandmother was giving away the last thing Noor had any claim to.

She went in.

The night library was not what she expected. She'd pictured something institutional, fluorescent, sad. Instead it was warm and half-lit, populated by a strange congregation: a huge man asleep across three chairs of periodicals with an open magazine rising and falling on his chest; a woman in a nurse's scrubs reading with the intensity of someone postponing sleep as a personal enemy; a teenager doing homework at a table covered in energy drink cans. A librarian — youngish, tired-eyed, careful — looked up from the desk.

"I'm looking for Odile Vasseur," Noor said. "I'm her granddaughter."

Something flickered across the librarian's face — recognition, and under it, guilt? Good, Noor thought. Guilt was appropriate.

"She's downstairs. In the archive room. I can take you—"

"No need."

She found the stairs herself. Found the room. Stood in the doorway unannounced, the way you enter a room when you want to see it before it arranges itself for you.

Her grandmother was laughing.

That was the thing that undid her before she'd said a word. Not the scene itself — the old woman and the librarian bent over a notebook, papers everywhere, a chalkboard propped against the shelving covered in symbols Noor half-recognized from childhood kitchen-table lessons she'd refused to sit through — but the laugh. Noor knew all of her grandmother's laughs the way you know the voices of a house. There was the polite laugh for doctors. The dry laugh for her son-in-law, Noor's father, dead now eight years. The laugh she'd used at family dinners after the rupture, a laugh with a wall built into it. This was none of those. This was the laugh from before — from the summers in the coastal house, from when Noor was seven and Odile had taught her to whistle through a blade of grass, from when the word *Lumençal* was just the music her grandmother hummed while cooking and not yet the battlefield it became.

Her grandmother hadn't laughed like that since Noor was a child. Since before Elias. Since before everything.

And she was laughing like that for a stranger.

"Grand-mère," Noor said, and watched the laugh die in the old woman's throat.

Odile recovered fast; she always had. "Noor." Her chin came up, the old imperiousness sliding on like armor. "You found me."

"You didn't tell me where you'd be. I had to find out from—" From a voicemail from the university's linguistics department, actually, a cheerful graduate student wanting to schedule a *documentation session*, asking if Noor, as next of kin, could confirm her grandmother's capacity to consent. That call had done more than anything else to put her in this car. "Who is this?" She nodded at the librarian, who had risen halfway out of their chair with the expression of someone caught stealing.

"Cyrus Marchetti. My student."

"Your *student*." Noor stepped into the room. It smelled like chalk and bread and something floral she couldn't place. "Grand-mère, can I talk to you? Alone?"

"Whatever you have to say, you can say here."

Fine. "You're seventy-nine years old. You fainted in March. Your doctor called me — did you know that? Twice. You're giving away the language — *our* language, our family's — to someone you met three weeks ago in a library, and the university is already circling, and you didn't think that was something your own granddaughter might want to know about?"

"The language is not ours," Odile said, very quietly, in the voice she used when she was most dangerous. "That is precisely the mistake everyone has always made. Languages don't belong to families. Families belong to languages, if they're lucky, and we—" her voice caught, barely, "—we were not lucky. We let it go. All of us. You included, Noor. I offered to teach you when you were nine. You said it sounded like old people talking."

"I was nine."

"And he was never too old to learn," Odile said, "and look how that ended."

The name hung in the room. Noor felt it land in her chest like a stone dropped down a well — Elias, great-uncle Elias, the one photograph turned face-down in her grandmother's hallway, the one subject that emptied every family dinner. She hadn't come here to fight about Elias. She'd come here because she was terrified, and terror in the Vasseur family had always dressed itself as anger.

The librarian — Cyrus — spoke for the first time. "Maybe I should give you two the room."

"No," Odile and Noor said together, and then glared at each other, which was almost funny, which Noor refused to find funny.

"Stay," Odile said to Cyrus, and then, to Noor, softer, in a tone Noor hadn't heard aimed at her in years: "Stay too. Sit down. If you're going to guard the door, at least learn what you're guarding."

"I'm not here to learn."

"No," Odile agreed. "You're here because you're frightened. Sit down anyway. Fear learns well; it has excellent reasons to pay attention." She picked up the chalk and wrote a single word on the board, in letters tall and sure, and said it aloud in a voice that made the room change temperature:

"*Albessa.*"

"What does it mean?" Noor asked, hating herself for asking.

"The light before dawn," her grandmother said. "The hour when the night admits it's losing." She looked at her granddaughter over her shoulder, and her eyes were wet and fierce at once. "We are in *albessa*, little one. All of us. The question is what we do with the last of the dark."

Noor did not sit down. But she did not leave, either. She stood by the door with her arms crossed while the lesson resumed around her — the librarian repeating sounds, her grandmother correcting, the chalk whispering — and she told herself she was staying to protect her grandmother's interests.

She stayed past dawn.

And when she got back to her car, her hands were shaking, and she could not have said — not honestly, not even to herself — whether it was rage she was feeling, or grief, or the beginnings of something far more dangerous than either.

Because in the margin of her grandmother's notebook, half-hidden under a paperweight, Noor had seen a name written in faded ink, over and over, like a wound worried by a thumb.

*Elias. Elias. Elias.*
