## 5. The Word for Goodbye Said Properly

The archive room had learned our shape by the third week of lessons. The lamp on the long table now angled toward Odile's chair without anyone adjusting it; the second chair had migrated closer, so that when she leaned forward to demonstrate a sound, her hand could find my forearm and press there — *here, feel it here* — as if language were something you caught through the skin rather than the ear. A tin of shortbread had appeared, unexplained, its lid dented as though it had traveled far. Noor's scarf hung on the coat hook some nights even when Noor herself did not come.

I had stopped telling myself this meant nothing. That was the danger of it. I had begun, in the small hours after she left, to write down what we'd covered, and my handwriting had gone loose and eager, a schoolboy's hand, someone's hand who wanted very much to be there again tomorrow.

On Thursday night the rain came down in ropes against the high windows, and Odile arrived with her collar wet and her eyes bright, carrying nothing but a small notebook bound in cracked green leather. She set it on the table between us like a card player laying down a winning hand.

"Tonight," she said, "the word."

"You've said that before."

"I have said that before," she agreed, "and each time I found a reason to teach you something easier first. Bread. Grief. The seventeen kinds of light that fall through a window at different hours." She unwound her scarf. "But you have been patient, and patience is a debt, and I am too old to carry debts."

She opened the notebook. The pages were soft at the edges, foxed with age, and the ink inside had faded from black to the brown of old tea. She turned past decades of entries until she reached one near the end, where the writing grew larger, more deliberate, the letters of someone writing slowly so the word would not escape the page.

"Lumençal has a word," she said, "that your languages do not. English says goodbye and means *I am leaving*. French says au revoir and means *I intend to return*, which is often a lie told politely. But Lumençal—" she touched the page— "Lumençal has *sòlvença*."

The word sat in the air between us. It sounded like water over stones, like something being washed.

"It does not mean solvency," she said, anticipating me. "It only borrowed the shape. *Sòlvença* is a goodbye said properly. Do you understand what I mean by properly? Not a goodbye that ends a thing. A goodbye that *releases* it."

"Releases," I repeated.

"When two people part badly — or part incompletely, which is worse — they carry each other. You know this." Her eyes were steady on mine. "You carry the argument you never finished. You carry the letter you never sent. Every time you say their name inside your head, you are still holding them, and they are still holding you, and neither of you can put your arms down. *Sòlvença* puts the arms down. Both pairs. The speaker releases the listener, and the listener releases the speaker, and what was carried becomes what was loved, and can be set down gently, the way you set down a sleeping child."

The rain filled the silence that followed. I realized I had not moved in some time.

"And it works both ways," I said finally. "You can't say it alone."

"No. That is its cruelty and its mercy. It requires the other person to be present, or at least to have been present once, and to have heard it. A *sòlvença* spoken into an empty room is only noise." She smiled, but it was a thin smile, stretched over something. "Which is why I have never said it aloud. Not once in sixty years."

"Not ever?"

"Not ever." She closed the notebook but kept her hand flat upon it, the way one keeps a hand on a door that might open onto weather. "I have said it in my head perhaps ten thousand times. To the same person. Always the same person."

I waited. Outside, a bus sighed past on the wet street below, its lights smearing gold across the ceiling of the archive room.

"Who?" I asked, because the question had been standing in me for weeks, patient as the rain.

Odile looked at me then with an expression I had not seen on her before — all the wit drained out of it, leaving something older and more frightened underneath, the face beneath the face. And she said, "That is not a lesson, Cyrus. That is a wound, and wounds are not curriculum."

"But you're going to say it to them. Someday. That's why you're learning—" I stopped, hearing myself. "That's why you needed a student. Someone to practice with. Someone to say it back to you until it's true."

Her hand tightened on the notebook. For a moment I thought she would be angry; she had a talent for anger, wore it like good jewelry. Instead she laughed, one short note, almost a sob wearing a disguise.

"You are not stupid," she said. "This is inconvenient for me. Yes. I am eighty-one years old and I am running out of the only currency that matters, which is memory, and I need someone whose mouth knows the sounds, because a *sòlvença* said badly is worse than none at all. It must be exact. It must be *clean*. And when the day comes, if it comes, I will need a witness who understands what they are witnessing." She drew a breath. "So. We practice. You will speak the listener's part, and I will speak mine, and we will say it wrong forty times so that someday one of us can say it right, once."

"To whom?" I asked again, softer.

"Cyrus." My name in Lumençal had a weight I hadn't earned yet — *Sí-rus*, the s falling away at the end like something let go. "Some doors you knock on. Some doors you wait beside until they open from the inside. Be the second kind."

* * *

We practiced. Or we began to.

The structure of *sòlvença* was simple the way a knife is simple. Three movements: naming what was shared, naming what was broken, letting go of the difference between them. Odile spoke her lines and I echoed them, stumbling, my tongue thick around vowels that lived in the back of the throat, and each time I got a phrase right her whole face changed — lit, briefly, like a window at dusk when someone inside turns on a lamp.

"*Ço qu'avèm partejat, l'ai vist,*" she said. *What we shared, I saw it.*

"*Ço qu'avèm partejat, l'ai vist,*" I answered.

"*Ço qu'es romput, lo daissi anar.*" *What broke, I let it go.*

And I answered, and she corrected the third vowel, and we went again, and the room grew warm with repetition, the rain keeping time outside like a metronome no one had asked for. Twice Noor appeared in the doorway with coffee and stayed leaning against the frame, saying nothing, watching her grandmother's mouth move in the old shapes, and I saw her hand come up to rest against her own sternum as though something in there had shifted position.

It was nearly four in the morning when it happened.

Odile was mid-phrase — she had been speaking about Elias, no, she had not been speaking about Elias, she had been speaking about the grammar of release, how the final syllable must fall and not be caught — and I remember her hands were moving, describing the falling of the sound, and then her hands stopped.

Not slowed. Stopped, mid-air, fingers still curved around a word.

"Cyrus," she said. Her voice was perfectly level. That was the terrible part, how level it was. "Why are we here?"

"We're practicing *sòlvença*," I said. "The goodbye word. You were showing me how the last syllable falls—"

"Yes." A pause. Her eyes moved across the table, the notebook, the cold tea, my face, cataloguing, searching. "Yes. Of course. The last syllable falls." But her voice had gone thin, a wire under tension, and I watched her reach for something behind her eyes and find the shelf empty where it should have been full. "What comes before the falling?"

"*Ço qu'es romput, lo daissi anar,*" I said quietly. *What broke, I let it go.*

"*Ço qu'es romput...*" she began, and stopped, and her hand rose to her mouth, and for three seconds — I counted them, I will count them for the rest of my life — Odile Vasseur, the last fluent speaker of a language of ten thousand precise names for light, could not find the word for *broken* in the only language that held it.

Then it came back. I saw it arrive, saw her shoulders drop half an inch, saw the color return beneath her skin. "*Lo daissi anar,*" she finished, and laughed, a small horrified laugh. "There it is. Hiding like a cat under a bed. Where was I."

"Teaching me to let the last syllable fall."

"Mm." She looked at her own hands as if they belonged to a stranger who had been borrowing them. Then she straightened, and the jewelry of her wit went back on, clasp by clasp. "Well. You see why we hurry."

Noor had come off the doorframe. She stood at the edge of the lamplight with her jaw tight and her eyes wet and furious, and when Odile said, brightly, "Noor, chérie, tell Cyrus what you called the rain on the way here, he needs the vocabulary," Noor opened her mouth, closed it, and walked out of the room. We heard the stairwell door bang.

Odile did not follow her. She smoothed the green notebook flat on the table, and her hand trembled, just slightly, and she pretended it didn't, and I let her pretend, because that seemed to be the arrangement — the unspoken treaty of the warm room.

"Again," she said. "From the beginning. And this time, Cyrus, when you speak the listener's part — mean it. Whatever it costs you to mean it. Because the word is worthless otherwise, and I have waited sixty years, and I do not intend to waste my remaining lessons on worthlessness."

We began again. *Ço qu'avèm partejat, l'ai vist.* What we shared, I saw it.

And it was only later, walking home through the rinsed streets at dawn, that I understood what I had agreed to — that somewhere there was a person Odile had been rehearsing for six decades, a person she owed a clean goodbye to, and that the lapses were coming faster than any of us were admitting, and that the next time her hands stopped mid-air, the word might not come back from under the bed at all.

I did not yet know there was a tape. I did not yet know the name Elias. But I knew, climbing my stairs in the grey light, that the warm room had a crack in it now, and that everything we loved was going to try to get out through it.
