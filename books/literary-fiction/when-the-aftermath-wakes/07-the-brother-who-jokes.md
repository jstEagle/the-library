## 7. The Brother Who Jokes

Theo Ishikawa had a theory about jokes, which was that they were load-bearing.

You could take a family — his family, say: a father who communicated primarily through the placement of dishes in a dishwasher, a mother who had loved them all in a dialect none of them ever fully learned to speak, a sister who asked questions the way other people filed lawsuits — and you could build an entire structure out of it, walls and roof and a functioning chimney, as long as somewhere in the foundation you set a joke or two, poured deep, so that when the weight came down, something gave before anything cracked.

He'd been laying jokes into foundations since he was nine years old. That was the year his grandmother died and he'd discovered, at the funeral, that if he made his cousin Danny laugh during the eulogy, Danny stopped crying, and then Theo stopped crying, and then for about ninety seconds nobody in the back pew was crying at all. Ninety seconds. He'd been chasing that ninety seconds his whole life, professionally now, behind the bar of his own restaurant, where he told the story of how he'd once flambéed his own eyebrows to a table of strangers every Friday night, and every Friday night it worked, and every Friday night he got his ninety seconds, and the building stood.

Three days after his mother's funeral, the building was making noises he didn't like.

* * *

It started, as most bad things in Theo's life did, with a delivery.

He'd come by the house on Marigold Street to drop off food — this was his love language, braised short ribs and a note that said *eat, don't argue* — and he'd found Mara in the living room surrounded by what she called an inventory and what looked, to him, like the aftermath of a very tidy burglary. Everything catalogued. Everything boxed. Their mother's life reduced to a spreadsheet with columns like *condition* and *provenance*, because of course Mara had made a spreadsheet. Mara made spreadsheets the way their mother had made silence: comprehensively, and without being asked.

"What's this one?" he'd asked, tapping a box labeled MISC. — STUDY, and Mara had gone still in a way that Theo recognized from twenty years of restaurant work. It was the stillness of a server standing next to a table where someone had just said something unforgivable. The stillness of *I will deal with this after service.*

"Paperwork," she'd said. "Old paperwork."

And Theo, who had built a career on reading faces across tables, had smiled and said, "Mom kept receipts from the Reagan administration? God. She really was going to return that fondue set," and he'd carried his short ribs into the kitchen, and he had not thought about it again for almost four hours, which for him was a kind of discipline.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing Theo had never said out loud, not to Kenji, not to Mara, not to the priest who'd heard his confession exactly twice and both times fallen asleep partway through.

He already knew there was something in the study.

* * *

Seven years ago. He could date it precisely, which was unusual for him; Theo's memory was famously decorative, full of good stories worn smooth by retelling. But that week he remembered like a scar remembers weather.

The restaurant — Kettle & Crow, his life's work, his beautiful money-burning child — was eleven days from dying. The 2008 crash had come through the dining room like a slow tide, pulling customers out chair by chair, and Theo had spent eleven days doing arithmetic he was bad at with numbers he hated, and the answer kept coming up *closed*. He'd lain awake doing versions of it. Sell the lease. Sell the car. Sell the ridiculous copper bar top he'd overpaid for because it made him feel like a man with a future.

And then his mother had appeared at the restaurant on a Tuesday afternoon, wearing her gray coat, carrying a manila envelope, and she'd sat at the corner table — table four, the wobbly one he'd never fixed because fixing it would have meant admitting he noticed things — and she'd slid the envelope across the copper like she was dealing cards.

"There's a loan," she'd said. "From the estate."

"The estate," Theo had repeated. "Mom, Grandpa's estate has been closed since I was in middle school."

"There are funds," she'd said, in the voice she used when a conversation was over before it began, "that were never distributed. This is a loan, Theodore. It will be repaid. And you will not ask me where it comes from."

And Theo — twenty-nine years old, terrified, in love with a failing restaurant the way you're only in love with things that can hurt you — had looked at the envelope, and looked at his mother's face, and made the choice that had been making small quiet payments against his soul ever since.

He hadn't asked.

He'd signed papers he only half-read. He'd noticed that the check came from an account with a bank he'd never heard his parents mention. He'd noticed that the promissory note named him as borrower but named no lender he could find in any registry, just a trust name, *the Verrecchia arrangement*, which he'd assumed was some tax thing, some immigrant-family legal architecture, the kind of old-world scaffolding you didn't poke at, like wiring in a prewar building. He'd noticed his mother watching him sign with an expression he'd never seen on her before and never saw again — not pride, not relief, something older and sadder, the face of a woman handing someone a lit match and hoping the rain holds off.

And he'd taken the money, and saved the restaurant, and never once, in seven years, let himself finish the thought.

That was the skill, actually. Not asking was easy. Anyone could not ask. The real craft — the thing Theo had refined to an art — was *not wondering*. You had to catch the question early, while it was still small and soft, and redirect it. Give it a better life somewhere else. His mother's undistributed funds became, in the country of Theo's mind, a land deal. An insurance settlement. A lucky stock his grandfather had bought from a guy at a racetrack. Every explanation was flimsy and every explanation was fine, because the alternative explanations all ended in the same place: his mother's face on that Tuesday, and the word *never* sitting in the air between them like smoke.

* * *

Now he stood in his mother's kitchen, three days after her funeral, eating a cold dumpling straight from the container because grief suspended the normal rules of plate usage, and he watched his sister through the doorway.

Mara was on the phone in the study. He couldn't hear the words, but he could hear the *shape* of them — the pauses too long, the voice pitched low and level, the levelness itself a tell. Mara leveled her voice the way other people raised it. When Mara sounded calmest, that was when you checked the exits.

"You're staring," said Kenji, coming up beside him. His father held a dish towel he wasn't using, the way he always did, holding the towel like a prop in a play about a man comfortable in kitchens.

"I'm observing," Theo said. "There's a difference. Staring is rude. Observing is free."

"She's taking it hard."

"She's taking it *administratively*." Theo popped another dumpling. "She's grieving in Excel. Did you see the boxes? There are labels. There's a labeling system. Mom died four days ago and Mara's already got her indexed like a library."

Kenji made a sound that was either a laugh or the memory of one. "Your mother would have appreciated the indexing."

"Mom would have hated it. Mom would have said—" and Theo stopped, because finishing that sentence required knowing what Mom would have said, and he was confronting, freshly, the fact that he didn't. Forty years of Sundays and he had his mother down to maybe six expressions and one recipe he could reproduce, and the rest of her — the interior rooms — he'd never toured. He'd lived in the house his whole childhood and never gone past the hallway.

"Theo." His father said his name the way he said it when Theo was eight and had broken something. Gently. Which was worse.

"Yeah?"

"Your sister is thorough. Thorough people find things."

Theo looked at him. Kenji was looking at the dish towel with tremendous focus, folding and unfolding a crease that already existed.

"Dad. Find what? What things?"

"Nothing." Kenji set the towel down with the precision of a man disarming himself. "I'm saying be patient with her. Grief makes people dig. Your mother used to say—" and there he stopped too, and Theo felt the floor of the conversation give slightly, both of them stepping back from the same soft spot at the same time, the way you learn to in a house where certain boards creak.

"Used to say what?" Theo asked.

"That some boxes stay shut until you're ready to carry what's in them." Kenji picked the towel back up. "Eat your dumplings, Theodore. They're getting sad."

* * *

That night Theo couldn't sleep, which was rare; sleep was another of his talents, right up there with deflection and knife skills. He lay in his apartment above the restaurant listening to the walk-in cooler hum its one note, and he did the thing he never did.

He wondered.

Not the managed kind. The real kind, the kind he usually caught early and drowned. He lay there and let the question grow to its full adult size, and it stood over his bed like a creditor: *Where did the money come from?*

He tried the old answers. Land deal — no, he'd have heard about a land deal, the family gossip network, thin as it was, ran entirely through his aunt Rosemary and she'd have mentioned acreage. Insurance — no, insurance companies sent letters, and his mother had handed him cash-adjacent paper with no letters attached. Racetrack stocks — okay, that one had never survived daylight anyway.

And underneath the failed answers, patient as water, was the other thing. The thing he'd stepped around for seven years the way you step around a sleeping dog.

His mother had said *you will not ask me where it comes from.* Not *I won't tell you.* Not *it's complicated.* She'd said *you will not ask*, which was the grammar of concealment, the verb tense of shame. People with clean money said *come by the bank, we'll do the paperwork.* People with dirty money said *don't ask.*

And there was more, there was sediment under all of it, little grains he'd swallowed years ago without chewing: the way his mother had gone silent — truly silent, a silence with mass — whenever Alder Bay came up on the news, which it occasionally did, mostly around anniversaries of some fire or flood Theo had never bothered to learn the details of. The way the family photo albums had a gap in the late eighties, a few years thin as a famine, as if someone had curated even their memories. The way his grandfather — cheerful, generous Grandpa Emilio, dead before Theo turned ten — had been spoken of afterward only in the past-perfect, only in finished sentences, as though whatever he'd done had been concluded, settled, *handled*.

Handled. His mother's favorite word. *It's handled, Theodore.* He'd always heard it as competence. Lying there in the dark above his restaurant, he heard it for the first time as what it might also have been: a door, closed from the inside.

"Okay," Theo said out loud, to the ceiling, to nobody. "Okay. But here's the thing."

Here was the thing. Here was the thing he'd tell Mara if she ever showed him what was in those boxes, the defense he'd been drafting for seven years without admitting he was drafting it:

He'd had a restaurant full of people depending on him. Line cooks with kids. A dishwasher named Miguel sending money home. Eleven days from the end. And his mother — his cold, careful, unknowable mother — had driven across town in her gray coat and put her whole self, whatever her self was built on, under the foundation of his life. Whatever the money was, it had become Miguel's kid's braces and Rosa's citizenship lawyer and the ninety seconds every Friday night when a room full of strangers laughed together. You couldn't un-ring that. You couldn't audit a miracle.

Complicity, he thought, was just love that had read the fine print late.

He fell asleep sometime after three, and dreamed of table four, the wobbly one, and woke with the answer to the wobble on the tip of his tongue and lost it in the shower.

* * *

Friday he cooked. It was what he did instead of praying. He went to the house on Marigold Street with ingredients and took over the kitchen, because a house in mourning needs someone standing at a stove; it gives the mourners somewhere to drift toward. Kenji drifted. Even Mara drifted, eventually, drawn by the smell of dashi and the sight of her brother actually being useful, and for forty minutes the three of them existed in the old configuration — Dad at the counter pretending to supervise, Mara chopping scallions with surgical precision, Theo narrating his own cooking like a game show host — and it was so close to bearable that Theo almost missed it.

Almost. Because halfway through the meal, Mara set down her chopsticks, and looked at their father, and said, in the level voice, the calm voice, the voice Theo now understood was a warning siren with the volume broken:

"Dad. I need to ask you about Alder Bay."

Theo's hand stopped moving. The kitchen went very quiet, the particular quiet of a room where everyone has just realized the dog has been asleep under the table the whole time.

Kenji looked at his daughter for a long moment. Then he looked at his hands.

And Theo, who had spent seven years perfecting the art of not wondering, felt the question stand up inside him at last, full-grown, wide awake, and ask itself.
