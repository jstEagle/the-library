## 1. The Inventory of Unsaid Things

The Meridian Branch Library did not close at nine like the other branches. It closed at two in the morning, and even then it never fully closed—Jude Ashgrove saw to that. They kept one lamp burning at the reference desk, a green-shaded banker's lamp older than the building itself, and they left the front door unlocked for the last ten minutes of every night, a courtesy no one had asked for and everyone who needed it understood.

The insomniacs came at their own hours. There was Dr. Sable, retired from something he never named, who read cardiology journals he had no use for and fell asleep sitting up with his glasses sliding down his nose until the alarm on his watch buzzed him awake at 1:40 sharp. There was a woman called only Miss Ferro by the day staff, though she told Jude once, in the hushed voice people reserve for confessions made under fluorescent light, that her name was Beatrice and she hadn't used it since her husband died. She read romance novels in strict alphabetical order by author, and when she finished an author's complete works she started over with someone new, as if love were a language you could only keep by translating it endlessly.

And there was the boy—seventeen, maybe eighteen—who came in around midnight most nights smelling faintly of fryer oil from the diner where he worked the closing shift, and who sat in the far carrel by the window and did not read anything at all. He just sat. Jude had catalogued him too, in the private ledger, under *things I have not said*: *I know why you're here. I was here once myself, in another city, in another life. The library is the only building that doesn't ask what happened to you.*

That was the ledger. Not a real ledger—not leather-bound, not official, nothing anyone could subpoena or shelve. It lived in three composition notebooks in the bottom drawer of Jude's desk at home, written in a shorthand of their own devising, dated like an archive, cross-referenced like an obsession. Fifteen years of silences, itemized.

*Entry 4,112: Did not tell Miss Ferro that her husband's obituary is digitized and I found it while verifying a citation. He looks kind. Did not say so.*

*Entry 4,113: Did not tell the boy in the window carrel that the fire exits are clearly marked and that I check them every night, twice, because of what fire does.*

*Entry 4,114: Did not tell my therapist—I stopped going eight months ago—that the ledger has become the truest account of my life. Everything I actually felt is in there, filed under everything I failed to say out loud.*

The ledger had begun the winter after the fire, when Jude was fifteen and living with an aunt in a city whose name they had worked hard to forget, and it had begun, in all honesty, as a symptom. The school counselor had suggested journaling. *Write down what you can't talk about,* she'd said, in the gentle voice adults used with children who had survived things, and Jude, literal-minded and hollowed out, had taken the instruction as a professional directive. An inventory. A finding aid for the collection of a life.

They had never stopped.

Now they were twenty-nine, and the inventory ran to three notebooks and a memory that functioned less like memory than like a climate-controlled vault. Jude could tell you the exact date they last spoke to Mrs. Okonkwo across the fence on Delmore Street—the second Sunday of October, fifteen years ago, when she'd handed over a plate of lemon bars and said, *You take care of your sister now*, and Jude had said, *I will*, and Wren had been dead four days already, though nobody knew it yet, because nobody ever found her.

The city where the Meridian Branch stood was not Hollow Creek. That was the point of it. It was a city of 1.2 million people who had no reason to learn your name, a city where the blue hour between sunset and full dark lasted exactly as long as it lasted everywhere but felt longer here, stretched thin over the interstate noise, and Jude had chosen it the way you choose a hiding place: not for comfort but for capacity. A city big enough to lose yourself in. A city where a person could work the night shift at a public library and be, in every meaningful sense, invisible.

Jude had gotten very good at invisibility. It was, if anything, their professional specialty. Cataloguing was the art of making things findable, and Jude had spent fifteen years perfecting the inverse craft: making themselves unfindable. No social media. No photographs. A phone number attached to nothing. When patrons asked personal questions—and the insomniacs always eventually did—Jude answered with the mild deflection of a reference librarian: *Let me see what I can find on that.* Their name was common enough. Their history was sealed. The house on Delmore Street had stood empty and gutted for fifteen years behind its chain-link fence, and the county had finally, apparently, decided to do something about it.

But Jude didn't know that yet. Not at 11:52 on a Tuesday night in March, when the certified letter arrived—not arrived, really; it had arrived days ago at the apartment and Jude had let it sit on the kitchen table among the takeout menus, because certified mail meant someone wanted proof you'd received something, and Jude had built a whole life on the principle of receiving nothing—but became aware of it, rather, at 11:52, when they finally picked it up on their way out the door to the night shift, turned it over once, registered the return address—*COUNTY OF HOLLOW CREEK, OFFICE OF THE COMMISSIONERS*—and put it in their coat pocket, unopened, the way you pocket a stone you intend later to throw into deep water.

Dread, in Jude's experience, wore the costume of routine better than any other emotion. You could carry almost anything into a workday if you folded it small enough and put it in a pocket.

* * *

The Meridian Branch at midnight had a sound Jude thought of as the building breathing. The HVAC cycled. The old radiators ticked as they cooled. Somewhere in the stacks, a book would settle on its shelf with a papery sigh, and if you were new to the night shift this would frighten you the first dozen times, and then one night you'd realize the building was simply digesting its day, and after that you'd find it companionable.

Jude found everything companionable now. That was the trick of the long silence—it stopped being a wound and became a temperament.

At 12:20, Dr. Sable arrived, coat buttoned wrong, and took his seat in the periodicals section without acknowledging Jude, which was his way of acknowledging Jude. At 12:35, Miss Ferro—Beatrice—came in from the cold with a paperback clutched to her chest like a hymnal and nodded once in Jude's direction. At 1:10, the boy slipped through the unlocked door during Jude's deliberate ten-minute absence from the desk, and went to his carrel, and sat.

Jude shelved. That was most of the night shift, honestly—shelving, shifting, checking the fire exits, walking the long aisles under the emergency lights with a cart that squeaked on the third wheel. The Dewey hundreds moved through their hands: religion, social sciences, language. In the 900s, history, where the past got sorted by continent and century as if it stayed put. Jude had opinions about that. The past didn't stay anywhere. It circulated. It got checked out by people who returned it dog-eared and coffee-stained, annotated in pencil in margins, and sometimes it never came back at all, and you filed a claim, and the claim went nowhere, and after a while you marked it *lost* and ordered a replacement copy that was never quite the same book.

Around 1:30, Miss Ferro approached the desk with her next romance—a historical, judging by the man with the loosened cravat on the cover—and while Jude checked it out, she lingered, the way she did every few weeks, hovering at the edge of conversation like a moth testing glass.

"You look tired tonight," she said.

"I'm always tired at 1:30."

"No." Beatrice tilted her head, studying Jude with the frank appraisal of a woman who had outlived the need for politeness. "Tired like something's following you."

Jude slid the book across the desk. "It's due in three weeks. There's a hold list, so don't renew it twice."

"Deflection," said Beatrice, but she took the book, and smiled, and went back to her chair, and Jude added to the mental ledger: *Entry 4,115: She saw it. Did not confirm. Did not deny. Filed under: how long can a person be a locked room before the building itself starts rattling?*

Because that was the thing Jude never wrote down, the entry beneath all the entries, the one that predated the notebooks: the silences weren't peace. They were pressure. Fifteen years of unsaid things stacked floor upon floor inside a person, and Jude had always known, in the way you know a load-bearing wall is cracked, that the structure would hold right up until the moment it didn't.

* * *

At 1:58, Jude walked the building. This was ritual: lights off in sequence, east wing first, then the reading room, leaving the green lamp at the desk burning until the very end. Dr. Sable had gone at 1:40, woken by his watch, grumbling gently. The boy was still in his carrel, and Jude paused at the end of the aisle, as they did every night, weighing the unsaid thing against the said.

Tonight—for reasons they couldn't have named, reasons that would only become legible later, the way a burn pattern becomes legible only to someone who knows what accelerant looks like—Jude broke pattern.

"Fire exits are in the back left," they said. "If you ever need them."

The boy looked up. His face in the window light was younger than his hands, which were stained with ink from the diner's order tickets. "Okay," he said, uncertain.

"And the building doesn't close until two," Jude added. "So you don't have to rush."

He stared. Then, quietly: "Thanks." And went back to sitting, which was his form of reading, and Jude walked away with their heart going strangely fast, having said aloud, for the first time in years, a thing they'd been carrying.

Small. It was a small thing. But the ledger demanded updating, and updating it required opening the drawer, and opening the drawer meant standing in the back office at 2:04 a.m. with the composition notebooks in hand, and it was there, pen uncapped, that Jude remembered the letter in their coat pocket.

* * *

They opened it at the reference desk under the green lamp, with the building dark around them and the blue hour still hours away, and they read it the way they read everything now—twice, slowly, hunting the gap between what the words said and what they meant.

*NOTICE OF PUBLIC AUCTION. Pursuant to Chapter 6201 of the County Code, the following property, condemned and held in trust by the County of Hollow Creek, will be sold at public auction to the highest bidder:* and then the address, and Jude's hand went still on the desk so completely that the pen rolled off the edge and clattered on the floor, and the sound in the silent building was enormous.

*1421 Delmore Street, Hollow Creek.*

Their house. The ruin. The gutted, fenced, fifteen-year wound of a house, with its dark window where Wren's room had been, scheduled for sale on the twenty-sixth day of April at ten o'clock in the morning on the courthouse steps, *sold as-is, where-is, without warranty of any kind.*

Jude read it a third time. A fourth. The words didn't change. Words from the county never changed; that was their nature—they were mortar, not blood. *Condemned and held in trust.* As if the county had been keeping the house safe all these years. As if trust were what had happened there.

The lamp hummed. Somewhere in the stacks the building sighed and settled. At the window carrel, the boy sat motionless in the dark, keeping his own vigil, and Jude understood suddenly that they were the same, all of them—the insomniacs, the night staff, the whole quiet economy of people who couldn't stop watching the doors—guarding something, waiting for something, unable to sleep inside their own lives.

Fifteen years, Jude thought. Fifteen years of inventory, of unsaid things, of a silence maintained like a rare book in controlled humidity. And now a piece of paper with a county seal proposed to sell the origin of all of it—to some stranger, some developer, some buyer who would knock down the last physical evidence that the Ashgroves had ever existed, that Wren had ever had a bedroom with a window facing east, that a fire had taken a family and returned only one child, incomplete.

The letter said the auction was in six weeks.

Jude sat under the green lamp until nearly four, not moving, while outside the windows the sky performed its slow, indifferent turn toward morning, and the dread that had ridden in their coat pocket all night finally unfolded itself, stretched, and took its true shape—which was not dread at all, but a question, arriving late and unwelcome:

*Who, exactly, had been holding their house in trust—and what, in fifteen years, had the county been keeping inside it?*

The ledger stayed in the drawer that night, unopened. Some entries, Jude understood, had to be earned first.
