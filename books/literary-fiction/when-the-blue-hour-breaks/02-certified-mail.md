## 2. Certified Mail

The letter sat on Jude's kitchen table for three days before they opened it.

Not because they didn't know what it was. They knew from the moment the postal carrier's knuckles hit the door—the specific double-knock reserved for things requiring a signature, things that could not simply be left in a mailbox like ordinary weather. Jude had watched through the peephole as the carrier held up the envelope, waited the statutory fifteen seconds, and slid the notice of attempted delivery into the slot. The whole transaction took less than a minute. Jude spent the rest of that day at work, cataloguing a bequest of forty-seven boxes from a dead professor of classics, and thought about the envelope only every eleven minutes or so.

That was the trick of it. You didn't refuse to think about a thing. You just scheduled the thinking so tightly that it couldn't spread.

The second notice came on Thursday. The third on Monday. By then the envelope had been signed for at the post office window—Jude had gone after shift, told themselves it was only to stop the accumulation of orange slips—and carried home like something with a pulse.

They set it on the table again. The kitchen was small and clean, the way rooms get when no one lives in them so much as passes through. A kettle. A single chair. A wall calendar Jude never wrote on but kept because blankness was also a kind of record: *this month, nothing happened; this month, I endured it.* Fifteen years of blank calendars stacked in a closet somewhere, an archive of absence.

Jude made tea they wouldn't drink and sat down across from the envelope as if it were a witness.

The return address meant nothing and everything: *County Administration Building, Office of Tax Delinquency & Property Disposition, Hollow Creek.* The paper inside would be cream stock, probably, with a seal embossed in blue. It would use words like *premises* and *hereinafter* and *as-is, where-is.* It would reduce a house to a parcel number.

It would say, in the language of paperwork, *your family is finished being remembered by us.*

Jude opened it on the third day because the alternative was opening it on the fourth, and some part of them—the part that kept ledgers, the part that counted—understood that deferral was not the same as refusal. Deferral was just refusal with interest.

*NOTICE OF SHERIFF'S SALE.*

*PARCEL 04-117-003. FORMERLY KNOWN AS 14 WREN HOLLOW ROAD.*

Wren Hollow Road. The street had been named for some ancestor of some other family entirely—a Wren who'd died in 1911, probably, a Wren with no relation to them—but their parents had always treated it as an omen, a good one. *We were meant to live here,* their mother used to say, tapping the street sign like it owed her money. *Meant to.*

The letter said the property had been condemned following structural assessment, that taxes in arrears totaled an amount so precise it could only have been calculated by someone who enjoyed arithmetic the way other people enjoyed cruelty, and that the premises would be offered at public auction on a date six weeks hence.

Six weeks. The past, which had floated unanchored for fifteen years, suddenly had a deadline. It was going to be sold to the highest bidder, gutted or razed, replaced by whatever Hollow Creek was becoming—condos, a brewery, a green space with a commemorative plaque. And whatever was still in that house—not furniture, not photographs, those had burned or been hauled away, but *something*, the residue, the ash-memory, the shape a body leaves in a bed—would belong to strangers.

Jude read the letter four times that night. Then they did what they always did with unbearable documents: they copied it out by hand into the ledger.

The ledger was a hardbound notebook, black, unlined, bought in bulk a decade ago. On its pages, in a hand too controlled to be called handwriting, Jude recorded the inventory of unsaid things. Not a diary—diaries confessed, and confession was a currency Jude had never trusted. This was different. This was a catalogue. Each entry numbered, dated, cross-referenced.

*Entry 1,412. Did not tell the postal carrier my name when he asked. Said "occupant."*

*Entry 1,413. Read the auction notice aloud once, alone, to hear how it sounded in a voice. It sounded like a verdict being read to an empty courtroom.*

*Entry 1,414. Have not said Wren's name in 214 days. (Last instance: correcting a coworker who mispronounced it. She said "Rin." I said "Wren," and then could not speak for the rest of the shift.)*

Some entries ran pages. Some were a single line. Entry 893, from nine years ago, was one word: *kettle.* Jude no longer remembered what unsaid thing attached to it, only that at the time the kettle's whistle had been unbearable, and that the unbearable thing had needed a number.

This was the sickness, if you wanted to call it that, and Jude did, privately, clinically, the way you'd diagnose a stranger. Most people let the unsaid things dissolve. They metabolized grief into anecdote, anecdote into small talk, small talk into silence, and the silence healed over like skin. Jude's silences didn't heal. They accumulated. They demanded housing. So Jude built them a library—one notebook at a time—and became, in effect, the night librarian of their own withheld life.

It was almost two a.m. when they finished copying the notice. The tea had gone cold in its cup, a brown coin at the bottom. Outside, the city performed its nightly argument with itself—sirens, a garbage truck, someone laughing on the corner with the particular laugh of the very drunk or the very free.

Jude looked at the date circled in the notice. Six weeks.

Then, in the margin of the ledger, beneath Entry 1,415 (*Have decided to go*), they wrote a smaller line, the kind of note a cataloguer makes when the collection has begun to misbehave:

*Buyer already identified? Verify.*

They didn't know yet why they wrote it. Instinct, maybe. Fifteen years of handling other people's estates had taught Jude that nothing about property is ever accidental—that behind every clean transaction there is usually a dirty handshake, and behind every dirty handshake, a signature.

* * *

The county records office occupied the ground floor of the Hollow Creek Administration Building, a poured-concrete structure from 1968 that looked like it had been designed by someone who hated both concrete and buildings. Jude drove out on a Tuesday, having requested two days' leave with a lie about a dental procedure. The lie went into the ledger that evening as Entry 1,417. The dental appointment itself would need to become real eventually, or the ledger would contain a false statement, and false statements in the ledger were—Jude understood this without articulating it—the beginning of the end.

The records office smelled of toner and old carpet. Fluorescent lights buzzed at a frequency that seemed calibrated to loosen fillings. A single clerk worked the counter, a woman maybe fifty with reading glasses pushed up into gray hair and a nameplate that said R. VANCE.

"Help you?"

"I'm looking for the file on a condemned property. Parcel 04-117-003."

Vance looked up. Something moved behind her eyes—not recognition exactly, but the approach of it, like weather on a horizon. "Which property is that?"

"The Ashgrove house." Jude heard themselves say the word plainly, and was surprised. In fifteen years they had not said *Ashgrove* aloud to anyone in Hollow Creek. "On Wren Hollow Road."

The clerk's hands stopped moving on the keyboard. For a moment the office was very quiet, just the hum of the lights and, somewhere in the back, a fax machine clearing its throat.

"You're one of the Ashgrove kids," Vance said. Not a question.

"The surviving one."

Something flickered across the woman's face—pity, or its bureaucratic cousin, discomfort—and she turned to her terminal with renewed attention, as if the screen required supervision. "Condemnation files are public record. I can pull it, but there's a copy fee if you want printouts."

"That's fine."

She typed. Jude stood at the counter and studied the notices pinned to the corkboard behind her: a budget hearing, a lost dog, a flyer for the courthouse portrait unveiling honoring the Honorable Ambrose Pell, *Thirty Years of Service to Hollow Creek.* Pell's face on the flyer was the face from Jude's childhood—silver-haired, benevolent, the face of a man who presided over juvenile cases with a jar of peppermints on his bench and a reputation for mercy that the town treasured like a relic. He'd spoken at the memorial service. He'd put his hand on fourteen-year-old Jude's shoulder and said, *If there's anything you ever need,* and the weight of that hand had felt less like comfort than like a paperweight, pinning something flat.

"Here we are." The printer began its slow mechanical breathing. "You said you wanted the full file?"

"Everything. Condemnation order, inspection reports, tax history. All of it."

Vance fed pages into a stack without looking at them, which struck Jude as odd. Clerks who handled condemnation files all day developed either calluses or curiosity, and curiosity would have made her glance. Her not-glancing had the quality of deliberate practice.

"Lot of interest in that parcel lately," she said, to the printer rather than to Jude.

"Interest?"

"Mm." She aligned the stack, tapped it square against the counter. "Third request this quarter. Usually a condemned house sits in the file drawer for years gathering dust, nobody cares till the bulldozer shows up. This one—" she shrugged, a gesture engineered to communicate nothing, "—people ask."

"Who asks?"

"A title company. An appraiser." She slid the copies across the counter along with a receipt. "And a fellow from Corliss Development, couple months back. Wanted to know the timeline. When it'd come to auction, whether there'd be any complications."

Jude took the papers. "Complications."

"His word." Vance finally met their eyes, and what was in her face now wasn't discomfort. It was something more careful than that—the expression of a person standing near a ledge and deciding, consciously, not to look down. "Corliss Development does a lot of business in this county. Buys distressed properties, flips them, builds. They're good for the tax base, the commissioners say." A pause, measured out like medicine. "They're friends of the court, you might say."

"Friends of the court," Jude repeated.

"Judge Pell sits on their charity board. The Hollow Creek Renewal Foundation." She said it the way you'd recite a fact printed on a monument—true, public, untouchable. "He's being honored next month, actually. Portrait's going up in the rotunda." She nodded toward the flyer behind her. "Thirty years."

The fluorescent light buzzed. Somewhere a phone rang twice and gave up.

"Ms. Vance," Jude said. "Can I ask you something off the record?"

"No." But she said it gently, and she didn't turn back to her terminal, and she didn't call for the next person in line, though there was no next person in line. The office was empty except for the two of them and the dead air conditioning and the portrait flyer with its benevolent silver face.

"Then on the record," Jude said. "Is it normal for a buyer to be lined up before an auction? Before the sale is even noticed?"

Vance was quiet long enough that the question might have gone unanswered forever. Then she picked up her stamp, inked it, and brought it down on the receipt with a sound like a small gavel.

"It's normal here," she said.

* * *

Jude read the file in the car, in the parking lot, with the windows cracked despite the cold, because the building's air had started to feel like water closing over their head.

Most of it was what they expected: the inspection report citing foundation failure and fire damage, the condemnation order, the tax arrears schedule. The fire investigator's report from fifteen years ago was thin—four pages, cause listed as *probable electrical malfunction, origin undetermined due to extent of damage.* Jude had never seen those four pages before. Their aunt had handled everything back then, or failed to handle it, and the file had lived here in this drawer through two administrations, through Jude's entire adult exile, waiting.

But it was the dates that stopped them. Not the contents. The dates.

The inspection report was dated March 3rd. The condemnation order, bearing the inspector's signature and a county stamp, was dated March 9th. Standard enough—six days between inspection and order, brisk but plausible.

Except the fire marshal's investigation hadn't closed until April 22nd. Jude knew this because the closure memo was in the same file, stapled behind everything else, and it referenced the inspection report by number, confirming the property's status as *already condemned pending final determination of cause.*

Already condemned. Before the cause was determined. Before anyone officially knew what had happened inside those walls, the county had signed the house's death certificate—and had done so using paperwork that predated the autopsy.

Jude sat with that for a long time. A delivery truck idled three spaces over. A man walked past with a sandwich. The world continued its indifferent errands.

It could be sloppiness. Bureaucracies were rivers of sloppiness; documents got backdated, signatures got collected in batches, clerks rubber-stamped stacks of paper without reading them. There was an innocent explanation. There was almost always an innocent explanation, and most of the time the innocent explanation was even true.

But the innocent explanation didn't account for Corliss Development asking about timelines two months ago. Didn't account for a judge's charity board. Didn't account for the way R. Vance had said *it's normal here* in the voice of a woman describing a tide she'd stopped fighting years ago.

Jude got out the ledger, right there in the front seat, and wrote:

*Entry 1,418. The house was declared dead before anyone said how it died. Filed under: questions I am no longer permitted not to ask.*

Then, below it, because the ledger was honest even when its keeper wished to be otherwise:

*Entry 1,419. I have not thought about the hall closet in fifteen years. Today I thought about it for forty minutes. What she kept in there. What burned in there. What the fire report says was never found.*

The pen hovered. There was one more entry, and Jude wrote it slowly, pressing hard, the way you press when you want the impression to go through several sheets and reach something underneath:

*Entry 1,420. If the fire was not electrical—if it was ever anything else—then the county helped bury it. And the county is selling the grave.*

Outside, the afternoon was turning the color of old paper. The Administration Building loomed in the rearview mirror, and above its entrance, carved into the lintel by some optimistic mason in 1968, the county motto: *THE RECORD SPEAKS FOR ITSELF.*

Jude started the car.

Six weeks to the auction. One file, four pages short of honest, sitting in their lap. And somewhere in the back of the drawer, in the place where R. Vance's careful eyes refused to look, the beginning of an answer to a question Jude had carried since they were fourteen:

*What was in the closet? What did the fire take—or what did it take the fire for?*

They pulled out of the lot and pointed the car toward the interstate, toward the city, toward the library and the ledger and the life built carefully on top of a hole. But their hands were shaking on the wheel, and they knew, with the certainty of a cataloguer who has just discovered a misfiled document in a closed collection, that the hole had never been empty.

Something was in it. And in six weeks, someone intended to pave it over.
