## 6. Return to Hollow Creek

Jude requested the leave in writing, which was itself a small confession. Nobody at the night library took vacations; the insomniacs depended on continuity the way drowning men depend on a rope, and Jude had been rope for eleven years without fraying once. The director, a mild man named Okafor who kept a bonsai on his desk and watered it with suspicious tenderness, read the form twice.

"Family matter?" he asked.

"The house," Jude said. "The Ashgrove house. It's being auctioned."

Something moved behind his face—recognition, then the careful blankness of a man deciding not to have recognized anything. "Ah," he said. "Hollow Creek." He signed it. "Take two weeks. Take three. The city will still be awake when you get back."

It would be. That was the problem with cities; they never gave you an excuse to leave them, only reasons to stay that accumulated like sediment. Jude went home to the apartment above the laundromat and packed in forty minutes: clothes, the certified letter, the ledger of unsaid things, and—after standing over the duffel for a long moment—the file from the county records office, the one with the condemnation order dated nine days before any investigator had set foot in the ashes.

They did not tell anyone where they were going. This was habit, not tradecraft. Fifteen years of telling no one had worn a groove so deep that secrecy now ran along it automatically, like water finding a channel cut long ago.

The car was eleven years old and smelled of cold coffee. Jude loaded it at four in the afternoon, in the blue hour, because they could not bear to leave at any other time of day. There was a superstition in this, or maybe just a signature. They drove east out of the city with the sun going down behind them, throwing their shadow ahead like a scout sent forward into country they didn't trust.

---

Hollow Creek announced itself the way old injuries announce weather: gradually, then all at once. First the highway signs changed—fewer billboards for casinos, more for orchards and antique barns. Then the land flattened into the river valley, corn stubble and fence lines and the water tower rising over the treeline with HOLLOW CREEK painted on it in letters that had been repainted sometime in the last decade, because the rust streaks beneath were younger than the ones in Jude's memory.

They had driven this road at fourteen, in the back seat of their father's sedan, arguing with Wren about whose turn it was to sit by the window. Wren always won. Wren always won arguments the way rivers won against banks—not through force but through patience, through simply continuing until everything else gave way.

Jude pulled off at the scenic overlook, the one with the plaque about the founding mill, and sat with the engine ticking and looked down at the town.

It was smaller than memory insisted. That was the first betrayal. In fifteen years of night thinking, Hollow Creek had grown to the size of a grief, which is to say infinite. From the overlook it was a grid of rooftops around a courthouse spire, cupped in the river's bend, smoke rising from two or three chimneys in the early dark. Ordinary. Forgettable, even, if you didn't know what was buried under it.

But something else was wrong, and it took Jude a full minute to name it. The edges of town were wrong. Where the trailer park used to be there was a development of identical houses with identical black shutters, each with a small manicured lawn and a stone facade that pretended at permanence. A sign at its entrance read THE MEADOWS AT HOLLOW CREEK, though there was no meadow and hadn't been since Jude was a child. Farther in, the old feed store had become a wine bar. The hardware store where Mr. Delgado had let Jude buy single nails for a nickel was a yoga studio with a chalkboard sandwich board out front advertising a class called Restorative Flow.

Gentrification, Jude thought, is just erasure with better lighting. The town had learned to renovate around its rot the way a body walls off infection—beautifully, functionally, and without ever removing anything.

They drove down into it.

---

Main Street had been rebranded, though the street itself refused the new name. Someone had installed decorative banners on the lampposts—HERITAGE & HARVEST, they said, in a font designed to look hand-lettered—and beneath them the buildings stood in various states of costume. The bank had a fresh coat of paint and a coffee counter inside. The five-and-dime was a boutique selling candles at prices that would have made the whole Ashgrove grocery budget blush. But between these renovated faces, the rot showed through like bone through skin: a storefront with papered windows and a FOR LEASE sign gone gray with weather; the old movie theater, marquee still reading the last film it ever showed, some comedy from nineteen years ago, its letters faded to ghosts.

And everywhere, on every surface, the same face.

Jude saw him first on a campaign-style yard sign—*Honoring a Lifetime of Service*—and then on a poster taped inside the wine bar window, and then, unavoidably, on a banner stretched across the courthouse steps themselves. Judge Ambrose Pell, silver-haired, kind-eyed, jaw like a ship's prow. The banner announced a portrait unveiling ceremony, open to the public, this Saturday at noon. *Celebrating Forty Years on the Bench.*

Forty years. Jude did the arithmetic without wanting to. Thirty-nine years ago, Pell had been a young judge already. Twenty-five years ago, he'd been the judge everyone brought their disputes to, the one who'd spoken at the memorial service for the Ashgrove fire, his voice breaking convincingly on the word *tragedy*. Jude remembered standing in the front row of the community hall in a borrowed black dress, fourteen years old and hollowed out, while Ambrose Pell put his hand on their shoulder and said, *If there's anything you need, you come to me.*

They had needed. They had never come. And now the town was going to gild him.

Jude parked in the lot behind the diner—which was still the diner, mercifully, though the neon in the window now said EST. 1962 as if longevity were a flavor—and walked Main Street end to end with their hands in their coat pockets, cataloguing. It was what they did. Fifteen years of inventory work had made the whole world into items awaiting description, and walking a familiar street was no different from shelving returns: here the bench where Wren used to eat her lunch upside down, legs hooked over the backrest, scandalizing Mrs. Pruitt; here the spot where the payphone had been, replaced by a QR code on a pole; here the alley mouth where—

Here the alley mouth where nothing happened. Here the ordinary brick wall of the ordinary pharmacy, smelling faintly of rain and old gum. Jude stood in front of it and felt the vertigo rise anyway, because familiarity itself had become untrustworthy. Every benign surface might be load-bearing. Every memory might have been edited by people with money and influence and forty years of practice.

A woman came out of the pharmacy with a small dog on a pink leash. She looked at Jude, looked again, slowed.

"Well," she said. "Well, I'll be."

Jude braced. This was the part they had dreaded on the whole drive: recognition, and the questions that came after it like birds after a plow.

"You're one of the Ashgrove children," the woman said. Not a question after all. Her dog sniffed Jude's shoe with professional thoroughness. "I taught Sunday school, years back. You sat in the second row and never sang." She said it without accusation, almost fondly. "I'm sorry about your folks. That was a hard year for everybody."

*A hard year for everybody.* Jude turned the phrase over and found it perfectly preserved, fossilized, exactly as it had been spoken in 1998 and 2003 and presumably last week. The town's grief vocabulary had not evolved at all. It had merely been dusted and put back on display, like the theater marquee.

"Thank you," Jude said, because there was no other available sentence.

"They're doing the portrait Saturday," the woman said, nodding up the street toward the banner. "Judge Pell. He confirmed my mother's estate papers, you know. Finest man this county's ever produced." She smiled, adjusted the leash. "You should come. Whole town will be there."

Whole town, Jude thought. Except the parts of it that burned.

She went on into the evening with her dog, and Jude stood under the HERITAGE & HARVEST banner feeling like a photograph left in a frame that had been rehung on a different wall. Everything fit. Nothing matched.

---

The motel at the edge of town was called the River Rest, which was optimistic on both counts. Jude checked in under their own name—they had considered an alias and rejected it, out of some stubborn principle they couldn't fully articulate—and carried the bag up outside stairs to room 14. From the window they could see the water tower, lit now against the dark, and beyond it the black line of trees that marked the river.

They unpacked the ledger first. Set it on the nightstand like a guest of honor. Then the county file, and they read the condemnation order again by lamplight, the way you reread a ransom note looking for the detail that breaks the case. *Structure deemed unsafe following preliminary assessment. Signed:* and the signature, and the date. Nine days before the fire marshal's investigation officially opened. Nine days before anyone could have known the house was unsafe, because until it burned, nobody had thought it was anything but a house.

Unless someone had known it was going to burn.

Jude closed the file. Sat with their hands flat on the cover, breathing the motel smell of industrial lavender and other people's cigarettes. Outside, a truck went past on the county road, headlights sweeping the ceiling.

Sleep, as always, declined the invitation. Around two in the morning Jude gave up, dressed, and drove.

---

There was no reason to go to the house. They knew that. There was no plan in it, no investigative purpose; the auction file told them nothing the ruins could, and Mrs. Okonkwo's sentence—*your sister came back once*—was still sitting in Jude's chest like a swallowed coal, waiting for daylight and a kitchen table to give up its heat. Tomorrow. Tomorrow they would knock on her door. Tonight was only the body insisting on its own pilgrimage, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

Turned out of the county road onto Fenwick Street at 2:40 in the morning, and the streetlights here were the old sodium kind, orange and moth-haunted, unreplaced by the LED retrofits that had reached the rest of town. The gentrification had not penetrated this far. Fenwick Street remained what it had always been: modest houses, chain-link fences, the occasional porch light burning for no one.

Number 47 stood at the end of the block.

Fifteen years had not taken it. That was the thing Jude had never been able to explain to anyone, the thing therapists had nodded at gently and wrongly: the house was still *there*. Burned to its bones, roofless, windowless, its interior open to the sky like a dollhouse built for a giant's inspection—but standing. The chimney rose intact against the stars. The doorframe held its shape around a doorway filled with plywood and graffiti. Weeds grew in the living room, waist-high, silvered by frost.

Jude stopped the car two houses down and did not get out. Just sat, engine off, breath fogging the windshield in slow pulses.

From here they could see the layout the way they'd seen it a thousand times from this exact angle, arriving home in the back seat: the kitchen window on the left, Wren's bedroom above it, the parents' room facing the street, Jude's own small room at the back overlooking the yard. The fire had started—it had *officially* started—in the wiring behind the kitchen wall. That was the verdict. Faulty wiring, older home, terrible accident, a hard year for everybody.

But Wren's window had been dark. Jude had stood on this lawn at fourteen, in socks, screaming up at that window while the heat pushed them back, and the window had been dark, and no glass broke outward, and no body was ever found in the rubble of a room too small to hide a absence that size. The town had needed the accident. The town had gotten the accident. And the girl who had hidden her journals in the hall closet—who had written everything down, even then, even at sixteen, in spiral notebooks she defended like state secrets—had simply ceased to exist, and the paperwork had been signed before the ash cooled, and the beloved judge had put his hand on a child's shoulder and offered help that was never meant to be collected.

Jude's hands had gone cold on the steering wheel. They realized they were crying only when a drop hit the county file and bloomed there, darkening the cardboard.

*Not yet,* they told themselves. *Not tonight.* Tonight was for seeing. Tomorrow was for asking. The ledger stayed shut in the bag; some entries had to be earned in person.

They sat until the sky began its slow bruise toward gray, until a milk truck rattled past and a porch light two doors down clicked off, until Fenwick Street began pretending to be an ordinary place again. Then Jude started the car and drove back to the River Rest, past the courthouse, where the banner hung pale in the pre-dawn light:

**CELEBRATING FORTY YEARS — JUDGE AMBROSE PELL — PORTRAIT UNVEILING SATURDAY**

Beneath it, on the bulletin board by the courthouse doors, half-hidden behind a flyer for a bake sale, was a smaller notice Jude hadn't seen in the daylight crowd of announcements. County letterhead. A hearing date. *Public comment period regarding the disposition of condemned properties, Fenwick corridor.*

The Fenwick corridor. Their street. Not just the house, then. The whole block, queued up like the rest.

Jude parked, sat in the lot watching the courthouse windows go gold with the first sun, and understood with total clarity that they had not come home at all. Homes were places that wanted you back. They had come to a crime scene that had spent twenty-five years learning to look like a hometown—and on Saturday, the whole town would gather on those steps to hang a portrait of the man who'd signed it.

Two days. Two days to find Mrs. Okonkwo, and make her say it again, slowly, everything she'd seen.

Jude got out, locked the car, and went to knock on the one door in Hollow Creek that had never lied to them.
