## 2. A Name Nobody Uses

My mother had planned her own wake the way she'd planned everything else in life: adequately, and with an eye toward not drawing attention.

There were forty chairs set out for what turned out to be ninety mourners, so people stood along the walls of the parish hall holding paper cups of sweet wine like they'd been handed evidence at a crime scene. There was a slideshow she'd apparently commissioned from the photographer who did first communions — a man named Duarte whose entire artistic range was "slightly to the left" — cycling through photographs of her at intervals of roughly a decade. In each one she stood slightly apart from whatever group she was standing in, as if she'd been photoshopped in by someone who didn't quite understand where shoulders go.

The priest said she was "a woman of quiet faith," which was true in the sense that the ocean is quietly wet. Uncle Teo gave a eulogy that lasted eleven minutes and mentioned fishing twice, grief once, and my mother's actual personality zero times. It was, I thought, the most honest thing anyone would say all day: we had all known her, and none of us could say what knowing her had consisted of.

I stood near the coffee urn doing what I do best, which is being looked at by people who want something from me I can't identify. Vela Mar is the kind of town where everyone remembers your christening but no one remembers your birthday, and they all remembered me — the boy who left, the one who went to the city and wrote things nobody read and came back only for funerals. Fifteen years since I'd stood on this particular linoleum. The town had aged around me like a photograph left in a window: same faces, sun-bleached, warped slightly at the edges.

They came at me in a slow rotation. Old classmates now thick through the middle, asking if I was staying long. Women who'd been girls when I left, looking at me with the specific pity reserved for men who write novels. A man from the fish market who shook my hand with a grip that suggested he was checking it for freshness.

And then, somewhere between the third cup of wine and the fourth, a stranger.

He came in without ceremony, which in Vela Mar is itself a kind of ceremony. Everyone else had arrived in clusters, in cars, in the loose confederation of families that makes up this town's entire social architecture. This man arrived alone, on foot, from the direction of the water, and he entered the hall the way weather enters a room — gradually, and then all at once.

He was old in the way that fishermen are old, which is to say his age was illegible. He could have been sixty or eighty; the sea doesn't timestamp its work, it just erodes. His face was a topography of squinting. His hands were enormous, knuckles swollen into pale knots, the nails cut short and yellowed. He wore a jacket that had once been navy and was now the color of a storm deciding. He did not sign the guest book. He did not take wine. He walked directly across the hall toward me, parting the crowd not because they moved for him but because they seemed to contract away from him, the way you'd edge back from a dog of uncertain temperament.

"You're the son," he said. Not a question. He said it the way you'd say *you're the one*.

"I'm Silas Quintana."

He looked at me for a long moment. There was something in the look I couldn't parse — not pity, not curiosity exactly. Recognition, maybe, but recognition of what? I was certain I'd never seen him before. Certain the way you're certain about your own name.

"My condolences," he said. "Your mother was—" and here he stopped, and the pause had weight to it, the pause of a man editing himself in real time, discarding whole sentences behind his teeth. "She was good to people who couldn't repay her."

"That sounds like her," I said, because it did, and because I didn't know what else to say, and because there's a script for these moments and I have never once deviated from it successfully.

"She was," he said, and something crossed his face then — I want to call it tenderness, but it was tenderness with a fracture running through it, like a plate that's been glued and still holds tea. "Good to people who couldn't repay her. Remember that about her. When the rest of it comes out."

"The rest of what?"

But he wasn't listening anymore, or rather he was listening to something else, some interior broadcast, and he took both my hands in his — those ruined, magnificent hands, cold as mooring rope — and held them a beat too long.

"Condolences to the Solanos," he said. Clearly. At full volume. In a hall of ninety people.

The word went through me strangely, the way a wrong note goes through a musician — not painful exactly, but structurally alarming, a small seismic event in a building I'd assumed was sound. *Solanos.* I ran it back. Solano. My family name was Quintana. Had always been Quintana. My mother was born a Quintana, married a Quintana — briefly, unhappily, before my father decamped to the mainland and out of all records — and died a Quintana. There was no Solano anywhere in the ledger of my life. And yet the word landed on me with the unmistakable weight of a name, the way a word stops being a word when someone aims it at you.

Behind me, a cup hit the floor.

I turned. Uncle Teo was standing three meters away with his hand still half-open, staring at the stranger with an expression I had never seen on him before and hope never to see again. Teo has exactly two expressions — affable and more affable — a man who has spent seventy years making himself easy to be around. What was on his face now was neither. It was white. Not pale — white, drained, the color of the underside of a fish, and beneath the whiteness something moving, quick and panicked, like a man reading bad news over someone's shoulder.

"Ivo." Teo said the name like a diagnosis. So they knew each other. "You shouldn't— this isn't—"

"Solanos," the stranger said again, softer now, almost gently, watching Teo the way you'd watch a lock you were trying to pick. "It's all right, Teo. It's just a name. Names are the cheapest thing there is."

"It's a wake," Teo said, and his voice cracked on the second syllable, which frightened me more than anything else that day, including the death itself. "Have some respect. Come outside. Please."

What happened next happened fast and low, the way these things do. Teo took the stranger — Ivo — by the elbow, a grip that was half courtesy and half arrest, and steered him toward the side door. The hall had gone quiet in that particular way rooms go quiet when everyone is pretending not to listen: the murmur continued but thinned, conversations becoming cover fire. I caught fragments. Someone said *old Ivo*, someone said *he's back, then*, someone shushed someone else with real violence. A woman near the dessert table made the sign of the cross with the brisk efficiency of a woman swatting a fly.

I followed them out.

The side door opened onto the alley behind the parish hall, all damp stone and cigarette ends and the smell of the harbor underneath everything, that permanent brine-and-diesel note that I had forgotten I knew until it walked back into my lungs. Ivo stood against the wall with the patience of a man who has been steered into alleys before. Teo released his elbow and stepped back and put his hand over his mouth, and for a moment the two of them just breathed at each other.

"You can't come here saying that," Teo said. "Not today. Not in front of—"

"In front of him?" Ivo turned his head and looked at me, and up close his eyes were extraordinary, a pale gray-green, the eyes of a man who has spent sixty years looking at the horizon and found it wanting. "He's standing right there, Teo. He heard me. That was rather the point."

"He's grieving."

"We're all grieving." Ivo said it flatly, without self-pity, and it landed like a fact rather than a comfort. "Forty years, some of us. You think today is the first funeral?" He pushed off the wall. He was taller than I'd judged inside, or maybe he just carried himself differently outdoors, under open sky, where he seemed to belong in a way he hadn't in the hall. "I came to pay respects. I paid them. I'll not come again."

"Ivo—"

"Don't." He raised one of those hands, and Teo stopped as if the hand were a wall. "Thirty years, Teo. Thirty years of envelopes, yes? Thirty years of your sister's little arrangements. I know what they cost you. I've never thanked you and I'm not going to start now, it would insult us both." Something passed between them then, some current too old and too insulated for me to feel the voltage of, and Ivo's voice dropped. "But the house is coming down. I heard. Everyone heard. And when it comes down, whatever your family thinks it's keeping in the ground comes up with the foundations. You don't get to choose the hour forever. Nobody gets to choose the hour forever."

Then he looked at me again, and this time he held it, and I understood that everything he'd said to Teo had been said for my benefit, angled like a mirror.

"Your mother," he said, "kept better than she was kept. Tell her I said so, wherever you keep her now." And he touched two fingers to his forehead — not quite a salute, not quite a blessing, some third thing older than either — and walked off down the alley toward the water, unhurried, his shadow long in the late light, and the town closed behind him like water behind a hull.

Teo stood with his back against the alley wall, breathing.

"Uncle," I said. "Who was that?"

"A fisherman. Nobody. Half-mad, they say." He said it quickly, the sentences coming out pre-assembled, a statement he'd clearly drafted years ago and kept in a drawer against exactly this occasion. Which told me something. People don't draft alibis for nobodies.

"What did he mean, Solano?"

"Nothing. He's confused. He's old, Silas, he gets names mixed up, he called Father Anselmo 'Bishop' for a decade—"

"He said it twice. He said it looking at me."

Teo took his glasses off and cleaned them on his shirt, which is what he does instead of answering questions; I have watched him clean those glasses through every difficult conversation of my life, and I realized, standing in that alley smelling the harbor, that I had spent fifteen years mistaking the cleaning of glasses for a lack of answers, when actually it was the shape answers take in this family before they're denied.

"Go back inside," he said. "People will talk."

"People are already talking."

"Let them talk about nothing, then. Nothing travels well. It's the only thing that does." He put the glasses back on and became, visibly, effortfully, Uncle Teo again — the shrug reassembling itself across his shoulders like a coat. "Your mother wouldn't want a scene at her wake."

And there it was. The trump card, played from beyond the grave, and played well, because how do you argue with that? How do you say *actually, I think she'd want the truth* when the truth is precisely the thing she spent seventy years declining to say? My mother's ghost settled over the alley like weather, and Teo and I both stood in it, and neither of us mentioned it.

Inside, the wake resumed. The slideshow looped. Duarte's photographs cycled: my mother at twenty, apart from the group; at thirty-five, apart from the group; at fifty, holding me at arm's length like something she was checking for damage. I shook hands. I accepted casseroles. I said *thank you for coming* forty times until the words wore smooth and meaningless, pebbles in a stream. And the whole time, underneath the liturgy of condolence, one syllable-pair rang in me like a struck glass:

*Solanos.*

Here is the thing I need to confess, because this chapter of my life begins here: it wasn't entirely unfamiliar. That's the vertigo of it. The word itself meant nothing — and yet it landed on some old scar tissue, some place in me already bruised, and the pain was less surprise than recognition. Like hearing a song you don't know and somehow knowing the chorus.

Because there was a night, fifteen years ago. I was seventeen. I've kept it folded up small all this time, the way you keep a receipt for something you're ashamed of having bought. My mother at the kitchen table after midnight, thinking I was asleep, a letter open in front of her and money on the table — actual banknotes, counted into a stack — and she was crying, silently, the way she did everything, economically, without witnesses. And when I came in she covered the letter with her hand so fast it was almost violent, and she said, "Homework?" and I said, "Yes," and I went upstairs, and I never asked. Never. Not once, in all the years after. She thanked me by never mentioning it, and I thanked her by never remembering it, and between us we built a silence so well-constructed it won architectural awards.

That's the inheritance, I understand now. Not the house. Not the money, such as it was. The silence. Passed down like a casserole dish, with instructions: *never ask, never mention, wash before returning.*

At the door, as the wake wound down, Marta Figueira — the librarian, a woman built like a question mark and sharp as a tack — paused beside me with her coat already buttoned.

"Strange fellow, that Ivo," she said, conversational, eyes forward. "Hasn't set foot in this town in thirty years, far as anyone knows. Comes back for your mother's wake of all things." She adjusted her bag on her shoulder. "You know, if you ever find yourself wondering about things, the archive keeps odd hours, but I'm always there Thursdays."

"I'm not wondering about anything," I said.

"No," Marta agreed pleasantly. "Of course not. Nobody ever is." And she left, and the door swung shut behind her, and I stood alone in the parish hall with the paper cups and the looping slideshow and my mother's face, apart from the group, forever.

That night I sat in the rented car outside the shuttered house — my house now, God help me, mine and Teo's, with the demolition notice stapled to the gate like a diagnosis — and I said the word out loud to the windshield, testing it, tasting it.

"Solano."

Two syllables. Cheapest thing there is, the fisherman said. But I could feel it working in me already, the way water works in a foundation, patient, finding every crack, and I knew — with the certainty you feel in your body before your mind consents to it — that I was not going to let this one drain away.

I got out of the car and stood at the gate in the dark, looking at the windows of the house where a woman had spent her whole life keeping a door shut, and I reached for the key.
