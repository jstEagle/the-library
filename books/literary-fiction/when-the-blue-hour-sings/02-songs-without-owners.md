## 2. Songs Without Owners

My apartment is a fourth-floor walk-up in a building that predates the elevator's dignity and possibly its own plumbing. The radiator knocks at irregular intervals like a neighbor asking to borrow something he'll never return, and the window faces a brick wall close enough that I could shake hands with it if we were on speaking terms. It is not the kind of place you show people. It is the kind of place you keep.

I got home from the library at seven-forty in the morning, which is technically yesterday for most functioning citizens and tomorrow for me. I ate cereal standing up because sitting down felt like an admission that the day had started. Then I did what I always do after a shift: I stood in the middle of my living room, which doubles as my dining room, my office, and my monument to deferred decisions, and I looked at the shelf.

The songbook was where it always is. Third shelf, spine turned inward, wedged between a water-damaged collection of Neruda I bought to impress a woman in 2014 and a dictionary I have never once opened because the internet exists. Spine inward. That part matters. I've kept it that way for ten years — long enough that if you asked me why, I'd say something about protecting the binding from light, which is even true, sort of, the way a locked door is true when what you mean is *keep out*.

Here is the thing you should understand about me before we go any further: I am very good at not doing things. I don't mean laziness. Laziness is easy and honest. I mean the disciplined, artisanal kind of avoidance — the kind with technique, with craft. I can circle a decision for months without ever letting myself notice I'm circling. I once spent two years "meaning to" get a cavity filled and developed an entire chewing strategy around it, all on one side of my mouth, like a man politely declining half his own face. So when I tell you that I stood in front of that shelf on a Tuesday morning, exhausted, unwashed, with the radiator knocking its borrowed-money knock, and that I took the songbook down — understand that this was not nothing. This was, by my standards, practically an act of violence.

The cover is blue. Was blue. It's mostly gray now, the color of a thing that has been held too many times by hands that needed holding. Amara Okonkwo, it says on the first page, in her handwriting, which slanted forward like it was always in a hurry to get somewhere she never quite arrived. Below the name, no title. She never titled it. Titles are promises, and my mother did not make promises lightly; she made them the way other people defuse bombs.

I sat down on the couch with it in my lap and did not open it.

This is also part of the ritual. You have to understand the ritual to understand me, and I'm not sure I want you to understand me, but here we both are. The ritual goes: take the book down. Hold it. Let your thumbs rest on the page edges the way you'd rest them on a sleeping animal. Feel the particular weight of it — heavier than paper should be, because grief adds grams, I'm convinced of it, some conversion rate science hasn't gotten around to discovering. And then, after a while, put it back.

A thousand times, give or take. Ten years, three hundred mornings a year, minus the mornings I worked doubles or pretended I didn't live here. I've done the math before, late at night, shelving biographies of people who actually finished things. A thousand mornings of holding a book I would not read.

Except this morning something was different, and I want to be precise about what, because precision is the only inheritance I got that I know how to use. What was different was that my hands didn't move to put it back. They just stayed there, on the cover, and the radiator knocked, and the brick wall outside did its brick wall impression, and somewhere below us a delivery truck performed its daily aria of reverse-beeping, and I sat there like a man waiting for permission from a room.

I opened it.

* * *

The first page is the only page I know. I could draw it from memory, which is its own kind of damning. My mother's name. The date — March 3rd, no year, because years are for people keeping records and she insisted she was keeping songs. And beneath that, the first line of the first song, written in ink that has faded from black to the brown of old tea:

*The blue hour is when the city admits what it wants.*

That's it. That's as far as I've ever let myself go. One line. I've read that line so many times it has stopped being words and become texture, like a stair rail worn smooth by palms. The blue hour — she used to talk about it, that stretch of dawn when the sky can't decide between night and day, when the streetlights give up but the sun hasn't committed. She loved it the way some people love a person who's bad for them. *Everything true happens in the blue hour, Nna m,* she said once. Everything else is just scheduling.

Nna m. My child. She called me that in Igbo when she was tired, which was often, and tender, which was more often than she probably intended, given everything.

I looked at the second line. I want you to appreciate this: I looked at it. My eyes went there. *And the city wants—*

And I closed the book.

Not slammed it. Closed it, gently, the way you'd close a door on a sleeping person, and then I sat there hating myself with a fluency that comes only from practice. Because here's what lives past that second line, and I knew it the way you know a bruise is there without pressing it: her voice. Not metaphorically. Her actual voice, humming, which is the sound I have spent ten years arranging my entire life to avoid hearing.

* * *

She hummed constantly. That's the fragment that surfaces first, always, the way a stone surfaces in a field after every rain. She hummed while cooking, standing over rice and stew in our kitchen that was too small for two people and exactly right for one woman and her shadow of a son. She hummed while hanging laundry, pinning sheets to the rack by the window, and the sheets would billow around her like sails and her humming would come through the fabric slightly muffled, slightly sea-sick, and I would lie on the couch and feel like I was inside the music instead of beside it.

She hummed in the bathroom. She hummed at red lights, tapping the steering wheel on beats two and four like she was scolding the song for dragging. She hummed when she thought I was asleep, standing at my bedroom door, and I would fake sleep harder, breathing the deep fraudulent breaths of a child who understood, without having words for it, that being witnessed listening was somehow more dangerous than listening itself.

Because here's the thing about her humming: it wasn't idle. Other people hum to fill silence. Amara hummed to test things. She'd hum a phrase, stop, hum it again altered — a note bent, a rhythm shifted — and if you interrupted her mid-revision she'd look at you with an expression I can only describe as *apologetic fury*, like you'd walked through wet cement onto her fresh ideas. She was working in there. All those years I thought she was just filling our small apartment with sound, she was actually building something, room by room, in a country only she could see.

And she never recorded any of it. Never. Not a cassette, not a voicemail, not one of those answering-machine messages people used to leave themselves. When I asked her why — I must have been twelve, old enough to ask questions, too young to survive the answers — she stirred the pot and said, *Some songs are like letters, nna m. They're addressed to somebody.* And I said, addressed to who? And she said, *When you know how to read, you'll find out.*

I have thought about that sentence maybe ten thousand times since she died, and I have never once decided whether it was a promise or a deferral. With her it was usually both. That was her whole method, honestly. Love delivered with an escape hatch built in. Tenderness with a fire exit.

* * *

Here is another fragment, because they come in fragments now, ten years downstream. Memory doesn't give you scenes anymore; it gives you shards, and you carry them around in your pockets until they wear grooves in your thighs.

Fragment: her hands. Long fingers, short nails, a burn scar on the left wrist from a pot of pepper soup circa 1998 that she referred to, always, as "the incident." Those hands writing in the songbook at the kitchen table while I did homework across from her, and the strange competitive shame I felt watching her write music while I wrote book reports — the sense that she was doing the real version of whatever I was faking.

Fragment: a melody I cannot name and cannot forget. Four notes descending, then a leap up that lands soft. She hummed it during the last year, more than any other, and sometimes stopped in the middle and pressed her lips together like she'd said too much. I have caught myself humming it as an adult, on the night bus, in the shower, once in the middle of reshelving the 780s, and each time I've clamped my mouth shut like I'd bitten foil. Because that melody isn't mine. Nothing in that book is mine. That's the arrangement. That's the deal I struck with the dead: you stay closed, I stay quiet, and neither of us has to find out what's inside.

Fragment: the hospital, which I will not describe except to say that hospitals are designed by people who assume you'll only ever visit them as a tourist, and the vending machine coffee tasted like punishment, and she squeezed my hand with a strength that surprised us both, and she said — this is verbatim, I have checked the recording in my head a thousand times, the fidelity never degrades, which is its own cruelty — she said, *Don't read it yet. You'll know when.*

Then she died, and I did not know when, apparently, for ten years running.

* * *

So: Tuesday morning, fourth floor, radiator knocking, songbook open to page one in my lap, and me having just fled from line two like a man retreating from a border checkpoint. I set the book on the cushion beside me, face down, which felt disrespectful, then face up, which felt worse, and finally closed it and balanced it on my knee like a tray.

The apartment did its apartment sounds. Pipes. Someone's television bleeding through the wall, a game show audience applauding on cue, delighted by nothing. Downstairs, Mrs. Adeyemi's radio, which she keeps tuned to a jazz station at volumes that suggest she is either deaf or at war with the neighbors, and which I have complained about zero times in six years because her radio is the closest thing my building has to a heartbeat.

I should mention that I almost called my aunt again. Auntie Ngozi, my mother's sister, who lives two states away and calls me on Sundays with the regularity of tide charts. Every call ends the same way: she says, *You should come visit, Wren,* and I say, *Soon, Auntie,* and we both pretend this is a plan rather than a treaty. There's a reason I keep not visiting, and the reason has a shape, and the shape is roughly rectangular, and it lives on my third shelf with its spine turned inward. Ngozi knew Amara before me. Ngozi knew the whole story, or enough of it, and every time we talk I can hear her editing herself around the gaps, choosing her sentences like a woman crossing a river on stones. I've started doing the same thing back to her. Two editors, one family, zero drafts finished.

I picked up my phone. I typed: *Auntie, do you remember Mom's songbook* — and then I deleted it. Typed: *Random question, was Mom ever* — deleted. Typed: *How are you* — sent nothing, obviously, because how are you is a question you ask when you don't have the courage to ask the real one, and my aunt would smell the cowardice through the phone the way she smelled rain coming, which was unnervingly well.

I put the phone face-down on the table. Face-down phones, face-inward books. My whole life is objects turned away from me.

* * *

Let me tell you what I'm afraid of, since apparently we're doing honesty now, this once, in the privacy of a chapter nobody in my life will ever read. I'm afraid of the second line. Not the words — words I can handle, I shelve words for a living, I'm practically a wordsmith's janitor. I'm afraid of what happens after the words. Because I know how reading works. I know that somewhere around page twenty there will be a song I recognize, one of the melodies she tested on me through my fake-sleeping door, and I will hear her voice attach itself to it, unbidden, permanent, and then she will be in the room with me — actually in it, not the polite memorial version, the real bossy humming revision-making version — and I do not know if I survive that.

People think grief gets easier. Grief does not get easier. Grief gets *furniture*. You build your life around it, you route your days past it, you stop noticing it the way you stop noticing a support beam, until one day you decide to renovate and discover the beam is load-bearing and the whole structure comes down or nothing changes at all. Reading that book is renovation. Ten years ago I chose nothing changes at all, and I chose it so thoroughly I forgot it was a choice, which is the trick, the great trick, the one I perform nightly under fluorescent lights while telling myself I'm just a night librarian, just a quiet guy, just a man whose mother liked to hum.

But this morning I opened it. First time in — I tried to count and couldn't, which frightened me more than the opening did. Weeks? Months? Had there been a season where I didn't touch it at all and didn't notice? The beam was already settling. I could feel the house adjusting.

* * *

I made tea I didn't drink. I opened the book again — second offense, escalating — and read the first line again, and this time I let my eyes drop to the second line and I read exactly four more words before my chest did the thing, the tightening, the fist-around-the-sternum, and I set the book down and breathed like they teach you in the pamphlets at the free clinic: in for four, hold for four, out for eight, pretend you're fine for the rest of your natural life.

*And the city wants to sing.*

Four words. That's all I got. Somewhere in that book there are, I estimated wildly, sixty pages, forty songs, a decade of my mother's secret architecture, and my total holdings amounted to nine words across two lines and a memory of laundry billowing like sails.

It was noon. I hadn't slept. Outside my brick-wall window the light had gone from blue hour to ordinary hour, the sky having made its decision, as skies do, without consulting anyone. I put the songbook back on the shelf. Spine inward. Old habit, old treaty, old lie.

And then I got in my car and drove home from work, which sounds like a contradiction unless you know my schedule, and anyway the point is I was on the freeway at dawn, because that's when I drive, that's when I've always driven, the blue hour, her favorite hour, the hour I chose for reasons I told myself were practical — less traffic, better light — and the radio was on, because the radio is always on, white noise against the noise, and I was thinking about the second line, *and the city wants to sing*, thinking about how I'd fled from five words like they were armed —

— when the DJ said, *"That was 'Blue Hour,' the new single from Cass Meridian,"* and the piano came in, four notes descending, then a leap up that landed soft, and I knew it. I knew it the way you know your own front door in the dark. I knew it the way you know a bruise.

I pulled over. I don't remember deciding to pull over. My hands did it, the way they'd held the book, the way they'd refused to put it back, and I sat on the shoulder of the freeway with the hazards ticking like a countdown while a stranger on the radio sang my mother's unfinished song, note for note, all the way to the end.
