## 13. The Untranslatable Sentence

The scholar's name was Professor Eze, and I found him the way you find anyone in this city who actually knows things: not through the internet, which is a rumor mill with a search bar, but through the library.

I'd spent two nights in the special collections after my shift ended, pulling every reference work on Igbo language and oral tradition that hadn't been checked out since the Clinton administration. In the margin of one of them, in pencil, someone had written a name and a phone number with an area code so old it felt like an artifact. *For serious inquiries only,* the marginal note said, in handwriting that slanted like it was always leaning into a strong wind.

I called at four in the afternoon, which felt like the polite hour for serious inquiries. A woman answered—his daughter, it turned out—and after I explained who I was and what I wanted, there was a long silence on the line, the kind of silence that is actually a person deciding something.

"He doesn't do translation work anymore," she said. "He says the language has been translated enough. But—" another pause, "—if it's a family matter, come Saturday morning. He likes family matters. He says everything else is just business wearing a dictionary."

I said it was a family matter. It was the truest sentence I'd said in weeks.

* * *

Professor Eze lived in a narrow brick house in a neighborhood where the porches leaned at the same angle as the old men who sat on them. His daughter, Adaeze, met me at the door and walked me through a hallway lined with photographs—graduations, weddings, a younger version of the professor shaking hands with people whose faces I half-recognized from book jackets—and into a study that smelled of camphor and old paper, the two best smells in the world, in my professional opinion.

The professor himself was small and enormous at the same time, the way certain old men are: a body that had been folded down by decades, and a presence that filled the room to the ceiling. He was sitting in a wingback chair with a blanket over his knees, and he looked at me for a long moment before he spoke.

"You have her face," he said.

My whole body went cold. "You knew my mother?"

"I knew *of* her," he said, and waved me into the chair across from him. "Sit. You are the Okonkwo boy. Amara's son. Adaeze told me the name, and I thought—no, it cannot be, that woman had a voice like a river and a temper like the dry season. But here you are, with her exact way of standing in a doorway like you're not sure the room wants you."

"I—" I sat down, because my legs had opinions of their own. "She's dead. Ten years now."

"I know," he said, not unkindly. "The dead do not stop being known, young man. They only stop being *here*. There is a difference, and grief is the tax we pay on it." He held out his hand. "You brought the sentence?"

I had brought it three ways: a photocopy of the notebook page, my own careful transcription, and the notebook itself, which I'd carried across the city in a canvas bag like a bomb. I gave him the photocopy first, because handing over the original felt like handing over a kidney.

He put on his glasses and read.

I watched his face the way you watch a seismograph. For a long time there was nothing—just an old man reading Igbo, which was, for him, presumably as remarkable as breathing. But then something moved behind his eyes. A small tremor. He read it again.

"Say it aloud for me," he said. "Your mother's pronunciation. You must have heard her say it, even if you didn't understand it."

"I never heard her say it," I said. "She wrote it and closed the book. I don't think anyone ever heard it."

He looked up at me over the glasses, and something in his expression shifted from curiosity to something almost like pity. "Then you are asking me to translate a sentence that has only ever existed in silence. Do you understand what you're asking?"

"No," I admitted. "That's why I'm here."

* * *

What he did then, I will try to describe, though I'm aware I'll get it wrong, the way you get a dream wrong by telling it.

He read the sentence aloud. My mother's handwriting—her small, slanted, impatient script, the letters of a woman who thought faster than her pen could go—and his voice, low and unhurried, gave it a body. The sounds were round and heavy and they landed in the room like stones dropped into deep water. I didn't understand a word, and I understood all of it, because I'd spent ten years hearing my mother hum in a language I'd never learned, and this was that humming, finally given consonants.

Then he set the page down and said, "This sentence cannot be translated."

"Everything can be translated," I said. "Badly."

"Ah." He smiled, and the smile was the smile of a man who has been waiting his whole career for someone to say the wrong thing so he could say the right one. "Everything can be *paraphrased*. Translation is different. Translation is carrying a thing across a river without getting it wet. Some things are dry on one side and wet on the other, and no amount of skill changes that." He tapped the photocopy. "This sentence is built on a verb that carries two meanings at once. In Igbo, the construction is—forgive me, I will simplify, because your Igbo is, I am guessing, somewhere between absent and shameful."

"It's absent," I said. "My mother spoke it to me as a lullaby language. I absorbed the melody and none of the meaning. Which, in retrospect, was the whole tragedy in miniature."

He ignored this, or filed it away, or possibly both. "The verb can mean *to keep* and it can mean *to hold against one's will*. Same word. The sentence says, depending on how you lean into it: *You kept me singing*—a gift, a gratitude, a farewell. Or: *You held me singing when I wanted to stop*—an accusation, a wound, a door closing. The grammar refuses to choose. The tone refuses to choose. Your mother wrote a sentence that is both a thank-you and a knife, and she did it so well that no honest translator can pick one meaning without lying."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere outside, a dog was losing an argument with a squirrel.

"Which one is it?" I asked. "Which did she mean?"

The professor took off his glasses and polished them on the blanket, which I understood, even then, to be a man buying himself time. "That," he said, "is not a question about language."

"It's the only question I have."

"Then let me give you the only answer language has." He leaned forward. "A sentence like this is not a container with one true content. It is a mirror with two faces. The person who wrote it meant both, or meant neither, or meant one on Monday and the other by Friday, because that is what human hearts do—they hold farewells and accusations in the same hand and cannot always tell which is which. Your mother wrote this sentence to a specific person, yes? About a specific wound, a specific gratitude?"

"To someone unnamed," I said. "The whole notebook is dated and annotated except this page. No name. No date. Just the sentence, at the end, like a door she shut behind her."

"And who do you *want* it to be addressed to?"

The question landed harder than it had any right to. "I don't know. My aunt, maybe. She was the one who—there are things my aunt isn't telling me. Or Theo Marsh, the man who's been singing her songs. Or—" I heard my own voice do the thing it does when it's about to say something it's been avoiding. "Or me. Maybe it was to me. She died and left me a notebook full of songs and one sentence I couldn't read, and maybe the sentence was for me, and maybe it was a thank-you, and maybe it was—"

"Or maybe it was an accusation," the professor said gently, "and you have spent ten years unable to read it because some part of you knew that."

I didn't say anything. There was nothing to say that wouldn't come out as a sound.

He let the silence sit between us the way you let bread rise. Then he said, "I will tell you what I tell my students, the few I still tolerate. When a sentence carries two meanings, the meaning is not in the sentence. The meaning is in the relationship between the speaker and the listener. The words are a lock with two keys. You cannot find the true one by studying the lock. You find it by asking: who held the other key, and what did they do with it?"

"You're saying I have to figure out who it's for before I can know what it says."

"I am saying," he said, "that your mother wrote you a sentence that cannot be settled by a dictionary, and I suspect—forgive an old man's presumption—I suspect she knew exactly what she was doing. A woman who writes a bilingual double-entendre as her final word is not leaving a message. She is leaving a *task*. She is saying: you will have to come find me, and to find me you will have to find everyone I ever loved or failed, and you will have to decide what I meant, because I refuse to decide for you." He smiled, and this time there was real warmth in it, and real sorrow underneath. "That is not cruelty. That is the last gift of a complicated woman. She made you her translator. And a translator, young man, must live inside both meanings or translate nothing."

* * *

Adaeze brought tea—strong, sweet, in glasses instead of cups, which felt like a small act of hospitality aimed directly at my mother's ghost—and for a while the three of us sat without talking. The professor dozed a little, his chin dipping toward the blanket, and I sat with the photocopy in my lap and felt the sentence rearranging itself in my chest like furniture in a dark room.

I had come expecting an answer. I had come with the naive, library-trained faith that every text has a reading, that somewhere in the stacks the true meaning waits like a misfiled book, and all you need is the right call number. Ten years I'd kept this sentence sealed, and I'd told myself it was grief. It wasn't. It was fear. Because as long as I couldn't read it, it could still be the thank-you. As long as it stayed untranslatable, my mother's last word to the world could still be love.

The professor opened one eye. "You are still here."

"I'm afraid to leave."

"Good," he said. "Fear means the question matters. Now. One more thing, and then Adaeze will feed you, because you have the look of a man who forgets meals." He sat up as much as his spine allowed. "The verb. The one that means *keep* and *hold against one's will*. I want you to understand that this ambiguity is not an accident of grammar. It is a very old ambiguity. In the old songs—the ones the women sang at funerals, at partings, at the edge of the compound when someone was leaving for a place they might not return from—the same verb appears. *She kept us singing. She held us singing.* The mourners meant both. That was the point. Grief is not one thing. It is the two things refusing to separate." He looked at me over the glasses again. "Your mother knew the old songs. I can tell from the way she built the sentence. So I will tell you what the old singers would tell you: do not choose. Carry both. The day you choose one meaning is the day you stop telling the truth about her."

"But the truth about her," I said, "is exactly what everyone keeps refusing to give me. My aunt. Theo Marsh. Everyone loved her so much they turned her into a locked room."

"Then you are the one holding the key," the professor said, "and you have been standing in the hallway for ten years because you are afraid of what the room looks like with the lights on."

I want to report that I argued with him. I want to report that I had a clever rebuttal, some wry deflection, one of my almost-sentences that lets me exit a conversation without ever having fully entered it. But the truth is I just sat there in a stranger's wingback chair, holding a photocopy of my mother's handwriting, and let an old man say the truest thing anyone had said to me in a decade.

"Who was she?" I asked finally. "Not the songs. Not the legend. Who was she, to the people who knew her?"

The professor was quiet for a long time. Outside, the dog and squirrel had reached some kind of détente.

"I met her once," he said. "Years ago. She came to a lecture I gave on translation, of all things, and afterward she asked me a question I have never forgotten. She asked: *If you translate a song, does the original die, or does it multiply?* And I gave her the professor's answer, the long one, the one with citations. And she laughed and said, *That's not what I asked.*" He shook his head slowly. "She was a woman who asked questions she already knew the answers to, and then waited to see if you would catch up. I think everyone who loved her spent years catching up. I think some of them are still catching up now."

* * *

Adaeze fed me jollof rice and stewed greens in a kitchen that smelled like every good thing my childhood didn't have enough of, and she told me, in the offhand way people tell you important things, that her father had stopped taking translation requests after his wife died, because "he translated her death certificate and said it was the first time the language failed him completely."

"He says your mother's sentence is the second time," she added, refilling my glass. "He hasn't stopped talking about it since you left the room. That's how I knew to keep feeding you. He only talks when something matters."

When I stood to go, the professor was asleep in his chair, the photocopy still on his lap, one hand resting on it lightly, the way you rest a hand on a sleeping child.

At the door, Adaeze said, "He wants you to come back. After. He says a translator should report in."

"Report on what?"

"On what you find," she said. "On who the sentence was for. He says the sentence isn't finished until it's been answered."

I walked home through the early dark with the notebook against my chest, and the city did what the city does—lit up, hummed, pretended to be simple. But I wasn't pretending anymore. I knew now that the sentence was a mirror with two faces, and I knew which face I'd been afraid of, and I knew, with the terrible clarity that arrives around mile three of a long walk, exactly whose door I had to knock on next.

My aunt had held the other key. My aunt had known my mother longer than anyone alive, had been there before the songs, before the silence, before me—and she had gone quiet the moment she saw the songbook, quiet in the specific way of a person who recognizes a debt.

The sentence wasn't finished until it was answered. And I was suddenly, coldly certain that the person who could answer it wasn't a professor, wasn't a singer, wasn't a thief.

It was the woman who had helped my mother disappear—and who had never told me what she disappeared *from*.
