## 16. The Archive at Night

The special collections room lives in the basement of the library, which is the kind of joke the universe makes when it thinks no one is listening. Everything precious, everything worth keeping, buried under the feet of people who come looking for bestsellers and bathroom keys. I'd worked nights on the main floor for six years before the head of acquisitions learned my name—learned it, that is, from a scheduling spreadsheet, not from any act of human recognition—and discovered I was the only employee who didn't mind the dead hours. She offered me the archive shift the way you offer someone a chore disguised as an honor.

I took it because basements are honest. Nothing down here pretends to be anything other than what it is.

That night, though, I wasn't there for honesty. I was there for ammunition.

* * *

Here is what I had by then: a songbook with Amara's handwriting, dated in her own stubborn hand on every page. A stranger named Cass Meridian singing those songs to strangers. A man named Theo Marsh who claimed she'd left them to him. An aunt who had helped my mother disappear and called it love. And a sentence in Igbo that I still couldn't read without my chest doing something structural.

What I did not have was proof. Not the kind that mattered. Dates in a notebook are dates in a notebook; Theo could say she wrote them later, or earlier, or that the notebook itself was a forgery of grief. Grief makes a terrible witness. It remembers what it needs to.

But libraries remember everything. That's the whole point of us.

The copyright records weren't technically part of our collection—they were federal, public, theoretically searchable from anywhere—but the library kept microfilm and print archives of registration records going back decades, donated by a music publisher that went under in the nineties and left its paper corpse to us like a body to a medical school. I'd catalogued part of that donation myself, back when I still believed avoidance was a personality and not a wound. Somewhere in those boxes, if Amara had ever registered anything, would be her signature. Her legal name. The state's cold acknowledgment that she had existed as something more than a woman who hummed in kitchens.

I pulled the first box at 11:40 p.m. and told myself I was just being thorough.

* * *

There's a particular loneliness to archival work that I have always found comforting and that night found unbearable. You sit alone with the accumulated paperwork of dead ambitions. Every folder is someone who once believed a song or a story or an invention deserved protecting, and most of them are wrong—not because the work was bad but because the world doesn't reward protection, it rewards luck wearing protection's clothes. I used to find that consoling. That night every folder felt like a mirror.

Okonkwo. Okafor. Okafor-Obi. I moved through the O's with the mechanical patience of ten years of night shifts, and I will admit—I had assumed I'd find nothing. That was the safer bet. If Amara never registered the songs, then Theo's claim rested on nothing but his word and Cass's career rested on nothing but theft, and the whole ugly architecture stayed simple. Thief, victim, aunt who lied. I knew how to live inside simple.

At 1:15 a.m., I found the folder.

OKONKWO, AMARA C. — MARSH, THEO R. — JOINT WORK.

I sat very still for a while. Long enough that the fluorescent hum seemed to change pitch, though of course it didn't; I did.

Joint work. Filed jointly. Which meant that at some point, my mother—the woman whose songs I had guarded like relics, whose voice existed in my memory only as humming, half-songs, the ghost of melodies through a kitchen doorway—had walked into an office with Theo Marsh and signed her name beside his. Had stood next to him in some government building and declared to the republic that this music belonged to both of them.

I read the date three times. Then a fourth, as if repetition were a form of disbelief that eventually converts to acceptance. It was dated eight years before she died. Eight years before me finding the radio. Two years after I was born.

She hadn't written these songs in secret. She hadn't written them alone. She had written them with him, registered them with him, and then—

Then the amendment.

It was clipped behind the original, on thinner paper, the kind of form that gets filled out by people whose lives are rearranging themselves faster than their stationery. AMENDMENT TO CERTIFICATE OF REGISTRATION. The joint names struck through in careful administrative ink. In the space where two names had been, one remained: Okonkwo, Amara C., sole author and claimant.

No explanation. No reason given. Just the bureaucratic equivalent of a door closing—one name erased, one name left standing, and no record anywhere of who asked for the erasure or why.

I want to tell you I felt vindicated. Part of me did—a hot, small, shameful part that wanted Theo Marsh to be exactly what he appeared to be, because a thief is easier to hate than a collaborator, and a collaborator means my mother chose someone over silence, chose partnership over the fortress she built around herself, and where was I in that picture? Where was the son she sang to? Why did the man get a name on a certificate and I got a locked songbook?

But mostly what I felt was vertigo. Because the document cut both ways, and documents always do. It proved the songs were hers—it proved she had fought, legally, formally, to make them hers alone. But it also proved they had been his first. Or theirs. And it raised a question I had been carefully not asking since Ngozi said his name: why would a woman amend a copyright to remove a man unless something had happened between them that required removal?

Some truths are heavier than silence, Ngozi had warned me. She hadn't mentioned that some truths are heavier than both.

* * *

I did what any reasonable person does at 2 a.m. confronted with evidence that dismantles their moral architecture: I made tea in the staff kitchen and stared at the vending machine like it owed me an apology.

The basement has a window, actually—one narrow slit at ground level, frosted, that shows the ankles of the city walking past. I watched ankles for a while. Businessmen, insomniacs, joggers, all of them moving through the blue hour at the speed of people who believe tomorrow will resemble today. I envied them their uncomplicated relationships to intellectual property.

Here's the thing about hope: I'd been carrying a version of it for weeks without admitting it was hope. The version where Theo was simply a thief. Where the songs were purely hers, where my guardianship of the songbook had been righteous rather than cowardly, where restoring Amara's name would be restoration rather than excavation. Hope as a load-bearing wall. And now I was holding a piece of paper that put a crack straight through it, and the building was making noises.

Because the amendment also meant she *cared*. She cared enough to file paperwork, to pay fees, to navigate bureaucracy—a woman Ngozi described as allergic to institutions, a woman who supposedly wanted nothing from the world. She wanted this. She wanted the songs to be hers, officially, permanently, in the eyes of whatever authority counts such things. Which meant the songs mattered to her in a way that went beyond humming in kitchens. Which meant that when she died and left the songbook to me without a word, she was leaving me something she considered property. An estate. A claim.

And claims can be contested. That's what claims are *for*.

* * *

I photocopied the amendment twice, then a third time because the second came out too light, and then I sat with the originals in their folder and thought about what a strange thing evidence is. For ten years I had treated the songbook as scripture—closed, sacred, not to be interpreted. Now I was treating it as a case file. Same object. Different reader. The book hadn't changed. I had changed around it, the way a river changes around a stone, and I suppose that's growth, but nobody tells you growth feels like erosion.

Around 3 a.m.—the hour that belongs to no one, my hour, the hour I used to think of as mine alone—I did something I hadn't done since chapter one of this whole disaster. I opened the songbook.

Not past the first page. I'm not cured. But I held it in the archive's flat light and looked at it as an artifact instead of a relic. The water damage on the cover. The way her handwriting slants left when she's writing fast and upright when she's being careful. The dates. And I noticed, for the first time, what I should have noticed years ago: the earliest songs in the book are written in two hands.

Two. Hands.

Amara's, unmistakable, the handwriting I'd know blindfolded. And beneath it, in pencil, in a smaller, more tentative script—chords. Corrections. Occasional whole lines, erased and rewritten, the ghost-text of collaboration. The same hand that had signed OKONKWO, AMARA C. — MARSH, THEO R. had shared these pages with another hand entirely, and I had spent a decade calling the result *hers*, singular, mine to guard.

I closed the book gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping person.

* * *

The scholar—Dr. Eze, the elderly Igbo linguist I'd consulted about the sentence—had told me that language resists translation when it holds two truths at once. I'd nodded like I understood. Sitting in the archive with a photocopy warming in my hand, I finally understood. The sentence resisted translation because it was doing two things simultaneously. And so was this certificate. And so, probably, was my mother, all her life: farewell and accusation, authorship and surrender, the woman who filed the amendment and the woman who let a man keep singing her songs after she was gone.

Theo had said he recorded them because he believed they were left to him. I had heard that as a lie. Now I heard it as a reading—a man interpreting an ambiguous text, the way we all interpret the texts of the dead, choosing the meaning that lets us live with ourselves. Maybe he was wrong. Maybe he was right. The amendment said sole author. The original filing said otherwise. The law, it turns out, keeps both receipts.

Hope complicated by evidence. That was the beat, if beats exist, and I was starting to suspect they do. Not hope destroyed—hope *complicated*, which is worse, because destruction at least ends things and complication just goes on.

At 4:50 a.m., I packed up. Photocopies in my bag. Songbook against my chest like the world's most anxious briefcase. And as I climbed the stairs toward the gray beginning of morning, my phone buzzed.

Cass Meridian. We had exchanged numbers the week before, two conspirators in a war neither of us had chosen, and she had sent exactly four texts since, each one shorter than the last, as if words were costing her something by the syllable.

This one said:

**He knows you've been asking questions. My label got a call. We need to talk. Tonight.**

I stood at the top of the stairs, holding my mother's contested legacy against my ribs, and typed back the longest message I had ever sent anyone:

*I'll bring the documents.*
