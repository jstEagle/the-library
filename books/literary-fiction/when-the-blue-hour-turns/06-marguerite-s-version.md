## 6. Marguerite's Version

The drive to Rosemere took forty minutes, which was thirty-five minutes longer than Julian Veren wanted it to take and exactly as long as he needed.

He had learned this about himself in the past year: that he required the drive. The conservatory gave him no privacy — a director's office has glass walls even when they're plaster — and his own apartment had stopped being a refuge sometime around the third notice from the endowment auditors. But the car was neutral territory. In the car he could rehearse.

He rehearsed the way his mother had taught him to rehearse, which is to say ruthlessly, with an imaginary audience of people who wished him harm. *Mother, I need to ask you something.* No. Too abrupt. She would hear the urgency and sharpen against it like a blade against a strop. *Mother, something odd has come up regarding the old practice wing—* Better. Casual. The word "odd" doing quiet work, suggesting curiosity rather than accusation. Marguerite Veren had spent ninety-one years never once being accused of anything, and Julian had no intention of being the first. Not because she didn't deserve it. Because accusations, in her presence, behaved like water thrown at a cat: they arrived with great force and left everyone but the cat wet.

He parked under the copper beech that had been dropping bronze leaves on the Veren cars since before there were Veren cars, and sat for a moment with his hands at ten and two, a man preparing to perform.

* * *

Rosemere smelled the way it always had: lavender, beeswax, and beneath both, faintly, the medicinal sweetness of the good tea they brought his mother on her better days. The nurse on duty — Priya, whose name his mother refused to learn and whom his mother nonetheless liked best of anyone in the building — met him in the hall with the particular expression nurses develop for adult children of difficult patients. It was a look of profound professional sympathy, the sympathy one extends to someone standing in a rainstorm without an umbrella who has insisted, repeatedly, that they enjoy the rain.

"She's having a good day," Priya said. "Sharp as anything. She's been correcting my pronunciation for an hour."

"Of what?"

"Everything. My name, mostly."

Julian smiled. It was one of the smiles he kept in inventory — the fond-son smile, rueful, slightly self-deprecating, the smile that said *yes, isn't she marvelous, and yes, it is exhausting, and no, I wouldn't change her.* He had worn that smile at board meetings, at galas, at his father's funeral. It fit so well he sometimes forgot it was a costume.

His mother was in the sunroom, propped in her chair by the window like a duchess reviewing a parade she had personally ordered. She was small now — she had been shrinking for years, the way powerful people do, condensing themselves into a denser substance — but her hands lay folded in her lap with the authority of two scepters. Her eyes found him before he'd cleared the doorway.

"There you are," she said. "You look terrible."

"Good afternoon to you too, Mother."

"You look like your grandfather did the year the bank called. Sit down. You're looming, and you loom badly. Your father loomed beautifully; it's a lost art."

He sat. The chair across from her was low, deliberately or not, so that she addressed him from above. This had been true in every house she'd ever lived in, regardless of furniture.

"I've come to ask you something," he said, deploying the rehearsal.

"Obviously." She turned her face back to the window. October light came through the glass and made her translucent at the edges, a woman already half-turned into memory. "You only visit when you want something, darling. You've done it since you were six. When you were happy you stayed away entirely, which I chose to interpret as confidence." A pause, delicate as a knife being set down. "Well?"

"The practice wing," Julian said. "At the conservatory. They're clearing it out before the demolition assessment. And some letters have turned up."

He watched her hands. He had learned, over years of board negotiations and donor dinners, that people's faces lie fluently but their hands stammer. His mother's hands did not move at all.

"Letters," she repeated.

"A box of them. Behind a wardrobe panel. Old correspondence." He kept his voice light, conversational, a man discussing plumbing. "Addressed to you."

"To me." She said it the way she said *Brahms* — with mild distaste, as though the thing itself were fine but its admirers tiresome. "How peculiar. People wrote to me constantly in those days. Half the city wanted money and the other half wanted to be thanked for giving it. I answered what interested me and let the rest sit. Your father was the sentimentalist; I was the archivist."

"These weren't donations letters."

"No?"

"They were from—" He stopped. Here was the strange part, the part he had turned over all night like a stone looking for what lived under it. "They were from Ansel Ishikawa's mother. Setsuko Ishikawa."

And there — there — the smallest event. His mother's thumb moved across the back of her other hand, one slow stroke, like a woman smoothing a wrinkle out of silk. Then stillness again, total, serene.

"Ishikawa," Marguerite said. "The pianist. The sad story. What was it — his nerves? Such a shame. Talent like that, wasted. I remember your father took it very hard."

Julian felt the sentence go past him like a train he was standing too close to, wind and noise and a face in the window that might have been anything.

"Mother," he said carefully, "you told me once — you told me you barely knew the family. That Father handled the scholarship endowment and you signed where he pointed."

"And did I?" she asked pleasantly.

"That's what I'm asking you."

"Darling, I'm ninety-one. I have signed where people pointed for six decades. If you're asking whether I remember every piece of paper that crossed my desk during the Ford administration, I'm afraid your faith in my omniscience flatters me, but it also insults my honesty, and I have always valued my honesty more than almost anything." She lifted her chin. "Almost everything," she repeated, savoring it. "That's the joke, Julian. Do keep up."

* * *

He stayed two hours. He drank tea he didn't want and ate a scone he tasted not at all, and he circled the question the way he'd been taught to circle a difficult passage — not attacking it head-on but approaching from either side, softening it, waiting for it to loosen. His mother enjoyed this enormously. He could see that she did. Conversation with Julian, when Julian wanted something, was the last sport available to her, and she played it the way she had once played bridge: ruthlessly, elegantly, and with visible pleasure in her own superiority.

She offered him memories instead of answers. The gala of 1987, when the chandelier shed a crystal onto the dessert table and your father laughed until he cried. The year the roof leaked over the organ and the organist wept. Ansel Ishikawa at fifteen, playing in the small hall — *extraordinary, of course, everyone said so, though I always thought he rushed the left hand* — and Julian went cold at that, at the specificity, at a detail no casual acquaintance would hold for thirty years. *Rushed the left hand.* Who remembers a boy's left hand?

But when he asked directly — Mother, did Setsuko Ishikawa write to you, did she write to you many times, did we answer her — his mother looked at him with such clear, unclouded, mildly amused blue eyes that the questions died in the air between them like moths against glass.

"Julian," she said, and took his hand, and her grip was still strong, still the grip that had held his wrist above the keys when he was four. "If that poor woman wrote to us and we failed her, then that is a sorrow, and I am sorry for it, and I will say a prayer for her soul, which I assume needs one, since she raised a boy who broke. But if you are asking me whether I conspired in the ruin of a child—" she released his hand and folded hers again, scepters returned to their case "—then you may look at me and tell me what you see."

He looked. He saw his mother. He saw the woman who had chaired three restoration campaigns and endowed a library and never, in his entire life, lied to him — or so he had always believed, though he understood now, sitting in the low chair in the October light, that he had believed it partly because she had never been in a position where lying was necessary. Power doesn't require lies. Power requires only that other people prefer the truth you've given them.

"I see my mother," he said.

"Then stop interrogating a dying woman about stationery," she said, "and tell me about the endowment, because Priya reads the newspaper aloud to me and I may be old, Julian, but I can add."

* * *

In the car afterward he sat under the copper beech and did not start the engine.

Here was the trouble, and he turned it over honestly, because dishonesty with himself was the one luxury he had never permitted — the one discipline his mother had actually instilled. The trouble was this: he could not find the seam in her. Twenty years of reading donors, trustees, liars, and lawyers had trained him to locate the place where a person's account of themselves goes soft, the hesitation, the over-explanation, the detail volunteered too eagerly. His mother had shown none of it. She had been, by every measure he possessed, telling the truth.

Which meant either that she was innocent, or that she was so far beyond his instruments that his instruments read her as clear sky.

He thought about the thumb moving across her hand. One stroke. He thought: *that could be nothing. That could be arthritis remembering itself. That could be a woman hearing an unpleasant name after thirty years.* He thought: *I want it to be nothing.* And there it was, the real finding, the one he sat with while the leaves came down around the car like slow bronze rain: he wanted her innocent. Not for her sake. For his. Because if Marguerite Veren had engineered the destruction of Ansel Ishikawa to protect her own son's career, then Julian had spent twenty years standing on a stage built over a grave, and every note he had ever played — every speech, every gala, every tribute to the Veren legacy — had been the sound of the grave being tamped down.

He had suspected. That was the word he finally allowed himself, alone in the car, the word he'd been circling since the wardrobe. He had suspected for years, in the way you suspect a house has dry rot: you don't investigate, because investigating obligates you. There had been a season — he was nineteen, home from Vienna, full of himself and someone else's opinions — when he'd asked his father, casually, terribly, why the Ishikawa boy's debut program had been changed at the last minute, why the hall had been switched, why the reviews that should have launched him had landed instead like obituaries. His father had gone very still and then very kind, which was worse, and had said: *Julian, the boy wasn't ready. These things happen. It's nobody's fault.* And Julian, who was nineteen and wanted to believe the world was administered rather than rigged, had accepted it, and had carried the acceptance so deep that he'd mistaken it for ignorance.

It was not ignorance. It had never been ignorance. It was a debt, and debts of silence accrue interest, and the interest had compounded quietly for two decades until a janitor pried open a wall and the whole balance came due at once.

He started the engine. On the drive back he did not rehearse. He listened instead to the sound the car made, tire-hum and wind, and thought about how his mother had said *I will say a prayer for her soul,* present tense, generous, as though Setsuko Ishikawa were safely dead and beyond correspondence. He hadn't corrected her. He hadn't told her that Setsuko Ishikawa had died four years ago in a rented room in Sacramento with a box of unsent letters behind a wardrobe, and that her son now cleaned the floors of the institution that had eaten them both.

He hadn't told her because telling her would have been a move, and moves get answered, and he was suddenly, freshly afraid of what his mother might answer with. Ninety-one years old, hands like paper, and still he would not turn his back on her in a room. That was inheritance too.

* * *

The conservatory at dusk was his favorite version of it, which he acknowledged was a betrayal of every principle he claimed to hold. The limestone went gold, then rose, then that particular bruised violet the brochures called *the blue hour* and the development office had tried, twice, to trademark. Students drifted through the corridors with instrument cases slung like coffins and weapons, depending on the hour. Somewhere upstairs someone was hacking through the same eight bars of Rachmaninoff with the patience of a woodpecker, and Julian stood in the entrance hall listening to it and felt the old ache, the one that had no name he was willing to use.

Because here was the thing he had never said aloud, not to his mother, not to his father, not to the mirror: he had been the lesser pianist. Everyone in the building had known it, including, eventually, him. Ansel Ishikawa at fifteen played the way weather happens — you didn't evaluate it, you endured it, you were changed by it. And Julian had been good, genuinely good, conservatory-good, competition-finalist-good, the kind of good that requires a family willing to spend everything to keep the road clear. His parents had spent everything. They had spent the endowment's goodwill and the faculty's independence and, if the box behind the wardrobe meant what it meant, a boy's mind.

And the terrible arithmetic, the arithmetic he could not make come out any way but cruel: it had worked. Julian's career had been modest and respectable and exactly sufficient, and when his hands — adequate hands, honest hands — had decided they preferred conducting administration to conducting orchestras, the institution had simply absorbed him, the way an organism absorbs a splinter. Director at forty-four. The Veren name over the door. Everything paid for by a boy who rushed the left hand.

He walked up to his office and unlocked the drawer where he kept the things he couldn't file: the auditor's letter, the second auditor's letter, and — wrapped now in a page of the *Gazette* like something perishable — the single sheet Ansel Ishikawa had handed him in the dark of the gala kitchen, folded in quarters, smelling faintly of mop water and lemon oil. His mother's handwriting. *Please. I am begging you as one mother to another. Tell me what he did wrong.*

One mother to another. And Marguerite, in the sunroom, offering prayers for her soul.

Julian sat down at his desk and put his head briefly in his hands, a posture he permitted himself eleven seconds, timed, because self-pity on a schedule was the only kind that didn't spread.

Then he straightened, because there was work, and the work had a shape now, and the shape was this: if the letters existed — if his mother had kept them, answered them, refused them, filed them behind a wardrobe like a body — then somewhere there might be more. His mother kept duplicates of everything; she duplicated her duplicate keys; she had once mailed him a copy of a photograph he was holding. If there was a box behind a wardrobe, there was a second box, or a folder, or a locked drawer at Rosemere, and it would be found — by the demolition assessors, by the journalists already sniffing at the endowment, by anyone — unless he found it first.

Not to destroy it, he told himself. To understand it. To know the whole truth before anyone else got to hand him a partial one.

He recognized the sentence. His father had said nearly the same thing, once, about the debut program. *I just want to know what happened, Julian. So we can protect everyone properly.*

Julian Veren turned off his office light and went down through the blue hour to the condemned wing, carrying a flashlight, telling himself it was only to look.
