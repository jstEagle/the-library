## 10. Nadia

The trouble with befriending a student, Ansel had learned over the past few months, was that friendship required answers.

He had managed to avoid it for most of his life. His mother had asked him questions — *what are you feeling, Anselm, tell me what you're feeling* — and he had answered with music, which she accepted because she was his mother and mothers accept currency they know is counterfeit. The conservatory had never asked him anything at all; it had simply told him what he was, and he had agreed, the way a mirror agrees with your face. And after the collapse, after the silence, nobody in the world had wanted to know why he quit, because the answer was already written into the story everyone preferred: prodigies burn out, prodigies are candles, candles do what candles do. It was almost kind, the way people stopped asking. It was also the deepest insult of his life, though it had taken him twenty years to feel the insult under the mercy.

Nadia Okonkwo did not stop asking. This was, he suspected, the entire reason he liked her.

She was seventeen, small-boned and ferocious, with the particular posture of someone who had been told since childhood that she was gifted and had decided to treat the information as a job offer rather than a compliment. She was at the conservatory on scholarship — half scholarship, then three-quarters, then whatever fraction the board was currently pretending not to resent — and she practiced in the evenings in the old wing, the condemned one, because the new practice rooms had booking systems and waiting lists and the old wing had only dust and excellent acoustics and him, pushing his mop through the corridor like a ghost who'd taken a vocational turn.

She had started talking to him in October. Not *at* him, the way students talked to janitors, that ambient rudeness of the young, but actually talking: about her teacher, who wanted her to sound more "spontaneous," a word Ansel privately translated as *more obediently wild*; about the Rachmaninoff she was butchering and knew she was butchering; about her mother, who worked nights and slept days and had never once heard her play live. Ansel had answered in monosyllables for two weeks, out of policy. Then one evening she played the Rachmaninoff's second movement and missed the same phrase the same way for the fourth time, and he heard himself say, from the doorway, "You're breathing wrong."

She turned around on the bench. "I'm breathing fine."

"You're breathing like someone carrying something heavy up stairs. You have to breathe like someone setting something down." He demonstrated, absurdly, with his arms full of nothing, and she laughed — a real laugh, unguarded, the kind of laugh that made him feel both useful and dangerous — and then she breathed differently and the phrase unlocked, just like that, four bars falling open like a window.

After that it was over. He was done for. Twice a week, sometimes three, she played and he pretended to mop and offered corrections from the doorway, and she took them all, and neither of them ever said aloud what they were doing, which was teaching and being taught, which was the only thing he had ever been good for and the thing he had sworn off the way other men swear off drink.

Tonight was Thursday, and Thursday meant Chopin, and Ansel came down the corridor at seven with his cart already parked by the stairwell because he'd learned that the squeak of its wheel ruined her pianissimos. The old wing smelled of cold plaster and radiator dust. Her light was on in Practice Room Seven.

Not Seven. He corrected himself. She was in Nine. Seven stayed dark. Seven had stayed dark since the night he'd gone in there and played four bars of the piece he'd never finished and come out shaking like a man who'd touched a wire he thought was dead. He kept the mop bucket away from Seven's door now, gave it a wide berth, an old man's superstition dressed as efficiency.

In Nine, Nadia was working the Ballade in G minor, and she was working it badly, which meant she was working it honestly, which meant she was having a bad day.

"Again?" he said from the doorway.

She didn't turn around. "Again."

He listened. She got through the first page clean, then tightened at the development — shoulders first, then wrists, then the whole architecture of her collapsing inward like a folding chair — and the music went from speech to recitation. He knew that tightening. He had invented it, practically. There was a whole taxonomy of fear encoded in the way young pianists' bodies betrayed them, and he could read it the way his mother had once read weather in her knees.

"Stop," he said gently.

"I can fix it."

"You can't fix it while you're frightened of it. Play me the passage you love instead. The one you'd play if no one were grading you."

She sat still for a moment. Then, without ceremony, without the preparatory hunch, she played the second theme — the singing one, the one every teenager loves because it sounds like being understood — and it was beautiful, and it was easy, and her hands looked like they belonged to someone else entirely, someone unafraid.

"There," he said. "That person. Bring that person back for the hard part."

She swiveled on the bench, and in the overhead fluorescent her face was suddenly very serious, the way faces go when a joke is about to be set aside. "Can I ask you something?"

"You usually do."

"Why did you quit?"

The question landed in the room between them, plain as a dropped glass.

Ansel felt his face begin the maneuver before his mind consented to it — the small upward pull at the corners, the softening of the eyes, the expression he had spent two decades perfecting, the smile that ended conversations. It was a good smile. People trusted it. It said *nothing here, move along, isn't the floor shiny* — and it had gotten him through class reunions and hospital corridors and the one terrible afternoon when a journalist had found him fixing boilers in Cleveland. He deployed it now with all its old craftsmanship.

"Oh," he said, "you know how it goes. Hands, life, the usual. Somebody has to keep this place standing, and apparently it's me."

Nadia looked at him.

She was seventeen, and she saw through it in perhaps four seconds, which was faster than the journalist, faster than his sister, faster than anyone except his mother, who had built the smile with him and therefore knew its seams.

"That's not an answer," she said. "That's a door closing. I've watched my mom do it. I know what doors look like."

"Nadia—"

"You play corrections like somebody who could be playing anything. You breathe when I breathe. Last week you hummed the cello line under the Brahms before I even got there, and you weren't thinking about it, you were *in* it. Nobody quits that. People get quit *from*." She folded her arms, and there was something in her face that wasn't challenge exactly — it was more frightened than that, or hopeful, hope and fright being neighbors. "What happened to you?"

The radiator ticked. Somewhere above them a pipe knocked twice, the building's old complaint, its arthritic conversation with itself. Ansel stood in the doorway with his mop like a man holding a prop he'd forgotten the purpose of.

And here was the thing he discovered, standing there: he wanted to tell her. That was the danger. Not the question — he'd survived questions for twenty years — but the wanting. Because Nadia was seventeen and brilliant and on a scholarship that some men in a conference room discussed as a line item, and because he had recently read eleven letters in his dead mother's handwriting that proved his own ruin had been a decision, made by people who signed their names with fountain pens, and because caring about this girl's future meant admitting that futures could be taken, deliberately, administratively, by people who called it stewardship.

Tenderness, he was beginning to understand, was not a soft thing. Tenderness was exposure. To care about her was to stand again in the path of the machine.

"It's late," he said, and heard his own voice do the other trick, the gentle deflection, the voice of a man easing a child away from a hot stove.

"No," she said. "It's not. It's eight-fifteen. You stay till ten. I've seen your schedule taped inside your closet door in the supply room, don't make that face, I was looking for spare strings for the upright." She stood up from the bench. She was so small. She was so entirely not small. "Mr. Ishikawa."

Nobody had called him that in this building in twenty years. The name went through him like a struck tuning fork.

"My mother says you should never ask a man a question unless you're prepared to sit down for the answer," she said. "So I'm sitting down." And she sat, right there on the floor, cross-legged, in her coat, in the middle of Practice Room Nine, and looked up at him with the patience of someone whose mother works nights and therefore knows exactly how long a person can wait.

Ansel laughed. He couldn't help it — a short, cracked, genuine laugh, the first one the building had gotten out of him. "You're going to catch cold on that floor."

"Probably."

"The radiators in this wing haven't worked properly since—" He stopped. Since before you were born. Since before the debut. Since the autumn the Verens decided their son needed protecting and their patronage needed pruning, and a nineteen-year-old walked onto a stage in this building and his hands left him in front of four hundred people, and afterward polite letters were exchanged in rooms he would not enter for two decades. "Since a long time," he finished.

He leaned the mop against the doorframe. It felt like laying down a shield.

"All right," he said. "But we do it my way. I talk, you listen, and if I stop, you let me stop. Deal?"

"Deal."

He didn't sit. He wasn't ready to sit; sitting was a commitment and he intended to leave himself an exit, some old habits being load-bearing. He stood by the window instead, where the glass had fogged at the corners, and looked out at the parking lot, at the sodium lamp turning the wet asphalt the color of weak tea.

"I didn't quit," he said. "That's the first thing. Everyone thinks quitting is a decision, and sometimes it is, but mostly it's a thing that happens to you, like weather. I was nineteen. I was supposed to debut here — this hall, the big one. There was a piece. My piece. Unfinished, but the Verens wanted it on the program anyway, unfinished, because a prodigy playing his own work was better publicity than a prodigy playing Beethoven like everybody else." He heard his voice flatten out, go reportorial, and let it. Reportorial was survivable. "Something happened that night. I've spent twenty years calling it nerves, because nerves is a word that doesn't require anyone to be guilty. My hands stopped answering. That's all. That's the whole story as I told it to myself for twenty years."

Nadia hadn't moved. In the window's reflection he could see her, small and still on the floor, her chin on her knees.

"And now?" she asked softly.

*Now* was a box behind a wardrobe panel. *Now* was his mother's handwriting, growing smaller and more desperate across eleven envelopes, addressed to Marguerite Veren, returned unopened or answered in two courteous paragraphs that might as well have been returned. *Now* was the sentence he had read six times by flashlight, the one where his mother wrote: *You must understand what is at stake for your family, Mrs. Veren, but I beg you to understand what is at stake for mine.* And beneath it, in the margin, in pencil, in a hand he recognized as Marguerite Veren's from the gala programs: *We all stake what we can afford.*

"Now," he said, "I'm not sure the weather story is true."

He expected her to push. She was built to push; it was her best quality. But she surprised him. She just nodded slowly, the way you nod at a piece of music that has modulated somewhere you didn't expect, and said, "My mom used to say the truth keeps. Like dough. You can leave it, but it keeps rising."

"Your mother sounds wise."

"She sounds tired. It comes out sounding the same." Nadia unfolded herself and stood, brushing dust from her coat, and for a moment she was awkward again, seventeen again, a kid in a cold room. "Can I ask one more thing? Not the story. Just — practical."

"Practical."

"If you could play again — really play, not doorway corrections — would you want to?"

The question was worse than the first one. The first one was about the past, which was sealed, which had administrators. This one was about the future, which was open, which terrified him precisely because it was open. He thought of Practice Room Seven down the hall, dark and patient. He thought of four bars. He thought of the way his hands had shaken and the way, underneath the shaking, they had known the music — known it the way scar tissue knows the wound, intimately, unwillingly.

"Go home, Nadia," he said. "It's late, and you have a lesson tomorrow, and if you show up with bags under your eyes, Dr. Feld will blame me, and I'll deny everything."

"That's a door," she said, gathering her music.

"It's a very nice door. Solid oak."

She paused at the threshold, bag over her shoulder, and looked back at him with an expression he couldn't file anywhere — part affection, part audit. "Thursday," she said. "Ballade. I'm bringing that person back for the hard part, so you'd better be here."

"I'm always here," he said, and heard how it sounded, and didn't take it back.

Her footsteps faded down the corridor. A door closed at the far end. Then the building settled into its night voice — pipes ticking, wind testing the old windows, the particular silence of a place that holds music all day and digests it after hours.

Ansel stood alone in Room Nine. On the piano's closed lid, faint in the dust, was the print of her hand where she'd braced herself getting up. He looked at it for a long time.

Then he took out his phone — the cheap one, the burner-adjacent one he used for nothing but the schedule and his sister's calls — and checked the message that had come in at 6:40, while he'd been listening to her butcher Chopin, and which he had not opened until now, because opening it would make it real.

**Julian Veren:** We need to talk. Not at the office. Tomorrow, 8pm, the boathouse. Come alone. — J

Below it, sent nine minutes later, one more line:

**Julian Veren:** Someone's been asking about the practice wing.

Ansel put the phone away. Down the corridor, Practice Room Seven waited in the dark, patient as a held breath, and outside the fogged window the sky over the parking lot was turning that color he had no name for anymore — not night, not quite day, the hour between, when things look like what they're becoming rather than what they've been.
