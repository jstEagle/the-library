## 12. Marguerite's Confession, Partial

The good days arrived without warning, like weather. Marguerite Veren had learned not to trust them—by afternoon the fog would come back through her mind the way damp came through the north wall of the estate—but in the mornings, some of them, she woke clear as a struck bell, and on those mornings she summoned Odile.

Not Odile Pemberton—the other one, the nurse, though it was a small cruelty of coincidence that they shared a name. Her Odile was a solid Belgian woman with forearms like a cellist and no patience for sentiment, which was precisely why Marguerite trusted her with this project. Sentiment was what everyone else brought her: flowers, grandchildren's photographs, priests. What Marguerite wanted was a stenographer.

"Are we doing the tape today?" Odile asked, setting down the breakfast tray. "Or are we having porridge like a person?"

"Both. The machine is in the drawer."

"The machine is in the drawer because you hid it from me last Tuesday when you were convinced I was an auditor from the tax office."

"I was *not* convinced. I was being cautious." Marguerite sat up against her pillows and arranged herself, which took longer than it used to. Arranging oneself was now a full-body undertaking. "A woman of my position learns caution late, but she does learn it."

"Your position," said Odile, retrieving the recorder, "is horizontal."

"My position is *historical*," Marguerite said, with dignity, and pressed the button.

* * *

The trouble with dictating a memoir, she discovered, was that memory did not arrive in order. It arrived the way guests arrived at her parties used to—in clusters, uninvited, some of them drunk.

"Begin with Julian," Odile prompted, because Marguerite had taught her that much. Begin with Julian, or begin with the conservatory; those were the two subjects on which Marguerite's voice changed.

"I will begin with the piano," Marguerite said. "Because everything begins with the piano, whatever my son tells his accountants."

She closed her eyes and the room went away. This was the mercy of the good days: the room could be made to go away.

"When Henri and I bought the building on Delacroix Street, it was a warehouse. A *warehouse*, Odile. Rats in the rafters and a smell I will not describe before lunch. Everyone said we were fools. Henri's brothers said it loudest, which is how you know who your family is—they tell you the truth only when they can watch it hurt. And we put pianos in it. Forty-one pianos. I counted them myself, walking the corridors at night when the workmen had gone, touching the lids. You could hear the whole building breathing. That is not a metaphor. Old wood breathes. Anyone who has spent a night alone in a concert hall knows this and will not say so, because it sounds mad, but it is simply true. The building breathed, and I thought: here is something worth the rest of my life."

She opened her eyes. The ward was still there, beige and humming. Odile was watching her with the expression she reserved for moments when Marguerite became, briefly, someone other than a difficult old woman—a kind of startled respect, quickly folded away.

"You loved it," Odile said. Not a question.

"I built it," Marguerite said. "Love is what people say afterward."

* * *

On the next good day, she dictated about the boys.

This was harder. She noticed herself circling it, the way her driver circled the block when she was late and he did not wish to say so. She began three times.

"The year Ansel Ishikawa came to us, he was eleven years old and his shoes were held together with electrical tape. His mother sat in the back of the audition hall with her hands folded like a woman in church, except she wasn't praying, she was listening—I could see it, she was hearing every note twice, once with her ears and once with her heart, and both hearings were breaking her. Do you know what I thought? I thought: there is the mother I would have been. And then I thought: thank God I am not, because look what it costs."

Odile said nothing. Odile had learned that on the tapes, silence was a question.

"We gave him everything. Scholarship, stipend, the good practice rooms, the teacher everyone wanted. Henri said we were buying a racehorse. Henri was a philistine but he was not wrong about the economics. And the boy—Ansel—" Here Marguerite paused, and the pause lasted long enough that Odile glanced up. "The boy played like the building sounded. Like something breathing. I have heard Horowitz. I have heard Rubinstein, live, twice. I am not confusing myself. The child was better than either of them at eleven, and if you think that is a grandmother's boast, then you never stood outside Practice Room Seven in 1979 and heard him play the Schubert."

"And Julian?" Odile asked gently.

"Julian worked," Marguerite said, and her voice did the thing it did—firmed, brightened, became a wall with wallpaper on it. "People forget that. They remember that he was mine, and they assume. But Julian practiced four hours a day from the age of six. He won competitions. Real ones, judged by strangers. He earned his place at that school if any child ever earned anything, and I will dictate that sentence as many times as necessary, because it is true and because history is written by whoever bothers to write it down. Which is why we are writing it down."

* * *

It was on the fourth good day that she reached the season of the debut, and here the circling stopped working. Memory laid it out flat, all at once, the way a dealer lays out cards.

She told it to Odile in fragments, stopping the tape twice, once for water and once because her hands were shaking and she did not want the machine to hear.

"Understand the situation," she said, after the water. "Henri's shipping company was dying. He would not say so at dinner, but I read the ledgers after he slept—we had that kind of marriage, honest in the margins. Everything we had left was in the conservatory. Everything. And the conservatory's future, its endowment, its name, the whole fragile architecture of it, rested on producing a prodigy. One prodigy. That is what patrons understood in those days. Not forty-one pianos. One name on the poster.

"And we had two."

She stopped again. Outside the window, October was doing its slow gold work on the garden she could no longer walk in.

"Julian was nineteen and very good. Ansel was seventeen and—other. There is no polite word for the difference. Good is a thing you can measure. Other is a thing you can only survive. Every jury knew it within four bars. Every jury, and therefore every donor, and therefore the whole future of the institution was going to walk out the door on the arm of a boy whose shoes were held together with tape, and my son—my brilliant, hardworking, *earned* son—was going to be the footnote. The patron's son who almost. Do you know what they would have written about Julian? Nothing. Nothing is what they would have written."

"So what did you do?" Odile asked. She asked it quietly, the way you ask a patient to describe the pain.

Marguerite looked at her for a long moment. On the good days, she was capable of looking at things directly. That was the tragedy of the good days, she sometimes thought: they gave you just enough clarity to see the cliff, and never enough strength to climb back up.

"I protected my family," she said. "I made some calls. I spoke to people about scheduling, about programming, about the debut program—about a piece too large, a hall too cold, a jury already inclined toward a European candidate. Small things. Administrative things. A dozen decisions, each defensible, each deniable, none of them—*none* of them—intended to break anyone. And the debut went badly. Badly in the way debuts go badly; it happens, it happened to Schnabel, it happens. And Ansel was young, and proud, and his hands—" She stopped. Started again. "His hands failed him. That part I did not do. Whatever else, that part I did not do."

"Did you know what it would cost him?"

"I knew what it would cost *us* if I did nothing." The wall with the wallpaper. "And I will tell you something else, Odile, and you may write it down verbatim, because it is the truest sentence I own: I believed I was saving him too. From that life. From a life where your entire worth is decided at seventeen by tired men in a cold hall. I watched what that life did to Julian—it hollowed him, it is hollowing him still—and I thought, at least Ansel gets to be ordinary. At least he gets to be *free*. People said cruel things later, that we destroyed him. We interrupted him. There is a difference. Interruption is survivable. I survived being interrupted my entire life."

"Did he ever thank you for it?"

The question landed in the room and lay there. Marguerite found she could not pick it up.

"He sent a card when Henri died," she said finally. "Very brief. Very correct. His mother wrote to me for years afterward—did I mention that? Letters. Asking for help, asking for consideration, asking for a teaching post, asking for a hearing. Polite letters. Desperate letters dressed as polite ones, which is the hardest kind to refuse, because refusing them makes *you* the monster in the story. I refused them. All of them. I had Odile—that is, I had the secretary—file them unanswered. Some questions answer themselves if you refuse to open them."

She pressed the stop button herself, that time.

* * *

On the fifth good day, she dictated about love, because Odile asked her to, and because the fog was coming early and she wanted the important things down first.

"Here is what nobody understands about power," Marguerite said. "They think it is appetite. It is not. It is *arithmetic*. Someone must decide, and the deciding falls to whoever can bear it. Henri could not bear it—he took to his boat. The board could not bear it—they took to their golf. So it fell to me, and I bore it, and bearing it meant choosing between two boys, and I chose, and I have been choosing the same choice every day since, every morning when I wake and remember what I am. That is love, Odile. Not the feeling. The *re-choosing*. I re-chose Julian every day for fifty years. Show me a mother who has done more."

"And Ansel?"

"Ansel I saved from gratitude," Marguerite said. "Which is the cruelest gift of all, and someday, if he is half as intelligent as he was at eleven, he will understand it."

She did not say whether she believed he would forgive her. On the tapes, she never said that. There were limits even to arithmetic.

* * *

The fog came that afternoon, as it always did, and on the sixth morning Marguerite woke into gray water and could not find the recorder. She accused Odile of stealing it, then forgave her, then accused her again, then wept for twenty minutes over a photograph of a building.

But on the seventh morning the bell struck clear again, and she pressed the button before Odile had finished pouring the tea.

"Addendum," she said. "To yesterday's—no. To the fourth session. The debut. There is a detail I omitted, and a memoir with omissions is just gossip with better binding.

"After the debut, after the reviews, after the boy withdrew—I wrote to his mother. One letter. I want that recorded. One letter, offering money. Quietly, generously, no conditions—or conditions, yes, there were conditions, the condition being distance, the condition being that the Ishikawas take their grief somewhere it wouldn't sit across the table from my son at faculty dinners. She refused it. Did you know that? Refused the money. Returned the check. And kept writing her letters instead, year after year, as if words were a currency anyone honored."

Marguerite paused. Somewhere below, in the house that was too large and too mortgaged, a door closed.

"She kept the letters," Marguerite said slowly. "I have always known she kept them. A woman like that keeps everything—it is her whole character, keeping. And I have thought, these last months, lying here with my arithmetic: what does a kept letter become, after forty years? Does it die? Or does it wait?"

She laughed then, briefly, a sound like a branch cracking.

"Julian thinks I don't notice things. Julian has thought that since he was six, and it has been convenient for us both. But last week he asked me—casually, over the eggs, casually the way a man asks about a fire alarm—whether I had ever corresponded with the Ishikawa family. And I denied it. Of course I denied it. Denial is the family instrument; we play it better than the piano. But I saw his face, Odile. I saw my son do the arithmetic."

She pressed stop. Then, after a moment, pressed record again, and her voice came out smaller, almost girlish, the voice from the warehouse with the rats.

"If he finds them," Marguerite said, "he will try to buy the boy off. It is the only move he knows; I taught it to him myself. And Ansel Ishikawa—" she searched for the word, and found it, and hated it, and used it anyway— "Ansel Ishikawa has already refused me once. God help us both if he refuses twice."

She switched off the machine and asked for her porridge, like a person.
