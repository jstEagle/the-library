## 3. Nine Years of Almost

The trouble with grief is that it doesn't keep regular hours. It has no shift schedule, no punch card, no union rep to complain to when you work it overtime. Grief is a freelancer. It shows up when it wants, invoices you for things you didn't order, and leaves its equipment scattered across your life — a chord progression here, a coffee mug there, the specific way someone said your name like it was the first word of a sentence she never got to finish.

I was thinking about this at 3:47 in the morning, three nights after the radio played her song, sitting in my apartment with the lights off because turning them on felt like an admission that I intended to be awake much longer, which I did.

Here is what I had not told anyone: I knew that melody before the radio played it. Not from any recording — there is no recording, that's rather the point of me — but from a kitchen on Delancey Street, nine years ago, when Juniper Vale stood at my stove burning rice and humming sixteen bars of something unfinished into the steam.

She hummed it once. Maybe twice. And then she died, and the melody went with her, and for nine years I carried it around like a coin from a country that no longer exists — valuable nowhere, spendable nowhere, but mine.

Until a pirate frequency decided it wasn't.

* * *

People assume collaborators fall in love the way people assume rivers flow downhill: obviously, inevitably, and without anyone having to make a decision about it. Juniper and I were the counterexample. We collaborated for four years and managed to remain almost-lovers the entire time, which is a feat of engineering I still don't fully understand. It's like building a bridge and then deciding, every single day, not to cross it.

We met at the Night Branch, actually. She came in asking for — I swear this is true — "everything the city has ever failed to say out loud." Most patrons who ask for impossible things get redirected to the self-help section. I took her seriously, which was either the best or worst decision of my life, depending on the day.

"I catalog absences," I told her. "Letters unsent. Apologies unmade. The pauses in other people's conversations."

"That's the saddest job description I've ever heard," she said, delighted. "I want to write songs about it."

And that was the whole courtship, more or less. Four years of writing songs about silence while maintaining one of our own.

Her name suited her. Juniper: sharp-needled, evergreen, the kind of tree that survives where nothing else bothers growing. She wrote melodies the way other people confessed sins — quickly, quietly, and only after making sure no one official was listening. She had a voice like a screen door in summer, creaky and familiar and slightly broken in a way that made everything behind it feel more real.

I wrote words. She wrote music. The division of labor was clean until it wasn't, which is how most divisions of labor go.

* * *

Present tense again: 4:15 a.m., and I've given up on sleep and started doing what I do instead of sleeping, which is inventory.

This is a habit from the library. When something threatens to overwhelm the shelves, you catalog it. You assign it a number, a location, a condition report. You make the chaos legible or at least pretend to. So here is my condition report on Juniper Vale, item by item:

Item one: her handwriting, which slanted left like it was trying to escape the page.

Item two: the way she tested lyrics by singing them to the refrigerator, on the theory that appliances were the only audience honest enough to tell her the truth.

Item three: the argument.

I'll get to the argument. I'm getting to it. It's just that the argument sits in the middle of my memory the way a stone sits in the middle of a stream, and everything else flows around it, and eventually you have to look directly at the thing all the water is avoiding.

But first, item four: the kitchen on Delancey Street. Because the argument happened in that kitchen, and so did the song, and the two events are fused now like bones that healed wrong.

* * *

We rented the Delancey Street apartment together in our second year, which everyone we knew interpreted as a declaration and which we insisted, with the practiced casualness of liars, was purely financial. Two artists, one rent. Basic arithmetic. Nothing to see here.

It was a terrible apartment. The radiator had opinions. The oven ran forty degrees hot, which is why Juniper burned the rice that night, and the windows faced a brick wall eight feet away, which meant we lived our entire lives there in a kind of intimate exile, close enough to touch the world and unable to see it.

I loved that apartment. I have never said that out loud before, and now I suppose I have, to a page, which is the safest possible confession.

The night of the song — the real night, nine years ago — she'd come home from a gig that had gone badly. A bar owner had asked her to play something "upbeat," and she'd played him a ballad about his own loneliness, on purpose, and gotten stiffed for it. She came in furious in the quiet way, the way that doesn't raise its voice, just lowers everything else.

"Burnt rice again," she announced, dumping the pot into the sink like evidence at a trial.

"You could set a timer."

"Timers are for people who trust the future." She said things like that. She thought they were funny. Sometimes they were.

Then she put water on for tea she'd forget to drink, leaned against the counter, and started humming.

I know exactly where I was standing. By the bookshelf, holding a novel I'd been pretending to read for an hour. Sixteen bars, minor key, rising at the end like a question that hadn't decided whether it wanted an answer. She hummed it twice through, eyes half-closed, tapping the counter with two fingers, and then she stopped and looked at me with an expression I have spent nine years misreading.

"Well?" she said.

"It needs words," I said.

"Obviously it needs words. That's your department." She pointed the wooden spoon at me. "I'm not writing this one alone."

That was the arrangement, always. She refused to finish anything solo. "A song finished alone is a diary entry," she said. "A song finished together is a conversation." She had maybe four completed songs in her whole life, and every one of them had my fingerprints somewhere in the lyrics.

So the melody sat in the kitchen between us, unfinished, waiting for me, and I said — and I want to be very precise here, because precision is all I have left —

I said, "Tomorrow. I'm tired tonight."

And she laughed and said, "You're always tired. You catalog silences for a living; of course you're tired."

And I said something back. This is the part I can't get right anymore. I've turned it over so many times the edges are worn smooth. I think I said something about how not every melody deserved words, or maybe I said something about her being difficult, or maybe — and this version arrives most often at 4 a.m., wearing its most convincing costume — maybe I said, "Some songs should stay unfinished."

Which would have been a joke. It was probably a joke. But jokes are just truths wearing costumes too, and she went quiet in a way I noticed and chose not to investigate, because investigating would have meant admitting the evening had tilted into something serious, and serious was the country we'd spent four years declining visas to.

* * *

Here is the shape of the almost, since I'm being thorough:

There was the night in our first winter when she fell asleep on my shoulder during a storm and I stayed perfectly still for three hours because moving would have ended it, and my arm went numb, and I have never been prouder of a numb arm before or since.

There was the time she dedicated a song to "my favorite librarian" from a stage in front of forty strangers, and I sat in the back feeling like a lit window.

There was the night I almost said it — the actual word, the actual sentence — standing outside her bedroom door with my hand raised to knock, and I heard her crying inside, softly, over something I never learned, and I lowered my hand and went to bed, because I had decided that what she needed was a collaborator, not another demand.

I told myself that for years. What she needed was space. What she needed was stability. What she needed was me, reliably, platonically, permanently — a load-bearing wall, not a window.

Guilt wears devotion like a mask, and the mask fits so well you forget there's a face under it. I was devoted. I was also a coward with excellent posture. Both things were true, and only one of them got a eulogy.

* * *

4:52 a.m. The city outside my window is doing its insomniac breathing — delivery trucks, a siren winding down somewhere in the east, the particular hush of ten thousand people lying awake pretending otherwise. The insomnia has been getting worse for years; I'd know, I've catalogued it. Half my recent acquisitions at the Night Branch are letters written between 2 and 5 a.m. The night shift of the human heart keeps expanding. Nobody's sleeping, and everybody's writing.

Three nights ago, a radio station that shouldn't exist played a melody that was never recorded, hummed once in a kitchen that's been someone else's problem for nearly a decade.

I've been telling myself it was coincidence. Some songwriter independently arrived at those sixteen bars. Melodies repeat; there are only so many ways up a scale. This is the story I've been telling myself, and I am a professional cataloguer of fictions, and even I don't believe it.

Because there was a seventeenth bar. She added it that night, right at the end, after the question-mark rise — a falling phrase, resolving downward, like an answer arriving late. And the radio played the seventeenth bar.

Nobody improvises a resolution they've never heard.

* * *

Now the argument. The stone in the stream. Fine.

The argument happened eleven days before she died, in the same kitchen, and it was about the stupidest possible thing, which is how the important arguments always dress themselves.

She wanted us to submit to a label. A small one, a decent one, a man named Ferro who ran things out of a converted garage in Red Hook and paid his artists in actual money and occasional sincerity. She'd been talking to him for months. He wanted a demo. Three songs, finished, recorded, done.

"We don't have three finished songs," I said.

"We have two and a half." She slid a notebook across the table. My notebook. My handwriting. "Finish the half."

"The half isn't ready."

"It's been 'not ready' for a year, Coral. Everything of ours is 'not ready.' Do you know why? Because as long as it's not ready, nobody can hear it, and as long as nobody can hear it, nobody can reject it, and as long as nobody can reject it, we never have to find out if we're actually any good." She wasn't shouting. Juniper never shouted. She did something worse, which was to be precisely, surgically correct. "You don't protect the songs. You protect yourself. The songs are collateral damage."

I said — and here memory does me the courtesy of perfect clarity, because shame records in high fidelity — I said: "Not everything needs to be finished to matter."

And she looked at me for a long moment, and something went out of her face, some small pilot light, and she said, "No. I guess it doesn't," and picked up her coat and left, and I washed the dishes, because that's what I did instead of feelings, and I assumed she'd come back tomorrow, because she always came back tomorrow.

Eleven days later she was in a hospital bed with something the doctors had names for and I refused to, and I didn't go.

Understand: I meant to go. I composed the visit the way I composed everything, endlessly, in draft. I rehearsed apologies in the shower. I bought flowers and let them die on my table because bringing dying flowers felt wrong and buying new ones felt like starting over, and I couldn't start over, the whole point was that I'd already failed the first attempt. I told myself I'd go when I knew what to say. I told myself she'd understand. I told myself the visit needed to be finished before I could make it, which is the same disease she'd diagnosed, terminal case, patient cataloguing his own silence from inside it.

She died on a Tuesday. I found out Wednesday, from a voicemail, because I had taken my phone off the hook — this was the era when you could do such a thing — because I couldn't bear the possibility that the call would come while I was unprepared.

It came anyway. Calls are patient. They wait for you to be at your least ready and then they arrive.

At the funeral, her mother handed me a box of her notebooks and said, "She'd have wanted you to have these," and I said thank you, and I never opened them. Nine years. The box is in my closet. I know exactly where it is the way you know exactly where a wound is.

Why didn't I open them? Same reason I didn't visit. Opening them would make it real, would make it finished, would require me to find out whether the last thing she thought of me was that I was a coward who mistook hoarding for love.

Some questions you don't ask because you fear the answer. Some you don't ask because you fear the question will change the answer retroactively. And some — the worst ones — you don't ask because as long as they're unanswered, the person is still, in some unbearable technical sense, speaking to you.

* * *

5:30 a.m. The sky over the brick wall across the street is doing its blue-hour thing, that color that isn't night and isn't morning, the color the city wears when it can't decide. Blue hour. Juniper had a song fragment about it — not the melody, a different one — "the hour that belongs to nobody, so it belongs to us."

I've been sitting here reconstructing her like a man rebuilding a house from photographs. And I notice what I'm doing, because noticing is my profession: I am curating. Every memory I've laid out tonight is polished, arranged, labeled. Here is the charming radiatior anecdote. Here is the noble numb arm. Here is the tragic unopened box. It's a good exhibit. It's the exhibit of a man who loved well and lost unfairly.

It is also, I suspect, a lie of selection.

Because there were days she drove me insane. Days I resented her certainty. Days I fantasized about a life with quieter music in it. There was a whole marriage's worth of friction compressed into our almost, and I've sanded all of it down, because the dead can't object to their biographies, and the living curator gets final cut.

The radio played her unfinished song three nights ago. Tonight I caught myself humming the seventeenth bar while making tea I also forgot to drink, and the bar sounded different in my mouth than it does in my head, thinner, like a photocopy of a photocopy. Memory degrades. That's its job. That's how it makes room.

Unless something is keeping it fresh.

I keep coming back to that word the frequency used, buried in the static between plays, so faint I might have invented it: *unfinished*. The announcer — if it was an announcer, if it was a person at all — kept saying the collection was of *unfinished* songs. As though there were others. As though there were a shelf.

As though somewhere, someone is keeping her seventeen bars exactly as they were, un-degraded, un-curated, perfect — and playing them to whoever's awake enough to hear.

I should be terrified. A signal that shouldn't exist, carrying a song that was never recorded, broadcast on a frequency that isn't licensed to anyone. Every rational bone in my body files this under *haunted* and recommends avoidance.

But I haven't heard the seventeenth bar in nine years, and hearing it felt like being spoken to, and I have spent nearly a decade cataloguing other people's unsent letters, and I know exactly what I'm looking at.

Someone sent one. Someone finally sent one.

Tomorrow night — tonight, technically, in a few hours — I'm going to walk the route home from the library at 4 a.m. with the cheap radio from my desk drawer, and I'm going to find that frequency again.

And if the song plays, I'm going to listen for the eighteenth bar. The one that never existed. The one she never wrote.

Because if it's there, this isn't a broadcast.

It's an invitation.
