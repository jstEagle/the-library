## 14. The Argument, Recovered

The archive was in the basement of the municipal records annex, which is the kind of sentence that should end with *and no one has ever loved anything there*, except that someone had. Someone had loved these papers enough to box them, label them, and carry them across town in the rain nine years ago, and then — this is the part that undid me — never come back to look at them.

I know because I checked the access log. The donation had been catalogued on a Tuesday in October and touched by human hands exactly once since: mine, that morning, when I signed my name in a ledger that hadn't been signed in four years. My handwriting looked shaky even to me, like a confession written under fluorescent light.

*Eastwick, C. — Juniper Vale papers — research.*

Research. Sure. Let's call it that. Let's pretend I wasn't there because a dead woman's song had been getting shorter every week on a frequency that didn't exist, and because a receptionist made of somebody else's stolen voice had told me that completion frees and preservation enslaves, and because I had run out of places to look for the truth except the one place I'd been avoiding for nearly a decade.

Avoidance is a skill. I'd honed it. Nine years of cataloguing other people's unsent letters at the Night Branch will teach you every technique of not-opening-things, and here I was, standing in front of eleven boxes of not-opened things with my own name practically engraved on them.

The archivist on duty was a young man named Pell who wore his cardigan like armor and asked me if I needed help.

"No," I said. "Yes. Maybe. Do you have coffee?"

"We have a water fountain."

"Then we're both suffering," I said, and he almost smiled, and I took the first box to a reading table under a lamp that buzzed at a frequency I chose to believe was unrelated to everything else in my life lately.

Box one: lyric fragments on napkins, receipts, the backs of setlists. Juniper wrote on whatever was nearest, which meant her entire creative process could be reconstructed from the trash of venues she'd played. A napkin from the Blue Heron with three lines crossed out and one line circled twice — *you keep the door open so the leaving looks like weather* — and I had to put the napkin down flat on the table and breathe at it for a while, because I remembered the night she wrote that. Or I remembered remembering it. There was a difference now, and the difference had edges.

That was the thing about the missing hours. You don't grieve what you can't feel the shape of. I knew afternoons were gone — whole stretches of us, vanished like Kell had reached into my head with a pair of dull scissors — but knowing isn't the same as aching. Aching requires a contour. And so I sat there in the basement of the municipal records annex, holding a napkin, trying to ache properly, trying to reconstruct the outline of an afternoon from its paper shadow.

It was like being handed a photograph of your own house taken from a window you've never looked out of.

* * *

By the third hour I had a system, because grief without method is just drowning with extra steps. Fragments in one pile. Correspondence in another. Business — contracts, venue bookings, a cease-and-desist from a man who claimed her bridge section plagiarized his 1987 jingle for a mattress store (it did not; his jingle was bad) — in a third.

The correspondence pile was thin. Juniper didn't write letters. She said letters were songs that gave up. But there were postcards, mostly to her sister, mostly weather reports disguised as affection: *Raining here. Thinking of you anyway.* That was Juniper's whole epistolary range, and I loved her for it, and I hated how easy she made it look, saying the small true thing instead of the large false one.

Because that was our whole problem, in the end. She said small true things. I said large false ones. Or worse — I said nothing, elaborately, for years, and called it devotion.

The fourth box was heavier than the others. I noted that the way you note anything now, with suspicion, as if the world had started leaving me clues on purpose. Inside, wrapped in a 2014 issue of a music magazine that had folded two years later, was a folder. Manila. No label. And inside the folder:

A lyric sheet. Full lyrics, four verses, in Juniper's hand — the tight, slanted hand she used when she meant something, as opposed to the loose scrawl she used for grocery lists and lies. The title at the top wasn't a title yet, just a date and a place: *March — the kitchen, yours.*

And beneath the fourth verse, a chord notation I recognized the way you recognize your own heartbeat: wrong. It resolved nowhere. It hung. It was the musical equivalent of a held breath, and I had heard it every night for weeks on a frequency that shouldn't exist, that unresolved hanging chord at the end, the reason the song couldn't finish, the reason Kell's broadcast frayed at its edges like wet paper.

She never finished it. Not because she couldn't. Because the ending required an answer, and the answer was me, and I wasn't there.

I read the fourth verse standing up, because sitting felt dishonest.

*You said the quiet part was mercy.*
*You said the silence kept me safe.*
*But I was building you a doorway*
*and you were measuring the grave.*

I put my hand flat on the table. The lamp buzzed. Somewhere upstairs a door closed, and I flinched like the building itself was editing me.

*You were measuring the grave.* Nine years I'd told myself the story: that I stayed away from the hospital to protect her, that silence was kindness, that the argument the night before she died was a small thing, a squall, nothing structural. I had built an entire architecture of forgiveness on that foundation, and here was a napkin-adjacent piece of paper informing me that she had experienced it as a man digging a hole.

Not cruelty. That's the thing. I hadn't been cruel. I'd been careful, and careful, from the inside of the person being protected, looks exactly like abandonment wearing a cardigan.

* * *

There was something else in the folder. Under the lyric sheet. An envelope, unsealed, addressed in that same tight hand:

*Coral Eastwick, Night Branch Library, or wherever they're hiding.*

*Or wherever they're hiding.* Even her insults were accurate.

I want to tell you I opened it immediately. I want to tell you my hands were steady. What actually happened is I read my own name four times, stood up, sat down, stood up again, walked to the water fountain and drank water that tasted like pennies and regret, and came back and opened it with the care of a man defusing something, which is what it was.

*Coral.*

*If you're reading this, either I'm dead or you finally learned to open things, and honestly, given the odds I've watched you beat, I'm betting on dead.*

*I've started this letter six times. This is the version where I stop being nice, because nice hasn't worked. Nice got me a best friend who hums my melodies back at me wrong on purpose to make me laugh and who cannot, in nine years of knowing me, say one true thing out loud in daylight.*

*Here's what I know. You love me. I've known since the kitchen, since the humming, since before, probably since the night you catalogued my demo tape under "miscellaneous" because filing it properly would have meant admitting it mattered. You love me the way you file things — perfectly, completely, and somewhere I can't reach.*

*Last night you told me the silence was mercy. You said if you said it out loud it would make it heavy, and I'd have to carry it along with everything else, and you didn't want to be one more weight. Coral. Listen to me, wherever you are, whenever you are. I have carried diagnoses. I have carried a piano up three flights. I can carry one sentence. What I can't carry is the not-knowing, the way you look at me like I'm already a memory you're curating. You don't get to decide what breaks me. That was never your job. That was never anyone's job.*

*So here it is, the small true thing, since apparently I have to go first even in writing: I wanted the life with you in it. Not adjacent. In it. And I think you wanted it too, and I think you'll spend years telling yourself a gentler story, and I need you to know that I noticed, that I always noticed, that noticing was the thing I did best besides the songs.*

*Finish the song. Not for me. For you. The fourth verse doesn't resolve because I wrote it angry and I ran out of time to write it forgiven. That's your half. That was always your half. You owe me an ending, Eastwick, and you owe yourself one more than that.*

*Don't preserve me. I'm not a recording.*

*— J.*

P.S. If you're reading this at the library, check the misc drawer. You cowards always hide things in miscellaneous.

I read it three times. The third time was the worst, because by then I'd stopped hoping it would change.

*Don't preserve me. I'm not a recording.*

Kell's whole cathedral, his long evening, his shelves of trapped voices — and she'd written the indictment against it nine years early, in ballpoint, to me specifically, and I had delivered it to a basement and never come back down the stairs.

Truth arriving too late to be comfortable. That's the phrase Odile would use, if Odile used phrases like that, which she wouldn't; she'd say something gentler and mean more. But that's what this was. Not revelation — confirmation. Every terrible thing I'd suspected about myself, notarized in her handwriting, dated eight days before the hospital.

Eight days. She'd written this eight days before the end and left it in a folder instead of sending it, and I understood why with a sympathy that hurt worse than anger would have: she was doing what I did. Protecting. Deciding for me what I could carry. We were two people playing the same cowardly instrument in different keys, and the duet had killed the song.

* * *

I photocopied nothing. Some things shouldn't be duplicated; Kell taught me that much. I copied the fourth verse into my notebook by hand, though — the inventory against theft, my own ledger now — and I sat with the letter until the lamp's buzzing became the only sound in the world and Pell came down the stairs to say the annex closed in twenty minutes.

"Find what you needed?" he asked.

"No," I said. "I found what I deserved."

He didn't ask. Good kid.

Outside, the city was doing its 6 p.m. impression of a functioning place, everyone streaming toward dinners and trains and lives, and I stood on the steps with the letter's words running through me like dye through water. *You don't get to decide what breaks me.* I thought about the kitchen-memory, the visits, all those nights I'd stepped through the broadcast and said only safe things while she hummed, unaware, preserved at the exact temperature of my cowardice. I'd been curating her. Filing her under miscellaneous. Doing in the memory exactly what I'd done in life, with better production values.

And I thought about the fourth verse's hanging chord, and about what Odile said — *completion frees, preservation enslaves* — and about the letter's last instruction, the one that landed heaviest because it was also, I realized, the answer to the question I'd been afraid to ask:

*That's your half. That was always your half.*

The song didn't need finishing by me alone. It needed finishing *to her* — the argument answered, the silence broken, the verse resolved out loud where she could hear it. Which meant going back through the broadcast one more time. Which meant facing Kell, whose broadcasts had been getting shorter, whose patience was a leash I'd mistaken for generosity.

My phone buzzed. Unknown number. A text, four words:

*Come alone tonight. — A.K.*

I looked at the letter in my hand, then at the message, and for the first time in nine years, the two documents agreed on something: the evening was over. The long evening was over.

I typed back: *I'm bringing the truth. Hope your archive has room.*

And started walking toward the station that isn't there.
