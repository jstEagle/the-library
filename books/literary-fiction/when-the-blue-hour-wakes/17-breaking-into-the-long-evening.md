## 17. Breaking Into the Long Evening

**Coral**

The blue hour lasted eleven minutes that night. I know because I timed it, because timing things is what I do instead of sleeping, instead of grieving properly, instead of any of the sane alternatives to walking into a building that officially does not exist.

We gathered at the corner of Verrone and Ninth at 4:12 a.m., which is when the frequency opens widest — Odile's finding, confirmed by three weeks of Theo's obsessive logs. The widow, Marta, brought a thermos of coffee she admitted she'd laced with something to steady her hands. The retired DJ, Sal, brought a portable cassette recorder "for evidence or eulogy, whichever applies." Theo brought his brother's Walkman, worn smooth at the corners from nine years of thumbs.

And I brought the inventory.

I'd rewritten it four times since Odile showed me the drawer of blank spots. Every memory I refused to lose, transcribed in my smallest hand: Juniper's laugh starting in her nose before it reached her mouth. The way she said *obviously* when she meant the opposite. The argument — all of it now, even the parts that made me look small. The hospital. The letter. If Kell was going to take anything else out of me, he'd have to take it past a written record. Paper doesn't forget just because a person does.

"You look terrible," Marta said, handing me coffee.

"I've been practicing being brave," I said. "It shows."

"It shows like a head cold."

Odile arrived last, out of uniform for the first time since I'd known her — no receptionist's cardigan, no fixed smile. She wore a coat too big for her, and under it she looked less substantial than air, like a photograph left too long in a window. She'd told us why. A song consumed whole leaves only so much behind. What remained of Odile existed mostly inside Kell's archive, shelved between a jingle for a defunct mattress company and an opera singer's final aria. Tonight we were going to introduce them to each other again.

"The transmitter room is below the studio," she said. "Kell keeps the master there because he's sentimental about basements. Everything important to him happened in one." Her voice did something complicated on the word *him*. "The archive is between us and it. He'll have felt us coming since midnight. He always knows. That's not the problem."

"What's the problem?" Theo asked.

"The archive knows you better than he does." She looked at each of us in turn. "It will offer you exactly what you came here pretending not to want. Your job is to refuse it while it's offering. Not after. While."

Sal snorted. "I've been refusing offers my whole career. How hard can it be?"

Nobody laughed, including Sal.

* * *

**Odile**

I want to say something true about fear, since I am apparently the group's designated expert on the subject, having been afraid continuously since 1974.

Here is the truth: courage is not a solo performance. I learned this the hard way, alone, in a recording booth, singing a song Ambrose Kell told me would make me immortal. It did, technically. But immortality achieved alone is just loneliness with better acoustics. What I had never had — what none of us in his collection ever had — was someone standing beside us saying *I hear you, and I'm not leaving.*

Tonight I had five someones. Six counting the thermos.

The station looked as it always looked from the street: dead, boarded, a realtor's sign faded to the color of weak tea. But at 4:23, in the exact center of the blue hour, the boards were boards the way a curtain is a wall. Coral touched the plywood and it gave like skin.

"After you," they said to me, which was polite, considering I was the ghost.

Inside smelled the way it always smells: warm dust, vacuum tubes, the faint ozone of a signal being held against its will. The lobby lights were on. They are always on. Ambrose believes a lit lobby suggests business, and Ambrose has believed it's 1963 for sixty years.

We went down. The stairs to the basement are concrete and they go on longer than the building's footprint allows, which is the kind of architecture Ambrose does without noticing, the way other men tap their feet. Halfway down, the humming started.

Not the song. Not yet. Just the archive, warming up. Shelves full of trapped voices don't creak; they *anticipate.* I could feel them rearranging themselves below us like an orchestra tuning for a conductor who never arrives.

"Stay close," I said. "And whatever you hear down there — however much it sounds like yours —"

"We don't answer," Coral said.

"We don't answer," I agreed, and hated how much it sounded like a prayer.

* * *

**Coral**

The basement was not a basement. Basements have walls. This had shelves, floor to unseen ceiling, receding into a dark that wasn't empty but *populated*, and every shelf hummed.

They weren't recordings. I'd expected recordings — tapes, discs, the physical furniture of sound. These were voices the way a pressed flower is still a flower: flattened, preserved, and unmistakably once-alive. As we walked the aisle toward the transmitter door, they began to notice us.

Marta heard her husband first. I saw it hit her — the whole body flinch of a woman hearing a voice she'd buried twice, once at the funeral and once at the drawer where Odile showed her the blank spot where his laugh used to be. "Eduardo?" she said, and then caught herself, both hands over her mouth.

"Don't answer," Odile said gently, taking her elbow.

But the shelves had found the seam. Eduardo's voice — or the thing wearing Eduardo's cadence — said, *Marta, I'm cold down here, why did you stop listening,* and Marta made a sound I hope never to hear again, and kept walking, which was the bravest thing I witnessed all night, and I witnessed several genuinely heroic acts, including my own, eventually, allegedly.

Theo heard his brother. Of course he did. The Walkman in his hands clicked on by itself and played back a voice saying *come find me, Theo, I'm right here, I never left,* and Theo — sixteen years old, running on grief and energy drinks — stopped dead in the aisle.

"Theo." I put myself between him and the shelf. "That's not Danny."

"How do you know?"

"Because Danny isn't in a basement in Verrone and Ninth. Because the thing that keeps people here isn't love, it's *ownership*, and Kell never owned your brother. He owned the song your brother almost finished. That's all this is. An unfinished thing, wearing a finished face."

Theo's jaw worked. Then he did something that undid me: he pressed play on the Walkman anyway — but he played *his own* recording, the one of himself humming the melody back, the one that had nearly gotten him killed inside the broadcast. His brother's voice on the shelf faltered, skipped, lost the thread.

"You can't hold someone who won't stay still," Theo said, to the shelf, to Kell, to nobody. And walked on.

Sal heard static that resolved, briefly, into his own younger voice reading a sign-off from 1981. He saluted it. "Nice read, kid," he said, and didn't break stride. I decided then that if I survived the night I was going to ask him for stories until one of us died properly.

And me? I heard the kitchen.

Not Juniper. Worse. I heard *myself* — my own voice from nine years ago, saying the cowardly safe things I'd said inside the second verse, and then saying the truer ones from the fourth, and then a third version I'd never said at all, some ideal apology composed by a shelf that had studied my guilt the way scholars study scripture. *You could have stayed perfect in there forever,* my own voice offered. *One more visit. We'll fix the ending this time.*

"That ending isn't mine to fix," I said out loud, and my voice cracked in the middle, and the shelf went quiet, satisfied or defeated, I couldn't tell.

The transmitter door was ahead. Steel, unmarked, warm to the touch like something breathing behind it. Odile put her hand flat against it and closed her eyes.

"He's awake," she said. "He's been awake for hours. He's been *listening to us listen.*"

"Can he stop us?" Marta asked.

Odile opened her eyes. For the first time since I'd met her, she looked less like a fragment and more like a blade.

"No," she said. "But he can make us want to stop ourselves. That's always been his whole method. He's never taken a single song by force. He waits at the door of your wanting and holds it open." She turned the handle. "So tonight we walk through our own wanting and keep going. All of us. Together. Nobody stops."

* * *

**Odile**

I have stood in that doorway many times, on the far side, greeting visitors, telling them they had appointments from years ago. Standing on this side, with living people at my back, I understood for the first time what the visitors must have seen when they looked at me: a woman-shaped invitation. I had been the lobby light. I had been the plywood that gives like skin.

I intend, if there is anything left of me after tonight, to be a door that locks.

The transmitter room was small and gold-lit and Ambrose Kell stood in front of the master console with his hands in his pockets, looking for all the world like a man interrupted while choosing a record. Behind him, the console's dials glowed the color of the blue hour itself, and threaded through the machine, visible now, undeniable, ran the song — Juniper's song, frayed at its edges, glowing like a wire about to fail.

"Coral," he said warmly. "And guests. You should have called ahead. Odile keeps the book."

"They know what I keep," I said.

"Marta." He nodded to her, kind as a doctor. "Salvatore. Theodore. Coral Eastwick, cataloguer of absences." His eyes settled on Coral and softened into something I recognized because I had once mistaken it for respect. "You've fed it well. Do you know it's stronger than anything I've collected in fifty years? Love does that. Most people bring me longing, which spoils. You brought the real thing." He stepped aside from the console, gesturing, hostly. "So here is my last offer, freely given: walk away, and the song stays intact forever. Every verse. Her voice, her kitchen, her humming. Preserved. Perfect. Yours."

I watched Coral look at the console. I watched them look at the fraying song. I watched them understand — finally, fully — that *forever* was the price tag, not the gift.

"No," Coral said.

Ambrose smiled the smile of a man who has heard no ten thousand times and outlived every person who said it.

"No," Coral said again, louder, and behind them Marta said it, and Sal, and Theo, four voices, then five with mine, a chord, an unfinished song refusing to end on his terms —

— and the shelves upstairs began, all together, to sing.

* * *

**Coral**

Later I would try to describe what the archive sounded like when it rose, and I would fail, the way you fail to describe a color to someone in a different colored room. It was every unfinished thing at once — jingles and arias and half-written lullabies, decades of almosts pressing down through the floor, and beneath it all, rising through the gold light, one clear thread:

Juniper's melody. Unraveling. Running out.

The console dials spun. Kell turned toward the sound with an expression I hadn't anticipated and wasn't ready for, and wouldn't process until the next chapter of this disaster, which is a terrible thing to realize mid-crisis: he wasn't angry.

He was afraid. Like a man hearing his own name called from a house he burned down long ago.

"The microphone," Odile said, pointing. In the corner, an old broadcast mic on a boom arm, live, its ON AIR sign flickering like a pulse. "Coral. It has to be out loud. It has to be *now.*"

I stepped toward it. The song frayed another bar. Somewhere above us, six floors of trapped voices held their breath.

I opened my mouth to sing the first verse — and the lights went out, all of them, and in the dark, very close, very gentle, Ambrose Kell said:

"You should know what finishing it costs her first."
