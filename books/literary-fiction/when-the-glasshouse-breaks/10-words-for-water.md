## 10. Words for Water

Maren Oduya had taught children before, decades ago, in a classroom with a leaking roof and forty-one students who learned Kivuli songs the way they learned everything else — loudly, badly, with joy. She had taught missionaries, who learned it the way thieves learn the layout of a house. She had taught her niece's daughter, who learned three words and then discovered television.

She had never taught anyone like Emrys Ishikawa.

The critic arrived every Tuesday and Friday at four o'clock precisely, climbing the stairs above the laundromat with the careful tread of someone who had read somewhere that stairs were beneath them but had decided not to say so. They always brought something. The first week it was pastries from a bakery whose name Maren recognized from her own reading — she had, out of spite and thoroughness, read every review Emrys had ever written, which meant she now knew the city's restaurants better than the health inspector. The second week, oolong tea. The third week, a jar of honey so expensive it should have come with its own security detail.

"You don't have to bring things," she told them.

"I know," Emrys said. "It's a reflex. I spent twenty years being comped."

"Then we will work on that too. A language cannot live in a person who is always being paid to leave."

Emrys had looked at her then with an expression she was beginning to catalogue — the small honest flinch that crossed their face before the elegant deflection arrived. She was learning to wait out the deflection. It always came, and it always passed, like weather.

Today there was no gift. Today Emrys stood in her doorway holding nothing, and Maren felt something loosen in her chest that she refused to examine until later, alone, when she could be embarrassed about it properly.

"Sit," she said. "Today is water."

* * *

Here is what Maren knew about water, or rather here is what Kivuli knew, because the language had opinions on the matter that predated her by centuries and would outlast her, though she was doing her damnedest to make that last part untrue.

English had one word for water and made it do all the work, like an underpaid servant. Rain, river, tear, tide — English split those off and left *water* to cover everything else, the way a family splits off the successful children and leaves one at home to care for the mother.

Kivuli had forty words. Not forty synonyms — Maren was strict about this, and had once thrown a linguist out of her apartment for using the word *synonym* in her presence — forty words for water depending on who was drinking it, and what the water owed them, and what they owed the water.

There was *maji ya uzima*, the water of the living, drunk by the healthy. There was *maji ya huzuni*, grief-water, which mourners drank so that sorrow would have somewhere to go besides the heart. There was water-word for the first sip given to a guest, and a different word for the second, because the second sip meant the guest intended to stay, and staying was a serious business requiring its own vocabulary. There was a word for rain that fell on a grave, and a word for rain that fell on a wedding, and these two rains were not the same rain even when they fell on the same afternoon.

And there was *maji ya kula*, which Maren saved for last, as she saved all dangerous things.

"Say it back," she said.

"*Maji ya kula.*"

"Slower. The *k* is not a stop, it is a decision. You are deciding to swallow."

Emrys said it again, slower, and this time the consonant landed where it belonged, and Maren felt the small electric satisfaction of a teacher whose student has done the thing — a feeling she had assumed was gone from her life along with her hearing in the left ear, her garden, and most of her hope.

"What does it mean?" Emrys asked. They always asked. It was their best habit and their worst one.

"It means water you drink while eating. But more than that." Maren got up, joints announcing themselves floor by floor, and poured two glasses from the jug she kept on the sill. "In Kivuli, you cannot say this word unless there is food. And you cannot eat without saying it, if you are speaking properly. It is the word that ties the throat to the table." She handed Emrys a glass. "Your reviews used to tie the mouth to nothing at all. Beautiful sentences going nowhere, like a road that ends in a field."

"That's fair," Emrys said, and drank, and said the word over the glass without being prompted, and Maren had to turn to the chalkboard and pretend to study her own handwriting for a moment.

* * *

By the sixth week, Emrys was dreaming in fragments.

They told her about it on a Tuesday, standing in her doorway with rain on their shoulders and no umbrella, looking rumpled and slightly haunted, like a man who had been interviewed overnight by authorities he couldn't name.

"I dreamed someone was speaking to me," Emrys said. "I couldn't see them. And I understood — not the words, exactly, but I understood that they were asking me something, and that the answer mattered, and I woke up trying to answer and the answer was in Kivuli and I don't know what I said."

"What did it sound like?"

"Like water going downhill," Emrys said, and then looked startled at themselves, because that was nearly a Kivuli image, and they knew it, and Maren watched them realize it.

"Good," she said. "That's how it starts. First the language visits you. Then it moves in. Then it starts rearranging your furniture." She put the kettle on. "My grandmother said learning a language is like letting a stranger sleep in your house. At first you lie awake listening. Eventually you forget they weren't always there."

"And after that?"

"After that," Maren said, pouring, "you can't remember how you lived in a house with only one room."

* * *

The salt came on a Friday.

Maren knew this because Emrys told her, and the telling took a long time, and she let it take as long as it needed, because some stories arrive already broken and you must not handle them roughly.

They were doing *maji ya bahari* — sea-water, the word for water that belongs to no one, which was a whole lesson in itself and had made Emrys quiet for ten minutes afterward, which Maren had noted with interest and filed away. And Emrys, mid-sentence, stopped. Put down the pencil. Stared at the middle distance with the expression of a man hearing his name called from another room.

"Maren," they said. "Can I tell you something strange?"

"You can tell me seven strange things. I'm eighty-one. Strange is my main diet."

"I tasted something. Just now. When you said the word for sea-water." Their voice had gone careful, the way voices go when the speaker is afraid the thing will evaporate if spoken too firmly. "Salt. Actual salt. On my tongue. It lasted maybe two seconds and it's gone, but it was — it was *there*, Maren. I haven't tasted anything in seven years. Seven years of eating professionally and tasting nothing, and just now a word put salt in my mouth."

Maren set down her chalk. She had known this might happen. Her grandmother had spoken of it — the tongue remembering what the mind attends to — but knowing a thing is coming is different from watching it walk through your door, and she found she had to press her hand flat against the table to keep herself still.

"Tell me exactly," she said. "Not the taste. The moment before."

"We were saying the word. I was thinking about the sea — actually thinking about it, the cold of it, the way it gets in your nose. And then—" Emrys spread their hands. "Then it was in my mouth. Like someone had set a glass down inside me."

"And how did that feel? Truly. Not the review version."

Emrys laughed, a short startled sound. "You've been waiting weeks to use that on me."

"I've been waiting years to use that on somebody. Answer."

"How did it feel." Emrys looked down at their hands. "Like being caught. Like I'd been getting away with something for seven years and the sea finally noticed." A pause. "And like grief, a little. Because if it could come back, then it was never gone, was it? It was just waiting for me to pay attention. Which means the seven years were—"

"Were yours," Maren said. "Yours to have spent differently, yes. That is the cost of attention, child. It shows you what you were ignoring." She softened, because the flinch had come and she had waited it out enough times now to know what lived underneath it. "But hear the other half. If it was waiting, then it was faithful. Your tongue kept faith with you for seven years. That is not a small thing. Some people lose a sense and never get it back because nothing they loved was worth tasting."

Emrys was silent for a long moment. The laundromat below thumped its slow drumbeat, dryers turning, the building's patient heart.

"So it's the language," they said finally. "Speaking it does this."

"The language doesn't *do* anything. Kivuli is not a medicine." Maren tapped the chalkboard, the sentence on it, the one Emrys had stopped asking about and started simply sitting with, which was its own kind of progress. "The language is a lamp. You are the one who finally turned it on. Attention is the same act as tasting — I told you this would happen, or I meant to, and if I forgot, blame my age and enjoy the surprise."

"You didn't tell me."

"No. Some doors a student must find themselves, or they'll believe the teacher built them." She picked up the chalk again. "Now. We are not finished with sea-water, and you are not finished being astonished, and both of those can happen at the same time. *Maji ya bahari*. Again. And this time mean it."

Emrys said it. And though Maren could not taste what they tasted — though no one alive could follow them into that particular room anymore — she saw it land. Saw the shoulders drop, the breath go out, the face do what faces do when a person stops performing hunger and simply feels it.

Cautious, she reminded herself. Be cautious. Hope was a crop that failed if you overwatered it, and she had buried too many hopes in this apartment to plant one carelessly. One taste in six weeks was a crack in a wall, not a door. Tongues were treacherous real estate. Grief gave things back and took them away again on its own schedule, and she had watched it do so to better students than Emrys — well. To one other student, once, long ago, and she did not finish that thought, because finishing it led to the hospital bill in the drawer, and the drawer stayed closed on lesson days.

Still. Still.

When Emrys left that evening, pausing at the top of the stairs to say the water-words goodbye — *maji ya safari*, journey-water, the sip taken before travel, which Maren had taught them two weeks ago and which they now offered back to her with the gravity of a man handing over documents — she stood in the doorway longer than she needed to, listening to the footsteps descend, and permitted herself, briefly, cautiously, the size of a coin, a single unit of hope.

* * *

That night she catalogued the new words in her notebook, the old exercise book with the soft cover, adding to the list that had grown shorter instead of longer for seven years and was now, impossibly, growing longer again. Forty-one entries this month. She wrote each one twice, once in Kivuli and once in the plain English that could never hold it, and beside *maji ya bahari* she wrote: *E.I. tasted salt. Six weeks. Grandmother would have called this a sign. I call it a start, and I am checking it twice.*

She was blowing out the lamp when she heard Emrys's voice below her window, faint, on the street, talking to themselves the way people do when they think no one is listening. She couldn't make out the words. She didn't need to. The cadence was Kivuli. The rhythm of it went up the stairwell of the air and into her room like smoke under a door, and Maren Oduya sat down on her bed and pressed the notebook to her chest and let herself shake, quietly, the way you shake when something you'd stopped asking for knocks anyway.

Then, because hope without discipline is just weather, she opened the drawer.

The hospital bill sat where it always sat, folded twice, dated five months ago, with the specialist's appointment card behind it that she had not used and would not use, because appointments cost money she was spending on cassettes and time she was spending on Tuesdays and Fridays. She looked at it the way you look at a dog you love that has bitten someone. Then she closed the drawer, and lay back, and planned Friday's lesson.

Friday was *maji ya mikono* — the water you pour over a guest's hands before they eat, the washing, the welcome. It required a basin. It required her to teach Emrys that in Kivuli you cannot feed a person you have not washed, and that the washing is not hygiene, it is a promise: *what happens at this table binds us both.*

And Friday, she decided, she would also answer the question Emrys had been circling for weeks without quite landing — the question about the sentence on the chalkboard, about what it meant, about why Maren had written it up there in the first week and then gone silent as a well whenever it came up. Emrys hadn't pushed. That was the trouble with critics; they were patient the way cats are patient, and just as certain that everything in the house eventually belonged to them.

Well. Let them ask. There were things Maren was almost ready to say, and a story from 1961 that she had carried alone for sixty years, and a name she had not spoken aloud since her mother died — a name that belonged to a company, and a company that was, if the newspapers on the laundromat counter were to be believed, about to give itself a very shiny award.

She fell asleep planning the basin, the water, the words.

Above her, in the smaller dark, Emrys Ishikawa slept too, and dreamed, and in the dream the sea spoke to them again in a language that was becoming less foreign every night, and held out a glass, and this time Emrys reached for it before waking.
