## 4. The Glasshouse in Winter

The path to the glasshouse had not been walked in some time, and Bram Fairweather could tell this the way he could tell most things: by measurement.

Gravel that should have sat at a depth of fifty millimetres now sat at thirty-five, having migrated over the years into the borders on either side, where it lay like spilled teeth. The weeds along the edges — dandelion, groundsel, something thuggish and umbelliferous he couldn't name without his book — had colonized the margins with the confidence of squatters who know the landlord is dead. He stepped carefully anyway. Not because he feared the weeds. Because his mother had kept this path swept for forty years, and it seemed rude to trample it carelessly on his first visit since her funeral.

Three days since the funeral. Two days since Teo had driven back to Manchester with the urn riding shotgun in a seatbelt, which was either deeply sentimental or deeply Teo. One day since Nell had said, over washing-up, "You should go out to the glasshouse before you leave," in the tone of a woman who has already decided the matter and is merely allowing him the fiction of choice.

So here he was, on a grey Thursday morning, standing at the door of the largest thing his family had ever built and the only thing they never talked about.

* * *

The Verre glasshouse — though nobody called it that; in Bram's childhood it had been simply "the big greenhouse," as if there were others, as if anything else on the property deserved the word — was a Victorian structure of white-painted iron and glass, ninety feet long, built by some great-grandfather who had apparently looked at the Somerset sky and found it insufficient. It had the proportions of a church and, in its current state, the atmosphere of one too: cold light falling in shafts, dust hanging in the air like a congregation that refused to disperse.

Bram did what he always did when entering a structure. He stopped, and he read it.

This was the habit Nell found both endearing and exhausting, the one where he'd stand in a doorway for a full minute saying nothing while his eyes moved along the ridge line, down the glazing bars, across the floor, taking inventory. She'd once asked him what he was doing and he'd said, "Checking the tolerances," and she'd said, "In your mother's kitchen?" and he'd said yes, actually, the lintel above the window had a worrying deflection, and she'd put her head in her hands and laughed until she cried, which he had privately counted among the better moments of their marriage.

The glasshouse read well, considering. The iron frame was sound — he could see it in the way the structure stood, no lean, no sag, the ridge true from end to end. Some panes were broken, maybe a dozen, the gaps stuffed over the years with cardboard, plastic sheeting, one piece of hardboard painted green, which suggested someone — his mother, surely — had conducted a decades-long campaign of improvised maintenance with whatever came to hand. A pane near the far end had been replaced with what appeared to be the glass from a picture frame. You could still see, faintly, the ghost of whatever photograph had once lived behind it: a pale rectangle, sun-bleached, anonymous.

He made a note to come back with his level. Then he remembered there was no hurry about anything anymore, and put the notebook away unopened, which felt like holding a door open for someone who wasn't there.

* * *

His mother had spent the last eleven years of her life mostly in here.

Everyone said so. The neighbours said it with the particular delicacy people use when they mean *she became strange*. Teo said it with relief, because it meant she wasn't in the main house finding fault with his life choices. Nell had said it once, kindly: "She liked it in there. It was the only place she seemed to be listening."

Bram had visited, of course. He'd stood in this aisle and watched Edith Fairweather — small, upright, cardigan buttoned to the throat even in August — move among her benches with a watering can and an expression he had never once been able to parse. They had spoken. About watering schedules. About the pH of the soil. About whether the vine needed thinning. Never about anything else, and he had told himself for years that this was simply how she was, how they were, a mother and son whose entire emotional range fit comfortably inside a discussion of drainage.

Now she was dead, and the discussions were over forever, and he was beginning to suspect that the drainage talk had been doing a great deal of load-bearing work for both of them.

Her benches were still here. Most were empty, but along the south wall a few survivors persisted — a rosemary gone leggy and wild, a tray of something bulbous and dormant, a pelargonium that had clearly given up on dignity and was growing sideways out of its pot in search of light. And on the potting bench at the far end, exactly where she had always kept them, the ledgers.

There were fourteen of them. He knew because he counted, twice, because counting was what his hands did when his chest felt like this — tight, under-inspected, due for failure. Cloth-bound, the spines faded from blue to the colour of old dishwater, each labelled in her upright hand: *1979–80*, *1980–81*, on through the years like rungs of a ladder going down into the dark.

He carried three of them to the bench by the door, where the light was best, and opened the earliest one.

* * *

Edith Fairweather had kept records the way other people kept faith.

Every plant in the glasshouse had an entry. Not just a name — a history. *Tomato 'Ailsa Craig', sown 14 March, pricked out 2 April, first fruit 11 July.* But then beneath the horticultural facts, in the same hand, smaller, almost shy: *Slow starter. Sulked through June. Came good in the end.* A fuchsia recorded as *planted too deep, my fault, forgiven.* A row of chrysanthemums noted as *Teo's favourites, he doesn't know I know.*

Bram sat down slowly on the potting stool, which groaned but held, and turned pages.

It was, he realized with a sensation like a floorboard giving slightly underfoot, a diary. All these years he'd assumed the ledgers were inventory — his mother's version of his own inspection reports, numbers and tolerances and nothing human. And here she was, between the lines of a cabbage record, being funny. Being tender. Being a person he had apparently never met, living thirty feet from his childhood bedroom.

*Sweet peas, sown by the boy, 6 April.* He frowned. What boy? Teo had never sown anything in his life; Teo considered soil a substance that happened to other people. He read on. *The boy again, 13 April. Enthusiastic, untidy. Good hands though.* And later that summer: *Sweet peas thriving. The boy left in September. Garden quieter.*

September. Some seasonal worker, then. Some village lad hired for the summer, long ago, forgotten by everyone including, presumably, himself. Bram noted it the way he noted all anomalies — filed, flagged, not yet understood — and moved on.

The middle years blurred past. Frost dates. Germination times. A running feud with a wren that nested in the ridge vent every spring and was documented with escalating respect: *the wren returns, year four, I have stopped objecting.* His mother's whole interior weather, laid out in pencil beside rows of figures. He found himself laughing once, alone, at an entry about a delivery man who had backed a lorry into the cold frames — *Mr. Prowse, again. Told him next time I shall bill him in cauliflowers* — and the laugh came out strange in the empty glasshouse, too loud, echoing off all that glass, and he stopped.

That was the trouble with rooms like this. They held sound the way they held heat, reluctantly, then all at once.

* * *

By early afternoon he had worked through eleven of the fourteen ledgers, sitting on the cold stool with his coat on, reading his mother's secret self in chronological order like a man intercepting letters. The entries got shorter as the years went on. The plants got fewer. Her handwriting stayed perfectly upright throughout, which struck him as its own kind of testimony: whatever was happening inside Edith Fairweather, it was not going to be permitted to affect the descenders.

The twelfth ledger covered the years Bram had been away, newly married, learning to be a husband the way he learned everything — by inspection, by measurement, by checking the joints for movement. In these pages the garden notes thinned to almost nothing. *Geraniums, dead. Did not water them. Could not be bothered.* This from a woman who had once billed a lorry driver in cauliflowers. Something had gone out of her, and the ledger recorded its departure without comment, the way a seismograph records an earthquake it cannot explain.

The thirteenth ledger was nearly blank. A few entries a year. Then, near the end, in a hand suddenly less certain:

*The cottage is shut up. That is done.*

And below it, nothing for six pages, and then:

*I have paid what can be paid. It does not pay for anything.*

Bram read those two lines four times. He looked around the glasshouse as if it might explain — the empty benches, the green-painted hardboard, the ghost of the photograph in the window pane. *The cottage.* There was a gardener's cottage at the edge of the grounds, he remembered that much, a low stone building half-swallowed by ivy, locked for as long as he could remember. As a child he'd assumed it was where the tools lived. He had never once seen anyone go in or out.

*Paid what can be paid.* Paid whom? For what?

He sat with the ledger open on his knees and felt the particular vertigo of a man who has inspected a bridge a thousand times and only today noticed the crack. Small. Hairline. Exactly the kind of thing you could walk past for years, provided you never looked properly, provided you kept your eyes on the deck and your hands in your pockets and your whole attention on the parts of the structure that behaved.

His mother had not behaved. Somewhere in these pages, in this room, his mother had failed at something large enough to require payment, and quiet enough to survive forty years of family dinners.

* * *

The last ledger was the thinnest. Three years' worth, and in it perhaps twenty entries total, the handwriting still upright but slower now, the pencil pressed lighter, as if the hand itself were convalescing.

*Rosemary, still going. Refuses to die. Good.*

*Wren, year nineteen. We understand each other.*

*Panes east side, four broken in the storm. Boarded them myself. Nobody comes, so nobody sees, and the plants don't mind.*

Nobody comes. Bram set the book down and looked at the length of the glasshouse, ninety feet of it, built to be full of people and plants and purpose, and tried to imagine his mother in here alone through the winters, boarding her own windows, talking to a wren. He thought of all the times he'd visited and discussed drainage. He thought of Nell saying, *it was the only place she seemed to be listening,* and wondered who, exactly, his mother had been listening to, if not the plants, if not the wren, if not the son who came twice a year and checked her lintels.

The final entry was dated eight days before her death.

No plant name. No figure. No weather note, no wren. Just a single line, written larger than any other entry in fourteen volumes, the pencil pressed hard enough to emboss the page behind it, as though she had needed the words to hold their shape through whatever came next:

*Tell her I'm sorry.*

Bram read it. Read it again. Turned the page — blank. Turned back — the same five words, waiting.

*Her.*

Not *him.* Not *them.* *Her.* Some specific woman, known to Edith Fairweather, owed an apology so heavy it had displaced forty years of botany to make room for it. His mother had died with a message unsent, addressed to a woman Bram could not name, and she had hidden the instruction in the one place she knew no one would ever look — inside a record of sweet peas and frost dates, in the private language of a woman who had spent her whole life saying true things only where she thought no one was reading.

He closed the ledger gently, the way you close a door on a sleeping person.

Outside, the light had gone the colour of weak tea. Somewhere in the village a dog was barking at nothing, rhythmically, like a hammer test. Bram stood in the aisle of the glasshouse his family never spoke of, holding his mother's last words to a stranger, and understood with sudden professional clarity that he was standing inside a structure with a fault at its foundation — old, hidden, load-bearing — and that everything above it, the house and the name and the family and the careful silence of forty years, had been bearing that fault's weight all along.

He tucked the ledgers under his arm — all fourteen, because you didn't leave evidence at the scene — and went to find the lights in the main house coming on, and Nell at the kitchen window watching for him, and the question he did not yet know how to ask forming itself slowly, pane by pane, in the darkening glass.
