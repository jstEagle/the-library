## 9. The Photographs

The study smelled the way it always had — pipe tobacco long since abandoned but never quite surrendered, lemon polish, and beneath both, the faint mineral smell of paper that has been sitting still for decades. Bram stood in the doorway for a full minute before entering, the way he did on bridges before committing his weight to them. Some rooms you tested first.

Edith's desk sat under the window like a patient animal, its leather top worn pale where her forearms had rested for fifty years of correspondence, ledgers, and what she had called *administering*. The chair was pushed in at an angle that suggested she'd meant to come back. Everything in this house suggested she'd meant to come back.

He had been avoiding this room for three days. He'd cleaned out the kitchen with Nell, sorted the linens, argued mildly with Teo about whether their mother's collection of commemorative spoons was rubbish or heritage (Teo: rubbish; Bram, unexpectedly and without being able to defend the position: heritage). But the study he had left shut, the way you leave a tooth alone because you already know what the dentist will say.

Now, on a grey Thursday with rain ticking against the glass like a slow meter, he went in.

* * *

He started with the desk because starting anywhere else would have felt like snooping, whereas the desk felt like administration. He was, after all, an administrator of sorts now — the estate had to be inventoried, valued, eventually divided. He told himself this while opening the shallow drawer where Edith kept her pens, all of them aligned nib-forward like soldiers who had been yelled at.

Beneath a tray of rubber bands that had perished into sticky amber threads, he found the keys.

There were six of them, small, brass, each tagged in her upright hand: *Garden Cottage. Cellar. Blue Cabinet. Deed Box. Green Trunk.* And one untagged key, smaller than the rest, which bothered him the way an unlabeled wire bothers an electrician. He set it aside and made a mental note — one of his lists, the ones Nell teased him about, the ones that kept him from lying awake cataloguing instead. *Find out what the small key opens.* He wrote it on the pad by the phone, because mental notes were how things got lost.

The blue cabinet stood against the north wall, between the bookshelves. It was a handsome piece, Victorian, mahogany gone dark as strong tea, and Bram had never once seen it open. As a boy he'd assumed it held tax records. He had built entire theories around that cabinet the way other children built forts. Tax records, he'd decided, and possibly the good silver, and possibly — this theory had lasted one thrilling afternoon when he was nine — nothing at all, because the cabinet was a fake front for a door into another house entirely.

The third key from the tag-ring fit the lock. It turned with a sound like a knuckle cracking.

Inside were photograph albums. Eleven of them, cloth-bound, spines sun-faded in a gradient from green to grey, arranged chronologically with a precision that made his chest ache. Of course they were chronological. His mother had alphabetized her spice rack. She had once reorganized Teo's toy soldiers by regiment while he was at school, and Teo had cried, and she had said, *You'll thank me when you're older,* and Teo, now forty-six, had never thanked her.

Bram carried the albums to the window seat and began, in order, at the beginning.

* * *

The earliest album was mostly black-and-white, corners mounted in those little triangular hinges of gummed paper. A wedding — not Edith's; someone's, some cousin's, faces he didn't know. A christening. A car that looked like it had been designed by someone angry at aerodynamics. He turned the pages slowly, half reading, the way you skim a document looking for the clause that matters.

He found the clause on page fourteen.

A garden party, midsummer by the light, everyone in pale clothes holding glasses of something. On the lawn of Verre House — Fairweather House, he corrected himself automatically, then didn't, because the correction didn't take, which unsettled him more than the photograph did. There were perhaps twenty people in the picture, arranged in the loose crescent people form when they're pretending not to pose. And along the back edge, near the glasshouse wall, there was a gap.

Not a tear. Not damage. A gap with clean edges, a rectangle of album page showing through, brown and blank, exactly the size and shape of a standing person.

Bram held the page up to the window light. The cut was surgical. No ragged tearing, no haste. Someone had sat down — probably at this very desk, with a blade and a steel ruler, because that was how Edith did everything — and removed a person from history with better craftsmanship than most surgeons manage.

He turned the page. Another gathering, this one on the terrace, summer dresses and a gramophone. Near the glasshouse door, second gap. Same height. Same width. Same careful shoulders cut away.

He went faster then, the way you walk faster past a house where something happened once. Album two: gaps. Album three: gaps, sometimes two together, side by side, two silhouettes' worth of absence standing near the glasshouse in 1963, 1964, 1967. Whoever they were, they had been cropped out so consistently that the empty spaces themselves became recognizable — a signature in reverse. You could have identified the missing person by their absence the way birdwatchers identify owls by the shape of the silence.

Bram sat back on the window seat and laughed once, a short, appalled sound that the study absorbed without comment.

His mother had spent — what? Hours? Days? — cutting people out of photographs. With a ruler. He tried to imagine it: Edith Fairweather, seventy years old, reading glasses on, doing her administering, sliding a blade along a steel edge through the neck of some forgotten man, and then squaring off the corners, and then turning the page and doing it again. It should have been monstrous. Instead, horribly, he could see it perfectly. He could even see her humming.

That was the part that frightened him. Not the act. The domesticity of it.

* * *

Nell appeared at the door around four o'clock with tea, which was her way of checking on him without admitting to checking on him. They had been married nineteen years and had developed an entire language of logistics. Tea meant *are you all right*. He took the cup, which meant *not especially, but I'm working.*

"Any luck?" she asked. She meant the search for paperwork — the deeds, the insurance, the tangle of documents Teo kept insisting existed and Bram kept failing to find.

"Photographs," he said.

"Ah." She came and looked over his shoulder at the open album, at the brown rectangles. Her breath did something small. "Good Lord."

"I know."

"She cut them *out*?"

"With a ruler, I'd bet money."

Nell was quiet a moment. Then, in the tone she used for observations she wasn't sure she wanted credited to her: "It's not the cutting that gets me. It's that she kept the albums. If you wanted something gone, you'd burn the whole page. Burn the album." She touched the edge of one of the gaps, very lightly, the way she touched things she suspected were fragile — old plaster, other people's grief. "She wanted the pictures and not the people. That's a decision. That's a *policy*."

Bram looked up at her. Nineteen years, and she could still do this — walk into a room, look at what he'd been staring at for hours, and name it in one sentence. He felt the familiar gratitude, and underneath it the familiar grief, and underneath that the thing he never let surface, which was the question of why two people this fluent in each other had stopped speaking plainly somewhere around year eleven.

"Thank you," he said, which was not what he meant, and she nodded, which meant she knew, and she left him to it, which was its own kind of sentence.

* * *

By seven the light had gone blue-grey and he had worked through eight albums. He'd made a list, naturally — a grid in his inspection notebook, date, event, number of excisions, position relative to glasshouse. Fourteen gaps across eight albums. Always near the glasshouse. In three photographs the gap stood directly beside Edith herself, young and laughing, her arm extended toward the empty space at an angle that could only mean one thing: she had been touching whoever stood there. Around the shoulder, possibly. Taking an arm. The kind of casual contact that doesn't happen between employers and staff, whatever the village might have said about the old days.

He got up, stretched until his spine reported in, and fetched the last albums. Nine, ten, eleven. The later ones thinned out — fewer parties, fewer people, the family contracting decade by decade the way rivers do before they vanish into gravel. Album eleven covered the last fifteen years and contained almost nothing: a few shots of the garden, Teo's graduation, Bram's own wedding, Nell in her dress looking at the camera with an expression he had never noticed before and now couldn't stop noticing. Hope, he thought. That was hope, caught a half-second before the shutter. Nobody had cut anything out of these later pages. There was no one left to cut.

Except.

Album nine. Christmas, 1971, judging by the tree and the terrible wallpaper. The household gathered in the conservatory — the little antechamber off the glasshouse where his mother had taken her breakfast every morning of her life. Edith, forty-ish, formidable, holding a cup and saucer. Teo aged about five on someone's knee. And at the edge of the frame, beside the glasshouse door, a figure half-cut-away — but this time the blade had slipped, or the hand had hesitated, or the ruler had shifted, because the cutout was incomplete. A strip remained attached, maybe four centimeters wide, running from hip to shoulder.

And on the strip: a hand. A young man's hand, work-worn even in miniature, gripping a pair of pruning shears.

Bram stared at it for a long time. The lamplight made the little metal blades glint, and the glint moved when he moved his head, so that the hand seemed almost to be offering the shears to him across fifty years — *here, you'll be needing these, there's deadwood everywhere.*

He thought about the gardener. Because there had been a gardener — hadn't there? A fact so structural to his childhood that he'd never examined it, the way you never examine a foundation. There had been tomatoes that appeared in August. There had been roses along the south wall that bloomed impossibly late. There had been a smell of loam and green stems in the potting shed, and a pair of gloves too big for anyone in the family, and — yes — a voice, once, singing something foreign and low beyond the hedge rows, a voice that had stopped the moment young Bram crashed through the bushes chasing a ball, and had not resumed.

He had never known the gardener's name. He had accepted this absence the way children accept everything, and then he had carried the acceptance into adulthood without ever weighing it. Thirty years of tomatoes and roses and a singing voice, and no name. He was a man whose profession was inspecting structures for hidden defects, and he had lived his entire life inside one without running a single test.

"What was the gardener called?" he asked the empty study. "What was his *name*?"

The study, which had absorbed worse questions, said nothing.

* * *

He worked until nearly midnight. He photographed every gap with his phone, dated them, cross-referenced against the family calendar Edith had kept religiously in the kitchen drawer — *Fête, June 12. Dr. Halloway, Tuesday. Garden party, staff + household, July 3.* Staff plus household. Written in her own hand. Staff had been invited to the garden party. Staff had stood next to Edith with her hand on his sleeve. Staff had then been removed from the record with a straightedge and a steady nerve.

At half eleven he found the envelope.

It was in the back pocket of album nine, flat and yellowed, unsealed, containing a single cut-out figure — the only excision she had kept rather than discarded or burned. A young man, maybe thirty, dark-haired, sleeves rolled, standing in front of the glasshouse with one hand resting on the frame as if it were a horse he was fond of. He was smiling at someone outside the frame — at Edith, presumably, wherever she'd been standing — and the smile was the worst part. It was a real smile. Unposed. The smile of a man who belonged exactly where he was standing.

On the back of the photograph, in pencil, in Edith's writing, smaller than any of her ledgers' confident numerals:

*M. — last summer before.*

Before. Before what. The word hung there, unfinished, the way expansion joints hang between bridge sections — designed to absorb movement, to let two massive things stand adjacent without crushing each other. His mother had written *before* and then stopped, and the stopping had held for fifty years, absorbing all the movement between what the family was and what it pretended to be.

Bram sat in his mother's chair, in his mother's study, holding a photograph of a man the family had amputated from itself, and he understood — with the flat, cold clarity he usually reserved for load calculations — that the demolition he needed to worry about was not the glasshouse.

Somewhere below him, the old clock in the hall struck twelve. And a floor beneath that, or so it seemed, a car door closed — the heavy, deliberate slam of Teo's Audi, arriving early, arriving unannounced, arriving the way Teo did everything lately, which was like a man with a deadline.

Bram slid the photograph of M. into his notebook, between the pages of his grid, evidence bagged. Then he went down to find out what his brother wanted, knowing — the way you know a structure is compromised before the cracks show — that it was going to be about money.
