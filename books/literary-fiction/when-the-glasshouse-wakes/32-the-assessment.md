## 32. The Assessment

The assessor's van arrived at nine-forty on a Tuesday morning, which Bram considered a promising sign. Men who came at nine-forty were men who had eaten breakfast and were not in a hurry. Men who came at seven-thirty wanted to be somewhere else by lunch, and men who wanted to be somewhere else by lunch saw what they expected to see.

He had been awake since five. Not from nerves — or not only from nerves — but because he had spent the previous evening doing what he always did before an inspection: reading his own report aloud in the kitchen, testing each sentence for load-bearing capacity. Nell would have teased him about it once. *You rehearse your paperwork, Bram. Some people rehearse wedding toasts.* But Nell was gone now, her boxes gone with her, and the kitchen held only the sound of his own voice and the tick of the Aga cooling, which he had not yet learned to relight and was beginning to suspect he never would.

The report sat on the passenger seat of his own car, bound in a clear plastic sleeve because he was not an animal. Forty-one pages. Three hundred and twelve photographs. Load calculations for every major structural member of the glasshouse, dated, cross-referenced, and annotated in the tidy hand that had won him two commendations and one marriage proposal — Nell had said, watching him fill out a form at the registry office, that she'd never seen anyone love a checkbox so sincerely.

At nine-forty-one, the van door opened and a woman got out.

Bram had been expecting a man named Derek. The letterhead had said *Derek Halloway, Structural Assessment Services*, and Bram had spent three days preparing himself for a Derek: a man in a fleece vest who would walk the perimeter with his hands in his pockets, say *hmm* at intervals, and write *beyond economic repair* on a clipboard without ever touching anything.

The woman who climbed out was perhaps fifty, short, built like someone who had carried a lot of things up a lot of ladders, wearing steel-capped boots and a high-visibility jacket over a cardigan. She looked at the glasshouse for a long moment before she looked at Bram, and when she did look at it, Bram felt something loosen in his chest — because she didn't look at it the way Teo looked at it, or the way the developer's brochure photographed it, all decay and liability. She looked at it the way you look at an old dog. With respect, and a kind of preemptive grief.

"You'll be Mr. Fairweather," she said. "I'm Priya Chandrasekaran. Derek's my business partner. He's got flu." She said this the way other people said *he's got a yacht*, as though it explained everything about the universe's general unfairness. "So you get me instead. I should warn you I'm slower than Derek and more annoying."

"Slower is good," Bram said. "Annoying I can live with."

"Everyone says that on day one." She pulled a clipboard from the van — an actual clipboard, which raised her further in his estimation — and squinted up at the eastern elevation, where the storm-cracked panes had been replaced with Odile's carefully fitted boards. "That's new."

"Last month's storm. We boarded it ourselves."

"We?"

"The owner. And me."

Priya wrote something down. Bram resisted the urge to read it upside down, mostly.

* * *

She began, as he would have begun, at the foundations.

This was the part he'd been dreading and the part he'd worked hardest on, because the foundation was where the developer's survey had landed its killing blow: *subsidence evident along the northern wall; frost heave; probable failure of original footings.* Bram had spent four days in February with a spirit level, a torch, and a borrowed borescope, and had found something else entirely. The footings were not failing. They were *moving* — slowly, seasonally, the way Victorian builders had intended them to move, sitting on a bed of compacted gravel that acted as a buffer against frost. The house above them flexed. That was the whole trick of the place. It wasn't rigid. It breathed.

"Whoever built this understood the ground," Priya said, crouched at the northern corner with her torch. "Most people fight the clay. This lot made peace with it." She glanced up at him. "You did the borescope work yourself?"

"I did."

"Where's your firm? I don't recognize the letterhead."

"I'm a bridge inspector. County council, twenty-six years." He hesitated, then added, "Retired last spring. Technically. My retirement appears to have been aspirational."

Priya snorted. "They always are. Half my colleagues retired and then turned up doing consultancy within the year, complaining about their knees and loving every minute." She stood, knees cracking audibly, and gave him a look. "You know this isn't standard practice. Submitting your own structural report to counter an assessment. Most people just ring us up and shout."

"I thought about shouting."

"And?"

"And I decided the building deserved better than shouting."

Something shifted in her face — not warmth exactly, but recognition. The look of one careful person meeting another across a muddy flowerbed. "Right," she said. "Show me your evidence. All of it. And Mr. Fairweather — if there's a weak point in your argument, I will find it. Not because I want to demolish your glasshouse. Because if I sign off on saving it and it falls on somebody's head in five years, it's my name on the paper."

"That," Bram said, "is exactly the right reason."

* * *

They spent the morning inside, and it was the strangest inspection of Bram's career, because for the first time he was not the one holding the torch.

Priya worked the way he imagined Odile worked — patiently, thoroughly, with occasional mutters that were either curses or praise. She tested the glazing bars with a moisture meter. She measured the deflection in the central ridge beam and found it, as Bram's report promised, well within tolerance. She rapped the cast iron columns with a small hammer and listened to them the way a doctor listens to a chest, and at column seven she stopped, rapped again, and frowned.

"Bogus," she said.

Bram's stomach dropped. "Sorry?"

"This one's a replacement. Different casting — see the seam? Originals are 1850s, this is turn of the century. Somebody repaired it and never recorded it." She peered closer. "Good repair, mind. Better than good. They matched the fluting." She straightened up and looked around the glasshouse with new interest. "There's history in this building people bothered to keep quiet about. You don't spend that kind of care on a structure you're planning to abandon."

"No," Bram said. "You don't."

He thought of Edith's ledgers, the second column, the payments made and stopped and resumed. He thought of Matteo Marchetti's cottage, sealed like a tomb and preserved like a shrine. His mother had kept accounts of her conscience in the margins of plant records, and apparently someone — Matteo, surely, or Edith herself under his instruction — had replaced a cracked column sometime around 1902 and told no one, because telling no one was how love survived in that family.

"You've gone somewhere," Priya observed.

"Just thinking about who did the repair."

"Family firm?"

"Family gardener," Bram said, and left it there.

* * *

They broke for lunch at half twelve, sitting on the tailgate of her van with sandwiches Bram had made at six a.m. — because a man who rehearses his paperwork also prepares lunch for assessors, and because feeding people was the one domestic skill he'd kept sharp through the long quiet years of the marriage. Cheese pickle for him, egg and cress for her, because he'd asked her assistant on the phone what she took and had received a fifteen-minute answer involving her opinions on supermarket meal deals.

"These are excellent," Priya said, with suspicion. "Did your wife make these?"

"I made them."

"Hm." She chewed. "My husband hasn't made me a sandwich since 2011. I'm going to tell him about you specifically."

"He'll be delighted."

"He'll be furious, which is nearly the same thing in our house." She brushed crumbs off her jacket and nodded back toward the glasshouse. "Your report's good, Fairweather. Annoyingly good. I found two typos and a photograph out of sequence, and that's the entire list of complaints. The deflection analysis alone is better than what my own office produces, and I'm including Derek in that assessment, and Derek would agree if he weren't currently mainlining Lemsip."

"But," Bram said.

"But nothing yet. I haven't finished." She looked at the glasshouse, its boarded east face catching the pale afternoon light. "Here's my problem. The order on my desk says assess for demolition. The developer's survey says beyond economic repair. Your report says structurally sound with restoration costs comparable to any listed Victorian glasshouse in the country, of which there are forty-one currently being restored, several of them worse than this." She turned to him. "So either the developer's surveyor is incompetent, or he was told what conclusion to reach. Which do you think it was?"

"I think," Bram said carefully, "that surveys are commissioned by people who have already decided what they cost."

"Mm. Diplomatic. I'd have said 'paid to lie,' but then I'm not the one whose brother commissioned the thing." She watched him go still. "Oh, come on. Small county. The developer's agent mentioned the family name when he booked us. Teo Fairweather, very keen, very helpful, offered to meet me on site and walk me through the hazards." She finished her sandwich. "I declined. I prefer to make up my own hazards."

Bram laughed — unexpectedly, helplessly, the first real laugh in weeks. "You'd have liked my mother," he said, and then, hearing himself, added, "Actually, no. You'd have terrified each other into mutual respect and then never spoken again."

"A common arrangement in my experience." Priya hopped off the tailgate. "Right. Afternoon round. Show me your load calcs on the ridge beam, and then I want to see the cottage."

"The cottage?"

"The outbuilding. It's on the site plan. If I'm assessing the whole estate for demolition, I assess the whole estate." A pause. "Unless there's a reason not to."

"There's no reason not to," Bram said. "It's just — it's locked. And it belongs to someone else now. Or it will, shortly, if the solicitor does what the codicil says."

"Codicil?" Priya's eyebrows went up. "Fairweather, you keep burying the lede. Is there anything else on this property I should know about?"

Bram considered. The letters. The tape. The photographs with their careful excisions. The village leaving flowers at a dead man's gate.

"Yes," he said. "Quite a lot. But most of it isn't structural."

"Pity. Structural problems I can fix." She picked up her clipboard. "Come on, then. Show me your numbers."

* * *

The afternoon went the way the best inspections go, which is to say it became a conversation between two people taking turns being right. Priya challenged his frost-heave analysis; he showed her the seasonal movement data, eighteen months of it, gathered with a ruler and a fixed datum point and a patience that had driven Odile to leave biscuits at the north corner like offerings. Priya challenged his cost estimates; he produced quotes from three restoration firms, one of whom had restored the Kew palm house and had wept, reportedly, upon seeing the Verre ironwork. Priya challenged his assumption that the glazing could be sourced; he produced Odile's research, forty pages on heritage glass manufacturers, annotated in handwriting so precise it looked typeset.

"She sounds thorough," Priya said, reading.

"She's the owner."

"The owner did the research?"

"The owner does everything. I hold the ladder."

Priya looked at him over the top of her clipboard for a moment. "You know," she said, "in thirty years of this job, I can count on one hand the number of owners who held the ladder themselves. Usually they send a representative. Or nobody." She tucked the research notes into her folder with unusual gentleness. "Tell her the ladder-holding has been noted."

By four o'clock they stood at the northern corner again, where they'd started, and Priya closed her clipboard with a snap that sounded, to Bram's strung-out ears, like a verdict.

"Well?" he asked. He hadn't meant to ask. He'd meant to wait, dignified, for however many weeks the paperwork took. But the question escaped him the way questions do when you've been holding them too tightly.

Priya didn't make him suffer. "I'm reversing the recommendation," she said. "Structure sound, restoration viable, demolition not justified. I'll file it tomorrow." She held up a finger before he could speak. "Understand what this means. The developer can commission a second opinion, and probably will. There may be a challenge. Bureaucracy doesn't bend gracefully — it bends the way old iron bends, with creaking and complaint and the strong possibility of snapping back. But my report stands on its merits, and your report stands behind mine, and between the two of us there are roughly sixty years of careful work saying this building stays." She put out her hand. "It was a pleasure, Mr. Fairweather. Genuinely. I don't often get to save something."

Bram shook her hand and found, to his mild embarrassment, that his throat had closed. "Thank you," he managed.

"Don't thank me. Thank whoever taught you to measure things properly." She paused at the van door. "And Fairweather — the column. The 1902 replacement. Whoever did that work loved this building. Make sure that gets written down somewhere. Structures remember who cared for them, even if the paperwork doesn't."

Then she was gone, taillights fading down the drive, and Bram stood alone in the yard with the light going gold behind the glasshouse and his heart doing something unprofessional and unmeasured in his chest.

* * *

He found Odile in the potting shed, which she had claimed in October and organized with a rigor that would have satisfied his mother and appalled her in equal measure. She was pricking out seedlings — Matteo's seedlings, the descendants of seeds found in tins in the cottage, tomatoes and sweet peas and something unmarked that she refused to guess at until it flowered.

"It's done," Bram said. "Recommendation reversed. Filed tomorrow."

Odile's hands went still over the tray. For a moment she said nothing, and Bram had learned enough by now to let the silence be what it was — not doubt, not gratitude, but the pause of a woman who had spent her whole life expecting doors to close and needed a moment whenever one opened.

"They'll challenge it," she said finally.

"Probably."

"The developer has money. Lawyers."

"Probably. But Priya Chandrasekaran has a clipboard and thirty years of careful work, and I have seen what happens when those two things meet money in a planning committee." He leaned against the doorframe, feeling the tiredness arrive now that it was safe to feel it. "Also, she said to tell you the ladder-holding has been noted."

Something moved through Odile's face — amusement, quickly suppressed, and beneath it something warmer. "She met you and didn't run away?"

"She threatened to tell her husband about my sandwiches."

"High praise."

"The highest." He watched her press another seedling into the compost, gentle and exact. "Odile. When they challenge it — and they will — there'll be hearings. Forms. Me testifying about load tolerances in a room full of people who think a ridge beam is something you smoke." He hesitated. "You don't have to do this alone. Whatever comes next."

She looked up at him then, and in the low light of the shed, surrounded by the small green insistence of things growing, she almost smiled.

"Bram," she said, "have you met me?"

He laughed. Outside, the light was failing, and the glasshouse stood dark against the sky, boarded and patient and — for the first time in a hundred and fifty years, officially, on paper — staying.

But when he walked back to the house, he found Teo's car in the drive, and Teo himself on the front step, holding a letter, and the look on his brother's face was not the look of a man bearing news that could wait until morning.
