## 38. What Bram Builds Next

The footbridge was called the Tanners' Bridge, though no one alive could remember a tanner, and it crossed the Mill Brook at the exact point where the brook stopped pretending to be picturesque and started being mud. It was eleven metres of green-painted steel and rotting oak decking, built in 1911 by someone who had cared about it and maintained since by nobody who did.

Bram found it on a Tuesday walk he had started taking because Nell used to take walks and he had discovered that walking without her was a way of keeping her habit alive, like watering a plant left behind. He stood in the middle of it and did what he always did: put his palm flat on the railing and listened with his hand. The railing answered immediately. Loose at the third post. Corrosion blooming under the paint like a bruise under makeup. The decking gave a soft, apologetic flex under his weight — not dangerous yet, but the particular softness of wood that has begun negotiating its retirement.

He got out his notebook before he remembered he wasn't working.

Then he thought: why not?

The council office was a converted chapel with damp in one corner and a woman named Priya who handled everything from potholes to parish fetes and regarded all of it with the same level, unimpressed competence. She looked at Bram's card — *B. Fairweather, Chartered Bridge Inspector* — then at him, then back at the card, as if checking for a discrepancy.

"You want to fix the Tanners' Bridge," she said. "Voluntarily."

"Professionally. But yes."

"Nobody's fallen through it yet, if that's what's motivating you."

"That's actually the opposite of what motivates me."

She almost smiled. "We've got a quote for replacement. Steel and composite. Eighteen months out, assuming the budget survives contact with February." She tapped the file. "You're telling me you can keep the old one standing until then?"

"I'm telling you I can make it better than it's been in forty years. Whether it stands after that is your decision, not mine. I just don't like watching something sound be condemned for being old."

Priya looked at him for a long moment, the way people had started looking at him lately — as if he'd said something slightly more than the words themselves contained. "There's a materials budget," she said finally. "Small. And we'd need your inspection reports filed properly."

"They'll be the most boring documents you've ever read."

"Wonderful," she said, with genuine feeling. "Boring is my favourite genre."

* * *

The work took him through October and into November, and it was the best he'd felt in years, possibly ever, which he found suspicious and then decided not to examine too closely, in the same way you don't look directly at a bird you want to stay on the feeder.

He worked alone mostly, which suited him. Grit-blasting the corrosion back to clean steel. Cutting out the rotted decking board by board, numbering each one in chalk so the replacements would sit true. The villagers began stopping as they passed — an old man who remembered when the bridge was painted navy; two teenagers who tested every new board with their heels like quality-control inspectors; a woman with a terrier who informed him, daily, that the dog approved of progress but disapproved of noise.

"You're the one from the big house," the old man said one morning, watching Bram torque bolts. "Verre House."

"It's Fairweather House, actually."

The old man gave him a look of tremendous patience. "That's what I said."

Bram let it go. He was learning to let things go the way you learn any craft — badly at first, then with practice, then without noticing.

His hands learned the bridge the way his hands knew everything: by load paths. Where the weight went. What carried what. A footbridge was honest work — nothing hidden in it, no expansion joints concealing movement, no bolt heads painted over trouble. Everything it did, it did in the open. He found himself thinking, more than once, that he'd have done well to marry a structure like this instead of — no. That wasn't fair. That wasn't fair to Nell or to himself or to structures, which never pretended to be anything other than what they were, which was rather the point against them.

On Sundays he walked up the lane to the glasshouse.

* * *

The restoration had changed the building's voice. That was the only word Bram could find for it. For years the glasshouse had sounded the way derelict buildings sound — a held breath, a waiting-for-something-to-fall. Now it creaked and ticked and settled like an old house full of people, because it was. Odile had hired half the village, it seemed: the Henderson brothers on the glazing, a joiner named Ruth whose grandfather had allegedly delivered coal to Matteo Marchetti, a retired schoolteacher who came twice a week "to do the labels" and took the labels very seriously indeed.

Bram would arrive mid-morning with coffee in a thermos and be absorbed into whatever needed doing without ceremony. Nobody treated him as the former owner's son, exactly. They treated him as the man who was good with his hands and knew about loads, which was, he was beginning to understand, the only identity he'd ever actually wanted.

"The eastern ridge is holding," Odile reported the first Sunday of November, walking him along the new glazing bars with her hands behind her back like a foreman twice her age. "Ruth says your bracing detail saved us four hundred pounds in timber."

"Tell her it was copied off a Victorian who knew what he was doing."

"He probably learned it from my father."

"Probably," Bram said, and found that the sentence sat comfortably in his mouth now, where eight months ago it would have been a stone.

They paused at the staging bench where the seedlings lived — Matteo's seeds, rescued from tins in the cottage, coaxed into green by Odile and the schoolteacher and, apparently, a degree of stubbornness that was clearly hereditary. Little rows of them under mist, each with a hand-lettered label. *Tomato, King Edward. Bean, Scarlet Runner. Pea, unknown — cottage row three.*

"You could grow the unknown ones out and name them yourself," Bram said.

Odile considered this. "I'd call them after whoever asks properly," she said, and they both laughed, because Edith's tape had become, among the three of them, a kind of private shorthand — the dead woman's voice reduced to a single instruction, which was more than most of the living managed.

She walked him out past the potting shed, where a small brass plate had gone up beside the door. Two names. *M. Marchetti — E. Verre.* Not Fairweather. The village had taken its side of the argument decades ago; the plate simply made it official.

"My solicitor keeps asking me why I didn't fight for the main house," Bram said. "Teo's welcome to it. All those rooms for one man and his regrets."

"And you're happy with a bridge and a bench?"

"A bridge and a bench and a thermos." He shrugged. "Turns out that's the whole inventory. I spent forty years thinking belonging was something you inherited, like a name on a deed. It isn't. It's something people give you, Sunday by Sunday, cup of tea by cup of tea. You can't buy it, can't sue for it, can't put it in a will." He nodded at the plate. "She tried, though. In her way. The codicil was the closest Edith Verre ever came to giving anything freely."

"She'd have hated hearing you say that."

"She'd have hated it enormously," Bram agreed, pleased.

* * *

The lists had changed too.

He still wrote them — some habits were structural, load-bearing, not to be demolished without a full survey — but the notebook's title page now read, in his careful block capitals, **THINGS SAID**, and the entries underneath were different animals entirely.

*Nell — said:* *that I understood. That staying would have meant disappearing, and I'd watched what disappearing does. Said thank you. Said goodbye at the car like it was a door and not a wall.*

*Teo — said:* *Edith Verre. Out loud. First time in thirty years. Watched him shake Odile's hand and mean it.*

*Mother — said:* *the confession about the boy in the garden. Said it before she could absolve me, which is the only way a confession works. Heard her answer anyway, secondhand, through Odile: children do what children do.*

*Odile — said:* *nothing yet, really. Which is fine. Some accounts open slowly.*

Some nights he added entries that surprised him. *Said to the terrier woman that her dog has excellent taste in decking.* *Said to Priya that boring is underrated.* *Said to myself, standing on the Tanners' Bridge at dusk, that this is enough — and, remarkably, believed it.*

The old lists had been inventories of debt. These were more like maintenance logs. Things checked, things tightened, things reported in good order. He wondered if Nell kept lists. He hoped she did. He hoped hers were full of things said too, somewhere far away, in whatever structure she was building for herself. She'd sent one postcard — from somewhere coastal, a lighthouse, no return address, just: *The light here turns every ninety seconds. Thought you'd appreciate the engineering. — N.* He'd laughed for a full minute, alone in the kitchen, and pinned it above his desk, and it was the most honest piece of correspondence either of them had managed in twenty years.

* * *

In December the frost came hard and the village went quiet and the bridge work moved indoors — well, into the shed, where the replacement boards cured under blankets like convalescents. Bram fell into a rhythm that would have horrified his younger self: mornings on paperwork, afternoons in the shed, Sundays at the glasshouse, evenings reading engineering journals like novels and sometimes actual novels, which he'd forgotten you were allowed to do.

It was Teo who broke the rhythm, as Teo reliably did, arriving on a wet Wednesday with his coat steaming and the expression of a man carrying news he hadn't decided how to feel about yet.

"They want to do something for the anniversary," he said, without preamble, dripping on the mat. "The village committee. Priya's lot. A year since Mum died, and apparently there's going to be... something. At the glasshouse. An opening, a dedication, I don't know. There's a leaflet." He produced one, damp and slightly warped. On it, a drawing of the glasshouse in ink, and beneath it: *One Year On: Remembering Edith Verre & Matteo Marchetti — All Welcome.*

"All welcome," Bram repeated.

"Including us, presumably. Including me." Teo sat down heavily at the kitchen table, in the chair that had been his since childhood, in the house that was now honestly his, smaller than he'd expected a life to be and somehow sufficient. "I signed the papers, Bram. I said her name out loud. I thought that was — I thought that was the end of it. Penance completed, account closed, move along."

"And?"

"And now there's going to be a *ceremony*." He said the word like it was a diagnosis. "With speeches. With the whole village looking at us. With Odile making some address about stewardship while I stand there being the son who helped keep everyone out for thirty years." He rubbed his face. "Do you know what the paper called it? 'The Verre Reconciliation.' Reconciliation. As if it's finished. As if it's a thing that finishes."

Bram made tea, because some situations admitted no other response. He set the mug in front of his brother and sat across from him, and for a moment neither spoke, and the silence between them — which for decades had been a wall, then a demolition site, then a cleared field — was now just a silence, ordinary, even comfortable.

"Here's what I know about structures," Bram said eventually. "Nothing is finished. That's not pessimism, that's physics. Everything moves. Everything carries load. The question is never whether a thing is done, it's whether it's maintained." He turned his mug a quarter-turn, a habit Nell had once found maddening. "A year is just an inspection interval, Teo. Somebody looks at everything, writes down what's sound and what needs work, and everybody keeps going."

Teo stared into his tea. "And the speech part."

"You could say something."

"What would I even say? 'Sorry my mother erased your father and sorry I guarded the erasure'?" His laugh came out cracked. "I've been practising in the car. You know what comes out? Nothing. Thirty years of nothing, and when I open my mouth to fix it, there's just — nothing there."

"Then say that," Bram said. "Say the nothing. It's the first honest thing most of us manage."

Teo looked up sharply, ready to be wounded, and found instead that Bram was smiling — not mockingly, but with the specific gentleness of a man who had stood on that same ledge not long ago and been talked down by a stranger with pruning shears in her family tree.

"When is it?" Bram asked.

"End of the month. The anniversary week." Teo hesitated, then pushed the leaflet across the table. "Odile wants us all to write something. For the ledger. She's got Mum's old plant ledgers behind glass in the archive room now, and she wants — she said, 'closing entries.' From all three of us. Debts acknowledged, account closed, her words." He exhaled. "I've been staring at a blank page for a week, Bram. You're the one who's good with lists."

Bram picked up the leaflet. Rain ticked against the kitchen window. Somewhere up the lane, under frost and glass, the seedlings were doing their slow, patient arithmetic, and a brass plate held two names that had waited eighty years to be read together.

"I'll bring my notebook," he said.

* * *

That Sunday, snow came early — fat, unhurried flakes falling straight down through still air, the kind of snow that seems to have nowhere else to be. Bram walked up the lane with his collar up and found the glasshouse glowing against the grey like a lantern someone had forgotten to blow out.

Inside, the light was green-gold and the air smelled of wet soil and cut timber. Odile was on the staging bench, wrapping the tender seedlings in fleece, moving down the rows with the unhurried tenderness of someone closing a shop she intended to reopen forever. The schoolteacher had gone home. The heaters ticked. Outside, the snow began to gather on the new panes, each flake landing with a silence so complete it was almost audible.

"There's a ceremony," Bram said, taking up a roll of fleece and starting on the far end of the bench. "End of the month. Teo's terrified. I think I am too, a bit."

"Good," Odile said. "Terrified means you're planning to say something real." She tucked a corner of fleece around a tomato seedling no taller than his thumb. "Bring your ledger. Bring his. We'll close the account properly — all three columns."

"And after it's closed?"

Odile straightened, and through the fogged glass behind her the snow thickened, filling the world beyond the panes until the glasshouse seemed to float, lit and warm, in a white nothing — the whole structure bearing its weight lightly, exactly as designed, exactly as repaired.

"After it's closed," she said, "we start the spring sowing list."
