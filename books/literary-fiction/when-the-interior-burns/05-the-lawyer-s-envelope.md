## 5. The Lawyer's Envelope

The office of Merle Ostrander, Attorney at Law, occupied the second floor of a converted bank building on Corliss Street, and it smelled the way Bram imagined all lawyers' offices smelled: paper dust, lemon polish, and the faint metallic tang of a radiator that had been fighting October since 1987. The waiting room held four chairs upholstered in a green so dark it was nearly black, and Bram sat in one of them for eleven minutes past the appointed hour, watching Dale across the room. Dale sat like a man in a pew—spine straight, hands folded over his knee, his good suit smelling of the dry cleaner's starch rather than any cologne. He had worn the same suit to the funeral. He would probably wear it to his own.

Bram had dressed carefully that morning, which meant dressing invisibly. Charcoal slacks, a sweater the color of wet slate, no jewelry except their mother's thin gold chain, which they had put on without deciding to and now could not bring themselves to remove. It lay against their collarbone like a small cold hand.

The receptionist—a young woman with a nameplate reading KAYLEE who seemed too cheerful for the building's whole architectural temperament—finally opened the inner door and said, "Mr. Ostrander will see you now," as though announcing them at a dinner party neither of them wanted to attend.

* * *

Ostrander himself was older than Bram remembered, though Bram's only memory of him was from fourteen years ago, when their father's will had been read in this same room. He was a long man, folded into a short chair, with reading glasses pushed up into white hair and a voice like gravel poured slowly. On the desk before him sat two objects: a leather folder, and beside it, slightly apart, as if it had been placed there last and reluctantly, a heavy cream-colored envelope sealed with wax.

Bram stared at the wax. Nobody used wax seals. The seal was a message in itself, a small theatrical gesture, and it was so unlike their mother—the woman who had signed birthday cards *Love, Mom* in handwriting so level it might have been typed—that Bram felt the first true vertigo of the week, worse than the funeral, worse than the casket, because this small object on a lawyer's desk suggested that Vera Hartwell had, at some point, sat down and thought about drama. About effect. About being understood after she was gone.

"Thank you both for coming," Ostrander said, shuffling papers he clearly already knew by heart. "I'll keep this brief. Your mother's estate is not complicated, but it has—one unusual provision." His eyes flicked up, first to Dale, then to Bram, and something in them was careful, the way a man's eyes are careful when he has rehearsed a sentence and hopes not to need its second half.

"The house on Delano Road," he continued, "and the associated savings accounts—modest, I'll say, nothing anyone needs to argue about—pass wholly to Bram."

Bram heard Dale exhale through his nose. Not a sigh exactly. A punctuation mark.

"Dale," Ostrander went on, "you receive the brokerage account your mother maintained jointly with your uncle Raymond until his death, plus her vehicle and the contents of the safe deposit box at First Mercantile. Both bequests are, within a few hundred dollars, equivalent. She was precise about that. She asked me to tell you she was precise about that."

"She'd want us to know she counted," Dale said. His voice was flat, agreeable, the voice of a man confirming a receipt. "That sounds like her."

Bram said nothing. Their mother had counted everything her whole life—calories, steps, the minutes Bram spent on the phone, the exact distance between herself and any subject that mattered—and the idea that she had carried that arithmetic into death, balancing her children against each other on a ledger, was somehow more intimate than anything she had ever said to them alive.

"There is one further item." Ostrander's hand moved toward the envelope and stopped just short of it, hovering, as if the wax were warm. "Your mother left instructions regarding this some years ago. It is addressed—" he turned it so they could read the block capitals, unmistakably hers, level as type—"'to be opened by whoever asks the right question.'"

The radiator ticked. Somewhere below them, in the old bank's bones, a door closed.

"That's it?" Dale said. "That's the provision? An envelope with a riddle on it?"

"It's the provision," Ostrander said. "She was specific. I am not to open it, not to disclose its contents, not to suggest what the question might be. If no one asks it within one year of her death, my instructions are to destroy it unopened." He laid it back down. "I told her at the time that this was unconventional. She told me that most of her life had been unconventional and that I simply hadn't been invited to see it."

Bram's skin prickled. *Most of her life had been unconventional.* Their mother, who had eaten dinner at six-fifteen every night of Bram's childhood. Who had owned four identical cardigans. Who had answered every question about her past—young people don't remember, we moved around, your father knew all that—with the same smooth deflection Bram themselves had inherited and deployed at the funeral like a family heirloom.

"What kind of question?" Bram asked. Their own voice surprised them—hoarse, too eager. They heard the hunger in it and hated the hunger and could not stop it.

"I couldn't say," Ostrander said, and for a moment his professional composure thinned and Bram saw underneath it an old man who had liked their mother, or been fascinated by her, or possibly feared her a little. "I genuinely couldn't. She didn't tell me. She said if I knew, I might hint, and she didn't want hints. She wanted—" he searched for the word— "*earnestness*. That's the word she used. She said whoever asked would have to mean it."

Dale laughed. It was a short, hard sound, the laugh he used at family dinners when someone suggested something foolish, like hosting Thanksgiving somewhere other than his house.

"This is a game," he said. "This is Mom playing games from beyond the grave. You want to know what that is?" He pointed at the envelope without touching it, the way you point at a spider. "That's guilt. That's a woman who couldn't say things out loud her whole life, so she makes a puzzle out of it and lets a lawyer hold it. It's cruel, is what it is. She strung us along alive and now she's going to do it dead."

"Dale," Bram started.

"No." He turned, and Bram saw that under the bluster his face was genuinely upset—not grieving, exactly, but *disturbed*, the way a man is disturbed when furniture has been moved in a familiar room. "You know what I think? I think we should ask Mr. Ostrander to shred it today. Right now. Both of us, together, we say burn it, and it's done, and we settle the estate like normal people instead of playing scavenger hunt with her leftovers."

"You can't do that," Ostrander said mildly. "Either of you alone could demand it be preserved by asking a question. Neither of you alone can compel me to destroy it while the other objects. Your mother anticipated that, too." He almost smiled. "As I said. Precise."

The silence that followed had texture. Bram sat inside it and felt the shape of the thing their mother had built: a lock with no key offered, only the promise that a key existed, somewhere, for someone willing to look. And Dale already turning away from it, already reaching for the ordinary, the settled, the saleable.

"Well, I'm not asking any questions," Dale announced, standing, gathering his coat over his arm with brisk finality. "I've got nothing to ask. She gave me what she gave me and I'm grateful, and I'd thank you"—this to Bram, and his eyes when they met Bram's were not angry but pleading, which was worse—"*I would thank you* to let it go. Sign the listing papers. Sell the house. Let her rest. Some things a person takes to the grave on purpose, Bram, and it's not our business to dig them back up."

"Some things," Bram repeated quietly.

"She was private. That's all this is. Privacy." Dale shrugged his coat on. "Not everything hidden is a secret, you know. Sometimes it's just nobody else's damn business."

He shook Ostrander's hand, thanked him in the tone of a man concluding a church service, and stood in the doorway waiting for Bram with the terrible patience of the righteous.

Bram looked at the envelope.

It was nothing, objectively. Cream paper, wax the color of dried blood, their mother's capital letters. It weighed perhaps an ounce. But looking at it, Bram felt the same pull they'd felt all week in the house on Delano Road—the missing photographs, the absent letters, the decades sanded smooth—and understood, with a clarity that arrived like cold water, that their mother had known exactly what she was doing. She had spent a lifetime closing doors. And then, at the end, she had left one door ajar and written a sign on it, and the sign did not say *enter*. It said *ask*.

"Bram." Dale, still in the doorway. "Come on. Lunch. There's a place on Fifth, we'll talk about the realtor, get this wrapped up by Friday like we said."

Bram rose. Crossed the room. And as they passed the desk, as Ostrander watched with his careful eyes and made no move either way—as if the whole arrangement had been designed so that no one would ever make a move, only permit one—Bram picked up the envelope and slid it inside their coat, against their ribs, unopened.

They hadn't decided to. Their hands had decided, the way hands decide things, and only afterward did Bram understand the decision: not to open it, not yet. Opening it here, under the fluorescent hum, with Dale's disapproval already loaded and aimed, would make it Dale's story—*Bram couldn't leave well enough alone, Bram blew up the family over an envelope*—before it ever became their mother's. Whatever was in there deserved better than an audience. It deserved the house, maybe. The quiet rooms. The catalog half-finished on the dining table.

Or—and this thought arrived later, on the stairs, with Dale's car pulling away from the curb below—it deserved to stay shut until Bram was certain they wanted to know. Because a question, once asked, could not be unasked, and their mother had built her whole life on the principle that some knowledge changes the knower permanently. Bram had spent thirty-nine years being the person shaped by that principle. They were not sure they were done being that person. But they were no longer sure they wanted to survive as them, either.

Outside, October light lay flat on Corliss Street. Kaylee buzzed the door locked behind them. In their coat, against their heart, the envelope rode like a second pulse.

Bram got in the car, set the envelope on the passenger seat, and buckled it in—then laughed once, a strange cracked sound at their own superstition, and drove home the long way, past the school where their mother had volunteered every Thursday, past the diner where she'd taken them for pancakes after their father's funeral and said, unprompted, *your dad never liked surprises, and neither do I, remember that about us.*

*Remember that about us.*

Bram remembered. And driving, watching the envelope sit patient and sealed in the seat beside them, they began—for the first time—to wonder what kind of woman spends forty years telling her children not to expect surprises, and then, from beyond the grave, mails them one.
