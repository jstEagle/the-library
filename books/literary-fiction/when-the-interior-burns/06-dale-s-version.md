## 6. Dale's Version

Dale Hartwell believed in the power of a good steak the way other men believed in prayer. You put the meat on the grill, you tended it, you turned it at the right moments, and by the time it came off the heat everything that needed saying had been said or had been made unnecessary. He had learned this from their father, who had never once in forty years of Sundays said anything difficult over anything less than a ribeye.

So when Bram called to say they needed to talk about the house, Dale did what he always did. He invited them to dinner.

* * *

The restaurant was the same one he'd taken Karen to on their first date, the same one where he'd told his son about college, the same one where he'd raised a glass after the funeral and said, "She lived a good life," and watched the whole table nod like churchgoers. The Copper Kettle. Wood paneling, brass fixtures, booths worn smooth by decades of elbows. A place where nothing changed, which was Dale's highest compliment for any institution.

He arrived early because he always arrived early. He ordered water for Bram and a beer for himself and sat with his back to the wall so he could see the door, another habit from his father, though his father had claimed it was about exits and Dale suspected it was really about arrivals—who was coming, who might be watching, whether the room contained anyone he needed to be better than.

Bram came in at six sharp, shaking rain off their coat, and Dale felt the familiar small irritation he'd never learned to name. His sibling looked tired. Not grieving-tired, which would have been appropriate, but something else—wired, preoccupied, like a person carrying a secret in each pocket and afraid of jingling.

"You look rough," Dale said, which in his language was concern.

"Long week." Bram slid into the booth across from him. "Thanks for doing this."

"Well." Dale spread his napkin across his lap with a single decisive motion. "Family."

He let the word sit there. It was one of his favorite words, family, because it required nothing of him except repetition. Family meant Sunday dinners and birthday cards signed by all three of them—him, Karen, Tyler—and it meant the house on Corliss Street and the way things had always been done. Family was a load-bearing word. You didn't examine it. You leaned on it.

The waitress came and he ordered for both of them without asking—the ribeye for himself, the salmon for Bram, medium-rare, because he remembered, because remembering details like that was how Dale demonstrated love: through logistics.

"So," he said, when she'd gone. "The lawyer called me yesterday."

Bram's glass paused halfway to their mouth. "About what?"

"The paperwork. For the sale." Dale kept his voice easy, conversational, the tone of a man discussing gutters. "There's an appraisal contingency, some title stuff, and apparently your signature's holding up the whole chain. The buyers want to close by end of the month."

"I know. I've got the packet."

"And?"

"And I'm reading it."

Dale laughed—a short, warm bark designed to include Bram in the joke. "Reading it. It's a standard sale agreement, Bram. It's not *War and Peace*. You sign, we split the proceeds per the will, everybody moves on." He took a pull of his beer. "Mom would've wanted it handled fast. She hated loose ends. Remember how she used to go through the mail at the kitchen table every single night? Never let a bill sit overnight. Never."

"Sure," Bram said.

It was the *sure* that bothered Dale. Not disagreement—disagreement he knew how to fight. It was this new quality in Bram's voice, a kind of careful flatness, like someone walking across ice and testing each step before committing weight. Dale had spent his whole life around people who were either with him or against him, and Bram had always been with him, quietly, reliably, the way weather is reliable. This wasn't that.

"Look," he said, leaning forward, forearms on the table, deploying the posture he used at work when he needed a vendor to understand that the conversation was friendly but finite. "I know this has been hard. Losing her. And I know you two didn't—" he waved a hand, searching for the gentlest available phrase, "—didn't talk as much as some mothers and kids do. But that doesn't mean you drag it out. That's not grief, Bram. Grief is clean. Grief is crying at the funeral and going home and getting up Monday. What you're doing, sitting alone in that big house making lists of her spoons—that's not grief. That's stalling."

"I'm cataloging the estate. It's what executors do."

"It's what people do when they don't want to let go." Dale softened his voice deliberately. He was good at this part, the pivot from pressure to tenderness, the one-two punch their father had thrown his whole life. "I get it. I do. That house is full of... stuff. Memories. But memories aren't in the house, kiddo. They're in here." He tapped his chest. "You could burn the place down tomorrow and Mom would still be right here."

It was a good line. He'd been polishing it since Tuesday.

The food came and he attacked his steak with the satisfaction of a man whose world was correctly arranged. Medium-rare, char on the outside, exactly as ordered. Some things, thank God, still obeyed.

"Can I ask you something?" Bram said, cutting their salmon into very small pieces.

"Anything."

"What do you remember about Mom before we were born?"

Dale chewed. Swallowed. Took his time, because the question deserved consideration, and also because he liked the sound of his own memory laid out properly, like silverware.

"Not much to tell," he said finally. "She met Dad in—what, '68? '69?—right after he got back. She was working at the insurance office downtown, filing, typing. Dad came in to add flood coverage to the shop and couldn't take his eyes off her. Told that story a hundred times." He smiled at the ritual of it. "They married in '71. I came along in '73, you in '76. She stayed home till you started school, then went back to part-time bookkeeping for Mr. Aldous. Kept the books for what, twenty years? Retired when he sold the business. Gardened. Cooked. Volunteered at the library on Thursdays." He shrugged. "Stable. Ordinary. Good. Most people don't get a life that holds together like that, and she did. We should be grateful, not suspicious."

"Suspicious," Bram repeated. "Did I say I was suspicious?"

"You didn't have to." Dale set down his fork. Here it was, then, whatever this was, surfacing at last. "You've been asking questions. At the lawyer's. At the bank—I heard you called the bank, Bram, about old accounts. Karen's cousin works there. Now you're asking about before we were born." He shook his head slowly, sorrowfully, a man disappointed rather than angry, which was far more effective. "Our mother just died. Our mother, who fed this town's worth of casseroles and never asked anybody for anything, and within two weeks you're digging through her past like she owed somebody money."

"She hummed songs," Bram said quietly. "Songs I've never heard anywhere else. And last week one of them came on the radio."

Dale stared at them.

For a moment—just a moment—something moved behind his face. Something quick and cold, a fish turning under ice. Then it was gone, and what remained was the expression Dale had worn his entire adult life, the one Karen called his boardroom face and Tyler called Dad's *no* face: pleasant, immovable, faintly amused at the idea that reality might be negotiable.

"Bram." He said the name gently, the way you'd speak to someone standing too close to a ledge. "Mom hummed all kinds of things. Hymns. Show tunes. Whatever was on the radio while she washed dishes. You hear three notes of something on some late-night station and suddenly our childhood is a mystery novel?" He laughed again, but shorter this time. "That station plays covers, deep cuts, nobody-knows-who junk. Some session singer probably did a version of something Mom picked up somewhere. That's all it is. That's all it can be."

"You didn't even ask which song."

"Because it doesn't matter." And then, catching himself, softening: "Because it *can't* matter. Look—I loved her too. I know it doesn't always look like it, the way I keep things moving, but I loved her. And the best thing we can do for her now is finish what she started. She had her affairs in order, Bram. Perfectly in order. Will, accounts, instructions for the house. You know why? Because she wanted no mess. No drama. She wanted us to close the door gently and get on with living. Signing those papers isn't betraying her. It's the last thing she asked of us."

The waitress refilled their waters. Dale waited until she'd gone.

"Sign the papers," he said. "Friday, if you can. Let's be done by Thanksgiving. You'll feel better. I promise you'll feel better once it's behind you."

Bram looked at him for a long moment, and Dale had the strange, dislocating sensation of being studied—not argued with, *studied*—like a specimen under glass. Their eyes were their mother's eyes. He'd never noticed that before, or had noticed and filed it away unexamined, and now it sat in front of him across a basket of bread, patient and unreadable.

"Dale," Bram said. "When I was little, Mom disappeared every spring. Two, three days, sometimes a week. Dad covered—said she was visiting her sister. But she didn't have a sister. Did you ever wonder about that?"

"No," Dale said.

It came out instantly, automatically, and he heard it for what it was even as he said it—not an answer but a wall. He reached for the beer and found it empty and signaled the waitress anyway, needing something in his hand.

"Never once?" Bram pressed, softly.

"Kids don't wonder about their parents' errands." He was aware his voice had climbed half a register. He brought it back down. "And adults don't dig up thirty-year-old errands to avoid signing a real estate form. Whatever you think you're doing—whatever grief is making you do—it ends badly. Questions like these don't have answers, Bram. They just have damage. Trust me. Some doors are closed because there's nothing behind them."

"Or because somebody locked them."

"*Bram.*"

The name cracked out louder than he intended. A couple at the next booth glanced over. Dale lowered his voice, smoothed his tie, reassembled himself piece by piece the way he'd done in a hundred meetings, a dozen funerals, one marriage counseling session he never mentioned.

"I'm going to say this once," he said, "and then we're going to eat our dinner like civilized people. Whatever you found in that house—and I'm sure you found *something*, houses are full of somethings—you bring it to me before you bring it to anyone else. Not the lawyer. Not strangers. Me. Because I'm the one who knows the story of this family, start to finish, and the story is simple. Dad worked. Mom kept the home. We grew up. She died. That's it. That's the whole thing. There's no hidden chapter, and if you go looking for one, you won't find what you imagine. You'll find nothing, and you'll have burned down the only version of her that was ever real."

He stopped, breathing a little hard, surprised at his own heat. Across the table Bram hadn't flinched, hadn't blinked, just watched him with those eyes, and Dale understood suddenly, with a sensation like the floor tilting, that his certainty—which had carried him through every negotiation, every crisis, every eulogy—was not landing the way it always landed. Bram wasn't convinced. Bram wasn't even arguing. Bram was *taking notes*.

"Okay," Bram said at last, mildly. "Eat your dinner."

They ate. Dale talked about Tyler's promotion and Karen's garden and the new deck he was planning, filling the silence the way he filled all silences, with the well-maintained furniture of his ordinary life. Bram nodded in the right places. Paid half the check before he could stop them.

In the parking lot, under the buzzing sodium lights, they stood by their cars in the rain-smelling dark.

"Friday," Dale said. "Right? You'll call Whitcomb and tell him Friday."

"I'll call him," Bram said.

Which was not yes. Dale knew the difference; he'd spent thirty years hearing it from clients and vendors and, lately, from his own son. But he let it go, because the alternative was pressing, and pressing Bram tonight felt like pushing on a door he suddenly, irrationally, did not want to open.

He watched their taillights merge onto the boulevard and stood a moment longer than he needed to, keys in hand, rain starting again, fine and cold.

Then he got in his car, and before he pulled out, before he allowed himself to drive anywhere, he did the thing he had done only twice in his adult life, both times alone, both times quickly: he opened the glove box, moved the registration and the tire gauge aside, and checked that the envelope was still there—the yellowed, brittle envelope he'd taken from his father's desk drawer the week after the funeral fifteen years ago, sealed with tape gone amber, with four words typed across the front in his mother's old typewriter ribbon:

*FOR DALE. WHEN HE ASKS.*

He had never asked. He had decided, long ago, that he never would.

His phone buzzed in the cupholder. A text from Bram, three words, and Dale read them twice before his hands began, very slightly, to shake:

*I found Junie.*
