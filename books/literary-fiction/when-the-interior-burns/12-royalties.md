## 12. Royalties

The laundromat below Junie's room stopped its dryers at eleven, and the silence that followed was the kind she had learned to work inside—thick, humming at the edges, a held breath of a building. She sat cross-legged on the bed with the banker's box from her mother's things open in front of her, and she told herself she was only organizing. That was the lie she'd been telling for three days now, ever since Bram Hartwell's voicemail had cracked something open in her chest that would not close.

*Organizing.* As if a person could file away a name like V. Marsh. As if you could alphabetize a mother who had been standing in your kitchen every spring of your childhood, teaching you harmonies, and then drive back to a life so secret it didn't have her real name on it.

The box smelled of cedar and old paper. Vera Marsh's papers—or rather, the papers Vera Marsh had been allowed to keep, which turned out to be almost nothing. A songbook with chords penciled in a hand Junie recognized from birthday cards. A photograph she hadn't looked at again since the night she found it, because looking at it felt like pressing on a bruise to check if it still hurt. It did. Her mother laughing among strangers, head thrown back, holding a guitar like an extension of her own arm, and Junie had no memory of that woman at all. The woman Junie knew wore cardigans and apologized when she laughed too loud.

Junie set the photograph face-down and pulled out the folder beneath it.

It was thin. That was the first thing she noticed—the way a wound is thin before you understand how deep it goes. Four sheets of paper, carbon copies, the kind that came off a machine with a roller and a smell of solvent. Work-for-hire agreements. She had seen the phrase before, in the fine print of session contracts she'd signed herself over fifteen years of singing other people's choruses in Nashville rooms that smelled exactly like this box: dust and ambition and somebody else's money.

*The undersigned hereby assigns all right, title, and interest...*

Her mother's signature sat at the bottom of each one. *Vera M. Hartwell.* Not Marsh. Hartwell—the married name, the family name, the name that meant the woman who made tuna casserole and went quiet at dinner parties. Junie ran her finger along the ink and felt the strangeness of it, that her mother had signed away songs under a name that hid her, as though even the theft required a disguise.

Four contracts. Four dates between 1978 and 1980. And on each one, in the party-of-the-first-part line, a company name she didn't recognize: **Bluegate Music, Inc.**

She said it out loud to the empty room, testing it. "Bluegate."

Nothing about it rang true. Nothing about it rang at all. It sounded like a name someone invented in ten minutes because a form required one—a gate that wasn't blue, music that wasn't there.

She should have called Bram. She had Bram's number now, written on the pad by the phone downstairs, and she'd composed four voicemails in her head and sent none of them. But this—whatever this was—she needed to hold alone first. She needed to know what she was carrying before she handed any of it to a stranger who happened to share her blood.

She got up, pulled the desk lamp closer, and began, methodically, to do what she did best: read the room. Fifteen years of session work had taught her that the truth in the music business never lived on the page where they wanted you to look. It lived in the margins. In the addresses. In the notary stamps and the registered agent lines, the small print where people thought nobody ever looked because nobody ever did.

Except singers. Singers learned to read everything, because everything had been read *about* them first.

* * *

Bluegate Music, Incorporated. Registered in Delaware—that much she could find at the public library the next morning, hunched over a terminal while a librarian watched her with the wary patience of someone used to people crying at computers. Delaware registration told her nothing except that whoever built Bluegate had wanted it invisible, and Delaware was where you went when invisibility was the point.

But the contracts themselves gave her more. Each one listed a resident agent—an address in Nashville, on Woodmont Boulevard. And beneath the corporate signature block, where an officer's name belonged, the same name appeared on all four documents, typed and then signed in a slanted, confident hand:

**Gerald Hartwell.**

Hartwell.

She sat very still in the library's fluorescent hum and let the word do what words do when they finally arrive after years of circling. Hartwell. The name on the birth certificate folded inside her wallet for thirty-one years, the one she'd shown no one. *Father's name: unknown.* Mother: Vera. And here was a Hartwell—not Bram's father, she'd already gathered that much from their halting phone call, but some uncle, some brother-in-law—standing as the officer of the company that owned her mother's songs.

Owned. Past tense, she reminded herself. Companies dissolved. The seventies were fifty years ago. Maybe Bluegate was dust, a dead shell, nothing left to be furious about.

She wrote down the address anyway. Old habit. You always wrote down the address.

* * *

The building on Woodmont was glass and beige brick, the kind of office park that housed dentists and insurance adjusters and, on the second floor behind frosted glass, the successor firm to a publishing administration company called Meridian Songs—which, according to the young man at the front desk, had "acquired or administered" several hundred catalogs going back decades. His name tag said TYLER. He had the eager, frightened helpfulness of someone on his third week.

"I'm trying to trace an old catalog," Junie said. "Bluegate Music. It would've been active late seventies, early eighties."

Tyler clicked through screens. Junie watched his face the way she watched producers' faces in the booth—for the flicker, the half-second where interest becomes caution. She saw it. Right on schedule.

"Bluegate," he repeated. "That's... huh. That one's flagged."

"Flagged."

"Like—it's not active anymore, but it's not closed either. It's one of those ones where the royalties are still moving but nobody's supposed to ask questions." He said it lightly, the way people say damning things when they don't understand the weight of them. "There's a distribution entity attached. Payments route through it quarterly. I probably shouldn't—" He caught himself, glanced at the frosted glass. "Who's the writer? I can look up a specific song maybe."

"Vera Marsh," Junie said. Then, because lying had never once come naturally to her: "And Vera Hartwell. Same person."

His fingers moved. The screen filled with titles, and Junie leaned in and read her childhood.

*"Winter Kitchen." "The Long Way Around." "Salt." "Come Down, Morning."*

"Come Down, Morning," she whispered. Her mother used to sing it in the car, windows down, on the drives to nowhere she took when the springs got bad. Junie had thought it was hers—her mother's own, private, a song that existed only in the space between a woman and her daughter. It had never occurred to her that it existed anywhere else. That it had been *taken* somewhere else.

"There's recordings?" she asked. Her voice came out strange.

"Covers, mostly. 'Come Down, Morning' charted twice—once in '81, once in '94. Country act out of Knoxville, then a re-cut by..." He squinted. "Delia Rae Fontaine. That one did pretty well. You'd know it."

She did know it. She'd sung harmony on a Delia Rae Fontaine tribute night at the Bluebird two years ago. She had stood on a stage and performed her mother's stolen song for a room full of people who applauded, and somewhere in the dark, money had moved into an account with a Hartwell's name on it.

"How much?" The question escaped before she could dress it up. "Per quarter. Roughly."

Tyler hesitated. He was twenty-three and terrified and decent, and Junie watched him decide, in real time, which master he served. "I really can't—" He lowered his voice. "But it's public record once it hits the PRO distributions, if you know the account holder. I mean. Anyone can look up who a publisher pays. That part's just... there."

He printed her a sheet with the catalog number and the administering entity—**Meridian Songs LLC, a division of Halloway & Pratt Administration**—and a phone number, and Junie took it like a summons.

* * *

She spent the evening doing what Tyler had accidentally taught her to do. The performing rights organization's public database was clunky and honest, the last honest thing in the chain. She searched Bluegate Music. She searched Meridian Songs. And there it was, laid out in the flat gray language of royalty statements: quarterly distributions, dating back past the limits of the online records, paid to a single payee.

**Hartwell Family Trust. Trustee: G. Hartwell (1979–2001). Successor trustee: D. Hartwell (2001–present).**

D. Hartwell.

Dale.

Junie put the laptop down on the bed and stood up, because sitting suddenly felt impossible, because her body had decided that this information could not be received horizontally, could not be received calmly, could not be received as anything but what it was.

Her mother's songs had earned money every three months for forty-four years. Every quarter, like a heartbeat. "Come Down, Morning" alone had been cut by at least two acts, probably more buried deeper in the files. Session fees, mechanicals, sync licenses—somebody's commercial, somebody's movie scene, somebody's wedding band playing a cover—and every dollar, every quarter, flowing without interruption into a trust run first by the brother-in-law who'd co-signed the work-for-hire contracts and then by his nephew. Dale. The brother who, according to Bram, spoke of their family history the way tour guides speak of monuments: stable, complete, nothing hidden, nothing owed.

*Nothing owed.* Except a lifetime of quarters. Except a woman who died in April with four carbon copies and a locked cedar chest and a daughter she visited in secret every spring like a criminal returning to the scene of the only honest thing she'd ever done.

Junie walked to the window. Below, the laundromat's sign buzzed its dying neon hum, washing the parking lot in intermittent pink. A man loaded his dryer. A woman folded towels with the automatic grace of repetition. Ordinary life, continuing, indifferent—and underneath all of it, everywhere, Junie understood now, money moving in channels dug long ago by people who never intended anyone to look.

Her mother had known. That was the thing that arrived last and cut deepest. Vera had kept the contracts. You don't keep proof of a fair deal in a cedar chest under a lock for forty years. She had kept them the way you keep evidence, or the way you keep a confession—unable to use it, unable to destroy it. She had signed her songs away under a false name and gone home and raised a son and secretly raised a daughter and hummed the stolen melodies while she cooked, stopping whenever anyone entered, and she had died with all of it unsaid.

Not unsaid, Junie corrected herself. Bram had said there was a letter. An envelope at the lawyer's office, sealed, waiting for whoever asked the right question. Their mother had written it down in the end. She just hadn't been brave enough to say it while her mouth still worked.

Junie pressed her forehead against the cool glass and breathed until the shaking stopped. Fury was useful; she knew that much about herself. Fury was fuel. But fury undirected burned the house you were standing in, and she had spent her whole life with barely a roof, and she was done burning her own shelter down.

So she did what she always did with a fire that wouldn't go out. She gave it a shape. She sat back down, opened the notebook she used for setlists, and on a fresh page she drew a line. At the top she wrote *Vera Marsh — works*, and beneath it she began listing every title Tyler's screen had shown her, and beside each title, the covers, the years, the acts. It took hours. When she finished, the page looked like what it was: a ledger. Forty-four years of quarters, itemized. A debt with a name on every line.

At the bottom, she wrote the total—not in dollars, because she couldn't know the dollars yet, but in the only currency that mattered:

*Owed: everything. To: me.*

Then she picked up her phone, found the number she'd been not-calling for three days, and dialed Bram Hartwell before she could talk herself out of it.

It rang twice. "Hello?" Bram's voice, careful, awake at midnight, as if they'd been waiting.

"It's Junie." She looked down at the ledger, at her mother's titles marching down the page like soldiers finally counted. "I found where the money goes. And Bram—you need to sit down for this, because I don't think your brother knows what his name is on."
