## 5. The Director's Corridor

Tobias Kessler had given four hundred and eleven speeches in his life, by his own rough count, and he had learned that the secret to all of them was the same: never let them see you count.

He stood at the podium in the boardroom on the third floor of the Aldergate Conservatory, morning light coming through the tall windows in long dusty columns, and looked out at the faces of the faculty assembled before him. Some of them were genuinely fond of him. Some of them tolerated him the way one tolerates weather. A few — the theory department, always the theory department — regarded him with the flat patience of people who had already composed their objections and were merely waiting for him to finish so they could file them.

"Friends," he began, because he always began that way, and because it was true more often than not. "Colleagues. Keepers of the flame, and I mean that literally, because half of you have complained about the boiler this month."

A ripple of laughter. Good. He needed it warm in here for what came next.

"I'll come to the point, because I know you all have rehearsals, and because Elena has been making a face at me since I started talking." Elena Vasquez, head of strings, did not deny it. "The Board has voted. As of next spring, the old rehearsal hall — Hall C, the one we all pretend is called something grander than it is — will be demolished."

The room went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when everyone knew it was coming but hoped someone else would say it first.

"The new wing needs the footprint," Tobias continued. "We've been promised acoustics that will make this building sing like it hasn't since 1962. There will be practice rooms with actual soundproofing. There will be a recording studio that doesn't double as a storage closet for folding chairs." He paused, let the smile settle. "And before any of you ask — yes, there will be a farewell gala. One last concert in that hall. We're going to give it the send-off it deserves."

He said *it deserves* and heard, underneath his own voice, the other sentence. The one he never said. *It deserves to come down.* He had been hearing that sentence for twenty years, in the voice of no one in particular, sometimes in his father's voice, which was worse.

He finished the announcement, took questions with the ease of long practice — yes, the gala would be open to alumni; yes, there would be an archival display; no, Professor Okafor, the organ was not salvageable, please stop asking about the organ — and then he stood at the window afterward while the room emptied, watching the faculty scatter across the courtyard below like notes released from a chord.

Four hundred and eleven speeches. He'd counted the hall among his accomplishments for most of them. *Home to two generations of musicians,* the brochures said. *Where careers begin.*

One career had ended there too. His brochures didn't mention that.

* * *

His office still smelled faintly of his predecessor, which was to say of pipe tobacco and ambition, though both men had been gone long enough that the smell was probably memory rather than chemistry. Tobias hung his jacket on the back of the door and sat down at the desk and did what he did every day at eleven-fifteen, which was nothing, briefly, deliberately. Two minutes of nothing. His assistant thought he was reviewing correspondence. His therapist — three sessions, then he'd stopped, telling himself he didn't have time, which was the most expensive lie he owned — would have called it avoidance dressed as meditation.

On the wall across from him hung a photograph from the gala of his tenth year as director. He was mid-laugh in it, hand on the shoulder of a scholarship student, and he looked, in the photograph, like a man who had never once rehearsed a laugh. He kept it there because it was useful. People believed photographs.

There was a knock. His assistant, Priya, leaned in with a tablet and the day's small catastrophes: the roof over Practice Room 7 was leaking again; the caterer for the gala wanted a decision on the canapés by Friday; a donor named Mrs. Abernathy had left three voicemails and was, per Priya, "in one of her moods."

"Tell Mrs. Abernathy I'll call her myself," Tobias said. "Tell her the truth — that I'm terrified of her and honored she keeps calling."

Priya smiled the way staff smile at directors who are charming before ten a.m., which is to say politely and without belief, and left.

He should have felt triumphant this morning. That was the strange part. The demolition had been his proposal, three years running, argued through committees and preservationists and one truly memorable letter-writing campaign from the class of 1989. He had won. The hall was coming down. And instead of triumph he felt the specific exhaustion of a man who has finally reached the end of a very long corridor only to find another corridor.

Because here was the thing he would not name, not to Priya, not to the board, not to himself at eleven-fifteen: he had proposed the demolition for the right reasons, and he had also proposed it for the wrong one, and the wrong one lived in the building the way damp lives in stone. You couldn't see it. You could smell it.

Hall C. Where everything went wrong. Where a boy named Felix Kavanagh had stood on a stage with a broken E-string and a face like a closed door, and where Tobias — seventeen years old, brilliant, terrified, his father's son — had watched the machinery start up and had not stopped it.

Twenty years. He could say the number easily now. It was the details that had teeth.

* * *

At noon he did his walk.

Every director develops rituals, and Tobias's was the corridor tour — forty minutes, twice a week, no agenda, popping into rehearsal rooms unannounced the way a good doctor does rounds. The students had learned to expect him. The younger ones straightened when he appeared; the older ones barely looked up, which he considered the higher compliment. He gave out encouragement the way other men gave out business cards — freely, warmly, and with a certain professional blankness behind the eyes.

"You're rushing," he told a quartet in Room 12, tapping the doorframe in time. "You're all rushing together, which is at least democratic, but the composer is spinning."

"It's marked *con moto*," said the second violinist, a girl with defiant eyebrows.

"*Con moto*, not *con panic*. Again, from thirty-one."

He moved on. He paused outside the percussion studio to listen to someone practicing a marimba transcription of Bach with more enthusiasm than accuracy. He signed a form for a student whose scholarship paperwork had stalled. He complimented a bassoonist on her tone and meant it. This was the part of the job he loved without reservation, the part that had made him take it in the first place: the building full of young people in the act of becoming, each of them convinced their struggles were unprecedented, each of them correct.

He was passing the service corridor behind Hall C — the narrow one, lined with mop sinks and notice boards and the faint institutional smell of floor wax — when he saw the janitor.

Tobias knew most of the custodial staff by name. He made a point of it; his father had never learned a single servant's name in his life, and Tobias had decided somewhere in his thirties that he would be the opposite of his father even in the smallest particulars, especially in those. But this one he didn't know. Tall, gray at the temples, pushing a cart with the unhurried economy of a man who had done these corridors ten thousand times. Head slightly bowed. Hands resting on the cart handle in a way that —

Tobias stopped walking.

It was nothing. It was the way the man held his hands, thumbs curved inward, fingers loose and arched, the posture of someone who had spent a lifetime around instruments. Musicians' hands did that even after they stopped playing; Tobias had seen it in retired surgeons, in old teachers, in his own reflection sometimes when he wasn't paying attention. Plenty of failed conservatory students ended up mopping floors. It was practically a genre.

But the man had turned his head at the sound of footsteps, and for half a second — less than half a second — Tobias's whole body went cold, the way it went cold at certain chords, certain dates on the calendar, the way it had gone cold this morning saying *it deserves*.

"Good afternoon," the janitor said. Neutral. Courteous. Eyes that gave away nothing, or that Tobias could not read, which amounted to the same thing.

"Afternoon," Tobias managed. "I don't think we've met. Kessler. I run the place, allegedly."

"Felix." A pause. "Night shift, mostly."

"Felix. Well — thank you for what you do. The building runs on people nobody sees."

Something moved behind the man's eyes then, quick and unreadable, like a fish under ice. "That's kind," he said. "Most people don't notice the floors until they're dirty."

Tobias laughed, because the line deserved a laugh, and walked on, and did not look back, and thought about looking back for the rest of the afternoon.

In his office again, he sat down and pulled the staff roster up on his computer and scrolled the custodial list with his heart doing something arrhythmic and embarrassing. There it was: F. Kavanagh, night custodian, hired fourteen months ago.

Kavanagh.

It was a common enough name. It was a common enough name. There were thousands of Kavanaghs. And Felix — well. Felix Kavanagh had vanished off the face of the earth twenty years ago, and if he had resurfaced anywhere, it would not be here, pushing a mop through the corridors he had once owned, in the building that had eaten him alive. No one would do that. No sane person would choose to haunt the scene.

Unless, said a voice in Tobias's head that sounded unpleasantly like his father, he isn't haunting. Unless he's watching.

Tobias closed the laptop. He sat in the quiet of his office with the photograph of his rehearsed laugh on the wall and the afternoon light going amber, and he tried to reconstruct the half-second in the corridor. Had there been recognition in the man's face? Or had there been nothing — polite, professional nothing? He honestly could not tell, and the inability frightened him more than either answer, because it meant he no longer trusted his own memory of Felix Kavanagh's face. Twenty years will do that. Twenty years will sand a person down to a silhouette.

What he remembered instead, treacherously, was the music. The duo they'd played at twelve — Marta Ilves's arrangement, the two violins chasing each other through the middle movement like brothers arguing in a language only they spoke. He remembered Felix's intonation, perfect even then, perfect in a way that made Tobias practice four hours a day out of love and fury in equal measure. He remembered standing backstage at the audition, twenty years ago, hearing the string go, hearing the silence after it, hearing his father's voice in the wings saying *well, these things happen* in a tone that meant *I made sure they happened.*

He had never said it aloud. Not once. Not to his wife, who had left anyway; not to his therapist, whom he'd fired before the third session could get dangerous; not to God, whom he addressed mostly through complaints about the boiler.

He had drafted a resignation letter once, seven years ago, at two in the morning, after the conservatory named a scholarship after his father. He'd written four paragraphs and deleted them before dawn, and the deletion had felt like a kind of death, and the next morning he'd given speech number two hundred and ninety-something and everyone said he was wonderful.

* * *

That evening he stayed late. He told himself it was the gala planning — the program needed shaping, and there was a question about whether to invite alumni soloists or keep it to current students — but the truth was he didn't want to go home to the empty apartment where the silence had opinions.

He ended up, as he often did without deciding to, in the gallery above Hall C. From up there, through the glass, the old hall looked almost gentle: the worn parquet, the rows of folding chairs stacked against the walls, the stage where a single work light burned. Demolition notices were already taped to the doors downstairs. In six months this room would be dust and memory and a rendering in an architect's portfolio.

He pressed his palm flat against the cool glass.

"Give it a good send-off," he said aloud to the empty hall, testing the phrase. It sounded fine. It sounded like a brochure.

Then, because the building was empty and no one could hear him, he said the other thing, the one he never said, so quietly it barely qualified as sound:

"I'm sorry."

The hall received it the way halls receive everything — acoustically, impartially, without forgiveness or judgment. Somewhere below, a radiator knocked twice and went silent, and Tobias Kessler, director, keeper of the flame, turned off the work light and went home.

He did not see the figure at the far end of the ground-floor corridor, cart parked, listening to the silence where a violin used to be. He did not know that the night shift had begun.
