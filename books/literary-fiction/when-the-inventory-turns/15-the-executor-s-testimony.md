## 15. The Executor's Testimony

Felix

The executor's name was Halvard Nilsen, and he lived forty minutes east of the city in a house that looked like it had been designed by a man who had given up on aesthetics around 1987 and never looked back. Dee found him the way she found everything—through a chain of public records, a defunct law firm, and a church newsletter that mentioned his retirement from probate work in a sentence that also praised his lefse.

"He answered on the second ring," she said, sliding into the passenger seat of the conservatory's ancient maintenance van, which Felix had signed out under the pretext of a supply run. "Retired executors are apparently the friendliest people alive. He said, and I quote, 'I've been waiting twenty years for someone to ask me about Marta.'"

Felix's hands went still on the wheel. "He said that?"

"Word for word. Then he invited us for coffee and mentioned he'd make the good kind, not the church-basement kind. I think we're being courted."

The drive out was gray and flat, the kind of November morning that made the world look like an unfinished sketch. Dee talked the whole way—not nervously, but in the way of a person who had decided that silence between two people carrying a secret was a luxury they couldn't afford. She told him about her years at legal aid, about the client who'd taught her that the truth was usually less dramatic than the cover story but infinitely more inconvenient.

"Most people think cover-ups fail because someone talks," she said, watching the strip malls go by. "They don't. They fail because someone keeps a box."

"A box?"

"Every cover-up has a box. A file, a letter, a ledger. Something somebody couldn't throw away because throwing it away would mean admitting what it was. The cover-up doesn't fail when the box is found. It fails when someone finally *opens* it." She glanced at him. "You opened yours."

"I opened half of mine."

"Then let's go find the other half. Nilsen probably has a whole closet."

* * *

Dee

Halvard Nilsen was seventy-eight years old and built like a friendly refrigerator, with a face so open it seemed almost indecent in a man who'd spent his career among wills and grievances. His house smelled of coffee, cardamom, and old paper—the last one so strongly that Dee suspected the paper had colonized the cardamom.

He shook Felix's hand with both of his own and held on a moment too long, studying his face.

"You were her student," Nilsen said. Not a question.

Felix went very still in the way Dee had learned to read as *bracing*. "I was one of her students."

"No." Nilsen released his hand and stepped back, pleased with himself in the mild way of a man who had spent decades being right on paper. "You were *the* student. She kept your programs. All of them. Laminated, some of them. In a man who laminates a concert program, that's not filing. That's devotion." He waved them toward the kitchen. "Sit. The good coffee's already on."

The kitchen table was cleared except for three cups and a single cardboard file box, the plain legal kind, with MARTA ILVES — PERSONAL written on the lid in a hand that shook slightly on the last letters.

"I was her executor for eleven years," Nilsen said, pouring with the steady economy of a man who had done this ten thousand times. "Which meant I handled her taxes, her bills, her subscriptions, and, for the last six years of her life, an increasingly large volume of correspondence with the Aldergate Conservatory." He set the pot down and looked at Felix over the rim of his own cup. "She asked me, near the end, to make sure that if anyone ever came asking—the right anyone, she said—I should give them this box and tell them everything. She said the right anyone would know what the box was for before I explained it."

Dee watched Felix's face. He was looking at the box the way people look at a door they've been afraid of for years.

"How do you know we're the right anyone?" Felix asked quietly.

Nilsen smiled, and it was a kind smile, but there was iron under it. "Because you flinched when I said *laminated*. And because your friend here has been cataloguing my kitchen like it's a crime scene since she walked in, which tells me she's a lawyer, or was one, and lawyers only come out this far into the suburbs for one of two reasons: money or justice. I don't have money."

* * *

Felix

The box was not large. That was the first thing Felix noticed, and it undid him more than anything else in the room—twenty years of a woman's conscience, and it fit in a shoebox-sized container that had once held reams of legal paper. Marta Ilves, who had filled entire cabinets with annotated scores, who had once told him that a musician's life was measured in what she left *unplayed*—all her fighting fit in one small box.

He lifted the lid with hands that did not shake, because he had trained them not to, and that training had cost him more than he could calculate.

On top lay a folder of letters, all addressed to the same office: The Office of the President, Aldergate Conservatory. He read the dates the way he read a score—first the structure, then the detail. 2019. 2020. Three in 2021. One in 2022, four months before she died.

"She wrote to them every year," Nilsen said. "Formally. Requesting that the conservatory reopen the inquiry into the events of March fourteenth, two thousand and—" He stopped, and Dee saw Felix's jaw set. "You know the date."

"Everyone at Aldergate knows the date," Felix said. "It's a ghost story they tell first-years. *The year the prodigy broke.*"

"They tell it wrong," Nilsen said flatly.

Dee leaned forward. "What did they answer?"

Nilsen pulled the letters out one by one, laying them on the table like evidence, which, Dee thought, was exactly what they were. "The first year, a form letter. The second year, a form letter signed by a different person. The third year, a meeting—she flew to the city for it, at seventy-one, with a hip that was going bad. She met with two board members and the vice president of institutional affairs. She presented her case. She had documents." He tapped the folder. "She came home and told me it had gone well. She told me that the way people tell you things have gone well when they're trying to convince themselves, so that later, when it falls apart, they can at least say they believed."

"And the fourth year?" Dee asked.

"The fourth year she got a letter from the board's counsel reminding her that the matter had been *conclusively resolved* under prior leadership, that reopening it would expose the institution to liability, and that the board trusted she would respect the conservatory's need to move forward." Nilsen's voice had gone quiet, which Dee recognized from her courtroom years: the quiet of controlled anger. "She read it to me over the phone. Twice. Then she said—and I wrote this down, because I knew even then it would matter—she said, *'They have learned to call a locked door a resolution.'*"

Felix had not touched the letters. His hands were flat on the table, and Dee understood suddenly that if he picked one up and it was what he feared, he would not be able to put it down again, and some part of him knew it.

"Read the last one," Nilsen said gently. "The one from 2022. She revised it eleven times. I have the drafts. She wanted it to be *fair*."

* * *

Dee

Felix read it aloud, because Dee suspected he needed to hear it in his own voice to believe it was real.

*To the Board of Trustees:*

*I write for the final time. I am seventy-four years old, I am tired, and I have run out of ways to say this politely, which was never my gift anyway.*

*I taught at your conservatory for thirty-one years. I built the string program you now put on your fundraising materials. I taught two boys in that hall, and one of them was destroyed, and the other one you made a director. I have spent twenty years knowing which one was guilty, and it was neither of them.*

*The sabotage of the audition was arranged by a member of your board. I will not put the name in a letter, because letters get filed and names get protected. But I will say this: I have spoken with three people who know what I know, and I am the only one of us still willing to say it out loud, and I am running out of time to be willing.*

*You did not answer my letters because you did not need to. That is what power is. But I want it on the record, in your files, in your own institutional memory, that this institution was told. Repeatedly. By the person who had the standing to be believed.*

*When the truth comes out—and it will, because the truth is patient and boards are not—I want you to remember that you had a choice every single year, and every single year you chose the building over the boy.*

*Marta Ilves*

The kitchen was silent. Nilsen poured more coffee nobody asked for, because his hands needed something to do.

"Blocked," Dee said finally, and the word tasted wrong, too small. "That's not blocked. That's—she told them. In writing. For years. And they sat on it."

"They didn't just sit on it." Nilsen sat down heavily. "After the 2021 meeting, one of the board members—Aldous Wren, you may know the name, his son runs the place now—" Felix made a sound that wasn't quite a word. "—contacted me directly. Executor to executor, he said, colleague to colleague. He asked me, very courteously, whether Marta was 'entirely well.' Whether her judgment was 'what it had been.' He suggested that her correspondence might be handled more gently if her affairs were in 'steady hands.' He was offering to have her declared incompetent, Mrs.—"

"Dee."

"—Dee. He was offering it *to me*. As if I were for sale. As if we were all for sale, and some of us simply hadn't seen the price list." Nilsen's laugh was short and without humor. "I told him her judgment was the finest I'd encountered in forty years of probate work, and that if he contacted me again I would consider it interference with an estate and act accordingly. He never wrote again. But the conservatory's letters stopped being answered after that, and Marta stopped writing them, and I have thought every day since about whether I should have done more."

"You did what she asked," Felix said. His voice was rough. "She asked you to keep the box."

"She asked me to keep the box and *wait*." Nilsen looked at him with those indecently open eyes. "She said the right anyone would come. She said, 'He'll be wearing work clothes, Halvard. He always hated being looked at. He'll come when the building starts to come down, because he'll want to see it before it's gone.' And here you are, in a janitor's jacket, and the hall comes down in three weeks." He spread his hands. "I'm not a sentimental man. But I'd like the record to show I noticed."

* * *

Felix

They stayed another hour. Nilsen gave them copies of everything—the letters, the board's replies, a notarized statement of his own account of the 2021 meeting, even the name of the stenographer Marta had hired in her last year to record a formal deposition of her testimony, which Nilsen believed still existed somewhere in the estate's remaining storage.

"Lot forty-four," he said, when Felix asked what else had been sold. "The correspondence cases. I argued against selling them. The estate needed liquidity, and the family—she had no family, that's the tragedy of it, so the *institution* stepped in as a kind of family, which tells you everything about institutions—wanted the house cleared. I saved what I could. This box is what I could."

In the van on the drive back, Dee held the folder on her lap and didn't speak for a long time, which for Dee was its own kind of event.

"She tried," Felix said finally, to the windshield. "Twenty years I thought— I built my whole silence on the idea that nobody knew. That it was mine to carry alone. And she was writing them *every year*. Fighting a locked door with a letter opener."

"She didn't just try." Dee turned in her seat to face him. "Felix. Listen to what's actually in this folder. She named the mechanism. A board member arranged it, the board buried it, and the board's *heir* is the man who just handed you a gala slot to keep you charming and quiet. This isn't a ghost story anymore. It's a paper trail with a living witness." She tapped the folder. "And a stenographer's deposition that might still exist."

"The storage unit," Felix said slowly. "The estate's remaining storage. Nilsen said the conservatory handled the clear-out."

Dee smiled, and it was not entirely a warm smile. "Which means the conservatory's own inventory list will show exactly what they took possession of. And I know a guy who does inventory for a living." She looked at him. "Three weeks, Felix. The hall comes down in three weeks. So the question isn't whether the truth is patient. The question is whether you are."

Felix drove on through the gray, the folder between them like a third passenger, and found that for the first time in twenty years, the answer came without rehearsal.

"I'm done being patient," he said. "Let's find out what they took."
