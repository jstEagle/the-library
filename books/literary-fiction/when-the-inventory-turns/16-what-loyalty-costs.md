## 16. What Loyalty Costs

The trouble with evidence, Felix decided somewhere around three in the morning on the loading dock behind Aldergate's east wing, was that it didn't come with instructions.

He had spent the evening doing what he'd done every night for eleven months: pushing a mop through corridors that remembered his footsteps from another life, emptying bins in practice rooms where students left pencil shavings and half-eaten granola bars and, once, memorably, a single ballet slipper with no explanation. The work was simple and the work was good and the work had never once asked him who he used to be.

But now he had Marta's letters in a cardboard box under his cot in the janitor's room, and Marta's ledger in his locker, and Dee's printout folded into his jacket pocket like a splinter working its way toward the skin, and every corridor had changed key. He mopped the same floor and heard different music under it.

The loading dock was where he went to think, because it was the one place at Aldergate with no acoustics to speak of—concrete and chain-link and the dumpster humming its one note of decay—and because nobody practiced violin at three in the morning on a loading dock, which meant nobody could hear what his hands did when they thought he wasn't watching them. Tonight they were doing it again: the fourth finger of his left hand pressing an imaginary string, rocking slightly, finding a vibrato in the cold air. Traitors. Twenty years and they still rehearsed without him.

"You look like a man losing an argument with himself," said Dee.

She came out the side door with two paper cups and the particular walk she used when she'd already decided how the conversation was going to go. She handed him a cup. It was tea, which she made in the break room by pouring hot water over whatever bag had been left in the communal tin longest, so it tasted faintly of everyone else's decisions. He drank it anyway. That was friendship, as far as he'd learned it: drinking the tea.

"I'm not losing," Felix said. "I'm considering my options."

"Uh-huh." She sat down on an overturned milk crate she kept out here for exactly this purpose, which told him something about how often this purpose arose. "You've been 'considering your options' for nine days. I know because you've been mopping the same twelve feet of corridor outside the director's office for nine days. The man thinks we have a flooding problem."

"He noticed?"

"He asked me if we had a plumbing issue. I said no. He looked at me the way people look at me when they think I'm lying, which is a mistake people only make once." She blew on her tea. "So. Options."

Felix looked out past the chain-link at the parking lot, empty except for Tobias's car, which sat under the one working floodlight like something posed for a photograph. Even his car looked successful. It probably had a name.

"Here's what happens," Felix said, and heard his own voice go flat and reasonable, the voice of a man reading a weather report about his own life. "I take the ledger and the letters to the board. Or to a journalist. Or to whoever still has a conscience over there, assuming the position isn't extinct. And then—" he counted on his fingers, an old habit from arguing with Marta, who had taught him that arguments should be built like sonata forms, "—the conservatory spends two years being a scandal instead of a school. Donors leave. The board eats itself. Some very good teachers start updating their résumés. The demolition of the hall gets moved up, not stopped, because nothing makes an institution demolish a building faster than the building becoming famous for the wrong reason. And a hundred and forty students who had nothing to do with any of it spend their conservatory years explaining to strangers why their school is a headline."

"That's option one."

"Option two is I do nothing. I finish my shifts. The hall comes down in March. The gala happens, I play my little human-interest slot, the janitor who plays, everybody claps, and twenty years from now some other prodigy stands in some other hall and gets destroyed by somebody's father, and nobody says anything, because saying things costs."

Dee was quiet for a moment. Out in the lot, the floodlight buzzed. Somewhere inside the building, faintly, through the fire door, someone was practicing scales—the late-night kind, the kind played by students who believed, in the way only nineteen-year-olds can believe, that the difference between them and greatness was another hour.

"Okay," Dee said. "Now say the third option."

"There isn't a third option."

"There's always a third option. There's the one you're not saying because it's the one you want." She turned on the crate to face him fully, and Felix recognized the posture—he'd seen it across kitchen tables his whole life, the posture of women preparing to be right. "But before we get there. I want to ask you something, and I want you to actually answer it, not perform an answer at me."

"That sounds ominous."

"It is. Here it is." She set her cup down on the concrete with a small deliberate click, like a judge's gavel played by someone with excellent manners. "All this wreckage you just described. The donors, the board, the students, the legacy. Whose comfort is your silence protecting? Really. Because from where I sit—and I sat in courtrooms for six years, Felix, I watched a lot of people explain why justice was inconvenient—it sounds like the list of people you're protecting has exactly one name on it, and it isn't yours, and it isn't Marta's, and it sure as hell isn't the hundred and forty students."

"Tobias was twelve when his father started building him," Felix said. "He was seventeen when—"

"When he stood in a room and watched, or didn't watch, or however it happened, and let them hang it on you." Dee's voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing about Dee; she never raised her voice, she just lowered everything else around it until her ordinary tone sounded like a verdict. "And then he built a career on top of your grave and became the director of the place that buried you, and now he's offering you five minutes of stage time at his farewell party like he's tossing a coin to a street musician. And your big worry is the *donors*."

"The students are real," Felix said. "That part isn't an excuse. I mop up after them. I know their names. There's a girl in Practice Room 7—my old room—who practices the same four bars of the Sibelius for forty minutes some nights, and she's getting it, slowly, the way you get anything, and she has no idea that the room she's sweating in was built on—" He stopped. His hands had gone still, finally, both of them, as if even his body understood that this was the sentence that mattered. "Marta built things here too. Whatever her guilt was, whatever her silence cost, she taught for thirty years. Half the working musicians in this city passed through her studio. If I burn the institution down, I don't burn Tobias. Tobias lands on his feet; men like Tobias always land on their feet, they have fathers for that. I burn *her*. Everything she actually made, versus everything she failed to fix."

"And that," Dee said, "is the most eloquent excuse I have ever heard anyone make."

Felix flinched. He would have denied it, but she was already talking.

"No, listen. I'm not saying it's nothing. I'm saying look at the shape of it. You've spent twenty years protecting a man who never protected you, and now that you finally have a reason to stop, you've found a way to keep doing it that lets you feel noble about it. Loyalty is a beautiful word, Felix. It's also the word people use when they mean fear with better manners." She picked her tea back up. "Whose comfort is it protecting? Say the name."

The floodlight buzzed. The scales inside had stopped; the student had gone to bed, or given up, or broken through—one of the three endings every night of practice contains.

"You don't know what it was like," Felix said, and hated the sentence as it left him, because it was true and insufficient in equal measure, the worst ratio a sentence can achieve.

"You're right. I don't." Dee stood, brushed off her jeans, and looked at him with an expression that was, infuriatingly, gentle. "But I know what it's like to have a file full of truth and a very good reason not to open it. I did legal aid for six years. You know why I quit? Not the pay—I knew about the pay going in. I quit because I got good at losing. I got so good at losing that I started pre-losing. Cases I could've fought, I'd settle early, tell myself it was mercy, tell myself the system was bigger than me. And maybe it was. But every time, the person whose comfort I was really protecting was mine. Losing on purpose doesn't hurt. That's the whole sales pitch."

She put her hand briefly on his shoulder—a weight, not a pat—and headed for the door. Then she paused, hand on the push bar.

"For what it's worth," she said, "the executor called me back today. Rosa Vinter. She'll see us Thursday morning, her office, before her first appointment. I told her we were researching Marta Ilves's estate for a memorial project, which is technically true the way a loaded gun is technically a paperweight." A beat. "Thursday, Felix. Come or don't. But decide which man is coming—the one who wants his name back, or the one who wants permission to keep hiding. They can't both fit in her waiting room."

* * *

He didn't sleep after that, which was fine; sleep and he had been in an open marriage for years. Instead he sat on the edge of his cot with the box of letters and read the one he had read eleven times already, the one dated fourteen years ago, eight years after the scandal, in which Marta's handwriting—which had always looked like a storm deciding to be polite—said:

*I saw him in the corridor today, grown, successful, giving a masterclass to children. He shook my hand. I let him. Understand what a coward does with a long enough runway: he turns it into a career.*

*Every year I tell myself I will write it all down and send it. Every year I put the letter in the drawer instead. I tell myself it is for the school. I am beginning to suspect the school is a very convenient drawer.*

Felix put the letter down and laughed, once, without much humor in it. Even Marta, from beyond the veil, from inside the auction lot, had gotten there first. *A very convenient drawer.* He had been telling himself for nine days that he was weighing options, performing due diligence, honoring complexity. What he had been doing was standing in front of a drawer.

He thought about the girl in Practice Room 7 again—not as an argument this time, but as a girl. Four bars of Sibelius, forty minutes, night after night. He thought about what she would think if she knew the history of her room. He suspected—this was the thing, the thing he kept circling and refusing to land on—he suspected she would not crumble. He suspected she would want to know. Students were sturdier than institutions gave them credit for; it was the institutions that were fragile, and the institutions had arranged things so that their fragility was always billed to someone else.

He thought about Tobias's face in the office, ten days ago, offering the gala slot with that warm, exhausted charm. *The janitor who plays.* And underneath it, unmistakable if you'd known him since you were both small enough to share a music stand: not recognition. Relief. Tobias had looked at Felix and seen a problem solving itself—a ghost agreeing to haunt politely, on schedule, with a bow in its hand. He hadn't seen a witness. He'd seen an audience member.

That was the detail that tipped it, more than the letters, more than the ledger, more than Dee's verdicts. Not anger. Arithmetic. Twenty years of loyalty, and the interest it had earned was a five-minute novelty slot and a man's relief that the past had learned manners.

Felix got up, opened his locker, and took out the annotation ledger. Under the emergency light it looked smaller than it did in memory, a working document, coffee-ringed, honest. He turned to the last written page—that final date, the week of the audition, the entry that stopped mid-thought the way a door stops mid-swing—and then to the blank pages after it, all those years of white space, Marta's silence rendered in paper.

He found a pen in the mop cart.

He didn't write yet. He just held the pen over the page and felt the whole weight of the choice gather in his wrist: the school, the students, the legacy, the donors, the drawer, all of it balanced against four words he could write tonight or Thursday or onstage in front of four hundred people.

*It wasn't me.*

His phone buzzed. Dee: *Thursday. 8am. Bring the ledger.*

And below it, a second message, arriving a minute later, from the number he'd saved years ago and never deleted, because deletion was a skill he'd never acquired:

*Tobias:* Saw the lighting crew's schedule for the gala — they want to run your soundcheck Friday afternoon in the hall. Can you do 4pm? Also — do you still play the Franck? Asking for programming reasons.

Felix stared at it for a long time. *Asking for programming reasons.* As if the Franck—the duo, *their* duo, the piece they'd butchered together at twelve and perfected at sixteen and never played again after seventeen—as if it were a scheduling matter. As if the man were choosing repertoire while Felix was choosing whether to end him.

He typed back: *4pm works. And yes. I still play the Franck.*

Then he closed the ledger, set the pen on top of it like a bookmark placed at the exact spot where a story stops pretending to be about the past, and lay back on the cot to wait for Thursday, listening to the building breathe around him—the radiators ticking, the pipes settling, the old hall two floors up holding its breath along with him, three days from the soundcheck, four days from the gala, one day from an executor's office where a woman named Rosa Vinter was presumably sharpening whatever it was executors sharpened.

Friday at four, he thought, in the dark. Tobias wanted a soundcheck.

He was going to give the hall one.
