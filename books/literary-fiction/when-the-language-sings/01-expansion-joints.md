## 1. Expansion Joints

The bridge over the Ångermanälven had been moving for thirty-one years, and Oscar Nystrom had come to measure it.

He arrived at six in the morning, before the traffic, when the steel was still cold enough to tell the truth. The fog sat low on the water like something the river was keeping. He parked his van on the gravel shoulder, pulled on his hi-vis vest, and stood for a moment at the railing with his thermos, doing what he always did first: nothing. Looking. A bridge told you everything in the first five minutes if you were willing to stand still and be lied to slowly after that.

Below him the water ran brown and patient. Above him the deck hummed faintly, a sound below sound, the resonance of a structure holding itself together against its own weight. Most people never heard it. Most people drove across bridges the way they drove across anything — as if permanence were a fact rather than an argument the engineers had won temporarily.

Oscar opened his case and took out the caliper gauge, the feeler stock, the notebook with its grid of columns he'd ruled himself because no commercial form ever gave him enough room for numbers. On the inside cover, in pencil so small you'd miss it unless you knew to look, he kept a running date list: every inspection of this span since 2019. Fourteen visits. The pencil marks were neat, evenly spaced, identical. That was the point of them.

"Good morning," he said to the bridge. He said it quietly, out of habit rather than belief. His father had talked to machines; Oscar had sworn off the habit and then grown into it anyway, the way a man grows into his father's coat without deciding to.

The expansion joints were the reason he was here. Joint number three, specifically — the one at the northern abutment, where the deck met the earth and was permitted, by design, to move. Everything about a bridge came down to this paradox, and Oscar had built a career on it: a structure only survives by allowing itself to change. Steel expands in summer heat, contracts in winter cold, breathes under load, shifts in wind. If you welded it rigid, it would tear itself apart within a season. So you built in gaps. You built in permission. You measured the gaps obsessively, because a gap that widens is a structure dying politely, one millimeter per year, and nobody notices until the day the road dips and a truck driver feels it through his steering wheel and calls someone, and by then you are measuring failure instead of movement.

Joint three had moved 4.2 millimeters since his last visit in October. He knelt at the edge of the lane, set the gauge into the groove, and read the number twice, then a third time, because 4.2 was outside the seasonal band and the band was the whole language. In April, after winter contraction, a healthy joint should sit between 38 and 44 millimeters. This one read 48.1.

He wrote it down. His handwriting did not change when he was worried. That was a discipline he'd taught himself at twenty-four, on his first solo inspection, when he'd found a cracked gusset plate and noticed afterward that his notes looked like they'd been written during an earthquake. Numbers should not flinch, he'd decided. Whatever the man writing them felt, the figures owed the record their composure.

4.2 millimeters of unexpected movement. He crouched there in the fog and let the number mean what it meant.

It could be nothing. That was the honest engineering answer, and he gave it its due. Drainage blockage above the bearing seat, ice heave residue, thermal lag from the cold snap in February. He would check all three. He spent the next two hours doing exactly that, working methodically along the abutment, clearing grit from the drainage channel with a wire brush, photographing the bearing, tapping the concrete with a hammer and listening to the note it returned — solid, solid, solid, a slightly duller note near the parapet that he circled in chalk and would return to in June. Competence was mostly this: showing up, doing the boring checks, refusing the drama of conclusion. Anyone could find a catastrophe. The job was finding out there wasn't one, again, and meaning it.

But underneath the work, in the place where he kept things he didn't say, another measurement was running.

Marta's side of the bed had gained eleven centimeters since Christmas.

He hadn't measured it, not literally — he wanted to be clear with himself about that, though clarity was slippery at six in the morning on a bridge in fog. But he knew distances the way other men knew faces, and he knew that her pillow now sat closer to the window than it used to, that the strip of mattress between them, which had once been warm and negotiable, had become a demilitarized zone she crossed only in sleep, unconsciously, her body seeking heat her waking self declined. Eleven centimeters. Maybe twelve. He thought about getting a tape measure and decided that was the kind of thing a man does shortly before he stops being married, and he wasn't ready to know which side of that line he stood on.

* * *

By nine the fog had lifted and the sun had come up hard and white on the water, and the first commuters were crossing overhead, tires singing on the deck plates. Oscar sat in the open back of his van with his notebook on his knees, transcribing field notes into clean columns, drinking coffee that had gone bitter in the thermos.

The joint data went into its table. 48.1 millimeters, deviation +4.1, trend flag: monitor. He added a note in the remarks column — *possible drainage contribution, cleared channel, recheck June* — and then sat looking at the page, because the table was complete and complete tables end conversations, and some part of him did not want the conversation to end.

His phone buzzed against the wheel well. Marta.

He answered on the third ring, which was his version of spontaneity. "Nystrom."

"It's me." Her voice had the morning-radio smoothness she used when she was deciding something. "You're at the bridge?"

"The Ångermanälven span. Joint three's moving more than it should."

"Mm." A pause with weight in it. "Listen, I won't take long. I got the call last night. The Malmö position. They want me."

Oscar looked at the water. A heron stood in the shallows downstream, absolutely still, pretending to be part of the landscape, which was a strategy he recognized.

"That's good," he said. "That's the one you wanted."

"It's the one I applied to, yes." Another pause. He could hear her rearranging herself around whatever she'd planned to say, choosing a smaller sentence. "They'll need an answer within two weeks. I wanted you to hear it from me before I started thinking out loud to people."

"Right. Yes. Thanks." He heard himself say *thanks*, as if she'd forwarded him an email, and hated the word even as it left his mouth, and said nothing to replace it.

"I have to go in. We'll talk tonight?"

"We'll talk tonight," he said, and hung up, and sat with the phone face-down on his knee for a long time while the heron watched the water and the water ignored everyone.

Here was what he had not said: *Take it.* Here was what else he had not said: *Don't.* Here was what he had actually said, translated from Nystrom into human: *I acknowledge receipt of information.*

He opened the notebook again and looked at the joint table, at the tidy row of measurements going back fourteen visits, each one a small faithful act of attention. You could read a bridge's whole biography in a column like that. Every number was a sentence. Movement was communication; that was the first thing they taught you. A structure that stops moving isn't stable, it's dead, and a structure that moves too much is trying to tell you something in the only voice it has. The job was never the measuring. The job was listening to what the measurement said and answering honestly.

4.2 millimeters of unexplained drift. He knew what he'd write in the report. He had no idea what he'd say at dinner.

* * *

At midday he climbed down into the box girder, because the underside was where bridges kept their secrets and he trusted undersides more than surfaces. Inside it was cool and dim, lit by the access hatches' gray squares, and the air smelled of rust, birdlime, and old paint — the smell he associated, without wanting to, with his childhood. Not any specific memory. Just the smell itself, which had lived in his father's coveralls, in the garage on Fjällgatan where Henrik had been allowed to hold the flashlight and Oscar had been allowed to watch, and Livia had not been allowed at all, though she'd come anyway and sat on the stairs, and their father had pretended not to see her, which was its own kind of allowance, the strange currency of that house.

He shook it off. Work. He walked the length of the girder checking welds with a bright LED lamp, marking nothing, finding nothing, the steel monotonous and reassuring. Halfway along, where the girder deepened over the pier, he stopped at the access hatch that opened onto the bearing shelf and lay flat on the grating to look up at the bearing itself — the great steel kidney on which the entire northern half of the bridge rested, transferring ten thousand tons of living load into the pier and thence into rock that had been patient for four hundred million years and could wait a little longer.

The bearing was fine. He could see that immediately, the way you can see that a sleeping animal is breathing. Clean lines, even wear, the PTFE surface intact. Which meant the movement at joint three was coming from somewhere else, and he made a note to pull the survey history when he got home, and then, lying on his back in the dark under a bridge, with dust sifting down through the lamplight, he let himself think the thing he'd driven three hours north to be able to think.

Three years. He and Marta had been married three years, and he could date the widening of the gap with the same precision he brought to steel. Year one: they touched feet under the duvet as a matter of course, an unconscious treaty renewed nightly. Year two: the treaty held but required intention on cold nights, one of them migrating toward the other, a small deliberate warmth. Year three, this year: the middle ground had become neutral territory, and crossing it happened less, and when it happened neither of them remarked on it, and the not-remarking was the loudest thing in the apartment.

It wasn't coldness. That was what he couldn't explain to anyone, including possibly himself. Marta laughed at his jokes — the few he made. She cooked well and ate his cooking generously. They functioned. They were, by every external measure, a competent structure. Load paths continuous, no visible cracking, deflections within tolerance. Any inspector passing through their life would sign the report without comment.

But he was not any inspector. He was the one who lay on gratings in the dark and listened for the dull note, and he had heard it. Somewhere in the last year a load path had quietly rerouted itself, and the structure was carrying her weight — or his — through a different member than the one designed for it, and the member was adequate, adequate, adequate, and adequacy over decades is how bridges die.

And the terrible professional truth, the one he'd never said aloud to anyone, was this: he didn't know whether the movement was damage or adaptation. Some structures live longer because they learn to move differently. Maybe the distance in the bed was Marta adapting to a husband who had never once, in three years, asked her a question he didn't already know the answer to. Maybe the gap wasn't decay. Maybe it was her expansion joint, her permitted movement, the space she'd engineered so the marriage wouldn't tear itself apart — and the horror was that he couldn't rule it out, because he had never asked, and asking was the one inspection he seemed constitutionally unable to perform.

His father had never asked Livia why she left. That thought arrived uninvited, the way thoughts do in dark places, and he let it pass through without stopping it, the way you let a bird cross a beam of light. It was true, and it was irrelevant, and both facts coexisted in him without friction, which was perhaps the most accurate description of Oscar Nystrom anyone could give.

* * *

He finished the underside at four, emerged blinking into the long northern light, and packed his gear with the economy of a man who has done ten thousand times what he is doing. Before closing the case he took out the feeler gauge one more time and went back to joint three, because a reading taken twice in different conditions is worth four taken once, and because he wanted, though he'd have denied it, one more minute with the number.

48.3 millimeters. Two-tenths wider than the morning, which was within instrument error and also, possibly, not.

He wrote it down. Then, in the margin, in pencil, where no one else would ever read it, he wrote: *Ask her.*

He looked at the words for a while. They sat there on the page, small and gray and impossible, the way the important ones always did. Ask her what? That was the problem with the instruction. It contained no protocol. There was no column for it, no tolerance band, no seasonal adjustment. He could recite the acceptance criteria for a welded connection from memory. He could not construct a single sentence that began with what he felt and ended anywhere true.

In the end he erased it. Not decisively — he rubbed it out lightly, so that the ghost of the letters stayed pressed into the paper, legible if you tilted the page against the light. He told himself he'd rewrite it properly later, in ink, when he'd worked out the wording. He believed this the way you believe a bridge will hold: provisionally, on evidence, until the day it doesn't.

He drove south with the windows down. At the toll plaza outside Sundsvall his phone buzzed again and he checked it at a red light, expecting Marta, and it was Henrik instead — a text, because Henrik communicated exclusively in texts of bureaucratic brevity:

*Auction listing for Fjällgatan went live this morning. Viewings start Saturday. Call me.*

Oscar read it three times. The light turned green behind him and someone honked, and he put the phone in the cupholder face-up, where he could see the message glowing, and pulled back into traffic, and did not call anyone, and did not think about the house, and thought about nothing else for two hundred kilometers.
