## 1. The Inventory of Silence

The house smelled the way it always had—cold salt and beeswax and something faintly medicinal, like the ghost of a cough drop dissolved in the air itself—and Casper stood in the doorway of it for a long moment before going in, because the doorway was the last place where the house was still only a house.

Behind them, the gravel lot was emptying. The last of the mourners were folding themselves into sensible cars, and Casper could see old Mrs. Brandt's taillights wobbling down the lane toward the coast road, and beyond that the sea, gray today, indifferent, doing its enormous work without comment. Someone had said *she'd have wanted a smaller service* at the reception, and someone else had said *she never liked fuss*, and Casper had smiled at both of them, the smile, the one they'd been perfecting since they were nine years old and learned that a smile could end a conversation before it began.

*How are you holding up?*

The smile.

*She was a remarkable woman.*

The smile.

*It's so good of you to come back and take care of everything.*

The smile, deployed like a lock turning. It worked on strangers. It had worked, Casper realized with a small cold surprise, on Anders too, all week—Anders, who had flown in from Copenhagen, shaken every hand, wept once, briefly, beautifully, at the right moment during the service, and then announced he had to be back at the office by Thursday and left before the coffee was cleared. Their mother's son. The loyal one. He'd hugged Casper at the door of the church and held on a beat too long and said, *Call me if anything's—complicated,* and Casper had smiled, and Anders had gotten into his rental car believing, apparently, that everything was settled.

The house was not settled. The house was forty years of a woman's life arranged in its final order, waiting to be counted.

Casper hung their coat on the hook by the door—the hook Marit had installed at exactly Casper's childhood height and never moved, so that Casper, at thirty-four, still bent slightly to reach it—and stood in the hall listening to the particular silence of a house that had been lived in alone. It was not an empty silence. Empty houses sounded hollow. This one sounded *held*, like a breath someone was keeping.

"Okay, Mamma," Casper said aloud, to no one, because the alternative was not speaking, and not speaking was not yet survivable. "Let's see what you left."

* * *

They started in the kitchen, because kitchens are honest.

Marit Hartwell had kept a house the way she did everything—precisely, without display. The drawers opened on order: knives by size, the good scissors wrapped in cloth, a tin of buttons that had outlived every garment they'd ever belonged to. Casper catalogued on a yellow legal pad, because Anders had insisted on a lawyer for the estate and the lawyer had insisted on an inventory, and because the act of writing *one tin, assorted buttons* felt like a way of touching their mother without breaking.

The refrigerator held milk, butter, half a lemon in film, and a jar of herring that Casper opened and immediately closed. On the door, under magnets shaped like fish, three things: a grocery list in her small upright hand (*eggs, rye, lamp oil*—lamp oil, still, in the age of electricity, for the lamp in the window that she lit every night of Casper's childhood and, apparently, every night after Casper left); a postcard of the harbor, unposted, no message; and a photograph Casper had never seen.

It was a photograph of the two of them—Marit and Casper, age maybe six—standing on the rocks below the house. Casper had no memory of it being taken. In it, their mother was looking not at the camera but down at the child, with an expression Casper had never once seen on her living face: unguarded, open, almost startled by her own tenderness. Casper looked at it for a long time. Then they took it off the refrigerator and put it in their jacket pocket, which was not cataloguing, and which they did anyway.

The living room took two hours. Books first—she'd had more of them than Casper remembered, or perhaps she'd acquired them in the years Casper had stopped coming home as often, which was a thought Casper set down carefully and did not pick up again. Novels in Danish and Norwegian, some English. Gardening manuals. A shelf of maritime histories that surprised Casper; Marit had never spoken of the sea except to complain about the damp it put in the walls. And one shelf, at the top, of books in a language Casper didn't recognize—thin volumes, cloth-bound, spines cracked from use, the titles printed in a script of strange hooked letters and slashed vowels.

Casper pulled one down. *Vintersanger,* said the spine, or something like it. Inside, the type marched in double columns, and every few pages a hand had written in pencil—*her* hand, unmistakably, the same hand that had written *eggs, rye, lamp oil*—marginal notes in the same unrecognizable letters, and occasionally, beneath them, in English: *the tide that runs against the wind.* *to speak into a gale.* *the last of a kind.*

Casper stood in the living room of the house they'd grown up in, holding a book in a language their mother had never mentioned, and felt the first hairline crack run through the numbness.

She'd hidden it well, whatever it was. Or rather—she hadn't hidden it at all. The books had sat on an open shelf for God knew how many years, in plain view, and Casper had visited this house dozens of times and never once looked up. That was the thing about their mother. She hadn't lied, exactly. She had simply arranged the truth on a high shelf and trusted no one to reach for it.

The smile Casper gave the empty room was the old one, the conversation-ending one, and it shook at the corners, and no one was there to see it fail.

* * *

By late afternoon the light had gone the color of pewter and Casper had worked through the bedroom—closets of good wool, decades of the same coat in successive weights, a shoebox of letters from Anders, from Grethe, from a cousin in Bergen—and had arrived at the small back room that had been, in Casper's childhood, the sewing room, and after that, unexplained, simply *closed.*

The door stuck. It had always stuck. Casper shouldered it the way they had as a child, when the room had still been forbidden in the soft unspoken way their mother made things forbidden—never a rule, never a word, just a stillness in her body whenever the subject came near—and the door gave with a sound like a held breath released.

Dust, but not much. The room had been *used*. A desk under the window, facing the sea. A lamp. A straight-backed chair worn pale at the edges of the seat. And on the desk, squared to its edges with the precision Casper knew better than their own heartbeat, a row of notebooks.

Eleven of them. Cheap school notebooks, the kind sold in the stationer's in town, their covers soft with handling. The first was labeled in her hand: a date, twenty-two years ago. The last was labeled with a date from five weeks before she died.

Twenty-two years. Casper did the arithmetic without wanting to. Twenty-two years was the age Anders had been when Halvard's boat went down. It was the age Casper had been when they'd stood at the funeral—no, not a funeral, there had been no body, there had been a *service*, a stone in the churchyard with a name and two dates and between them nothing but the sea's blank verdict—twelve years old, holding their mother's hand, feeling her hand not shake and understanding, in the way children understand, that the not-shaking was the effort of a lifetime.

Twenty-two years. The notebooks began the month their father died.

Casper's hands were not steady. They sat down in the worn chair—the chair their mother had sat in, the chair still holding the shape of her sitting—and opened the first notebook.

The script was the same as the books on the shelf. Those hooked letters, those slashed vowels, marching across the lines in her upright hand, page after page after page. Here and there an English word surfaced like a swimmer: *tide*, *net*, *name*, *mother*. Once, on a page near the beginning, a full sentence in English, underlined twice: *There are words in this language for things we feel and have no way to say. That is why she learned it. That is why I must.*

*She.* Casper read the sentence four times. *That is why she learned it.* Learned what? Learned—no. The sentence was in the notebook. The notebook was in Marit's hand. Unless.

Casper turned back to the cover. The date was there, in her hand. The handwriting inside was hers, they were almost sure, and then they turned pages faster, looking, and found a passage where the letters changed—looser, older, a man's slant—and changed back again, and it occurred to Casper with a slow, vertiginous dread that they were looking not at one person's writing but at a *correspondence*, a conversation conducted across years, in a language Casper could not read, between their mother and someone whose name Casper did not know, about something that had begun the week their father died.

The light failed. Casper did not get up to turn on the lamp. They sat in the darkening room with the notebook open on their knees like a bird with a broken wing, and the house around them did its held-breath silence, and the sea worked at the rocks below, and Casper understood that the inventory they had come to make—the legal one, the one for the lawyer, *one tin, assorted buttons*—was a fiction, a child's game, an inventory of surfaces. The real inventory was here. Eleven notebooks. One locked drawer.

Because there was a locked drawer. Casper had noticed it an hour ago and catalogued it without thinking: *desk, oak, one drawer locked.* Their mother had died suddenly, at her kitchen table, a cup of tea going cold beside a crossword half finished, and whatever opened that drawer had died with her—or so Casper assumed, until now, sitting in her chair, in her room, holding her secret in a language they couldn't speak.

Casper tried the drawer. Locked, of course. They tried the top of the desk frame, the old hiding places every child of this house knew—the loose board under the window seat, the tin in the kitchen that had held the spare keys until it didn't. Nothing.

It was full dark when Casper finally stood, stiff, and carried the eleven notebooks out of the sewing room and up to their childhood bedroom, and set them on the bed in a row, and looked at them for a long time.

On the inside cover of the first notebook, beneath the date, in their mother's hand, small, almost hidden, was an address. Not in the strange script—in English, in the handwriting she used for the outside world. A name and a place.

*A. Vinter. The old boathouse, below the headland, Vesterhavn.*

Casper knew the boathouse. Everyone in town knew the boathouse; it had been derelict for as long as Casper could remember, a black hulk of a building at the end of the shingle, the kind of place children dared each other to approach. Someone lived there. Someone had lived there all along, apparently, above the water, while Casper grew up and left and came back and left again, and their mother had walked—had she walked? taken the bus?—down to that derelict place for twenty-two years, filling notebook after notebook in a language that had no business existing inside the life Marit Hartwell had shown her children.

Casper lay down on the narrow bed, on top of the covers, still in their funeral clothes, and did not sleep. The notebooks sat on the bed beside them like eleven small closed doors.

At some point past three, the moon came out over the water and threw a pale lane across the floor, and Casper got up, and took the first notebook off the row, and opened it again to the underlined sentence, and read it one more time in the moonlight.

*That is why she learned it. That is why I must.*

Whoever *she* was, she had learned it for a reason. And Marit had learned it for a reason. And the reasons were in these pages, and in a locked drawer, and in a boathouse at the end of the shingle, in the keeping of a stranger whose name their mother had written down as though she'd always meant for someone to find it.

As though, Casper thought, she had meant for *them* to find it.

Outside, the tide turned. Casper heard it—the long exhale of the water pulling back from the rocks—and lay awake listening, in the house of a woman they had loved and, it was now unbearably clear, never known, until the gray light came up over the headland and made the room honest again.

In the morning, Casper took the notebook with the address, put on their coat, bent for the hook by the door, and went out to find the man who spoke alone.
