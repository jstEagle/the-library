## 5. Tam Buys Back Time

The house had been on Tam's radar for eleven days, which in developer time was practically a marriage.

He'd done everything right. He'd pulled the parcel records over coffee at a place that served espresso in thimble-sized cups because the owner believed small portions signaled sophistication. He'd run comps on the whole block — the corner lot with the new build, the duplex two doors down, the sad little bungalow behind the hedge that sold last spring for an amount that made Tam laugh out loud in his car. He'd drafted three versions of the pitch to his partners, each one cleaner than the last: *teardown, regrade, four-unit infill, we're looking at numbers that don't exist anywhere else in this zip code.*

The Alder Street house was, professionally speaking, a gift. Structurally tired, cosmetically tragic, sitting on dirt worth more than every memory inside it combined. Any appraiser would tell him the same thing. The kindest thing anyone could do for that property was a bulldozer and a fresh start.

Tam told himself this on Tuesday morning, standing in the gravel driveway with his clipboard and his best jacket, telling himself it very firmly, the way you'd talk to a dog afraid of thunder.

*It's just a house.*

He let himself in with the lockbox code the probate lawyer had sent over — Tam had gotten himself onto every distribution list connected to the estate within forty-eight hours of the notice, a feat of administrative enthusiasm he was not proud of but did not regret — and stepped into the front hall.

The smell hit him first. Old plaster and dust and underneath it something faintly sweet, like the ghost of apples left too long in a bowl. His mother's kitchen smell. Thirty years gone and the house still remembered her.

"Okay," Tam said out loud, to no one, in the bright voice he used for open houses. "Okay, great. Lots of natural light."

There was almost no natural light. Every curtain was drawn.

He worked the rooms in order, because order was how Tam handled things: living room, dining room, kitchen, down. He measured nothing. He wrote *load-bearing wall?* on his clipboard twice without checking anything load-bearing. In the kitchen he stood for a long moment looking at the spot where the refrigerator used to be, where the family calendar used to hang, where his father used to lift him up to mark off the days before his birthday, and he wrote *potential for island* and his handwriting came out shaky and he hated himself for it.

That was when he heard the front door.

Not the lockbox rattle of a stranger. Something slower. Careful. A key-turn rhythm he knew from somewhere deep, some afternoon thirty years ago when he'd been small enough to be home alone for exactly eleven minutes and had spent all of them terrified.

Tam's stomach dropped through the floorboards into whatever crawlspace the house kept its secrets in.

"Noor?" he called, before he could stop himself, before he could arrange his face into anything useful.

His sibling appeared in the kitchen doorway holding a manila folder and wearing the expression Noor wore everywhere — pleasant, closed, a door painted on a wall. Thirty years of night shifts had polished Noor into something smooth and unreadable, and Tam had spent most of his adult life admiring it from a distance and being quietly broken by it.

"You got the access list too," Noor said.

"I'm a professional." Tam spread his arms, gesturing at the kitchen, the dust, the lightless windows. "Assessing contents. Very glamorous work. You wouldn't believe what people leave behind. I once found a full set of encyclopedias in a crawl space, 1963 edition, someone had hollowed out the V volume for—"

"Tam."

"—cash, actually, which, honestly, respect—"

"*Tam.*"

He stopped. The sales patter died in his throat the way it always did around Noor, eventually, like a radio losing signal. He set the clipboard down on the counter — the counter where their mother had rolled pie crust and lied beautifully for decades, though only one of them knew about the lying yet, and Tam intended to keep it that way for as long as physics allowed.

"I didn't think you'd come back here," he said.

"I didn't think you'd buy it."

"Bid," Tam corrected. "I'm going to *bid* on it. There's a difference. Bidding is speculative. Buying is commitment." He grinned, because grinning was free and usually worked. "Besides, somebody responsible should get it. Somebody who'll do right by the block. You've seen the comps, probably. Whole street's turning over."

Noor looked at him. That was the thing about Noor — they didn't argue, they just *looked*, patient as a librarian waiting out a noisy patron, and Tam felt the grin slide off his face like something melting.

"You hate this house," Noor said.

"I love this house," Tam said, too fast, too loud. "I have extremely warm feelings toward this house. This house is where I learned to ride a bike. This house is where Dad—" He stopped. The sentence sat there in the dusty air, unfinished, the way it had been unfinished for thirty years, the way the whole family left sentences about Dad unfinished, a house built entirely of ellipses.

*Where Dad walked out,* the sentence was supposed to end. Everyone agreed on that part. Dad walked out on a Tuesday in October and never came back, and Mom packed them up within the month, and they never spoke of it, and Tam had been seven years old and had cried for weeks and been told by both older siblings, separately, gently, *he's not coming back, Tam, you have to be brave.*

He'd been brave. Look at him. Bravest guy in the county. Owned a firm, drove a nice car, could sell a condemned building to its own grandmother.

"So what's the plan," Noor said. Not a question. Noor didn't really ask questions; questions invited answers, and answers were dangerous. "Demolish?"

"Renovate or rebuild, depends on what the—"

"The curtains are still here," Noor said. They crossed to the window and touched the fabric, yellowed now, sun-bleached roses. "Mom sewed these. She complained about them for twenty years and never replaced them."

"People do that," Tam said. "People keep things."

"Yes," Noor said softly. "They do."

* * *

They ended up in the backyard, because neither of them could stand the kitchen anymore, though neither said so. The grass was knee-high and gone to seed. The oak tree was bigger. Everything else was smaller, the way childhood geography shrinks when you return to it as an adult — the fence lower, the yard narrower, the whole world of it fitting inside a space that took Tam four strides to cross.

When they were kids this yard had been enormous. It had held a swing set, a garden, a badminton net, a dog named Captain who was afraid of the vacuum. Now it held weeds and one oak tree and two middle-aged siblings who hadn't stood in the same place in years.

"Wren won't talk to me," Noor said.

"Wren doesn't talk to anybody. Wren talks *at* people, in complete sentences, with citations." Tam picked a seed head off the grass and shredded it. "She's fine. She's just — she processes slowly. Like a very dignified computer."

"She hung up on me."

"That's Wren's version of a long conversation."

Noor almost smiled. Tam caught it — the flicker, the old thing, the thing they'd had when they were kids and he'd follow Noor around the library where they worked summers, shelving cart returns, and Noor would let him stay even though he talked too much, because Noor understood that some people talked so the silence wouldn't get them.

He loved his sibling. It occurred to him, standing in the ruined yard, that he had organized his entire adult life to avoid saying so in any way that mattered.

"Listen," Tam said. "About the auction."

"Don't offer me money."

"I wasn't going to offer you money."

"You were absolutely going to offer me money. You had the number ready. I could see it behind your eyes. It has commas in it."

Tam laughed despite himself — a real laugh, surprised out of him, the kind he hadn't produced in this yard since he was nine. "Fine. Yes. I was going to offer you money. Because money is what I have, Noor. It's my whole toolkit. Some people have words, some people have — whatever you have, that librarian stare — I have money and charm, and you're immune to both, so I'm standing in a weed field with nothing."

"What would the money be for?"

And there it was. The question, simple and terrible, hanging between them like the badminton net that used to.

Tam looked at the oak tree. He thought about the shoebox. He thought about the postcards — Lisbon, the stamps, the cheerful looping handwriting, twenty years of them, *Don't tell Tam* — which he had found at nineteen, going through Mom's things after the funeral, too early in the morning, alone, and read all of them in one sitting on the floor of her closet with his heart going like a trapped bird.

He thought about deciding, at nineteen, that some boxes should be taped shut forever. He thought about how he'd told no one, and how the silence had grown around the secret like bone around a break, until he couldn't tell if it was protecting him or deforming him.

He thought about the fact that Noor was standing ten feet away and did not know, and that every day Tam didn't say it was a day he chose the lie again.

"For peace," Tam said finally. "The money would be for peace. Everybody walks away, house comes down, we put the whole — the whole *era* — in the ground, and we get brunch sometimes. Actual brunch. With mimosas. You'd be amazed what mimosas can fix."

Noor watched him for a long moment. The morning light came through the oak leaves and moved across their face in little shifting coins.

"You know something," Noor said. Not accusing. Worse than accusing — *observing*, the way they observed patrons, the way they observed everything, cataloguing without judgment, which was somehow the most frightening thing in the world.

"I know a lot of things," Tam said lightly. "I know the zoning variance on this lot is a nightmare. I know Mrs. Okafor still lives on the corner and still thinks I'm adorable, which, fair. I know you haven't asked me a real question since 2019."

"Tam."

"And I know," he said, and his voice did something he didn't authorize, dropped and roughened, "that there are things Mom wanted buried, and I promised her, okay? Before she died. I made her a promise, and I keep my promises, and I need you — I'm asking you, Noor, I'm actually asking — to let me keep this one."

The yard went quiet. Somewhere down the street a dog barked twice and gave up.

Noor looked at him, and Tam saw something move behind the librarian calm — hurt, maybe, or recognition, the look of someone realizing the person in front of them is carrying something heavy and refusing to show it.

"You sound scared," Noor said.

"I sound like a guy who wants brunch."

"Noor," Tam said, using their own name against them, the oldest trick. "Please."

For a second — one second — he thought it might work. That Noor would nod, and pocket the folder, and walk out of the yard, and Tam would demolish the house and tape the box shut one more decade.

Then Noor glanced down at the manila folder in their hands, and Tam saw the corner of a postcard peeking out of it, and felt the ground tilt under him.

Because the card in Noor's folder was addressed in their mother's handwriting.

And Tam had burned his stack in a coffee can at nineteen, at two in the morning, crying, certain he was the only one who'd ever know.

"How many did you find?" Noor asked quietly.

Tam didn't answer. Behind them, up in the oak, a crow said something rude and lifted off, and the branches swayed, and neither of them moved.
