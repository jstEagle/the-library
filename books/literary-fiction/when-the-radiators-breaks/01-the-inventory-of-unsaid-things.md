## 1. The Inventory of Unsaid Things

The Kestrel house had been waiting for me, though neither of us knew it yet.

I want to be careful with that sentence, because I am not a mystical woman. I don't read auras. I don't believe the dead linger in wallpaper or that grief soaks into floorboards like tea into a coaster. But I do believe that houses keep records — not in any ghostly way, just mechanically, physically, the way a vinyl record keeps a song in its grooves. A house is a machine for remembering, and most people walk through one hearing nothing but their own footsteps.

I hear the rest of it.

My name is Ulyana Blackwood, and for nineteen years I have tuned pianos and, when asked — rarely, and usually by the desperate — steam radiators. The pianos are honest work. A piano wants to be understood; it holds still while you listen to it. Radiators are different. Radiators are old men muttering in the next room, and you have to earn what they tell you.

The Kestrel house stood at the end of Corliss Street like a sentence someone had started and abandoned. Three stories, brick gone the color of dried blood, a porch roof sagging with the particular dignity of things that have decided not to apologize for their condition. The realtor had met me on the sidewalk, a brisk young man named Dev who shook my hand twice because he'd forgotten he already had.

"You're sure you can get these running?" he said. "Buyer wants heat by inspection. The place has been empty eleven years."

"Empty," I said, "is a strong word."

He laughed, assuming I was being charming. I was being accurate.

* * *

Inside, the air had the taste all long-empty houses share: dust, cold plaster, and something faintly sweet, like fruit left too long in a bowl nobody claims. Dev's heels clacked across the entry hall and every surface answered him — the floorboards spoke, the stair rail hummed under his hand, the front door complained in B-flat when it closed behind us.

"Radiators are mostly original," Dev said, consulting his phone. "Steam system, 1912 or thereabouts. Estate says tune them, bleed them, whatever you do. Just make them quiet."

I almost smiled. Nobody had ever hired me to make radiators quiet. People hired me to make them *work*, which was a different profession entirely, and one I practiced badly on purpose.

"I'll need the day," I said. "Possibly two. And I'll need everyone to leave."

Dev left with visible relief, the way people exit rooms containing both cobwebs and women with opinions.

And then it was just me and the house, and I did what I always do first, before touching a single valve. I walked through every room and listened.

* * *

People think tuning is about pitch. It isn't. Tuning is about inventory. You cannot fix a sound until you know exactly what sounds are present, and so my first hour in any building is spent cataloging — every knock, tick, hiss, groan, and gurgle, each one noted in the small green notebook I've carried since I was twenty-three. Over the years the notebooks have become something else, something I don't advertise. Because an inventory of sounds, kept honestly and long enough, becomes an inventory of everything else.

Here is what I mean. When Mrs. Okafor on Delancey Street told me her radiator "just makes a little tapping, probably nothing," I wrote down: *little tapping, irregular, water hammer, needs pitch adjustment* — and also: *husband's chair still pushed against the wall six months after the funeral; she talks to the tapping.* When the Hollands' boiler knocked in triplets, I wrote down: *air in line, third-floor riser* — and also: *they are fighting about money and have decided to fight about the thermostat instead.*

A house tells you what a family won't. Every unsaid thing has to go somewhere, and it goes into the pipes.

So my notebook for the Kestrel house began, as they all do, with the mechanical truth:

*Entry hall radiator: slow knock, patient, like a knuckle on a door. Needs bleeding.*
*Parlor, east wall: high thin hiss, continuous even cold. Valve seat worn. Lonely sound.*
*Dining room: gurgle, low, conversational. Almost like humming.*

That last note I underlined, and then felt foolish for underlining, and then underlined it again anyway.

By noon I had cataloged fourteen radiators, and fourteen was wrong. The estate paperwork listed fourteen. My ears counted fifteen. There was a sound coming from below — faint, rhythmic, rising up through the kitchen floor like something breathing under a blanket — that belonged to no radiator I'd yet visited.

I stood in the kitchen with my notebook open and let myself feel the thing I usually kept folded very small: that I had driven three hours to this town, that I would sleep tonight in a motel with a vibrating ice machine, that no one on earth expected me anywhere or missed me anywhere, and that the only conversation waiting for me was with a house scheduled for demolition.

My mother grew up forty miles from here. I want that on the record now, at the start, because she never once put it on any record herself. In fifty-eight years of living, Marion Blackwood mentioned this region exactly four times in my presence, each time with the flat finality of a door closing: *We didn't stay long. It wasn't for us.* Four sentences to account for the first thirty years of her life. I had done the math on that silence many times, usually at night, usually alone, and the math never came out even. An inventory of unsaid things, and hers was the largest single holding.

I closed the notebook. I went to find the fifteenth radiator.

* * *

The basement stairs were behind the kitchen, a door painted shut sometime in the Eisenhower administration. I shouldered it until it gave with a crack like a knuckle cracking, and the dark below exhaled — cold stone, coal dust, machine oil, and underneath it all that sweetness again, stronger here, like apples.

I found the light switch on the fourth try. A bare bulb woke reluctantly overhead, and the basement assembled itself out of shadow: a coal chute bricked over, shelves of jars with labels faded to whispers, and along the far wall, the spine of the house — the main pipe, thick as my arm, running the length of the ceiling with branches reaching up into the walls like roots drawn in reverse.

And there, in the corner, half-hidden behind a sheet-draped workbench: the fifteenth radiator. Smaller than the others, ornate, cast iron scrolled like wedding cake. Its valve was unlike anything I'd seen — brass gone green, stamped with letters I couldn't read from where I stood.

It was making the sound.

Not knocking. Not hissing. *Speaking*, or nearly — a patterned thing, knock-knock-pause, knock, knock-knock-pause, with a rise and fall to it that no pressure differential on earth produces. Steam does not have cadence. Steam does not have *phrases*. I stood in the cold with my pen hovering over the notebook for a full minute before I wrote anything, because the only honest entry was:

*Fifteenth radiator: talking.*

Then, beneath it, because I am a professional and professionals commit: *Investigate.*

I took out my phone and recorded ninety seconds of it, standing close enough to feel the iron's chill against my cheek. On playback, tinny and small, it was somehow worse — more deliberate, more like a voice heard through a wall, someone singing very softly to themselves in another room, in another language, in a house where you thought you were alone.

I played it four times. On the fifth, I caught myself swaying slightly, keeping time with it, and stopped.

That was when the floorboards overhead creaked — once, deliberately, the way a person shifts their weight when they've been standing still a long time. Then footsteps, crossing the kitchen above me, unhurried. Then the scrape of the basement door I'd broken open, and a voice from the top of the stairs, older than the pipes and just as patient:

"You found her, then."

I looked up into the dimness at the shape of a man I hadn't seen arrive, hadn't heard a car announce, hadn't been told to expect. He stood with one hand on the rail like he'd been standing at the top of those stairs for years, waiting for someone to come down and listen.

"Fifteen radiators," I said, because it was the only true thing I had. "The paperwork says fourteen."

"The paperwork," the old man said, beginning slowly down, "says a great many things."
