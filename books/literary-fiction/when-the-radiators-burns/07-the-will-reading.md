## 7. The Will Reading

The lawyer's office smelled of lemon polish and old money, which is to say it smelled of my aunt Margot's perfume layered over forty years of other people's grief.

I arrived early because I'd spent twenty years arriving late to everything that mattered, and I was trying, in my fumbling middle-aged way, to correct the record. The receptionist asked if I was family. I said yes before I could think of a better answer, and then hated how easily the word came out, like a coin I'd kept in my pocket so long the face had worn smooth.

Margot was already there, of course. Margot was always already there; I suspected she'd been born mid-sentence, correcting a nurse. She wore black the way other women wear red — as an announcement. Her mourning was tailored. Her grief had been dry-cleaned.

"Juniper." She said my name the way you'd set down something fragile you didn't trust yourself not to drop. "You came."

"It's my father's will, Aunt Margot. Where else would I be?"

"Anywhere," she said pleasantly. "Historically speaking, anywhere has been your preference."

There it was. Twenty years compressed into one sentence, delivered with the smile of a woman who'd rehearsed it in the car. I sat down across from her and did what I'd learned to do in rehearsal halls: I breathed from the diaphragm and waited for my cue.

Elias came in last, holding his coat over his arm like a man who hadn't decided whether he was staying. He'd been invited, I gathered, by the lawyers themselves — *all interested parties*, the letter had said, a phrase that apparently now included him. He looked around the room the way I looked around rehearsal rooms at dawn: cataloguing exits, memorizing faces, deciding which version of himself to be.

When his eyes found mine, something passed between us that I couldn't name yet. Recognition, maybe. Or its draft form.

* * *

Mr. Halloran — the lawyer, silver-haired, kind in the practiced way of men who deliver bad news for a living — cleared his throat and opened a folder thick enough to stop a door.

"This is the last will and testament of Alistair Edmund Thornbury," he read, "executed the fourteenth of March, three years past."

Three years past. My father had written this while alive, while walking past me with a mop bucket in his hands — no, he hadn't known about the mop bucket, that indignity was mine alone — while alive and knowing exactly what he wanted to say and choosing, characteristically, to say it through an intermediary. Even dead, the man couldn't speak directly. He'd outsourced his voice to paper. It was the most honest thing he'd ever done.

"To the Thornbury Conservatory of Music," Halloran continued, "I leave the building entire, its grounds, its endowment holdings, and my voting shares, on the condition that the institution never bear any name but Thornbury."

Margot nodded along, satisfied. The building was the family. The family was the building. Everything else was furniture.

"To Mr. Elias Vane, I leave my instruments — the 1897 Steinway, the Guarneri violin, and all instruments held in the practice residence — together with their maintenance fund, and the contents of the locked practice room, save one item herein otherwise specified."

I felt the room tilt slightly. Not the piano — I'd expected the piano, or feared it, or both. The violin. The Guarneri. The instrument my father played on Sunday mornings when he thought no one was listening, the one he'd once told me had more honest days than most people. He'd left it to a stranger.

Or not a stranger. That was the thing I kept forgetting and remembering, like a tongue probing a broken tooth. Elias Vane, who stood by the window with his jaw tight, looking less like a man receiving an inheritance than a man being handed evidence.

Margot's hand went flat on the table. I watched it happen in slow motion, the way you watch weather come in.

* * *

"To my daughter, Juniper Rose Thornbury—"

Here it was. Whatever it was. I braced for the apology, or worse, the non-apology shaped like one. I braced for money, which would have been its own insult, or for nothing, which would have confirmed twenty years of arithmetic.

"—I leave the sealed envelope held in the practice room, marked with her childhood name, and the following instruction, which I ask be read aloud."

Halloran adjusted his glasses. Somewhere behind me someone shifted in a chair.

"'Finish it.'"

Two words. Two syllables each, if you counted generously. My father's entire testament to his only child, the sum total of two decades of silence, and it wasn't even a sentence. No subject. No object. Just an imperative hanging in the air like a note cut off before resolution — and God help me, every musician in that room knew what an unresolved dominant feels like in the chest. You lean into it. You wait for the chord that never comes.

"That's it?" Margot said.

"That is the entirety of the provision regarding Ms. Thornbury," Halloran said carefully.

"No bequest. No funds. No—" She stopped herself, recalibrating, because saying *no explanation* out loud would have admitted there was something needing explaining. "An envelope. He left his daughter an envelope and a homework assignment."

"He left her a piece of music," Elias said quietly.

Everyone turned. He hadn't spoken until then; I think some of us had half-forgotten he was in the room, which, given the contents of the will, seemed increasingly impossible.

"You don't know that," Margot said.

"I know his handwriting on manuscript paper when I see it described." His voice stayed level, but I heard the effort in it, the way you hear the effort in a pianist playing pianissimo — the strength required to make almost no sound. "He worked on something for years. He wouldn't tell me what. He called it *the correction*."

*The correction.* The word landed in my sternum. Twenty years ago I'd walked off a stage mid-concerto, and somewhere in the years after, my father had sat down to write — what? An apology? A rebuttal? A second attempt at the conversation we'd never finished?

Margot recovered first, because Margot always recovered first. "Well. How touching. And entirely beside the point, because the point—" and here she drew a document from her handbag, because of course she'd brought a document to a will reading, the woman traveled armed, "—is that my brother was not of sound mind in his final years, and these provisions reflect an obsession rather than an estate plan."

"Margot," Halloran began.

"A stranger gets the Guarneri. A stranger who appeared in his life — when, Mr. Vane, fifteen years ago? Sixteen? — and insinuated himself into my brother's confidence while his actual family—" her hand swept toward me without quite landing, the gesture of a woman pointing at a stain she refused to touch, "—was shut out. And his daughter receives an envelope. I'm sorry, but I've seen this before. Lonely men, aging men, and the young people who learn to smile at them."

Elias went very still. I knew that stillness. It was the stillness of a person deciding whether to fight or fold, and I recognized it because I'd lived inside it for twenty years, and something in me — something old and unpracticed and furious — stood up before I'd decided to.

"Don't," I said.

Margot blinked at me. In our family, I understood suddenly, I was a rumor, a cautionary tale, a chair left empty. I was not supposed to have a voice. Voices were for people who'd stayed.

"Don't do that," I said again, steadier now. "Don't turn him into a con artist because it's easier than turning him into what he actually is."

"And what is that, Juniper?"

"The son he chose," I said, "which tells you everything about the children he was given."

The silence afterward had texture. Halloran took his glasses off and polished them, a man giving the room privacy while pretending to be busy. Margot's face performed several small calculations and settled on wounded dignity.

"You have no idea what you're talking about," she said to me, softly, and there — under the softness — was something that sounded almost like fear. "None. You were a child. You know nothing about what that name cost this family."

*That name.* Thornbury-Vane. The name nobody used. The name the stranger at the funeral had said like a password, the name on photographs my mother had hidden, the name Margot had just reached for and snatched back like a child caught near a stove.

"What name?" I asked. "What did it cost?"

But Margot had already closed the door behind her eyes. She gathered her papers, wished Halloran a good afternoon in a voice like frosted glass, and paused at the door just long enough to say, to me, not to the room:

"Stay out of the practice room, Juniper. Some things were locked for a reason."

And then she was gone, trailing lemon polish and old money, leaving the rest of us sitting in the residue of a will that had given away a building, a fortune in instruments, and — to me — two words and a locked box.

* * *

Elias walked me out. Neither of us suggested it; we simply ended up side by side on the courthouse steps, two people whose inheritance, combined, amounted to a piano, a violin, an envelope, and an imperative.

"Finish it," he said. "That's what he wrote you?"

"That's what he wrote me."

"Do you know what *it* is?"

I thought about lying. It was my native language, after all; I'd spoken it fluently for two decades, hiding a famous name behind a lanyard, hiding a famous childhood behind a mop. But Elias had stood in Margot's crosshairs and hadn't flinched, and there was something about a person who takes a hit meant for you that makes lying feel like spitting.

"I think so," I said. "And I think it's the reason I'm terrified to open that envelope."

He nodded slowly, and then he said the thing that hooked itself into me and didn't let go for the rest of that winter:

"When I was twelve, he made me play scales for four hours because I'd rushed a passage. At the end he said, 'Speed is just fear wearing nice shoes.' I thought it was about music." He looked out at the street, at the ordinary traffic going about its ordinary business. "It took me sixteen years to figure out he was talking about himself."

We stood there a moment longer, two orphans of the same silence, and then he touched his hat-brim — an odd, formal, old-fashioned gesture I'd later learn he got from my father — and walked away down the steps.

Behind me, in my bag, the lawyer's copy of the envelope inventory listed a single item, sealed, marked in faded pencil with a name I hadn't answered to since I was seventeen.

*June-bug.*

I went home. I did not sleep. And at four in the morning I found myself standing outside the conservatory with my key card, looking up at the dark windows of the practice wing, hearing my aunt's voice: *some things were locked for a reason.*

The trouble with reasons, I thought, letting myself in, is that they're almost never good ones.
