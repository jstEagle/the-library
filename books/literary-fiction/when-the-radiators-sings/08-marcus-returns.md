## 8. Marcus Returns

I flew in on a Tuesday because Tuesdays are cheap and because efficiency is the only religion I've kept. Land at 11:40, sign the listing paperwork by 2:00, meet the auction house people at 3:30, dinner alone at a bar with a good bourbon list by 7:00. That was the plan. I'd built the plan on the plane, in the seat with the extra legroom I pay for, and I want to state for the record that the plan was good. The plan was airtight. The plan did not include Juniper Sandoval sleeping in the back bedroom of 44 Marigold Street like a squatter with a college degree.

My mother bought that house in 1998, which I know because I paid for it—not the purchase, the *keeping* of it. Every year the tax bill came and every year I paid it and told myself it was the cost of being a decent son. Then two years ago something in me did the math differently. The house was worth four hundred and change, the taxes were nine grand a year, and my mother was calling me by her dead sister's name more often than by mine. I let the notices pile up. I told myself it was an oversight. An oversight is a thing you do once by accident. I did it for twenty-six months on purpose, and I have a spreadsheet to prove it, which is the most honest thing about the whole arrangement.

The county auction was supposed to be a formality. My plan was to bid, take title, list, sell, and be on a plane back to Phoenix inside of a month with a check and a clear conscience, or at least a conscience that had been laundered enough to pass inspection.

So when I pulled up to the curb at 4:15—running late, because the auction house people wanted to talk about *staging*, a word I have come to hate—and saw a light on in the upstairs window, I assumed a realtor had left something running. Then I saw the garden.

My mother's garden had died the year after she moved into the memory care place. I'd driven past it a dozen times since, and it had been a brown, defeated thing, weeds standing where the tomatoes used to stand like they were proud of the achievement. Now somebody had pulled the weeds. Somebody had staked the tomato cages back up, and there were green things in them, ambitious green things, and a watering can sitting by the porch steps with water still beading on its spout.

I sat in the rental car for a minute. This is a thing I do now, sit in cars before entering buildings. My therapist—yes, I have a therapist, Marcus Amaro, developer, has a therapist, put it in the newsletter—says it's a transitional behavior, that I'm giving my nervous system time to catch up to my calendar. I say it's the only part of my day where nobody wants anything from me. Either way, I sat, and I looked at that garden, and I felt the first hairline crack in the plan.

The front door was unlocked. I want you to understand what that means in a house that's been vacant. An unlocked door on a vacant house is a liability. An unlocked door is a code violation of the soul. I pushed it open and called out, "Hello?" in the voice I use on contractors, the one that says I own the problem you're standing inside of.

"Kitchen," said a voice I hadn't heard in fifteen years.

* *

Juniper Sandoval was standing at my mother's counter with a clipboard, because of course she was. Juniper with a clipboard is the most Juniper thing in the universe, more Juniper than Juniper herself. She was wearing work clothes and reading glasses I didn't know she owned, and she had annotated something, because there were notes in three colors of ink on whatever was clipped to that board, and when she looked up at me she did not look surprised, which annoyed me more than surprise would have.

"You're Marcus," she said.

"You're living in my house."

"Your mother's house." She took off the glasses. "Legally, the county's house. Morally—" She shrugged. "I was going to say morally it's nobody's, but that's not true either."

"Is that an inspection report?" I nodded at the clipboard. "Because if you're doing a pre-auction inspection, that's actually helpful, I can—"

"I'm not doing it for you."

"Then who's it for?"

She looked at me the way you look at a man who has asked a question he already knows the answer to and is hoping the answer will change if he asks it out loud. "For the house," she said. "Somebody should know what's wrong with it before they decide what it's worth."

And there it is. That's the whole woman, right there, in case you were wondering. Juniper Sandoval has spent her entire adult life learning exactly what things are worth and refusing, with a stubbornness I can only describe as religious, to say it out loud. I know this because my mother told me, every Christmas, every phone call, every time Juniper's name came up, which was often, because my mother collected people the way other women collect spoons, and Juniper was the spoon she was proudest of.

"Are you eating?" Juniper asked. "There's soup."

"There's *soup*."

"I made soup. It's what you do when there's a kitchen."

"It's what *she* did when there was a kitchen," I said, and heard my own voice do something ugly on the word *she*, and Juniper heard it too, and neither of us said anything for a second, and in that second I understood that we were going to have a lot of these seconds, she and I, two people standing in the wreckage of the same woman's love, counting the pieces.

"I'll have soup," I said.

* *

Here is what I saw, over soup, while I pretended I wasn't taking inventory, because inventory is what I do, it's the whole job, you walk into a property and your eyes just *do* it, square footage, sightlines, deferred maintenance, comps:

The house is a disaster. The foundation has a crack in the southeast corner you could lose a cat in. There's water staining on the ceiling of the front room shaped, I swear to God, like the Virgin Mary, which the right buyer would pay a premium for and the wrong buyer would use as a negotiation lever. The porch rails are rotten. The wiring is a museum exhibit. As a property, 44 Marigold Street is a tear-down wearing a costume, and any appraiser with a clipboard—and Juniper would appreciate this—would tell you the land is the only real value.

Here is what else I saw:

A sleeping bag and a pillow on the bed in the room that used to be Juniper's, back when my mother's house ran on a system I never fully understood, a system in which the back bedroom belonged to whichever cousin had been most recently abandoned and the good couch belonged to whoever had been most recently dumped and my room—the only room with a door that locked—belonged to me, the actual child of the actual owner, a distinction that sounds like it should have meant something and meant almost nothing.

A mug in the dish rack with a chip on the handle. I knew that mug. I had chipped that mug, in 2003, drying it too fast, and my mother had said, "Now it has a story," which is what she said about everything broken, which is why nothing in that house ever got thrown away, which is why the garage was a fire hazard.

A pair of Juniper's boots by the door, set side by side, toes out, the way my mother used to line up all our shoes like we were a family instead of a holding pattern.

"Your mother would have hated this," I said, meaning the water stain, the crack, the rot. "She'd have been up on the roof herself with a tube of caulk and a prayer."

"She'd have loved the stain," Juniper said. "She'd have put a candle under it."

"That's what I said."

"That's not what you said."

* *

I stayed at the Marriott because I am not a man who sleeps in properties he doesn't own, and also because I needed a door that locked, old habits. But I came back Wednesday morning with coffee, because I told myself I needed to walk the property with her, get her read on the inspection, be thorough. Thoroughness. Efficiency. The liturgy.

She was on the roof.

I want to be clear: Juniper Sandoval, bridge inspector, was on the roof of 44 Marigold Street at eight in the morning, in a harness she'd clearly bought used, and when I came around the side of the house she looked down at me with the mild expression of a person observing weather.

"There's soft decking on the north slope," she called down. "About four feet by six. The rest is fine for another five years if nobody lets a gutter clog again."

"Get down from there."

"In a minute."

"Juniper. If you fall on this property, it's my liability."

She came down the ladder with the unhurried competence of someone who does this for a living, which she does, and stood in front of me, and took the coffee I was holding out before I'd fully decided to offer it, which was somehow both rude and gracious.

"Liability," she said, tasting the word. "That's what this house is to you. A liability with a listing price."

"It's an asset with problems."

"Same thing," she said, "depending on whether you're the one holding it."

We walked the property. She showed me everything—the crack, the stain, the wiring, a patch of the basement floor that flexed underfoot in a way that made even me, a man who has walked on a thousand condemned floors, want to step lightly. And the whole time she was doing it, I was watching her do it, and the whole time I was watching her do it, I was aware that this was the trap. Because here's the thing about Juniper Sandoval: my mother didn't just take her in. My mother *made* her, the way you make anything, out of what's lying around. Juniper came to that house at eleven years old with a mother in prison and a face like a closed door, and my mother looked at that closed door and said, "This one stays," and then spent ten years doing the slow carpentry of getting it to open. I was already there when Juniper arrived. I was seventeen and furious about my father's absence, and I resented every stray my mother brought home because every stray was proof that my mother's love was not a thing you were born to but a thing she *gave away*, freely, to whoever showed up broken enough.

I left at nineteen. I have been leaving at nineteen for thirty years.

"You're doing the thing," Juniper said.

"What thing?"

"Where you look at me like I'm a line item."

"I'm looking at you like you're a person who's squatting in a house I intend to sell."

"You're looking at me," she said, "like you're trying to figure out what my price is."

I didn't answer, which was an answer, and she nodded like I'd confirmed something she'd already written on the clipboard in three colors of ink.

* *

Thursday I met with the auction house and set the reserve. I'm not going to tell you the number. I'll tell you this: it was a good number, a number calculated to do exactly what numbers do, which is sort the people in the room into two piles, the ones who can and the ones who can't. I told myself, driving back to the house, that it was just business. I have told myself that sentence so many times it has worn smooth, like a stone in a river, like a thing that used to be sharp and is now easy to hold.

Juniper was on the porch when I got there, sitting on the top step in the last of the light, and she had that look people get when they've been crying and have decided you will not be told about it.

"Ruthie called me," she said.

My mother. Ruthie Amaro. The woman who bought this house to keep it from strangers and then, in the fog, let it slip through her hands, and who calls Juniper some afternoons lucid and some afternoons not, and who today, apparently, had been lucid enough to say something that made Juniper sit on a porch step and cry where she thought no one would see.

"What did she say?"

Juniper looked at me for a long moment. "She said to tell you she knows what you're doing. And that she did it first. And that—" Here Juniper's voice did something I've never heard it do, something unpracticed. "And that the house was never the thing she was keeping. It was the door. And doors are for opening, Marcus, not appraising."

I sat down on the step below her, because my legs decided that without consulting me. The garden smelled like wet dirt and tomato leaves, a smell that reached back through thirty years and grabbed me by the collar. Somewhere down the block a dog was losing an argument with a squirrel.

"I have debts," I said. It came out before I could stop it, and once it was out I heard how it sounded, how it was meant to sound, an explanation, an alibi, a man holding up his spreadsheet to the dark. "You don't know what my last two years look like. You don't know what the market did. You don't—"

"I know you chipped the mug," Juniper said. "In 2003. Drying it too fast. She kept it because it had a story."

I looked at her.

"I know everything that happened in this house," she said quietly. "That's what you don't understand. You left, and you think leaving means it stopped. It didn't stop. She kept going. She took in Alma when Alma's people kicked her out. She took in Dev. She took in Bree's whole situation. She took in *me*, twice, the second time when I was thirty-one and my marriage was already a corpse I hadn't buried. And every single one of us, Marcus, she told us about you. The boy with the locked room. How he'd come back someday. How the door was always—"

She stopped. She didn't say the word. She didn't have to, because the word was *open*, and we both sat there on the steps of the house neither of us owned, in the smell of the garden my mother had planted and Juniper had resurrected, and I understood that I had flown two thousand miles to sell a house and had instead walked into the one property I have never been able to appraise.

"I should get back to the Marriott," I said.

"You should have soup," she said. "There's soup."

There was soup. I had soup. And when I left, past ten, she was standing in the doorway with the light on behind her, and I got in the rental car and sat there—transitional behavior, nervous system, whatever you want to call it—and did not drive away for a long time.

The reserve stands. The plan stands. I fly out Sunday.

But I want it on the record that when I looked up at the house one last time, the light in the back bedroom was still on, and it was her room, and it had always been her room, and some part of me—the part that was seventeen and furious, the part that locked the door—knew exactly what that light was going to cost me, and turned off my phone, and drove to the Marriott anyway.
