## 9. The Auctioneer's Son

The auction house was called Hargrove & Sons, which turned out to be a lie twice over: there was no Hargrove anymore — he'd died in 2011, according to the plaque in the lobby that I read with the particular attention of a person avoiding eye contact — and there were no sons, plural. There was one son. Elias. He sat behind a desk that had been his father's, in an office that smelled like lemon oil and old paper, and he looked at me the way librarians look at someone who's been reshelving books by the color of their spines.

"You're the Sandoval estate," he said. Not a question. "Yara Sandoval."

"That's me."

"Sit down." He gestured at a chair upholstered in something that had once been green. "Can I get you coffee? It's terrible. It's the only honest thing about this building."

"I'll take it."

He poured two cups from a machine that groaned like it resented its job, and handed mine over, and for a moment we just held our terrible coffees in the kind of silence I usually charge rent for. But this wasn't my library. This was his territory, and he let the silence sit there between us like a third party at a negotiation.

"My father sold your father's hardware store," Elias said. "Nineteen ninety-eight. You probably don't remember."

"I remember the store."

"Everybody remembers the store. Nobody remembers the sale." He smiled, thin and professional. "That's the business, mostly. Things people loved become line items. My whole job is making sure nobody cries until after the check clears."

I drank the terrible coffee. It was terrible in a committed way, the way things are when they've stopped trying to please anyone.

"I'm here about the house," I said. "On Radiator Street."

"I know why you're here. You're here to ask me to delay it, or derail it, or discover some fatal flaw in the process that lets you walk out of here with your childhood intact." He set his cup down. "And I'm going to tell you, before anything else, that I can't do any of those things. The notice went out. The creditors filed. The county has a date. That machinery doesn't have a reverse gear."

"Okay," I said.

"But," he said.

There it was. The *but*. In fifteen years of cataloguing silences I had developed a fine ear for them, and Elias's *but* was not a silence at all — it was a door, propped open on purpose, waiting to see if I'd walk through it.

* * *

Here is what I knew going in, because I had spent four days in the house and three nights failing to sleep in it: the estate was solvent on paper and drowning in practice. My father had died owing eleven thousand dollars in medical debt and back taxes, and the house — assessed, generously, at sixty-one thousand — would go to auction to cover it, plus the county's fees, plus Hargrove & Sons' commission, plus whatever else attaches itself to a dead man's name like burrs. Whatever was left over would be split between Danny and me, per the will, which my father had drafted himself on a legal form he'd bought at the same hardware store he used to own. The will was one page long. It said, in effect: *the house pays what I owe, and the children split the rest.* It did not say which children. It did not account for the fact that one of them lived in the basement of the asset being liquidated.

Danny hadn't signed anything. Of course he hadn't. Signing meant agreeing that the house could be sold, and the house was his ledger made physical, his proof, his exhibit A in a case he'd been arguing to the neighborhood for fifteen years. Every day he didn't sign was another entry in the column marked *what they owe me.*

So I'd come to the auction house the way you go to a doctor whose diagnosis you already know, hoping for a second opinion that says something kinder.

Elias gave me the second opinion.

"There's a provision," he said, leaning back until his chair complained. "Section 4-something-or-other, I'd have to pull the exact citation, but it exists. If an heir files a petition claiming the decedent lacked testamentary capacity — that he wasn't competent when he executed the will — the court can stay the sale pending review. Ninety days, sometimes more. Sometimes the review kills the sale entirely."

My heart did something unprofessional. "What does the review involve?"

"Evaluations. Records. Testimony." He watched me over the rim of his cup. "You'd need a doctor willing to say your father couldn't understand what he was signing. You'd need to stand up in front of a judge and say it too. Under oath."

"He was sick," I said. "At the end. He was — "

"Was he incompetent?" Elias asked. Gently, almost. The way you ask a question you already know the answer to, so the other person has to be the one who says it out loud.

I thought about my father signing that will. I thought about the handwriting — steady, careful, the same hand that had kept forty years of receipts in labeled boxes. I thought about how he'd written *the children* without distinguishing between them, as if we were interchangeable, as if fifteen years of silence and one basement apartment evened out into a fair division. He understood exactly what he was signing. That was the unbearable part. He'd understood everything, always, and had simply decided not to say it.

"No," I said. "He wasn't incompetent."

"No," Elias agreed. "By all accounts he was sharp as a tack right up until the last month. I've seen the medical records. His doctor will say so under oath if anyone asks." He spread his hands. "But the petition doesn't require you to win. It requires you to file. And filing buys you time. Ninety days is ninety days. Houses burn down in ninety days. Buyers walk away. Counties lose paperwork. Miracles happen, occasionally, though I wouldn't build a retirement plan on them."

"And if it fails?"

"Then the sale happens anyway, later, and you've spent money you don't have on a lawyer, and everyone in that courtroom knows you stood up and called your dead father a fool to buy yourself a season." He shrugged. "Also, technically, you'd have committed what the court calls a bad-faith filing, which carries penalties, which I mention only because I'm contractually obligated to mention it."

The radiator in his office — an old cast-iron thing, painted institutional green, ticking softly to itself — chose that moment to knock twice, the way radiators do, like a fist on a door from the inside. I flinched. Elias noticed. He was the kind of man who noticed things; it was probably genetic.

* * *

Let me be honest about what happened next, because honesty is supposed to be the theme of this whole miserable homecoming, and I might as well start practicing in low-stakes rooms.

I wanted it.

Not the truth of it — I knew it was a lie before Elias finished describing it. What I wanted was the shape of it. The clean geometry. Ninety days. A season bought with a signature instead of a confession. I sat there doing the arithmetic I'd been doing since the letter arrived, the arithmetic where every path led through a room I didn't want to enter, and here, suddenly, was a corridor around it. File the petition. Stall the clock. Let the winter pass. Let Vance — the developer who'd already sent a letter sniffing around the property, whom I'd met exactly once and already disliked on principle — find another block to pave. Let Danny keep his basement and his ledger and his story. Let me go back to the library, to the 3 a.m. quiet, to the inventory of unsaid things, which would grow by exactly one entry: *To Dad: I called you incompetent to save myself, and you weren't even alive to hear it.*

It was such a tidy lie. That's what seduced me — not the money, not even the time, but the tidiness. Lies are seductive in direct proportion to how much administrative work they save you. The truth required a hearing, witnesses, Marcus, the letters I hadn't found yet, Danny's face across a kitchen table. The lie required a form and a filing fee.

"How much time could I really get?" I heard myself ask.

Elias studied me for a long moment. Then he got up, closed his office door — a gesture that should have warned me, and did warn me, and which I ignored, because that's what doors are for when you're already halfway through them — and sat down again with his elbows on his father's desk.

"Between us," he said, "my father built this business on complicated estates. The messy ones. The ones with two brothers who hate each other and a will written on a napkin. I inherited the clients along with the desk." He tapped a folder on the blotter. "Your file came across my desk three weeks ago. I've been waiting for you to come in, because they always come in. The heir who wants to stop the machine. And I'll tell you what I tell all of them, which is more than I'm obligated to tell you."

"Which is?"

"The technicality works. I've seen it work four times. Three of those four, the family ended up worse off than if they'd let the sale happen — more debt, more lawyers, more years of living inside a lawsuit instead of a life. The fourth time, a woman saved her mother's farm, and she deserved it, because her claim was true." He looked at me steadily. "Your claim isn't true. You know it isn't. I can see you know it. So the question isn't whether the technicality works. The question is what kind of person files it anyway, knowing that."

The radiator knocked again. Twice. Patient. Like it had all the time in the world and expected me to use it well.

"Why are you telling me this?" I asked. "If you profit either way — the commission comes out of the sale whenever it happens —"

"Because my father spent thirty years selling other people's pasts and died owing money himself, and I've had a lot of time to think about what that means." Elias smiled, and for the first time it reached his eyes, and it was a tired smile, an end-of-shift smile. "Because somebody in this town should occasionally tell the truth in a room where lying is cheaper. Consider it a hobby."

* * *

I walked out of Hargrove & Sons with the terrible coffee still half-drunk behind me and the petition form folded in my coat pocket — Elias had given it to me anyway, saying, "Take it. Temptation keeps better in a pocket than in a drawer." Which was either wisdom or cruelty, and I suspected, in him, the distinction had worn away years ago.

Outside, Radiator Street was six blocks east, and I walked toward it without deciding to, the way water finds the low ground. The afternoon was the gray kind that rust-belt towns manufacture locally, and the houses sat in it with their shoulders up. Mrs. Okonkwo was scraping ice off her steps and nodded at me, and I nodded back, and neither of us added anything to the conversation, which was, for this street, practically a summit.

Danny's window — the basement window, the one with the blanket tacked over it that I'd pretended not to see — glowed faintly as I passed. He was down there. He was always down there, keeping his accounts, telling his version to anyone who'd hold still for it. And I stood on the sidewalk outside my childhood home holding a form that would let me steal ninety more days of this stalemate, and I understood, with the clarity that apparently only arrives when you're standing in cold air holding a lie in your pocket, that the petition wasn't really about the house at all.

It was about never having to take the stand.

Because the estate hearing — the one Elias described, the one where claims got aired and testimony got given — was coming regardless. The auction required it. And at that hearing, Danny would tell his story, the polished one, the one the diner already served with eggs: the noble brother who stayed, the selfish one who fled, the mother who abandoned them, the father who drank. Fifteen years of his ledger, entered into the public record. And I would sit there and let it happen, the way I'd let it happen at every kitchen table and church basement and barstool for fifteen years, adding entries to the inventory, keeping the silence that had once protected him and now only protected me.

Or I wouldn't.

The form crinkled in my pocket when I moved. I took it out, right there on the sidewalk, and looked at it — just a county document, boxes and lines, a small bureaucratic machine for manufacturing time. All I had to do was check a box asserting something false about a dead man who had never once lied to me, who had hidden my mother's letters in a wall rather than let us read them, who had — no. I didn't know that yet. I didn't know about the letters yet. All I knew then was the box, and the pen in my breast pocket, and the strange gravity of easy exits.

I didn't fill it out. I didn't throw it away either. I folded it back along its creases and put it in my pocket, because I am not a good person in a single decisive scene; I am a librarian, and I shelve things, and I tell myself I'll deal with them later.

From the basement window, the glow shifted. Danny, moving past the glass. He couldn't have seen me — the blanket covered most of it — but I stood there a beat longer than sense allowed, the way you pause outside a door when you think someone inside might be listening.

Then my phone buzzed. Noor. Third call today, unanswered. I watched her name light up the screen — *Noor (do not answer while lying to yourself)* was not actually what the contact said, but it might as well have been — and I put the phone back in my pocket, next to the form, where the temptation and the friendship lay together quietly, both of them waiting to see what I'd do.

Behind me, in the walls of the house on Radiator Street, something ticked. Old pipes. Old heat. Or the house itself, counting.
