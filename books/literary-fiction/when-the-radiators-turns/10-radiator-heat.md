## 10. Radiator Heat

The cold came down from Canada like a debt collector, no warning, no courtesy call. I heard it before I felt it — the pipes in the walls ticking a different rhythm than usual, faster, like something small and panicked running through the house's veins.

I'd been living in the basement for six years by then, long enough to know the house's moods the way you know a dog's. The furnace had been dying for three winters. Dad used to kick it — actually kick it, once, with his work boot, and say, "Not yet, you son of a bitch," and the thing would wheeze back to life out of what I can only assume was spite or fear. Dad was dead four months now, and nobody was kicking anything, and at eleven that night the boiler gave its last cough and quit.

I knew it because the silence changed. A house this old is never quiet — there's always water moving somewhere, always the radiator's slow conversation with itself. When that stops, the quiet underneath it is a different animal. It's the quiet of something dead.

I went upstairs to check the thermostat, mostly as an excuse to move my legs. That's when I saw the light under the kitchen door. Yara was up. Of course Yara was up. My sibling kept hours like a hospital or a lighthouse — awake at all the wrong times, shining at nothing.

I should have gone back downstairs. Six years of practice going back downstairs. But the basement was already forty degrees and falling, and I told myself it was about the temperature, which was true, and not about the light, which was also true, and I opened the kitchen door.

Yara sat at the table with a notebook and two cups of tea, and I swear on everything I own — which, if we're being honest per the ledger, isn't much — one of those cups was facing the empty chair across from them. Facing the door. Facing me.

"Boiler's dead," I said.

"I know. I've been listening to it die for an hour."

"It does a whole performance."

"So did Dad." They pushed the second cup an inch toward me. Chamomile. They remembered. Fifteen years, and they remembered how I took my tea, and neither of us said anything about that, because some facts are too heavy to pick up casually.

I sat down. The chair didn't creak, but I heard it anyway.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about grief: it doesn't heat evenly. It pools in rooms. The kitchen was our mother's room. She died in it every day for years before she actually left — standing at that stove, humming off-key, burning the rice on purpose because she claimed she liked it crunchy, which was a lie she told so often it became true, which is a thing our family has always been good at.

The cold made the kitchen honest. You could see your breath in it. Yara and I sat there steaming like two cups of coffee ourselves, and outside the wind found the gap under the back door and played it like a reed.

"We should call someone," Yara said.

"At midnight? Emergency rate's triple."

"You have triple?"

"I have a ledger," I said, "not a fortune." This got the smallest huff out of them, which I counted as a victory and entered nowhere, because the ledger only holds debts, and I wasn't sure yet whether a laugh from Yara Sandoval was a credit or a debit.

We sat. The wind worked the door. And then the radiators started.

That's the thing people don't understand about steam heat — when the boiler dies, the pipes don't go silent all at once. There's residual pressure, pockets of it, and as the system cools the water finds its way down and the air finds its way up and the radiators knock and groan and tick for hours, a whole dying language, spoken slower and slower until it stops.

Ours started up around midnight. Clank. Pause. Clank-clank. Pause. Tick-tick-tick-tick, fading.

And Yara looked up, and I looked up, and we both heard it: *da-da-DUM, da-da-da-da-dum.*

"Oh no," Yara said.

"Don't," I said.

"The Radiator Song," they said, because Yara has never once in their life left a thing alone when leaving it alone was an option.

There was a game, when we were kids. Our mother invented it, the winter the boiler first started failing, because she couldn't afford the repair and refused to let us be scared of the noise. Every time the radiator knocked, she'd freeze mid-motion — stirring, folding, whatever — and announce, "The radiator's talking. What's it saying?" And we'd have to translate. Danny says it wants soup. Danny says it's mad at the bathtub. One night I said, "It's saying Mama's laugh sounds like that," and she laughed exactly like the radiator, clank-clank, and then cried a little into the dishwater, and I was eight and didn't know yet that those two things could live in the same person at the same time.

"It's saying," I said, against my better judgment, "that somebody owes it money."

Yara's mouth twitched. "It's saying the mortgage is paid but the love wasn't."

"That's dark for a children's game."

"We were dark children."

*Clank,* said the radiator, obligingly. *Da-da-DUM.*

"Mama's laugh," we both said, at the same time, in the same flat tone, and then neither of us spoke for a while, because the game had done what games do — opened a door we'd boarded over — and the draft coming through it was worse than the cold.

* * *

Around one in the morning, Yara got up and found the quilts in the hall closet. Mom's quilts. She made them out of Dad's old work shirts, which he pretended to hate and secretly loved, wearing his own sleeves around his shoulders on Sundays. Yara dropped one on my shoulders without ceremony and wrapped the other around themselves, and we must have looked like two ghosts who'd agreed to haunt the same kitchen.

"There's candles in the drawer," I said. "Dad kept them for storms."

"He kept them for fifteen years of storms that never came."

"They came. He just didn't light them."

Yara lit one anyway, set it on the table between us, and the flame threw our shadows up the cabinets, huge and wavering, two giants sitting very still. In candlelight everyone looks like they're about to confess something. Maybe that's why churches use them.

"Mrs. Okonkwo brought me a casserole today," Yara said. "She also informed me, in the manner of a woman delivering a subpoena, that you've been living here. In the basement. For years."

"Six."

"Six." They turned the notebook toward themselves but didn't write. "You could have called me."

"Could you have picked up?"

The candle flickered. Somewhere in the walls, water settled with a sigh.

"That's fair," Yara said finally, which surprised me. Fifteen years ago, that sentence would have started a war. Grief ages people in dog years, maybe. Or maybe Yara had spent fifteen years in a library learning what I'd learned in a basement: that most fights are just two people guarding the same wound from opposite sides.

"Why the basement?" they asked. "The house was empty. The bedrooms were empty."

Because the bedrooms were theirs, I didn't say. Because Mom's room still had her hairpins in the drawer and I couldn't walk past it every day. Because Dad slept ten feet above me and I listened to his heart get slower for six years like a man monitoring a stock he couldn't sell. Because the basement was where I put the ledger, and the ledger needed darkness the way mushrooms do.

"Drafts," I said. "I like drafts."

"You're lying."

"I'm editing."

That got the huff again. Two laughs in one night. At this rate I'd have to open a new page in the ledger.

* * *

Here is what I want written down somewhere, even if only here, even if only in the margin of my own head: I did not plan what happened next. I want that on the record, because later — after the hearing, after everything — people will say Danny Sandoval set a trap, laid a snare of cold air and chamomile and childhood songs. I didn't. Traps require a hunter who knows what he wants. All I knew was that the kitchen was freezing and my sibling was warm-shaped across the table and the radiator was speaking our mother's laugh and I was so tired, so bone-tired, of carrying a story alone.

"Tell me about the library," I said instead. Coward's question. Safe question.

So they did. Quietly, in the voice people use in libraries even when they're not in one. About the 3 a.m. patrons — the insomniacs, the students, the man who comes in every Tuesday to read cookbooks he never cooks from. About the sound of the book carts at night. About how they catalogue silences, which I thought was a metaphor until they explained it was practically a job description: the gaps in conversations, the pauses in audiobooks, the shelf where nobody ever stands.

"You inventory silence," I said.

"Somebody has to. Nobody else counts it."

"Mom counted it."

Yara went still. The candle leaned.

"She used to say," I went on, and my voice did something strange, went low and careful like I was walking on the ice over the creek, "that the loudest thing in this house was everything we didn't say. She'd stand right there—" I pointed at the spot by the stove, stupidly, as if the spot could testify— "and she'd say, 'This kitchen is full of unsaid things, Danny. You could furnish a whole other house with them.'"

"And then she furnished a whole other life with them," Yara said. Flat. The old flatness, the armor-plated version.

And there it was. The fork in the road, sitting right there on the table between the candle and the cold cup of chamomile. I could take the fork. I could say: *That's not what happened, Yara. I know what really happened that night. I've known for fifteen years, and I let you carry it, and I built a ledger out of letting you carry it, and I am so far down that road that the road is the only home I have.* I could say it, and the kitchen would fill up with unsaid things becoming said things, and the pressure change might crack the windows.

Or I could stay quiet, and keep the story, because the story was the only thing I owned outright, free and clear, no lien against it.

The radiator knocked. *Clank.* Mama's laugh.

"What are you doing here, Yara?" I asked. Not the question. A smaller one, a decoy. "Really. You had fifteen years. You had a whole city. Why come back for a house?"

They looked at the candle for a long time. Long enough that I thought they wouldn't answer, and I recognized the look, because I wear it daily — the look of a person deciding how much truth the room can hold.

"Because houses remember," they said. "And I wanted to hear what this one remembers before someone else owns the memory."

Well. That was almost it. That was circling it like a hawk circles.

"Yara." My voice cracked on the second syllable and I hated it. "If you're going to tell the town a story about this family — at the hearing, whenever — I need to know which version you're telling."

"Which version do you want?"

"I want the one that's true."

They looked at me then, straight on, and their eyes in the candlelight were Mom's eyes, exact, and I understood that they were asking me the same question back, silently, the way our mother used to ask things — by waiting, by making the silence so loud you had to fill it.

I didn't fill it. I stood up, said the boiler guy opens at seven, said goodnight in the voice you use for strangers at bus stops, and took my quilt down to a basement that had dropped below fifty degrees, where I lay on my cot fully dressed under two blankets and stared at the ceiling beams and listened to the house cool above me, tick by tick by tick, our mother's laugh slowing down, slowing down, almost stopped.

Almost.

Because near dawn I heard footsteps overhead — Yara, pacing the kitchen, pacing and stopping, pacing and stopping — and then the scratch of a pen, fast, urgent, the sound of someone writing something down before it could be taken back.

In the morning there was a note on the basement door. Four words, in Yara's small librarian handwriting:

*Marcus called. We should talk.*

I read it twice. Then I went and checked the ledger, because my hands wanted something familiar to hold, and I noticed — for the first time in six years — that the last page was still unfinished. Half a sentence, waiting.

I wondered, standing there in the cold, who was supposed to finish it.
