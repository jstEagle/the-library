## 2. A Funeral in a Sleepless City

The city did not pause for my mother's death, which was fitting, since it had never paused for her life either. At six a.m. on the morning of the funeral I stood at the window of her house—my house now, though the paperwork hadn't caught up to the fact—and watched the delivery vans make their rounds below, the joggers threading through the gray light, the bakery on the corner already exhaling its first warm breath of bread into the cold. Somewhere a jackhammer started up, right on schedule, as if grief were a shift change and the world had clocked in.

I had slept four hours. This felt generous.

The funeral home had asked me, twice, whether I wanted an open casket. I said no both times with the same smile I used on patrons who wanted to argue about overdue fines—a smile that ends conversations. My mother would have appreciated that. She spent seventy-one years perfecting the art of being unlooked-at, and she would have hated nothing more than an audience at the end of it, all those eyes on her face, reading her like a page she hadn't approved for circulation.

So we burned her closed, and I wore the black dress I kept for occasions I refused to have, and I drove across town to a crematorium that smelled faintly of lilies and floor wax, and I counted the chairs.

That's the thing nobody tells you about funerals in a city that never sleeps: attendance is a lottery. Half the people who might have come were working nights. The other half had moved away years ago, or died themselves, or simply drifted out of orbit the way people do when the gravity holding them together was always one person's quiet effort. My mother had been the sun of a very small solar system, and most of the planets had long ago achieved escape velocity.

Eleven chairs filled. Twelve, counting the man in the back row.

But I'm getting ahead of myself.

* * *

The service was set for ten, which meant that by nine-forty-five I was standing at the front of the small chapel doing what I do best: arranging things. Hymnals squared. Order-of-service cards fanned at precise intervals. The framed photograph of my mother—I'd chosen one from maybe fifteen years back, before the gray took over completely, when her face still remembered how to be surprised—centered on its easel with the obsessive care of a woman shelving books by a system only she understood.

Daniel arrived at nine-fifty, smelling of cologne applied in a moving car. He kissed my cheek, squeezed my shoulder, and said, "You okay?" in the tone of a man asking about weather in another country. I said I was fine. We are a family of fine. We could publish an anthology.

"You should sit," he said. "You don't need to fuss."

Fussing is not what this is, I wanted to tell him. Fussing implies anxiety. This is inventory. When the world loses its shape, you count what's left. It's why I'm good at my job. Every night at the Verrone Library I walk the stacks and count—the returned, the misfiled, the missing—and the counting is how I know the night has held. That the walls are where I left them. That nothing else has been taken.

My mother died on a Tuesday, between the second pot of coffee and the first patron of the evening, and the first thing I did after hanging up the phone was reshelve a biography of some minor industrialist that a man had abandoned face-down on the reading table. Spine cracked. I remember thinking: she would hate that. And then I thought: I am shelving a book while my mother dies somewhere in the space between the ringing and the answering, and this is apparently who I am.

The chaplain was a hired stranger, a pleasant round man with a voice like warm milk. He'd met my mother once, for forty minutes, three days before she died. He spoke about her anyway, fluently, the way professionals do—"a woman of quiet dignity," "devoted to her family," "a private soul"—and every sentence was true the way a stock photograph is true. Generic. Interchangeable. A eulogy from a kit.

Then it was my turn.

* * *

I want to be honest about the eulogy, because honesty is the whole point of what came later, and because the eulogy was the last performance of the old regime, the final act of a government that had ruled our family for forty years. Its policy was simple: say less. Its currency was silence. And I was its most loyal civil servant.

I walked to the podium. Eleven faces turned toward me—twelve, but I hadn't done the full count yet—and I saw Daniel in the front row with his hands folded like a schoolboy, and Mrs. Okafor from next door, who had brought my mother soup every winter solstice for two decades without ever being invited inside, and a scattering of others whose names I knew in the way you know the names of seasonal weather patterns.

"I'm Rosalind," I said. "Rosalind Blackwood. Margaret was my mother."

Even that felt like a disclosure. Even that felt like handing out copies of a restricted document.

Here is what I said:

"My mother was born in 1951, in a house she described to me exactly once, and then never again. She liked her tea strong enough to stand a spoon in. She read the obituaries first, every morning, and claimed it was to 'check who got out.' She worked thirty years as a bookkeeper and balanced every ledger down to the penny, and I believe she considered arithmetic a moral discipline. She raised two children. She was careful with money and more careful with words. She taught me that a person can survive almost anything if they keep their accounts in order."

Here is what I did not say:

I did not say where the house was. I did not say her maiden name, or that I wasn't entirely sure *was* her maiden name. I did not say anything about my father, because there was no slot for him in the official record—he was a closed stack, a reference-only item, requested and denied so many times in my childhood that I stopped requesting around age nine. I did not say that she had a scar on her left forearm she never explained, or that she flinched—not visibly, not dramatically, just a small recalibration of the face—whenever anyone struck a match near her. I did not say that she once, when I was seven, called me by a name that wasn't mine, in a voice that wasn't hers, and went white as paper when I corrected her, and made me promise—swear, hand on the Bible we kept for no discernible religious purpose—that I would never repeat it.

I did not say that I loved her and resented her in equal measure, and that the resentment was mostly a form of love with nowhere to go.

"She was private," I told the eleven faces. "She believed privacy was a kindness. To herself, and to us. Some people will tell you secrets are burdens. My mother believed they were architecture. You build your house out of them, and you live in it, and you keep the roof repaired, and you don't ask the house why it was built this way."

Mrs. Okafor dabbed her eyes. Daniel looked at his shoes. The warm-milk chaplain nodded along as though I were delivering scripture.

And in the back row, a man watched me the way you watch a door you're not sure is locked.

* * *

I noticed him during the hymn, which was a mistake on someone's part—there was no hymn my mother had ever sanctioned, so I'd picked one at random from the chapel's book, and we sang it badly, eleven voices unsure of the melody, and he didn't sing at all.

He sat in the last row, slightly off-center, the way people sit when they want to see everything and be seen by no one. Dark coat, no umbrella despite the drizzle outside, hair that suggested a barber rather than a mirror. Forty-ish, maybe older—it was hard to tell, because he had one of those faces that seemed to be waiting for permission to show its age. He wasn't crying. Everyone else was crying, or performing the adjacent behaviors—sniffing, throat-clearing, the strategic study of order-of-service cards—but he was doing none of it. He was watching me.

Not looking. Watching. There's a difference, and librarians know it. Looking is passive; it happens to you. Watching has intent behind it, a question behind the eyes, and his question had a shape I recognized from decades of night shifts: he was checking something. Comparing. The way a patron checks a call number against a shelf, verifying that the item matches its record.

He was checking me against something.

I did what any reasonable woman does when a stranger audits her at her mother's funeral: I decided I was imagining it. Grief makes the mind inventive. I'd once become convinced, around hour nine of a bad night shift, that the portrait of Aldous Verrone himself was following me with his eyes, and I'd apologized to it aloud before catching myself. The mind, unsupervised, invents company.

But he kept doing it. Through the chaplain's closing words, through the moment when the curtains drew back and the coffin slid into the wall with a hydraulic sigh—my mother, the great architect of silence, disappearing into the building's own architecture—he kept his eyes on me with a focus that had nothing to do with mourning and everything to do with recognition. Or the search for it.

When the service ended and the room began its slow evacuation, he didn't move. He let the others file past him—Mrs. Okafor pressed my hands and said something kind about strength; Daniel shook the chaplain's hand with the vigor of a man concluding a business deal—and still he sat, until the chapel was nearly empty, and then he rose and stood at the back with his hands in his coat pockets, and for one absurd moment I thought he was going to approach the coffin-curtain and pay respects to a wall.

Instead he looked at me one last time. Directly. No pretense of discretion.

And there was something in his face that undid me, though I couldn't have named it then. Not hostility. Not grief. Something worse: *familiarity*. The look of someone seeing a face they've studied from photographs, or dreams, or some third category I didn't yet know existed. He looked at me the way you look at a house you grew up in, now occupied by strangers.

Then he turned and walked out into the drizzle, and I stood at the front of the chapel beside my mother's empty easel, and felt the first crack go through the composure I'd been laying down like mortar for forty years.

It was a small crack. Barely structural. Just a tremor in the hands as I gathered the order-of-service cards, a wrongness in the arithmetic—eleven mourners, twelve chairs, one face I couldn't shelve anywhere.

"Who was that?" I asked Daniel, in the parking lot, nodding toward the retreating dark coat.

Daniel followed my gaze. And here is the detail I would return to, later, at three in the morning, turning it over like a coin with two identical sides: Daniel didn't squint. Didn't search his memory. He looked at the man's back for exactly one second and said, with a smoothness that should have been my first clue:

"No idea."

* * *

Outside, the city went on not sleeping. Traffic hissed past the crematorium gates. A bus sighed at its stop, swallowed its passengers, moved on. Somewhere nearby the jackhammer from that morning had found a new rhythm, patient as a pulse. Nobody slowed down. Nobody marked the fact that Margaret Blackwood had just been converted to smoke and arithmetic, that a woman who had spent her whole life keeping accounts had finally been settled.

I stood under the portico and watched the man in the dark coat cross the street. He didn't look back. But at the far curb he paused—just briefly, just long enough—and took something from his inside pocket. An envelope, or a card. He checked it against something, some inner ledger, and then put it away, and was gone behind the buses.

"Ready?" Daniel said, keys already in hand.

"One minute," I said.

I needed the minute. I needed to stand in the rain-smell and the traffic-noise and feel the crack widen by a millimeter, the way cracks do when you ignore them, silently, industriously, all on their own. My mother had taught me that a person can survive almost anything if they keep their accounts in order. What she had not taught me—what perhaps no one had taught her—was what to do when the audit comes due. When someone arrives at your door with a record that doesn't match yours, and asks, politely, with terrible patience, to see the originals.

I got in the car. Daniel pulled out into traffic, and the crematorium shrank in the side mirror, and I told myself the man meant nothing, that grief was making me fanciful, that tomorrow I would go back to the library and the night shift and the beautiful, load-bearing silence of my ordinary life.

Three days later, going through my mother's coats for the donation bag, I found an envelope in the lining of the black wool one. Inside was a photograph, old and soft-cornered, of three children on a porch steps in summer light.

Two of them I knew.

The third was the man from the back row.
