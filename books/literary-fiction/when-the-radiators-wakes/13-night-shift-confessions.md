## 13. Night Shift Confessions

*Rosalind*

The storm came in off the river around eleven, the way storms do in this city—without apology, without warning, and with a certain theatrical flair that suggested it had been rehearsing. I was at the front desk reconciling the returns cart when the lights died all at once, and the Verrone Library exhaled into darkness like an old woman finally allowed to stop holding her breath.

I have worked night shifts for thirty years. I know every sound this building makes: the elevator's arthritic groan, the radiator's midnight complaints, the particular creak of the third-floor stacks settling as though the books themselves are trying to get comfortable. What I had never heard, until that night, was silence. Total, electrical silence. The emergency generator, I would learn later, had been knocked out by a transformer fire two blocks over. That night, all I knew was that the library had gone dark and quiet and utterly still, and that somewhere in the reading room, a man who claimed to be my brother was sitting exactly where he always sat.

I found him by candlelight—I keep candles in the desk drawer for occasions I'd never imagined, which is perhaps why I'd never imagined them—and he didn't startle when the flame found his face. He just looked up from the book in his hands, some water-stained history of the city's transit system, and said, "Well. Now we're even."

"Even for what?"

"You've been watching me read for three weeks. Now I've watched you sneak up on me in the dark." He closed the book. "We're trapped, by the way. The magnetic locks on the doors run on electricity. We're here until the power comes back or until someone with a crowbar takes an interest."

I tested the front doors myself, because Ivo Vasquez—or Ivo Blackwood, or whoever he was deciding to be that week—had a talent for saying things that made me want to prove him wrong. He was right. The doors held like a promise neither of us intended to keep.

So there we were. Two adults, one candle, a building full of books, and a storm rattling the windows like a creditor at the door.

"We could wait in the reading room," I said. "It's the warmest spot. The radiators hold heat for hours after they shut down."

"The radiators," he repeated, and something moved across his face—not quite a smile, more like the memory of one. "Our father used to talk to them."

"I know."

"You know because you remember, or you know because I told you?"

And that was the question, wasn't it. Three weeks of clippings left on my desk, of photographs laid out like evidence, of careful, hostile scholarship about our shared past—and underneath all of it, the thing we hadn't done yet, which was simply to talk. To trade the small change of a childhood instead of the large bills.

"Both," I said. "Come on. If we're going to be locked in overnight, we might as well do it properly. There's a kettle in the staff room and a gas ring that doesn't need the mains."

* * *

*Ivo*

She made tea the way our mother used to—water first, then the bag, then a long impatient stare at the pot as if she could bully it into steeping faster. I watched her hands do it and felt something in my chest turn over, the way soil turns before frost.

The staff room was small and honest: a table scarred with decades of mug rings, a corkboard pinned with flyers for events ten years dead, a window going grey-white with rain. Rosalind lit two more candles off the first, and the room filled with a light that made everything look remembered rather than seen.

"My first crush," she said abruptly, "was a boy named Peter Osei, who came in every Thursday night to use the atlas collection. He was seventeen. I was fifteen and shelving in geography to avoid my own house." She wrapped her hands around her mug. "He had a way of licking his thumb before turning the big map pages. I used to follow him through Africa, country by country. I knew more about the Congo that year than any fifteen-year-old should."

"What happened?"

"He asked me to help him find a map of a town where his grandmother was born, and when I found it, he kissed me between the shelves, and I dropped an entire volume of *Baedeker* on my foot." She smiled into her tea. "Broke nothing but my dignity. He moved away that summer. I've never been able to look at an atlas since without a small, stupid warmth."

I laughed. It surprised me, the sound coming out of me, rusty as a gate in winter. She looked pleased, and then immediately suspicious of being pleased, which was so exactly like the woman in the photographs—the ones with the corners cut away—that I had to look at the window for a moment.

"My first crush," I said, "was Mrs. Ferro's daughter, Lucia. Ashford. You wouldn't know her."

"Ashford," I repeated. "Where Celia lives now. Where you went."

"After," I said, and let the word sit there, doing its work. "Lucia had a gap in her teeth and a habit of reading upside down—she'd hold the book toward you instead of herself, so you'd have to lean in close to read along. I spent an entire summer leaning in close. She never once noticed I wasn't reading."

"That's terrible."

"It's the whole story of my life, told in miniature." I turned my mug on the table. "Your turn again. Tell me something true about Mother. Not the version. Something small."

Rosalind was quiet long enough that the candle nearest her guttered and steadied.

"She sang while she ironed," she said finally. "But only the same four bars of something, over and over, like a record with a scratch. I used to think she'd forgotten the rest. Now I wonder if she was only allowed to remember four bars."

I put my hand flat on the table because otherwise it would have shaken. Four bars. I could hear them. I could hear them right now, in the rain, in the candlelight—a melody with no name, hummed low, the sound of a woman standing over an iron in a kitchen on Halloway Street.

"That's the song," I said. "That's—God. That's the song she hummed when she braided my hair before school. Four bars and then she'd stop, like she'd hit a wall."

Neither of us spoke. Outside, the storm threw itself against the glass, and inside, two people who had been strangers sat in the wreckage of a shared melody.

* * *

*Rosalind*

Here is what nobody tells you about grief: it has a sound, and the sound is a voice you'd swear you've forgotten until someone else says a sentence and it comes back whole.

"Tell me his voice," I said. "Father's. Daniel won't talk about him, and I've spent thirty years telling myself I remember, but I think I only remember the shape of remembering."

Ivo set down his tea. He has our mother's habit of pausing before hard things, gathering himself the way you'd gather papers before a wind.

"He talked to machines," Ivo said. "Not crazy-talk. Conversational. He'd stand in front of the boiler with his coffee and say, 'All right, sweetheart, what's wrong today,' like it was a sulking wife. And the radiators—" he huffed something that was almost a laugh, "—he swore they answered him. When they clanged awake in October he'd knock twice on the pipe and say, 'There she is.' Like greeting a horse."

"There she is," I whispered. I hadn't heard those words in thirty years and they arrived intact, complete, wearing their original paint. "He said that. He said that every year."

"And you thought you'd lost it."

"I thought I'd invented it."

"No." He leaned forward, candlelight carving his face into planes and shadow. "You kept it. All these years, in the dark, where they couldn't cut it out of you. That's what you did instead of photographs, Rosalind. You kept the sounds."

I wanted to cry and refused to, mostly on principle, partly because I suspected that once started I might not stop until spring. So instead I asked him the question I'd been carrying since the crematorium.

"What did it smell like? The house. After."

He understood immediately. Of course he did; he'd been there. I had been taken away that weekend—to an aunt, to a story about a holiday—and by the time I came back there was no Halloway Street, only a flat above a dry cleaner's and a mother who never said the word *fire* in any language.

"Wet ash," he said. "Wet ash and burned sugar. There had been a tin of sweets in the pantry, and the smell of melted sugar got into everything, into the brick, into the garden soil. For years afterward, whenever it rained in Ashford, the whole street smelled faintly of burned sugar, and I couldn't tell anyone why, because I was the only one who knew there'd ever been a house at all."

"Burned sugar," I said. "I thought that was the bakery on Corben Street. I built a whole childhood around a bakery that was never the point."

"That's what she did." His voice was gentle, which somehow made it worse. "She gave you a replacement smell, a replacement street, a replacement everything. And you loved them sincerely. That's not your failure, Rosalind. That's craftsmanship."

I stood up then, because sitting still had become unbearable, and went to the window. The city outside was black from end to end—no lights anywhere, the whole sleepless metropolis finally forced to close its eyes—and in the black glass I could see the room behind me doubled: two candles, two mugs, two siblings sitting at a scarred table like survivors of a shipwreck comparing notes on the sinking.

"When I was small," I said to the glass, "after we moved, I used to wake up at night convinced I could smell smoke. I'd go stand in the kitchen and breathe and breathe, hunting for it. Mother would find me there and make cocoa and never once ask what I was looking for. I think she knew. I think she stood there watching me hunt for a house she'd erased, and she made cocoa, and she said nothing, for twenty years."

"Twenty-six," Ivo said softly. "She wrote to me for twenty-six years."

"I know. I've read them now." The letters from the basement archive, addressed to a demolished address, mailed faithfully into a void. "Every one of them ends the same way. *Don't come looking. Stay safe. Don't come looking.*"

"She wasn't protecting you from the truth. She was protecting you from Daniel's version of it."

"Maybe both things can be true."

"They usually are," he said. "That's what makes them unbearable."

* * *

*Ivo*

Somewhere past two in the morning, the storm eased from fury into sulk, and Rosalind brought out a blanket from the lost-and-found cabinet—gray wool, smelling faintly of other people's winters—and draped half of it over my shoulders without asking, the way you'd do for family, the way you'd only dare do for family.

We played the game children play in cars: would you rather. Would you rather have known, or have been spared. Would you rather have been the one who stayed, or the one who was taken. Would you rather have her lie, or her letters.

"I'd rather have the letters," she said. "Even late. Even addressed wrong. A love that arrives too late is still proof it existed."

"Easy for you. You got the woman. I got the envelope."

"You got the truth of her, though. I got a portrait she painted of herself. Which of us had the real mother?"

"That," I said, "is the cruelest question anyone's ever asked me, and I intend to steal it for later use."

She laughed—really laughed, the unguarded one, the one I'd bet money no one at this library had ever heard—and in the candlelight she looked suddenly, startlingly young. Fifteen, maybe. Shelving in geography. Following a boy through Africa.

Then her face changed. Not the smile ending; something else arriving. She was looking past me, toward the dark doorway of the staff room, and her hand came up slowly and touched my arm.

"Ivo," she said. "The lights."

Down the corridor, faint but unmistakable, the emergency strip over the exit sign flickered—once, twice—and steadied. Power returning. Somewhere below us, the building's old systems began to shudder back to life, pumps and relays clearing their throats.

And beneath it all, rising through the floors, through thirty years and one demolished house and every lie ever told in either of our names, came a deep metallic clang—the radiators waking, one by one, down the length of the building.

Rosalind's eyes went wide. Her lips moved, shaping words she hadn't spoken aloud since childhood.

"There she is," we said together.

And then, from the darkness at the end of the corridor, where no one had been standing all night, a third voice—dry, tired, unmistakable—said:

"Well. Isn't that a coincidence."

Daniel stepped into the candlelight, dripping rain, holding a crowbar.
