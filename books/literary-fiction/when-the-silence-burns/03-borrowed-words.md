## 3. Borrowed Words

The house smelled of my mother for eleven days after she died, and then it began to smell of dust and lemon polish and the particular staleness of rooms no one opens. I noticed the change the way you notice a clock stopping—not all at once, but as an absence that accumulates until the silence has a texture.

Daniel wanted to begin immediately. Of course he did. Daniel grieved the way he did everything else: efficiently, on a schedule, with a checklist he'd probably drafted in the hospital corridor while I was still learning how to breathe around the shape of her absence.

*Estate sale. Two weeks. I found a company that does everything—inventory, pricing, even the donation runs. We just sign.*

He sent it as a text, because Daniel had learned years ago that I answered texts and let phone calls ring into voicemail like water into sand. I stood in my mother's kitchen holding the phone, watching the light come through the window over the sink—the window she'd kept a basil plant in every summer of my childhood, though she never once cooked with it—and I thought: *he wants to sell the smell of her before it finishes leaving.*

*Not yet,* I wrote back. *I need to sort her papers first.*

*What papers? She was a retired seamstress, August. There are bills and there's a will. The lawyer has the will.*

But there were papers. There were always papers. My mother was a woman who kept things—not sentimentally, not in the way of people who preserve their children's baby teeth in cotton-lined boxes, but in the way of someone who understood that paper could be held against you, or held for you, depending on which century caught up with you first. She kept receipts from 1987 folded into the pages of phone books. She kept the brown envelope containing her naturalization certificate inside a biscuit tin inside a shoebox inside the closet of the sewing room, wrapped in a plastic bag against damp that never came. She kept her life in layers, the way you'd pack something fragile for a journey you weren't sure you'd survive.

I started in the sewing room because it was the smallest room and because I am, at heart, a coward who organizes her cowardice into efficient increments.

* * *

Her sewing machine sat under its vinyl cover like a sleeping animal. The treadle model, the old Singer, black and gold-leafed, that she'd brought—I realized I didn't know where from. That was the thing about my mother. Thirty years of Sunday dinners, thousands of hours in kitchens and cars and hospital waiting rooms, and what did I actually know? That she was born in Portugal. That she came to this country young. That she didn't talk about before. I had accepted these gaps the way you accept weather—as conditions, not questions.

The closet gave up its secrets slowly. Winter coats that still held her shape in the shoulders. A hatbox of buttons, each one sewn to its card with tiny, patient stitches. And at the back, behind a stack of pattern envelopes gone soft at the edges, a metal cash box, olive green, dented at one corner as if it had been dropped once, long ago, and carried anyway.

It wasn't locked. It had never been locked; the keyhole was painted shut. Inside: her passport, expired since before I was born, the photograph showing a young woman with severe eyebrows and eyes that looked not at the camera but slightly past it, as if the photographer were standing between her and something she needed to watch. Her naturalization certificate. A rosary wound so tight around its own beads it seemed to be strangling itself.

And beneath these, flat and separate, two documents.

The first was a deed. Not for this house—for a house in a place called Rabo de Peixe, on an island called São Miguel, in the Azores. The paper was old, the ink faded to the color of weak tea, but the language was unmistakable even to me, even after all these years of letting it drain out of me. *Casa e terreno.* House and land. And the name on it, in careful schoolroom script: *Maria Almeida da Costa.*

Almeida da Costa. Not Almeida. The name I'd grown up under was half a name, a name with a room missing.

The second document was an envelope. Sealed, though the seal had long since lost its stickiness and lifted at one corner like a healing wound. Yellowed. Addressed in Portuguese, in my mother's hand—but a younger hand than the one I knew, rounder, less hurried—to:

*Filomena Almeida da Costa*
*Rua do Sol, 14*
*São Miguel, Açores*

Filomena. I turned the name over. It wasn't one I'd ever heard spoken in our house. No aunt Filomena at holidays, no cousin in the Azores mentioned even once, even in the oblique way my mother sometimes gestured toward the past—a shrug, a change of subject, the smile that ended conversations.

I sat down on the floor of the sewing room with the deed in one hand and the envelope in the other, and the afternoon went on without me. Dust moved through the bars of window light. Somewhere outside, a neighbor's dog was barking at nothing, at everything, at the fact of a changed household.

My mother had owned a house. My mother had a sister. My mother had written a letter, sealed it, crossed an ocean, and never sent it—or sent it and kept this one, or—there was no way to know. The envelope weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

* * *

That night I dreamed in Portuguese.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is the only inheritance I trusted myself with. I didn't dream *about* Portuguese. I dreamed *in* it—whole sentences arriving in my sleep with the fluency I hadn't possessed awake since I was six or seven years old, since the years when I began performing incomprehension for the adults who said too much in front of me. *Não entendo,* I would say, the great lie of my childhood, delivered with the blank face I'd perfected, while inside me the words landed whole and burning. I understood everything. That was the problem. Understanding was the family disease, and pretending not to was my vaccination.

In the dream, someone was reading aloud in a kitchen I didn't recognize—stone walls, a table worn pale in the middle—and I understood every word, and the words were about the sea, and I woke with my own voice in my throat, mid-sentence, saying something I couldn't hold on to. It dissolved the way dreams do, but the shape of it stayed, the way a wave's shape stays in wet sand after the water goes.

I lay in the dark and thought about the envelope in the cash box downstairs, and I did not go down to it. That took more discipline than anything I had done in years.

* * *

Daniel came on Saturday with cardboard boxes and a label maker, because Daniel believed that any problem could be solved by being divided into labeled parts.

"You haven't started," he said, standing in the living room, surveying my mother's untouched house with the expression of a man assessing flood damage.

"I've started."

"August." He set down the boxes. "I know this is hard. But keeping her stuff in boxes in *our* houses isn't going to bring her back, and meanwhile the property taxes don't care about our feelings. The market's good right now. We sell the house, we split it, we're done by spring."

Done by spring. As if grief were a lease with a termination date. I watched him pull the label maker from his bag—the small domestic violence of it, the tape spitting out its tidy white strips—and felt something in me go very quiet and very firm, the way water goes firm when it decides to become ice.

"There are things we need to look at first," I said.

"Like what?"

I got the cash box and set it on the dining table between us. I put the deed beside it. Then the envelope, face up, so he could see the address, the language, the name.

Daniel picked up the deed, read it, frowned. "What is this?"

"It's a house. In the Azores. In her name."

"So? She's dead. We sell it."

"We don't even know what it is. We don't know if there's anyone—"

"August." He put the deed down with exaggerated gentleness, the tone he used when he decided I was being irrational, which was most tones. "Mom didn't have a sister. Mom didn't talk about Portugal. Mom spent forty years not talking about Portugal. You think some old piece of paper changes that?"

I looked at the envelope. At the name *Filomena* in my mother's younger handwriting. At the corner of the seal, lifted, waiting.

"Maybe," I said, "she spent forty years not talking about it because nobody ever asked in a language she could answer in."

Daniel stared at me. "You don't speak Portuguese."

And there it was—the sentence I had spent my whole life building for other people to say. *Você não fala português.* Except they said it in English, and it was worse in English, because in English it was true in the way that mattered to them: I didn't speak it well, I didn't speak it publicly, I didn't speak it at all if I could help it. What lived in me was older than speech. It was rhythm and vowel and the specific ache of certain words that English has no room for. It was my mother calling me in from the yard in a language I answered without thinking, and then, at the door, switching to English like changing out of wet clothes.

"I understand more than I say," I told him.

"That's not a reason to blow up the estate timeline." He was already repacking his bag. "Look. Take the week. Look at your papers. But August—" He paused at the door, and for one moment his face did something unguarded, something that looked briefly like our mother's tiredness. "We need to be done with this. Both of us. Some things you're better off not digging up."

He left. The door clicked. The house settled around his absence.

* * *

I waited until dark, which was absurd, and I knew it was absurd, and I did it anyway. Some rituals require the cover of night to feel permissible.

I sat at my mother's kitchen table with the envelope in front of me and a lamp pulled low, and I lifted the flap—it gave without resistance, forty years of glue having surrendered long ago—and drew out a single sheet, folded twice, soft as cloth from handling. She had held this letter many times. That was the first thing I understood. This was not a letter written once and abandoned; it was a letter returned to, read and reread, its creases going furry with touch.

The handwriting was hers, but younger, faster, the letters leaning forward as if hurrying somewhere.

*Minha irmã,*

*I write this and I do not send it, because to send it would be to ask forgiveness, and I have not yet decided if I want it.*

My Portuguese came up out of me slowly, word by word, like drowned things surfacing—some rising intact, some coming up rotted beyond recognition, some not coming up at all. *Irmã*, sister—that one surfaced clean. *Perdão*, forgiveness. But other words I had to hold up to the light and turn over, guessing at them from context, from sound, from the body-memory of my mother's mouth shaping them across Sunday dinners.

*Tu sabes o que ele queria de mim. Tu sabes o que a mãe disse. Eu escolhi o mar em vez da terra, e agora o mar é tudo o que tenho.*

*You know what he wanted from me. You know what Mother said. I chose the sea instead of the land, and now the sea is all I have.*

I stopped. My hands were not steady. The kitchen was very quiet, the refrigerator humming its one note, the streetlight through the window laying its familiar yellow bar across the table where my mother had sat for thirty years eating breakfast and saying nothing, saying nothing, saying nothing in two languages.

There was more. Pages more—the letter ran to four sheets, dense, the writing growing smaller and more cramped as it went, as if she were trying to fit everything before courage failed. And at the bottom of the last page, beneath a signature—*Maria*—a postscript in different ink, added later, the letters shakier:

*Se um dia alguém vier ter contigo com este nome, diz-lhe a verdade. Ela vai precisar dela mais do que eu.*

*If one day someone comes to you bearing this name, tell her the truth. She will need it more than I did.*

*Bearing this name.* Almeida da Costa. Half a name, a name with a room missing—and in the missing room, apparently, a sister named Filomena, on an island called São Miguel, holding a truth my mother had sealed away with instructions for its delivery.

I sat until the lamp burned the same yellow as the streetlight and I could no longer tell which light was which. Then I took out my phone, and my hands, which had shaken over the letter, were perfectly steady as I searched flights to Ponta Delgada.

There was one Thursday morning. One seat left.

I booked it before I could translate myself out of it.
