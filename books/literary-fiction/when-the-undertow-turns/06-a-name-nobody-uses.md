## 6. A Name Nobody Uses

The funeral home had the smell of lilies and carpet shampoo, a smell that tried very hard to be respectful and mostly succeeded in being institutional. Silas stood at the edge of the receiving line with a paper cup of weak coffee he hadn't asked for, watching mourners pass his aunt's pew like a slow river around a stone. His aunt Odette — his father's younger sister, the one relative who had ever called him on birthdays, at least until the calls stopped — sat with her hands folded in her lap as if she were holding something down.

He had come in his own suit after all. The motel's iron had scorched a faint brown crescent onto the cuff of the borrowed shirt, and he'd decided the mark was honest. Grief should leave a stain. His father's body, they said, had been recovered two miles downriver, tangled in the rebar of the old rail trestle, and Silas kept turning that over: a man who lived his whole life on the high dry side of town, and the river had come for him anyway. Or he had gone to it. The police report used the word *accidental*. Silas had read it three times in the motel room under the buzzing sign, and the word sat on the page like a coin balanced on its edge.

He was watching the doors when she came in.

She was maybe sixty, tall in a way that made the funeral home's low ceiling seem like an insult, wearing a headwrap of deep indigo with a single line of gold thread running through it like a seam of ore. She did not move down the receiving line. She moved *across* it, perpendicular, the way a current moves across a tide, and people parted for her without seeming to know they were doing it. Silas saw his aunt see her. Odette's folded hands went white at the knuckles, and she rose halfway out of the pew and then, strangely, sat back down, as if standing would confirm something she'd spent a lifetime denying.

The woman stopped in front of Odette. Silas was close enough now to hear, and the room had gone into that hush that funerals manufacture out of grief and air conditioning.

"I'm sorry for your loss," the woman said, and her voice was low and even, a voice used to being heard over water. "For the family's loss. For the Okonkwo-Vasquez line."

Silas heard the words and felt them land somewhere below his ribs. *Okonkwo-Vasquez.* His grandmother's name. The one on the yellowed baptismal certificate in a shoebox his aunt kept, the one nobody said aloud, the one his father had quietly dropped from his own signature sometime in Silas's boyhood, so that by the time Silas understood names were choices, the choice had already been made for all of them. Vasquez, his father had said, was enough. Vasquez was a name that fit on a form without explanation.

Nobody used the other one. Nobody had used it in forty years.

Odette said something Silas didn't catch — her mouth barely moved — and the woman turned her head, slowly, the way a heron turns, and looked directly at him.

There was no surprise in her face. That was the thing that undid him. She looked at him the way you look at a house you've been told about, checking it against the description, finding it accurate. Her eyes were the color of the river in November, and they held him the way the river holds a leaf: gently, and with total indifference to his plans.

"You're the inspector," she said.

"I—" He set down the coffee he hadn't drunk. "Who are you?"

"Adaeze." She let the name sit there, three syllables, unhurried. "Your father knew me. Before you knew him, if that's how you want to count it." She glanced at the casket at the front of the room, the closed casket, because there had not been much to leave open. "He looks peaceful. They always do, once the water's done with them."

Odette made a small sound, and Silas realized his aunt was praying, or doing whatever she did instead of praying, her lips moving over words that had no sound.

"I don't know what you're talking about," Silas said. It came out steadier than he felt. "Our name is Vasquez."

"Is it," Adaeze said. It wasn't a question. It was the way he himself said *load rating adequate* about a bridge he didn't believe in. "Walk with me a minute, inspector. Your aunt needs to sit with her God, and you and I need to stand somewhere the lilies can't reach us."

* * *

Outside, the afternoon had gone the color of old pewter, and the parking lot was full of cars from a town Silas had been cataloguing all week — what changed, what rotted. The Sonic was still there. The Sonic was always there. The lumberyard where his father had worked twenty years was a self-storage facility now, rows of orange doors like a wall of shut mouths.

Adaeze walked to the far edge of the lot, where a strip of grass gave way to the levee, and stood looking at the river. From up here you could see it only as a glinting absence between the trees, but she looked at it the way other people look at a person.

"You were four years old the last time you were on that water," she said. "You don't remember it. You were asleep in a net hammock on the deck of a boat called the *Mariana*, and your father sat up all night with a lantern because there was a storm coming and your grandmother was dying and he couldn't decide which thing to watch."

Silas felt the ground do something it had no right to do. The parking lot was flat. It was asphalt. And still it tilted.

"That's not—" He stopped. Started again. "I grew up on Delacroix Street. In a house with a carport. My father hated the water. He wouldn't take us to the lake. He wouldn't even—" His voice did something embarrassing, a crack in a load-bearing wall, and he hated it. "He taught me to swim in a YMCA pool. Chlorine. He said rivers were for people with no sense."

"He said that," Adaeze agreed, "to people with no sense."

The wind came off the levee and carried the smell of it — silt and diesel and something green and ancient underneath, the smell Silas had been breathing all week in his sleep without naming it, the smell his body recognized before his mind could object.

"There's a community," Adaeze said. "Downriver, past the rail trestle where they found him. Houseboats. Docks. Thirty-some families, give or take, though fewer every year. It's called the Undertow." She said the name the way you'd hand someone something heavy: here, hold this. "Your father helped build it. Not helped. He *founded* it. Him and your grandmother and eleven other people who the city had no use for and the banks had no patience for. They built a town on the water because the water was the only thing that would have them."

"That's not true." But even as he said it, Silas heard how young it sounded, how much it sounded like a child insisting the closet is empty. "My father was a foreman. He had a 401(k). He had a *boat ramp pass* he never used."

"Man can hold two lives in him," Adaeze said. "Most do. Most just let one of them die quietly and call that growing up." She turned to face him fully then, and up close he could see she was older than sixty, that the indigo wrap was hiding hair gone entirely white, that her hands were a laborer's hands, knuckles like river stones. "He didn't leave us because he hated us, Silas Vasquez. I want you to know that before you hear it some other way, because you will hear it some other way. He left us because he loved something else more, and love makes liars of everyone. He loved your mother. And your mother's people, and the bank, and the church on Delacroix Street, and the whole dry respectable world that looked at a man who lived on a houseboat and saw a vagrant. So he chose. He chose the world that would let him be your father in daylight."

"My mother died when I was six."

"I know," Adaeze said. "And after she died, he didn't come back. That's the part that's hard to forgive, and I'm not going to pretend I've forgiven it. He could have. The dock would have taken him back in a minute. We'd have lit every lamp on the water for him. Instead he sent money, sometimes, in envelopes with no return address, and then he stopped sending even that, and then he was just a man we used to know, walking around up here on the dry land, being nobody's founder."

The river glinted between the trees. Silas became aware that his hands were in his pockets, gripping his own thighs through the fabric, holding himself down the way you hold down a tarp in wind.

"Why are you telling me this," he said. "At his funeral. Why now."

"Because the city is going to take the dock," she said simply. "Six weeks, maybe less. Flood-control project. They've got paper for it, and paper is the one thing we never had enough of, because the man who kept our paper was your father, and he took it with him when he went up the hill." Her mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "And because you looked at me in there like a man who's been reading the wrong blueprints his whole life. I know that look. I've drawn it myself."

"I don't know what you think I can do."

"I don't think anything. I *know* what your grandmother left you." From inside her coat — a canvas coat, salt-stiffened, wrong for a funeral and right for her — she took two things. The first was a key: old, brass gone brown, with a wooden bead on a leather loop and a number stamped into the bow that Silas read upside down before he could stop himself. *7.* The second was an envelope, sealed, thick with paper, soft at the corners as if it had been carried against a body for a very long time. On its face, in a handwriting that slanted and looped and mixed two alphabets like braided rope, was written: *For the son, when he comes down.*

"When he comes down," Silas repeated.

"That's what she called it. Going down to the water. Everybody else moved up and away; she said they'd gone *up* the hill, and up the hill was where you went when you wanted to be something the river couldn't claim." Adaeze pressed both objects into his hands. Her grip was stronger than it had any right to be. "The key is to her boat. Number seven. It's still there. It's the only one still there that's hers, and it's held together by paint and stubbornness, and it will leak, and you will fix it, and that's how it works. That's how everything on the water works. You don't inherit a thing. You inherit the *fixing* of it."

"I'm not staying," Silas heard himself say. "I'm here for the service. I go back tomorrow. I have a bridge on Route 9 that needs—"

"Route 9," Adaeze said, and now the almost-smile came all the way, and it was kind, which was worse. "You measure gaps for a living. I watched you in that room, standing at the edge of your own family, measuring." She stepped back. The wind took her wrap's gold thread and made it flash. "The letter's in her hand. Some of it's Igbo, some of it's Spanish, some of it's river talk that never got written down anywhere but in us. I can read you most of it. Not all. Nobody living can read you all of it, and I'll tell you why some other day, when you've slept on the water at least one night."

"I'm not sleeping on the water."

"You're not sleeping anywhere right now, inspector. Look at your face." She was already turning away, moving back across the lot toward the funeral home doors, and the mourners parted for her again, that same unconscious tide. Over her shoulder she said, "Number seven. Past the trestle, follow the lanterns. Somebody's always got one lit for whoever's coming down. Even when nobody's coming down. *Especially* then."

* * *

He sat in the rental car for a long time after the service, the envelope on the passenger seat like a passenger. The sky had begun to give up its pewter in favor of a bruised violet, and the parking lot emptied by degrees until it was only him and the Sonic's sign, buzzing its red buzz.

He did not open the envelope. He told himself it was out of respect, and then he told himself it was out of caution, and finally, with the honesty that came to him only in parked cars, he admitted the truth: he was afraid that whatever was inside would be in a handwriting he recognized. His father's. And that it would prove Adaeze right, and that the proof would be the one thing he could not inspect, could not rate, could not sign off on and drive away from.

Instead he turned the key over in his fingers. Brass gone brown. The bead on the leather loop was worn smooth by somebody's thumb — his grandmother's, presumably, decades of worrying it, or holding it, or counting the days. *7.* A number, not a name. On Route 9 the bridges had numbers too, and he had spent eleven years learning everything about them except what they meant to anybody.

His phone buzzed. Dana. He looked at the screen and felt the familiar arithmetic begin — what he owed, what he could give, what silence would cost versus what words would — and for the first time he noticed the arithmetic itself, the way you notice an expansion joint only when the car begins to rattle.

He didn't answer. He'd call from the motel. He'd say the service was fine, the weather was fine, the drive was fine. He'd measure the gaps.

Then he started the car, and instead of turning left toward the motel and the highway and the life he had been maintaining, he turned right, down the hill, past the trestle where they'd found his father, following a road that narrowed and then gave up on paving altogether — and there, between the last two trees, the river opened out like a held breath finally released, and down its dark length, impossibly, patiently, a single lantern burned on a dock, lit for nobody, waiting for somebody.

Silas parked. He did not get out. The envelope sat beside him, and the key sat in his palm, warm now, as if the brass had been waiting all these years for a hand with the right temperature.

Down on the water, a figure stepped out of a houseboat's doorway and looked up the bank — not searching, Silas realized, just *watching*, the way you watch a road you've watched for forty years — and Silas understood, with a vertigo that was not unpleasant, that he had just been seen. That somewhere below, in a ledger or a memory or a lantern-lit doorway, a line had been waiting for his name. Both of his names.

He put the key in his pocket and got out of the car.
