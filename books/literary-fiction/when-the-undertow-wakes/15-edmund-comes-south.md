## 15. Edmund Comes South

The silence lasted eleven days, which was nine days longer than Edmund could bear and four days shorter than he'd promised himself it would last.

He counted them the way he counted everything now — in the gaps between things. The gap between Marguerite's toothbrush and his own on the bathroom shelf, still exactly two inches, neither of them willing to close the distance or widen it. The gap between the voicemail he'd left and the one he hadn't. *Marguerite, I think we should talk. I think —* and then the click, because he'd heard his own voice say *I think* twice and understood that he had nothing behind the words, nothing but the same flinty nothing his father had handed down like a watch that didn't keep time.

He told himself the drive was about the marriage. That was the story he arranged for it, the way his father had once arranged a family around an absence — carefully, deliberately, with the load-bearing walls left out. He was going south to find his wife. To bring her home. To say whatever needed saying in the car, on neutral ground, with the radio as witness.

But the M5 had other ideas. Somewhere past Bristol, somewhere in the long grey crawl of it, the story came apart in his hands, and he was honest with himself for perhaps the fourth time in his adult life: he wasn't driving toward Marguerite. He was driving toward the window. The one she had described — no, hadn't described, had refused to describe, which was worse — when he'd asked her, carefully, in the tone he used for asking about anything that mattered, *What's in Greyhaven, Marguerite?* and she had looked at him with something that was not anger and not pity but a kind of terrible patience, and said, *You know what's in Greyhaven. You've always known. You just never let yourself look through the glass.*

He'd told her she was being dramatic. He'd said it in the voice of a man defending the structural integrity of a building he suspected was condemned.

The coast road came up to meet him in the late afternoon, hedges leaning in like conspirators, the sea appearing and disappearing between gaps in the land the way a truth appears and disappears between the sentences of a careful family. Greyhaven announced itself first by smell — salt and diesel and old fish — and then by sight: a crooked harbour, a row of painted houses the colour of hard candy, gulls wheeling and screaming over it all as if they knew something.

He parked near the slipway and sat with his hands on the wheel.

He was fifty-eight years old and he had never once, in fifty-eight years, said his brother's name aloud. He tested it now, silently, the way you test a floorboard you've avoided your whole life. *Julian.* It didn't hurt, exactly. That was the strange part. It felt more like stepping into a room in his own house that had been locked since childhood and finding the furniture still under its dust sheets — everything exactly where it had been left, everything exactly as strange.

*He's dead,* Edmund said to the windscreen. *He drowned in 1983. That is the family position. That has always been the family position.*

And then, because he had driven four hundred miles to stop lying: *But Marguerite found the letters, and she went south, and she has not come back, and my father has not asked where she is, and that is the sound a lie makes when it starts to come down.*

* * *

He didn't plan it. Planning would have been a kind of permission, and he had spent his whole life understanding that certain things must never be given permission. So he simply walked — up from the harbour, along a lane smelling of elderflower and brine, following the only sign that mattered, the one for Waverley House Care Home, its paint blistered, its garden running gloriously to seed.

He saw the window from thirty yards.

It was a garden room, glass on two sides, and through it — through it — an old man sat in a wicker chair with a blanket over his knees, holding a piece of bread out flat on his palm while a gull stood on the armrest of the chair and took it with a gentleness Edmund would not have believed a gull capable of.

He had Edmund's hands.

That was the first thing, and it went through him like a crack through glass. Not Gerald's hands, not exactly — Gerald's hands had gone stiff and ornamental decades ago, hands for signing things. These were working hands, big-knuckled, scarred across the back, and Edmund had looked at those same hands every morning of his own life in the mirror while he shaved, and had never once wondered where they came from, because the answer had drowned when he was nine years old.

The man turned his head. He was laughing at the gull — Edmund could see the laugh before he could hear it, the shoulders going, the head tipping back — and in the turn of the head there was Gerald's jaw, and under the laugh there was something else, something Edmund recognised from photographs his mother had kept in a shoebox and then stopped keeping, something from before. From when there had been two of them.

His legs took him to the door before the rest of him voted.

* * *

The garden room smelled of tea and cut grass. The old man looked up when Edmund came in, and Edmund watched him do the arithmetic — the double-take, the stillness, the bread going forgotten in his open hand while the gull screamed its outrage and lifted off.

"Well," the old man said at last. His voice was roughened by salt and years, but underneath it Edmund heard a voice from a summer forty years gone, a voice calling across water. "You must be the younger one."

"Edmund."

"I know." The old man set the bread down. He didn't stand. Edmund understood, with a lurch, that he might not be able to, or that he was choosing to let this meeting come to him, and either way he respected it the way he respected load limits on bridges: absolutely, without argument. "You have his frown. Father's frown. I spent forty years being glad I didn't have it, and now look — it walks in through my door wearing a good coat."

"I'm not here for him."

"No," Julian said. "I don't suppose you are. Sit down, Edmund. You've driven a long way; I can see it in you. People arrive from long distances still going the speed of the road."

Edmund sat. There was a small table between them, and on it a mug of tea going cold, and a notebook, and a pair of reading glasses folded with a precision that made Edmund's chest ache, because he folded his own glasses exactly the same way, and had always believed it was his own small invention.

They looked at each other.

This was the part he had not prepared for, because he had not allowed himself to prepare: that there would be nothing to say, and that the nothing would be enormous. Two men shaped by the same absence from opposite sides of it. Julian had spent forty years being the absence; Edmund had spent forty years living in the house the absence had built. And now the absence was sitting in a wicker chair with crumbs on its blanket, watching him with eyes that were, God help them, kind.

"You came to see me," Julian said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't surprise either. It sounded like a man confirming a diagnosis he'd suspected for years.

"I came to find my wife."

"And found me instead." Julian nodded slowly. "That's how it goes. You go looking for one lost thing and the sea gives you another." He picked up his cold tea, considered it, drank it anyway. "She found me in the spring. Sat right where you're sitting. Looked at me the way you're looking at me — like she was checking me against something."

"The photographs."

"Mm. And against you, I'd guess." He smiled, and the smile undid Edmund more than the hands or the jaw, because it was Marguerite's smile — no. No, it was the smile Edmund saw in his own mirror on the rare occasions something genuine got through. "You're angry," Julian observed. "Not at me. At the room. At the whole arrangement."

"The arrangement," Edmund repeated. The words came out flat and strange.

"Forty years, and Father still runs it like a shipping concern. Everything stowed. Everything declared at customs as something it isn't." Julian set the mug down. "I used to think he did it out of shame. Now I think he did it out of terror. There's a difference, but it won't help you much. It never helped me."

"I was told you drowned."

"You were told I drowned, and you were a child, and you believed it, and then one day you were old enough to notice that nobody in that house ever said my name, and you did the most loyal thing a child can do." Julian's voice was gentle, and that gentleness was the worst thing yet. "You stopped asking."

Edmund found that his hands had made fists in his lap, his good coat, his careful life, his two-inches-of-cold-air toothbrush marriage, all of it gathered into his knuckles. "I was nine."

"I know how old you were. I counted your birthdays. Every one. Did you know that? Delia would get the local paper — she has her methods — and I'd read that a boy in Somerset had passed his exams, and a young man had married a bridge inspector, clever girl, and I'd have a drink on each one. Forty years of birthdays, Edmund. I was at the funeral, you know. Mother's. In the grounds crew. I carried a spade."

"You carried a spade at our mother's funeral."

"Somebody had to put her in the ground, and I wanted it to be family." Julian said it simply, without self-pity, and Edmund had to look away, out at the garden, at the gulls strutting the lawn like small opinionated landlords, because his eyes had gone hot and unforgivable.

* * *

They talked for two hours, and Edmund could not afterward have said about what. Small things. The fishing here. Whether the tea at Waverley House was as bad as it looked. Julian's heart, mentioned once, lightly, the way you mention weather coming. And under the small things, the large things moving like fish under ice — the storm, the boat, the ledgers, the money — never once surfaced, both brothers keeping them under with the same family instinct, the same inherited discipline of silence, and both of them knowing that the other knew.

It was nearly dusk when Julian said, "You should know that your wife didn't come here to break anything open. I thought she had, at first. Emissary, I assumed. Father's money sent to buy more silence." He paused. "She sat where you're sitting and she cried, Edmund. Not for me. For you, I think. For the boy who was told never to ask. She said—" and here Julian stopped, and Edmund watched him decide whether to give it up, and give it up he did, because whatever else forty years of erasure had done, it had not taught Julian Coldharbour to hoard, "—she said, *he guards a door he's afraid to open himself, and I have been standing in the hallway of that house for twenty years, and I am so tired of the hallway.*"

The words went into Edmund and found the load-bearing wall, the one he'd built at nine years old, the one his father had helped him build with that flinty grief and that single instruction — *we don't speak of Julian, we don't speak of that summer, we are a family that holds* — and he felt it move. Not fall. Move. The way a truss moves, he thought absurdly, the way Marguerite would say it: a structure doesn't fail all at once, it fails at the joint, the expansion joint, the place where it was designed to flex and has instead been bolted rigid until the stress has nowhere to go but through.

"I don't know how to do this," he said. It was the truest sentence he had spoken in twenty years.

"Nobody does," Julian said. "That's the whole secret. Nobody knows how to be a family. They only know how to hide from each other, or they learn how to stay." He reached across the small table — those hands, Edmund's hands, older and ruined and alive — and gripped Edmund's wrist, once, hard, and let go. "Come back, though. That's all. Whatever you decide about the rest of it, come back while you can. I ran out of winters. You haven't yet."

* * *

He walked down into Greyhaven in the dark with the harbour lights coming on one by one, and his legs were shaking, and he sat on a bollard by the slipway like a man twice his age and let the grief and the relief arrive together, unwelcome guests, elbowing each other in his chest.

Because that was the thing he could not have predicted, the thing no one had warned him of: it was relief. Forty years of a door in the cellar of himself, and it opened, and behind it was not a drowned boy, not a skeleton, not the ruin his father had promised — but an old man who folded his glasses the same way, and counted his birthdays, and carried a spade at their mother's funeral because he wanted it to be family.

*He was there the whole time. I could have had a brother the whole time.*

And underneath the relief, the grief, cold and rising like the tide coming into the harbour: for the forty years. For the boy told not to ask. For Marguerite in the hallway, tired, so tired, and for the voicemail he had never finished, and for the sentence forming in him now, out loud, to the black water slapping the harbour wall:

"He's alive. My brother is alive, and my father let us bury an empty grave, and I helped him hold the lid down for forty years."

A woman passing with a dog gave him a wide, practiced Greyhaven berth. The gulls had gone quiet. Out past the harbour arm, the sea worked at the darkness, and the forecast, he'd heard it on the car radio without listening, was turning.

His phone buzzed in his pocket. He took it out expecting Marguerite, hoping and dreading in equal measure, but it was his father: three words, no greeting, the old shipping-concern economy.

*Come home, Edmund.*

He looked at it a long time. Then, with a thumb that did not shake, that was, in fact, perfectly steady, he typed the reply — the first open act of rebellion of his entire careful life, four words, and he pressed send before the bolted joints of himself could flex and fail:

*He's alive, Father.*

The phone went dark in his hand. Out at sea, the first far-off thunder of the turning weather rolled in over the water, and Edmund sat on the bollard in the wind and waited, for the first time in forty years, to see what would break.
