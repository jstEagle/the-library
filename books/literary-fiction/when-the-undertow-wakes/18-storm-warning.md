## 18. Storm Warning

The barometer at the Greyhaven harbormaster's office had been falling for two days, and Marguerite had learned to read it the way she read anything — as a structure under load, a system telling you what it could bear and what it couldn't. Ninety-nine point four kilopascals by Tuesday morning. Ninety-eight point one by noon. The gulls had gone strange and silent over the harbor, sitting in dense congregations on the breakwater like an audience waiting for something to begin.

She'd taken the permanent post in September, and the town had absorbed her the way towns absorb newcomers who are useful: slowly, then all at once. She inspected the seawall on Mondays, the old truss bridge over the estuary on Thursdays, and in between she drove the coast road that Julian had once fled down in the dark with forged ledgers in a waterproof bag, learning its curves until they felt less like history and more like her own.

Now the radio in her truck said what everyone already knew: a deep low tracking up from the southwest, due to make landfall Thursday night. Forty years, almost to the week, since the other storm.

* * *

She found Delia on the harbor front, hauling a coil of rope twice her size toward the boat shed with the grim economy of a woman who had done this every autumn of her life and did not intend to be sentimental about this one.

"You're late," Delia said, without turning around. "Everyone else has their boats out or double-anchored. You're the only person in Greyhaven who still trusts the weather."

"I trust measurements."

"Measurements." Delia dropped the rope and finally looked at her, wind pulling at the grey strands escaping her scarf. "I was seventeen the last time a storm like this came through. I stood at that window—" she pointed at the cottage above the harbor, at the small square window where a girl had once watched a second boat leave in the dark— "and I watched him go out and I thought, well, he'll come back when it's over. That's what people do. They come back when it's over." She picked the rope up again. "Go see him. He won't say it, but he wants you there before the weather shuts the coast road. He pretends he doesn't count days, but he counts them."

* * *

The care home sat back from the sea behind a windbreak of stunted pines, and Julian was in the garden when she arrived, because he was always in the garden, wrapped in a blanket that made him look like a deposed king, feeding the gulls scraps from his lunch with the air of a man distributing patronage to undeserving subjects.

"You look terrible," he said, which was how he said good afternoon.

"You look worse."

"I'm allowed. I'm eighty-one and officially dead." He held out a crust. A gull took it with insulting precision. "Delia sent you."

"Delia mentioned you might want company before the storm."

"Delia mentioned nothing of the sort. Delia told me if I didn't let you visit I was a stubborn old wreck, which is true, so I've decided not to dispute it on principle." He studied her with Edmund's eyes — the same pale grey, the same way of looking slightly past a person first, as though checking the horizon before committing to the near field. "Sit down, Marguerite. The bench is wet but you strike me as someone who doesn't mind being uncomfortable if the view is honest."

She sat. For a while they watched the water, which was already wrong — not rough yet, but swollen, premonitory, the color of gunmetal with a long slow swell coming in from deep water that no local wind could account for.

"That swell," Julian said quietly. "That's the thing to watch. Wind you can argue with. That—" he nodded at the horizon, where the sky had begun to organize itself into long grey shelves— "that's just the sea remembering what it's going to do. We had a swell like that the night I left. I remember thinking the water looked rehearsed."

* * *

He was worse than he let on. She saw it in the way he stood up from the bench — the pause, the calculation, the hand finding the armrest rather than her shoulder because he would rather fail alone than be helped. She saw it at dinner, when he ate half of what was put in front of him and pushed the rest around with the concentration of a man doing sums he didn't like the answer to. She saw it most clearly when Sister Anneke, the charge nurse, caught Marguerite in the corridor after visiting hours and asked, in the flat kind voice medical professionals use for sentences they've rehearsed, whether she was family.

"Cousin," Marguerite said. It was close enough now.

Sister Anneke walked her to a small room with a kettle and two chairs and closed the door. "His heart," she said. "It's been failing for a while — we've managed it, but managing isn't the same as fixing. This weather won't help; cold snaps are hard on hearts the way they're hard on pipes. But honestly?" She folded her hands. "It isn't only the weather. Mr. Coldharbour has spent forty years holding very still around something enormous, and bodies pay for that. Whatever your family has been carrying — and I don't ask, I'm Dutch, we mind our own business beautifully — I'd carry it a little lighter, if you can. Hearts keep books too."

* * *

That night the first bands of the storm arrived, rain moving across the windows in horizontal sheets, and Marguerite sat in her rented room above the chandlery rereading Agnes's letters by lamplight while the wind tested the glass like a burglar working a lock.

She had read them so many times now that certain phrases lived in her the way load ratings lived in her — called up instantly, precisely, under stress. *They have given me a grave with no one in it, and I am expected to tend it.* From 1979, six months after the drowning. *Gerald says silence is a form of love. I have begun to suspect it is a form of ownership.* From 1984. And the last one, the one that had started everything, written in a hand gone thin and furious: *If I die before I tell him I know he is alive, then I chose wrongly, and I want it written somewhere that I knew.*

Outside, the harbor bell began to sound — three strokes, pause, three strokes — the warning pattern, though everyone in Greyhaven already knew. She thought about Edmund, four hours north, in the house they had sold, in whatever new smaller life he was building out of the wreckage of the old arrangement. She thought about Tom, who had shouted at Julian in October and had been coming down on weekends ever since, awkwardly, wordlessly, learning his father the way you learn a language as an adult — slowly, with an accent, but learning it. She thought about Gerald, who had refused everything, who had sent back Julian's single letter unopened, and who was up there in the family house with the storm coming and forty years of silence between him and any of them.

Time had changed shape. She had spent her whole life measuring tolerances — how much give a structure had, how much movement it could absorb before the movement became failure. She understood suddenly, sitting there with the letters fanned out on the bed and the wind rising, that this family had been living inside its tolerances for forty years. Everything holding. Nothing warm. And structures that hold forever at maximum load don't stay sound — they don't fail dramatically, either. They just get quieter. They stop ringing when you tap them.

Julian's heart was the whole family's heart, and it was running out of winter.

* * *

Wednesday morning the coast road was still open, barely, and Tom arrived in a car full of sandbags and plywood, looking like a man who had driven through the night, which he had.

"How is he?" was the first thing he said, before hello, before taking off his coat.

"Worse. He won't say it. The sister told me."

Tom set the sandbags down harder than necessary. "He told me last weekend he'd survived worse weather in a rowboat. In the dark. With a bag of stolen ledgers." His mouth twisted — fury and love ground together into something that had no clean name. "Forty years I thought he was dead, and now I get eighteen months with him and the sea wants those too."

"He's not going anywhere during a storm," Marguerite said. "He's too stubborn. He'll want to watch it."

And he did. By Wednesday evening, when the power flickered and the staff moved residents away from the seaward rooms, Julian argued his way into a chair by the big garden-facing window, wrapped in blankets, watching the pines bend. Marguerite sat with him. Delia arrived soaked from battening her cottage, bringing soup she pretended was leftover and thermoses of coffee she'd clearly made specially, and the four of them — the family Gerald had erased, reassembled by accident and persistence — watched the storm build like an audience watching a play they'd seen before and knew ended badly.

"It was a Tuesday," Julian said, apropos of nothing, somewhere past ten o'clock, his voice low under the wind. "The night I went into the water. People always assume storms are dramatic occasions. It was a Tuesday. I'd had supper. Gerald was doing figures at the long table. I remember the lamp on his hands." He turned the teacup in fingers that had grown translucent, the veins standing out like roots through shallow soil. "You know what I've never told anyone? I wasn't afraid of drowning. I was afraid of the boat being found empty. Of them deciding I'd run, and coming after Delia to find out where. So I left the rowboat adrift past the point, properly swamped, oars shipped, and I swam ashore at Miller's Cove and walked inland soaking wet and never once looked back at the water." A pause. "I've been looking back at it ever since, of course. That's the joke. You can leave the sea, but it keeps your forwarding address."

"Julian," Marguerite said carefully. "The doctor talked to me. About your heart."

"I know. She talked to me too. Dutch women are terrifyingly direct." He smiled, but it was the smile of a man conserving energy. "Another winter, she said. Maybe. If I'm careful. If I avoid excitement, exertion, and distress." He looked out at the black writhing garden. "Marguerite. I have spent forty years avoiding exactly those things. Careful is the disease, not the cure. So here is what I want, and I want you to hear it while I'm lucid, because I may not always be: I don't want another quiet year. I'd rather have one loud month. There are things unfinished. My father is dying up there in that house with my letter returned unopened on his desk, and I know that because Delia's nephew works at the post office, and small towns are wonderful. And my brother—" his voice caught, briefly, on the word, the first time Marguerite had heard it catch— "my brother shook my hand in the cove after the storm last month and we spoke plainly, mostly, and it was nearly enough, and *nearly* is the word that will be carved on my heart if we don't finish this."

The wind hit the window then, a fist of it, and the lights went out, and in the dark Julian laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him.

"There it is," he said. "Right on schedule. Forty years ago tonight, give or take, the lights went out in that house too, and Gerald lit candles and kept doing his figures by candlelight, and I stood in the hall with my coat on and nobody asked why." His hand found hers in the darkness, cold and dry and gripping with more strength than she'd expected. "Don't let us do that again. Don't let us sit in the dark being polite. Somebody light the damn candles and say the true thing."

* * *

The power stayed out through the night. Marguerite lay awake in her room listening to the storm work the coastline, and near dawn, in the trough between two great gusts, she heard the sea change — a deeper note underneath, the sound of water finding a new level — and she got up and looked out and saw, in the grey birth of the light, waves breaking clear over the end of the breakwater, and the harbor bell sounding wild and continuous now, no pattern at all.

Her phone buzzed against the table. Edmund.

She stared at the name for a long moment. They had spoken gently, distantly, since the divorce papers — two people who had dismantled a marriage without detonating it, checking occasionally that the other was still standing. She answered.

"The coast road's closed south of you," he said, without preamble. "Landslip at Harrow Point. I've been listening to the shipping forecast all night." A pause, filled with wind noise from his end too — the storm had reached the city, gentler, but present. "How is he? Julian. How is my brother?"

"Weaker. The doctor says—"

"I know what doctors say. I looked it up." Another pause. When he spoke again his voice had dropped into something rawer. "Marguerite, I dreamed about the cove last night. Both of us in it. I woke up and I couldn't stand it — all these things we've said *nearly*, all of it arranged so neatly, everyone surviving by disappearing a little, and him up there with the storm coming and time—" He stopped. Started again. "Gerald called me tonight. First time since August. He wouldn't say why he was calling. He just said, 'Is the old man still alive,' and when I said yes, he said 'hm' and hung up." A breath. "Marguerite, I think my father is frightened. I think he's been frightened for forty years and the storm's brought it up like a wreck off the bottom. And the road's closed, and I can't get to either of them, and I keep thinking about that night in 1979 — what if it was a Tuesday, what if he had his coat on, what if somebody could have just *asked*—"

"Edmund." She said it quietly, the way you speak to someone standing too close to an edge. "Come when the road opens. Not to fix anything. Just come. Bring nothing. Say the true thing."

Silence on the line, long enough that she checked the call hadn't dropped. Then, very low:

"What if the true thing breaks him?"

"Then he gets to be broken in front of people who love him," Marguerite said, "instead of preserved in front of people who don't quite. I've done both. Trust me on the difference."

When she hung up, the light had come up fully, ugly and silver, and the storm was at its height, and down in the harbor the fishing fleet rode out the surge on doubled lines, everything straining, everything holding, nothing guaranteed.

She dressed quickly, jammed a wool hat on, and went out into the wind to walk to the care home, because Julian should not wake alone on the anniversary of his death, and because she had promised him candles and true things, and the road south was closed, and time — she could feel it now, the way she felt a failing span under her feet, a faint wrongness in the rhythm, a vibration where there should be none —

time was running out.
