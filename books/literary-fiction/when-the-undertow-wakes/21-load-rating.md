## 21. Load Rating

The divorce papers came on a Tuesday, which felt right. Tuesdays were the day Marguerite had always scheduled the most routine inspections — the ones where nothing was wrong but everything needed looking at anyway. She signed them at the kitchen table of the Greyhaven motel, now her apartment, with a pen she'd bought from the harbour chandlery because her good pen had gone to Edmund in the division of things, along with the good knives, the good lamp, the good years.

It had been gentle. That was the strangest part. She had braced herself for the machinery of blame — the lawyers, the discovery, the slow excavation of grievances — and instead they had sat across from each other at a café in the town where they'd married, twenty-two years ago, and divided a life the way you divide a load across a span: carefully, so nothing buckles.

"I keep thinking," Edmund had said, stirring a coffee he wasn't drinking, "that we were like two people sharing a flat. Very polite about the shelves."

"Tenants," Marguerite said.

His mouth did something that was almost a smile. "Tenants of each other. We never actually moved in."

They had both laughed then — a small, startled sound, the first honest laugh they'd shared in years — and the laughter had hurt more than anger would have, because it proved how much they'd missed by being careful. There was no villain in it. Gerald had done what Gerald did; Agnes had done what love and fear made her do; and Marguerite and Edmund had simply never learned, either of them, how to be a person inside a marriage instead of a function of one. She had inspected bridges for two decades. She knew the difference between a structure that was sound and one that was merely standing. Their marriage had been standing.

"I'll always be grateful," Edmund said at the end, gathering the papers, "that you didn't let me keep not knowing. About Julian. About any of it." He paused at the door. "I hated you for about four months. Then I went to see him every other weekend, and I stopped hating anyone, really. Except Dad, some days. But he's just an old man in a big house now. The house won."

She had watched him walk to his car — a man lighter by one silence and one wife — and felt neither triumph nor grief, only the clean ache of a joint that had finally been allowed to move.

* * *

The permanent post came through in October. Principal Inspector, Coastal District — Greyhaven and the villages north of it. Her supervisor called it a demotion by geography; Marguerite called it the first decision of her life she'd made with her whole self. She rented a cottage on the hill above the harbour from a fisherman named Caskie who asked no questions and fixed the boiler without being asked twice, and she unpacked her boxes into a house that was smaller than her old kitchen and felt, somehow, larger than the whole of the Coldharbour estate.

The work suited her. Coastal spans were different creatures from inland trusses — salt in every joint, corrosion working invisibly beneath paint, the sea patient in a way no engineer could outwait. She liked that they demanded humility. You could not bully a bridge over water. You could only listen to it, test it, respect what the years had done to it, and mend what could be mended.

On her third week, she stood on the walkway beneath the old harbour viaduct with a hammer and a listening bar, tapping welds, and realized she was humming. She couldn't remember the last time she'd hummed.

Delia found her there, or perhaps had been sent — Julian had ways of knowing things. "He wants you on the porch Thursday," she said, arms crossed against the wind, her grey hair whipping loose from its plait. "Two o'clock. Bring your hands and leave your opinions."

"What's Thursday?"

Delia's face did the thing it did when she was deciding how much to give away — a flicker, a settlement. "Net-mending. He's been at it fifty years and he says your knots are an insult to rope. His words." She turned to go, then stopped. "He's having a good stretch. You come on the good stretches. That's the whole trick of it."

* * *

Julian sat on Delia's porch in a chair that had been built around him by decades of use, a half-mended net across his knees like an instrument. The autumn light came low and gold off the water and found every line in his face, and Marguerite thought, not for the first time, that he looked like what Gerald would have been if Gerald had ever once been happy.

"Sit." He nodded at a stool. "No — not there. Here, where I can see your hands."

She sat. He put the net in her lap and a wooden shuttle in her fingers and watched her attempt a sheet bend with the expression of a man witnessing a crime.

"Your mother-in-law couldn't knot either," he said. "Agnes. She tried, once, down at the old boathouse. Tied herself to the dinghy instead of the cleat and nearly went out on the tide." His voice was dry as driftwood, but there was something underneath it, moving like water under ice. "I had to cut her free. She made me promise never to tell Gerald. It was the first secret we ever kept together, my mother and I. We got better at it, unfortunately."

"Is that what you're teaching me? Secrets?"

"I'm teaching you to mend a net." He reached over — his hands were Edmund's hands, she thought again, the same long fingers, the same scarred knuckle, and the thought no longer startled her — and turned the shuttle in her grip. "Everything else you already know how to do. You took apart my family like a bad weld. Found the crack nobody wanted to see." He guided her fingers through the motion: under, around, through, snug. "A net's honest work. Every knot either holds or it doesn't. No one pretends a broken net is whole. They mend it or they lose the catch. Your lot never learned that."

"Edmund's learning."

"Edmund," Julian said, and something in the name was tender and rusted at once. "Edmund came south and sat in that chair where you're sitting and cried like a boy, and then blew his nose and asked me if I liked chess, as if we were going to be normal. Forty years of nothing, and he offers me chess." He shook his head. "It was the right thing to say. That's the joke of it."

The shuttle moved in her hands. Under, around, through, snug. The mesh closed where it had been torn.

"Again," Julian said. "Slower. The sea doesn't reward fast."

* * *

Thursdays became the spine of her week. Julian's good stretches came and went — some days he was sharp as a gaff, teaching her the names of every boat in the harbour and the scandals attached to them; other days his breath came short on the walk from Delia's kitchen and he sat longer than he worked, watching her hands with an expression she came to recognize as a man memorizing something. The doctor had said what doctors say. Marguerite had read enough inspection reports to translate: the structure was failing, the failure was not sudden, but it was not far.

Tom started coming around in November, back from the rig, restless in the way of a man whose father had been returned to him at sixty days' notice. He was Delia's son and Julian's in every way that mattered and some ways that hurt — he had Gerald's temper, which Julian found hilarious and Tom did not. He worked the net beside Marguerite in silence for three Thursdays before he spoke to her directly.

"You know," he said, squinting at a knot, "you're technically my cousin. Or something. Aunt-by-former-marriage. There's no word for what you are."

"Cousin," Marguerite said. "Let's not overcomplicate."

Tom grinned — Julian's grin, she realized, the exact geometry of it. "Cousin." He tested the word like a knot, pulled it taut. "Yeah. All right, cousin. Fair warning, though — in this family, cousin means I'll help you move furniture and also lie to you about how your net looks."

"It looks terrible," Julian said from his chair, eyes closed, sun on his face. "Both of you. It's a disgrace. Mending it is the only interesting thing that will happen to either of you this week, and you're botching it."

Delia, coming out with tea, said, "Leave them be, you old barnacle," and the four of them sat in the gold light with the ruined net between them like a shared meal, and Marguerite understood that this was what she had been building toward without knowing it — not a replacement family, nothing so tidy, but a load path. A way for the weight to travel. Everyone bearing a share.

* * *

Edmund came down the second weekend of December. He did it now without announcing it, a man learning late how to arrive instead of appear. He stood in Delia's doorway with a bottle of the whisky Julian had once mentioned in passing — a detail from a single conversation, held onto for two months, which was Edmund's way of loving things: carefully, at a distance, in perfect condition.

They stayed for supper. Marguerite watched her former husband help Delia carry dishes, watched him sit beside his brother and lose at chess and not mind, watched him laugh at one of Julian's terrible jokes about harbormasters, and felt what she had not expected to feel, which was relief without regret. There was a version of her, a year ago, who would have looked at this table and ached to be part of it as a wife. That woman had been measuring the wrong thing. She was at the table. She had never left it. The marriage had been the thing that left.

Walking her to her car afterward, Edmund stopped on the path and said, "The house sold. Dad's moving to a flat in Ashcombe. Smaller. He hates it, which I think is the healthiest thing that's ever happened to him." He looked out at the harbour, the masts nodding. "I keep thinking about what you said. Load rating. You told me once that a bridge isn't judged by whether it stands, it's judged by what it can carry."

"I say a lot of things on the job."

"You said our marriage never got load-rated. That we never found out what it could carry, because we never put anything on it." He turned to her, and his face in the porch light was open in a way it had never been across twenty-two years of dinners. "This does. All of this — him, her, Tom, you. It carries everything we've got and it doesn't even creak." He smiled, rueful. "I'm glad you found it first. I'd have walked past that window. I'd have walked past it my whole life."

"No, you wouldn't," Marguerite said. "You came south. That's the whole inspection, Edmund. You came."

* * *

Winter closed over Greyhaven the way it always did — grey water, grey sky, the town pulling its boats up and battening down. Marguerite worked the coastal spans in weather that tested her respect for the sea, and on the worst days, the ones when the wind came in off the water with teeth, she found herself driving past Delia's cottage on her way home, just to see the light in the front room, just to see the shape of him in the window.

In late January, on a Thursday, Julian taught her the last knot she didn't know — a stopper knot, the kind you tie at the end of a line so nothing runs out. He put it in her hands and closed her fingers over it and said, "That one's for endings. Everything frays, if you let it run. You put a stopper in and the rope keeps its length."

"Is this a lesson about rope or a lesson about you?"

"Both," he said, unbothered. "Everything I know comes in pairs now. I'm at the age where the metaphor and the fact have merged." He looked out at the harbour, where a storm front was stacking up on the horizon like furniture being moved in upstairs — the forecast said three days out, maybe four, a proper one, the kind the old men were already naming. Julian watched it with the expression of a man reading an old letter. "I knew a storm like that once," he said quietly. "It made me, and unmade everything else. The sea keeps one big wave for every life, I think. Mine came early. Yours—" he glanced at her, and his eyes were clear and kind and tired, "—yours came late, and you swam it better than I did."

"You'll ride this one out," she said. "You're too stubborn to let a bit of weather finish the job."

Julian smiled and patted the stopper knot in her hand. "Check the forecast, cousin's cousin. And mend that net. I want it done before the weather turns." He settled back in his chair, breath a little short, eyes on the darkening water. "I've a mind to see what it catches."

That night, Marguerite stood at her window above the harbour and watched the first outriders of the storm come in — cloud moving against the wind, the barometer falling, the gulls gone quiet and strange. The radio said it would make landfall by Friday. The same week, forty years on, almost to the day.

She thought of the cove, and the rowboat, and the man on Delia's porch with his stopper knots and his endings, and she felt the old dread rise in her like water in a lock — and beneath it, steady as bedrock, something else. Whatever the sea had planned, he would not face it unknown this time. He would face it known, and loved, and surrounded by the family that had finally learned to carry its own weight.

She checked the forecast again before bed. The storm had a name now.

It was coming straight for Greyhaven.
