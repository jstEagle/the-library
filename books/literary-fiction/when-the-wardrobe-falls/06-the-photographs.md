## 6. The Photographs

Lark found the shoebox on the ninth day after the funeral, which was also the fourth day of her stay in the house she had stopped calling home sometime in her twenties, and it would be dishonest to say she wasn't looking for something. She was an accountant. She looked for things professionally. She had spent eleven years finding money that other people hid — in shell companies, in second ledgers, in the generous gray space between what a thing was and what a thing claimed to be. Her mother had hidden things too, apparently. Lark just hadn't known to audit her.

The box was on the top shelf of Vivian's closet, behind a stack of winter sweaters that still smelled faintly of cedar and of the particular soap her mother had used for forty years — the plain ivory kind, nothing with a scent name, nothing that announced itself. The box itself was unremarkable: a woman's size seven, the brand printed in flaking gold on the side, one corner crushed as though it had been dropped once, long ago, and then handled carefully ever after. It was heavier than a shoebox should be.

Lark sat down on the edge of her parents' bed — her father's bed now, though he was at the bridge, because her father was always at the bridge; grief had not interrupted his schedule even slightly, which was either admirable or monstrous depending on the hour — and lifted the lid.

Photographs. Dozens of them. And none of them were of her.

That was the first thing she understood, before she understood anything else: there was not a single image in the box from Lark's childhood. No birthday parties, no school pictures, no beach trips, no first day of school with the sign held up in crayon. Her entire documented existence lived in albums downstairs, in the living room cabinet, arranged by year like annual reports. This box was a different ledger entirely.

The photographs were older. Faded at the edges, colors gone amber or gone altogether. A farmhouse — white clapboard, sagging porch, a truck rusting into the grass beside it. A woman standing on that porch with her hand shading her eyes, laughing at whoever held the camera. Not Vivian. Or — Lark turned it over. On the back, in pencil, in handwriting she did not know: *Mama, June '81.*

Her mother's handwriting was everywhere in this box, but it was a younger version of itself, looser, the letters leaning. Lark knew her mother's hand the way you know a signature on a check — *Vivian Thackeray*, neat, upright, the kind of writing taught by nuns or by fear. This was not that. This was someone scrawling captions on the backs of photographs in a life that had happened somewhere else.

*Wrenfield.* The word arrived in her head uninvited, the way it had arrived at the funeral, spoken by the woman in the gray coat who had touched the casket like she had a right to and left before anyone could make her explain it. Lark had taken the guest book partly out of spite — her father kept looking at it like it might bite him, and somebody in the family had to handle the actual objects of their lives — and Delia Marsh's entry was still in it, third from the bottom. *Delia Marsh. For Ruth.* No address. No condolence, really. Just the name, and the other name, sitting together like a confession.

She turned more photographs over. A man by a fence line, squinting into sun. Two boys on a tractor, maybe ten and fourteen, both grinning with the specific recklessness of boys who have not yet been told no often enough. A kitchen table crowded with people, elbows and dishes and a birthday cake with crooked letters. And in the corner of that one, half cut off by the frame, a girl of perhaps sixteen with dark hair pulled back, caught mid-laugh, one hand raised as if to block the camera and failing.

Lark's breath did something strange.

It was her face. Not resembled — *was*. The same narrow jaw, the same deep-set eyes, the same left eyebrow that arched higher than the right when she laughed, the small asymmetry she'd spent her whole life thinking of as hers alone, hers and nobody's, because she'd never met anyone related to her beyond her two parents and neither of them had it. There it was on a stranger's face in a stranger's kitchen in a photograph her mother had hidden above her sweaters for — how long? Decades. Through every year of Lark's childhood, this girl had been sleeping in cardboard six feet above where her mother slept, wearing Lark's face.

She set the photograph down on the bedspread with exaggerated care, the way you set down something that might go off.

Then she did what she did. She worked.

She spread the photographs across the bed in rows, chronological as best she could guess, dating them by film stock and fashion and the penciled years on the backs. She counted them — sixty-one, plus a folded newspaper clipping gone soft at the creases, and beneath everything, wrapped in a dish towel, a child's leather shoe, single, small, its buckle green with age. She made no decision about the shoe. Some items you log and set aside until you're ready.

The clipping was local — not this county, she checked, a county three hours west — and it was about a drowning. A boy. *Search Continues for Wrenfield Boy, 12.* The print was smeared where water or time had gotten to it, but the date was legible: August 1989. Lark had been four years old in August 1989. Her mother had been thirty-one, married three years, living here, raising her, driving her to daycare in the blue hatchback — and carrying this clipping in a shoebox like a wound she pressed on purpose.

Lark sat for a long time among the paper family. The light moved across the bed. Somewhere downstairs the refrigerator cycled on, the ordinary sound of a house pretending nothing had changed.

When her father's truck came up the drive at 6:14 — she heard it without hearing it, the way you hear a clock — she was still sitting there. He came up the stairs with his measured tread, the tread of a man who inspected everything, and stopped in the doorway of the bedroom, and she watched him take in the scene: the open closet, the sweaters displaced, the shoebox lid, the photographs fanned across the bed like evidence at a deposition.

His face did the thing it always did. It closed. Not all the way — her father was too careful a man to slam a door — but it drew shut along some seam she'd never been able to locate, the way a bridge deck closes its expansion joints in summer, absorbing movement so no one can see it happen.

"Lark," he said.

"Sit down, Dad."

"I can—"

"Sit. Down." She surprised herself with the sharpness of it. "You're going to want to be sitting."

He sat on the bench at the foot of the bed, hands on his knees, a man braced for a load rating. She almost laughed. Almost.

"Do you know these people?" She picked up the farmhouse photograph and held it out. He took it the way he took everything, deliberately, both hands, and studied it far longer than a picture of a house required, and she understood in that silence — before he said a word — that he was not studying the house. He was deciding what to tell her. Thirty seconds. She counted. She was very good at counting.

"No," he said finally.

"That's not what your face said." She took the photo back. "Your face said *I've seen this before and I hoped you wouldn't find it.*"

"Lark—"

"The girl." She held up the kitchen-table photograph, the one with the cake, the one with her own face laughing out of 1985. "Look at her. Look at her face, Dad. Whose is it?"

He looked. She watched him look, and watched the recognition move through him like weather, and watched him try to contain it, and fail, because whatever else her father was — and she was beginning to suspect the list was longer than she'd ever imagined — he had never been good at hiding things from her mother, and apparently he hadn't been much better at hiding things about her mother from himself.

"I don't know," he said.

"You don't know, or you didn't ask?"

The distinction landed. She saw it land, saw it hit him somewhere structural, and felt no satisfaction in it, only a cold clean anger that frightened her a little because it was so much bigger than this moment deserved and she knew — she *knew*, in the accountant part of her brain that never stopped running totals — that most of what she was feeling had nothing to do with him. He was simply the nearest load-bearing surface.

"There's a boy in this newspaper," she said. "Drowned in 1989. Name of Wrenfield. Mom was thirty-one. I was four. She was my mother, and she was somebody else's sister, or cousin, or — I don't know what, because there are sixty-one photographs up here and not one of them has ever been mentioned in my entire life." Her voice cracked on *mentioned* and she hated it and pushed through. "A woman named Delia Marsh came to the funeral and called her Ruth. *Ruth,* Dad. You stood right there. You heard it. And you let her walk out of that church without asking a single question."

"What was I supposed to do? Chase her through the parking lot?" His voice stayed level, which infuriated her more than shouting would have. "She was grieving. We were all grieving."

"We were all *standing there*," Lark said. "Grief is not a pass. You've been standing there my whole life. That's what you do, you stand there and you measure things and you write them down in those little notebooks and you never — " She stopped, because she had seen the notebooks once, years ago, a shelf of them in his workshop, dated like logs, and had assumed they were work papers, bridge notes, boring professional minutiae, and something in his expression now told her they were not only that. "What's in the notebooks, Dad?"

"That's not — those are mine."

"Whose house is this?"

"It's mine," he said quietly. "Until probate finishes. And then it's yours and mine, split. I read the will too, Lark. I'm slow, not stupid."

The cruelty of it surprised them both. She saw him register what he'd said, saw the apology assemble itself behind his eyes, and she waved it off before it could form, because she didn't want the apology. Apologies were lubricant. She wanted friction. Friction produced heat and heat revealed things.

"She hid this from us," Lark said. "From *you*. Thirty-five years, Dad. You slept next to a woman who had an entire family in a shoebox over her head, and you never once opened that closet?"

"People's closets are their own."

"That's not an answer, that's a bumper sticker." She stood, because sitting felt too much like being a child being managed. "Here's what I think. Tell me if I'm wrong. I pull records for a living. I think you noticed things. Little things, over the years — she wouldn't talk about her childhood, she flinched at certain names, she never went west on the highway, she got quiet every August — and I think you logged every single one of them in those notebooks, and I think you told yourself that noticing was a kind of love, and I think it was actually a kind of cowardice, because noticing costs nothing. Asking costs something. Asking means you might get an answer you'd have to *do* something about."

He was very still. Outside, a car passed on Harrow Street, tires humming on the old span, and Lark thought absurdly: *he checks that bridge more faithfully than he checked her.*

"You want me to have demanded her past," her father said at last, and his voice had gone low and rough in a way she'd heard maybe five times in her life. "Fine. Say I should have. Now you sit with the other half of it. If I'd pried it open — if I'd stood in this room and said *Vivian, tell me about the Wrenfields* — what do you think happens? You think she thanks me? You think she says *oh, Orsen, at last, let me show you the shoebox*?" He shook his head slowly. "She built that closet shelf herself. I helped her hang it. I handed her the screws. I knew exactly how high it was, and I knew why, and I let her have it, because the day I met her she told me one true thing about herself and it was *I need a life with no one in it from before.* And I loved her enough to honor that, and yes, maybe I loved myself enough not to fight it, and both of those things are true at the same time, and you can put that in one of your reports."

Lark stood there with the photograph of her own stolen face in her hand and felt the anger look around for somewhere else to go and find nowhere, because he had disarmed it, damn him, by telling the truth, or at least a truth, or at least his truth, which in this family appeared to be a renewable resource.

"The shoe," she said instead, because she couldn't carry all of it, so she carried the smallest thing. She unwrapped the dish towel and set the little leather shoe between them on the bedspread. Her father looked at it for a long moment, and then he reached out and touched the buckle with one finger, gently, the way he touched corroded steel, cataloguing damage.

"A child's shoe," he said. "Kept forty years."

"One shoe," Lark said. "Where's the other one?"

Neither of them answered. The question hung in the room, small and enormous, and Lark understood suddenly that it was not a rhetorical question, that it had an answer somewhere, in a county three hours west, in August 1989, in the mud of some riverbed or the bottom of some other box in some other closet, and that she was going to find it. Not because she forgave any of them — her mother for burying it, her father for letting it lie, the woman in the gray coat for arriving and detonating and leaving — but because an unreconciled account was the one thing she had never in her life been able to leave open overnight.

She gathered the photographs back into the box, all except the kitchen-table one, which she kept in her hand. At the door she paused.

"Dad. The guest book. Delia Marsh wrote *for Ruth.* Which means she knew her before she was Vivian. Which means she knows everything in this box and probably a great deal that isn't." She turned the photograph over once more, reading the penciled caption on the back — *Ruthie's birthday, the last good summer* — and felt the floor of her understanding tilt another degree. "And I'm going to find her. Tomorrow. Before you decide to stand there about it."

* * *

He followed her down the hall without meaning to, an old habit of trailing after his wife's moods, misapplied now to his daughter, and stood in the doorway of the guest room where Lark had installed herself with her laptop and the funeral guest book propped open against the lamp. She was already typing. Already building the file. He recognized the posture — shoulders forward, jaw set, the exact architecture of Vivian concentrating — and had to grip the doorframe.

"Delia Marsh," Lark said, not looking up. "There are four in the state. One's eighty-seven. One's in a correctional facility. One died in 2016." She turned the screen toward him. "And one lives forty minutes from here, outside town, near the diner on Route 9."

Orsen looked at the address for a long time. Forty minutes. All those days he'd driven past on inspection rounds, past the diner with its faded pie sign, while the answer to his wife sat inside it eating breakfast.

"Lark," he said, and his voice came out quieter than he intended. "I already know where she is."

His daughter looked up at him then, and he watched her arrive at the arithmetic — that he had tracked a stranger to a diner and sat across from her and come home and said nothing, that the notebooks on his shelf contained a conversation he had not mentioned, that the inventory of unsaid things in this family had just added a page — and he saw her decide, visibly, to be furious about it tomorrow.

"Then you're coming with me," she said, closing the laptop. "And this time, we're asking."
