## 13. Household Under Load

The request came on a Tuesday, delivered the way Delia Marsh delivered everything — plainly, without apology, as if the world had already agreed to it. She stood in Orsen's kitchen with her coat still on, a duffel bag at her feet, and said, "I'd like to stay a few weeks. To sort her effects. If you'll have me."

Orsen was holding a mug of coffee he had forgotten to drink. Steam rose off it in a thin, patient line, the way steam rises off a river in November, and he found himself watching it instead of the woman in front of him because looking at her was still like looking at a photograph of Vivian taken by someone who had never met her — the resemblance wrong in every particular and right in the aggregate.

"Her room's been closed since the funeral," he said.

"I know. I'm not asking you to open it for me. I'm asking you to let me be the one who opens it."

Behind him, at the table, Lark set down her pen. She had been working through the estate inventory all morning — columns of assets and appurtenances, her handwriting small and merciless — and the sound of that pen stopping was louder than anything Delia had said.

"No," Lark said.

Delia didn't turn. Orsen noticed that. She kept her eyes on him, as if the request had been made to him alone, and maybe it had, and maybe that was the whole problem.

"She's not family," Lark said. "She showed up at the funeral with a word nobody's ever heard and a photograph nobody's ever seen, and now she wants to sleep in my mother's room. That's not grief. That's trespass with good manners."

"Your mother left me things," Delia said, still to Orsen. "I'd like to find them before someone else does it for me."

"Then hire a lawyer. File a claim. Do it the way people do things."

Orsen looked at the duffel bag. It was army-green, old, the zipper replaced with a heavier one in a different color. A bag that had been repaired rather than replaced. A bag that had traveled. He thought about the notebooks in the upstairs drawer, thirty years of dated entries, and how not one of them had ever contained the word *Wrenfield* until the page tore. He had kept inventory all his life and it had kept nothing.

"She can stay," he said.

"Orsen—"

"Dad," Lark corrected, and the word landed between them like a dropped wrench.

"Two weeks," he said to Delia. "The room at the end of the hall. Sheets are in the hall closet, second shelf."

Delia picked up the duffel. As she passed Lark's chair she stopped — just for a moment, the way a pedestrian stops at a curb without needing to — and said, "You have her hands. Did you know that? The way you hold a pen. She held a fork like that."

Lark said nothing. But Orsen, watching, saw her left hand close around the pen a little tighter, as if someone had tried to take it and she had decided, on the spot, never to let go.

Lark's POV

The house had never been this full. Three adults, one bathroom schedule, and a silence that used to belong to one woman and now had to be divided three ways, like an estate.

Lark worked at the kitchen table because it was the only surface in the house that didn't feel haunted. She had spreadsheets open on her laptop and the funeral guest book beside her, and every morning she told herself she would return the guest book to the box in the garage where it belonged, and every morning she didn't, because page six held a signature in blue ink — *Delia Marsh* — and beneath it, in the space for *relationship to the deceased*, a single word: *kin*.

Not niece. Not family friend. *Kin*. A word from courtrooms and genealogies. A word her mother had never once used about anyone.

She heard Delia upstairs in the closed room. That was the worst part — the sounds. The soft drag of a drawer. The pause between drawer and drawer, which Lark understood, professionally, as the sound of someone photographing or cataloguing or photographing-while-cataloguing. She had watched enough evidence handlers work to know the rhythm. Delia was not grieving in that room. Delia was *processing* it.

On the fourth morning Lark climbed the stairs and stood in the doorway without knocking.

The room looked wrong. Not violated — wrong the way a room looks when someone has been *using* it. The bed was made with hospital corners her mother had never used. The window was open two inches, which Vivian Thackeray had never once allowed in thirty years of marriage, because drafts, she said, were how colds got in and how conversations got out. On the nightstand sat a glass of water, a book of county road maps, and — Lark's stomach dropped — the shoebox. The one from the top of the closet. Open. Empty except for tissue paper.

"Those were in the attic," Lark said.

"They were in the closet," Delia said. "Behind the winter coats. She always hid things behind the winter coats. Even when we were—" She stopped herself with the precision of a woman who had spent a lifetime editing.

"Even when you were what?"

Delia sat on the edge of the bed, hands flat on her knees. "I was going to show you. I was waiting for the right day."

"There's no right day. There's just days. Pick one."

Delia looked at her then — really looked, and Lark felt it like a searchlight, like being read for structural defects — and said, "Saturday. There's something you should see at the county archive, and there's something I should tell you, and I'd rather do both sober and in daylight."

"You're not going to tell me now?"

"No."

"Is that how she did it too? The not-telling? Is it genetic?"

Something moved through Delia's face then, fast, like a crack propagating through glass — there and gone, but Lark had spent her career finding cracks nobody else could see, and she saw it. Grief. Actual grief, unperformed, arriving at the mention of Vivian's habits as if the habits themselves were the wound.

"She told me once," Delia said quietly, "that the hardest part of leaving was that she was good at it. That she'd done it before and would know how to do it again, and that the knowing would keep her up at night more than the leaving ever did."

Lark stood in the doorway of her mother's room, in a house that now smelled like someone else's coffee, and heard a sentence her mother had apparently said to a stranger and never, not once, to her.

"Saturday," she said, and went downstairs, and did not work for an hour, and hated Delia Marsh slightly less, which was worse.

Orsen's POV

Orsen learned the new household the way he learned bridges: by walking it daily and noting what had changed.

Delia rose before six. He knew this because the floorboard outside his bedroom — the one he'd been meaning to fix for eleven years — spoke at 5:50 each morning, a single groan, and then the stairs, one at a time, her weight distributed differently than Vivian's had been, more heel, less edge. She made coffee but never finished the pot, leaving exactly two cups' worth, which he understood, after four days, to be a courtesy: enough for him and Lark, nothing wasted, nothing taken that wasn't offered. Lark left for the county records office at eight and returned at six with printouts and a look on her face that Orsen recognized from inspection reports — the look of someone who has found something in the data and is deciding whether to say so.

He himself left for the Harrow Street Bridge at seven, and came home at five-thirty, and the strange thing — the thing he would have written in a notebook if he'd still trusted notebooks — was that the house was easier with three people in it than it had been with two. Grief, he was discovering, was a load, and loads distributed across more members were loads each member bore less of. It was the most basic principle of his profession and it had taken him sixty-one years and a stranger's duffel bag to apply it to a house.

On the sixth evening he came home to find them in the kitchen together — Lark at the table, Delia at the counter — and the sight stopped him in the mudroom with his boots half off.

They were arguing about a casserole.

"You don't cover it for the first forty minutes," Delia was saying. "She only covered it at the end. For the crust."

"She covered it the whole time. I watched her. There was foil."

"There was foil *at the end*. You were a child. Children remember the foil because the foil is shiny. The recipe card says—"

"You've seen her recipe card?"

Delia went still. Orsen, still in the mudroom, went stiller.

"It came with some things she sent me," Delia said. "Years ago. A card, a recipe, no return address. I made it every winter and told myself it didn't mean anything."

Lark looked at her for a long time. Orsen could see his daughter running the calculation — the same one she ran on ledgers, the same one she was running on 1989 court records — weighing the cost of trust against the interest it might accrue.

"Forty minutes uncovered," Lark said finally. "Then foil. Fine. But if it burns, that's on your evidence."

Delia laughed. It was a small sound, rusty, like a gate opening after a season closed, and Orsen stood in the mudroom with one boot off and felt something in his chest he had no entry for: the sound of his daughter and his wife's abandoned niece building something in his kitchen out of a casserole and an argument, load-bearing, provisional, real.

He came in and hung up his coat and said, "There's foil in the second drawer," and neither of them looked at him, and it was, he thought, the best homecoming of his marriage.

Lark's POV

Saturday came, and with it the county archive, and with it the thing Delia had promised — though she delivered it in the car first, in a parking lot, with the engine off, as if the information needed a vehicle to arrive in.

"Your mother had a brother," Delia said. "My father. And a nephew who drowned in 1989. My brother."

Lark sat with her hands in her lap and did not say anything, because she had learned, in deposition rooms, that the first thing out of your mouth after a revelation is usually the thing you'll spend years correcting.

"He was eleven," Delia said. "His name was Peter. And your mother — Ruth, then — was the last person to see him alive. And she testified about it, and the testimony went nowhere, and the family never forgave her, and she left, and became Vivian, and built a whole life on the far side of a name." Delia's voice did not break. It thinned, the way steel thins under load — still holding, but measurably. "I grew up in the house that hated her. And I loved them anyway, and I loved her anyway, from a postcard a year, and now they're all dead or dying and I'm the only one left to ask the only question that matters, which is *why*, and she's not here to answer it."

Lark looked out the windshield at the archive building, gray limestone, its steps worn into shallow curves by a century of people coming to find out what actually happened.

"I found the payments," she said. "In the court records. Witnesses in the '89 inquest who received disbursements from an account tied to my grandfather. And a judge who was disbarred in '94. I've been building a file."

Delia turned to look at her, and for the first time since the funeral, Lark saw her look afraid.

"A file for what?"

"For the truth," Lark said. "It's the family business. Apparently."

They went up the steps together, two women who shared a dead woman and almost nothing else, and inside, under the fluorescent hum, the archivist brought out the box they'd requested — inquest records, 1989, Wrenfield — and set it on the table between them like a body.

Lark opened it. The first page was a witness list, typed on a machine that had been old when she was born.

The second page was a photograph.

And the boy in the photograph — eleven years old, squinting into a summer sun, standing on a dock with his hand raised in a wave — was wearing a shirt Lark knew. Knew it the way you know a thing you've seen a thousand times in a shoebox: the faded blue, the torn collar, the sailboat.

It was her shirt. It had been her shirt. Her mother had kept a dead boy's shirt for thirty years and Lark had worn it through the fourth grade believing it had been bought for her.

She looked up. Delia was already crying, silently, the way people cry who learned early that crying loudly costs too much.

"That's Peter's shirt," Delia said.

"I know," Lark said. "I wore it."

And somewhere across town, at that hour, a weld on the Harrow Street Bridge was holding traffic it had never been rated to hold, and a man with a clipboard was writing down a number he didn't believe — but that was Orsen's burden, and this was theirs, and the box was open, and the archive was quiet, and there were forty more pages to go.
