## 21. No Name Needed

The lease was one page. Delia read it twice at the kitchen table while Lark made coffee neither of them drank fast enough to stay warm, and when she got to the line marked *Tenant*, she did what she had been doing since the funeral, since the diner, since the night she first said the word *Wrenfield* aloud in a room full of strangers who thought they knew the dead.

She left it blank.

"You have to put something," Lark said, not looking up from the kettle. "Landlord's a stickler. Mr. Okafor. He'll call the county."

"Delia Marsh," Delia said. "For the lease. For the utilities, the tax forms, whatever else needs a handle." She set the pen down. "But not for anything that matters."

Lark turned then, and Delia watched her decide — watched the forensic accountant in her run the numbers on that sentence and find, to her own visible surprise, that they balanced. "Okay," Lark said. "Marsh for the paperwork. Nothing for the rest of it."

That was in October, six weeks after the county board hearing, five weeks after Orsen Thackeray stood in a fluorescent-lit room and named himself among the guilty, four weeks after the prosecutor's office called to say the 1989 inquest would be reopened. The house on Calder Street had been empty for most of it — Orsen sleeping badly in the bedroom he'd shared with Vivian for thirty-one years, the rest of the rooms holding their breath — until the night Delia stood in the driveway with two suitcases and a box of photographs and said, to no one in particular, *I could sort her effects. If that's a thing that would help.*

Lark had objected. Orsen had permitted it. And somewhere between the objection and the permission, the arrangement had stopped being temporary, the way water stops being a stream and becomes a river without anyone marking the place where it happened.

* * *

Delia's POV:

The room still smelled like her. That was the first thing, and Delia had not been prepared for it — lavender and something older underneath, wool, cedar, the ghost of a soap that hadn't been manufactured in a decade. Ruth Wrenfield had slept in this bed for forty years under another woman's name, and the room had absorbed her the way plaster absorbs a nail: completely, invisibly, holding.

Delia unpacked slowly. She had never had a room that was hers in any permanent sense — a childhood of borrowed bedrooms in other people's houses, the Wrenfield aunts and uncles trading her around like a duty nobody wanted but nobody would surrender, then apartments, then the long gray years of the Marsh arrangement, which was not adoption so much as custody with better furniture. She had learned to live out of suitcases the way other people learned to swim. You didn't think about it. You just did it.

But this room — she stood in the middle of it with her clothes in her hands and understood that she was going to stay. Not sort-and-go. Not closure-and-leave. Stay.

She told Lark on a Tuesday, over the accounting of Vivian's effects, which had become the accounting of everything.

"The lease says month to month," Delia said. "But I want you to know I'm not treating it that way."

Lark was elbow-deep in a box of Christmas ornaments, most of them handmade, most of them ugly, one — Delia saw her lift it — a felt wren with a crooked beak that Lark held for a long time before setting it aside, gently, in a pile that had no label yet.

"You could stay in the house," Lark said. "The whole house. Dad's not going to live here forever, and honestly he sleeps better at the apartment near the office since the hearing. It's a lot of house for one person."

"I know." Delia folded a sweater that didn't need folding. "But the room is the right size."

Lark looked at her. Delia had learned by then that Lark's silences were not her father's silences — Orsen's were inventory, a man counting what he couldn't spend; Lark's were audit, a woman checking the books before she signed. This one went on long enough that Delia could hear the furnace tick in the wall.

"The right size for what?" Lark asked.

"For a person who spent her whole life being somebody's claim on somebody else," Delia said. "The Wrenfields' claim on Ruth. Ruth's claim on me, the one she never made. Your dad's claim on the marriage. Yours on her." She shrugged, and it cost her more than the shrug showed. "One room. That's the first thing that's ever been exactly mine. I don't want to jinx it by taking the whole house."

Lark nodded slowly, and then she said the thing that Delia would think about, later, at odd hours, the way you think about a door you didn't know was unlocked:

"You know you don't have to earn the room by cataloguing her whole life, right? The photographs. The letters. You could just — live here. Nobody's grading you."

"I know," Delia said. And then, because it was true and because she was tired of not saying true things: "But somebody has to. She spent forty years making sure nobody did. Somebody has to say *this was Ruth Wrenfield, this is who she was, these are the people she loved and the ones she couldn't.* If nobody does it, then the Marsh version wins by default. And the Marsh version is that she ran away and never looked back." Delia's voice caught on the last word and she let it. "She looked back every day for forty years. I have the letters to prove it."

* * *

Lark's POV:

They started the catalogue on a Sunday in November, at the kitchen table, because the kitchen table had become — without anyone voting on it — the place where the family's unfinished business got done. The trial by kitchen table had happened there. The reading of the letters had been planned there. The prosecutor's calls came there, Lark's laptop open to the scanned court records, her mother's testimony from 1989 glowing on the screen in typewriter font: *I saw my father take Daniel to the water. I saw him come back alone.*

Now the table held the shoebox, and the shoebox held the evidence of a life.

Two hundred and eleven photographs. Lark had counted, because counting was what she did, because her mother's discredited testimony had taught her — had taught them all — that the only testimony that survives is the kind with numbers attached. She'd built a spreadsheet, because of course she had. Columns: *Date (est.), Location (est.), Subjects, Source, Confidence.* Her mother would have laughed, or cried, or both. Her mother had kept these photos loose in a shoebox for forty years, unlabeled, unexplained — the one inventory in the house that was never taken — and Lark was going to fix that if it killed her.

"Okay," Delia said. She'd brought her own supplies: archival sleeves, ordered online, and a set of soft pencils, the kind museum archivists used, which she'd bought from an actual museum supplier because Delia did nothing halfway once she'd decided it mattered. "This one first." She held up a photograph of the farmhouse — the Wrenfield place, they knew that much now, from the letters — a wide gray building with a porch sagging on the east side, and in front of it, a woman in a housedress with her hand on the shoulder of a boy of maybe ten.

"That's Daniel," Delia said. Her voice did the thing it did when she named him — went flat and careful, a voice walking on ice. "My brother. That's the porch where—" She stopped. Started again. "That's the porch."

Lark wrote it down. *Subject: Daniel Wrenfield. Location: Wrenfield farm, porch. Confidence: high.* Under *Source* she wrote *Delia M.* and then looked up. "Do you want your name in the record?"

Delia considered it. This was the question underneath everything now, Lark had come to see — the question the whole winter had been organized around. Who was Delia Marsh, when she wasn't filling out forms? A Wrenfield by blood, raised by Wrenfields, carrying a Wrenfield grief. The prosecutor's office had her down as a surviving relative. The reopened inquest had her down as next of kin to a boy dead thirty-five years. And here, in this kitchen, in this town where nobody had known her name eight months ago, she was the woman in Vivian's old room.

"Put *Delia*," she said finally. "No surname. Like the lease."

"You can't file a legal record with no surname."

"It's not a legal record. It's ours." Delia's chin came up, and Lark saw in it something she recognized from the photographs — the set of Ruth Wrenfield's jaw at eighteen, on the courthouse steps, about to testify against her own father with the whole county watching. "That's the whole point. Everything official already has a version of her. This one's just ours."

Lark wrote *Delia* and nothing else, and found that her hand didn't hesitate.

They worked through the afternoon. It went like this: Delia knew the people, Lark knew the system. Delia would hold a photograph and the names would come — aunts, uncles, the cousins, the neighbors, the boy who drowned and the girl who saw it. Lark would cross-reference against the letters, against the court records, against the newspaper clippings she'd pulled from the county archive on microfilm, building confidence levels, flagging contradictions. Twice they found photographs that contradicted the official story — a date on the back in Vivian's handwriting that put her at the farm a full year after she supposedly cut all contact; a picture of her father, Gerald Wrenfield, at a church picnic, standing next to the judge who would later be disbarred, the two of them laughing, and Lark had felt the hair rise on her arms and had photographed it with her phone and sent it to the prosecutor before her coffee got cold.

But mostly it wasn't like that. Mostly it was two women at a table on a gray afternoon, saying names out loud so they wouldn't be lost.

"This one I don't know," Delia said, holding a faded snapshot of three girls on a dock, arms around each other, squinting into sun. "Ruth is the tall one. The other two—"

"Wait." Lark leaned in. "The one on the left. Look at her hands."

"What about her hands?"

"She's holding a camera. And there—" Lark pointed to a smudge of detail in the background, a station wagon with a dealer sticker in the window. "Dad has a photo from 1983, our driveway, the same car. Same dealer sticker. That's the Hendersons' place, two miles from here. Mom knew the Hendersons. She bought eggs from them for twenty years." Lark sat back, and something moved through her that wasn't quite grief and wasn't quite joy, some current that ran between the two. "She didn't cut them off. She just — folded them into the new life. Quietly. The way she did everything."

Delia was quiet a moment. Then: "She was good at that. Keeping two things true at once." Her thumb moved along the edge of the photograph, careful, not quite touching the image. "The letters say she was afraid of it. Being two people. But I think maybe she was also — I don't know. Proud of it. Like she'd gotten away with the one crime she never committed."

Lark laughed — a short, surprised sound — and then found she was crying a little, and then found that Delia had reached across the table and put her hand over hers, and that she was letting her.

Delia's POV:

In December, Mr. Okafor came by about the water heater, and while he was in the basement Lark asked him, casually, what name was on the lease, because she wanted to update the utilities.

"Marsh," Okafor said. "Why?"

"No reason," Lark said.

But that night, over the photographs, she said: "I looked it up. Marsh. It's not a family name of yours — the Marshes were just the couple the county placed you with. You picked it yourself, didn't you? At eighteen, when you aged out."

Delia turned a photograph face-down on the sleeve. "There was a marsh behind the last group home. I used to go out there and watch the herons. Nobody could follow me in — too soft, too wet. It was the only place that was mine." She shrugged. "It was that or heron. Marsh seemed more like a name."

"You could take Wrenfield now," Lark said. "Legally. With the inquest reopening, you've got standing, documentation, the whole—"

"No."

The word came out fast and final, and Delia heard how it sounded and made herself say the rest of it slower. "The Wrenfields don't get to have given me a name. They didn't earn it. Ruth earned something, but she gave it up to save me, and if I take it back now it's like saying the sacrifice was just — a transaction I reversed." She looked at her own hands, at the pencil in them, at the photograph of Daniel on the dock. "I'd rather be the marsh. The soft wet place nobody can follow you into. That's honest. That's what I actually am."

Lark was quiet for a long time. Then she opened her laptop, and Delia braced for the spreadsheet, the counterargument, the audit.

Instead Lark turned the screen around. She'd opened the catalogue file, the master one, and scrolled to the header, where the project title sat in bold.

*The Wrenfield Photographs: A Catalogue.*

"Been meaning to change that," Lark said. She deleted it, thought for a moment, and typed: *Photographs of Ruth. Catalogued by Delia and Lark.*

No surname. Delia looked at it for a long moment, and then at Lark, and neither of them said anything, because there wasn't anything to say that the screen hadn't already said.

* * *

Lark's POV:

Her father started coming by on Thursdays. Nobody scheduled it; it simply accreted, the way his silences had once accreted into a marriage. He'd arrive at six with takeout he pretended was for himself, eat at the kitchen table while they worked, and — this was the new thing, the thing Lark was still learning how to hold — he'd talk.

Not much. Orsen Thackeray's talking was still a rationed resource, a man paying out a currency he'd hoarded for sixty years. But he'd look at the photographs they'd sleeved and say things like, "That's the Harrow Street bridge behind her, 1978, before the second span. I climbed it that year. First inspection I ever did." Or: "She kept that sweater until the elbows went. I offered to buy her a new one and she said no, it had already been through everything, it could handle the elbows." Small true things, offered plainly, without the old millimeter-measuring. Lark had spent her whole childhood translating her father's silences, and now here he was, speaking a little, and she found the translation table in her head was obsolete, and found she didn't miss it.

One Thursday in January, he stood over the photograph of Ruth on the courthouse steps — eighteen years old, chin up, the man with the child beside her — and said, quietly, "I never asked her about her life before me. Thirty-one years. I told myself it was respect." He set the photo down with the exactness of a man who had spent his life setting things down. "It was fear. I want that in the record, since you two are keeping one."

Delia, without looking up from her sleeves, said, "Noted. Date, time, witness."

And Orsen Thackeray — retired, disgraced, lighter than Lark had ever seen him — laughed. Actually laughed, a short rusty sound like a hinge remembering it could swing.

* * *

Delia's POV:

By February the catalogue had reached one hundred and ninety photographs, and the wall of Vivian's old room — Delia's room — had become something like a map. Not a shrine; Lark had been firm about that, and right. A map. Faces and places arranged in sequence, the farm years, the flight, the new town, the bridge, the house on Calder Street, the forty years of a woman living two lives in one body and leaving evidence of both in a shoebox.

Delia stood in front of it some nights before bed, the way other people prayed.

She had stopped waiting to feel forgiven, or to forgive. That was the thing nobody told you about grief and guilt and thirty-five years of borrowed bedrooms: the feeling didn't come first. The decision came first, and then you lived inside the decision until it hardened into something you could stand on. She had decided, in the fall, to stay. She had decided, at the kitchen table, to be the marsh. She had decided, photograph by photograph, that Ruth Wrenfield's silence had been a load-bearing structure too — imperfect, over-stressed, never rated for what it actually carried — and that it had held anyway, and that holding was the whole of what she could ask of the dead.

In late February, a thick envelope came from the prosecutor's office. Lark opened it at the kitchen table, read the first page, and went very still.

"What," Delia said.

"The inquest." Lark's voice was careful, the way it got when the numbers were too big to say fast. "They've set the reopening. Findings will be vacated. Ruth's testimony — they're going to enter it into the record as credible. Posthumously." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and fierce at once. "There's a hearing. April. They asked if the family wants to attend. They asked—" she stopped, laughed, wiped her face with the heel of her hand, "—they asked if *the Wrenfield family* wishes to make a statement."

Delia sat down across from her. Outside, past the kitchen window, she could see the river, and the skeleton of the old bridge coming down piece by piece, and beyond it — she'd read about it in the county paper, everyone had — the new span going up, piers rising out of the water like held breath.

"The Wrenfield family," Delia said, tasting the words. "That's not us."

"No," Lark agreed. "It's really not."

They looked at each other across the table, across the sleeves and pencils and two hundred photographs of a woman who had been three people and been none of them safely, and Delia felt the decision harden under her feet, load-bearing, honest, hers.

"Then we'll have to tell them who to make the statement out to," she said. "No name. Just — the people who catalogued her."

Lark smiled and reached for a pen, and on the back of the envelope, before she wrote anything else, she drew a small blank line — a space to be filled in later, or never — and above it she wrote the only word either of them needed:

*Present.*
