## 2. Inventory of a Departure

The key turned in the front door of Halloran House the way it had always turned—halfway, then a stubborn pause, then the give, the small surrender of the lock that had never once been oiled in my lifetime. I stood on the threshold with my clipboard against my chest like a shield and let the smell of the place reach me before I let myself see it.

Cedar. Old rain. Beneath both, faintly, the ghost of onions cooked low in butter, a smell no house should have retained for twelve years and yet there it was, folded into the plaster like a pressed flower.

I am a professional appraiser of other people's pleasures. I have walked into a thousand dining rooms and described, in language so polished it functions as a mirror, meals I cannot taste. I know how to enter a room and make it yield numbers. That is the whole of my competence: I assign value to things I cannot feel.

So I did what I know how to do. I clicked my pen. I wrote *foyer, 12 x 14, original oak floorboards, minor water damage northeast corner,* and the pen moved and the room stayed at arm's length and for eleven minutes I was fine.

* * *

The auction house had sent me a template. *Itemized inventory required within fourteen days of receipt of notice. Categories: Furniture, Art, Linens, Kitchenware, Books, Miscellaneous.* Miscellaneous. As if a life could be sorted into six boxes, with a seventh for everything that refused.

I worked from the back of the house forward, a trick I'd learned from estate sales—start where the dead person was least themselves, end where they were most. For my mother, that meant starting in the study, where she did the taxes and hated every minute of it, and ending in the kitchen.

The study was easy. A desk, walnut, water-ringed. A lamp with a bent green shade. Filing cabinets that I did not open, because opening them would mean learning what my aunt Sable had already learned about the debts, and I had decided, with the discipline of twelve years' practice, to know only what was necessary. The letter from the auctioneers sat in my bag like a stone. *Halloran House and contents to be sold at public auction, thirty (30) days from date of notice.* Thirty days. I had filed four restaurant reviews in that span of time without breaking a sweat. I could empty a house.

The living room was harder, because the living room had the piano, and the piano had the scratch on its lid shaped like a river delta that my father claimed was a map to somewhere and my mother claimed was where he'd dragged his ring across the varnish, and they had argued about it the way they argued about everything—affectionately, at first, and then, in the last year, not.

*Piano, upright, 1920s, one owner, cosmetic damage,* I wrote. *One owner.* Whose? The piano had outlasted them both, the way furniture does, indifferent and loyal at once.

I catalogued the dining room without sitting in any of the chairs. I catalogued the hall mirrors, the coat rack, the umbrella stand still holding a black umbrella gone to mold, an object so perfectly symbolic that I made a note to describe it in a review someday, if I ever wrote a review that described anything true.

* * *

By late afternoon I had reached the kitchen, and I made myself stand in it.

This was the room I had been circling all day, the room the whole house bent toward, and I stood in the doorway like a guest. The stove was a Wedgewood, white enamel gone cream at the edges. Copper pans hung from a rail my father had installed the year I turned six, and they had darkened to the color of old coins, and I wanted, with a physical ache I located somewhere behind my sternum, to take one down and polish it and cook something in it and taste it.

I cannot taste. I want to be precise about this, because people misunderstand. The machinery of appetite still works; I get hungry the way anyone does. What's gone is the translation—the moment flavor becomes language. Food arrives on my tongue as texture and temperature and nothing else, a letter written in a script I can no longer read. Twelve years ago, on a night I will come to, the script went blank, and I have made a career out of never once admitting it in print.

*Stove, Wedgewood, functional condition unknown,* I wrote. *Copper pans, set of nine, heavy patina.*

My mother's recipe box sat on the counter where she'd left it. I opened it. Index cards in her handwriting, which I had forgotten the exact shape of and recognized instantly, the way you recognize a face in a crowd you weren't looking at—*Cardamom cake, for X., doubles well, do not overbeat.* The instructions were ordinary. The annotations were not. Every card had a second voice in it, a marginal life: *too sweet for S., cut sugar.* *He liked this burnt. Make it burnt.* I stood in my dead mother's kitchen holding a card that said *He liked this burnt* and I did not know who he was, and I did know, and I put the box in my bag instead of the Miscellaneous pile, an act I told myself was professional judgment.

* * *

The stairs creaked in the same four places. Fourth step, seventh, ninth, the landing. I had thought I'd forgotten the house. I had told people I'd forgotten the house. But my feet knew it the way a tongue knows a lost tooth, and each creak arrived a half-second before I expected it, so that climbing felt like being anticipated.

My childhood bedroom had been stripped years ago—Sable's doing, I suspected, or my mother's in one of her clearing moods. A bed frame. A window with the same crack in the corner pane, a crack I had caused with a baseball at eleven and never confessed. I stood there a moment and felt nothing I could name, which I recorded, privately, as nostalgia. Nostalgia is the feeling I use when the real feeling is too large to fit through the door.

The hallway at the end of the hall had two doors. One was my parents' room, which I did not enter. I am telling you the truth of the day: I put my hand on the knob, and I took it off, and I wrote *master bedroom, contents TBD* on the clipboard, and I turned to the other door instead.

The other door had never been a room, exactly. It was my mother's wardrobe annex—a walk-in closet the size of a small bedroom, cedar-lined, where she kept not only clothes but the family's overflow: winter coats, boxes of photographs, the good linens, a sewing machine, and, in my memory, a smell I could not place until this moment, standing in front of it, when I placed it. Cedar and old rain. The whole house smelled the way this closet smelled. The house had been marinating in her.

The door was shut. That was the first wrong thing. My mother had never closed that door in her life; she said a closed closet went stale, that clothes needed to breathe, that a house with shut doors was a house keeping secrets. The door was shut, and the brass hook that had held it open for thirty years hung empty.

I tried the knob. Locked.

I stood in the hallway with my clipboard and my pen and my professional composure, and I heard myself say, out loud, to an empty house, "That's not possible."

Because I knew that lock. Everyone who grew up in this house knew that lock. It was a small brass cylinder, older than the door, and there was exactly one key to it in the world, and my mother wore that key on a chain around her neck until the day she died, and Sable had buried her with it—I had assumed—or lost it, or kept it, but it was not a key that traveled. It was not a key that could be here, on the outside of the door, waiting.

I reached into my coat pocket for my own keys, purely by habit, to let myself out the front and sit in the car and breathe.

They were in my hand before I understood what I was holding.

My keyring is not an interesting object. Front door of my apartment, office, a bike lock for a bike I sold in 2019, a bottle opener shaped like a fish. And one key I have never been able to account for—small, brass, older than the others, worn smooth at the shoulder. I have carried it for twelve years. I have moved apartments four times and transferred it, ring to ring, without ever deciding to. When people ask—and no one asks, because no one interrogates a man's keyring—I would have said it was nothing, a key to a place that no longer exists.

The brass key on my ring was the size and shape of the lock in front of me. I knew it the way my feet knew the stairs.

I want to say I stood there a long time, weighing it. I did not. The house was going quiet around me, the way houses do at dusk, settling, and something in the settling felt less like physics than like patience. I slid the key off the ring. It was warm. Keys are not warm. I have thought about this every day since, and I will report only what happened: it sat in my palm warm as a held hand, and my own hand was shaking, and the shaking was not fear, or was fear wearing the costume of something else, hunger maybe, or hope, two words I had not honestly applied to myself in twelve years.

I put the key in the lock.

It did not turn. Halfway, then the pause, the same stubborn pause the front door made, the family pause, the lock asking its question. And then, from the other side of the door—faint, muffled, unmistakable—came a sound.

Singing.

A woman's voice, low, unhurried, half-lost in the cedar, reciting something that was not quite a song and not quite a list. Numbers. Measurements. A rhythm like a recipe being read aloud to someone who needed it read slowly.

I did not open the door. I stood with my forehead against the wood, listening, until the voice faded or until I stopped being able to hear it, I still don't know which, and then I took out my phone and did the only sensible thing, the professional thing, the thing I do instead of feeling: I pressed record, set the phone on the hallway floor, and went to find out who had let themselves into my mother's closet.

The doorbell rang. Through the front window, in the blue dark, I could see a car I recognized, and a woman getting out of it with a briefcase and an expression I recognized harder—my aunt Sable, arriving uninvited, on the one night the house had started to talk.
