## 4. Recipes for a Lullaby

I didn't sleep in the house the first night. I told myself it was because the guest room mattress had gone soft as wet cardboard, but that was a lie with good posture. The truth was simpler and worse: I was afraid of the wardrobe.

Not of it, exactly. Of what I'd heard through its doors that afternoon—a thread of sound so faint I'd blamed the plumbing, the wind, my own head. A woman's voice, reciting measurements. My mother's cadence, unmistakable even at a whisper's distance: the way she rounded off numbers like she was tucking them into bed. *Two hundred grams. Two hundred, no more.*

So I drove to the inn in town, ate a dinner I couldn't taste—sea bass, some emulsion, something pickled to prove the chef had ideas—and wrote four hundred words in my notebook praising its "quiet confidence." Then I lay on an unfamiliar bed and stared at the ceiling and thought about brass keys until three in the morning.

The second night, I stayed.

* * *

Halloran House at night is a different building than Halloran House by day. By day it performs decay charmingly—dust motes like stage glitter, light doing its sentimental work on the worn stair treads. At night the performance ends. The rooms stop being rooms and become held breath. I walked from the guest room to my mother's bedroom with my phone flashlight on, feeling like a burglar in my own inheritance.

The wardrobe stood where it had always stood, against the north wall, taller than me since before I could remember. Cedar and camphor and, underneath, something warmer—vanilla, maybe, or the ghost of vanilla, which is a thing I shouldn't have been able to smell at all, given that my nose has spent twelve years filing everything under *nothing*.

I put my hand on the door.

And it sang.

Not faintly this time. Not plumbing, not wind. A voice, clear as a radio tuned perfectly between stations, coming from inside the wardrobe the way heat comes off a stove—not located exactly, just present everywhere near it.

*"Flour,"* said my mother, *"two hundred grams, sifted twice, and don't let it clump, Xanthe watches me do this every Sunday and still asks why."*

I sat down on the floor. There was nowhere else for my body to go.

*"Salt—one pinch, one, you always use two, Declan, this is why yours tastes like apology."*

My father's name, dropped into a recipe like a spice. I pressed record on my phone with hands that did not feel like mine.

*"Butter, cold, the size of walnuts. Rub it in with your fingertips only—the palms are too warm, they'll melt it, and then you'll have a biscuit that remembers being dough instead of becoming itself."*

She paused. In the pause there was a sound I knew: her breathing, slightly nasal, the small whistle on the inhale from the deviated septum she never got fixed because she said it made her voice interesting.

*"Don't tell the child about the second batch. The first ones are for practice. Practice is private. Everyone should be allowed to be bad at something without an audience."*

The voice moved on—sugar now, demerara, *"for the crunch, for the sound it makes, listen"*—and I realized I was crying in the way I cry, which is to say not at all, which is to say my eyes were dry and my chest was caving in like a mine shaft. Twelve years of elegant evasion and a dead woman talking about biscuits had brought the whole structure down.

Because here is what I had forgotten, or buried, or professionally amputated: my mother cooked the way other people pray. Out loud. Continuously. Narrating every measurement, every correction, every small act of faith in chemistry. The kitchen was her confessional and her pulpit, and I had grown up inside the sermon without once writing it down.

Now the wardrobe was giving the sermon back.

* * *

By two a.m. I had forty minutes of recording and a new problem: I could taste things.

Not real things. The water glass by my bed tasted of nothing, as always. But when her voice said *lemon zest, fine, like snow*, my mouth flooded with lemon—bright, oily, almost painful, the zest of the specific lemons from the box in the pantry of 1998, the ones with the sticker from the greengrocer on Main Street. When she said *brown butter, watch it, watch it, watch it until it goes quiet*, I tasted hazelnut and danger. Phantom flavors, arriving on schedule, punctual as grief.

I sat on the floor of my childhood bedroom—I had migrated there without deciding to, the way you migrate toward a fire—and tasted a decade of Sundays.

This is what I need you to understand, whoever you are, whatever you think you know about restaurant critics: I have spent twelve years describing food I cannot taste. I built a vocabulary like a scaffold around an empty lot. *Notes of stone fruit. A whisper of smoke. The sauce arrives like a well-rehearsed argument.* I became fluent in a language whose country I'd been deported from. And every review, every glowing, weightless, noncommittal paragraph, was a small betrayal of every cook who meant it—who stood over their stove tasting, adjusting, meaning.

My mother would have caught me in a sentence. She'd have handed me a spoon and said, *Taste it. Tell me what's missing.* And I'd have said salt, guessing, and she'd have known, and said nothing, and loved me anyway, which was its own kind of devastating.

*"Vanilla,"* the wardrobe sang, at some hour past three. *"One pod, split lengthwise. Scrape the seeds with the back of the knife, not the edge—you're not punishing it. You're asking it to open."*

I recorded until my phone died.

* * *

The songs came in order, I discovered over the following nights, though not chronological order. The wardrobe kept its own filing system: recipes grouped by season, perhaps, or by mood, or by some logic only cedar understands. Night one was biscuits. Night two, a stock—"bones roasted dark, dark, darker than you're comfortable with, comfort is not flavor"—interlaced with a fragment I played eleven times:

*"He said he'd make the caramel himself this year. He promised her. Write that down, Marguerite says write everything down, but some things you write down so you can stop carrying them."*

Marguerite. Not me. Someone else—or herself, in third person, the way she talked to the recorder when she was tired. I made a note: *find out who Marguerite is.* Another note: *who is 'her'?* Another note, written smaller: *caramel.*

I did not want to know about the caramel. I wanted the biscuits. I wanted the safe songs, the flour-and-salt songs, the lullabies with measurements. But the wardrobe was not a jukebox. It played what it played, and somewhere in its cedar throat, coiled like a hair in drain, was the night my tongue went silent.

I kept recording anyway. That's the confession of this chapter, if chapters can confess: I knew there was poison in the well and I drank nightly, because the water tasted—finally, impossibly—of something.

* * *

On the fourth night, Sable's headlights swept the bedroom wall at midnight, and I heard her car door slam with the particular violence of a woman who has driven ninety minutes to say something she could have said on the phone.

I met her in the hall, phone still warm in my pocket, forty hours of recordings hidden against my hip like contraband.

"You're staying here," she said. Not a question. Her coat was still buttoned. Under the porch light through the window her face looked carved from something less forgiving than wood.

"I'm cataloguing."

"At midnight."

"The acoustics are better."

Something crossed her face—fast, gone, but I caught it. Not anger. Recognition. She looked, for half a second, like a woman hearing a song she'd hoped everyone had forgotten.

"Those recordings," she said carefully, "are part of the estate. Contents sell as-is. Lot by lot. You know that."

"I know what a cataloguing clause is, Aunt Sable."

"Do you?" She took off one glove, finger by finger. "Then you know that whatever you've found in that wardrobe belongs to the auction house now. To the bidders. To whoever pays." She hung the glove over the banister like a small executed animal. "Some things should be sold fast and untouched, Xanthe. Some things rot slower in the dark."

"In your letter, the house was 'a burden.' In your calls, it's 'an opportunity.' Now it's 'some things.'" I stepped closer. "What is it, exactly? What's in there?"

She looked at the door of my mother's bedroom. Behind it, the wardrobe waited with the patience of furniture that has outlived everyone who loved it.

"Eat something," she said finally, "and get some sleep. Appraisers come Thursday." And she walked back out into the cold, leaving the front door open behind her long enough that the hallway filled with November, and I stood there understanding two things at once.

That my aunt knew what the wardrobe held.

And that she was more afraid of it than I was—which, given that I hadn't slept properly in four days, was saying something.

Behind me, faint through the closed door, my mother began to sing again. Not a recipe this time.

A name. Mine.
