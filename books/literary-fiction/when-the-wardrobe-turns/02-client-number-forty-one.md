## 2. Client Number Forty-One

The bell above my door had a voice like a struck coin, and on the last Tuesday of October it rang for the forty-first time that year.

I knew this because I kept a ledger. Not the tax ledger — though I kept that too, in a drawer that smelled of rubber bands and old receipts — but the other one, the real one, bound in green cloth gone soft as felt at the corners, its spine cracked from years of being opened by hands that were always slightly trembling. Client Number One had been a man who couldn't tell his mother he was leaving the priesthood. Client Number Twelve had wanted to un-invite her sister to a wedding without anyone ever knowing she'd done it. Client Number Thirty-Three had dictated, over four sessions and two boxes of tissues, an apology so long it had to be sent in installments, like a serial novel. And now the bell rang again, and I set down my pen mid-word — *I understand why you could not* — and looked up.

The woman in my doorway was small in the way that old trees are small: not diminished, but pruned down to what would hold. She wore a coat the color of wet slate, wool, heavy for October, buttoned to the throat despite the mildness outside. Her hair was white and cut short and practical, and her face had the particular geography of someone who had once been beautiful in a way that required no maintenance — high bones, deep-set eyes, a mouth that seemed permanently on the verge of deciding something. She carried nothing. No handbag, no envelope, no folder of documents the way most clients arrived, clutching their unsaid things like contraband.

She looked at my window display — the brass stencil letters spelling SANDBURG & ISHIKAWA, LETTERS COMPOSED, ALL OCCASIONS, which was a lie twice over, since there had never been a Sandburg and I composed only the occasions people brought me — and then at me, with the frank appraisal of someone buying a horse.

"You're the letter-writer," she said. Not a question. Her accent was faintly foreign, or perhaps just old; certain vowels rounded where mine flattened.

"I'm Kestrel Ishikawa. Please, sit down."

She didn't sit. She crossed the room instead, slowly, taking inventory of it the way I took inventory of clients: the desk scarred with ring-stains from cups belonging to previous tenants, the filing cabinet whose second drawer stuck unless you lifted while pulling, the wall of postcards pinned above the radiator — blank ones, all of them, forty-one years' worth of blanks I never mailed because they were already addressed. She stopped in front of the postcards longer than politeness required. I felt their silence at my back like a draft.

"Forty-one," she said, reading the small pencil numbers I kept in the corner of each one. "You number them."

"It's a system."

"It's a confession," she said, and turned around. "You count your griefs so you'll know if any go missing."

I should have shown her the door then. That's what I want to say, all these months later, when I understand what she saw when she looked at me. But the truth is less dramatic: I said, "How can I help you?" in the tone I used for everyone, the tone that promised competence and cost nothing, and she finally sat down in the client's chair across from my desk, folding her slate-colored coat around her knees like a bird settling.

"My name is Odile Vann." She pronounced it the French way, oh-DEEL, letting the second syllable fall off a small cliff. "I am eighty-one years old. I have outlived my husband, my sister, every friend I started life with, and two doctors who told me my heart was strong. My heart is not strong. It is merely stubborn, and even stubbornness is running out of arguments." She said all this the way other people recited their phone numbers. "I am here about a language."

"A eulogy?" I asked. "A letter to family? I've written both in languages I don't speak — I work from transliteration, and—"

"No." She cut me off with a small motion of one hand, precise as a page turn. "You misunderstand me entirely, Mr. Ishikawa. Or Ms. I can't tell and it doesn't matter. I am not here to commission anything. I am here to hire you as a student."

The radiator ticked. Somewhere below us, in the bakery that shared our building, someone dropped a tray, and the clatter came up through the floorboards like a small earthquake.

"A student," I repeated.

"Of Ashkari." She said the word and the room changed temperature. I want to write that I recognized it, that some ancestral bell tolled in me, but the truth is I had never heard the word before in my life. It sounded like water going over stones. "It is the language I was born into. It is also the language I will die in, alone, because there is no one left alive who speaks it. There is no written form. There are no textbooks. There is no Duolingo owl, thank God. There is only me, and my mouth, and whatever time remains to me, which is less than either of us would prefer."

I reached for my ledger, because reaching for the ledger is what I do when I don't know what else to do with my hands. "Mrs. Vann—"

"Odile."

"Ms. Vann. What you're describing is a preservation project. That's work for a university, a linguistics department, an archive. They have recording equipment. They have graduate students who need dissertations. I write apology letters."

"Yes," she said. "I know what you write. I've read your work."

"There's nothing to read. It's confidential."

"Not the letters themselves. The shape of them." She leaned forward, and up close her eyes were extraordinary — pale gray-green, the color of the sea before weather. "My niece's husband's business partner received an apology last spring so precisely calibrated, so exactingly worded, that he wept and signed the settlement. He told everyone the letter must have been written by the man himself, because no stranger could have known those things. But a stranger did know them. A stranger listened to a guilty man for three hours and found the sentence he was actually trying to say, buried under the six sentences he thought he was saying. That was you."

"That was craft," I said. "It's a trade."

"It is a trade in unsaid things," Odile Vann said. "And I have spent fifty years sitting on the largest unsaid thing in my possession, and I am running out of time to do anything about it except die, which is not a solution, it is merely an ending." She sat back. "So. Here is my offer. You will come to my apartment three times a week. I will teach you Ashkari. All of it. Every word I carry. In exchange, I will pay you—" and she named a figure so large that I actually laughed, one short syllable of disbelief, before I caught myself.

"That's absurd," I said. "That's — no one pays that for lessons. That's a salary. That's several salaries."

"The fee reflects the difficulty of the position."

"The position is impossible. You can't teach someone a language in — how long are we talking? Months? A year? Language acquisition takes years, immersion, thousands of hours—"

"Then I will give you thousands of hours. Three times a week until I stop, and after I stop, tapes. I have been recording myself for eleven years. Four hundred hours of me talking to a microphone in an empty apartment, telling stories to no one, because the alternative was letting the words rot in my head like fruit on an unpicked tree." Her voice didn't waver. That was the terrible thing about her, I would learn: nothing wavered. Everything was delivered flat and finished, like items read off a list. "You are the only person I have found who listens for a living. Everyone else listens in order to reply. You listen in order to repeat back to people the things they cannot make themselves say. That is the skill. That is the whole skill. The rest is vocabulary, and vocabulary is memorization, and you strike me as a man who is very good at memorizing things he doesn't want to feel."

That landed somewhere it shouldn't have. I picked up my pen and put it down again.

"Ms. Vann. I'm flattered, in a way, but the answer is no. I don't take on projects. I take on commissions. A commission has a deliverable. What's the deliverable here?"

She looked at me for a long moment, and something moved behind her eyes — not softening, exactly. Recalculation.

"The deliverable," she said slowly, "is that when I die, there will be two people who have heard the language spoken, instead of none. Two mouths it has lived in. That is the entire deliverable. That is more than most languages get. Most languages die in one mouth and are buried with it, and no one even marks the grave."

"And why me? Genuinely. You could advertise. You could find an actual linguist, someone young, someone with—"

"Because a linguist would want to study it. Publish papers. Argue about phonemes with colleagues who have never heard it. A linguist would make Ashkari into a specimen." She stood, abruptly, unfolding from the chair with a creak of joints and dignity. "I need someone who will treat it as a grief. Because that is what it is. It is a grief with grammar. And you, Mr. Ishikawa, are clearly fluent in grief. I saw it the moment I walked in. It's hanging on your wall in forty-one numbered pieces."

The radiator ticked. The bakery smell — butter, yeast, something browning — seeped up through the floor, warm and indifferent to everything happening above it.

"I'll consider it," I said, which is what I say when I mean no.

"No, you won't," she said, not unkindly. "You'll turn it over tonight while you're addressing a postcard to a house that burned down seven years ago — yes, I know about that too, this neighborhood talks, the postal clerk is my neighbor's nephew — and you'll decide that it's invasive, and strange, and that the fee is absurd, and you will be correct on all three counts. And then tomorrow you will find yourself wondering what a language sounds like that has no written form, and wondering is a door, and doors are the one thing you can't un-open." She buttoned her coat to the throat with two efficient motions. "I'll come back Thursday. If the answer is still no, I'll accept it with grace, and I will die on schedule, and Ashkari will die with me, and neither of us will blame you. But I think you should know what you'd be refusing."

"And what's that?"

She paused at the door, one hand on the frame, and looked back at me with those sea-before-weather eyes, and for a moment — half a second, maybe less — the flatness cracked, and underneath it was something so raw and enormous that I actually looked away first.

"Everything," she said. "You'd be refusing everything I have left."

The bell gave its coin-struck ring as she left. I sat at my desk for a long time, the afternoon light going amber and then gray, the unfinished apology still open in front of me — *I understand why you could not* — and I found I couldn't remember what came next. Not in the letter. In the sentence. In my own sentence, the one I'd been not-writing for seven years, forty-one postcards long, addressed to a house that no longer existed.

I told myself the irritation I felt was irritation. I told myself the fee was absurd, the request invasive, the woman theatrical, the whole thing a bad fit for a careful trade.

I told myself a great many things that night, standing at my kitchen counter with a postcard and a pen, and none of them were the truth, which was simply this:

I wanted to know what the language sounded like.

* * *

Thursday came the way Thursdays do — with rain, and with Odile Vann standing outside my door at nine o'clock sharp, dry under a black umbrella, holding a single sheet of paper covered front and back in handwriting so fine it looked typeset.

"I brought a list," she said, by way of good morning. "One hundred words in Ashkari that have no English equivalents. To show you what you'd be refusing."

I hadn't said yes. I want that on the record. I had said nothing at all, which in my trade is a professional discipline and in my life is a disease.

"Ms. Vann—"

"Odile." She stepped past me into the office, hung her wet umbrella on the hook that had held nothing but dust since I'd rented the place, and laid the paper on my desk with both hands, the way you lay down a winning card or a dead thing. "Read the first one aloud. Just the first one. If you can read it aloud and feel nothing, I will leave and never come back, and you will have my blessing and my pity in equal measure."

The word at the top of the list was built of sounds I had no keys for — something between a breath and a hum, marked with diacritics she'd invented herself, little flags and bridges over the letters like scaffolding around a building under construction.

"How do I pronounce this?"

"You can't. Not yet. That's rather the point." She sat down in the client's chair, folded her hands, and waited. Outside, the rain wrote its own illegible letters against the glass. "Take your time, Mr. Ishikawa. Time is the one thing I have plenty of opinions about and very little of."

I looked down at the hundred words I couldn't say, arranged in her tiny perfect hand, and felt the specific vertigo of a man standing at the edge of a language — and heard myself ask, before I could stop it, in a voice quieter than I intended:

"What does it mean?"
