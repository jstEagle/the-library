## 17. Two Griefs, One Table

The lesson was scheduled for Thursdays, but grief does not keep a calendar, and neither, as it turned out, did Odile.

I arrived on a Wednesday because I could not bear the shape of my own apartment another hour. The dead-letter office had sent its second notice — *final disposition pending* — and the words sat in my chest like a stone swallowed whole. I had not yet gone to retrieve the postcards. I told myself I would go Thursday, after the lesson, when I had the language to hold them in. This was a lie dressed as a plan, and I think Odile knew it the moment I walked in.

She opened the door before I knocked. She was wearing a cardigan the color of weak tea and her hair was pinned up with what appeared to be a pencil, and she looked at me the way she sometimes did — that long, unembarrassed appraisal, as though I were a sentence she was diagramming.

"You're early," she said.

"I couldn't sleep."

"Good. Tired students remember more. The mind loosens its grip on what it was holding." She stepped aside to let me in. "Also, you look terrible. Sit. I made soup."

The apartment smelled of bay leaf and steam and the particular dust of old paper, which I had come to understand was simply the smell of Odile herself. Her kitchen table — where all our lessons happened — was cleared except for two bowls, two spoons, and a single index card lying face-down between them like a card turned in a game of patience.

I sat. She ladled soup. For a while there was only the small percussion of spoons against ceramic, the radiator ticking, the gray light of the window doing its gray work on everything it touched.

"You didn't bring your notebook," she observed.

I looked down. She was right. In eleven weeks of lessons I had never once arrived without it. The notebook was my armor, my ledger, my proof of purpose — every new word entered in my careful hand, dated, cross-referenced, *inventoried*. Without it I was just a person sitting at a table with an old woman, and there was nowhere for the conversation to hide.

"I forgot it," I said.

"No," Odile said, not unkindly. "You didn't forget it. You left it. There's a difference, and the difference is the lesson." She pushed the index card across the table, still face-down. "But not yet. First, soup. Then you will tell me why the dead-letter office is eating you alive, because you have the face of a woman being audited by her own ghosts, and I am too old to pretend I can't read faces."

I should say here that I had never told Odile about the fire. Eleven weeks. A hundred and forty-some words of Ashkari in my mouth, and I had never once said my brother's name aloud to her. She knew there was an address I wrote to — she had seen the postcards once, early on, when my bag spilled and I snatched them back too quickly — but she had never asked, and I had never offered, and we had built our whole friendship on that mutual courtesy of silence, two people agreeing not to look in each other's drawers.

But the stone in my chest had shifted. The dead-letter notice had moved it. And somewhere between my apartment and her door, in the cold walk up Rue Delacroix, the silence had stopped feeling like a discipline and started feeling like a weight I was asking her to help me carry without telling her what it was.

"His name was Ren," I said.

Odile did not move. She had the stillness of people who have lived alone a long time, who learned that motion is a kind of noise.

"My brother. His name was Ren Ishikawa. He was twenty-two when the house burned. I was twenty-six, and I was awake, and I knew — I *knew* — something was wrong with the wiring, the way the lights had been flickering for a week, the smell, the way the outlets in the kitchen were warm to the touch. I noticed all of it. I inventoried all of it. That's what I do, Odile. I notice the things people can't say and I write them down for other people to deliver. And I noticed every sign of that fire for a week, and I said nothing, because I didn't want to be the person who made a fuss. I didn't want to be wrong out loud."

The soup went cold between us. I hadn't touched mine. Neither had she.

"He was asleep in the back room. The fire started in the kitchen wall at three in the morning and by the time it reached him there wasn't — the investigators used a word, *flashover*, which is a word I have never been able to write for a client, not once in my whole career. I write other people's deaths all the time. Sympathy cards. Eulogies. Letters from the dying to the living. But that word —" My hands were doing something on the table, some small useless motion, and I made them stop. "I have never once been able to say it to anyone."

Odile reached across the table and turned the index card over.

It was blank.

"Look at it," she said. "This is what you have been doing for seven years. A card with nothing on it, addressed to a house that isn't there. I have watched you carry it since the day you spilled your bag in my doorway. You think I didn't see? I am ninety-one years old, Kestrel. I have spent my whole life reading what people do not say. It is the only language I am fluent in besides Ashkari, and between the two of them, silence is the harder one." She set the blank card down between our bowls. "Now. I am going to teach you something, and it is not on any of my hundred-word lists, because I was saving it. I save things too. We are the same, you and I. We are both warehouses."

She rose, slowly, one hand on the table, and went to the shelf above her sink where she kept the materials of our lessons — not a textbook, there was no textbook, Ashkari had never been written down until it started dying in her mouth — but stacks of index cards, rubber-banded, annotated in her looping hand. She returned with a small stack bound in green rubber and sat back down with the care of a woman setting down something fragile.

"Ashkari conjugates its verbs," she said. "As you know. You conjugate for person, for number, for time. You conjugate for distance — whether the thing you speak of is near you or far from you, whether you can touch it or only point at it. You learned that in week three."

"*Seda* for what's close. *Sedal* for what's far."

"Good. And you learned the conjugation for the absent — for the person who has gone away and may come back." She held up one finger. "*Sedash.* Gone, but the door is not closed. Your postcards, Kestrel, if you had written them in Ashkari, they would have been in *sedash*. Writing to someone gone but not closed. That is the tense of the missing."

The word went through me like a needle finding a nerve I hadn't known was exposed. Seven years of postcards, and there had been a grammar for them all along, in a language I hadn't yet learned, waiting.

"But there is a fourth form," Odile said. Her voice changed. It didn't soften, exactly. It *leveled*, the way water levels, finding its true height. "The mourning conjugation. We call it *sedaneth*. When the subject of your verb is dead — truly dead, known dead, not missing — the verb itself changes shape. Not just a suffix. The whole spine of the word bends. *Seda*, I keep. *Sedash*, I keep for someone who is gone. *Sedaneth* — I keep for someone who is dead, and I know it, and I am speaking anyway."

"Speaking to them," I said. "Or about them?"

"Both. That is the point. In English you can hide. You say *I loved him* and the loving happened in the past, in some sealed room, and everyone politely agrees not to ask which room. In Ashkari you cannot say *I love my brother* in the mourning form without the whole verb bending to carry the death. The word itself grieves. Every time you say it. Every time, the language makes you do it again." She laid her hand flat on the table. "People think that would be cruel. A language that forces you to re-grieve with every sentence. But I will tell you what I have learned in ninety-one years: it is the opposite of cruel. It is the opposite of what you have been doing."

"The postcards."

"The postcards. Seven years of *sedash*. Writing to a door that closed a long time ago and pretending it didn't. You built a whole ritual out of the wrong conjugation, Kestrel. You have been writing to Ren in the tense of the missing, because the tense of the dead was too heavy to say. So you mailed your grief to an empty lot and called it devotion." She said it without cruelty. That was the terrible thing about Odile — she could say anything, and it landed like a diagnosis, not an accusation. "But the dead-letter office is going to take them from you. And you know what I think? I think some part of you is relieved. Because the ritual was never for him. He was dead the day after the fire. The ritual was for you — a way to keep the verb unbent. A way to keep saying *I keep him* in the missing form, so you never had to bend the word and say *I keep him* in the form that means he is gone and I know it and I am speaking anyway."

I sat with that. The radiator ticked. Outside, a delivery truck downshifted on the hill, and somewhere a dog was barking at nothing, or at everything.

"Say it," I said. "The mourning form. Say *keep* in it. I want to hear it."

"*Sedaneth*," Odile said.

The word bent. I don't know how else to describe it. The vowels dropped and darkened, the whole architecture of it leaned, and it sounded less like a word than like a word carrying something — a word with a body slung across its shoulders. My eyes were hot. I had not cried about Ren in four years. I had cried *around* him, at commercials, at other people's funerals, in the safe sideways ways, but not at him, not directly, because I had learned early that grief aimed straight is grief that knocks you down and I had a business to run, letters to write, other people's unsayable things to carry.

"Say my sentence," I said. My voice came out wrong, gravel and water. "Say it in Ashkari. *I keep my brother.* In the mourning form."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she said it — slow, syllable by syllable, making me repeat each piece before she joined them — and when the whole sentence finally stood assembled in my mouth, I understood what she had meant about the verb grieving. I could feel the bend of it in my own throat, the way the word for *keep* leaned under the weight of *dead*, and my brother was in the sentence, my actual brother, Ren, who burned at three in the morning because his sister didn't want to make a fuss — and the sentence held him. That was the thing. The sentence held him without dropping him, the way the language was built to do, the way I had never been built to do.

I put my face in my hands and cried at Odile Vann's kitchen table, into the smell of bay leaf and old paper, while a ninety-one-year-old woman sat across from me and did not touch me and did not look away and did not say *there, there*, because she understood, better than anyone I had ever met, that some things should not be comforted. They should be *conjugated*.

* * *

It took a long time for my breathing to come back to its ordinary shape. Odile refilled my soup bowl without asking, the way family does, and I ate it warm this time, and the eating of it felt like a small act of being alive that I had somehow earned back by crying.

"I have a question," she said, when my bowl was empty. "And you may refuse to answer. But I am going to ask it, because we are past the age of politeness, you and I. The signs you noticed — the flickering lights, the warm outlets. You noticed them for a week. Why didn't you say?"

I had asked myself this question maybe ten thousand times and never once out loud. Out loud, it would have to have an answer, and answers are doors, and I had spent my whole life keeping doors shut.

"Because I was afraid of being wrong," I said. "Because calling someone about the wiring means admitting you noticed, and noticing is — it's a kind of exposure. If I said *I think the house is going to burn down* and it didn't, then I was the anxious one, the dramatic one, the one who sees disaster in a flickering bulb. And if it did —" My voice caught on the impossible arithmetic. "If it did, then I was the one who knew. Either way I'd be seen. So I said nothing, and the nothing was comfortable right up until it killed him."

"And now you write letters for a living," Odile said. "You say the unsayable things — but only for other people. Only at a fee. Only at arm's length, in someone else's voice, signed by someone else's hand." She nodded slowly, as if a long suspicion had been confirmed. "You built a whole trade out of the exact shape of your wound. You say everything, Kestrel, and you say none of it. It is the most elaborate silence I have ever seen. It is a silence with a filing system."

I laughed. It surprised me — a real laugh, cracked down the middle, the kind that comes out of people on the far side of crying. "That's what I do. I'm a silence with a filing system."

"Yes. And I am its mirror." She was quiet a moment. Her hands, folded on the table, had the map-veined look of very old hands, hands that had held more than they were built for. "You have given me eleven weeks of your evenings and you have never asked me why I need a student. You think it is because I am the last speaker and I do not want the language to die. That is true. But it is not the whole truth, and the whole truth is heavier, and you have just shown me your heaviest thing, so it is cowardly of me to keep holding mine."

"You don't have to —"

"I do have to. That is the whole point of what I just taught you. *Sedaneth* exists so that no one carries the dead silently. The language forbids it. And I have been breaking that rule for fifty years." She looked at the blank index card between us, the one she had turned over, the one with nothing on it. "There is a sentence in Ashkari. One sentence. I have not said it in any language but Ashkari since the year I said it, and I have not said it in Ashkari since the last person who could have understood it died. It is the reason I need a student. Not the language in general. That sentence. It cannot go into the ground with me, Kestrel. But it also cannot be translated — not into French, not into English, not into anything — because in every other language it becomes either a confession or an excuse, and it is neither, it is *both*, and only Ashkari is built to hold both at once."

The stone in my chest turned over. "What does it say?"

"Not yet." She said it gently, but it was iron under the gentleness. "Not tonight. Tonight you learned the mourning conjugation, and that is enough weight for one table. But I will tell you this much, because you have earned this much: when I finally teach it to you, you will understand why I watched you carry those blank postcards for eleven weeks and said nothing. We are the same, you and I. We both have a sentence we have not said. Mine is in Ashkari. Yours is in English, and it is seven years long, and it is addressed to a burned house." She gathered the index cards, the green rubber band, the bowls, the ordinary debris of an extraordinary evening. "The difference is that mine has an expiration date. I am ninety-one. Yours, you could carry to your grave and no one would ever know it was there. That is what frightens me about you, Kestrel Ishikawa. Not that you might fail to learn my language. That you are so *good* at silence that you could die fluent in it and no one would ever hear the sentence you never said."

I walked home through the cold with her words wrapped around me like the tea-colored cardigan she hadn't offered me, though I had needed it. At my building, I stood in the doorway a long time, looking at the dead-letter notice on my table, at the blank postcards stacked beside it.

Then I sat down and, for the first time in seven years, I did not write to the burned address.

I wrote to a living person. I wrote to the postal clerk, a woman named Beatrice with ink-stained fingers who had shown me, three weeks ago, the shelf where my postcards waited for destruction. One line. In English, plain as a nail:

*Please don't destroy them yet. I'm coming — and I think I finally know what they're for.*

I did not sleep that night. But it was a different kind of not-sleeping than the one that had sent me to Odile's door. Somewhere across the city, in an apartment that smelled of bay leaf and old paper, an old woman was keeping a sentence the way I had kept my brother — in the wrong tense, in the sealed room — and I understood now that she had not taken me as a student so much as she had recognized a fellow carrier, and had decided, with the terrifying practicality of the dying, that two griefs at one table weigh less than one grief carried alone.

The notice said final disposition in nine days.

I would be there when they opened the shelf.
