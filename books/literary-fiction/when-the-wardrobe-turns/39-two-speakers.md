## 39. Two Speakers

In the last weeks, we stopped speaking anything else.

It was not a decision so much as a surrender, the way a river does not decide to reach the sea. Kestrel arrived one morning in March with the light still gray and thin, and instead of *good morning* they said — haltingly, the vowels too round, the consonants arriving a half-beat late — *ashkari veni*, and I understood that we had crossed some border neither of us had announced. From that day on, English became the language of the outside world: doctors, nurses, the television murmuring its weather. Inside my apartment, inside the small warm territory we had built out of grammar and patience, there was only Ashkari.

I will tell you what it is like to hear your dying language spoken badly by someone you love. It is like watching a child walk for the first time across a floor you have swept all your life. Every stumble is a miracle. Every miracle is also a wound, because you know the floor better than anyone, and you know how much of it the child will never see.

Kestrel's Ashkari was terrible. I want that recorded somewhere, in case the tapes survive me and someone mistakes fluency for ease. They said *water* as though it were a stone they were setting down carefully; they said *mother* with an accent that belonged to no country on earth. But they said it. Fifty years I had been the only mouth in the world making these sounds, and now there were two, and the second one laughed every time it tripped, and the laughing was itself a kind of grammar — the conjugation of joy, which Ashkari marks more precisely than any language I have ever heard of, though perhaps I am biased, having loved only this one.

*You sound like a heron learning to sing,* I told them one morning, and they wrote it down before they even translated it, because by then they knew: everything I said might be the last time anyone said it.

* * *

We recorded everything.

The little machine sat on the table between us like a third guest, its red eye open whenever we spoke. Kestrel had bought it in February, after the doctors used the word *trajectory* in that gentle way doctors use terrible words, and at first I resented it. A language is not a specimen. It is not meant to live in a box. But then I thought of the two archival tapes, the dead strangers whose voices had made me weep in a room that smelled of dust and reel-to-reel plastic, and I understood: the box is not where a language lives. It is where it waits. The living happens at the table, between mouths, in the space where a mistake becomes a joke becomes a memory becomes a word only the two of us share.

So we recorded. We recorded the names of tools I had not spoken aloud since Rue Delacroix — the word for the awl Tomas used, the word for the long-handled brush Sefa held in her teeth when her hands were busy. We recorded the songs. There were only four songs, and two of them were lullabies, and one was a joke set to music that isn't funny in any other language because the humor lives entirely in a pun that requires knowing both the word for *bread* and the word for *belonging*, which are nearly identical, which was, I think, the whole point of whoever invented it.

Kestrel sang the bread song badly. Then again. Then again, until the machine had eleven versions, and I said, *the twelfth will be perfect,* and they said, *no, the twelfth will be mine,* and I did not argue, because they were right. That is what inheritance means in Ashkari — not what you are given but what you carry so it isn't heavy alone — and a carried thing always arrives slightly changed, marked by the carrier's shoulders.

Some days the recording was the only thing that got us through. My body had become a series of negotiations: the stairs, the kettle, the distance from bed to window. On the bad days I could not hold the teacup without both hands, and Kestrel would sit closer, and we would speak anyway, about nothing — the color of the light, the neighbor's dog, whether the word for *Thursday* should really be translated as *the day of debts* or *the day of counting* — and the red eye of the machine would blink and blink, keeping its patient record of two people refusing to be quiet.

*Why do you keep talking when you're tired?* Kestrel asked once, in their careful, accented way.

*Because silence was my sentence,* I said. *Fifty years of it. I am not going to serve a single extra day.*

* * *

There is a conjugation I taught them late, almost at the end, that I had been saving the way you save the last of something. It is the form verbs take when two people speak them together — not plural exactly, not *we*, but something more intimate than *we*. In Ashkari, if you say *I remember* alone, it is one shape of the verb. If you say it meaning *I remember and you remember with me*, the ending changes, softens, gains a sound like breath leaving the mouth. There is no English word for this ending. The closest thing in the world to it is the look two people exchange over a grave when one of them begins to laugh and cannot stop.

I taught Kestrel this ending on a Tuesday in April, and they repeated it wrong four times, and on the fifth try they got it, and something went through me like weather. Because I realized: I was no longer the only person alive who could make that sound correctly. If I died that night — and the nights had begun to feel like auditions — the sound would survive. Not in a box. In a throat. In a throat that would someday, if I had done my work, teach another throat.

That night I dreamed in Ashkari for the first time in years without grief in it. I dreamed of the kitchen on Rue Delacroix, the yellow one, where Sefa burned the rice and blamed the stove and the stove, being a stove, said nothing, and everyone laughed in six different registers because each of us laughed differently and the language kept all six. In the dream I was standing at the counter and someone behind me was singing the bread song badly, with a heron's accent, and I woke smiling, which my body had nearly forgotten how to do, and I lay in the gray morning listening to the radiator tick and thought: *soon.* Not soon for dying. Soon for being finished. There is a difference, and Ashkari has a word for the difference, and I made Kestrel write it down three times.

* * *

Marguerite came on Sundays. This had become law without either of us passing it. She would arrive with bread or flowers or complaints about her sister, and she would sit in the good chair, and she would listen to us speak our strange syllables at each other, and at first she had worn the expression of a woman watching a séance. But the weeks softened her. One Sunday she asked — in English, carefully, the way you ask a question you've been carrying a while — *what does that word mean, the one you keep saying?*

Kestrel looked at me. It was the word for *kept*, the first word, the root word, the word the whole language stands on like a house on its foundation stones. I gave Marguerite the translation and watched her turn it over, this woman who had spent a lifetime believing she came from a ruin, learning that she came from a word that means *held*.

After that she started asking for one word per visit. Just one. She wrote them in the margin of her newspaper, in ballpoint, in a hand very like her mother's, and I did not tell her that, because some inheritances must arrive on their own schedule, unannounced, the way spring arrives under winter — already fully decided, merely waiting for the light.

* * *

The last good day was a Monday.

I want to be precise about this, because precision is all I have left to give. Monday the ninth of May. The chestnut trees along the street had gone white-green with new blossom, and the light through my window had that particular quality of late spring, thick and golden, as though the air itself had been sweetened. Kestrel arrived at ten with the recorder and a bag of oranges, and I was strong enough to sit at the table, and we worked for three hours.

We finished the inventory.

This was a project we had begun in March without naming it: a list of every Ashkari word I could still retrieve, written phonetically, defined, cross-referenced, sung where singing applied. It ran to four hundred and eleven entries. Some were whole essays — the mourning conjugations alone took nine pages. Some were single lines: *a word for the smell of rain on hot stone; a word for the pause before someone tells you bad news gently; a word for a guest who has stayed so long they are family, and the different word for family who visits so rarely they are guests.*

At entry four hundred and eleven, Kestrel put down the pen and read the list from the beginning, silently, lips moving, and I watched them do it and felt something I can only describe as the sensation of a heavy object being set down after fifty years of holding. Not gone. Set down. In someone else's hands, where it could be carried so it wasn't heavy alone.

*Say something,* I told them. *Anything. In Ashkari. Not from the list.*

They thought. The light moved on the wall. Outside, a bicycle bell rang twice, bright as a struck glass.

Then Kestrel said — slowly, deliberately, in the language that had lived in one mouth for half a century and now, trembling, lived in two:

*I am here. You are here. The words are between us, and what is between us cannot fall.*

It was not a sentence I had ever taught them. It was built, grammatically, perfectly, out of pieces I had — but the sentence itself was theirs. New. The first new Ashkari sentence spoken on earth since 1972. I pressed my hands flat on the table so the shaking in them would have somewhere to go, and I answered with the old response-form, the paired conjugation, the ending like breath leaving the mouth, and the recorder's red eye blinked and blinked, keeping its faithful record of a language doubling itself in real time, quietly, at a kitchen table, over oranges.

* * *

Tuesday I could not get up.

It was not dramatic. That is the thing about the end of things; they rarely announce themselves with trumpets. I simply woke and my body had closed like a shop, the way bodies do, the shutters coming down one joint at a time. The doctor came and used his gentle terrible words. Marguerite called Kestrel, and Kestrel arrived within the hour, and the two of them arranged themselves around my bed like bookends, like guardians, like — I searched for the word, drifting — like the two halves of a conjugation.

I did not want English at the end. I want that known. The nurses spoke to me in English and I let their voices wash past like traffic. But when Kestrel leaned close, I spoke Ashkari, and they answered in Ashkari, clumsy and clear, and we talked for most of that Tuesday about nothing important — the bread song, the color of the light, whether the twelfth recording had finally been theirs. My voice was small. Theirs was careful. Between the two of us we made a sound that had not existed in the world for fifty years: a conversation. Two speakers. The minimum number a language needs to stay alive, achieved in a narrow bed in a small room while the chestnut blossoms drifted past the window like slow white snow falling upward.

Near evening I grew tired, and I told them so in the honest way of my language, which has no polite fiction for decline. And because I was tired, and because tiredness makes truth cheap, I said the other thing too — the thing I had been saving even from myself:

*When I am gone, do not keep the language in a box. Speak it to strangers. Speak it badly. Speak it in markets and kitchens and buses. A language kept pure dies clean, and I did not survive what I survived for anything clean. Let it get dirty. Let it change. Let it be hers, and hers, and his — whoever comes. That is the only forgiveness I will accept: use.*

Kestrel repeated it back to me, every word, correct. Then they added the paired ending — *and I remember with you* — to a sentence I had spoken alone, and the room filled with a warmth that had nothing to do with the radiator.

I slept. That is the last I can tell you with certainty: I slept, with the language doubled behind my ribs, with the tape reels stacked on the table like a small city of voices, with Marguerite's ballpoint words accumulating in newspaper margins across town, with a student who stayed — because that is what the word for *student* means, one who stays, and they had stayed, they had stayed, they had stayed.

They tell me it was a Tuesday. Of course it was. Tuesdays were always the day of counting, and I had finally counted everything: four hundred and eleven words, five adults traded for one child, fifty years of silence, one sentence carried, one heir found, one language saved — not pure, not clean, but alive, doubled, breathing in a throat that was not mine and would soon be breathing it into others.

The last thing I remember is the sound of Kestrel practicing the bread song in the chair beside my bed, softly, getting it wrong, getting it wonderfully, unforgivably, beautifully wrong — and me drifting off thinking that the mistake was the proof. The proof that it was no longer mine alone. The proof that it would survive me exactly to the degree that it stopped sounding like me.

* * *

What happened next, I know only the way the dead know things — which is to say, I don't, and this chapter ends where I end. But the tapes exist. Four hundred and eleven words exist. A woman named Marguerite has begun writing words in margins, and there is a granddaughter, a small girl with quick hands and quicker questions, who has been asking why the tall quiet letter-writer keeps humming a song about bread.

The language is doubled. The counting is done.

And somewhere across town, on a Wednesday morning, a door is opening onto a courtyard where the light falls just so, and a voice — accented, imperfect, alive — is about to say the first word.
