## 5. The Demolition Notice

My mother kept her papers the way she kept her spices: alphabetized, labeled in two languages, and guarded with a ferocity that suggested each envelope contained either gold or shame. For thirty years I had assumed it was gold. It took her death to teach me it was mostly shame, filed neatly under S.

I found the demolition notice on a Tuesday, eleven days after the funeral, in a folder marked *UTILITIES — MISC.* between a water bill from 1998 and a warranty for a toaster we had never owned. The municipal letterhead was blue and cheerful, the way official cruelty always is. *Notice of Order to Vacate and Demolish. Property: 14 Anahit Street. Structure deemed unsafe and uninhabitable. Owner or heirs shall have six (6) weeks from date of this notice to file objection.*

Six weeks. The date at the bottom was stamped three weeks ago.

I sat on the kitchen floor of my mother's apartment—the same linoleum, the same faint smell of cardamom baked into the walls—and read the letter four times, as though repetition might change the address. It didn't. 14 Anahit Street. The house with the fig tree. The house we had left with the lights on, because my father could not bear to turn them off, as if electricity were a kind of prayer and someone might still come home.

*Owner or heirs.* My mother had never mentioned it. Not once, not in ten years of Sunday phone calls that were themselves exercises in saying nothing beautifully. She had known the house was coming down—had known for weeks, maybe months—and she had carried that knowledge the way she carried everything else about the old country: silently, close to the body, like a stone sewn into a coat lining.

And then there was Elias.

The name arrived in my head uninvited, the way he himself had arrived at the funeral, standing at the edge of the receiving line like a man who has rehearsed a sentence his whole life and finally found the room to say it in. *My condolences to the Vardanyan family.* Uncle Garabed had gone the color of candle wax. Aunt Siranoush's glass had hit the floor and everyone had pretended, with tremendous collective discipline, that glasses break themselves.

He had known. That was the thought that would not leave me alone, pacing the kitchen while the kettle screamed itself hoarse. He had known the house was coming down—that was why he'd appeared now, after decades, holding an old photograph like a passport back into a country that no longer existed on any map. A man does not show up at a stranger's funeral carrying a demolition schedule by accident. Either he had received the same letter—which meant he was family, or heir, or something the word *heir* couldn't quite hold—or he had gone looking for it, which meant he had been looking for us.

Either possibility frightened me more than the other, which is its own peculiar arithmetic.

* * *

I did what I always do when frightened: I made a list. My editor once told me my reviews read like love letters written by an accountant, and she meant it as praise. Lists are how I keep the world at a polite distance—everything itemized, nothing actually touched.

1. Who is Elias?
2. How did he know Mother?
3. Why now?
4. What else is in these papers?

Question four was the coward's question, so naturally I answered it first.

I spent the evening going through the folders with the methodical tenderness of an archaeologist who suspects the dig site is also a grave. Bank statements, all in order. Deeds, transferred twice—once when they fled, once when my father died, the name on the documents changing each time like a snake shedding skin: Vardanyan to Vardan to, finally, Almeida, my mother's last concession, signed in her careful schoolroom hand. Photographs I'd seen before, and photographs I hadn't: my parents young and squinting in front of a car I didn't recognize; a christening; a table crowded with faces I should have known and didn't, everyone mid-laugh, everyone certain the camera was lying about how happy they were.

And then, in a folder marked simply *H*, a hospital bracelet. Small. A child's wrist. Dated twenty-six years ago.

I put it down the way you put down something hot. Twenty-six years. Elias looked twenty-six. I told myself it was coincidence, that bracelets travel, that hospitals are full of wrists. But the number sat in my chest like a swallowed coin.

* * *

Uncle Garabed agreed to meet me for lunch, which surprised me until I understood that being asked questions is a form of flattery to a man who has spent seventy years perfecting his answers.

He chose the restaurant—a place with linen napkins and a sommelier who feared him—and ordered for both of us before I'd opened the menu, which was also an answer of sorts: *I know what you want better than you do.*

"You look thin," he said. "Grief agrees with some people. You are not one of them."

"Thank you, Uncle."

"The review you wrote last week. The little place with the fish." He waved a hand, encompassing my entire career. "Very nice. Very—" he searched for the word, "—tasteful."

From anyone else, a compliment. From Garabed, I heard the second meaning underneath, the way you hear water moving behind a wall. In our family, *tasteful* had always been code for *silent*. We were a tasteful people. We did not make scenes. We changed our names and thanked the new country for taking us, and we did not say the old one aloud except in whispers over card tables, and even then only the good parts.

"Uncle," I said, when the fish had been dismantled, "do you remember 14 Anahit Street?"

The pause lasted exactly as long as a man needs to decide whether to lie quickly or slowly. He chose slowly.

"The old house," he said fondly, spreading his hands as though presenting it. "Of course. The fig tree. Your father's oven. A beautiful garden. We were happy there, before—" and here came the wave again, the gesture that could erase a country, a decade, a brother. "Before circumstances."

"When did you last see it?"

"Why do you ask?"

"A letter came. To Mother's apartment. The city is demolishing it. Six weeks."

Something moved across his face—not surprise. Confirmation. He had known too. Of course he had known; the letters go to heirs, and Garabed had made himself the keeper of heirs the way other men keep bees. He cut his fish with great attention.

"These things happen," he said. "Old houses fall down. Better it falls than stands there making people sad."

"It doesn't make me sad."

"No?" He looked up, and for a moment the charm dropped away entirely, and what was underneath was not cruelty but fear, very old and very well maintained, like a roof patched every winter for fifty years. "Then what does it make you, Beatrix jan?"

I had prepared three careful questions and deployed none of them. Instead I said: "There was a man at the funeral. Tall, dark hair, late twenties. He called us Vardanyan."

The fork stopped. Around us the restaurant went on clinking and murmuring, oblivious, a world away from the small frozen territory of our table.

"I don't recall," Garabed said.

"He knew the name."

"Many people know many names." He signaled the waiter for the check, which was itself a verdict. "You should eat more. And Beatrix—" he paused, paying, leaving a tip that was generous and exact, "—some doors are locked because the house behind them burned down long ago. Opening them only lets out the smoke."

"And if someone is standing in the smoke?"

He kissed both my cheeks at the door, warmly, publicly, the picture of a devoted uncle. "You were always the imaginative one," he said. "Like your brother."

It was the first time in ten years anyone in my family had said the words *your brother* to me aloud. He walked to his car without hurrying, a man who believed, with his whole body, that he had won the exchange.

* * *

Aunt Siranoush was harder. Garabed lied like a diplomat; Siranoush lied like a grandmother knitting—you could watch every stitch and still not find where the pattern turned.

She invited me for coffee, which in our family means an interrogation conducted by both parties simultaneously. She set out the little cups, the cardamom, the plate of sugared almonds that appears at every occasion of grief or joy because in our taxonomy the two are adjacent.

"The tall boy," I said, before the coffee was even poured. "At the funeral."

Her hands went still over the pot. "What boy?"

"You saw him. Your glass—"

"My glass slipped. Glasses slip." She poured with tremendous concentration. "You drink. You're too thin. Grief does not agree with you."

"So Garabed says."

"We are a family that agrees," she said, and there it was—the whole architecture in one sentence, stated plainly enough that for a moment I almost admired it. We agree. We agree on what happened and what didn't, on which names are spoken and which are buried, on which truths get served at the reception and which stay in the kitchen. We had agreed, wordlessly, for thirty years, and the agreement had held the way a dam holds: perfectly, right up until it doesn't.

"Auntie." I reached across and covered her hand, and felt her pulse jumping like a bird under my fingers. "The house on Anahit Street comes down in five weeks. Someone sent a letter to Mother. Someone stood at her funeral and used our real name. If you know who he is—if you know anything—you can tell me. Whatever it is, I'm not a child anymore."

She looked at my hand on hers for a long time. When she spoke, her voice had gone soft and far away, the voice she used for the old country.

"You have your mother's way of asking questions," she said. "Politely. Like a knife wrapped in a napkin." She withdrew her hand gently and lifted her cup. "Drink your coffee, Beatrix. Then turn the cup over. You'll see the grounds tell nothing. That is their gift."

We drank. We talked about her knees. At the door she hugged me hard, harder than usual, and whispered into my hair, so quietly I might have invented it:

"Not everything torn out was torn out to hurt you. Some pages are removed to protect the book."

* * *

That night I went back through the papers one more time, and this time I found what I had been too rattled to see: the demolition notice had been opened. The envelope, tucked behind the letter, bore my mother's initials in the corner where she always marked correspondence she'd dealt with—*R.A.*, in her tidy hand. She had read it. She had filed it. She had done nothing, which for my mother was the loudest possible action.

And beneath the folder marked *H*, pressed flat as a leaf, a second photograph I had missed. The same fig tree, the same garden—but different children. Or rather: the same two children, older. Aram perhaps fourteen, gangly and scowling at the sun. And beside him a girl of nine or ten, gap-toothed, squinting, caught mid-laugh with dirt on her knees.

Me. It was me. I had no memory of the photograph and every memory of the tree—the bark smooth as skin where we'd climbed it, the fruit splitting purple in August, the way Aram taught me to eat them bent forward so the juice ran onto the ground instead of our shirts, and how he'd said, *this is how you eat a fig, Trixie, like you mean it.*

I sat with the photograph until the lamp hummed and the apartment went blue with dusk. Two children by a fig tree. The same photograph Elias had carried at the funeral—I was almost sure of it now, sure in the way you are sure of a taste you can't name. He had stood in that receiving line holding my childhood in his hand.

Five weeks. The house would be dust in five weeks, and whatever was still inside it—the oven, the walls, whatever my mother had left behind when she closed the door and let the lights burn for no one—would go with it.

I took out a fresh page and wrote, at the top, in my most disciplined hand:

*Find him.*

Underneath, after a moment, I added: *Before the machines do.*

Outside, somewhere below my window, the city went on eating and arguing and falling in love, indifferent, and I began—methodically, like an accountant writing a love letter—to make a list of everyone who might know where a man named Elias goes when he wants to find a family that erased him.
