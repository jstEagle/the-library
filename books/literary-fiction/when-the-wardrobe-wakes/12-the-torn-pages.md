## 12. The Torn Pages

The recipe book had been sitting on my kitchen table for three days before I worked up the courage to open it again.

It's a strange kind of cowardice, being afraid of a notebook. This one was cheap—a school exercise book, the kind with the marbled cover and the stitched spine that goes soft with age—and it smelled of cardamom and dust and something older underneath, something like the inside of a drawer where important things are kept. My mother's handwriting marched across the pages in Armenian script, neat as fence posts, and I could not read a word of it.

I want to be honest about this, because honesty is the thing I'm practicing now, badly, like scales on a piano: I could read Armenian once. I could read it at seven, at eight, at nine, sitting cross-legged on the kitchen floor of the house on Vosgan Street while my mother stirred something fragrant and quizzed me from the stove. *What does this say, Beatrix? Sound it out.* And I would sound it out, letter by patient letter, and she would laugh when I got it wrong and kiss the top of my head when I got it right.

Then we came here, and the name changed, and somewhere in the machinery of becoming someone else—the new surname worn like an overcoat buttoned to the chin—I let the reading go. It went the way taste went later: not all at once but by degrees, each day a little less, until one morning I reached for it and found only smooth wall where the door had been.

So there I sat, a woman of forty-one, illiterate in her own mother's tongue, holding a book full of her mother's voice and unable to hear it.

* * *

Mrs. Sarkissian lived two doors down from the old house on Vosgan Street, in a narrow brick row house with a fig tree of her own—smaller than ours, more stubborn, trained against the south wall like a child made to stand straight for photographs. I remembered her from childhood as a mountain: broad, immovable, smelling of coffee and wool, capable of silencing my brother with one raised eyebrow, which was a feat no one else on earth had ever managed.

She was smaller now. The mountain had weathered into hills. But when she opened her door on Thursday morning and saw me standing on her step with the exercise book clutched to my chest like a shield, her eyes went wide, then wet, then fierce, all within the space of a single breath.

"Look at you," she said. "Look at this face. You have your grandmother's chin, do you know that? And you're standing on my step like a stranger. Come in before the cold gets in. Come, come."

Her hallway was exactly as hallways are in the homes of people who left everything once and never again trusted the world to hold what they loved: crowded, warm, and defended. Photographs climbed the walls in mismatched frames. A lace runner protected a table already wearing a cloth. On the sideboard stood a dish of sugar cubes and a brass tray polished to a shine you could check your teeth in.

"You'll take coffee," she said. It was not a question. Mrs. Sarkissian did not traffic in questions where hospitality was concerned; questions implied doubt, and doubt was an insult to both parties.

"I—yes. Thank you."

"Sit." She pointed me toward the sofa, disappeared into the kitchen, and returned with coffee in cups so small they seemed designed for dolls, along with a plate of something I recognized before I saw it—*paklava*, cut in diamonds, glistening. She set it down and watched me with the frank attention of a woman who has spent eighty years watching people eat and drawing conclusions.

I took a piece. I chewed. Filo, walnut, honey, the ghost of orange blossom—all present, all accounted for, reported dutifully by a tongue that might as well have been filing a memo. Somewhere beneath the numbness, I understood, something was weeping. It just couldn't get a message through.

"So," said Mrs. Sarkissian, settling into the chair opposite me with the sigh of a woman taking her rightful throne. "You've come about the house."

I blinked. "How did you—"

"Because everyone comes about the house." She waved a hand, rings flashing. "The city sent its papers. The boy came first—he's been coming for weeks, that one, Elias, polite as anything, asking could he measure things, could he photograph things. Then your uncle Garabed telephoned twice, wanting to know if I'd spoken to anyone, which of course meant he wanted to know what I'd told anyone." Her mouth compressed into a line that suggested exactly how much she had enjoyed those calls. "And now you. The whole family arriving one at a time like guests at a funeral." She paused, and something gentler moved across her face. "Which I suppose it is."

I set down my cup. "Mrs. Sarkissian, I found my mother's recipe book after the funeral. There are pages torn out. And I can't—" My voice did something embarrassing in the middle of the sentence, and I steadied it. "I can't read the writing. It's Armenian. I used to be able to. I don't anymore."

She looked at me for a long moment. Not with pity—that would have been unbearable—but with a kind of recognition, the look of one exile acknowledging another, because that is what we were, all of us who traded our letters for safety: exiles from ourselves.

"Give it here," she said.

* * *

She put on glasses with gold chains and held the book under the lamp, and her hands—swollen knuckles, brown spots, steady as bedrock—turned the pages with a tenderness that undid me more than any words could have. She read silently at first, lips moving, and I watched her face change. Soften. Tighten. Soften again.

"This is Anahid's hand," she murmured. "Your mother. We wrote to each other for years, you know. After everyone scattered. Letters, then cards, then just at New Year's. Hers always smelled of cinnamon. She said she kept the envelopes in the spice cupboard so the postman's hands wouldn't be the last ones to touch them." She glanced up. "You didn't know she wrote to people?"

"I didn't know she wrote to anyone."

"Hm." That syllable contained multitudes. "Well. Here is *boghassa*—the sweet bread, for Easter. Here is *kufta*, the way her mother made it, with the butter clarified twice because once wasn't clean enough for God, she used to joke." A small smile. "Here is lentil soup. She has written at the top—wait, let me see—she's written, '*For Aram, who asked for this every cold Sunday and complained it was too hot and ate three bowls.*'"

The room tilted very slightly on its axis.

"Aram," I repeated.

"He was born in February," Mrs. Sarkissian said, turning another page. "Cold Sundays were his whole biography, that child." She kept reading, and I realized she hadn't heard me—or rather, she had heard me perfectly and was choosing, with great diplomatic skill, to keep going, because stopping would mean looking at me, and looking at me would mean seeing whatever my face was doing.

But then she stopped anyway.

She had reached the torn pages. Or rather, the place where pages had been torn out—the stubs of them still visible in the stitching, ragged-edged, torn recently, I thought, or maybe not, maybe decades ago, torn by a hand that wanted them gone and couldn't quite bring itself to cut cleanly. Mrs. Sarkissian ran her thumb over the stubs the way you'd touch a scar.

"She tore these," I said. "Or someone did."

"Anahid tore nothing," Mrs. Sarkissian said flatly. "Anahid kept everything. She kept your baby shoes, I'd bet money on it. She kept ticket stubs from buses you rode once." She frowned at the stubs. "Someone else did this. Someone who went through her things." She looked up sharply. "Did anyone else handle her papers?"

"My uncle helped sort the house. Before the estate lawyer came."

"Mm." The syllable again, colder this time. "Well. What survives survives. Listen—"

And she began to read aloud.

* * *

Here is what I learned about my mother in the next hour, sitting in a stranger's-warm living room while coffee went cold and paklava went untouched:

I learned that she measured nothing. Every recipe was a rumor of a recipe—"*flour until it feels right,*" "*salt the way the sea taught us,*" "*cook until your husband asks what smells so good, then ten minutes more.*" I learned that she had written jokes in the margins, little arrows pointing at instructions, and Mrs. Sarkissian translated them and laughed at her own translations, and I laughed too, wetly, helplessly, at a dead woman's humor arriving thirty years late.

And I learned about Aram.

Because interspersed among the dishes—between the dolma and the rice pudding, wedged in the way grief wedges itself between errands—were entries that were not recipes at all. They were dated. They were dated *after*. After the estrangement, after the silence, after the family closed around his absence like water closing over a stone.

Mrs. Sarkissian read them slowly, giving each sentence its weight, and I will set them down here as faithfully as memory allows, though memory is a poor stenographer and my hands were shaking.

*"March 14th. Aram would be twenty-nine today. I made the lentil soup and could not eat it. Garabed says time heals. Garabed has never lost anything but his hair."*

*"June 2nd. Saw a boy on the bus with Aram's walk. Followed him two stops past my own like a fool. He got off and I sat there and the driver asked if I was well. I said yes. I am learning to say yes to questions that deserve no."*

*"September. Beatrix's review in the paper today. I bought six copies. I don't understand half the words she uses but I understand her. She writes about food the way her father talked about weather—carefully, so you can't tell what he loves. I wish she'd just say it. I wish we all would."*

I made a sound. Mrs. Sarkissian paused, looked at me over her glasses, and continued, because she understood, I think, that stopping would be the cruelest thing she could do.

*"December 24th. Left the porch light on. Stupid. He doesn't know where we live anymore. We made sure of that, didn't we. Turned it off. Turned it back on. Left it on."*

*"April 9th. Wrote him a letter. Addressed it to the old name, the true name, care of the last address Lena's mother gave me. Did not send it. Put it in this book instead, where at least the book won't judge me. Recipe for forgiveness, page unknown: begin with the truth, add salt, wait forever."*

"There are letters," I whispered. "In the book?"

"Not this book." Mrs. Sarkissian turned another page, and her voice had gone rough. "But she told me once, over coffee, that she wrote him every year on his birthday and every year on yours, and never sent a single one. She said—" Here the old woman stopped and pressed her lips together, gathering herself the way you gather spilled rice, grain by grain. "She said, *'If I send them, then it's done, and if it's done, then he's really gone. As long as they're unsent, he's only far away.'*"

The clock on her mantel ticked. Somewhere outside a dog barked twice and gave up on it.

"She never stopped," I said. It wasn't a question either, but it needed saying aloud, in a room, to a witness. "Ten years. She wrote to him for ten years and mailed nothing."

"And he never received a word." Mrs. Sarkissian removed her glasses and rubbed the bridge of her nose. "You know what I think? I think unsent letters are heavier than sent ones. A sent letter can be refused, returned, burned. An unsent letter just sits in you, getting denser. Your mother was carrying a decade of mail, child. No wonder her heart gave out." She said it plainly, without drama, the way old women deliver the hardest truths—as inventory.

I looked down at the exercise book in her lap, at the marching script I couldn't read, at the torn stubs where someone had decided even this much was too much truth to leave lying around. And I thought about my own reviews, all those years of them—*the chef gestures toward tradition without quite committing; the room suggests a story it declines to tell*—and understood, with a clarity that felt less like insight than like a bone setting, where I had learned it. Elegance as evasion. Style as a porch light you turn off so no one knocks.

My mother had at least left the light on.

"The torn pages," I said. "Can you tell what's missing? By what comes before and after?"

Mrs. Sarkissian studied the stubs, tilting the book toward the lamp. "Before this tear, a recipe for *topik*—the stuffed onions, festival food. After it, an entry dated two years ago." Her thumb hovered. "Whatever was between them, someone wanted it gone. Something from the middle years." She looked at me, and her eyes behind the glasses were shrewd and sorry at once. "You should ask the boy. Elias. He's been going through that house like a man looking for a specific stone in a wall of stones. Maybe he knows what's missing. Maybe—" she hesitated, and chose her next words with visible care— "maybe what's missing is why he came."

* * *

I walked home the long way, past the house on Vosgan Street.

The demolition notice was still stapled to the fence, sun-bleached now, its corners curling like old leaves. Behind it the windows stared their blank stare, and the fig tree leaned over the yard heavy with fruit nobody was picking. Elias's bicycle was propped against the gate. Through a downstairs window I could see him moving, carrying something carefully, the way you carry a sleeping child.

I stood at the fence for a long time, holding my mother's book against my chest, feeling the weight of all those unsent letters I hadn't even read yet—only heard secondhand, translated, filtered through an old friend's trembling voice. Ten years of birthday letters. Ten years of *he's only far away.*

He wasn't far away. I knew now where he had been all along: four years dead, unclaimed, waiting in a storage unit for someone to stop being afraid. Elias knew. Elias had known everything, probably, since before he knocked on our grief at the funeral and spoke the name that made my aunt drop a glass.

Why come to us, then? Why stand in a reception hall full of euphemisms and offer condolences in the true name, and walk away before a single question could form?

Unless he hadn't come for answers at all. Unless he'd come to see, with his own eyes, whether we were worth the letters his father never got.

The front door of the house opened. Elias stepped out onto the sagging porch, arms full of something wrapped in a blanket, and saw me at the fence. Neither of us moved. The fig tree dropped a leaf between us, unhurried, like a period at the end of a long sentence.

"You've been to see Mrs. Sarkissian," he called. Not a question. News travels fast in neighborhoods built by people who survived by knowing everything.

"She read some of it to me," I said. "There are pages missing."

Something changed in his face—guardedness shifting, recalibrating, deciding. He came down the steps slowly, the bundle balanced against his hip, and stopped on his side of the fence, close enough that I could see the photograph-shaped bulge in his jacket pocket, the one he carried everywhere like a passport.

"I know which pages," he said quietly. "I've been trying to find them for three weeks." He looked back at the house, then at me, and what was in his eyes was not anger, for once. It was something rawer. "They were torn out before your mother died, Ms. Almeida. I checked with the estate lawyer. Which means somebody in your family read what she wrote about my father—and decided you should never see it."

He unwrapped a corner of the blanket. Inside, dust-furred and unmistakable, lay a fistful of paper scraps—torn edges, fragments of marbled cover, a corner of handwriting in Armenian script.

"Because I found these," Elias said, "in Uncle Garabed's fireplace."
