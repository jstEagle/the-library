## 4. Postage Due

The lawyer's office smelled of lemon polish and the particular despair of a man who had spent thirty years watching people sign away things they loved. Mr. Okonkwo—call me Dele, everyone does—laid the papers out in a fan like a tarot reading, and I suppose in a way it was one. Here is your past. Here is your future. Pick the card that doesn't burn.

"You understand what you're doing," he said. Not a question. Lawyers phrase their dread as declaratives.

"I'm relinquishing my claim to Fairweather House and all associated lands, funds, and obligations," I recited, "in favor of a trust established for the maintenance of the property as a community archive, per the attached schedule." I had written the schedule myself, at two in the morning, in the kind of handwriting I usually reserved for postcards. Eleven years of cataloguing what the house had cost us had given me a certain fluency in its assets. The orchard. The east field. The piano with the water stain shaped like Portugal.

Dele slid the pen across the desk. "Your aunt will be furious."

"My aunt has been furious since 1987. It's practically a load-bearing emotion in that house."

He almost smiled. Then he watched me sign, and the smile went wherever lawyers keep the parts of themselves that once wanted to be poets. Three signatures. Initial in the margin. Date. My hand didn't shake, which surprised me. I had expected ceremony, trembling, some cinematic moment where the past reached up through the paper and grabbed my wrist. Instead it was just ink, doing what ink does—making a thing true by refusing to be undone.

"There," Dele said, when it was done. "That's the first time I've seen someone look lighter after one of these."

"That's because most people are signing away houses," I said. "I'm signing away a silence. It appreciates in value, you know. Compound interest. Eleven years of not saying a thing turns out to be worth quite a lot."

He didn't ask what I meant. Good lawyers never do.

* * *

Elias Vann was buried on a Thursday in early October, under a sky the color of dishwater, in a cemetery where the grass grew in the stubborn, mown-over way grass does when nobody visiting has the heart to complain about it. I paid for the funeral the way I imagine you pay for anything you can't apologize for enough: quietly, in full, in advance. The funeral home director had asked, with the delicacy of his profession, whether I was family. I said I was the estate. It was true in more ways than I wanted to count.

Marisol came. Of course she came. She wore a gray coat and held a paper cup of coffee like it was the last warm thing in the county, and when the small crowd—smaller than it should have been, because the world had spent thirty years believing a lie about Elias Vann—began to disperse, she found me by the gate the way she'd found me at the radio station: without hurrying, as if she'd always known where I'd be standing.

"You paid for all this," she said.

"I owed him a great deal."

"You owed him the truth. You paid that too. Late, but paid." She looked out at the headstone. *Elias Vann, 1954–2024. Beloved.* I had insisted on *beloved*. The stonecutter had asked, beloved by whom, and I had said, by me, eventually, which he'd found either touching or alarming. "The song," Marisol said. "You told them at the station it was his. His and your brother's. You gave up the credit."

"There was no credit to give. It was never recorded. It lived in one cassette and two heads, and now it lives in one cassette and whoever plays it."

"It's a beautiful song."

"It's a true one. Those are rarer."

She studied me with the particular patience of someone who has sat with the dying and learned that everything important takes exactly as long as it takes. "You know what I keep thinking about?" she said. "He asked me to play that tape for weeks before I found the station. Weeks. And when it finally aired, he was too far gone to hear it. I used to think that was the saddest timing I'd ever witnessed." She sipped her coffee. "But then you showed up at his bedside and told him the truth while he could still nod. So maybe the timing was fine. Maybe the song wasn't for him to hear. Maybe it was bait."

"For me."

"For whoever was still carrying it." She crushed the cup and dropped it in the bin by the gate with a neat, economical motion. "You wrote him postcards, didn't you. Eleven years. You told me that in the hospice hallway, and I've been turning it over ever since. All those confessions, addressed to a house." She looked at me. "You know the post office has a whole department for mail to the dead. Dead Letter Office. They read everything. They just can't reply."

"That's the job I hired myself for," I said. "I'm resigning."

* * *

The shoebox sat on my kitchen table for three weeks after the funeral while I decided what to do with it. Two hundred and six postcards, each one a small rectangle of cowardice rendered in careful cursive. *Dear Teo, today I saw a dog that looked like yours. Dear Teo, I still haven't told them. Dear Teo, the truth is I heard you arguing with Aunt Renata about the will and I went to bed and let the house keep its own counsel and by morning it was speaking in flames.*

I could have burned them. There was a certain symmetry available. I could have buried them in the east field, which now belonged to the community trust, which now belonged, in the way of such things, to everyone and no one. I could have mailed them all at once, a flood of the unsent, and let the postal service's Dead Letter Office drown in my particular weather.

Instead I read them. All of them, in order, over three evenings, the way you'd read the minutes of a meeting you'd been having with yourself for a decade. And what struck me—not the grief, I'd budgeted for the grief—was how good they were. How *written*. Eleven years of practicing honesty on an audience of one dead man had made me a better liar to everyone living, and a better writer to everyone at all. There was a novel in that box. There had been a novel in that box the whole time, and I had been so busy using it as a confessional that I never noticed it was also a manuscript.

*When the Weather Sings*, I wrote across the first page of the first notebook, because a thing needs a title before it can be forgiven.

* * *

The postcard I bought last was a tourist one, of all things—found in a rack outside the pharmacy, glossy, mass-produced, a photograph of this town at golden hour looking better than it has any right to. On the back, in the corner where the publisher prints *Greetings from*, someone had stamped, in cheerful block letters, a price reduction sticker that wouldn't peel off. I bought it anyway. Perfection is for people who haven't burned anything down.

I sat at the kitchen table with the box beside me—lighter now, its contents promoted from evidence to archive—and I addressed it. To myself. My own name, my own street, my own postcode, spelled correctly, which took two tries because my hand kept wanting to write an address that no longer exists, a house that is a garden now, a garden that is a memorial, a memorial that is finally, at long last, just a place where things grow.

*Dear Xanthe,* I wrote.

*You told the truth. It didn't fix anything, which is the first thing they don't put on the posters, and it fixed everything, which is the second. The house is a library now. Elias has a headstone with the right word on it. Marisol plays the song on the radio every year on the anniversary, and every year someone calls in to ask what it's called, and every year she says: "It's called What the Weather Sings, and it was written by two brothers and the man who loved one of them, on the last night the old world worked." You are not in that sentence by name. You are in it anyway. That's what forgiveness looks like from the inside: not an acquittal. An inclusion.*

*No postage due. Nothing withheld. This one is sent.*

*—X.*

I walked it to the postbox on Mill Street, the blue one with the rusted slot that has eaten my confessions by proxy for a decade without ever being asked to. I stood there a moment with the card in my hand, feeling the small absurd weight of it—thirty grams, maybe, of glossy paper and finished business.

Then I let go.

The slot took it the way the slot takes everything: without comment, without ceremony, without asking what it cost. Somewhere inside, it landed on the pile of ordinary mail, bills and birthdays and catalogues, indistinguishable from the correspondence of anyone whose weather had ever cleared.

I stood there a moment longer, hands in my pockets, listening. The wind came down Mill Street the way it always does in October, through the linden trees, and if you listened right—really right—it sounded almost like a melody. Two brothers and the man who loved one of them had heard it once, on the last night the old world worked, and written it down.

I'm writing it down too. That's the whole book. That's the whole weather.

The box rattled faintly behind me, settling its new letter into place, and I turned for home.
