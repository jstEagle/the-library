## 7. Justice Outside the Law

The rain came in off the water sideways that morning, and I stood under the awning of the Salt Hollow bait shop watching a man cross the street like he had all the time in the world and none of it to spare.

I'd been in enough coastal towns to know the walk. Fishermen moved with the sea's rhythm — a rolling gait, shoulders loose, eyes always flicking to the horizon even when they were talking to you. This man didn't have it. He walked like a man from inland trying to imitate one, boots too clean for the mud of a working harbor, jacket too new for the salt air. He stopped at the corner, checked something on his phone, and looked up at the row of buildings across the way with the particular attention of someone reading an address he already knew.

He was asking after Fen. I knew it the way you know weather before it breaks — not by evidence but by pressure. For three days now, Zephyrine and I had been following the trail north: the landlady in Gullwick who remembered a quiet boarder who paid cash and never gave a surname; the crew photo tacked inside the chandlery at Bracken Point, a tattooed forearm half-turned from the camera, the scar tissue catching the light; the bar tab in Ternmouth signed with a backwards *F*, a small stubborn signature of a man who had taught himself to write left-handed so his handwriting wouldn't match the one the world was looking for. Each stop had confirmed more. Each stop had cost Zephyrine something she couldn't name — composure, maybe, or the seven-year architecture of grief she'd built her life inside. She was quieter now. Quieter than a person should be who was two towns away from a brother she'd mourned alive.

And someone else was walking the same trail behind us.

I'd noticed it first in Gullwick. The landlady had mentioned, offhand, that we were the second people that month to ask about her boarder. The first had been a woman, she said, or maybe a man — she wasn't sure, and that vagueness itself had snagged in me like a hook. Then in Bracken Point, the chandlery owner said someone had offered him fifty dollars for the crew photo. In Ternmouth, the bartender couldn't remember faces but remembered money, and someone had bought every copy of the harbor registry going back five years.

Now here was this man, crossing the street toward the bait shop where I'd asked my own questions twenty minutes ago, and I understood that the trail we'd been following had been followed before us — and that whoever walked it wasn't looking to reunite anyone with their family.

* * *

His name was Merrick Voss. He told me himself, later, standing too close in the doorway of the Salt Hollow diner, rain dripping off his collar onto the linoleum. But first he sat down across from Zephyrine and me like he'd been invited, ordered coffee he didn't drink, and laid a photograph on the table between us.

It was old. Creased into quarters that had been opened so many times the folds had gone soft as cloth. A young man grinned out of it — broad-faced, sunburned, one arm slung around the shoulder of a woman laughing just out of frame.

"My brother," Voss said. "Dale."

Zephyrine went very still. I watched her hands under the table, the knuckles whitening around the strap of her bag, and I understood before she said a word that she recognized the name. Whatever the fire had taken, whatever the family had buried along with Zephyr's name — Dale Voss was part of it. Part of what Zephyr did that night, the thing that made erasure the price of survival.

"Dale died in a house fire," Voss said. "Fifteen years ago. Up the coast." His voice was flat, rehearsed, the voice of a man who had told this story to himself so many times it had worn smooth as river stone. "The official finding was accident. Faulty wiring. But the courts never asked why my brother was in that house at two in the morning. Never asked about the argument. Never asked about the boy who ran out the back door with burns on his arms and disappeared off the face of the earth."

"Mr. Voss," I said carefully, "we're not—"

"I know who you are." He turned to Zephyrine, and there it was — not hatred exactly, but something older and more patient than hatred. Grief that had calcified into purpose. "You're the sister. You've got the look. Same eyes." He tapped the photograph once, hard, like knocking on a door. "I spent fourteen years believing that boy died in the fire too. It was easier. Then three weeks ago a man in Ternmouth tells me he saw a deckhand with burn scars matching the description, working boats, keeping to himself. And now I find out a woman and an old fisherman have been walking the same coast asking the same questions." He leaned back. "So you tell me. Is he alive?"

The rain filled the silence against the diner windows. Somewhere in the kitchen a radio played low, a song about leaving that nobody was listening to.

Zephyrine's voice, when it came, was barely above the sound of the weather. "Why does it matter to you?"

"Because my mother died last spring," Voss said, "still waiting for someone to be held responsible. Because the law looked at that fire and saw an accident, and I looked at it and saw a choice somebody made. And because if he's alive — if he's been alive this whole time, breathing the same air, eating the same fish, walking free while my brother is ash—" His jaw worked. "Then somebody owes a reckoning. Maybe not the courts' kind. The courts had their chance."

* * *

After he left — and he left without threats, which was worse; he left like a man who had gotten what he came for, confirmation, coordinates, a direction — Zephyrine and I sat in the car in the diner lot and the wipers ticked back and forth like a metronome counting down something.

"You knew," she said finally. Not accusing. Just knowing.

"I suspected." I kept my eyes on the gray sheet of the harbor. "In Ternmouth. When the bartender mentioned someone buying the registries. I hoped it was a journalist. Or a bill collector. Hope's cheap that way."

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"Because you would have done exactly what you're doing right now," I said. "Which is deciding whether to keep driving."

She turned to look at me then, and I saw how much the journey had changed her. Seven years of postcards written in a dead brother's voice, seven years of grief managed into performance — and now the performance was cracking open, and underneath it was something raw and unresolved, love and fury braided so tight you couldn't tell which strand was holding. She had come this far to say goodbye. Now the goodbye had a shadow attached, a man with a folded photograph and fourteen years of arithmetic to settle.

"If we find him first," she said slowly, "and Voss follows us—"

"Then we lead Voss straight to him."

"And if we stop—"

"Then Voss finds him alone, and nobody's there when it happens."

The wipers ticked. A gull hung motionless over the breakwater, riding wind it hadn't chosen.

"There's a third option," I said, and hated myself a little for how practical it sounded. "We go to the police. Tell them what we know. Let the law do what it didn't do fifteen years ago."

She was quiet a long moment. "Do you believe the law would do anything? After all this time?"

No. I didn't. Statutes of limitation, cold cases, a fire officially ruled accidental — the machinery of justice had already ground past this particular grain of sand, and everyone involved knew it. That was the whole point of Voss. That was why men like Voss existed: because the law's mercy and the law's neglect are often indistinguishable from the outside, and grief doesn't read case files.

"He was a kid," Zephyrine said suddenly, fiercely, and her voice broke on it. "Whatever happened that night — he was seventeen years old, and our parents—" She stopped. Swallowed. There were things she still wasn't ready to tell me, threads from Chapter Four of her own history that she kept close, and I didn't push. "He paid. God knows he paid. Fifteen years without a name. Fifteen years writing postcards to no one because he couldn't sign his own."

"That may be true," I said, "and it may also be true that Dale Voss's brother has a claim. Both things can sit in the same boat. That's the trouble with justice outside the law — everybody thinks they're the judge."

* * *

That night I walked the harbor alone while Zephyrine slept badly in the room we'd taken above the chandlery. Salt Hollow after dark was a handful of lights strung along black water, lobster boats knocking gently against their moorings, the smell of diesel and brine and, faintly, woodsmoke — and the woodsmoke did something to me, tightened something in my chest, because I thought of a boy running out of a burning house with his arms on fire and his name already beginning to die.

I'm not a good man. I want that recorded somewhere, even if only in the privacy of my own telling. I fished these waters thirty years and I've seen what men do to each other over debts real and imagined, and I've mostly looked away, because looking away is how you stay welcome in small towns. When Corliss found me — well. That's another story, and it isn't this one.

But I knew what I had to decide, and I decided it there on the wet boards of the harbor, with the boats knocking their slow wooden heartbeat.

I would not protect Fen from Dale Voss's brother. Whatever reckoning was owed, I had no right to stand between a grieving man and the truth — and no right, either, to hand him a loaded opportunity. What I could do was refuse to let it happen in the dark. If Voss was going to find Fen, it would happen with witnesses, in daylight, with Zephyrine standing between them — because whatever else her brother had done, he had a sister who had written him postcards for seven years, and a man who answers to nothing deserves at least one person answering for him.

And I would get there first.

I went back up to the room, woke Zephyrine gently, and told her to pack. Her eyes in the lamplight were wide and terrified and resolved, all at once, the way eyes get when a person stops being able to turn back.

"How far?" she asked.

"Half a day's drive," I said. "Maybe less, if the storm holds off."

Downstairs, through the window over the sink, I saw headlights sweep the far end of the harbor road — moving north, unhurried, toward the same town we were bound for. Voss wasn't waiting for us to lead him anymore.

He'd found the trail's end on his own.
