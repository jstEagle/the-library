## 4. Verre-sur-Mer Smells Like Salt and Secrets

The M2 spat her out onto the coast road just after nine, and the first thing Theo noticed was that she had been holding her breath since Ashford.

She noticed it the way she noticed most things now — late, clinically, from a slight distance, as though her body were a kitchen she no longer worked in and could only inspect. Her knuckles were white on the steering wheel. She uncurled them one by one and let the windows down, and the sea came in: brine and diesel and bladderwrack, cold and enormous, the smell of every summer of her childhood arriving at once like a creditor.

Verre-sur-Mer. Glass-on-the-sea. The name had always been a small joke the town played on itself — nothing about it was glass, nothing about it was clear. It was a grey-stone town with a working harbour and a tourist season that lasted six weeks and a memory that, she was beginning to understand, had been curated.

The letters were on the passenger seat in a canvas tote she'd bought at the motorway services, because she could not bring herself to put her mother's handwriting in the boot. Thirty-nine remained. She had read the first one eleven more times on the drive, and each reading rearranged the furniture of her childhood a little further. *I saw what I saw, and I said what they told me to say.* Her mother. Her mother, who had corrected Theo's table manners with the severity of a magistrate, who had taught her that a lie was a debt that compounded.

The road dropped toward the sea and there it was, the town, laid out below her like a place in a snow globe someone had forgotten to shake: the harbour crescent, the breakwater, the church spire, the terraced streets climbing the hill in rows like teeth. The ferry terminal was still there, its ramp rusted to the colour of dried blood. The courthouse stood at the top of Fore Street with its Portland stone gone the yellow of old teeth, and even from this distance it seemed to be watching the water, the way it had watched in 1991, the way it had watched while her mother — twelve years old, freckled, terrified — had stood in the witness box and said the words that ended a man's life.

Theo pulled into the car park by the sea wall and sat with the engine ticking and did not move for four minutes.

*You don't have to get out of the car,* she told herself. *You can drive back. You can post the letters anonymously. You can hire someone.*

But she had flown nineteen hundred miles to stand in a prison visiting room and say her surname to a dying man, and she had learned something in that room that no letter had taught her: that the truth was not a document. It was a body. It had to be carried, by someone, into rooms where it was not wanted.

She got out of the car.

* * *

The high street had died the way English seaside high streets die — slowly, then all at once. The ironmonger was a phone shop. The phone shop was a vape shop. The bookshop her mother had loved, the one with the bell over the door and the owner who let eight-year-old Theo read in the corner for hours without buying anything, was now a charity shop with a mannequin in the window wearing a wedding dress the colour of weak tea.

But it was the chippy that stopped her.

There was no chippy.

The building was still there — the narrow, tall-fronted premises halfway down Harbour Street, wedged between the estate agent and what had once been a post office — but the fryers were gone, the counter was gone, the smell that had been the smell of Friday nights for her entire childhood was gone. The window now displayed a hand-painted sign: **VERRE SUR MER ANTIQUES & BRIC-A-BRAC — EVERYTHING MUST GO**. In the glass, faintly, she could still see the ghost of the old lettering where the vinyl had been peeled away: *MAREK'S — FISH & CHIPS — SINCE 1968*.

Marek's. Where her father had taken her every Friday, and where her mother had come to collect them both at seven o'clock sharp, and where Aldo Ferreira had — she counted back, and the arithmetic of it made her dizzy — Aldo Ferreira had worked the fryers before the fire, before the trial, before the whole town agreed to forget his name. She remembered him now, or thought she did: big shoulders, forearms like hams, a laugh that came up from the chest, and the way he used to slide her an extra portion of scraps when her father wasn't looking and put one finger to his lips. *For the little madame. She has good taste, this one.*

She had been six. She had tasted everything then.

Theo stood in front of the dead chippy with her hands in her coat pockets and felt something in her chest crack along a hairline she hadn't known was there. It wasn't grief, exactly. It was more like the moment in a dream when you realize the house you're walking through is your childhood home but every room is slightly wrong — the doors open the wrong way, the stairs go down where the stairs went up — and you understand that the house is a reconstruction, built by someone who read about your life but never lived it.

"Everything must go," she said quietly, to the window. "Yes. I'm beginning to see."

* * *

The courthouse was open. She hadn't expected it to be — she'd imagined standing outside it, taking its measure from the pavement like a tourist of her own catastrophe — but there was a notice taped inside the door about registry office hours, and the heavy oak door swung at her touch with a sound like a held note.

Inside smelled of floor wax and cold stone and, beneath it, something older: the smell of verdicts. The entrance hall was dim, the light coming down through a fanlight in dusty blades. A staircase rose ahead of her, and to the left, behind a glass partition, a woman in a cardigan looked up from a computer and said, "Can I help you, love?" in the tone of someone who has helped nine hundred people and will help nine hundred more.

"I used to live here," Theo said, and heard how strange it sounded, how insufficient. "Years ago. I just — wanted to look."

"Registry's Tuesdays and Thursdays. If it's records you're after, you'll want the county archive in Dover."

"It's not records." She looked past the woman, up the staircase, to where a corridor must have led to Courtroom One. "I wanted to see the building."

The woman's eyes did something small and quick — a flicker, a recalibration. "You'll be one of the Jouberts, then." Not a question.

The floor tilted. "I'm sorry?"

"Fore Street. The solicitor's house. You've got his chin." The woman said it easily, pleasantly, the way people in small towns say things that are actually alarms. "Your dad was in and out of here for years. Good man, by all accounts. Sad business, him passing."

"Thank you," Theo said. Her voice came out level. She had built a career on a level voice; it was the one instrument the numbness hadn't taken. "Do you know if Courtroom One is —"

"Closed for refurbishment. Been closed two years." The woman was already looking back at her screen, and her tone had cooled by two degrees, the way a door cools when it begins to close. "There's nothing much to see, love."

There was nothing much to see. Theo stood in the entrance hall of the building where her mother's testimony had been weighed and found sufficient, where a man had been sentenced to a life that was now, at this very moment, down to weeks, and she felt the building's indifference the way you feel a current under calm water. The courthouse had processed Aldo Ferreira the way a tide processes a hull — without malice, without memory, without stopping.

Outside, she stood on the courthouse steps and looked down Fore Street to the harbour, and the wind came up the hill and pushed her hair back, and she thought: *She walked up this street. Twelve years old. Someone walked beside her.*

She didn't yet know who. But the letters did, and the letters were in the car, thirty-nine of them, patient as landmines.

* * *

She went to the house on Fore Street last, because she knew she would, and because she was afraid to, and because those two facts had been arguing in her chest since Ashford.

The house had been sold within a year of her father's death — she'd signed the papers from London, remotely, efficiently, the way she did everything that hurt — and it had been re-fronted and re-windowed by someone with money and no memory. The door was now a glossy grey. The sash windows were casements. There was a EV charger on the wall where the rose bush had been, the rose bush her mother had deadheaded every October with the secateurs she kept wrapped in a tea towel.

But the house still had its bones, and the bones were right, and Theo stood across the street looking at it while the gulls screamed their one note over the rooftops.

"Can I help you?"

The voice came from the garden next door — the Pellowes' garden, she realized, though the hydrangeas were gone and there was gravel now, gravel and a fire pit — and the man standing in it was old, and broad, and wearing a fleece with a boat club crest, and it took her a full three seconds to place him because he had been forty-five the last time she saw him and forty-five is a country you cannot return to.

Mr. Pellowes. Dennis. Who had taught her to gut mackerel off the harbour wall and told her the sea kept better records than any court.

"Hello, Mr. Pellowes," she said. "It's Theo. Theo Joubert."

And here was the thing she would turn over for the rest of the day, the thing she would examine from every angle like a stone with something underneath it: he looked at her, and she watched him decide not to know her.

It happened in about a second and a half. First the recognition — she saw it land, saw his eyebrows start their journey up — and then the arrest of it, the deliberate closing of the face like a shop shutter, and then the polite blankness of a man addressing a stranger. "Don't think I have the pleasure," he said. "Are you looking at the house? It's sold, I'm afraid. The agent's number's on the board."

"I grew up here," Theo said. "Number eleven. I'm Ted Joubert's daughter."

The blankness held, but it was working now; she could see the effort in it, the way you can see a man rowing against a tide and calling it a stroll. "Joubert," he repeated, as if tasting an unfamiliar word. "Was that the family with the — no. No, I'm not sure I ever — we were away a lot, those years. Boating." He smiled. It was a good smile. It ended the conversation. It was, Theo thought with a cold professional admiration, exactly the smile she used at dinner parties when someone asked about her personal life — the smile that ends conversations. She'd thought she invented it. Apparently it was endemic. Apparently it was in the water.

"Of course," she said. "My mistake. Enjoy the evening."

"You too, love. Mind the wind, it's picking up."

She walked back down Fore Street and he watched her go — she could feel it, the specific pressure of a gaze between shoulder blades, a sensation she'd known in this town since she was a child and had somehow, impossibly, missed.

* * *

The light was going gold and then copper as she walked the harbour wall, and the town was closing itself up for the evening the way it always had — shutters half down, nets stacked, the smell of frying coming from one lone chippy near the slipway, a successor, not the original, trading on a name that wasn't Marek's.

At the far end of the wall, where the breakwater met the old lighthouse, a man was standing looking at the water with his hands in the pockets of an oilskin coat, and Theo knew before she could see his face who it was, because there was only one person in Verre-sur-Mer who stood like that, feet planted, head slightly down, as if the wind were a colleague he was listening to.

Silas Renard. The harbormaster. Retired, her mother's letters said, but harbormasters don't really retire; they just change which water they watch.

She was twenty feet away when he spoke, without turning.

"Wind's coming round to the northeast," he said. "Means rain by morning. Means the boats will stay in." A pause. "You'll be wanting the harbour office. It's shut."

"I'm not wanting the harbour office, Mr. Renard."

"Then you're wanting something else, and I expect I know what." He turned then, and his face was old the way driftwood is old — greyed, cracked, entirely itself. His eyes went past her shoulder, down the wall, the way she'd come, and she understood that he had watched her whole pilgrimage: the chippy, the courthouse, the house, Dennis Pellowes's shuttered face. He had watched it the way a man watches weather.

"My mother," Theo said, "wrote some letters."

The wind pushed between them, carrying the smell of the sea, and Silas Renard looked at her for a long moment, and then out at the water, and then back, and when he spoke his voice was quiet and level and it carried anyway, the way quiet voices do when the words have been waiting thirty years.

"I know," he said. "I helped her post the first one."

* * *

She made him say it twice.

They stood at the end of the breakwater with the light dying and the first drops coming in off the northeast, and Silas Renard told her, in the flat unhurried voice of a man reading a tide table, that in the winter of 1991 her mother — not yet her mother, a frightened girl of twelve who had already said her piece in a courtroom and been told she'd done a brave thing — had come to the harbour office one evening, white as milk, and asked him what a stamp cost for a prison.

"I told her the price," he said. "And I watched her write the address. And I asked her, girl, I asked her plain, whether she was sure. And she said—" his voice caught on it, the first crack in the flatness, "—she said, *I'm sure I'm not sure, Mr. Renard, but I'll be sure later, and I don't want to lose the letter in the meantime.*"

Theo's eyes were stinging. "So you posted it."

"No." He looked at the water. "That's the thing you need to understand about this town, Theo Joubert. Nobody posted anything. Nobody said anything. Nobody saw anything, not even the ones who saw. I kept the letter for her. In the ledger drawer. And she came back for it a week later and took it home, and I never asked, and she never said, and that was thirty years of it — the not asking. This whole town is built on not asking." He turned to face her fully, and his eyes were the pale grey of the sea before weather. "Your mother wrote forty letters and mailed none of them, and I kept the first one in a drawer with the tide tables for a fortnight like a coward, and if you've come here thinking you'll find someone to tell you it's simple—"

"I don't," Theo said. "I've stopped expecting simple."

"Good." He nodded once, slowly, and the nod had the finality of a mooring line being made fast. "Then come to the cottage tomorrow. Not tonight. Tonight you'll go to wherever you're staying and you'll sit with it, because what I've got to show you isn't letters. Letters are what people choose to remember. What I've got is what they couldn't help recording." He was already turning away, coat collar up against the rain. "Nine o'clock. And Theo—"

She waited.

"Whatever Pellowes told you tonight — he told you nothing, and it cost him something to tell you nothing, and he'll be at the Boatman's by eight telling himself he did right. That's the shape of the thing you're fighting. It's not a villain. It's forty years of men telling themselves they did right." He paused at the top of the harbour steps, a dark shape against the last copper light. "Some of them did right. That's what makes it heavy."

Then the rain came properly, and he was gone, and Theo stood alone on the breakwater with thirty-nine letters in a canvas tote getting spotted with water, and the sea below her was black and moving and smelled of salt and something older than salt.

She thought about her mother at twelve, standing where Theo stood now, holding an unstamped letter, asking the price of a prison.

She thought: *I'm the one who posts them.*

She turned for the shore, and behind her the lighthouse came on, one white blade sweeping the dark water, patient as a sentence being served.
