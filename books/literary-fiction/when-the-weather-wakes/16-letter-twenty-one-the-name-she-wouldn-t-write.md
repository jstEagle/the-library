## 16. Letter Twenty-One: The Name She Wouldn't Write

The envelope was different from the others, and Theo noticed it before she touched it.

She had spent two evenings now cataloguing the box with the methodical tenderness of an archivist handling relics — each letter removed, photographed, logged in a notebook she'd bought from the stationer's on the high street, the one that had been there since her childhood and still smelled of paper and glue. Thirty-nine envelopes remained after the theft. She had learned their geography: the early ones stiff and formal, written in her mother's careful schoolroom hand; the middle ones looser, the ink sometimes blotted where the pen had paused too long; the late ones hurried, almost frantic, as though the words were being chased out of her.

This one — number twenty-one in her mother's own numbering, pencilled small in the corner of each envelope like a private filing system no postman would ever see — this one had been sealed with wax. A bead of dark red wax, cracked down the middle, pressed with the blunt end of a teaspoon rather than any proper seal. Her mother had wanted this letter *closed*. Not posted. Closed.

Theo sat at the kitchen table of the house in Verre-sur-Mer with the curtains drawn against the grey afternoon and the sea making its low argument against the seawall. She had not turned on the lights. There was something about reading these letters in half-darkness that felt honest — as though full illumination would be a kind of trespass, or worse, a kind of comfort.

She broke the wax with her thumbnail. It flaked onto the table like dried blood.

*Dear Aldo,*

*I have started this letter eleven times. I have burned six of them in the sink. I am going to write it now without stopping because if I stop I will not start again.*

*You asked me once, years ago, why I never asked questions about the hotel's money. You said I was the only person in Verre-sur-Mer who didn't ask. I want you to know that I did ask. I just asked quietly, and I listened to the answers, and then I wished I hadn't.*

*Marguerite was drowning. Everyone knew it and everyone pretended they didn't, the way this town pretends about everything — politely, with casseroles. The Ferry Hotel was losing money hand over fist. The winter trade died when the ferry route changed, and Marguerite was too proud to sell, and too proud to admit she'd been sold something rotten to begin with. Because here is what I found out, Aldo, here is what has been sitting in my chest for three years like a swallowed stone:*

*Marguerite did not own that hotel alone.*

Theo stopped. Her hand was steady but her breathing wasn't. She set the page flat under the lamp she'd finally allowed herself, angling it so the faded blue ink threw no shadow across the lines.

*There was a silent partner. Everyone in the trade knew there had to be — the hotel was refinanced twice in five years, and Marguerite's name was never enough security for that kind of money. But the partner's name appears nowhere. Not in the land registry, not in the licensing file, not in the insurance schedule. I know because I looked. I looked because Father Okafor came to the house one evening — do you remember him? He buried more secrets than people in this town — and he told me, in that voice he used when he wanted to be heard without ever having said anything, that a man had come to him to make his confession and left without confessing. A man who talked about fire insurance and double indemnity and a building that was worth more dead than alive.*

*A building that was insured, three months before the fire, for nearly double its market value.*

*Father Okafor wouldn't say the man's name. He said the seal of the confessional was the only thing in Verre-sur-Mer that still worked properly. But he said something else, and I have carried it every day since: he said, "Child, when the truth costs someone everything, the town will agree the price was fair."*

*I think the fire was arranged. I think the silent partner needed the hotel to burn, and I think Marguerite either knew and couldn't stop it, or didn't know and paid for it, and either way she is dead and you are in prison and the man whose signature should be on all of this is drinking whisky somewhere above the smoke line.*

*I have tried to write his name. I have tried four times on this page alone. My hand will not do it. That sounds mad. I know it sounds mad. But it is as if the name belongs to someone whose life would end if I wrote it, and mine too, and I cannot — I cannot —*

Here the handwriting changed. The letters grew smaller, cramped, pressing into the ruled lines as though the page itself might give way. Theo leaned closer until her nose nearly touched the paper, and read the next passage twice, three times, her lips moving silently.

*Here is what I can tell you instead of the name. Here are the facts, laid out like evidence, because I was raised by a policeman's widow and facts are the only inheritance I trust:*

*One. The policy on the Ferry Hotel was rewritten in March 1991. I saw the renewal notice in Marguerite's office when I delivered the linen. The premium tripled. Marguerite laughed about it and said her "backer" insisted on full cover, and she made a face when she said backer, the way you make a face when you owe someone you dislike.*

*Two. The fire began in the east storeroom. Everyone says the kitchen, because the kitchen is where Ferreira's fingerprints were, but the first flames were in the east storeroom and the east storeroom door was locked from the outside. I know because I helped carry linens through it that afternoon and Mr. Pell locked it behind us, same as always.*

*Three. Mr. Pell does not exist. Or rather, the man called Mr. Pell exists, but the name is wrong. I checked the staff ledger. No Pell was ever employed at the Ferry Hotel. The man who locked doors and carried keys and stood at Marguerite's shoulder for two years — nobody hired him. Nobody remembers hiring him. He arrived, and then he was simply part of the furniture, and now he is gone, vanished the week after the fire like smoke going up a chimney.*

*Four. And this is the one that frightens me most, so I will write it small: the night of the fire, I saw a car I recognised parked in the lane behind the hotel. I will not say whose car. I have already said too much. But I was twelve years old and I was where I should not have been and I saw it, and later, when they needed a witness, they used me — they took what I saw and they bent it, and I let them bend it, God forgive me, I let them bend it because I was frightened and because they told me it was the truth.*

*So: the name. I cannot write it. But I can tell you how to find it, and I am telling you now because I don't know how much longer I can hold this, and if anything happens to me, someone must know where the stone is buried.*

*Ask the question backwards. Don't ask who owned the hotel. Ask who profited. Follow the money past Marguerite's debts, past the insurance payout, past the rebuild — because the hotel was rebuilt, Aldo, within eighteen months, bigger and better, and the company that rebuilt it shares a director with the company that insured it. One director. One name sitting quietly on both boards like a spider in the corner of two webs.*

*Find the spider. Write the name yourself. Then burn this letter, because I haven't the courage to, and because I love you, and because loving you has already cost us both more than we ever agreed to pay.*

*Yours, always, in the way that doesn't count and won't stop,*

And then nothing.

The signature line was torn away.

Not cut — torn, raggedly, by hand, a strip perhaps four centimetres deep running the width of the page, taking with it whatever her mother had signed herself. Theo held the letter up to the lamp. The tear was old; the fibres had yellowed at the edges, browned the way paper browns when it has been handled wet. Torn long ago, then. Torn by her mother's own hands, most likely, in this very house, decades before her daughter was born into the consequences.

But that wasn't all. Theo turned the stripless page over, tilted it against the light, and saw them: indentations. Ghost-writing. Whatever had been written on the sheet above this one had pressed through — the way a name presses through a notepad when you write hard enough.

She fetched the pencil from her notebook and the soft graphite stick she'd bought for rubbing brasses in churches as a girl, and she shaded the blank space below the final line with slow, even strokes, holding her breath as though breath might smudge the past.

The ghost-writing rose up pale grey out of the shading:

*E.V.*

Two letters. An initial and a letter. Nothing more. Whoever had written on the sheet above had written those two letters — or a name beginning with them — hard enough to emboss the page beneath, and then, presumably, torn the signature away, and then sealed the whole thing in wax with the back of a spoon, and put it in a box behind a wardrobe, and kept it for thirty years like a coal banked in ash.

Theo sat back. The chair creaked. Outside, the sea went on with its one long sentence.

*E.V.*

She said it aloud to the empty kitchen, testing it, tasting it the way she once tasted everything — professionally, hungrily, hunting the note underneath the note. Two letters. Half a name. In a town of barely four thousand souls, how many men could claim an E and a V?

She got up, found the phone directory in the drawer by the fridge — the house still kept one, her mother never trusting anything that plugged in — and ran her finger down the Vs. Vasse. Vasseur. Villeneuve. Three families. She wrote them in her notebook, then crossed out Villeneuve because she remembered the Villeneuves were newcomers, arrived in the nineties after the fire, fish-and-chip people, harmless.

Vasse. Vasseur.

Her father's study was upstairs, untouched since the solicitors had catalogued it, everything squared and dust-sheeted like furniture in a house of mourning. She pulled the sheet off the bookshelves and ran her finger along the spines — law reports, local histories, a fat municipal volume: *Verre-sur-Mer: A Century of Trade and Shipping, 1890–1990.* She pulled it down and it fell open, as such books do, to a place it had been opened many times before. The photograph section. Seafront businesses. And there, third row, second image: the Ferry Hotel, resplendent in its pre-fire glory, and beneath it a caption listing the proprietors' association committee, 1988.

*M. Osei (Proprietress). E. Vasse (Hon. Treasurer).*

Theo's mouth went dry. E. Vasse. Edmund Vasse, if the parish records she'd skimmed at the library were right — the name surfaced now from some half-read register of burials, a death notice she'd photographed without thinking, filed without looking. Dead, then. Dead these ten years or more. The spider in the corner of two webs, folded into the churchyard grass while the town went on calling the fire an accident and a cook an arsonist.

She stood in her father's study with the dust sheet pooling around her feet and felt the fury arrive — not the hot kind, the useful kind. The kind that sharpens. Because this was the shape of it now, unmistakable: her mother had known. Had traced the money, named the mechanism, identified the man — and had torn away her own signature rather than write his name, and sealed the cowardice in wax, and hidden it behind a wardrobe, and let a man rot in prison for thirty years rather than speak a syllable aloud.

No. Theo corrected herself, pressing her knuckles against her mouth. Not cowardice. Fear. There was a difference, and the difference had a body count either way.

Someone had already been through this box once. Someone had taken two letters. If the missing letters were earlier ones — numbers she'd noted as gaps in the sequence — then whoever took them knew the numbering, knew the box, knew the wardrobe panel. Family knowledge. Or knowledge passed down through a family.

*E.V.* Edmund Vasse was dead. But names, Theo thought, pulling her coat off the hook, grabbing her car keys, were like fires. They didn't die. They passed to whoever was standing closest when the wind changed.

She drove down through the town as the light failed, past the courthouse with its lit windows, past the seafront where the new hotel — bigger, better, rebuilt within eighteen months, just as her mother had written — glowed against the darkening water. On its façade, above the entrance, in letters of brushed steel that caught the last of the light:

**THE FERRY HOTEL — RESTORED 1993 — VASSE FAMILY, PROPRIETORS**

Theo pulled over. Sat with her hands on the wheel. Watched a man in a good coat lock the front doors with the unhurried ease of someone who has never once doubted that the building, the town, and the truth all belong to him.

He turned, noticed the idling car, and smiled at her across the darkening road — polite, curious, faintly amused.

It was, she would think later, a smile she recognised. She'd seen it in mirrors her whole life.

* * *

That night she could not sleep. She lay in her childhood bed listening to the house tick and settle, and at two in the morning she gave up, went down, and laid all thirty-nine letters out on the kitchen floor in sequence, a paper river running from the dresser to the door. She knelt among them with her notebook and began to build the map her mother had built and abandoned: dates, payments, the refinancings, the policy, the rebuild, the shared directorship. Where her mother's courage had failed, Theo's fury filled in the lines.

By dawn she had it — the whole architecture of it, sketched in pencil, every fact her mother had scattered across twenty years of unsent mail assembled into a single page. The insurance schedule. The locked storeroom. The man called Pell who had never existed. The car in the lane. The spider on both boards.

Everything except the name written out in full. Everything except proof that would survive a courtroom, a newspaper, a Vasse lawyer's smile.

She photographed the reconstructed timeline, then photographed it again, then emailed the photographs to herself, to her editor, and to a cloud account she created with a password she wrote nowhere. Method, she told herself. Fury is fuel; method is the engine. Her mother had burned six drafts in the sink. Theo would burn none.

At seven, she made coffee she couldn't taste and drank it anyway, standing at the window, watching the harbour wake. And it was then, watching a black saloon car draw slowly past the house — too slowly, once, and then again twenty minutes later — that she understood the other thing, the thing underneath the thing.

Edmund Vasse was dead. But the hotel bore the family name. The son, whoever he was, still owned half the seafront.

And someone in this town had already counted her mother's letters, and found two of them missing, and replaced the wardrobe panel so carefully that Theo herself had almost missed the join.

She picked up her notebook, tore out the page with the timeline, folded it into her coat pocket — not the bag, the coat, the coat went everywhere with her now — and stepped out into the salt wind to find out who Edmund Vasse had left behind.

The seafront office of Vasse Holdings opened at nine. She intended to be the first name in the book.
