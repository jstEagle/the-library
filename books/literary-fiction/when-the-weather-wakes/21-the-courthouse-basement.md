## 21. The Courthouse Basement

The courthouse at Verre-sur-Mer had not aged so much as settled, the way old houses do, sinking a few inches deeper into the earth each decade until the doorframes leaned and the windows no longer quite met their sills. Theo had passed it a hundred times as a girl without ever once wondering what was inside. Courthouses, in her childhood, had been scenery — grey stone, black railings, the flag snapping in the salt wind. They were where the town kept its verdicts, and verdicts, everyone agreed, were like the tide tables: fixed, published, not to be argued with.

Now she stood at the bottom of a stone staircase that smelled of cold plaster and mouse, waiting for Grace Osei to finish arguing with a man named Prendergast, who had been the court's records officer for so long that his cardigan seemed to have grown into him.

'I'm not saying you can't look,' Prendergast was saying for the fourth time. 'I'm saying there's a procedure. There's always a procedure.'

'And I'm saying,' Grace replied, with the patient ferocity of a woman who had spent thirty years being told to wait, 'that my mother was the deceased in this case, and I am her next of kin, and if you'd like me to fetch my solicitor and have this conversation again in a room with worse lighting, we can do that instead.'

Theo watched her and thought, not for the first time that week, that grief had done something structural to Grace Osei. It had not softened her. It had cured her, the way salt cures fish — firming everything, changing the flavour of a person permanently. Three days ago Grace had slapped her across the face in the churchyard. Two days ago Grace had sat at her kitchen table until two in the morning reading her mother's letters with a ruler under each line, as if the ruler could keep the words from moving. This morning Grace had telephoned the courthouse at eight sharp and asked, in a voice like a drawn blade, for the Osei case file.

'You understand,' Prendergast said, weakening, 'that some of these older files aren't complete. Paper degrades. Things get misfiled.'

'Yes,' Grace said. 'Things do get misfiled. Let's find out what things.'

He led them down a corridor that narrowed as it went, past a notice board of yellowed circulars — LOST CAT, BEACH SAFETY, a 1994 appeal for witnesses to a hit-and-run that had never been solved — and through a door marked ARCHIVE in paint so old the R had surrendered. Beyond it, the basement opened into a low room lined with steel shelving, each shelf crowded with grey archive boxes in no order Theo could discern. A single bulb burned at the far end. Somewhere a dehumidifier hummed its one note, faithfully, like a monk at prayer.

'1991,' Prendergast said, running a finger along shelf labels. 'Criminal. That'll be... here. And the coroner's file will be separate — that's over there. You'll sign the access book.'

He laid the box on a scarred wooden table beneath the bulb and retreated up the stairs with visible relief, as though leaving the room before the room could say anything incriminating.

Grace untied the string. Theo noticed her hands were perfectly steady, and understood that this was not calm. It was the steadiness of someone who had decided, finally and irreversibly, to look.

* * *

The file began innocently enough. An inventory sheet, typed on a manual typewriter, listing exhibits: fragments of window glass, a section of carpet, a charred ledger from the hotel office, samples of flooring from the ground-floor corridor. Photographs in glossy sleeves — the Ferry Hotel before, white and gracious above the water; the Ferry Hotel after, a blackened ribcage open to the sky. Theo had seen the after photographs in the microfiche at the library, but here they were larger, crueler. In one, a fireman sat on the sea wall with his helmet in his hands, and the expression on his face was not exhaustion. It was bewilderment.

'He didn't understand what he was looking at,' Theo said quietly.

'Firemen understand fire,' Grace said. 'That's the whole point of them.'

The inventory listed an item that made Theo's breath catch: *Exhibit 14 — Report of Scientific Officer, accelerant analysis, Dr. E. Mabaso, Home Office laboratory, Bristol.* She turned the pages until she found it, and read it twice, and then a third time, her lips moving slightly, the way she had read her mother's letters.

The report was dated the 14th of October, 1991 — eleven days after the fire. It was four pages long. It described samples taken from the corridor floor and the base of the stairwell. It found, in the corridor samples, 'a significant presence of an accelerant consistent with white spirit or a related hydrocarbon solvent, distributed in a pattern indicative of deliberate pouring.' It noted a second, smaller concentration at the stairwell. It concluded, in careful laboratory prose: *The distribution and quantity of accelerant detected are not consistent with an accidental origin, nor with a single point of ignition by an intruder entering via the rear door. The pattern suggests multiple points of origin within the ground floor.*

Multiple points of origin. Theo laid the page flat on the table and pressed her palms against it as if it might fly away.

'Aldo Ferreira was convicted of starting one fire,' she said. 'One. The prosecution's whole case was him and a can and a match.'

'Keep reading,' Grace said. Her voice had gone very quiet.

Because the report did not end there. There was a second document stapled behind it — a one-page summary, unsigned, on court letterhead, dated three weeks later. And the summary said something different. It said that the laboratory analysis had detected 'trace elements of accelerant consistent with contamination from cleaning materials stored on the premises.' It said the pattern was 'not inconsistent with accidental spread.' It described the evidence as, in the end, *inconclusive.*

'Inconclusive,' Grace read aloud, and the word came out of her like a bone. 'My mother burned to death and the evidence was inconclusive.'

'It wasn't,' Theo said. 'Look at the dates. Mabaso's report is the 14th. This summary is the 4th of November. Someone took a four-page report that said *arson, deliberate, multiple points* and turned it into one page that said *inconclusive, probably cleaning fluid.*'

'Who signs it?'

'No one. That's the point. The original report is signed, dated, on laboratory paper. This is on court letterhead with no name. It's not a report. It's a verdict about a report.'

Grace pulled the staple — gently, with her fingernails, as though the paper were a living thing — and laid the two documents side by side under the bulb. Four pages against one. Science against summary. The truth against the tidier thing that had been put in its place.

'Cleaning materials,' Theo said. 'Marguerite Osei ran the cleanest hotel on the coast. You think there was white spirit in the corridor? Of course there was white spirit in the corridor — there was white spirit in every cupboard in Verre-sur-Mer. That's not contamination. That's a story built out of something true enough to pass.'

* * *

They worked through the box for two hours, and the basement grew colder, or Theo grew colder, she couldn't tell which. The dehumidifier hummed. Once, footsteps crossed the floor above them, paused, and moved on, and both women looked up at the ceiling and said nothing.

It was Grace who found the fingerprints.

The trial had turned, in part, on prints — Ferreira's, found on a paraffin heater in the hotel's back store, a heater he had every reason to have touched in the ordinary course of his deliveries, but which the prosecution had presented as the smoking hand. Theo had read the trial coverage until her eyes ached. She knew the print evidence the way she knew a familiar menu.

What she had never known was that there had been a second set.

It was a lab submission form, folded in half and tucked inside the coroner's correspondence — misfiled, Prendergast would have said, if anyone ever asked, though no one ever would. Dated the 20th of October. A request for comparison of 'Set B' — prints lifted from the window frame of the hotel office, the office where the charred ledger had been kept, the office where the insurance papers lived. Set A was Ferreira's, from his arrest record. Set B was unidentified. The form noted, in a junior clerk's hand: *Set B does not match subject. Awaiting further enquiries re: comparison against hotel staff and associates.*

And there the enquiries ended. No comparison against staff. No comparison against associates. No result, no follow-up, no note saying the prints had been identified or eliminated. Set B had simply been filed and forgotten — or filed and *buried* — while the court heard only about Set A.

'He had a heater,' Grace said. 'They had a window.'

'They had a window into the office where the money papers were,' Theo said. 'And someone went through it, and left their fingerprints, and those fingerprints were never checked against anyone except the man they'd already decided to hang them on.'

She thought of her father then — she had been trying not to, all week, and failed in the way you always fail — and thought of his study, and the way he had handled paper. Her father had believed that documents were not records but instruments, and that an instrument's purpose was decided by the hand that held it. She had learned prose from him before she ever learned it from books.

Someone with legal access, she thought. Someone who could walk into this basement. Someone who could staple a summary over a report and fold a lab form into a coroner's file where no barrister would ever ask for it. This was not a crime committed in the dark. It was a crime committed with a key.

* * *

They sat for a while without speaking, the two documents and the two women arranged around the table like a séance. The bulb buzzed faintly. Somewhere in the walls, water moved through old pipes with a sound like slow breathing.

'You understand what this means,' Grace said at last. She was looking at the photographs — her mother, young and laughing, on the hotel steps, in a photograph that must have been an exhibit because it had been in the hotel, in a frame, when the fire came. 'It means it wasn't Aldo Ferreira. It means it was never Aldo Ferreira. And it means someone in this town — someone with a key to this building — made sure we'd never know.'

'It also means it can be undone,' Theo said. 'That's what this is. This is the undoing. A downgraded report, an unlogged set of prints — you can't un-see them once they're in a court record with dates on them. The dates are the fingerprints, Grace. Whoever did this left their own prints all over the paper.'

Grace gathered the photographs into a neat square and squared the edges against the table, twice, three times, the way her mother might once have squared a bedsheet.

'I spent my life hating that man,' she said. 'Every candle I lit for her, I lit with him in it. Thirty years.' She looked up, and her eyes were dry and terrible and clear. 'I want the name, Theo. Not the town's name. The name.'

Theo thought of a signature torn from a letter. She thought of her father's handwriting on a police liaison memo, neat and unhurried, the hand of a man who advised investigations the way he advised everything: quietly, and to his own advantage. She thought of Charles Vasse's warm voice on the telephone, asking after her health, offering to help.

'I'm close,' she said. And found, to her surprise, that it was true — that the letters and the ledger and this cold basement were converging like roads on a map, and at the crossing stood a name she could almost read.

They photocopied nothing — there was no copier down here, and Prendergast, hovering at the top of the stairs, would have had opinions — so Theo photographed every page with her phone, twice, checking each image, while Grace signed the access book in her careful schoolteacher's hand: *G. Osei, daughter of the deceased.* The date beneath it was the first date in thirty years, Theo thought, on which the file had been read by someone who wanted the truth rather than the verdict.

They were at the stairs when Theo stopped.

She had photographed the inventory sheet at the start, mechanically, the way you photograph everything. Now, replaying it in her mind, she saw what she had missed. The inventory listed fourteen exhibits. Exhibit 15 — the final line — read: *Canister, metal, five-litre, empty, recovered from foreshore below hotel, 3 October 1991.*

The canister. The prosecution had never mentioned a canister recovered from the foreshore. The newspapers had never mentioned it. The canister had been logged — and then it had vanished from the case, from the trial, from the story, as completely as a stone dropped into deep water.

'Grace,' Theo said. 'Item fifteen. There's no item fifteen in this box.'

Grace looked at the inventory, and at the box, and Theo watched her arrive at it — watched the understanding move through her face like weather.

'Someone took it,' Grace said. 'Out of a police exhibit. Out of a courthouse. Someone walked it out of here.'

'Or it was never here at all,' Theo said. 'Or it was here, and then it wasn't. Either way, someone with a key decided what the fire was made of — and then removed the one object that would have said it out loud.'

Above them, faintly, the courthouse clock struck four. The sound came down the stairwell and settled over the boxes like dust.

They climbed into the grey afternoon, and Theo stood on the courthouse steps a moment, breathing the salt air, feeling the weight of the phone in her pocket — forty photographs, a downgraded report, an unlogged set of prints, a missing canister. Proof, at last, that her mother's letters were not the grief-mad confessions of a dying woman. They were a record. The case was real now. It had weight and dates and paper.

Her mobile buzzed. A number she didn't know, local prefix.

She answered, and a voice she knew — warm, reasonable, faintly amused — said: 'Miss Joubert. Charles Vasse. I hear you've been spending time in our courthouse. Do give my regards to Prendergast — old boy's been down there so long he's practically an exhibit himself.' A pause, pleasant as a held door. 'I'd love to hear what you found.'
