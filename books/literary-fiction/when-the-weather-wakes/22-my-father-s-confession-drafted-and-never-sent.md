## 22. My Father's Confession, Drafted and Never Sent

The office had been sealed for eleven years.

Theo stood in the doorway of Joubert & Joubert — Solicitors, though the second Joubert had only ever been her grandfather's ghost in the letterhead — and breathed in the smell of a man's life left mid-sentence: cold pipe tobacco soaked into the carpet, legal pads gone soft with damp, a coat still hanging on the back of his chair as if he'd stepped out to post a letter and simply never come back. Which, in a way, was true. He'd walked out of this office on a Tuesday in March, driven home, sat down at the kitchen table, and died between the soup course and the cheese, so quietly that her mother finished serving before she noticed he wasn't chewing.

That had been eleven years ago. Her mother had lived another nine without ever once opening this door. Theo understood now that she hadn't been avoiding grief. She'd been guarding something.

The safe was behind the framed certificate from the Law Society, because her father had been a man who believed that hiding things in plain sight was not irony but craftsmanship. Theo had found the combination written — where else — in the back of her mother's recipe book, under *Coq au Vin*, in her father's small, upright hand: a date. The fourteenth of June, 1991. Not a birthday. Not an anniversary she knew. A date chosen the way you'd choose a headstone.

The dial turned under her fingers with a sound like a knuckle cracking. On the third number, the door swung open on a darkness that smelled of iron and old paper.

She had expected deeds. Insurance policies, the will's duplicate, maybe her grandmother's rings. What she found instead, sitting on top of the deed box like a thing placed there deliberately — placed to be found by someone patient enough to look, or brave enough, or guilty enough — was a single envelope.

No stamp. No address. Just her name, in her father's handwriting: *Théa.*

Not Theo. Théa. The name he'd given her, the name her mother had shortened within a week of her birth because, she used to say, *a girl called Théa will grow up thinking she's a goddess, and no one needs that much confidence.* He had never once used the short version. It was his small rebellion, renewed every time he signed a birthday card.

Her hands were steady until they weren't. She sat down in his chair — the leather exhaled under her, a ghost's sigh — and opened it.

*My daughter,*

*If you are reading this, then either I am dead and you have finally gone poking through things you were told to leave alone — you get that from me, God help you — or something has gone badly wrong and you have gone poking through them anyway. Either way, I find I am glad. I wrote this eleven times and burned ten of them, and the eleventh I could never bring myself to send, because sending it would make it real, and unsent, it is only a draft, and drafts can be forgiven.*

*But you should know what your mother carried. And what I did with it.*

*You were not yet born in June of 1991. Your mother was five months pregnant with you when the Ferry Hotel burned. I want you to hold that fact in front of everything else I am about to say, because it is the only mitigating circumstance I have, and even I do not believe it is enough.*

*On the night of the fire, Marguerite Osei died. Aldo Ferreira was convicted of killing her. Your mother testified that she saw Ferreira near the hotel's back stairs shortly before the flames took hold. Her testimony mattered. I know exactly how much it mattered, because I read the summing-up seventeen times, and the judge returned to it again and again like a man worrying a loose tooth. Remove her evidence and the case does not merely weaken. It dies.*

*I also know, now, that it was false.*

Theo stopped. The office around her seemed to recede, the window going grey with evening she didn't remember arriving. She read the sentence again. *I also know, now, that it was false.* Not *was mistaken.* Not *misremembered.* *False.* Her father had been a solicitor for thirty years; he chose words the way a surgeon chooses blades.

*Here is what I believe happened, assembled from fragments she gave me over years, some drunk, some weeping, one — only one — sober, which is the one I trust.*

*She was at the hotel that night. You will work out why soon enough; you always were too clever for your own comfort, another inheritance from me. She saw someone coming down the back stairs before the smoke started. It was not Ferreira. Ferreira's boat was still on the water; Silas Renard's tide tables would prove it if anyone ever dared look, and no one has dared, because looking is the one crime this town has never forgiven.*

*What she saw frightened her, and she told me, and I made a choice. I told her that if she went to the police with what she'd actually seen, she would be asked why she was there. She would be asked whom she was meeting. She would be asked, eventually, about letters — because those two wrote to each other the way people breathe, carelessly and constantly, and half of them would end up in an evidence bag. And then she would not be a witness. She would be a suspect. Perjury, conspiracy, possibly worse depending on how the Crown decided to read a love affair conducted with a man whose business partner had just burned to death in suspicious circumstances. Pregnant. Twenty-six years old. Her name in every paper from here to Le Havre.*

*I told her all of this not as a warning but as an instruction. That is the part I cannot forgive myself for, and I have stopped trying. I did not protect her from the truth. I protected her from the consequences of the truth, and I dressed the protection up as love, and she let me, because she was terrified and I was the person whose job — whose actual, paid, professional job — was to know how these things worked. I weaponised my competence against my wife. There is a special room in hell for men who do that, and I have furnished it comfortably.*

*And then I did more. This is the part I have never said aloud, not to her, not to God, not to the dark.*

*I advised the investigation. Formally — liaison counsel, a courtesy role, the kind of thing they give a respected local solicitor to keep the town calm. Informally, I did what liaison counsel does: I smoothed. When the accelerant report came back ambiguous, I was in the room when the decision was made to describe it as 'consistent with' rather than 'indicative of.' Those two phrases are separated by a man's whole life, and I watched them choose the wrong one and I said nothing, because saying something meant asking questions, and questions travel backwards toward witnesses, and witnesses led to my wife.*

*When a second set of fingerprints was logged off the stairwell door — partials, unusable, but logged — I noted who handled the exhibit book. I noted it the way you note weather. I did nothing. The entry was later lost. I have never asked whether it was lost or disappeared, because either answer indicts me.*

*I am not writing this to confess to a crime, because I don't believe I committed one. That is the most damning sentence in this letter. I committed no crime. I performed my function, flawlessly, for thirty years, and a man rots in Wolds because the system worked exactly as designed, and I was part of the design. If you take one thing from this, take that: it required no villain. It required a frightened woman, a loving man, and a town that wanted its story tidy. We supplied all three.*

Theo set the letter down on the desk, on top of a dust-free rectangle where a blotter had been, and pressed both hands flat against her thighs to stop them shaking.

The office light had gone fully blue-grey. Somewhere below, the sea did whatever the sea did at this hour — she couldn't hear it from here, but she felt it, the way you feel a train through a platform floor. Her father's chair held her the way it must have held him for decades: patiently, without judgment, which was somehow worse.

He had known. All those Sunday lunches, all those chess games he let her win at fourteen and stopped letting her win at sixteen, all those times he'd watched her eat — *you taste everything twice, Théa, once for pleasure and once for analysis* — he had known what her mother had done, and why, and he had built a life on top of it like a man building a house over a sinkhole, carefully, beautifully, knowing.

And her mother. Five months pregnant. With her. Theo put a hand flat against her own stomach, an absurd gesture, an echo across time. Her mother had stood in a witness box carrying her, and sworn an oath with her free hand, and lied, and the lie had been forged in fear for the child herself. Every year of Theo's life had been purchased with Aldo Ferreira's. Thirty years of birthdays, paid for by a man in a cell who had loved her mother honestly while her mother's honest husband taught her how to lie well.

She picked the letter up again. There was more.

*You will want to know what she saw. I will tell you what I know, which is less than she knew, because she stopped telling me after the trial, and our marriage thereafter was a treaty rather than a union — honourable, functional, and signed in blood neither of us mentioned.*

*She saw a man she recognised. She never gave me the name. She said once, late, that the name was 'written on money,' which I took to mean old family, seafront family, and there are only so many of those. I have my suspicion. I have had it since 1994, when Edmund Vasse bought the Osei family's claim against the insurers for a fraction of its value and everyone praised him for his generosity to a grieving household. Generosity. Yes. I have seen that word do a great deal of work in this town.*

*If you go looking — and you are reading this, so you are already looking — understand what you are walking into. Vasse's son has the charm I had and the ruthlessness I lacked. The town's silence is not cowardice; it is architecture. Verdicts are load-bearing here. Pull one out and you will find out what the building was resting on all along, and it may be resting on you.*

*One last thing, and then I will stop, because my hand hurts and because I have earned the right to stop.*

*Your mother is not the woman you think she is. Neither am I. But she is braver than I ever was — she wanted, for thirty years, to tell. I was the lock. If you hate someone for this, hate me. She was the fire; I was the man who boarded up the windows so no one could see it burning. And I would do it again. That is the truth underneath all the others, and I am ashamed of it in the morning and relieved by it at night, and I expect I will die exactly that way: ashamed and relieved, at the same table, between courses.*

*With all the love I misused,*

*Your father*

*P.S. — The combination to the safe is the date the accelerant report was reclassified. I thought you should know that your inheritance has a date of origin too.*

Theo read the postscript three times before its meaning landed, and when it landed, she laughed — one sharp, appalled note that bounced off the certificate frames and came back sounding like someone else.

The fourteenth of June, 1991. Not a birthday. Not an anniversary. The day the report changed. He had made her memorize it, in effect, filed it under *Coq au Vin* beside her mother's recipes, the two great domestic arts braided together: nourishment and concealment.

She sat in his chair until the dark was total, then turned on the desk lamp — it still worked, a small obscenity — and read the letter a fourth time, a fifth, transcribing it into her notebook in her own hand because some animal instinct told her not to trust machines, not to trust anything that could be remotely wiped. Outside, the harbour lights came on one by one, and she thought about Silas Renard saying *some verdicts are load-bearing, girl,* and understood that he had known precisely which beam her family was.

Her phone buzzed against the desk. An unknown local number. She almost didn't answer.

'Theo Joubert?' The voice was warm, modulated, a voice that had been trained somewhere expensive to sound untrained. 'Charles Vasse. I hear you've been doing some fascinating research into the old days. I'd love to buy you dinner — there's a little place on the front I think you'll appreciate. Say Thursday?'

Theo looked at the letter in her lap. *Written on money.*

'Thursday,' she heard herself say. 'I'd like that.'

She hung up and sat very still in her dead father's chair, holding his unsent confession, understanding that she had just accepted an invitation from the name her mother wouldn't write — and that he already knew she was reading.
