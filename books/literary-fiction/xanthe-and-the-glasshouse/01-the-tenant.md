## 1. The Tenant

The house had begun to smell of other people.

Theo noticed it on the first morning, standing in the hall with her coat still on, a suitcase at her feet like an offering she'd forgotten to make. Three weeks of closed windows had done it — the faint sweetness of cut flowers long past saving, the chalky breath of cold plaster, and beneath it all something older and less nameable, the smell of a house holding its breath. She had grown up inside this smell. Now it met her at the door like a landlady checking her references.

She set the suitcase down and did not pick it up again for some time.

*You'll want the east room,* her father had said on the phone, in the voice he used for arrangements — funerals, solicitors, the disposal of large objects. *Your mother's sewing room. I've had Mrs. Prynne air it.*

He had not said *your old room.* He had not said *home.* Theo had noted the omission the way she noted everything now, with a clerk's dull precision, entering it into the ledger she seemed to be keeping behind her eyes: *Item: one father, careful with words. Item: one house, breathing shallowly. Item: one daughter, unclaimed.*

She carried the suitcase up herself rather than call for Mrs. Prynne, whose footsteps she could hear somewhere below, moving through the kitchen with the brisk tenderness of a woman who had cooked for the dead and did not know when to stop. The east room was exactly as promised: aired, stripped, neutral. A bed made up in sheets Theo didn't recognize. Her mother's sewing table pushed against the wall, cleared of everything except a pincushion shaped like a tomato, its smile faded to a grimace. Someone — Mrs. Prynne, surely — had wiped every surface. The room had been prepared for a guest.

Theo sat down on the bed and understood, with the slow cold settling of a fact long known but never before stated, that this was what she was now. A guest. Three weeks after burying her mother, she had come home to find that home had been let out from under her, quietly, without notice, sometime during the thirty years she'd been away.

She unpacked anyway. Two shirts into the wardrobe. A toothbrush by the basin. She hung her coat on the back of the door, where it swung slightly, adjusting itself to the house's climate, and then she went downstairs to begin the inventory.

*

Her father kept the estate papers in the study, in a green filing cabinet older than she was, and it was there, on the second morning, that the cataloguing began in earnest. Not because anyone had asked her to do it. Because the alternative was sitting in the east room listening to the house tick and settle, and because grief, she was discovering, was a form of energy that demanded conversion — into walking, into lists, into the opening and closing of drawers.

She started with the rooms themselves. It seemed the only honest way. An appraiser's approach: nothing claimed, everything recorded. She took a notebook from the drawer of the hall table — one of her mother's, actually, a slim black thing with a ribbon marker, blank except for the first page where Ingrid Eriksen had written, years ago, in her small upright hand, *Bulbs ordered — see ledger* — and Theo ruled a line under that ghost entry and began.

*Ground floor,* she wrote. *Hall. Coat cupboard contains: umbrellas (3), one broken; boot scraper; box of dog leads, no dog since 2009.*

It was absurd and it was necessary. She moved through the drawing room noting the water stain on the ceiling shaped like a running hare, the piano nobody had played since her grandmother, its lid shut like a held tongue. In the dining room the table stood dressed for a dinner party that had never been cancelled so much as abandoned mid-thought — twelve chairs, the good china behind glass, dust on the crystal like frost on a window. She wrote: *Room built for more people than exist.*

In the library she found her father's reading glasses folded on the arm of his chair beside a book face-down, spine cracked at the halfway mark, and she felt the strange intimacy of it, the evidence of him living here alone these three weeks, marking his place each night in a book about canal engineering, and she closed the library door gently, as if leaving a sickroom.

But it was the glasshouse that pulled at her, the way it always had, visible from every south-facing window like a great recumbent animal of glass and white iron, its panes catching the low November light and throwing it back in pieces. From the terrace it looked asleep. Up close you could see the truth: whole bays clouded with algae, two panes boarded after the storm last spring, the guttering furred with moss. Inside, the air was warm and wet and smelled of loam and standing water and, faintly, of something medicinal — camphor, maybe, or the ghost of some treatment applied decades ago to something long since dead.

She walked the central path with her notebook open. Most of the benches were empty. At the far end, in the palm house where the heat still worked, a few survivors stood in their terracotta pots like pensioners waiting for a bus that had stopped coming: a cycad older than the century, a clivia her mother had talked to, two citrus trees grown gnarled and ornamental, fruiting stubbornly out of habit or spite. A handwritten label in the soil read, in her mother's hand, *Citrus — do not prune, Aldous.* The ink had run and dried and run again, weathered into something almost illegible, and Theo stood looking at it for a long moment, at the instruction issued across decades to a man who had presumably ignored it, and wrote in her notebook: *One label. One commandment. Compliance unknown.*

She laughed — a single syllable, startling in the humid silence — and the laugh turned quickly into something else, and she stood among the dying plants with her hand over her mouth until it passed.

*

Her father found her there. She heard him before she saw him, the particular sound of his tread on the gravel path outside, unhurried, deliberate, a man who had never once hurried anywhere in his life on the theory that hurry implied the possibility of being late. He appeared in the doorway of the palm house with his hands in his pockets, silhouetted against the grey sky, and looked at her looking at the citrus trees.

"You've found the labels," he said.

"Some of them."

"She labeled everything." He came down the path, his shoes leaving dark prints on the damp flagstones. "Toward the end she was labeling things that didn't need it. The spade. The wheelbarrow." He paused beside the cycad and rested one finger, very lightly, on a frond, the way a man might touch the shoulder of someone sleeping. "I keep expecting to turn a corner and find a label on me."

It was the closest thing to a joke she'd heard him make since the funeral, and it landed strangely, half-tender and half-warning, and Theo couldn't have said which half he intended.

"I'm making an inventory," she said. "Of the house. For the solicitor. Eventually."

"Eventually," her father repeated. He looked at her notebook, and she was aware of him reading it upside down, aware of the childish safety of the list, the umbrellas and the boot scraper, and she turned a page as though to say: nothing here but furniture. "Nell Okafor will want more than furniture eventually. But there's no rush." He said it smoothly, and then, watching her too carefully: "There's no rush, is there?"

"No rush," Theo agreed.

He nodded slowly, and she had the distinct sensation — familiar from childhood, from every conversation they had ever conducted across the buffer of her mother — that they were playing a game whose rules he had written and she had never been shown. "Mrs. Prynne does a lamb on Thursdays," he said. "You'll eat with me?"

"Yes," she said. "Thank you."

"Good." He touched the cycad frond once more, a benediction or a farewell, and left the way he'd come, unhurried, his footprints drying behind him on the stones.

She watched him go and entered it in the ledger behind her eyes: *Item: one father, asking questions that are not questions.*

*

The lamb on Thursday was excellent and nearly silent. They ate in the kitchen, which surprised her — the dining room with its twelve chairs apparently reserved now for ghosts and developers — and her father carved with the competence of a man performing surgery on something he respected. Mrs. Prynne had gone home. Rain came against the window in gusts. Twice her father opened his mouth to speak, and twice thought better of it, and Theo catalogued both aborted sentences.

On the third attempt he said: "How long are you staying?"

It was not, on its surface, unwelcoming. That was the skill of it. Delivered another way it could have meant *stay forever.* Delivered this way — mildly, over the rim of his wineglass, eyes on the rain — it meant something closer to *state your term, tenant.*

"I don't know," Theo said. "Until things are settled."

"The estate will take months. Years, if we're unlucky."

"Then perhaps years."

He set down the glass. He was not a large man but he filled rooms the way furniture does, by simply being dense where he stood. "You always did answer questions with weather," he said. "'Perhaps.' 'Possibly.' 'We'll see.' Your mother was the same. I spent forty years married to a forecast."

"And how was the climate?"

Something moved behind his face — too fast to name, gone before it arrived. "Changeable," he said, and refilled her glass without asking, which was its own kind of sentence.

Later, washing up while he dried — a domestic choreography unchanged since she was twelve, and therefore unbearable — he mentioned, casually, that he'd had a letter from a firm called Halecrest Developments. That they were interested in the estate. That he hadn't replied yet.

"Interested how?"

"Interested," he said, "the way people are interested when they smell blood in the water. Or roses. With Halecrest it's difficult to tell the difference." He handed her a plate to shelve. "Nothing for you to concern yourself with."

"I live here now."

"For now," he agreed pleasantly, and the word *tenant* hung between them, unspoken, enormous.

*

That night she lay awake in the east room listening to the house. It spoke constantly once you attended to it — pipes knocking somewhere in the walls like a polite visitor, the boiler's asthmatic cycling, wind finding the gap under the eaves and worrying it. Above her head, through the ceiling, lay her mother's room, untouched since the funeral directors had done what funeral directors do, and Theo found herself mapping it in the dark: the bed, the wardrobe along the north wall, the window with its view of the glasshouse roof shining in whatever light there was.

She had not been able to go in yet. She had stood at its threshold twice, notebook in hand, ready to begin the appraiser's work — *principal bedroom, north aspect, contents as follows* — and twice her body had declined to cross, some ancient jurisdiction asserting itself, the room still hers, still her mother's, still not yet history.

Tomorrow, she told the ceiling. Tomorrow I'll start at the top of the house and work down. Every room. Everything written down. Nothing claimed.

From the garden came a sound she knew from childhood and had not heard in thirty years: the wind moving through the glasshouse, a vast low exhalation, as if the building itself were breathing in its sleep. As a girl she had believed the glasshouse breathed. Her mother had never corrected her. Her mother had believed it too, probably. Her mother had believed in the aliveness of all kinds of things the rest of the family had agreed to call dead.

Theo slept finally near dawn, and dreamed of a corridor of doors, all of them labeled in her mother's handwriting, and none of them bearing her own name.

*

The next morning she climbed the back stair to the top of the house and began where she had promised herself she would: the attic, then the linen room, then, working down, the bedrooms, hers last of all. She catalogued trunks and christening gowns, a sled with a cracked runner, boxes of Christmas decorations wrapped in newspaper from 1987. In her childhood bedroom the wallpaper — yellow crocuses on a white ground, chosen by her mother, naturally — had faded to a pale memory of itself, and someone had pinned a corkboard over the worst patch, and on the corkboard were pushpins holding nothing, holes in a map of a country that no longer existed.

She wrote it all down. She was halfway through the landing cupboard — *one hot-water bottle, perished; one hatbox containing hats (2) and, inexplicably, a badminton racket* — when she heard her father's step on the stair below, pausing, she realized, directly outside her mother's closed door.

She went still. Through the floorboards she heard the handle turn, the door open, the long pause of a man standing in a room where everything was exactly as he had left it three weeks ago and nothing was ever going to be right again. Then the door closed, softly, and the step resumed, descending, and Theo stood in the dark of the landing cupboard among the hats and the badminton racket with her pen uncapped, understanding suddenly that she was not the only one taking inventory of this house.

They were both appraisers now. The difference was that she was pricing the past to let it go, and he was pricing it to keep it — and neither of them, she suspected, had yet opened the one room that mattered.

She finished the landing cupboard. Then she walked to her mother's door, put her hand flat against the painted wood — cool, smooth, indifferent — and stood there long enough for the house to register the touch, the way it registered everything, and keep it.

Inside, behind the wardrobe, something waited that she would not find for another two days: a loose panel, a tin box, forty-one letters addressed to a daughter who had stopped believing she was loved. But that morning Theo knew none of it. That morning she only knew that the house was full of rooms, and every room was full of her mother, and her mother was full of secrets, and the inventory had barely begun.

She took out her notebook, ruled a fresh line, and wrote:

*Principal bedroom. North aspect. Not yet entered.*

And beneath it, after a moment, in smaller letters, as if confessing to the page:

*I am afraid of what she kept.*
