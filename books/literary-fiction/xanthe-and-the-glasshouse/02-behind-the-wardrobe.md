## 2. Behind the Wardrobe

The wardrobe was the last thing in the room that still smelled of her mother.

Theo discovered this on the ninth day of clearing, when she opened its doors to inventory the contents and the scent came out to meet her — cedar and lavender and something underneath both, something warmer, like bread left to cool on a windowsill. She stood with her hand on the door for a long moment, breathing it in, aware of how absurd she must look: a forty-one-year-old woman inhaling a piece of furniture as though it might exhale back.

It didn't. Of course it didn't. The dead did not exhale. They left their smell behind in wood and wool and let the living do all the breathing.

She had been cataloguing the room the way she catalogued everything now — in the flat, procedural voice of an appraiser who had no personal stake in what she priced. *Wardrobe, mahogany veneer, circa 1930s, water damage to lower left panel.* *Dressing table, mirror foxed at edges.* *Jewelry box, contents: two strands of pearls, one gold locket (empty), assorted earrings, pairs unknown.* It was easier this way. If the room was inventory, then the room was not her mother's, and if the room was not her mother's, then her mother had not died in the house three weeks ago Tuesday, and if her mother had not died —

But the scent undid the procedure. Scent could not be itemized. Scent went straight past the ledger in her head and into the older rooms beneath it.

"Damn," she said quietly, to nobody, and began pulling out hangers.

* * *

Her mother's clothes were organized with a precision that made Theo's chest ache. Everything by season, everything by color within season, everything wrapped in dry-cleaner's plastic except the oldest things at the back, which hung loose and soft with age. There were coats here from before Theo's birth. A green wool dress with a tag from a shop in Copenhagen that had closed before Theo could walk. A man's cardigan, grey, far too large for Ingrid Eriksen, pushed to the very back corner where the rod met the wall, as though it had been hidden rather than stored.

Theo held the cardigan up. It smelled of nothing but dust and decades. Her father would not have worn anything so shapeless even in 1975; Aldous Eriksen had been born buttoned. She turned the collar back, looking for initials, a laundry mark, anything.

There was nothing. Just wool, and time.

She put it in the donation pile. Then took it out again. Then put it back in the pile, firmly, the way you close a door on a room you've decided not to think about.

By late afternoon the wardrobe stood empty, echoing, and Theo moved on to the floor of it — because her mother had been the sort of person who kept things under things, and Theo had learned over nine days that every surface in this house was a lid.

That was when her knuckles found the loose panel.

* * *

It was at the base of the wardrobe's back wall, low down where the mahogany veneer met the floor, a rectangle about the size of a hardback book. She had felt it give before she saw it — a small, yielding click under her fingers, like a knuckle cracking. She pressed again. The panel shifted, tilted inward at one corner.

For a moment she simply crouched there, her knees aching against the hardwood, the afternoon light going amber through the window behind her, and let herself feel the specific dread of a person who has lived long enough to know that hidden things are never good news. Hidden things were debts. Hidden things were diagnoses. Hidden things were other people's secrets, which were always heavier than your own because you hadn't chosen to carry them.

She worked the panel free with her fingernails. Behind it was a cavity lined with the pale raw wood of the wardrobe's skeleton, and in the cavity sat a tin.

A biscuit tin. Huntley & Palmers, the painted lid faded to the color of weak tea, a Victorian street scene worn almost to abstraction. Theo knew the tin. She knew it the way you know a face from a photograph you'd forgotten existed: it had once held shortbread, and it had sat on the high shelf of the pantry when she was small, and her mother had kept it out of reach not because of the biscuits but because of what else lived in it — buttons, Theo remembered suddenly, a whole civilization of buttons, and she and her mother had poured them out onto the kitchen table on winter afternoons and sorted them into families while the radio murmured.

Except this tin was heavy in a way buttons were not.

She lifted the lid. Inside, wrapped in a linen tea towel gone soft as skin, was a bundle of envelopes. Forty-one of them — she counted twice, because counting gave her hands something to do while her mind refused its work. Forty-one envelopes, tied with garden twine, each addressed in her mother's upright, unhurried hand.

Each addressed to *Miss T. Eriksen.*

Not posted. Not stamped. No dates on the outside, though the paper of the bottommost envelopes had yellowed to parchment while the top ones were still white. Forty-one letters spanning what looked like thirty years, written to a girl and then a woman who had lived eleven miles away for most of that time, who had telephoned every Sunday, who had visited at Christmas and in summer and who had never — not once — been told that there was mail waiting for her behind a panel in a wardrobe.

Theo sat down on the floor of her mother's bedroom with the tin in her lap and did not open it.

This was the part she would remember afterward, in the weeks that followed, when everything the tin contained had rearranged the architecture of her life: that her first instinct was not to read. Her first instinct was to put the panel back, push the tin into the cavity, press the wood home until it clicked, and go downstairs and tell Nell Okafor to schedule the estate sale and be done with it. Because there are doors that open onto rooms you can never leave, and some animal part of her — the part that had survived her childhood in this house, the part that had moved eleven miles away and built a life out of not asking questions — that part recognized the door for what it was.

But she was her mother's daughter. And her mother had hidden the letters where only someone emptying the wardrobe would find them, which meant they were meant to be found, which meant they were meant to be read, which meant the choice had been made for her years ago by a woman who was now ash in a columbarium niche with a brass plate bearing her name and her dates.

*Ingrid Astrid Eriksen. 1951–2024.*

Theo untied the twine.

* * *

The topmost envelope was the newest, and she opened it first, without deciding to — her hands simply did it, the way hands pick up a ringing phone.

Inside was a single sheet, folded in thirds. She unfolded it and found not words but a garden.

That was her first thought, absurdly: *a garden.* The page was covered in small drawings — flowers, rendered in ballpoint pen with a botanist's precision, each one distinct, each one labeled above or below with tiny numerals. A crocus marked *7–2*. A foxglove marked *19–1–14*. A row of sweet peas climbing the right margin, numbered like rungs. Between the flowers ran strings of figures, columns of them, tidy as accounting:

*3–15–15–20 / 8–5 / 18–1–9–14 / 6–1–12–12–19*

Theo stared at it until the numbers swam.

And then, slowly, from somewhere thirty-two years deep, the key rose up through the water of memory and broke the surface.

She was nine. It had been the winter of the measles — no, before that, the winter she'd had scarlet fever, confined to bed for three weeks, bored to the edge of delirium, and her mother had come in every evening after dinner with a pad of paper and said, *Right. If we're going to write to each other, we'd better make sure your father can't read it.* And they had invented it together, sprawled across the quilt: a cipher of flowers, because her mother loved flowers and Theo loved secrets, and each flower stood for a letter — *a* was aconite, *b* was bluebell, *c* was crocus — and the numbers said how many times each flower appeared in the message, or perhaps the numbers were the letters themselves, A-one through Z-twenty-six, dressed in petals so that a page of cipher looked like a page of botany. They had written notes to each other all that spring. *Meet me in the glasshouse. Bring the good scissors.* Theo had kept the key in her head for exactly as long as children keep such things, which is to say until the next game replaced it, and then the whole language had sunk without trace.

But languages don't sink. They wait.

*Aconite, bluebell, crocus, daffodil, elder, fern, foxglove...*

She fetched the atlas of British flora from the sitting-room shelf — her mother's copy, spine cracked, annotations in the margins in that same upright hand — and carried it back upstairs along with a legal pad and the good pen from her coat pocket. She spread the first letter on the floor beside the tin, kneeled over it like a surgeon, and began.

It was slow. The numbers weren't simple substitution — of course they weren't; her mother had spent thirty years refining a child's game, and there were layers, flowers inside flowers, numbers that referred to positions on the page itself, a self-referential trick that made Theo laugh out loud, alone, at eight o'clock at night, kneeling on the floor of a dead woman's bedroom. But the first line yielded within twenty minutes, and when it did, Theo stopped laughing.

*You were four when I first lied to you.*

She sat back on her heels. Outside, a car passed on the lane, headlights sweeping the ceiling, and the house settled around her with its usual sounds — pipes ticking, wind finding the gap in the north casement that no one had ever fixed because fixing it had always been next month's job and next month had finally run out.

Four. What had happened when she was four? Nothing. That was the answer her memory offered: nothing, a blur of garden and rain and being carried on someone's shoulders, her father's probably, though the shoulders in the memory were oddly slight. Four was nothing. Four was before everything.

Which meant four was when everything started.

She should stop. She thought it clearly, the way you think *I should sell the shares* or *I should not send that email*: a clean, rational sentence arriving too late to matter. She should put the letter down, put the letters back, tie the twine, replace the panel, and spend the rest of her life not knowing what her mother had lied about when she was four, because whatever it was, it had been worth thirty years of hiding and forty-one envelopes and a secret language maintained in private like a lamp kept lit in a window for someone who never came home.

Theo picked up the pen.

The second line took longer. The third longer still. The flowers thinned toward the bottom of the page as though her mother had grown tired, or careful, or afraid, and the last lines were dense with numbers, packed tight, a hedge of thorns around whatever grew at the center. By midnight she had the opening paragraph entire, transcribed onto the legal pad in her own hand, and she read it standing up because sitting felt suddenly wrong, because the words deserved a body that was vertical, attentive, braced.

*You were four when I first lied to you. You asked me why we couldn't tell Papa about our game, and I said it was because secrets are more fun. That was the lie. Not the only one, and not the largest, but the first, and I have owed you the truth of it since before you could read this. I am writing these letters because I cannot say the words aloud — I have tried, Xanthe, God knows I have tried, standing outside your door on nights when you were home, rehearsing — and because a thing written in our language can only ever belong to you. If you are reading this, then I am dead, and the wardrobe has done its work, and I am sorry for the cowardice of panels and tins. Here is the truth, in order, from the beginning. You will need the atlas. You will need patience. And you will need, before the end, a kind of courage I do not have, which is why I am leaving the last of it to you.*

Theo read it three times.

Then she became aware of two things at once, arriving together like guests who had coordinated: that her legs were shaking, and that the light in the hallway below was on.

She crossed to the bedroom door and looked down the stairwell. At the bottom, in the pool of lamplight from the study, her father sat in his chair with his reading glasses on, a folder of papers open on his knee, perfectly still. He had not heard her. He was not waiting up for her. He was simply there, awake at midnight in the house he had shared with Ingrid Eriksen for forty years, and Theo understood — with the sudden, sick clarity that comes when a familiar object is seen from a new angle — that he was keeping his own vigil, guarding his own silence, and that the house between them was full of locked rooms, and that she had just opened one.

She stepped back from the stairwell. Downstairs, faintly, she heard the sound of paper turning.

Back in the bedroom, the tin waited on the floor among the ghost-shapes of hangers, forty more envelopes still bound in their twine, thirty years of a hidden life stacked in order like sediment. Theo looked at them for a long time. Then she did the only sensible thing, the thing any careful person would do: she wrapped the tea towel back over the bundle, lowered it into the tin, and carried the tin out of her mother's bedroom and down the hall to her own room, where she hid it in the bottom of her suitcase, under the dirty laundry, behind the panel of her own locked door.

She did not sleep. She lay in the dark listening to the house breathe, and somewhere around three in the morning she got up, took the legal pad from the desk, and read her own transcription again by the light of her phone.

*You will need, before the end, a kind of courage I do not have.*

Below that final decoded line, her mother's original page held one more passage — a closing paragraph, denser than the rest, the flowers drawn smaller, the numbers running off the margin and up the side of the sheet as if the sentence had outgrown the page it was given. Theo had deciphered only its first word before exhaustion closed over her.

The word was *Xanthe.*

Her name. The name her mother had chosen, that her father had opposed and lost, that teachers had mispronounced for thirteen years and strangers had mangled ever since. The first word of the last paragraph, written in a cipher meant for no eyes but hers.

In the morning, Theo told herself, switching off the phone. In the morning she would fetch the atlas and the payroll ledgers and whatever else the beginning required, and she would start at the start, and she would read every word her mother had ever written to her.

In the morning. But already, lying in the dark of the house she did not own, she could feel the letters pulling at her like tide — forty-one envelopes, one for every year of her life minus one, and the terrible, vertiginous suspicion settling over her now, cold and certain as frost:

her mother had not been writing to her daughter at all.

She had been writing to the one person who could finish what she started.
