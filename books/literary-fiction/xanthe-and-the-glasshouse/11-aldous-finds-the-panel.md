## 11. Aldous Finds the Panel

Aldous Eriksen had spent his life in rooms that required him to be the most composed person in them, and he had learned early that composure was not a temperament but a discipline, like pruning: you cut back what showed, and what remained grew stronger for the cutting. He rose at six as he always had, even now, even with Ingrid eleven weeks in the churchyard and the house gone strange around him — too quiet in some corridors, too loud in others, her absence moving through it like a draft no amount of weatherstripping would fix.

He was in the study with the second coffee and the developer's letter when he heard Theo's footsteps overhead, crossing from the east wing toward her mother's room. He tracked her by sound the way he'd once tracked weather coming off the ridge: the pause at the landing, the long stillness outside the bedroom door that she thought he couldn't hear, the creak of the wardrobe.

The wardrobe. He set down his pen.

He had known about the panel since 1997. That was the year Ingrid repainted the bedroom — she painted everything herself, refused to let him hire anyone, said she liked knowing every inch of her own walls — and one evening he came up to carry the ladder down and found her standing before the open wardrobe with the back panel swung out on hidden hinges, a tin box in her hands, her face lit by the small lamp in a way he had never seen it lit in thirty years of marriage. She hadn't heard him. She was reading something, or writing something, and her expression was so unguarded, so entirely elsewhere, that Aldous stood in the doorway like a trespasser in his own house and understood, with the flat clarity of a man reading his own diagnosis, that there was a country inside his wife he had never been issued a visa for.

He had cleared his throat. She had closed the panel before he finished the sound. They had never spoken of it. That was the architecture of their marriage — load-bearing silences, walls that held because neither of them leaned on them.

He had assumed the box went into the ground with her, or would be burned, or would simply stay behind the panel until the house itself came down. He had not counted on Theo.

Theo, who had arrived three weeks after the funeral like an auditor, cataloguing rooms, photographing the glasshouse, asking Nell Okafor questions about probate in a voice carefully sanded free of anything personal. Theo, who had spent her childhood learning the cipher at Ingrid's knee — he remembered the two of them at the kitchen table, heads bent over the flower book, passing notes in a language of foxgloves and fives while he sat ten feet away reading the paper, excluded by design and pretending not to notice, which was its own kind of fluency.

Aldous put on his shoes, took the stairs slowly — his knees had opinions now — and walked down the corridor to Ingrid's room.

The door was open. The room smelled of lavender sachets and cold air, the particular smell of a space no longer lived in but maintained, like a chapel. Theo was kneeling at the wardrobe with her back to him, and the back panel was open, and the cavity behind it was empty.

She didn't hear him at first. He watched her reach into the dark rectangle, feel along its edges, check the corners with her fingertips — the thoroughness of someone confirming a loss — and then go very still, the way animals go still when they realize the shape in the doorway is a person.

"Aldous." She didn't turn all the way. "I was just—"

"Looking for the estate jewellery," he said. "I know. You told Mrs. Prynne."

The lie between them sat there, old and comfortable as a dog. He let it sit. Then he crossed the room and looked past her shoulder into the cavity: bare plywood, a faint rectangular shadow where the tin had rested for decades, and — this was what undid him, though his face did nothing — a single dried petal on the floor of it, papery and brown, fallen from whatever she'd taken out.

"When did you find it?" he asked.

"Find what?"

"Theo." He said her name the way he said *enough* at board meetings. "The panel was installed in the spring of 1997. I helped your mother hang the mirror on the opposite wall that same week. I have known about that compartment for twenty-eight years. What I don't know is how long you've known, and I would prefer — for both our sakes — that we not insult each other with the obvious."

She stood. She was taller than Ingrid, broader in the shoulders; she had her grandfather's build and, God help her, something of Ingrid's way of holding her chin when she was deciding whether to fight. Right now the chin was up.

"I found them three weeks ago," she said. "The night I started clearing this room."

*Them.* Not *it*. The plural slipped out of her like blood from under a bandage, and he watched her realize it, watched the small contraction around her eyes.

"How many?" he asked quietly.

"Why do you want to know?"

"Because they are letters written by my wife, in my house, during my marriage, and I am her widower and the executor of her estate, and whether you like it or not, that makes them my concern."

"They're addressed to me."

"So were her diaries. So were half the things in this house. Marriage doesn't respect addressing." He heard his own voice hardening and made himself soften it, made himself the reasonable one, because he could see already that this conversation was going to be a negotiation and negotiations were won by whoever needed the thing less. "Sit down, Theo."

"I'll stand."

Fine. He sat himself, on the edge of Ingrid's bed, which was a mistake — the mattress dipped under him with a familiar complaint, and for a moment he was a man sitting on his wife's bed in a room that still held her shape, and grief went through him like cold water through a wetsuit. He mastered it. He had practice.

"Your mother and I had an arrangement," he began.

"Don't." Theo's voice cracked on the word and reassembled itself. "Please don't tell me about your arrangement. I've been reading—"

She stopped. Too late. The sentence had already done its work.

"You've been reading," Aldous repeated. The room seemed to tilt slightly, the way a ship tilts when the ballast shifts. "How far?"

"That's not your business."

"How. Far." Each word placed separately, like stones across a stream. Because it mattered — God, it mattered — whether she had reached 1985 yet, whether she knew the name, whether she was sitting here with her chin up while carrying the one fact that could take the whole structure down. He thought of the payroll ledger in the potting shed, unshelved, he'd noticed, its spine out of line. He thought of the nursery two counties away, and the man in it, and the phone call five years ago that Aldous had paid for with three sleepless nights and a wire transfer he'd told the accountant was agricultural consulting.

"She wrote to you for forty years," he said, "and never posted a page of it. Do you understand what that means? It means she wanted a witness she controlled. A reader who couldn't answer back. Those letters weren't correspondence, Theo. They were a trial she conducted in secret, and you were the jury she kept in a tin."

"And you were what?" Theo said. "The defendant?"

The silence after that was the loudest of his life.

He should have lied. He was good at lying; he had built a marriage and an estate and a respectable life on load-bearing lies, and he knew their tolerances better than any engineer. But he was tired — eleven weeks tired, eleven years tired — and something in him, some exhausted honest muscle, chose that moment to fail.

"Yes," he said.

Theo stared at him. He watched the word land, watched her rearrange herself around it, and saw — with a sensation like watching ice shift on the lake in February — that she had suspected, and that suspicion confirmed was worse than ignorance, because now she had to live in a house where the thing she feared was furniture.

"You knew," she said. "All this time. You knew about—" She couldn't say the name. Interesting. Even now, armed and angry, she protected him from hearing it in her mouth, or perhaps protected herself.

"I hired him," Aldous said. "In 1978. Your mother interviewed him — she did the hiring for the glasshouse then, she said I killed plants by looking at them wrong. Yusuf Adeyemi. Botanist, trained at Ibadan, came with references thick as a Bible. He rebuilt the palm house collection in eighteen months. He taught your mother grafting. And yes, Theo. I knew. From the beginning? No. By the end — by the winter of '84 — I would have had to be blind, and I am many things, but I was never blind."

"Then why—"

"Why did I keep him on? Why didn't I end it?" Aldous laughed, and hated the sound, a dry bark with nothing in it. "Because ending it meant a scene, and scenes meant lawyers, and lawyers meant your grandfather, and your grandfather—" He stopped. Careful. Careful. There were things in the locked drawer she might or might not have found, and every sentence was a door he could choose not to open. "Your grandfather had opinions about scandal. This family's entire position rests on the appearance of solidity. A divorce in 1985, with the estate mortgaged to the eaves, would have ended us. The bank was already circling. One bad season, one whisper, and the glasshouse goes to developers thirty years early and your mother watches everything her father built sold off in lots."

"So instead you dismissed him. Without cause. In January."

"In January," Aldous agreed, "when the frost makes dismissal look like economy. Yes."

"And you threatened him."

The words dropped into the room like a stone into a well, and Aldous listened for the splash and heard none, and understood that she already knew — not guessed, *knew* — which meant she had been in his study, which meant the drawer, which meant the file, which meant the 2020 letter with his own signature on it, and the floor of the last forty years opened beneath him with a sound only he could hear.

"Theo," he said, and his voice came out wrong, thinner, and he heard it and despised it. "Whatever you think you found—"

"I found a deportation order that was engineered," she said. "Not threatened. Engineered. By Grandfather, with the solicitor who handled the mortgage. A man was going to be sent back to a country where he'd have been imprisoned, so that my mother would marry you. And five years ago you picked up the same lever and pulled it again, because you were afraid she was going to leave. That's not a marriage, Father. That's a hostage situation that lasted forty years."

"It was survival." He was on his feet now without remembering standing. "It was keeping the roof on, and the collections alive, and you fed and educated and — yes, it was ugly, and yes, it was a bargain, and everyone paid, including me, including *her*, and if you imagine for one moment that your mother was a passive party to it, then those letters you've been reading have failed you, because Ingrid Winterbourne-Eriksen negotiated harder than any man in this county and she got exactly what she—"

"What? What did she get?" Theo's eyes were wet and furious. "Forty years of a tin box behind a wardrobe?"

"Forty years of the glasshouse!" The words tore out of him louder than he intended, echoing off the painted walls Ingrid had rolled herself, and in the silence afterward he could hear the heating system cycling somewhere below, the old pipes knocking like a heartbeat. He lowered his voice. "Forty years of the palms, and the orchids, and the citrus, and the whole living archive her grandfather started. That's what she got. That's what *we* got. And now Marcus Hale wants to turn it into forty executive homes with a wellness centre, and you are sitting in her bedroom guarding a box of paper while the actual living thing — the thing she actually loved — has six weeks left on its offer."

"The offer expires in four," Theo said. "Nell checked."

The correction stung precisely because it was accurate. He recalibrated. "Four, then. Four weeks, and my daughter is playing archivist with documents that belong—"

"To whom?" Theo said. "Say it. Say the legal thing. I want to hear you say it."

And there it was — the trap she'd built while he talked, patient as her mother. Because he could say it, of course he could; he'd had counsel review it a week ago, quietly, through the firm in Leeds. Correspondence addressed to a third party, gifted posthumously, arguably inter vivos given the decades of intended delivery — Okafor would eat him alive and enjoy it. But the claim was colorable. The letters were written on estate stationery, in the estate's house, during the marriage; an aggressive executor could assert they formed part of the residuary estate, pending litigation, and litigation was a thing Aldous could afford longer than Theo could endure.

"The letters," he said slowly, "were written by Ingrid Eriksen, whose estate I administer. Until probate concludes, their status is unresolved. If they are correspondence — personal gifts to you — then they're yours, and I will accept that finding with grace. But if my solicitors conclude they are unpublished literary material forming part of the estate, then I am obliged to secure them, Theo. Obliged. It stops being personal at that point. It becomes fiduciary."

"Fiduciary," Theo repeated. She almost smiled, and the smile was the worst thing he'd seen all year, because it was Ingrid's smile, the one from the doorway in 1997, the one that meant *you are being read*. "You rehearsed that. You've already talked to a solicitor about my mother's letters. Before you even knew I had them."

Aldous said nothing, which was answer enough.

Theo turned back to the wardrobe. For a moment he thought she was going to hand him the empty panel's worth of nothing, some final gesture of contempt — but instead she closed it, gently, the way you close a coffin, and faced him.

"I'm not giving you the box," she said. "And I'm not going to pretend I don't know what's in it, or what you did, or what your father did. But I'll tell you what I told myself the night I found them: I'm not the jury, and I'm not the judge. I'm the translator. And I haven't finished translating."

"There's a last letter," Aldous said. It wasn't a question. He knew Ingrid; of course there was a last letter; Ingrid never left a sentence unfinished in her life except the ones that mattered.

Theo's face did something complicated. "There's a last sentence," she said. "It doesn't decode."

Something moved through Aldous then — fear, he supposed, though it felt more like vertigo, like the moment on the high ladder when you realize your hands are sweating. An unreadable sentence, in Ingrid's cipher, waiting in a tin box in his daughter's possession. Forty years of control, and at the end of it his wife had left a lock only Theo could open, and no key for him anywhere in the house.

"Then decode it," he said quietly. "Before Hale's people do the demolition survey. Whatever she wanted to say to you — say it fast, Theo. The clock doesn't care about confessions."

He left her standing among the empty hangers and went downstairs to his study, where the second coffee had gone cold, and sat for a long time looking at the developer's letter without reading it, listening to the pipes knock, and thinking — with a clarity that felt like falling — that for the first time in forty years, he no longer knew how the story ended, and the person holding the pen was not him.

* * *

That night he dreamed of the winter of 1984. In the dream he stood in the potting shed doorway watching Yusuf Adeyemi show a nine-year-old girl how to strike a cutting, and Ingrid laughed at something, and the laugh went through the frosted glass and out into the frozen garden, and Aldous understood that he was not in the dream at all — he was outside it, on the gravel, in the cold, a man looking in at a lit window — and he woke at four with his heart going like a trapped bird and lay in the dark listening to the house settle, and did not sleep again.

At seven he called his solicitor in Leeds and asked, in a voice he kept level by main force, how quickly one could file for an injunction.

"At once," the solicitor said, "if you have grounds."

Aldous looked out the window at the glasshouse, silvered with frost, holding its dying light.

"I'm working on it," he said.
