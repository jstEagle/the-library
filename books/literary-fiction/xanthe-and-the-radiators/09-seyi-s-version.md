## 9. Seyi's Version

The generator coughed twice and caught, and the house filled with that low diesel hum Seyi had known all his life, the sound beneath every memory, so constant in childhood that silence itself had seemed the interruption. He stood in the corridor outside Tamsin's room with two bottles of Star lager sweating in his right hand and listened to the small sounds she was making inside — paper, mostly, the dry whisper of paper being turned — and felt the old instinct rise in him like water into a basement.

His sister had been in this house nine days and already the house had changed its mind about her. He could hear it. The floorboards no longer creaked under her feet the way they did under strangers; they had begun to make way.

He knocked with his elbow because his hands were full.

"It's open," Tamsin said, and when he pushed the door she was sitting on the floor amid her grandmother's things like an archaeologist who had stopped pretending to work, her back against the bedframe, her laptop closed for once, a letter — one letter, loose, unnumbered — resting on her knee.

Seyi looked at that letter the way a man looks at a dog he knows has bitten someone before.

"I brought beer," he said. "You look like a woman who needs to not be doing whatever you're doing."

* * *

They drank on the veranda because the veranda was where their family had always done its real talking, out here where the bougainvillea dropped its papery red confetti and the compound wall kept the world at the polite distance Ibadan allowed. Their grandfather had built the veranda wide on purpose, Funmi used to say, wide enough for grief and gossip to sit side by side without touching. The radiators were visible through the window behind them, those absurd white iron ribs along the dining room wall, installed in 1954 by a man who had spent four winters in Leeds and come home determined that no child of his would ever again warm a sock at a coal fire. They had never once been hot. They were the most loved useless objects in the house, polished every month by whoever held the keys, and Seyi had always understood them as the family's truest portrait: British on the surface, cold at the center, and absolutely essential to keep shining.

"You've been quiet since Tuesday," Tamsin said. "Quieter than your normal quiet, which is already a professional-grade quiet."

"I've been working."

"You've been avoiding." She turned the bottle in her hand, not drinking. "You came a week after me. You haven't asked to see anything I've catalogued. You change the subject whenever I mention the roof, and the roof is the whole reason we're selling, Seyi, so."

"The roof is being handled."

"By whom?"

"By me." He smiled, and it was the smile that had gotten him through thirty-eight years of family functions, the easy younger-brother smile, the one that said *nothing here requires your concern*. "I have a guy. There's always a guy."

Tamsin set her bottle down on the cement with a click that sounded, in the evening hush, like a lock turning. "There's something behind the wardrobe," she said. "In the wall cavity. Did you know?"

And there it was. Nine days, and she had gone straight to it, straight through the house's skin into the place where everything was kept. Seyi looked out at the darkening compound, at the mango tree throwing its long shadow across the yard like a man lying down in the road on purpose, and understood that the conversation had already happened somewhere without him, that his sister had crossed a river while he slept and now stood on the far bank asking him, pleasantly, whether he'd ever noticed the water.

"Funmi told me there were rats in the walls," he said. "When we were kids. She said never put your hand in a hole you can't see the bottom of."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the answer I have."

"Seyi." Her voice changed then, dropped its London polish, dropped even the careful Ibadan vowels she'd been reassembling all week, and became the voice from the bunk beds, the voice that had once talked him down off the garage roof when he was eleven and certain he could fly. "Whatever you think I've found — whatever you're afraid I'll do with it — you should tell me what you know first. Because if you don't, I'm going to find out alone, and then we'll both be standing in the wreckage wondering why you didn't warn me."

He wanted to laugh. He wanted to say, *wreckage, listen to you, you've been here nine days.* Instead he heard himself say, "How many are there?"

"Forty-one."

The number went through him like a fishhook. Forty-one. He had found one. One letter, twenty-two years ago, folded into a hymnal in Nnedi's room while he was supposed to be napping, a boy of sixteen reading by torchlight words he was too young to carry, and he had carried them anyway, all this time, alone, because that was what he did — that was what he had *always* done — he carried things so his mother wouldn't have to.

"One," he said.

* * *

It had been December 2003. He remembered the year because of the harmattan, which had come late and mean that year, dust thick enough to taste, the sun a pale coin behind gauze. Adaeze had taken Tamsin to Lagos for a conference — translator things, certificates, the world of laminated badges that Tamsin lived in — and Seyi had been left at the house with Nnedi and Funmi for the holidays, a arrangement he had resented and then, within two days, secretly loved, because Nnedi in December was a different woman from Nnedi the rest of the year. In December she told stories. She sat in the kitchen shelling beans into a steel basin and the stories came out of her the way the husks did, thin and papery and endless, and sixteen-year-old Seyi, who at school in England had learned to be embarrassed of everything, sat at her feet and let himself be embarrassed of nothing.

She was resting one afternoon — the long afternoon sleep she took like medicine — and he had gone into her room to find a novel she'd promised him, and the hymnal was on the shelf where it always was, green cloth gone grey at the corners, and when he pulled it out a folded paper slid from between the pages of *Abide With Me* and landed face-up on the concrete floor.

He hadn't meant to read it. That was the thing he needed Tamsin to understand, though he knew she would understand it least, because Tamsin read everything, Tamsin had made a career of opening other people's sealed rooms. But he had read it, because it was addressed in Nnedi's hand and the address was not Ibadan, and a boy will always open what is not for him.

It was short. He could still recite it. Twenty-two years and he could still recite it, the way you can recite the prayer you said over a body.

*My son. If this reaches you, know that I have not forgotten. Your brother Chukwuemeka says the roads are not safe yet but I do not believe him anymore, I believe only that they do not want you home. Tell them at the mission that I will come myself. Tell them your mother is coming. I am coming, Xanthe. Wait for me.*

No envelope. No stamp. Just the fold, and the name — *Xanthe* — written in a hand that shook at the end of it, the ink pooling where the pen had rested too long, as if she had pressed the name into the paper hard enough to leave it in the wood beneath.

He had stood in his grandmother's room holding her secret while the harmattan scratched at the window screens, and he had done the arithmetic children do badly and adults do well: *Xanthe* was not anyone's name. Not his father's, not Funmi's, not any cousin's. A name that belonged to nobody was a name that belonged to somebody who had been removed.

Then Nnedi had woken, and called his name from the inner room in that voice that was never raised and was therefore never disobeyed, and he had walked in with the letter behind his back like the worst magician alive, and she had looked at him — looked *through* him, really, the way she could, her eyes going past the boy to the man he would become and finding him insufficient — and she had said, in Igbo, in the old dialect, slowly, so that he could not pretend to misunderstand:

*"Some letters are prayers. You do not open other people's prayers."*

And then she had held out her hand.

He gave it to her. Of course he gave it to her. And she folded it once more, smaller, and put it inside her blouse against her chest, and she said nothing else about it ever, not that day, not in the seventeen years she lived afterward, and neither did he, and the silence between them about that letter became a kind of furniture in their relationship, load-bearing, invisible, something they both leaned on without acknowledging it held anything up.

But he had gone back to the hymnal. That was his corruption, if he was honest, and he was trying, these days, in the last years of his thirties, to be honest. He had gone back the next afternoon and the afternoon after that, checking, watching, and in January, packing to return to school, he had checked one final time and found the hymnal empty and the shelf dusted and everything smooth as poured concrete, and he had understood that Nnedi had moved it, and that she knew he would look, and that her knowing was itself a message: *I see you looking. Look all you like. You will not find it again.*

Except he did find it again. Four years later. University break, 2007, the year before she died. He was helping Funmi reorganize the big bedroom after a leak, moving the wardrobe — *their* wardrobe, the same wardrobe, he realized much later, the same wardrobe Tamsin had moved nine days ago — and there in the cavity behind it, wedged in the dark like a bird that had flown in and died, was a bundle of papers tied with the blue ribbon from a Quality Street tin.

He didn't count them. He took one. Only one — the top one, the one that came away easiest — and he read it standing in the dust with Funmi's voice in the courtyard below calling instructions about the curtains, and this one was older, the paper browned, the dialect thicker, and it mentioned Enugu, and it mentioned a family named Okonkwo, and it said *they are good people but they are not his people*, and at the bottom, in a different ink, added years later maybe, someone — Nnedi, the same hand grown older — had written a single line that Seyi carried in his chest like a swallowed stone:

*Adaeze must never carry this weight. She is carrying enough.*

That was the sentence that decided everything. Not the mention of Xanthe, not the Okonkwos, not even the dawning, sickening shape of what the bundle was — forty-odd unanswered prayers to a brother none of them knew existed. It was that line about his mother. *She is carrying enough.* Because Seyi had spent his whole life watching Adaeze carry, and he knew exactly what she carried and how much of it was real and how much was architecture, and he knew — with the terrible clarity available only to children of strong mothers — that if she ever learned that her authority, her entire kingdom, her position as Nnedi's heir and the keeper of Nnedi's story, rested on top of a buried brother, the ground would not crack.

The ground would simply stop existing.

So he had put the bundle back. All of it. He had retied the ribbon the way he'd found it — he still remembered his fingers shaking, the bow coming out wrong twice — and he had slid the wardrobe back against the wall with Funmi shouting cheerfully from the courtyard, and he had gone downstairs and eaten rice and stewed goat and laughed at Funmi's joke about the pastor and the goat, and he had become, at twenty-one, a man who kept a grave in a wall.

* * *

"Say something," Tamsin said.

The veranda had gone fully dark around them while he talked. He hadn't meant to talk; he had meant to deflect, to pour more beer, to deploy the smile. But the story had come out of him the way stories come out of people in this house, after sundown, drawn by some gravity the architect had built into the veranda along with the width. Somewhere beyond the wall a radio played highlife, tinny and patient, a trumpet arguing gently with itself.

"I burned it," Seyi said. "The one I took in 2007. I burned it in the kitchen sink the night before I went back to school. Kerosene, matches, the whole ritual. I stood there watching the edges go orange and I remember thinking — this is the most adult thing I have ever done. Sixteen-year-old me found a letter and couldn't burn it. Twenty-one-year-old me could." He turned the bottle; it was empty; he hadn't noticed drinking it. "You want to know why? Not because I didn't believe her. I believed every word. Because I did the math. If Xanthe existed, then Nnedi's estate wasn't just ours. If Xanthe existed, then Mother's whole story — firstborn daughter, faithful heir, keeper of the flame — was a story someone had edited. And Mother doesn't survive edits, Tamsin. You've seen what happens when a sentence comes back to her with changes. Now imagine the sentence is her entire self."

"So you protected her."

"I protected her," he agreed. And then, because the veranda demanded it, because the house had waited twenty-two years to hear him say it: "Or I decided what she was allowed to be. Same action. Different word depending on who's telling it."

Tamsin was quiet for a long time. A gecko ticked across the ceiling above them, hunting the moths that orbited the bulb. When she finally spoke, her voice was gentle in a way that frightened him more than anger would have.

"There are forty-one letters, Seyi. I've translated six. They're not just to Xanthe. They're to everyone — to Funmi, to her brother, to the Okonkwos, to a lawyer whose name I don't recognize yet. She wrote to the whole family, over thirty years, asking them to bring him home, and not one letter was sent. Do you understand what that means?" She leaned forward, and the bulb caught the side of her face. "It means everyone failed to post them. It means the silence isn't one person's sin. It's a chorus. And I'm starting to hear names in it I know."

"Stop translating."

The words left him before he chose them, raw, unpolished, the voice from the garage roof reversed — now he was the one up on the ridge and she was below saying *you can't fly, Seyi, come down.*

"No," Tamsin said.

"Tamsin—"

"No." She stood, and picked up both empty bottles, and looked down at him with an expression he recognized from mirrors: the family face, the stubborn one, the one that ended conversations. "You burned one letter to protect a version of Mother that may not even be the one she wants. I'm not going to burn forty-one to protect a version of *all* of us." She paused at the door, and softened, just slightly, the way she always did, the way that had always undone him. "One more thing. The seventh letter mentions money. Where it went. Whose hands it passed through." She looked at him steadily. "Including, I think, a trust fund that paid for a flat in Islington."

She went inside. The door clicked shut with the same sound her bottle had made on the cement, a lock turning, and Seyi sat alone on the veranda in the diesel hum and the highlife trumpet and the smell of bougainvillea, and he thought about headlights.

Because that was the other thing he hadn't told her. That morning, driving in from the Lagos road, he had seen a car he didn't know turn off Ring Road toward the house — a big silver car, government-plate adjacent, the kind of car that arrived at family houses only when something was about to be decided — and he had watched it slow, and pause, and continue on. Reconnaissance. Someone measuring the distance.

And he had known, with the certainty of a man who has burned evidence and recognizes the smell of smoke approaching from upwind, exactly whose car it was.

His mother was coming to Ibadan.
