## 10. Adaeze's Silence

The taxi from the airport smelled of pine air freshener and old sun, and Adaeze sat with her handbag on her knees the way she had sat in taxis for forty years, upright, ankles crossed, a woman who had learned early that posture was a kind of currency. Through the window Ibadan unspooled — the same rust and green, the same hawkers lifting plantains like offerings, the same heat pressing its palm flat against the glass. She had left this city at nineteen with one trunk and a promise, and she had returned to it more times than she could count, always for funerals, always for the settling of accounts.

This time it was for Funmi. Her sister-in-law, her rival, her only witness. The last person alive who remembered everything exactly as it happened.

The driver took the turn onto the street too fast, as they always did, and the house rose up on the left the way it always did: grey-walled, wide-eaved, patient. Grandfather's house. The house with radiators under its windows like iron ribs, installed by a man who had loved England more than his own climate, and which no one had ever removed because removing them would have been an admission that he had been wrong about something. Adaeze looked at it and felt nothing she would have called feeling. That was her discipline. She had spent a lifetime converting feeling into posture.

She paid the driver, stood a moment in the road while the heat closed over her like water, and listened.

The house made a sound when family was inside it. She had known this since she was a bride — a low, breathing hum of voices through louvres, radio static, the clank of a pot, laughter with an edge on it. Now there was only a thin quiet, and beneath the quiet, faintly, the scrape of furniture being moved.

Furniture being moved.

Adaeze's stomach tightened before her mind supplied a reason. She climbed the steps, let herself in through the front door whose lock had not worked properly since 1994, and stood in the hallway smelling of dust and camphor and the ghost of Funmi's last illness — menthol, disinfectant, the sweetish trace of a body fighting itself.

"Tamsin?" she called, and heard how her own voice came out: level, warm, a mother's voice, pitched precisely so that whatever answer came back would find her already prepared.

"Up here!" Tamsin's voice, from the long room. From Nnedi's room.

Adaeze set down her bag. She did not hurry. Hurrying told people you were afraid, and she had never once, in sixty-eight years, allowed anyone to see her afraid — not her husband when the business wobbled, not Seyi when he failed his exams, not God himself, whom she addressed each morning with the brisk courtesy of a woman settling an invoice. She climbed the stairs at her own pace, one hand on the rail, counting the steps as she had counted them since girlhood. Fourteen. At the top, the corridor ran cool and dim toward the long room where her grandmother had slept for thirty years and died in her own bed, defiant, ninety-one, refusing until the final week to say any name but one.

The door was open. The wardrobe was out from the wall.

* * *

Later — years later, if anyone had ever asked her, and no one ever did — Adaeze would remember the moment not as sight but as temperature. The cavity behind the wardrobe, exposed like a wound unbandaged; the dark rectangle in the plaster; and Tamsin kneeling before it with a bundle in her hands, paper-yellowed, string-tied, letters fanned across the bedspread like the wings of dead birds.

Tamsin looked up. Her face did what faces do when they are caught: first blankness, then the calculation of what can be salvaged, then — and this was the part Adaeze would keep — a flicker of something almost like pity.

"Mummy." Tamsin rose, holding the bundle against her chest as though it were a child she meant to shield with her body. "You're early. I wasn't expecting—"

"What is that."

Not a question. Adaeze heard her own voice go flat, the voice she used on suppliers, on debtors, on the pastor who had once suggested, gently, that forgiveness might lighten her. She stepped into the room. The ceiling fan turned its slow circles overhead, stirring the smell of old paper.

"It's letters," Tamsin said. "Grandmother's. They were behind the wardrobe. Forty-one of them, Mummy. Addressed, sealed, never posted." She held one out, and her hand was not steady, and Adaeze noted the unsteadiness the way a banker notes a forged signature. "They're about Xanthe."

The name entered the room and the room changed around it.

Adaeze did not move. She watched herself from somewhere slightly above and behind her own body, a trick she had learned at fourteen and never lost: the self stepping back, observing, taking minutes. In that colder register she saw the letters, saw the cavity, saw the dust-free rectangle where the wardrobe's back had guarded the wall for decades, and she understood, with the terrible clarity that fear gives, that everything she had built was standing on this exact spot, and that her daughter was kneeling on it.

"Put them back," she said.

"Mummy—"

"Put. Them. Back." Each word laid down like a stone. "That is your grandmother's private correspondence. Private, Tamsin. Whatever she kept behind her own wall was hers to keep, and it is not yours to dig out of plaster because you are bored between flights."

"I'm not bored." Tamsin's chin came up — Seyi's chin, her father's stubbornness surfacing in her face like a reef at low tide. "These are letters begging someone to fetch a child home. A child everyone forgot. There are nine of them asking Uncle Emeka alone, Mummy. Nine. He read them or he didn't, and either way—"

"You don't know what you're reading." And here Adaeze's voice cracked, just once, a hairline fracture across the flat authority, and she hated herself for it, hated the crack the way she hated rain coming through a roof. She smoothed it over. "You are reading fragments in a dialect you barely speak, written by a frightened old woman during a war, and you are constructing a story out of them because stories are your trade. But stories have costs, nne m. This family has carried its story for fifty years. It is not yours to reopen."

"Whose is it, then?"

"The family's."

"And who is the family, Mummy? Sitting here, right now, today?"

Adaeze looked at her daughter — this careful, competent, translucent woman who had spent thirty years translating other people's sentences because she had never been given one of her own — and felt, beneath the fear, a tenderness so sharp it was nearly anger. She had done that. She and the whole apparatus of them. They had raised Tamsin to be a hinge, not a door.

"Family unity," Adaeze said quietly, "is not a slogan. It is a load-bearing wall. You do not knock holes in walls to see what's behind them. You live in the house."

"There's a boy in these letters," Tamsin said, and now her voice had gone soft, dangerous, the softness of someone who has stopped negotiating. "He was sent away in 1968. He wrote back. His letters came back marked undeliverable — did you know that, Mummy? Someone made sure they'd come back. And the money that was his went into the business, and the business fed all of us, and I have eaten from it my whole life without knowing what I was eating." She lifted the bundle. "I am going to finish reading them."

"No," Adaeze said, "you are not," and she heard, in the silence after, the fan turning, turning, turning, and knew that she had said the wrong thing, that forbidding was fuel, that Tamsin had inherited from her not only the posture but the obstinacy underneath it. She had taught her children this exact tone. God forgive me, she thought, I have taught them everything except how to stop.

* * *

That night Adaeze did not sleep. She lay in Funmi's guest room under a mosquito net gone grey with age, listening to the house tick and settle, and the past came for her the way it always came — not as memory but as weather, a pressure system moving in from the east, from 1971, from the year the war ended and the accounting began.

She had been fourteen. No — fifteen. The war had been over two years, and the family had driven to Enugu to inspect some property, a parcel of land Emeka wanted, and there had been a stop, an unscheduled stop, at a compound with a narrow gate and a frangipani tree, and a woman had come out wiping her hands on her wrapper, and behind the woman, half-hidden in the shade of the veranda, a boy.

He was perhaps ten. Thin in the way war children stayed thin, as if the body never quite believed the famine was over. He wore shorts too small for him and he stood with his weight on one hip, watching the visitors with an expression Adaeze had never seen on a child before and had never forgotten since: not hope, not fear, but assessment. He was deciding what they were. He had learned to decide quickly.

"That's him," the woman said, in the dialect, to Adaeze's father. "That's the Oyelaran boy. Nnedi's firstborn."

And Adaeze, standing beside her father in her good dress, had looked at the boy and the boy had looked at her, and something had passed between them — not recognition, nothing so dramatic, but a kind of question. His eyes had asked: *Are you from her? Are you the ones who were supposed to come?*

And she had known. Even at fifteen she had known — had pieced it together from overheard arguments, from the shape of silences at Christmas, from the way her grandmother's face went still whenever Enugu was mentioned. She had known that this was the boy, the arrangement, the shame, the reason her grandmother prayed longer than anyone and sang louder than anyone and gave money to every beggar at the junction as though paying down a debt.

Her father had spoken briefly with the woman about school fees — fees the family would pay, fees that arrived irregularly, fees that were themselves part of the arrangement, hush-money wearing the clothes of charity. And the boy had stood there the entire time, waiting for someone to say his name, to say *come*, to say anything at all to him.

No one did. Adaeze's father concluded his business. They turned to leave.

And the boy — she could see him now, fifty-three years later, lying awake in her dead sister-in-law's guest room — the boy had taken one step forward, off the veranda, into the light, as if movement might accomplish what words had not. One step. And the woman had caught his shoulder, gently, the way you catch someone you have caught ten thousand times, and he had stopped, and his face had done the thing that Adaeze had spent her whole life learning never to let her own face do.

It had closed. Like a shop at dusk. Shutters down, sign turned, nothing left outside but the painted name.

In the car afterward Adaeze had asked her father, "Who was that boy?" — and her father had looked at her in the rearview mirror with an expression she would spend decades decoding, an expression that was not anger but appeal, a man begging his daughter silently, *don't make me say it, don't make it real by saying it* — and Adaeze, who even at fifteen understood the economy of silence, who could already see that her place in this family depended on knowing things and not saying them, had looked away first.

She had said nothing. She was rewarded for saying nothing her whole life. The trust, the position, the respect, the seat at the head of every table — all of it flowed to her along the channel that her silence had dug.

And now her daughter was standing in the long room with the channel's ledger in her hands.

* * *

In the morning Adaeze rose early, bathed, dressed in her churchgoing wrapper although it was Thursday, and went downstairs to conduct the campaign she had conducted all her life: containment dressed as care.

She found Tamsin at the dining table with the letters spread in ordered rows, photographing them, and Professor Balogun's number written on the back of an envelope, and something in Adaeze's chest went cold and clean, the way it went cold before difficult board meetings.

"Sit," she said. "Eat first. Then we will talk."

Tamsin ate, watchful. Adaeze served her — egusi from the pot Chidera had left, pounded yam she made herself with hands that remembered — and while her daughter ate, she spoke, softly, reasonably, laying out the future the way she laid out cloth at the market: the sale, the shares, Seyi's debts, Chidera's pension, the family's name, the peace of the dead. She spoke of Funmi, who had suffered enough. She spoke of Nnedi, who had earned her rest. She spoke of unity, of protection, of love — and every word was true, that was the horror of it, every single word was true, and none of it was the truth.

"Mummy." Tamsin set down her spoon. "Why won't you even say his name?"

And Adaeze, who had not said the name aloud in fifty-three years, opened her mouth — and found that the silence she had built her life inside had become the walls of her, load-bearing, structural, and that to speak the name would bring the whole house down upon them both.

"Because," she said finally, rising, gathering the plates, her voice perfectly steady, "some names belong to the dead, and the dead cannot defend themselves."

But Tamsin was looking at her — really looking, the way translators look, seeing not the sentence but everything underneath it — and Adaeze knew, folding the napkin with hands that did not tremble because she would not permit them to tremble, that her daughter had already read the letter. The ninth one. The tenth. Whichever one it was. Somewhere in that yellowing bundle there was a line that said: *my daughter Adaeze saw him, in Enugu, she looked into his face and she looked away.*

Nnedi had known. Of course Nnedi had known. Grandmothers know everything; it is their final superpower.

Adaeze carried the plates to the kitchen, and at the doorway she paused, her back to her daughter, and said the only true weapon left to her:

"When you finish reading them all, nne m — and you will, I can see it, you have my stubbornness and I apologize for it — ask yourself one question. Ask yourself what you will do with a truth that has no home to live in. Because a truth without a family is just a noise in the dark." She turned her head, not quite around. "I chose the family. Every day, for fifty years, I chose it. Choose better than I did, if you can."

She washed the dishes. Behind her, in the dining room, she heard the camera click again, and again, and again — forty-one times, methodical, unstoppable — and she understood that the choice had already been made, and that it had been made, in a sense, fifty-three years ago, by a girl in a good dress who had looked away from a boy on a veranda, and that everything since had merely been the interest accruing.
