## 18. The Address in Enugu

The bus from Ibadan took eleven hours, and Tamsin spent all of them awake.

She had told herself she would sleep — that the road would rock her the way it had rocked her as a child, face against her mother's shoulder, the world arriving in fragments of dust and diesel and other people's conversations. But her mother's shoulder was a thousand kilometres behind her now, and the world refused to fragment. It insisted on its wholeness: the whole road, the whole country unspooling past the window, the whole story she carried in a canvas bag on her lap like something alive that might wake if she dozed.

Inside the bag were photocopies. The originals stayed locked in the wardrobe cavity at home — Chidera had insisted on that much ceremony, sliding the bundle back into its dark as if returning bones to the earth — but Tamsin had photographed every page, and Balogun had printed them for her at his own expense, muttering about archives and how history always travelled secondhand. Letter 14 held the address. *Ogui Road,* Nnedi had written, in that hand that leaned forward like a person listening. *The family of Obiora Eze. Ask for the boy they call Xanthe.*

Ask for the boy. As if the boy were still a boy. As if fifty-six years had not passed since the ink dried, since a woman sat in a room in Ibadan writing to an address she must have known was provisional, a wartime address, a hope with a street number on it.

The bus stopped at Ore, then Benin, then Onitsha, where the bridge over the Niger made everyone lean toward the windows. Tamsin leaned too. The river was brown and enormous and indifferent, moving the way rivers move — without memory, or with a kind of memory that forgives nothing and holds everything and tells no one. Her grandmother had crossed this water. Or had not been allowed to cross it. Somewhere in the letters there had been a line about the river; she could almost see it, could almost hear Nnedi's voice assembling it, and then it slipped away, one more word gone down into the brown water.

She got off in Enugu at six in the morning, when the light was still deciding what colour to be.

* * *

She had booked a guesthouse near Ogui New Layout because it was cheap and because proximity felt like courage. In truth she did nothing courageous for two days. She showered under a tap that produced either scalding or ice. She ate rice and stew at a buka whose radio played highlife from before her birth. She walked past the street twice — twice, she counted, twice, like a coward keeping records — and each time she looked at the gate and turned around before her feet could commit treason against her fear.

The compound was modest, exactly as the letters' silence had promised and nothing like the wealth the redirected inheritance should have built him. A low wall, whitewashed once and weathered since into a map of grey continents. A corrugated gate painted green, flaking at the hinges. Behind it, visible over the top: the crown of a mango tree, heavy with fruit, and the red roof of a bungalow with a chimney — a chimney, in Enugu, absurd and beloved, and Tamsin understood it instantly, understood the man who had built it, a boy raised among radiators he'd never been warm enough beside, a house in Ibadan pretending to be England while the boy inside it was sent away so the pretence could survive. Of course he'd built a chimney. Some inheritances you carry even when they've been stolen from you.

On the third morning she woke before dawn with the decision already made somewhere below language, in the body, in the place where translations begin. She dressed. She put the canvas bag over her shoulder. She drank water because her mouth was too dry for anything else, and she walked.

The street was waking up. A woman swept her threshold in long strokes, raising small gold clouds. A boy pushed a wheelbarrow of bread, calling out in a register of voice Tamsin didn't know and couldn't translate. A generator coughed awake two compounds down. Ordinary sounds. The ordinary world going on around the extraordinary thing she was about to do, which was, when she arrived at last at the green gate, nothing at all.

She stood there.

That was all. She stood at the gate of the compound on Ogui Road with her hand half-raised, and her hand would not finish the sentence. Knock, it was supposed to say. Knock, ask for the boy they call Xanthe. But her hand hung in the air between her hip and the iron, trembling slightly, and Tamsin stood in the early heat and listened to her own blood and thought: this is what my grandmother must have felt, every day, for forty years. This exact paralysis. The letter written, the address known, the hand that will not rise.

She tried translating herself out of it, the old trick, the professional reflex — find the word, and the word will hold you up. But the Igbo for *knock* had gone wherever the other words went. *Knọọ*? Was that right? Even her fear came to her now in English, that imperial tongue, that smooth borrowed coat, and she hated it and hid inside it.

What am I going to say to him?

She had rehearsed on the bus. *My name is Tamsin Oyelaran. My grandmother was Nnedi Okonkwo.* Rehearsed it until the words wore smooth as river stones, and now, at the gate, they were useless, because she had read the letters, all forty-one, and what could any introduction weigh against that? *I have your mother's voice in a bag. I have fifty-six years of her asking for you. I am the granddaughter of the people who didn't answer.* Every version of the speech was an accusation wearing her own mouth.

And underneath, quieter, the true terror, the one she hadn't let herself name: what if he is angry? What if he looks at me and sees the whole thieving family compressed into one sweating woman at his gate? What if he closes the door and I have to walk back down this street carrying everything, undelivered, again — becoming, at forty-seven, the final returned envelope?

A gecko watched her from the wall with flat golden eyes. Somewhere inside the compound, water was being poured. Someone was home. Someone had been home, probably, all along, all these days she'd walked past — perhaps watching her pass, the way she'd once watched the house in Ibadan breathe.

Her hand rose another inch. Fell.

She took out her phone instead — cowardice finding its instrument — and opened the photographs of the letters, and stood there reading letter nine through the screen, Nnedi's handwriting magnified by glass, the dialect flowing up at her now with terrible fluency, because Balogun had taught her well and grief was also a teacher:

*I have written to your uncle again. I have written to the mission. I have written to God, who does not keep a forwarding address. My son, if these letters reach you one day, know that I did not stop wanting you. Know only that wanting was not permitted to me.*

Tamsin pressed the phone against her chest and breathed.

* * *

It was the smell that broke the standoff, finally — frying plantain drifting over the wall, sweet and caramelizing, unbearably domestic — and with it some memory of her grandmother's kitchen, of Nnedi standing over oil with her sleeves rolled, humming, and Tamsin's eyes filled so suddenly that the gate swam and doubled, and in that blurred doubled moment she heard, from inside, footsteps.

Not hurrying. Unhurried footsteps, the gait of a man who has waited so long that waiting has become a way of walking.

They stopped on the other side of the gate.

Tamsin's heart performed a full catastrophe. She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, uselessly, and said — to a sheet of painted corrugated iron, to fifty-six years, to whatever was listening — "Good morning." In English. Of course in English. Her one fluent language, arriving precisely when she needed it least.

Silence. Then a voice, deep and dry as paper left in sun: "You are the one who walks past."

Not a question. Tamsin closed her eyes. "Yes," she said. "I'm sorry. I—"

"Three times," the voice said. "I counted."

"I counted too," she admitted, and heard — unmistakably, impossibly — a short exhalation that might, in a different life, in a different family, have been a laugh.

"The researcher called me in March," the voice went on. "About estate records. He asked if I knew the name Okonkwo, of Ibadan." A pause, in which the plantain crackled and the morning continued its indifferent business. "I said I knew the name. He asked how. I said, *a woman used to write to me here.* Fifty-one envelopes over nineteen years. All returned. *Addressee unknown*, they stamped, though I lived at this address the entire time. Do you understand? The entire time. They were returned to a house that no longer wanted them found."

Tamsin's throat closed. She thought of the cavity behind the wardrobe, the forty-one letters that had never left. Two incomplete halves of a correspondence, separated by a wall of plaster and a wall of silence, both sides writing into the dark.

"He asked for my name for his records," the voice said. "Xanthe Okonkwo. And yesterday evening a young man who sells phone credit told me a woman has been standing at my gate reading letters off her telephone, and crying." The footsteps shifted. "So. Before you knock, which you seem unable to do — tell me your name."

And Tamsin found that the rehearsed speech had burned away entirely, and what remained was the plainest sentence she had ever spoken in her life, the first sentence in years that she had not translated, softened, or smoothed for anyone's comfort:

"Tamsin. Tamsin Oyelaran. Nnedi was my grandmother."

The silence on the other side of the gate lasted long enough for her to count her own heartbeats — eleven, twelve, thirteen — long enough for her to believe the door would never open, that she had delivered her truth into the iron and would receive nothing back.

Then the bolt moved. Once, twice, unhurried, a sound like a sentence being unlocked.

The gate swung inward, and there he was.

An old man in a faded shirt the colour of weak tea, tall even stooped, with Nnedi's cheekbones — she saw them at once, saw her own face ghosted in his, the family's architecture printed in bone — and Nnedi's eyes, deep-set and level, eyes that had learned to look at things without flinching because flinching had never once helped. He held a watering can in one hand. Behind him the compound opened up: swept earth, the mango tree dropping its shade, pots of basil and scent leaf along the wall, and the bungalow with its ridiculous, faithful chimney.

He looked at her for a long time. She let him. It seemed the least she owed — to be looked at, fully, by the man her family had spent three generations not looking at.

"You have her hands," he said at last. "Nnedi's hands. I remember the hands." He set down the watering can. "Come inside. The plantain is nearly ready, and you look like someone who hasn't eaten since she decided to come."

It was not forgiveness. Tamsin understood that as she stepped across the threshold, her sandals crossing the line between the street and the swept earth, between the story she had inherited and the one she was walking into. It was something rarer and more frightening than forgiveness. It was hospitality offered without conditions yet — a table laid before the reckoning, grace extended in advance of anything she could say to earn it, and therefore unearnable, and therefore real.

He led her up the path, and just before the door he paused and said, without turning, in the dialect she was losing word by word, a sentence she caught only half of — enough to know it was welcome, not enough to know what else it held.

"Say it again slowly," she whispered. "Please. I'm losing it."

He turned then, and studied her the way scholars study damaged manuscripts — with patience, and something like recognition.

"So am I," said Xanthe. "We will lose it together, then. Come. Tell me everything. Start with the letters — start with how many."

"Forty-one," Tamsin said. "She wrote forty-one. None of them were posted."

The old man closed his eyes. When he opened them, they were wet and absolutely steady.

"Then we are even," he said. "I wrote fifty-one. Come inside, my sister's daughter. We have a great deal of undelivered mail to read."
