## 5. The Voss Legacy

The conservatory smelled the way her father had always described it: floor wax and cold radiators and something faintly sweet beneath it all, like apples going soft in a cellar. Camille Voss stood in the entrance hall with her coat still buttoned and let the smell have her for a moment. She had not been inside a conservatory since she was nine years old, when her father had carried her on his shoulders through these same corridors so she could touch the marble busts of composers whose names she'd learned to pronounce before she could ride a bicycle.

*Chopin. Rachmaninoff. Clara Schumann.* Her small fingers on cold stone. *Say it again, Camille. Again.*

He had been dead eleven weeks now, and she was here to build him a shrine.

"Ms. Voss." A young man in a lanyard hurried toward her, hand extended. "Director Halvorsen sends his apologies — budget meeting — but I'm to give you whatever you need. The exhibition space is ready whenever you are."

"The cases arrived?"

"This morning. All six. We put them in Hall B."

Hall B. The recital room. Of course. Her father had made his debut there at nineteen; he'd told the story so many times she could hear his cadence in it, the little pause before *nineteen*, the modest tilt of his head that wasn't modesty at all but architecture, load-bearing vanity disguised as humility. She followed the young man down the corridor, past practice rooms leaking scales and arpeggios into the hallway like water under doors, and thought: *he would hate this carpet* and *he would love this light* and did not examine which of those thoughts was grief and which was inventory.

Hall B was a long room with tall windows and parquet worn pale in two tracks leading from the door to the small stage, as if generations of performers had walked the exact same path, grooves cut by repetition, by ritual, by fear. The display cases stood in a row down the center, empty, their glass catching the morning. On a folding table nearby sat the crates she'd shipped from the house in Vermont: her father's manuscripts, his letters, the photographs, the baton he'd never used but kept anyway because someone famous had given it to him.

She began unpacking alone. The young man offered help twice and she declined twice with a smile she'd practiced at the funeral, and eventually he left her to it.

This was the part she loved, if love was the word. The curation. The deciding what a life meant by choosing which objects would stand for it. Her father's life was large enough to require editing — forty years of compositions, performances, students scattered across every continent like seed from a struck pod. Aurel Voss, the great teacher. Aurel Voss, the composer of the *Vermont Sonata*, the piece that had made his name when she was four years old and that she had heard him play, or rather heard recordings of him playing, more times than she could count. He never performed it live after 1979. He said the piece belonged to a version of himself he'd outgrown. She had believed that. She had repeated it, in program notes and interviews, in the eulogy.

She lifted the manuscript of the sonata from its crate — the fair copy, the one with his handwriting neat as engraving — and set it in the first case, angled toward the light. Centerpiece. Anchor. Everything else in the exhibition would orbit this.

Which left the other material. The box she'd been avoiding since the estate lawyers sent it: the folk manuscripts. That was what the lawyers called them, with the delicacy people use for things they suspect are embarrassing. A folder of handwritten scores found in her father's study desk, unsigned, undated, in an old-fashioned notation style. The auction house appraiser had looked at them for ten minutes and said, gently, that they appeared to be transcriptions of traditional tunes, possibly collected, possibly composed in imitation of folk idioms — common enough among mid-century composers, nothing scandalous, but also nothing valuable. Not her father's work. Not anyone's work, really. Just paper.

Camille opened the folder now, standing over the folding table.

Her father's handwriting looked back at her.

That was the thing no one at the auction house could have understood, the thing that had kept her awake two nights running. It *was* his handwriting. The same forward-leaning treble clefs, the same way he crossed his stems with a single decisive stroke, the same habit of writing dynamics above the staff instead of below. She knew his hand the way you know a face. She had watched it move across thousands of pages. These were written by Aurel Voss, note for note, and yet they were not his music — the idiom was wrong, older, plainer, full of open fifths and modal turns and a kind of patient repetition that had nothing to do with how her father thought. His mind was all development, all argument. This music didn't argue. It remembered.

*Sentimental folk manuscripts,* the lawyers had said. *Probably student exercises, or transcriptions. We'll keep them out of the catalogue unless you want them shown.*

She had almost said no. She had almost buried them in a drawer the way he apparently had.

But there were dates penciled on three of the pages — 1971, 1972, 1974 — and the *Vermont Sonata* was dated 1976, and she had spent eleven weeks learning to read her father's life like a score, and she could not stop herself from hearing the folk melodies underneath the sonata's second movement, transformed, developed, argued with, but *there*, the way a face is there in a grandchild. She had told herself it proved nothing. Composers steal from themselves. Composers collect. Bartók built a career on field recordings. It was homage, it was influence, it was —

It was the reason she'd brought the folder at all.

"Ms. Voss?"

She turned. A woman stood in the doorway of Hall B — fiftyish, gray-streaked hair pinned back carelessly, wearing the navy uniform of the cleaning staff and holding a mop the way some people hold instruments, loosely, as though it might be set down any moment for something better. Behind her, propped against the wall just outside the door, a yellow wet-floor sign.

"I'm sorry," the woman said. "I do the halls at nine. I can come back."

"No — please." Camille gestured vaguely at the floor. "Please, don't let me stop you. I'll only be in the way."

The woman came in without further ceremony and began along the far wall, methodical, unobtrusive, the mop whispering over the parquet. Camille turned back to her table and tried to resume being a curator. But the presence in the room changed its acoustics somehow. She found herself narrating silently, explaining each object as she placed it, the way her father used to explain things to her — *this is the pen he wrote with, this is the photograph from Vienna* — and she wondered why she needed an audience, even an indifferent one, even one who was only here for the floors.

She held up the folk manuscript dated 1974 and looked at it in the window light.

"You knew my father?" she asked, before she could decide not to. "Aurel Voss. Did you ever — he taught here for thirty years."

The mop paused. Resumed.

"He was before my time," the woman said. Her voice was low and even, a voice that had practiced being unremarkable. "Everyone here knows of him, though. There's a plaque in the east corridor."

"A plaque." Camille laughed, short and without humor. "Yes. There will be a much larger one by June. Bronze. They're very excited about bronze."

Silence, except the mop.

Camille looked down at the manuscript again, and heard herself say — to the cleaner, to the room, to no one — "These aren't his. Everyone assumes they are, because of the handwriting, but they're not his music. They're somebody's. Somebody who thought in a completely different language than he did. And he copied them out, page after page, and then two years later he wrote the sonata that made his name, and nobody has ever once asked where the melodies came from." She stopped, startled by her own voice, by how much had come out, by the word *copied*, which she had not permitted herself until this moment, in this room, to a stranger with a mop.

The mop had gone still.

Camille turned. The woman was watching her — not the way cleaners watch patrons, with polite blankness, but with a fixed, stricken attention, as if Camille had said something in a language only she understood and it had struck her somewhere vital. Her face had gone pale under its ordinary tiredness, and her hands had tightened on the mop handle until the knuckles showed, and her eyes — dark, wide, wet at the rims — were fixed not on Camille but on the manuscript in her hands, on the penciled date, on the open fifths marching down the page.

For one strange, suspended second, Camille had the impression of looking at a person standing at the edge of a sentence she had been refusing to speak for a very long time.

Then the woman blinked, and her face closed like a book, and she said, "I should finish the east hall," and turned back to her work with such deliberate steadiness that it was itself a kind of confession.

"Wait," Camille said. "I'm sorry — did I — do you know something about these?"

"There's a spot by the stage that needs a second pass," the woman said, moving away. "The wood's old. It holds everything."

She worked the rest of the room in silence, thorough and unhurried, and Camille stood among her father's papers feeling the ground shift by millimeters, the way ground shifts before you can prove it's moving. When the woman finally wheeled her bucket toward the door, Camille caught herself saying, "What's your name?"

The woman hesitated in the doorway, half-turned, the light from the corridor behind her.

"Edmund," she said. "I'm Edmund."

And she was gone, her footsteps quick and soft down the hall, leaving Camille alone in Hall B with six empty cases, a crate of evidence, and the sudden, unwelcome certainty that the story she had come here to tell — the clean, bronze-plated story of Aurel Voss, beloved teacher, great composer — had another version, and that version had just walked out of the room carrying a mop, and its name was Edmund.

* * *

That night Camille went back to the hotel and laid the folk manuscripts across the bed in chronological order, all eleven pages, and played recordings of the *Vermont Sonata* on her laptop with the score open beside her, and at two in the morning she found it beyond arguing: the melody from page seven, the one in the Dorian mode with the falling sixth, sitting inside the sonata's slow movement like a bone inside flesh — visible now that she'd seen it, impossible to unsee.

She searched the name *Edmund* in the conservatory's online staff directory and found nothing. She searched the archives index Marisol Reyes had mentioned in an email last week — the archivist had been persistent, oddly persistent, asking about exactly these manuscripts — and found a digitized program from 1974: a faculty recital, guest artist, *works for piano by Ilse Marchetti (1931–1977)*.

Marchetti. The name meant nothing. The handwriting on the program's title page, reproduced in facsimile, meant everything: the same forward-leaning clefs, the same crossed stems.

Her father had copied out a dead woman's music and signed it with his own hand, and the woman who cleaned the floors of the building that named a prize after him had looked at Camille's face as if she'd been waiting eleven weeks — or eleven years — for someone to ask.

Camille picked up her phone, found the email from the archivist, and began to type: *Ms. Reyes, I think we should meet sooner than planned. Also — does the name Marchetti mean anything to you?*

Outside, snow was starting to fall on the city, erasing the streets one flake at a time, and Camille Voss sat among her father's stolen pages and understood, with terrible clarity, that the exhibition she had come to build was going to become a trial instead — and that she had already decided, somewhere deep below argument, which side she would testify for.
