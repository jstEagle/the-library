## 6. Provenance

The archive lived in the basement of the municipal library, in a room that smelled of cold paper and older cold, a smell Edmund had come to think of as patience made physical. Marisol had given her a key on the second day, saying only, *You'll want to work when I'm not here. Everyone does.* And so Edmund came at night now, after her shift, still smelling faintly of floor wax, and sat beneath the humming fluorescent tube with her grandmother's scores spread before her like a patient awaiting diagnosis.

Marisol worked with a jeweler's hands and a detective's silence. She had photographed every page — forty-one leaves of staff paper, water-stained at the corners, the ink gone the brown of old tea — and she spent hours with them tilted toward the light, reading the pencil annotations beneath the notes. *Hold this back. Let it breathe. Like wind through wheat.* The handwriting was small and slanted leftward, as if leaning away from its own certainty.

"Your grandmother didn't trust her own instincts," Marisol said one night, tapping the margin. "Look — she writes herself instructions and then argues with them." She turned the page over. On the reverse, faintly, another line: *No. As written.*

"She argued with herself," Edmund said.

"And won, sometimes." Marisol looked up. Her eyes were dark and quick behind smudged glasses. "Edmund. These aren't copies. This is the compositional manuscript. There are revisions here you can't fake — false starts scraped out with a razor, measure numbers corrected mid-stream. A forger works from a finished score. This is music being *born* on the page."

Edmund felt something loosen in her chest, a band she'd been wearing so long she'd mistaken it for bone. "Then it's proven."

Marisol held up one finger — *wait* — and went to the filing cabinets along the far wall, the ones devoted to broadcast history. "Provenance isn't proof," she said, pulling drawers. "Provenance is a *story* the documents tell, and it has to be a story no one can reasonably interrupt. Manuscripts can be disputed. But if we can place these exact notes somewhere public, somewhere dated, before Voss ever claimed them—" Her hand stopped. She pulled a folder. "Here."

The folder was thin, its label typed: **KVMR — Sunday Recital Hour — 1971–1974 — Ines Aldana (piano)**.

Edmund's breath went strange.

She knew the program. Every child in that valley had known it; her grandmother's half hour on Sunday afternoons was as fixed as church for families who owned radios, which was nearly everyone. Edmund had been born too late to hear most of them live, but there had been tapes — reel-to-reel boxes in the front room of the house, labeled in that same slanted hand, played on winter evenings until the tape wore thin and the piano sounded underwater.

"Ines Aldana," Marisol read aloud, "performing her own compositions, broadcast March 14th, 1971. And again in '72. And '73." She laid the pages out in a row. "Three broadcasts. The station kept logs, and the logs list titles." She looked up, and Edmund saw the moment the archivist's caution broke open into something like joy. "*Sonata in E minor, 'The Orchard.'* Edmund — that's the first piece in your stack. That's the manuscript sitting right there."

They found the actual recordings two nights later, in a donation from the station when it changed hands in 1989: eleven reels, catalogued but never digitized, wrapped in acid-free sleeves nobody had opened in decades. Marisol threaded the first one through the playback machine with the reverence of a priest handling relics, and then lowered the needle, and the room filled with hiss, and then —

Her grandmother's voice. Not singing — speaking, the way she did before playing, a low warm murmur: *"This next one I wrote the summer the orchard behind our house bloomed twice..."*

And then the opening measures of the sonata, rising out of the static like something surfacing from deep water.

Edmund did not remember deciding to stand. She was standing. Her hand was pressed flat against the shelf, because the floor seemed unreliable. It was the same performance she had heard on those worn tapes as a child — the slight hesitation before the second theme, the way the left hand rushed just barely, humanly, ahead — but hearing it here, in an archive, attached to a date, a station, a logbook entry, transformed it. This was no longer memory. This was *evidence*. Her grandmother existed in the world's records. She had played this music into a microphone while Edmund's mother was a girl doing homework in the next room, and the signal had gone out over the valley, and the valley had listened, and now the tape survived to say: *this was hers, this was hers, this was hers.*

"It matches," Marisol said quietly, when the movement ended. She had the manuscript open beside the machine, following. "Every note. Even the hesitation — look, here, the pencil mark: *let it breathe.* She breathed exactly there." She stopped the tape. "This is as close to unbreakable as provenance gets. Broadcast record, dated, plus compositional manuscript in the composer's hand. Any honest court, any honest panel—"

"But?"

Marisol took off her glasses and rubbed the bridge of her nose, and Edmund understood that they had arrived at the part of the evening the archivist had been walking toward all week.

"But the auction isn't a court," Marisol said. "Read the terms again. Really read them."

Edmund had read them. She read them again that night at her kitchen table, under the lamp, while the radiator knocked its slow arrhythmia. The sale notice was elegant, printed on heavy stock, the Voss estate's letterhead embossed in gray. *The property known as Aldana House, together with contents, to be offered at public auction by order of the Estate of Aurel Voss.* And beneath, in the section titled **Conditions of Sale**, clause seven:

*Any claim of interest in the property or its contents must be lodged by a claimant with standing prior to the sale. Standing shall be established by documented legal or proprietary interest. Claims lodged without standing will be disregarded.*

Documented legal or proprietary interest. Edmund read the sentence four times, and each time it closed around her like a door.

Because here was the thing she had built so carefully, brick by brick, over fifteen years: she did not exist. Not legally, not publicly, not in any registry a lawyer would consult. She had surrendered her performing career under a cloud of rumor she'd never confirmed or denied; she had let her name lapse from the conservatory rolls; she paid taxes, rented rooms, held jobs under a name so ordinary it functioned as camouflage. Edmund Marchetti. A cleaner. A ghost with a mop. There were people at the conservatory who had worked beside her for years who could not have told you her surname.

And Ines Aldana's granddaughter — the girl in the photographs, the prodigy Voss had paraded through three continents — had no documented connection to anything. The house had passed to Voss decades ago, in a transaction Edmund had never examined because examining it would have meant asking the question underneath it: *how did he get it?* Her grandmother had died owing money, or so the family story went; Voss had been kind, everyone said, generous, he'd taken care of the arrangements. Edmund had been nineteen and drowning and had signed nothing and asked nothing and let the current take everything.

Now the current had deposited her here, holding proof of theft in one hand and a sales contract in the other, and the contract required precisely what she had spent her adult life destroying: a self. A name with papers attached to it. A person the machinery of law could see.

"They'll say the manuscripts are forgeries," Marisol said, when Edmund told her. "Camille Voss has already said it, publicly, about the 'folk manuscripts.' Once you lodge a claim, their lawyers will demand authentication, chain of custody, expert review. We can give them the broadcasts. But they'll counter with experts of their own, and Voss's estate has money and the conservatory's blessing, and you—" Marisol paused, choosing the word with visible care. "You are, legally speaking, very difficult to find."

"I'm right here," Edmund said.

"You're invisible," Marisol said, not unkindly. "It was a gift, once. Now it's a wall. To claim standing, you have to prove who you are — your relationship to Ines Aldana, your history with that house, your identity. Birth certificate, school records, the conservatory enrollment under your own name, the years Voss presented you as his student. All of it. You'd be handing them a map of yourself."

A map of herself. Edmund sat with that. Fifteen years of careful erasure, undone not by scandal but by paperwork. And worse — once her name entered the proceedings, it would enter the *record*. The plagiarism claim would surface. The question of what she knew, and when, and why she had said nothing for fifteen years. The lawyers would not need to prove she was lying; they would only need to make the room wonder whether the woman accusing Aurel Voss of theft was the same woman who had stood silently beside him while he committed it.

"They'll bury me in my own history," Edmund said.

"Or they'll offer you something not to dig it up," Marisol said. "Estates like this prefer settlement to exposure. If they sense the recordings exist—"

"Then we don't let them sense it."

"No," Marisol agreed. "But Edmund — listen to me. The claim has to be lodged before the sale. Ten days. And it has to be lodged by someone with standing, in their own name, with their face attached." She reached across the table and touched the top manuscript page, the slanted handwriting, the instruction to breathe. "Your grandmother's music is safe in the archive. The house is what's at stake. And the house will go to whoever the law can see."

That night Edmund walked home the long way, past the conservatory, its windows lit with practice-room lamps, the sound of scales leaking into the street like water through a cracked wall. Somewhere inside, students were becoming themselves, spending their names freely, signing programs, building the public selves she had dismantled. She thought of clause seven. *Standing shall be established.* As though standing were a thing you could establish, like a fence post, driven down into the ground of your life.

She had thought the hardest sentence would be the confession — *he stole, and I let him.* Now she understood there was a harder one, quieter, more bureaucratic, more devastating in its plainness: *I am Ines Aldana's granddaughter, and I am claiming what is mine.* Said aloud. Signed. Filed. Visible to every eye that had ever looked through her.

Ten days.

In the morning, on the conservatory bulletin board, between a recital announcement and a lost-glove notice, she saw the new posting: the memorial exhibition for Aurel Voss, curated by his daughter, opening in the main gallery. *Celebrating a life devoted to music.* Beneath the text, a photograph of Voss at the piano, young and luminous, and beside him — Edmund's stomach dropped — a girl of sixteen, caught mid-laugh at the keyboard, her hands poised above the keys.

Herself. Chosen, apparently, by Camille Voss herself, as the exhibition's emblem of the master's generosity.

The machinery was already moving. And it was moving toward her.

* * *

She found Camille in the gallery that afternoon, directing two technicians as they hung framed programs and photographs along the north wall. Edmund pushed her mop cart slowly along the corridor outside, the way she had done for years, invisible, listening — and heard Camille say, crisp and certain:

"The folk manuscripts stay out. Father collected curiosities; that doesn't make them significant. Half those pieces are probably fakes anyway — sentimental village stuff some dealer sold him." A pause, and then, lighter: "Though I suppose every great man needs his little myths."

Edmund stood in the corridor with her hands folded on the mop handle, and felt the ten days begin to burn down like a fuse.
