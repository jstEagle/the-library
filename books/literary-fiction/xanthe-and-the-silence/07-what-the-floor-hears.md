## 7. What the Floor Hears

The house on Rue de la Vigne had always smelled of cedar and old paper, and in the week after her father's death it smelled of nothing at all, as if the rooms had exhaled their last breath of him and were waiting, politely, to be emptied. Camille Voss walked through them with a legal pad and a felt-tip pen, cataloguing what the estate lawyers would want inventoried: the Bösendorfer in the music room, the first-edition scores in the glass case, the photographs of her father shaking hands with conductors whose names she had grown up reciting like saints.

She had come to curate. That was the word she kept using, to herself and to the conservatory's director, who had offered the east gallery for a memorial exhibition — *Aurel Voss: A Life in Music*. She would choose the photographs, frame the programs, arrange the manuscripts under glass with the small brass plaques her father would have wanted. *Sonata in E minor (1974)*. *Nocturne for Left Hand Alone*. The works that had made him, in the obituaries, "a composer's composer" — a phrase Camille had repeated so often in the past month that it had worn smooth, like a stone carried too long in the mouth.

But the house was not cooperating with the version of her father she had brought to preserve it.

It kept producing other things.

* * *

It began with the desk in the study, which she had been avoiding because the desk was where he had done his real work — not the composing, which he did at the piano, humming, but the correspondence, the negotiations, the careful architecture of reputation. She sat down in his chair on a Tuesday afternoon, opened the center drawer, and found it nearly empty: spare nibs, a rubber band gone brittle, a magnifying glass. The left drawer was locked.

She should have called a locksmith and waited. Instead she took the letter opener from the blotter and worked it into the seam, and the cheap brass lock gave with a sound like a knuckle cracking, and she felt — absurdly, shamefully — like a burglar in her own inheritance.

Inside there was a folder. Manila, soft-cornered, tied with string. On the tab, in her father's upright hand: *Correspondence — personal. Do not file.*

Her father had been a man who filed everything. He kept receipts from 1987. He kept the program from his own debut recital, annotated in pencil with the tempos he'd taken. A folder marked *do not file* was, in his taxonomy, a confession.

She untied the string.

There were perhaps twenty letters inside. Some she recognized — warm notes from former students, a card from a conductor, a birthday greeting with a musical quotation inked across the bottom. And then, near the bottom of the stack, an envelope that made her stop breathing for a moment, though she could not have said why.

It was addressed to her father in a hand she didn't know — small, slanted, pressed hard enough into the paper that she could feel the letters' ghosts on the reverse side when she turned it over. It was stamped. It was postmarked eleven years ago. And across its face, in red ink, in her father's handwriting, was written:

*Return to sender.*

He had never opened it.

Camille sat very still in his chair, the envelope balanced on her palm, feeling its weight — one sheet, maybe two — and understood that she was holding something her father had decided, deliberately, never to know. Her whole life he had taught her that ignorance was the only unforgivable laziness. *Read everything,* he told his students. *Listen twice. Never sign what you haven't read.* And here, in his locked drawer, was the one document he had refused to read, returned unopened, kept anyway. He had not thrown it away. Men throw away what means nothing. He had kept it the way you keep a tooth that has been pulled — proof that something once hurt.

She opened it.

*Dear Professor Voss,*

*I have started this letter many times. I have burned two versions in the sink of my apartment, which I tell myself is practice for burning everything else, including this memory, but we both know I am not capable of either.*

*You know why I am writing. You have known since the night I stood in your doorway and asked you about the E minor sonata, and you looked at me with such kindness, such terrible patience, and said, "Edmund, grief makes us imagine things." As if my grandmother's handwriting were something I imagined. As if the measures I copied out at her kitchen table when I was nine years old were a fever dream.*

*I said nothing then. I have said nothing for seven years. I practiced your nocturnes. I won the competition playing your sonata — her sonata — and I let them print your name beside mine in gold letters, and every time I saw it I thought: this is what it costs, and I paid it, and now I will be safe.*

*I was not safe. There is no safe on the other side of this. There is only the floor I clean now, and the silence I chose, and the woman I let disappear twice — once when she died, and once when I handed you her life's work and watched you sign your name to it.*

*I am not writing to accuse you. I think I am writing so that somewhere outside my own head, the sentence exists. So that it is true somewhere other than inside me.*

*I don't ask for anything. I only ask that you read this.*

*— Edmund Marchetti*

Camille read it three times. The third time her eyes stopped working properly and she had to press the heel of her hand against them and start again from *Dear Professor Voss*, and by the end of the page she was crying in her father's study with the particular, disorienting grief of a person discovering that the ground floor of her life had been built over a cellar she'd never known existed.

Edmund Marchetti. She knew the name the way you know a name from a half-remembered dream. It took her several minutes of sitting in the fading light before it assembled itself: the janitor. The night man at the conservatory, the quiet one with the mop and the downcast eyes whom everyone stepped around without seeing. She had seen him — she had seen *him* — just days ago, in the corridor outside the gallery, pausing by the door while she'd dismissed the folk manuscripts as sentimental forgeries. She remembered his face suddenly, vividly: not blank the way she'd assumed, but *held*, the way water holds itself behind glass. And she remembered thinking, absurdly, that he had looked at her as if he knew her.

He had known her. He had known her father. He had played — no. Had he? She searched the letter for it and found it in the middle: *I won the competition playing your sonata.* She set the letter down on the desk with hands that were not quite steady and went to the shelf where her father kept the bound volumes of programs, and pulled the year — eleven years ago — and ran her finger down the winners' list.

*First Prize: Edmund Marchetti.* Program: Sonata in E minor, Aurel Voss.

Gold letters. Just as the letter said.

* * *

She did not sleep that night. She sat in the study until two in the morning with the folder open on her knees, reading the other letters backward through time, and the story they told was worse than any single accusation, because it was a story of *maintenance*. Her father had not stolen the music in a single act of passion; he had curated the theft, tended it, defended it against every tremor of threat. Here was a note from a woman named Halvorsen, a musicologist, asking pointed questions about the sonata's harmonic language — "so unlike your earlier idiom, Aurel, almost folk-inflected; where does it come from?" — and here, clipped to it, her father's draft reply, courteous and devastating: *I spent a season collecting in the eastern valleys in my youth; the influence is real but subterranean.* A lie with footnotes.

Here was a letter from a lawyer, dated two years after the competition, regarding "the Marchetti matter," assuring him that the estate of the grandmother — one Ilse Marchetti, deceased — presented no claim, the works having been "registered and published under your name." Published. Camille mouthed the word. Her father had registered stolen music with the proper authorities, paid the fees, done everything correctly except the one thing that mattered.

And here — this was the one that finally broke something loose in her chest — a photograph. Old, creased, tucked behind the lawyer's letter as if hidden even from the drawer itself. A young woman at a piano in a plain room, caught mid-phrase, her face lit with the unguarded joy of someone who does not yet know she is being photographed. On the back, in pencil: *Ilse M., teaching, spring 1968.* Her father had kept it. All those years, he had kept the face of the woman he erased, folded behind the paperwork of the erasure, and Camille could not decide whether that was guilt or trophy or love or all three braided together into something she had no name for.

She thought of her childhood in this house: the concerts, the famous friends, the way her father would go quiet when certain music came on the radio — she had always believed it was reverence. Now she wondered if it was listening. Checking. Listening the way a man checks a wound.

At dawn she went to the piano and opened the fallboard and looked at the keys without touching them. In the gray light the instrument looked less like a treasure than like evidence.

* * *

By ten o'clock she had made calls she could not unmake. To the estate lawyers: hold the auction inventory, pending review. To the conservatory: postpone the exhibition planning meeting. To herself, standing at the window of the study with the phone still warm in her hand: *you are not protecting him anymore, you are burying him deeper, and you know the difference.*

Because that was the question that had kept her up — not whether the letter was true. She knew it was true. She had known it since the second reading, the way you know a key signature: instantly, structurally, beyond argument. The handwriting on the manuscript pages in the gallery storage — the ones she had called forgeries — she had seen them only in photographs, but she remembered now the strange rightness of them, the authority of a hand that wrote music the way other people breathed. Forgeries are usually stiff. Those pages had been alive.

No — the question was not whether. The question was what she owed. To her father, dead and unable to answer. To the truth, which had waited eleven years in a locked drawer. To the woman who had written *I only ask that you read this* and been refused even that.

Camille looked out at the garden, where the lilac her father had planted the year of his debut was coming into bloom without him, indifferent and beautiful, and she understood with sudden clarity that her father had left her the one thing he could never bring himself to do: the reading. Someone had to open the envelope. He couldn't. But he had kept it, and kept it, and kept it — and maybe, in some chamber of himself he never showed anyone, keeping it *was* the confession, the sentence existing somewhere outside his own head, waiting for a hand brave enough to untie the string.

His daughter's hand.

She gathered the folder, the photograph, the unopened-and-now-opened letter, and drove to the conservatory through morning traffic with them on the passenger seat like a patient she was transporting. At the security desk she asked, in a voice she barely recognized as her own, for the name of the night custodian.

The guard frowned. "Marchetti? He's not on tonight. Why?"

"Because," Camille said, and found that the sentence arrived whole, fully formed, having waited years in a drawer of its own, "I have something of his that was returned unopened. And I intend to deliver it."
