## 9. Translation as Testimony

The first thing you learn as a court translator is that testimony is a performance with a strict dress code. Everyone swears to tell the truth, and then everyone tells the truth the way they'd tell it at a dinner party where they owe someone money. My job was never to catch the lies. It was to make sure both languages lied in matching outfits.

So when I sat down across from Old Neni on that first morning—her kitchen smelling of woodsmoke and something medicinal I chose not to identify—I told myself this was translation work too. A dying woman singing songs into my notebook. Cultural preservation. The kind of thing they give small grants for and smaller attention.

I was wrong about that in a way that rearranged my spine.

* * *

Neni's house stood at the end of the lane past Sef's shop, low-roofed, its windows fogged from the inside like eyes going blind from within. Her daughter-in-law let me in without a word, which I took for either approval or surrender, and led me to a room where an old woman sat propped in a chair by the window, wrapped in wool despite the mildness of the season.

She looked at me for a long moment.

"You're late," she said—in her language, our language, the one I'd spent twenty years letting drain out of me like bathwater around a stopper that never quite fit. "Your mother was never late."

"I'm not—" I started, in the state tongue, and stopped. Correcting her felt like correcting weather. "I'm Emrys," I said instead, in hers. The word came out lopsided. Half the consonants had emigrated years ago; I could hear them living comfortably elsewhere now, sending nothing back.

"Emrys." She tried it, turned it over. "You speak like a person reading a letter written by someone else."

I laughed before I could decide whether it was cruel. It wasn't. It was accurate, which is worse, and she smiled at my laugh the way you smile at a child who has correctly identified a farm animal.

We began.

Dara Voss had lent me a cassette recorder and a stack of blank tapes, plus a lecture about azimuth alignment that I absorbed at roughly forty percent. The plan was simple: record everything, transcribe what I could, translate what survived transcription. Neni's memory was a coastline eroding in fast-forward—she'd lost decades from the middle outward, so that 1974 was gone entirely while 1953 sat vivid as a bruise—but the songs were anchored somewhere deeper than memory. She didn't recall them. She *was* them, the way you are your own heartbeat: not information, infrastructure.

The first song she gave me that morning was a harvest song I half-knew from childhood, or thought I did. My mother's version had been about wheat. Neni's version, sung slowly, with her eyes closed and her hands keeping time against the chair arms, was about wheat the way a map is about paper.

*"They counted the sheaves and wrote our names beside them,"* she sang, *"and the granary was full and the granary was empty, and the man who weighed said weigh again, and the man who wrote said write no more."*

I wrote it down. Then I translated it, loosely, into the state language, because that was the reflex—the same reflex that had softened my mother's words at official appointments thirty years ago, smoothing her anger into compliance, her grief into inconvenience. And as I wrote the smooth version, I noticed what I'd smoothed away.

Names.

There were names in the verse. Not characters' names—real ones. *Vessa. Ilm. The two brothers from the mill.* Names that meant nothing to me and would have meant nothing to any folklorist cataloguing harvest customs, because why would a wheat song name four specific people who had no business being in a wheat field?

Unless they weren't in a wheat field.

I asked Neni, casually, who Vessa was. She opened her eyes and looked at me with an expression I can only describe as patient disappointment, the look of a teacher watching a student fail a test they've already been given the answers to.

"Ask the song properly," she said. "You're translating it like a stranger."

* * *

That night, in the room Sef rented me above the shop—she'd refused payment, then charged me anyway, then left coins under my pillow like some punitive tooth fairy—I spread my notebook open and read the verse again. And again. And somewhere around the eleventh reading, with the lamp making a small hot circle in the dark and the village outside silent in the way villages are silent, which is to say not silent at all but listening, I stopped hearing a folk song.

I heard a report.

Consider it the way I finally did, line by line:

*"They counted the sheaves"* — a census. The state counted people the way it counted grain, and in the purge winters, being counted was the beginning of being taken.

*"and wrote our names beside them"* — registration lists. Neni hadn't invented names; she'd preserved them. Vessa. Ilm. The brothers from the mill. Four people, named in a song, carried in a mouth for fifty years because paper burns and mouths remember.

*"the granary was full and the granary was empty"* — I couldn't crack this one yet. It nagged at me all night, the way a word sits on the tip of the tongue except this was a whole image sitting on the tip of my understanding.

*"the man who weighed said weigh again"* — interrogation. Repeated questioning. The bureaucratic sadism of being processed twice because the first processing lost you.

*"and the man who wrote said write no more"* — and there it was, cold as a key turning. An order to stop recording. To erase. The state didn't just take people; it took the fact that they had existed to be taken.

My mother sang me this song. Sang it as a lullaby, with the names worn off or maybe never included in her version—or maybe included, and I'd forgotten them along with everything else, filed them in the same drawer where the language went to die. I lay in the narrow bed and stared at the ceiling and thought: *she was singing me a list of the dead, and I fell asleep to it.*

* * *

The next morning I came early. Neni was awake, unsurprised, already positioned by the window like a monument to waiting.

"The granary," I said, in her language, carefully, each word carried across the border like contraband. "Full and empty. What granary?"

She studied me for a while. Deciding something. I recognized the expression because I'd spent my whole professional life wearing its mirror image—the translator's face, weighing how much truth the moment can hold.

"There was a place," she said finally. "Past the quarry road, where the birches grow wrong. Too many, too close together. Planted, not grown." She paused. "You plant birches close together when you want them to hide something from the air."

My hands had gone very still on my knees. I made myself ask the next question, though every instinct I'd honed over twenty years of softening speech screamed at me to change the subject to bread or weather.

"How do you know?"

"Because I helped count them." Her voice didn't waver. That was the terrible part—not the content but the calm, the flatness of a woman reciting coordinates. "Forty-one. Before the frost came. We couldn't dig, so we covered them and planted the trees, and we made songs about it, because songs were the only paper they couldn't confiscate." She looked at me almost gently. "You think we sang because we loved singing. We sang because it was the only filing system left."

Forty-one. The number went into me like a splinter working toward the heart. Forty-one people, unrecorded, unmourned officially, dissolved by the state the way the state dissolved everything—administratively, thoroughly, with paperwork confirming they had never been paperwork to begin with.

And here was the thing that made me get up and walk to the window and stand there breathing until the shaking stopped: the grave had never been acknowledged. Not denied, exactly. Denied requires someone to deny it. It had simply never entered the record, and a thing that never enters the record cannot be investigated, mourned, compensated, or closed. Forty-one people in a stand of deliberately crowded birches, and the official history of this valley contained a gap shaped exactly like them.

Except it didn't anymore. Because yesterday an old woman had sung a wheat song into a cassette recorder, and today the transcript sat in my notebook in two languages, and a transcript is a document, and a document is a door.

* * *

Over the following days, the work changed character entirely. I stopped thinking of the sessions as preservation and started thinking of them—which sounds melodramatic, and I want to be precise about this—as exhumation.

Neni's repertoire, it turned out, was structured. The songs came in cycles, and the cycles mapped onto the purge years with a precision that made the hair on my arms rise. There were planting songs that recorded arrests—who was taken, from which house, in what month, identified by occupation and family rather than name where naming was too dangerous even in code. There was a wedding song whose verses tracked the tribunal itself: *"the bride wore grey, the groom wore grey, and the man with the book asked questions he already knew the answers to."* There was a spinning song, of all things, that encoded escape routes—actual directions, disguised as thread-counts and turns of the wheel, for the network that moved fugitives south. Someone's grandmother had memorized a map and set it to a tune so children could carry it through checkpoints without knowing what they carried.

"They used us," Neni said, when I pointed this out, sounding faintly offended on behalf of the children. "Of course they used us. Children forget faces. Children don't forget songs." She tapped her temple. "This was the safest bank in the province. No one robs a nursery rhyme."

I transcribed until my hand cramped. I cross-referenced dates against the few public records that survived—gazette notices, a regional almanac, the kind of dry documents historians use as ballast—and the songs matched. Not approximately. Exactly. When Neni sang that the miller's sons were taken "when the ice still held the cart ruts," the almanac confirmed a hard freeze in the second week of February. When she sang of a woman called only "the one who kept the school bell," the employment registers listed a school custodian arrested that spring, name redacted, fate unstated.

Every verse checked out. Every single one. Fifty years old, transmitted orally through singers who were themselves hunted, and more accurate than the archive.

I sat with that fact for a long time one evening, and what I felt wasn't triumph. It was closer to vertigo. Because if the songs were evidence—and they were, they were better evidence than most of what passes through courtrooms, corroborated, internally consistent, chain of custody running mouth to mouth to mouth—then they weren't just a heritage. They were a case. And a case needs a venue, and a venue means exposure, and exposure means that everything this valley had arranged around its wound—the silence, the politeness, Sef's flat refusals, the careful choreography of fifty years—would be dragged into the light and made to testify too.

I thought about Dara's illegal broadcast hour. I thought about the cassette labeled in handwriting I almost recognized. I thought about the young man standing at the edge of every photograph, half-turned away.

And I thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: *someone is going to have to decide what these songs are for.*

* * *

On the sixth day, Neni gave me a song I hadn't heard before—one she'd been holding back, I understood later, until she trusted what I'd do with the others. It was short. Six lines. She sang it facing the window, not me, her voice thinning on the high notes but refusing to bend.

*"Under the crooked birches, forty-one sleep.*
*The state says the field was always empty.*
*The state says the field was always empty.*
*The state says the field was always empty.*
*Count the trees. Count the trees.*
*Whoever counts the trees will find the arithmetic."*

A song with a repeated line like that isn't a song. It's a message with a melody attached—a dead drop set to music, designed to survive transmission even if the singer was taken mid-verse. Whoever wrote it had understood, with terrible clarity, that they might not live to point at the ground, so they built the pointing into the culture itself and trusted it to propagate.

I finished transcribing. I dated the page. I wrote *corroborated: almanac, Feb. freeze; quarry road geography confirmed by Sef (verbal),* because apparently I had become the kind of person who cites sources in a notebook, and then I sat there looking at the words *forty-one* in my own handwriting and felt the full weight of what I was holding.

This was no longer nostalgia. This was testimony. Legally cognizable, historically damning, morally radioactive testimony. If it reached a tribunal—if it reached anyone with authority and a conscience—it would force the state to acknowledge a mass grave, force the region to exhume and name and mourn, and force every person in Kell Hollow who had stayed silent to explain their silence under oath. Including Sef. Including, possibly, the man in the photographs. Including, if I was honest, me—because I had known the language once, and I had let it go, and a witness who discards the evidence is also making a choice.

"Neni," I said. "Do you understand what these songs are? What they could do?"

She turned from the window. For a moment her eyes were perfectly clear, the erosion reversed, and I saw the woman she must have been at twenty—someone you would follow into a snowfield without asking why.

"I know exactly what they are," she said. "I've been waiting fifty years for someone who could read the ledger." She reached over and closed my notebook with one dry hand, gently, the way you close a book you've decided someone isn't ready for. "Now go home and think hard about whether you're the courier or the censor. Both wear the same coat."

I walked back through the village in the blue dusk, past shuttered windows and smoke rising straight from chimneys, past Sef's shop with its light still on, past the lane that led, though I didn't yet know it led there, to a chapel with a man on its roof. In my bag, forty-one people rode in a spiral notebook, weightless as paper, heavy as the quarry road.

And behind me, in a chair by a window, the last living singer waited to learn whether she had spent her life building a weapon for someone who would dare to fire it.
