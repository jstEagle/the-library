## 8. Auction Day

The auction house had once been a furniture showroom, then a carpet warehouse, and now was neither, its floors a patchwork of mismatched tile that Edmund's eye catalogued the way it catalogued everything: cracked at the third row, heaved a quarter inch where the old loading dock used to be, load-bearing capacity of the mezzanine unknown and, frankly, suspect. Edmund stood at the back with a folding chair that wobbled on one shortened leg and told themselves, for the hundredth time since dawn, the plan.

Observe. Say nothing. Let the past be sold off quietly, lot by lot, to people who would never know what any of it meant. That had been the plan for thirty years, and the plan had worked beautifully. The plan had a perfect service record.

Edmund had dressed for invisibility: gray jacket, gray trousers, the whole ensemble of a man whom witnesses would later describe as "someone, I think, near the back." Priya had not been invited, or rather, Edmund had not invited her, which amounted to the same thing and was, they were beginning to understand, the whole architecture of their marriage in miniature.

The room filled slowly. Dealers with clipboards and the particular posture of people who had already decided what everything was worth. A woman in a quilted vest photographing lamps. Two sisters who had grown up four houses down from Zephyrine's and who, Edmund overheard, had come "for the good china, if it's real." A young couple measuring a dresser against an app on a phone, murmuring numbers.

At the front, Lot 1 through Lot 112 sat arranged on folding tables under fluorescent light, each item tagged in the auctioneer's orange stickers. Zephyrine's life, itemized. Edmund had done their own inventory weeks ago, in a notebook they carried now like a passport, and the two systems of accounting did not agree on anything except the facts.

Lot 12: the walnut sideboard. Auction estimate, one hundred to one hundred fifty dollars. Edmund's entry: *She refinished this herself the summer I was eleven, sanding until her hands went white, because Mrs. Okafor next door had one and Zephyrine refused to be outdone in walnut.* Lot 31: the brass lamp with the bent shade. Estimate, twenty dollars. Edmund's entry: *The shade bent the night she threw her shoe at the television when the referee missed the offside call, and she said, "That lamp has always been crooked, leave it alone," and for once I did.*

The auctioneer was a compact man named Denny with a voice like a well-oiled hinge and the relentless cheer of a person being paid by the lot. He began at nine sharp.

"Lot one, folks, a lovely mahogany whatnot shelf—"

It was not a lovely mahogany whatnot shelf. It was the shelf where Zephyrine kept her seed packets, organized by an inscrutable system she defended with her life, and where she kept, on the top shelf, a crayon drawing Edmund had made at nine years old of a house with too many windows, which she had framed, badly, in foil. The drawing was not among the lots. Edmund had checked the preview three times. Either it had been thrown away or it was in a box somewhere, and Edmund could not decide which possibility was worse.

*Say nothing. Let it go.*

The whatnot shelf went for forty dollars to the quilted vest. The sisters got the good china, which was not real, for sixty. The dresser went to the young couple, who high-fived, and Edmund found, to their surprise, that they were glad. It seemed like a dresser that wanted to be high-fived about.

* * *

The morning wore on. Edmund's notebook stayed closed in their jacket pocket. They bid on nothing. They raised their hand for nothing. They were a pillar, a pier, an abutment, bearing weight without complaint, and they hated it, and they did it anyway, because the doing of it was the oldest thing they knew how to do.

Around Lot 40, Denny's patter hit its stride, and Edmund began to understand what the room thought of Zephyrine Fairweather. It was not malice. It was something more efficient than malice. It was appraisal.

"Nice mid-century piece here, some wear, honest wear—"

Honest wear. The burn on the coffee table was from the iron when Zephyrine took up ironing her own curtains in a fit of thrift and immediately regretted it. The water ring was from the summer Edmund was fifteen and left a glass down without a coaster and received a lecture that lasted, conservatively, forty minutes and referenced the Treaty of Versailles.

"Contents of a single-owner estate, folks, everything must go—"

Everything must go. Edmund's hands were cold. The wobbly chair ticked against the floor with each shift of their weight, and twice the woman in front of them turned around.

At Lot 44, the room thinned. The dealers had their targets and were waiting, phones out, for the lots they'd circled. Denny's voice kept going, cheerful, metronomic.

"Lot forty-five, a box of miscellaneous garden tools—"

*The trowel she used to plant the marigolds the year I left for college, the year she stood in the driveway and waved until I couldn't see her in the mirror anymore, and I didn't look back because looking back felt like something a weaker person would do—*

"Lot forty-six, assorted linens, condition as found—"

*The tablecloth from Sunday tea, bleached nearly to nothing, soft as an apology.*

Edmund's heart was doing something structurally unsound.

"Lot forty-seven." Denny held it up between thumb and forefinger, and the room, which had been murmuring, went briefly, cruelly quiet, the quiet of people deciding whether to laugh.

The chipped blue teacup.

It looked small in his hand. It had always been small. It was white inside with a blue glaze running down the outside like weather, and the chip was at the rim, two o'clock, the chip Edmund had caused at nine years old with elbows that had not yet learned their own dimensions, and Zephyrine had not yelled. She had held it up to the light, turned it once, and said, "Well. Now it's mine," and drunk from the unchipped side ever after, and Edmund had spent thirty years not understanding that this was the single most important sentence anyone had ever said to them.

"Chipped porcelain teacup," Denny announced, with the honesty of a man who had appraisals to protect. "Some damage. Could be a pencil cup, could be a planter, who knows."

A man in the third row — Edmund had noticed him earlier, a dealer, mid-fifties, the kind of face that had priced ten thousand dead people's possessions and slept fine — raised a card without looking up from his phone.

"Five dollars, thank you sir, five dollars for the teacup—"

Something happened in Edmund's chest that they would later, in the notebook, describe as *a load transfer*. Something that had been bearing weight for decades suddenly shifted to a different member, and the whole structure registered the change.

"Six," said the quilted vest, half-laughing.

"Six dollars, six, do I hear—"

Edmund's hand went up. It went up on its own, the way a knee jumps. Their voice came out of them from somewhere below the sternum.

"Twenty dollars."

The word *twenty* came out cracked down the middle, and every head in the front half of the room turned, and Edmund, whose entire life had been an exercise in being unremarkable to witnesses, was suddenly, catastrophically *remarkable*: the gray man at the back, standing now, though they had no memory of standing, holding up a bidder card they had registered that morning under their own name, which they had done, they realized now, without ever admitting to themselves why.

The dealer in the third row finally looked up from his phone. He looked at Edmund. He looked at the teacup. He did the arithmetic of a man who could not imagine a reason.

"Twenty-two," he said.

"Twenty-five." Edmund's voice shook. They let it shake. It was the first honest sound they had made in a room full of people in thirty years.

The quilted vest whispered to her neighbor. Denny's eyebrows performed a small, delighted dance. "Twenty-five dollars, folks, we have a connoisseur of chipped porcelain—"

"Thirty," said the dealer, and there was something in it now, a test. Edmund had seen that look on bridges: the load applied to see what gives.

"Sixty."

A laugh ran through the room, not unkind. The sisters from four houses down had turned fully around. One of them squinted, and Edmund watched recognition arrive on her face like weather — *that's the Fairweather kid, the quiet one* — and she said nothing, and for once Edmund was grateful for someone else's silence.

"Sixty-five," said the dealer, and now he was annoyed, because sixty-five dollars was a stupid price for a chipped cup and they both knew it, and the auction had stopped being about the cup.

"Two hundred," Edmund said.

The room made a sound like a held breath released. Denny actually stepped back from the podium. "Two hundred dollars, ladies and gentlemen, two hundred for the teacup — do I hear two-fifty? Two hundred — going once—"

The dealer looked at Edmund for a long moment, and whatever he saw there — the shaking hands, the wet eyes, the gray man coming apart at a load he'd been carrying since he was nine — he apparently decided was not worth two hundred and five dollars. He shook his head, almost politely, and looked back at his phone.

"Going once. Going twice."

*Say something,* Edmund thought, with a clarity that felt like falling. *For once in your life, when it counts, say something out loud.*

"Sold," said Denny, "to number thirty-one, for two hundred dollars," and the room applauded — a small, puzzled, genuine applause, the applause people give when they've witnessed something they don't have a category for — and Edmund stood there in the aisle with a chipped blue teacup being carried toward them in a tissue-lined box, crying the way they had not cried at the funeral, which they had also attended in gray and also said nothing.

The woman in the quilted vest touched their arm as she passed. "Someone special?" she asked.

Edmund looked at the box in their hands. Two hundred dollars. Absurd. Zephyrine would have called it highway robbery and then hidden the cup in a sock drawer to protect it from burglars.

"My mother," Edmund said. Out loud. In a room full of strangers. The word *mother* sat in the air between them, and Edmund did not take it back, and did not soften it, and did not leave the room before the conversation ended.

The woman smiled and moved on. Edmund stood holding the box while Denny's hinge-voice moved on to Lot 48, a set of encyclopedias, *complete, folks, complete* — and Edmund's phone buzzed in their pocket.

Priya.

Edmund looked at the name on the screen for three full rings. Then they answered, and their voice was still wrecked, and they did not try to hide it.

"I'm coming home," they said. "There's something I have to tell you. I should have told you years ago. I'm coming home now."

There was a pause on the line long enough for Edmund to hear the kitchen clock, the one Priya wound on Sundays, ticking in the background of her silence.

"Edmund," she said, "what did you buy?"
