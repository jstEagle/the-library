## 5. What the Photograph Knew

The photograph sat in my jacket pocket for six days before I could look at it properly.

I'd told myself I was waiting for the right moment — good light, a quiet room, a steady hand. But the truth was simpler and uglier: I was afraid of the scratched-out face. Not because it might be someone terrible. Because it might be someone I knew.

On the seventh day, in the boiler room where the conservatory kept its forgotten things, I laid it flat on a workbench under the bare bulb and gave it the attention I'd been withholding. Five students by a radiator, arms slung over shoulders, all of them laughing at something outside the frame. Edmund Halloway stood behind them with his hands on his collar like a man adjusting himself for posterity. I knew four faces instantly: Zephyrine, nineteen and luminous; Tomas Reeve, already wearing the expression of a man rehearsing for power; Marcus Ellery, who'd left for Vienna and never come back; and me, twenty-two, grinning like the future was a place I had a reservation.

The fifth face was a wound. Someone had taken something sharp to the emulsion and worked at it until the features were gone, leaving only a gray scar where a person used to be. The scratching was furious, patient work. You don't do that in a moment of carelessness. You do it when you need someone erased so thoroughly that even you can't see them anymore.

I held the photograph up to the light. Under the bulb's angle, the scratches caught differently than the rest of the image — and beneath them, faint as breath on glass, the paper remembered what it had been made to forget. A jawline. The set of an ear. And at the bottom edge, half cut off by the frame, a hand resting on the radiator itself, fingers spread against the iron.

I'd seen that hand before. Two days after the funeral, when a stranger named Ada Merrow had pressed this photograph into my palm and said *Radiators* like a password and walked away into the rain.

* * *

Finding her took three days, which surprised me. I'd assumed she'd vanish — people who carry photographs like that usually do. But the conservatory's night porter, a retired cellist named Gruber who owed me for fixing his radiator every winter since I took the mop, mentioned a woman renting a room above the sheet-music shop on Vell Street. "Comes in asking about Halloway's papers," he said. "Been asking for weeks. Before he died, even."

Before he died. I turned that over all the way across town.

The shop was called Cantus & Sons, though there were no sons and hadn't been for years. Ada Merrow answered the door of the upstairs room herself, and she didn't pretend surprise. She looked at me the way you look at weather you've been expecting.

"You found the hand," she said.

"I found enough."

She stepped back and let me in. The room was small and severe: a cot, a kettle, and a card table buried under paper. Edmund's paper. Sketches, letters, programs going back thirty years. She'd built an archive out of a dead man's leavings, and she'd done it alone, in a rented room, while the conservatory he built mourned him like a saint.

"Sit down, Silas." She poured tea without asking whether I wanted any. "You have questions. I'm going to answer all of them, and then you're going to wish I hadn't."

"Start with the photograph."

"Edmund took it. Autumn, my last year." She turned it over in her hands, gentle, the way you touch a scar that's yours. "He gave copies to each of us. Then, after—" she stopped, started again. "After, he collected them. All but mine. I kept mine hidden, and he spent an evening with a penknife making sure no one else would ever see my face in it."

"Why?"

"Because I stopped existing." She said it without drama, which somehow made it worse. "You've heard the story, I imagine. The version they tell now. That there were five Radiators — you, Zephyrine, Tomas, Marcus, and a girl who flamed out and left. That's the polite one. The impolite one is that there were always five, and the fifth was me, and I didn't leave. I was expelled."

"For what?"

Ada looked at me for a long moment. Whatever she saw in my face made her decide I'd earned the whole weight of it.

"For refusing him." She wrapped both hands around her cup. "Edmund liked devotion, Silas. He required it. From the Radiators most of all — we weren't just students, we were his. And when I wouldn't be his, not in the way he wanted, he ended me. Quietly. No hearing, no record. One day I was his finest pupil and the next I was a cautionary tale he told other girls about ambition. He rewrote everything. Programs, letters, the photograph. Ten years of history, edited by one man with a penknife, and everyone let him, because the alternative was ending up like me."

The tea went cold in my hands. I thought of Edmund's hands on his collar in the photograph, adjusting himself for posterity, and understood that was exactly what he'd been doing. Posterity was a thing you could groom.

"And the scandal," I said. My voice came out flat. "Three years ago. The accusations. The—"

"The fabricated recordings? Yes." She set her cup down with a small, precise click. "I've spent two years pulling that thread, Silas, because when I read about your disgrace, I recognized the stitching. It was Edmund's technique. The anonymous letter, the 'evidence' arriving exactly when it would do maximum damage, the institution closing ranks before anyone could ask a question. I know how it works from the inside. Someone learned it from him too."

"Tomas."

"Or Zephyrine." She didn't flinch saying it. "Or both. Or someone protecting one of them. What I know is this: whatever they accused you of, the machinery behind it was older than either of you. It was Edmund's machine, run by his best student. And Tomas Reeve is now director of the conservatory, which is precisely where Edmund's machine would want to live."

I stood up. I don't remember deciding to stand; my body simply refused the chair. Three years of shame rearranged themselves in the space between one heartbeat and the next — the locked doors, the colleagues who wouldn't meet my eye, the way I'd learned to apologize with my posture. None of it had been true. None of it had ever been about me. I had spent three years polishing floors under the people who broke me, and I'd believed I deserved the mop.

"Say it plainly," I said. "Who did it."

"I can't. Not yet. I know the shape of it, not the signature." Ada rose too, and for the first time she looked tired, truly tired, a woman who'd been carrying a decade alone. "But I'll tell you what I do know, Silas, and you can decide what to do with it. Zephyrine knows more than she's said. When she saw me at the funeral, she went white — not with grief. With recognition. She knew me. Which means she knew what happened to me, which means she has known for ten years that Edmund was capable of erasing a person, and she stayed anyway. She flourished, actually. People who stay in houses like that always flourish a little, at first. It's the price of admission."

"She wouldn't—"

"Wouldn't she?" Ada's voice was almost kind. "You loved her. Love makes excellent cover. Ask yourself why the will paired you two on Edmund's score. Ask yourself why a man as careful as Edmund, a man who edited his own photograph, would leave his final work in the hands of the two people with the most reason to dig through his papers."

The room tilted slightly. I gripped the table's edge.

"He wanted this found," I said slowly. "Even dead, he wanted—"

"Maybe. Or maybe he never imagined anyone would get far enough to look. Either way, the annotations are in my handwriting, Silas. On his score. Pages I worked on before he expelled me, pages he never removed because removing them would mean admitting I existed. Every time you and Zephyrine turn those pages, you're reading my ghost. And she knows it. Watch her face next time."

* * *

I walked back across the city without feeling the cold. The streets did their evening business around me — lit windows, hurrying figures, a busker murdering a Vivaldi concerto outside the station — and none of it touched me, because inside my skull a single sentence kept turning like a phrase I couldn't resolve:

*Someone learned it from him too.*

At the conservatory, the halls were emptying. I pushed my cart through the marble corridors out of pure habit, past the practice rooms, past the closed doors behind which students became what they would become or didn't. Outside room 4, the one with the good piano, someone was playing — scales first, then the opening of Edmund's unfinished score, badly, a student sight-reading it from the copies Tomas had circulated for the memorial concert.

And then the playing stopped mid-phrase, and a voice I knew better than my own said, quietly, to no one: *"Not like that. Never like that."*

Zephyrine. Alone in the dark, correcting a ghost.

I stood in the corridor with my mop and my bucket and the photograph burning in my pocket, and I understood that tomorrow I would have to open a door I'd spent three years keeping shut — not the practice room door, but the one between what she'd told me and what was true.

I turned off the lights and let the dark keep her secret one more night.
