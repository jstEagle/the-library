## 7. Almost

I rehearsed it on the walk over, the way I'd rehearsed everything for three years. *Zephyrine, I know about Ada Merrow.* Four words, then silence, then whatever she gave me. That was the plan. Simple as a C major scale.

The conservatory at night is a different building. The marble gives back nothing; the radiators tick like patient clocks; somewhere a boiler breathes in the walls the way it always has, the way it did when we were nineteen and thought the building belonged to us because Edmund had given us keys. I let myself into the archive with the janitor's ring — the one honest advantage of my disgrace — and waited for her among Edmund's boxes.

She came at nine, exactly when my note said. She still keeps time like a metronome set by someone else's hand.

She saw the photograph lying open on the table and stopped walking. Just stopped, mid-stride, one hand still raised from pushing the door, and for a moment she was seventeen again, caught at something by a teacher who already knew.

"Where did you get that."

"Ada gave it to me. At the funeral." I turned it toward her. Five students by the radiator in the old east practice hall, Edmund's arm around the boy on his left — Tomas, young, already wearing authority like a borrowed coat. Me, grinning like an idiot. Zephyrine with her cello between her knees like something she was guarding. And the fifth figure, scratched out so hard the paper had torn through to the wall behind them. "You went white when she said the word. You knew her before she said it."

"Silas—"

"And this." I laid the score pages beside the photograph. The unfinished movement, Edmund's last, covered in annotations in a small slanted hand — fingering suggestions, a dynamic marking here, a whole passage rewritten there. "Ada's handwriting. From before her expulsion. It's all over his final work, and you told me Tuesday you didn't recognize it."

* * *

She sat down slowly, the way people sit when standing has become too much work. She put her hands flat on the table, then picked them up again, then put them down. I had never seen Zephyrine not know what to do with her hands.

"You have to understand what he was," she said finally. "Edmund. What he was to us."

"He was my teacher."

"He was everything." Her voice came out low and even, the performance voice, the one she used on students. Then she heard it herself and stopped. Started again, plainer. "He built us, Silas. He named us. Do you know what that's worth to someone who arrived here with nothing? The Radiators. We were warm because he said we were."

"The fifth Radiator," I said. "Tell me about her."

"She was before you." A pause that lasted too long. "Ada studied with him for two years. She was — gifted. Genuinely gifted. And then she wasn't anymore, and he removed her, and we were told never to speak of it. So we didn't."

"That's not an answer. That's obedience."

"Yes." She said it without flinching, which was worse than flinching. "It was obedience. Ten years of it."

"Why?"

"Because he asked." She looked at me then, straight on, and I understood she had decided — right there, in that second — to stop lying. I could see the decision land in her face like weather changing. "And because Tomas asked. And because my chair in the quartet, my studio, my name on the faculty list — all of it sits on ground he prepared. You don't dig under your own floor, Silas. Not when you've watched what happens to people who do."

"What happened to her?" I touched the torn place in the photograph. "What did she do, to be erased like this?"

Zephyrine was quiet for a long time. The radiator ticked. Somewhere above us a door closed, footsteps crossed a practice room, faded.

"She refused him," she said at last. "That's all. That's the whole crime. He made it — suggestive — and she wouldn't play along, and so she became unstable, ungrateful, difficult. He told us she'd been dismissed for cause. He told us her playing had declined. He told us—" Her voice cracked, just once, and she pressed on. "He told us enough that we were grateful not to be her."

"And you believed him."

"I *chose* to believe him. There's a difference and it took me ten years to learn it." She stood up, walked to the shelves, ran her fingers along the spines of scores she clearly couldn't read right now. "And then you came, and you were brilliant, and he loved you, and I told myself the Ada thing was old history, closed, finished. And then—"

"Then the scandal."

She turned around.

"I need you to hear all of it," she said. "Because if you hear half of it you'll hate me forever, and if you hear all of it you'll only hate me for a while, and I can live with *a while*. I've been living on it."

"Say it."

"The accusation against you. The complaint that ended your career." She swallowed. "It was written in Tomas's office. I know because I was there when he drafted it. It was about protecting the circle — Edmund had begun to fail, publicly, unpredictably, and Tomas decided the school needed one clean story, one fall, and you were the most visible of us. Your disgrace would draw every eye. No one would look at what Edmund was becoming, or what he'd done to Ada, or at Tomas consolidating power while the great man declined. You weren't guilty of anything, Silas. You were *useful*."

The room went very far away. I could hear my own pulse in my ears, slow, stupid, counting out time like a student waiting to be told the tempo.

"You knew," I said. "Three years ago. You knew it was staged, and you said nothing."

"I said nothing because saying something would have cost me everything, and I told myself you were gone anyway, that you'd fled, that nothing I did would bring you back—" She stopped. Pressed both hands over her face. When she lowered them her eyes were wet and furious, mostly at herself. "Yes. I knew. And I stayed. I took the studio. I took the position. I chose survival over you, and I have replayed that choice every single day for three years, and I would be lying to you now if I said I wouldn't make it again, because fear doesn't care how much you love someone. Fear wins. Fear won."

There it was. Everything I'd come for, handed to me whole, and it fit together so perfectly that I wished it didn't — because a broken story I could argue with, but this one was seamless, and seamless things are true.

"You watched them bury me," I said. "You came to the edge of the grave and you put a flower on it and you went home to your warm apartment and your good chair."

"Yes."

"One word, Zephyrine. One sentence to the board. That's all it would have taken."

"And it would have been my grave instead of yours, and Tomas would have dug it beautifully, and Edmund—" She shook her head. "I'm not asking you to forgive it. I'm telling you what I am. I'm the person who calculated. I've always been the person who calculates. You're the person who runs. Between us we've spent three years proving neither works."

* * *

I should have said it then. I want to be honest about that, since honesty is apparently the theme of my life now. There was a moment — she was standing by the shelves with her arms wrapped around herself, and the lamplight caught the wet on her cheeks, and she looked like every version of her I had ever loved, including the ones I hadn't met yet — there was a moment when the sentence rose in me, the real one, the one I'd carried for three years like a stone in my shoe.

*I love you. I loved you then. Even knowing this — especially knowing this.*

It got as far as my teeth.

But what came out instead was: "Did you ever write to me? In those three years. Did you ever even pick up a pen?"

Her face did something complicated. "Twice. I wrote twice. Both times I got as far as your name."

"And then?"

"And then I thought about what Tomas would do to you if I reached out. He kept a file on you, Silas. After you left. In case you ever came back and talked." She laughed, a terrible small sound. "So I protected you the only way I had left. By staying away. Isn't that a joke? My cowardice wore the costume of mercy, and I let it, because the costume fit."

I gathered the photograph off the table. My hands were steady, which surprised me; I'd expected them to shake. I slid the score pages back into their folder, Ada's small slanted hand disappearing under the cardboard, and I stood there holding the folder against my chest like a shield, like a door.

"Silas." She came toward me. "Say something. Say anything. Yell at me, tell me you hate me, tell me I'm a monster — just don't do the thing where you leave with the sentence still in your mouth. Please. Not this time. Not with me."

And God help me, I almost didn't. I almost opened. I felt the words climb my throat like heat.

But all I could see was the flower on the grave. All I could hear was three years of marble corridors and mop water and the sound of her teaching through a closed door, close enough to touch, close enough to say it, and never once saying it — and now I knew why I'd never said it, why I'd always stopped: some part of me had known. Some part of me had understood, all along, that the woman behind that door had chosen the institution over the man, and had chosen it again every morning, and would choose it tomorrow.

"I have to go," I said.

"Silas—"

"The memorial concert is Saturday. Tomas wants the score declared unperformable. Ada wants it premiered. And you—" I looked at her, and it hurt, it hurt like pressing on a bruise to prove it's real. "You haven't decided yet. Have you. Even now. Even after all of this, you're still calculating."

She didn't answer. That was the answer.

I walked to the door. Behind me I heard her say my name once more, quietly, the way you say a word you're trying not to lose, and I stood with my hand on the frame and the whole sentence assembled itself one more time, complete, ready, three years in the writing —

*I love you, and I forgive you, and I don't know if those can both be true, but I'd like to find out.*

Almost.

I walked out, and the door swung shut behind me, and the radiator in the archive ticked on without us, keeping time for no one.
