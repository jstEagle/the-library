## 8. Tomas Reeve's Version

The memorial concert was four days away, and Tomas Reeve had not yet written the eulogy.

This was not, he told himself, procrastination. It was discernment. A eulogy for Edmund Halloway could not be dashed off between meetings; it required the same rigor Edmund had demanded of them all. Every phrase load-bearing. Every silence intentional. He had drafted eleven versions in the past two weeks, and he had burned — literally burned, in the stone sink of his office, a habit he'd picked up from Edmund and never examined — the first nine.

The tenth and eleventh sat on his desk now, side by side, like two students who would not be told which of them was the favorite.

He picked up the tenth. Read the opening line. *Edmund Halloway did not teach music. He taught truth.*

He put it down.

It was a good line. It had been a good line for thirty years; he'd used a version of it in the lecture he gave every fall to the incoming fellows, the one about the Radiators, about the five students who had gathered in that drafty north practice room because the radiator there was the only one that worked, and how Edmund had named them for it — half joke, half christening — and how out of that accident had come the most consequential pedagogical lineage in the conservatory's history. Tomas had told that story so many times he no longer remembered whether he'd been in the room when the name was first spoken. He had begun to suspect that he had invented his own presence in it, the way a man invents a childhood.

The radiator story was true. That was the thing about Edmund: even his lies were built on true foundations, the way a forgery is built on a real canvas. The lie was always in the framing. In what was left outside the frame.

Tomas stood and went to the window. Below, in the blue hour before the building woke, a figure moved across the marble hall with a mop. He watched Silas Thornbury work the floor in long, even strokes, and felt the old satisfaction settle over him like a coat.

People would call it cruelty, what he had done. Some already whispered it. But cruelty was imprecise, and Tomas Reeve had never been an imprecise man. What he had done was triage.

Because here was the truth that no one wanted said aloud, the truth he would carry to his grave if necessary: Edmund's favoritism had been rot. Not metaphorical rot — structural. The old man had built the entire institution around a handful of chosen children and let the rest of the school starve in the shadow of their light. Tomas had watched it for two decades. He had watched Edmund hand Silas Thornbury a career the way you'd hand a boy a loaded instrument, and call it mentorship. He had watched Zephyrine Vale be loved in a manner that looked, from certain angles, exactly like being kept. He had watched the Radiators become less a circle than a court, and courts have only one law, which is proximity to the throne.

And courts, left unpruned, fall.

So when the opportunity came — when Silas's ambition and Zephyrine's silence and Edmund's blindness arranged themselves into a shape Tomas could work with — he had not created a scandal. He had merely redirected one. The letter, the altered manuscript, the anonymous complaint to the competition committee: those had been his, yes. He did not flinch from the word *forged*. But the rot they exposed — the favoritism, the closed doors, the way Edmund would have burned the school down for one boy's concerto — that rot had been real. Silas Thornbury had not been guilty of what Tomas accused him of. He had been guilty of what Tomas accused *Edmund* of, which was the same crime wearing a younger face.

Collateral. That was the honest word. A necessary purge requires a visible casualty, and Silas had been the most visible.

Tomas had made his peace with it three years ago, in this same office, at this same window. He had watched Silas carry a box of his own things across the courtyard, and he had felt — he had checked, carefully, the way you check a wound for fever — nothing that required treatment. The school was healthier. The fellowships had been redistributed. Two of the Radiators had flourished without the old man's gravity bending their orbits. The system worked better without its sun.

He had repeated this to himself so many times that it had worn smooth, like a stone carried in a pocket, and he had long ago stopped noticing that a stone carried in a pocket is a stone you never set down.

* * *

The archive was the problem. He had known it the moment the will was read.

Edmund had left the unfinished composition jointly to Silas and Zephyrine, and Tomas understood the old man's final move for what it was: one last act of favoritism, one last refusal to let the institution simply absorb its losses and move on. A dead man's hand reaching up out of the paper to rearrange the living. Tomas had spent three years building a conservatory where legacy was earned rather than bestowed, and Edmund's will had kicked a hole in the wall of it.

And now the archive was open, and Ada Merrow was in the building, and Zephyrine had gone white at a funeral over a single word, and Tomas had begun — for the first time in three years — to check his wounds for fever.

He went down to the archive on the third day, ostensibly to inventory the memorial program materials. The room was cold; the radiator in the corner had been dead for a decade, one of the small maintenance failures he had never gotten around to fixing, and he had never asked himself why. He found the manuscript where he knew it would be and turned to the early pages, the sketches, and there it was: the handwriting. Not Edmund's. Not Zephyrine's. A small, slanted, urgent hand that he had last seen twenty-two years ago on a stack of exercises Edmund had claimed as his own revisions.

Ada Merrow's hand.

Tomas had told himself, over the years, a version of the Ada story too, and like all his versions it was true in its bones. She had been expelled for cause. She had been disruptive. She had refused to accept the circle's structure. All of that was defensible, administratively. What he did not let himself hold was her face in the last meeting, or the way Edmund's voice had changed when he said her name afterward — a flatness Tomas had heard only once or twice in thirty years and had chosen, both times, not to translate.

He had been twenty-six. He had needed the circle more than he needed the truth of it. That was the transaction, and he had signed it, and he had spent two decades telling himself the signature was loyalty.

He closed the manuscript. He did not burn it. He noticed that he did not burn it, and the noticing felt like a symptom.

* * *

That night he sat in his office with the eleventh draft and forced himself to finish it.

*Edmund Halloway did not teach music. He taught truth.* No. He crossed it out and tried again. *We gather to honor a man who — * Who what. Who built five careers and erased a sixth. Who named a circle after a radiator because the radiator was the only warm thing in the room. Who loved his students the way a gardener loves a hedge: fiercely, and with shears.

Tomas wrote: *We gather to honor a man who told the truth.*

He read the sentence back. And then he sat very still, because his own hand had betrayed him — had written the word *truth* into the mouth of a man who had expelled a girl for refusing him and rewritten her out of a photograph, and the sentence sat there on the page like an accusation wearing a eulogy's clothes.

He tried to read past it. *A man who told the truth, even when the truth was costly — * His pen stopped. Costly to whom? The phrase had no object. It had never had an object. For thirty years the sentence had floated free of any cost at all, because the costs had been carried by other people: by Ada, expelled and erased. By Silas, exiled for a crime Tomas had authored. By Zephyrine, who had learned — God, when? — to survive by silence, and whom Tomas had counted on to keep surviving by it.

He thought of Zephyrine's face at the funeral. White, and then composed — the composition itself a confession, if you knew how to read it. He had taught her that composure. He had taught all of them that composure: the Radiator's first discipline, the ability to hold a note steady while the room burns. He had believed he was teaching strength.

He looked at the sentence again. *A man who told the truth.*

He could not say it. That was the discovery, arriving not as an argument but as a physical fact: his voice, if he opened his mouth in that hall on Friday night, would not produce those words. The grief was there — he was surprised by how much of it there was, a whole submerged continent of it, grief for Edmund, grief for the twenty-year-old who had signed the transaction, grief for the version of himself who had believed the purge was medicine and not just murder with a syllabus — but the grief and the sentence would not fit in the same mouth.

He burned the eleventh draft. The paper curled and went orange and was gone, and the stone sink held the ash, and Tomas Reeve sat in his office above the sleeping conservatory and understood, with the clarity he had always mistaken for his best quality, that he was going to be asked a question on Friday night in front of everyone, and that he did not yet know what his face would do.

Because Ada Merrow had requested a seat in the second row. And Zephyrine Vale had requested, in writing, to perform the unfinished movement — *the completed* unfinished movement, her phrasing, a small knife in an envelope — and the program still bore only her name, and Tomas had signed the printing order without reading it closely, the way he had stopped reading a great many things closely, and this morning a proof had arrived on his desk with two names on it.

*Zephyrine Vale. Silas Thornbury.*

And beneath them, in smaller type, a third line he had not approved, that the printer swore Zephyrine had added in the final revision:

*Restored from the sketches of Ada Merrow.*

Tomas read the proof twice. Outside, far below, a light came on in the marble hall — dawn, and the mop, and the man with it, beginning the floor he would finish and finish and finish.

Tomas reached for the phone to kill the program, and found that his hand — the hand that had forged a letter, that had signed the transaction, that had burned nine drafts — would not make the call.
