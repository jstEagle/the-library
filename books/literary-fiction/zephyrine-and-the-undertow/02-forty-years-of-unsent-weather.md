## 2. Forty Years of Unsent Weather

My mother's handwriting had two registers, and I'd spent thirty-four years believing I knew both. There was the public hand—the one on place cards and sympathy notes—upright, economical, a script that apologized for taking up space. And there was the private hand, the one on my childhood lunch bags: *Eat something green. Love, M.* Slightly looser. Still guarded.

The letters in the shoebox were in neither.

This was a third woman's handwriting entirely—slanted, urgent, the ink pressed so hard into the paper it dented the page beneath. The loops of her *y*s swung like pendulums. She wrote the way I'd never once seen her do anything: without checking first whether it was allowed.

*Dear Zephyrine,* the first letter began. *You'll laugh at me for writing this instead of saying it, but you always said I hid behind good manners like they were a flood wall.*

I sat on the floor of her bedroom with my back against the bed frame, and I read until the light went from amber to nothing, and then I read by the hallway light leaking through the door.

* * *

*March 14th*

*Dear Zephyrine,*

*You'll laugh at me for writing this instead of saying it, but you always said I hid behind good manners like they were a flood wall. So here is what I can't say over tea: I am afraid all the time now. Not of anything in particular. Of everything in general. Gerald says the business is fine, that the men asking questions are just men who ask questions, that Saltmarsh Row will still be here when we're old. But he's started locking his study, Zeph. My own husband locks a door against me, and I've started to be grateful for it, because whatever's behind it, I don't want to know if knowing means I have to choose.*

*I miss when our biggest problem was whether the tide would let us get to the chippy.*

*Yours, always,*
*M.*

* * *

The box held forty-one letters. I counted twice, because counting was easier than feeling. Forty-one letters spanning four decades, addressed to a woman named Zephyrine at an address on Saltmarsh Row, none of them stamped, none of them posted. A correspondence conducted entirely with herself. My mother had been writing to a ghost for longer than I'd been alive, and the ghost, as far as I could tell, had never written back.

Or couldn't.

I learned the shape of the rupture slowly, the way you learn the shape of a coastline—from the water side, one wave at a time. The early letters were warm, gossipy, full of names I didn't know: Deacon's dog, the Okafor twins, someone called Bree who apparently owed everyone money and was loved anyway. Then, around letter nine, the weather changed.

* * *

*August 2nd*

*Zephyrine,*

*They took Emeka on Tuesday. Two men in grey suits, no warrant anyone saw, and he was gone before the fish market opened. You weren't home. I knocked and knocked and then I did the worst thing, which was to feel relieved that you weren't home, because I don't know what my face does when I lie and I've never had to find out.*

*Gerald says it isn't connected. Gerald says a great many things in a voice designed to end conversations. But I saw the ledger page before he burned it—I wasn't snooping, I was looking for the electric bill—and I saw Emeka's name on it, and I saw what was written beside it, and I understood that my husband's respectability has a price list, and that last week I gave him the information he needed to afford it.*

*He asked me about the neighborhood meetings. Who attended. Who spoke loudest. He asked so gently, Zeph, the way you'd ask after someone's mother. And I answered, because I thought it was harmless, because I thought being useful was the same as being safe.*

*Tell your brother I'm sorry. Tell him even though it will make no difference, because some words are not for the person you wronged. They're for you, so you can live with the sound of them.*

*I won't sign this one. I don't deserve the signature.*

* * *

I put that letter down and went to the kitchen and drank a glass of water very slowly, standing at the sink my mother had kept immaculate for forty years, looking out at a garden she'd weeded on her knees every Sunday like a penance nobody had assigned.

Emeka. I searched the name on my phone with hands that wouldn't quite behave. Emeka Okonkwo-Adisa, the search results said, community organizer, Saltmarsh Row. Detained 1989 on fraud charges; charges contested; conviction overturned on appeal three years later, after which he emigrated and died abroad in 2011. There was a photograph. A young man with his sister's cheekbones and a smile that looked like it had opinions.

His sister. Zephyrine.

I sat down on my mother's spotless kitchen floor, which felt like the correct place to have a breakdown, and I understood that the house I grew up in was built on a foundation I'd never been allowed to inspect.

* * *

*June 9th (year unknown)*

*Zeph,*

*Forty years. Can you believe it? Forty years since I stood in your kitchen while you fried plantain and told me I was too thin and too polite and that both conditions were curable. I write to you every year on this date even though you've never received a single word, because Dr. Osei says grief needs somewhere to go and mine has decided to go to you.*

*Your daughter has a child now, I hear. Amara. I looked it up at the library like a criminal. I hope she has your temper and Emeka's stubbornness and none of my family's talent for silence.*

*I am so tired, Zeph. Tiredness is the tax on secrets. Nobody tells you that. Everyone imagines secrets as heavy things you carry, but really they're things that carry you—steering, always steering, away from certain streets, certain faces, certain questions at dinner parties. I have spent forty years being steered.*

*Theo has your laugh. Did I ever tell you that? No. How could I. He laughs with his whole chest, the way you used to, and every time it happens I feel like a thief who's broken into the wrong house and found the one honest thing inside it.*

* * *

"You've found them."

My father stood in the doorway of her bedroom. I hadn't heard the front door—he still had that gift, arriving like weather rather than a man. He was holding his coat over his arm and wearing the expression he wore at funerals and shareholder meetings, which were, I realized, the same expression.

"How long have you known?" I asked.

"Known." He set the coat down with great care, as if the coat were the delicate thing in the room. "Theo, those letters are forty years of a sick woman's—"

"Don't." I stood up. My knees cracked like the floorboards. "Don't do the voice. Don't do the thing where you make it smaller than it is."

He looked at the shoebox on the bed the way other men look at unexploded ordnance. "She was ill," he said quietly. "Toward the end especially. Confusion. Fixations."

"She wrote forty-one letters to a woman whose brother your business destroyed, and not one of them is confused. They're the clearest thing she ever wrote. I think they might be the only clear thing."

For a moment—one long, flickering moment—something moved behind his face. Something that might have been grief, or its poor relation, guilt. Then it closed, the way a shop closes, gates down, lights off.

"Burn them," he said.

"No."

"Theo." He stepped closer, and lowered his voice as though the neighbors could hear through walls that hadn't heard anything in forty years. "There are people still alive who remember those days. There are lawyers. There is the development—the Harrow Group is buying half of Saltmarsh Row, and if this surfaces now, if some journalist gets hold of—" He stopped himself. Recalibrated. When he spoke again it was almost tender, which frightened me more than shouting would have. "Son. For everyone's sake. Hers included. Let her rest."

"She rested for forty years," I said. "In a shoebox. Behind a wardrobe. While we ate dinner."

He picked up his coat. At the door he paused, and without turning around, delivered the sentence I would carry onto a bus two days later like luggage:

"You're either a Winterbourne or you're a story someone tells about one. Choose carefully."

Then he was gone, and the house did what it always did, which was absorb him without comment.

* * *

*Undated — the last letter*

*Zephyrine,*

*I am tired in a way sleep doesn't touch now, so I suppose this is the last one. I want to say it properly, finally, in case anywhere keeps a record of small cowardices: I informed on your brother. Me. Not Gerald, not his men. Me. I handed him the neighborhood on a tea tray because I was in love and in fear and I told myself those were different things.*

*I never sent these letters because sending them would have been for me, and I deserved nothing addressed to me. But if you're reading this—if anyone is reading this—then someone opened the wardrobe, and maybe that's the universe finally doing my confessing for me.*

*Find Amara. Tell her the truth costs everything except the self. I learned that too late to be brave, but not too late to be accurate.*

*All my love, which was real, which was the realest thing I owned,*
*Vivian*

*P.S. The tide comes back, Zeph. It always comes back. That's the whole lesson of living near water, and I never learned it until now.*

I read the postscript four times. Then I looked up the bus schedule to Saltmarsh Row, and discovered that the route itself was scheduled for termination at the end of the month—razed along with everything else, the last bus running Saturday.

Three days from now.

My father's voice was still in the room, or in me, or wherever his voice lived now: *choose carefully.* I folded my mother's last letter along its worn creases, put the lid on the shoebox, and booked nothing, planned nothing, packed nothing.

Some choices you don't make. You just wake up on Saturday already on the bus.
