## 3. The Last Bus to Saltmarsh Row

My father believes that a man who has made his peace with a thing should never have to look at it again. This is, I think, the entire architecture of his life: buy the view, close the curtains, call it taste.

So when I boarded the 47 on its second-to-last Saturday of existence—the council had already printed the notices, ROUTE WITHDRAWN, and the driver had the resigned serenity of a man driving a hearse for a bus route—I did not tell him. I told him I was going to the archive. My father approves of archives. Archives are where inconvenient things go to be quiet.

The bus emptied as it went south. By the time we crossed the old iron bridge, the passengers were the kind you only see on condemned routes: two women with shopping trolleys, a boy asleep against the glass with his mouth open to the world, and me, a man in a funeral suit holding a shoebox like a casserole dish at a wake. The river appeared between buildings in slices, brown and patient, and each slice looked exactly like my mother's handwriting—unhurried, slanting, going somewhere it had never been allowed to arrive.

Saltmarsh Row announced itself by smell before sight: brine, diesel, frying onions, and under all of it the sweet rot of a place that knows its lease is up. The houses were terraced brick the color of wet tea, most of them boarded, some of them wearing their windows like eyepatches. A developer's hoarding ran the length of the block—RIVERSIDE REGENERATION, ARTIST RENDERINGS OF PEOPLE WHO WILL NEVER LIVE HERE—and someone had spray-painted across the rendering's smiling couple: *WE WERE HERE.*

I found number 14 by process of elimination: it was the only door on the row standing open, and out of it came the sound of packing tape, that long tearing shriek, like the neighborhood being mummified one box at a time.

She was in the front room, up a ladder, lifting a curtain rail off the wall. She was perhaps fifty, close-cropped gray hair, forearms that suggested she'd done this move alone and intended to finish it alone. She looked down at me without coming down herself.

"You're blocking my light," she said.

"I'm sorry." I stepped aside. Then, because I am my mother's son and charm is the family reflex: "Actually, you're blocking mine."

The look she gave me could have stripped paint. She descended, folded the ladder with one hand, and said, "Winterbourne."

Not a question. That unsettled me more than anything else could have.

"How did you—"

"The jaw. You've got his jaw. And you flinched when I said the name, which means you know what it costs here." She wiped her hands on her jeans and held one out anyway, grudgingly, like a toll booth. "Amara."

Her hand was strong and dry and did not linger. Neither did mine.

"I found some letters," I said. "Behind my mother's wardrobe. They were addressed to your mother. Zephyrine."

Something moved through her face—not softening, more like weather changing over water. "Forty years," she said. "Mum kept every card, every note. When she died she had a drawer of things from your family. All of it from before. Nothing after." She looked at the shoebox under my arm. "And now you bring the rest."

"They were never sent. Any of them."

"I know." She turned away and picked up a roll of tape. "Because sending them would have required saying something out loud, and Winterbournes don't do out loud. They do behind wardrobes."

* * *

We worked. That was how it began—not with conversation but with the wordless choreography of clearing a dead woman's flat. She handed, I wrapped. Books. Photographs. A sewing machine so heavy it seemed to contain a moral lesson. Amara narrated nothing; she simply let me be useful, which I understood, eventually, was the interview, the audition, the sentence being passed.

It was on the third box that she finally sat down on the floor amid the bubble wrap and said, "Read me one."

"Read you—"

"One of hers. Your mother's. I've read Mum's side my whole life. Half a conversation." She patted the floor beside her. "Sit down, Theo Winterbourne. You look ridiculous hovering in a funeral suit."

I sat. I opened the shoebox. The letters were tied in bundles with ribbon gone brittle, and I chose one from the middle years, from before whatever silence fell, and read:

*"Zeph — The wisteria on your wall has come through my dreams again. In the dream I am picking it and Father says pick faster, we haven't got all season, and I wake with green stains on my fingers that aren't there. Do you think a person can owe a debt to a smell? Because I believe I owe one to that wall. Come Sunday. Bring the lemon cake and your terrible opinions about my husband. I have missed your terrible opinions the way other people miss weather. — V."*

Amara laughed—a short, startled sound, quickly suppressed, like something escaping a locked room. "Vivienne," she said. "Mum called her Viv. Said she was the only rich girl who knew how to be poor properly."

"She wasn't rich then?"

"Not until your father." Amara's face closed like a shop shutter. "You want to know what happened, don't you. You've read far enough to know there's a hole in the middle and you've come all this way to stand at the edge of it."

"I want to know what my mother spent forty years apologizing for."

"And what will you do when you know?" She leaned forward. "That's the real question. Everyone wants the story. Nobody wants the bill."

Before I could answer, her phone buzzed on the windowsill. She glanced at it and her whole body changed—shoulders dropping, jaw setting. "Speaking of bills," she said. "There's a car outside that's been circling twice. Big black German thing. That'll be for you."

* * *

My father does not enter rooms; he arrives at them, the way bad news arrives, already certain of its welcome. He came through the open door of number 14 without knocking, took in the bare walls, the boxes, his son sitting on the floor of a condemned house in a funeral suit, and his expression performed the full tragedy: disappointment, weariness, love-as-control.

"Theo. We're leaving."

"We're not."

"Don't do this here." His voice dropped to the register he used for boardrooms and funerals. "Whatever she's told you—"

"She hasn't told me anything yet. You're about to, though. I can see it loading."

He glanced at Amara, at the neighbors' faces appearing in doorways down the row—Saltmarsh Row had learned long ago to come out when a Winterbourne arrived, the way gulls come out when a trawler dumps its waste. "Get up," he said quietly. "For everyone's sake."

"There it is," I said. "Everyone's sake. Do you know what's in this box? Forty years of unsent letters. My mother writing to this woman—" I gestured at Amara, who had risen to her feet, arms folded, watching like a judge who already knew the verdict "—writing to her every year, every month sometimes, and never once posting one. Never once walking down this street. Do you know why?"

"Theo—"

"*Do you know why?*"

The whole row was listening now. Mrs. Okafor from number 11. The boy from the bus, awake, holding a bag of chips. Somewhere a dog, professionally interested.

My father looked at me for a long moment, and I watched him make the calculation he had made his whole life: the cost of the truth versus the cost of the scene. And I watched him choose the scene, because the scene, at least, he could leave.

"Your mother was ill," he said. "Grief makes people confused. This woman is filling your head with—"

"With what?" Amara said, stepping forward. "Say it. Say her name. Say Zephyrine's name and then say what you did to her brother, right here, in front of the street that remembers him."

The silence went on so long I could hear the river.

Then my father straightened his coat, looked at me the way you look at a company you've written off, and said, "When you're ready to behave like a Winterbourne, you know where home is. Until then—" he glanced around the room, at the peeling walls, the boxes, the witnesses "—don't bother."

He left. The door didn't slam; he was too controlled for that. It clicked shut, precise as a signature.

Down the row, somebody exhaled. Mrs. Okafor said something in Yoruba that needed no translation. The boy offered me a chip.

Amara sat back down on the floor beside me, closer than before. Her shoulder touched mine and stayed.

"He'll disown you properly at the meeting next week," she said. "Whole community's been called to vote on the demolition. Last one ever. He'll want an audience."

"Good," I said, and found, to my surprise, that I meant it. "I'll have something to read."

She looked at the shoebox between us, then at me, and for the first time her eyes weren't measuring me for a coffin. "You don't even know what's in the last ones yet."

"No," I said. "But I'm done reading them alone."

Outside, the light was going gold over the water, the way it must have gone forty years ago over two girls and a wall of wisteria, and down Saltmarsh Row the doors stayed open, one after another, like the street itself deciding to keep watch.
