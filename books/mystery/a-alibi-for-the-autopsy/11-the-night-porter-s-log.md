## 11. The Night Porter's Log

The archive room of the Saltmere Grand lived on the lower ground floor, behind the laundry, where the air was always warm and damp and smelled faintly of bleach and old paper. It had no window. It had a single bulb on a pull-chain and a row of grey steel cabinets that had outlived three managers and, if the dust on top of them was any measure, at least one decade of genuine neglect.

Tamsin had keys to all of it. That was the thing about being the night man — the hotel forgot what it had given you. Keys to the service corridors, keys to the storage rooms, keys to the archive nobody had asked about in years. He had held them for five years and never once used this particular one until now.

He told himself it was routine. Records request. The police had reopened the file; Callum had been very clear, very polite, very much like a man laying out cutlery before a meal, that the Saltmere's paperwork would need to be made available. Duty logs, incident reports, the lost-property book. Tamsin was simply getting ahead of it. Being useful. Being the concierge.

He pulled the chain. The bulb buzzed, flickered twice, and settled into a tired yellow glow.

The cabinets were organized by year, more or less, the way a drunk organizes a bookshelf — the intention visible, the execution abandoned somewhere around 2019. He found the box marked *NIGHT DUTY LOGS 2019–2020* on the second shelf, wedged behind a box of Christmas decorations that smelled of mould and cinnamon. He carried it to the folding table under the bulb and set it down, and for a moment he just stood there with his hands flat on the cardboard, listening to the laundry machinery thudding through the wall like a slow heartbeat.

Five years ago, on the night of the fourteenth of March, he had not been at the desk.

He had told the police he was. He had told them he'd sat at the desk from eleven until six, that he'd seen nothing, heard nothing, that the fog had come in off the flats and swallowed the whole coastline and the hotel with it. He'd said it in a flat, careful voice, in a room with grey light, and a detective whose name he'd forgotten had written it down and thanked him, and that had been the end of it. The file had closed like a door. Daniel Voss had walked out into the fog and never walked back in, and the sea had been declared the culprit, and the Saltmere Grand had gone on ringing its guests down to breakfast.

Tamsin had carried that lie the way you carry a stone in your boot — not crippling, not even painful most days, just always there, a weight you'd adjusted your whole gait around.

He opened the box.

* * *

The logs themselves were ledger books, A4, one per month, the kind with the marbled covers that the hotel had bought in bulk from a stationer in Saltmere town before everything went digital and then, in the way of hotels, half-digital and half-not. Each page was ruled into columns: time, entry, initials. The night porter's handwriting was supposed to be legible, unemotional, boring. Tamsin had been praised for it once, early on, by a manager who'd since died of a heart attack in the wine cellar. *Your log reads like a man who has nothing to hide, Iversen.* He'd laughed at the time. He thought about it now and felt the stone in his boot turn over.

March 2019. He found it a third of the way down the box, spine cracked, cover furred with dust. He blew on it and the dust rose in the bulb-light like something startled.

He turned the pages with the care of a man handling evidence, which, he supposed with a cold settling in his stomach, was exactly what he was doing.

The fourteenth. He read the entries from the top of the page, the day porter's neat block capitals giving way at 23:00 to his own slanted, slightly left-leaning hand. He knew his own handwriting the way he knew his own face in a mirror — including the flaws. The way his *t*s were crossed with a single impatient stroke. The way his *7*s had a continental slash he'd picked up from a Belgian chef years ago and never shaken. The way, when he was tired, his letters drifted downhill across the ruled lines like a row of guests who'd had too much at the bar.

The 23:00 entry. *Handover complete. All keys accounted for. Fog thick. Mrs. Ellery in 214 requested extra blanket, delivered.*

His initials. T.I.

He stared at it. It looked right. It *felt* wrong.

He turned the page back and forth, comparing. The blanket entry. The 01:00 round — *Exterior doors checked. Boathouse padlock secure.* The 03:00 entry — *Quiet. Radio on low.* The 06:00 — *Handover to day staff. Fog lifting.*

All of it in his handwriting. All of it, he understood, in his handwriting the way a forged painting is in the master's style — close enough to pass at a glance, and wrong in every particular if you knew what to look for.

He laid the ledger flat and leaned over it, close enough that his breath fogged the page, and began to read like a concierge reads a guest.

* * *

The first thing was the ink.

The hotel used the same pens at the desk for years — the cheap ballpoints from the box behind reception, blue, the kind that skipped and blobbed. His March entries were in that blue, that same slightly watery blue that faded where his hand had dragged across the page. But the 01:00 entry — *Boathouse padlock secure* — was in a different blue. Not obviously. Not if you were glancing. But under the archive room's yellow bulb, with his nose an inch from the paper, he could see it: a fraction darker, a fraction drier, the stroke more even, more confident. A better pen. Someone had sat down with a better pen and written *Tamsin Iversen* onto that night.

The second thing was the slant. His handwriting drifted downhill when he was tired, and he had been tired every night of that winter — he remembered the tiredness the way you remember a fever. But the 01:00 and 03:00 entries ran dead level along the ruled lines. Level as a spirit gauge. Nobody's tired handwriting is that disciplined. Only a forger's is, because a forger is concentrating, and concentration is the one thing you cannot fake in someone else's hand.

The third thing was the *7*. His continental slash. It was there, in the 03:00 entry, *Radio on low* — no, wait. He went back. There was no 7 in that entry. He hunted down the page. *03:47, corridor light 2nd floor replaced, bulb no. 17.* There it was. The slashed 7. Except the slash was wrong — too long, too deliberate, drawn with the flourish of a man who had practised it. Tamsin's own slashes were quick and slightly crooked, like everything else he did at three in the morning. This one was a signature. This one was someone showing off.

He sat back on the folding chair, and the chair complained under him, and the laundry thudded on, and Tamsin Iversen — who had spent five years believing he had committed a single sin, the sin of silence, the sin of the lie told in grey light — understood that he had been wrong about the shape of his own crime.

He had not merely lied. He had been *furnished*. Someone had built his alibi for him, brick by brick, in his own handwriting, and then let him carry it.

* * *

He did the thing he always did when his mind started to sprint: he made it walk. He took out his phone, photographed every page of the March ledger, then every page of February and April for comparison, then — because his gift for noticing had never once let him stop at the first answer — he went back through the box and pulled the log for March 2018 and March 2020 and laid them side by side on the table under the bulb, three years of his own nights, and studied them the way he'd study three guests in the lobby who might be the same man in different coats.

February 2019: downhill drift, watery blue, crooked slashes. His.

April 2019: the same. His.

March 2019, the fourteenth through the sixteenth: three nights of level lines, confident ink, practised slashes. Then, on the seventeenth, the handwriting stumbled back to itself — the drift returning, the pen changing, the *7*s going crooked again — like a man waking from anaesthetic.

Three nights. Someone had written him for three nights. The night Voss vanished, and the night either side, to give the lie a spine. That was craft. That was patience. That was someone who knew that a single forged entry is a thread and three is a rope.

And someone who knew, Tamsin thought, with the cold now fully settled, exactly where he had actually been. Because you don't build an alibi for a man unless you know he needs one. You don't forge *Boathouse padlock secure* at 01:00 unless you know the boathouse is the one place in the world that entry must never be checked against.

He hadn't been at the desk. He'd been in the boathouse with Odile, and the fog had come in, and he had seen — no. Not seen. *Chosen not to see.* Two figures at the water's edge, shapes without faces, and he had turned Odile's face gently back toward him with one hand and looked at her instead, because looking at her was easier than looking at the water, and he had told himself ever since that he hadn't seen enough to say.

Someone had known all of it. Someone had stood in the dark and watched him choose not to look, and had gone back inside, and had picked up a better pen.

* * *

He sat in the archive room for a long time. The bulb buzzed. The laundry finished its cycle and went quiet, and in the quiet he could hear the building itself — the old Saltmere settling its bones, pipes ticking, the sea somewhere beyond the walls doing whatever the sea did at this hour, which was, he thought, whatever it liked.

He made a list, because lists were how he kept from drowning. It was a short list of people who could have done it.

Someone with access to the desk and the log. Someone who could sit at the night porter's station for three nights running without being questioned — or who could take the ledger away and return it. Someone who knew his handwriting well enough to copy it, which meant someone who had seen a great deal of it. Someone who knew the boathouse, the padlock, the fog, the flats. Someone who had a reason to protect him — no. He crossed that out. Not to protect *him*. To protect the alibi that *he* would provide. To make sure that when the police came asking, the night man's testimony would hold water, because the night man's testimony was the wall the whole thing would be built against.

Someone who needed a witness more than they needed a friend.

His phone buzzed against the table. He looked at it without surprise, the way you look at weather you've been expecting. A message from Callum.

*Making progress on the paperwork? Would like the March 2019 logs first, if convenient. — RC*

Tamsin looked at the message for a long moment. Then he typed: *Found them. Reviewing now. You'll have them by end of day.*

He did not send it. He held his thumb over the button, and he thought about what he was holding in his hands — not a ledger, not anymore. A loaded thing. The only object in the world that proved his alibi had been manufactured, which meant the only object in the world that proved his testimony had been *arranged*, which meant the only object in the world that might, if he was very lucky and very careful, buy him a sliver of the truth about who had spent three nights in March writing him into a story he never agreed to be in.

Or it could bury him. A forged log in the night porter's own archive, in the night porter's own handwriting, discovered by the night porter the week the case reopened — Callum would look at that and see exactly what a man under pressure would want the police to see. The hunter was already watching him. He'd felt it in the interview room, in the small inconsistency Callum had caught and pocketed like a coin.

If he handed it over now, raw, he'd be handing Callum a reason to arrest him.

If he didn't hand it over at all, he'd be obstructing an inquiry for the second time in five years, and this time there would be no grey light to hide in.

The bulb flickered. Somewhere above him, the hotel was waking — the first clatter of the kitchen, the lift groaning up its shaft, a guest's suitcase wheels on the parquet two floors up, the thousand small sounds Tamsin had spent five years listening to and being invisible inside. His refuge. His stone in the boot.

He sent the message. *Found them. Reviewing now. You'll have them by end of day.* Then he put the phone face-down, and he did what he should have done the moment the bulb came on.

He checked the box for signs of disturbance.

The dust on the lid: broken, all right, but broken the way dust breaks when a box is pulled forward and pushed back — recently, and more than once. The dust on the shelf behind it: smooth, untouched. He lifted the box out again and ran his finger along the shelf and found, pressed into the grey felt of dust at the back, the clean rectangle where something else had sat until recently. Something ledger-sized. Something that had been taken out — days ago, judging by the faint bloom of new dust already forming at the edges — and not put back.

He crouched there on the cold archive floor with his finger in the dust, and the stone in his boot turned over one more time and lay still, and he understood the last thing there was to understand, or the first thing, depending on how you counted.

Whoever had written him into that night had come back for the ledger before he did. Had stood in this same room, under this same tired bulb, and known he would come looking. Had left him one month of March and taken the rest.

The question wasn't who had built his alibi. He was beginning to have his suspicions about that, and they had a familiar face, a charming smile, and a reason to be in the country again.

The question was what the missing ledgers said — and whether they said it in his handwriting too.

He photographed the empty space in the dust, closed the box, and turned off the bulb, and stood a moment in the dark listening to the sea, which was, as always, keeping its own records.
