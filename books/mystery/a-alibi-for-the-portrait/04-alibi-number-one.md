## 4. Alibi Number One

Dane Colter lived in a house that looked like it had been ironed.

Sable noticed that first—the way the hedges stood at attention along the walkway, the way the porch swing hung perfectly level on its chains, the way the American flag by the door had been folded with such military precision it might have been made of paper. Everything about 14 Larkspur Lane said: *nothing here has ever been out of place, and nothing ever will be.*

She thought of her own apartment in the city, where three mugs of cold coffee and a nest of case files competed for space with Theo's abandoned headphones, and felt a small, defiant warmth. Mess was honest. Mess admitted life happened to you.

The man who opened the door was seventy-something and built like a dock piling gone soft at the edges—still broad, still deliberate, but settled now, like furniture that had stopped being moved around. He wore a flannel shirt buttoned to the throat despite the mild October air, and his handshake was firm enough to be a warning.

"Miss Delacroix." His voice was exactly what she'd expected from the old news footage: gravel poured over patience. "Heard your show. You've got a good voice for radio. My wife listens."

"Thank you for seeing me, Sheriff."

"Retired sheriff," he corrected, stepping back to let her in. "Thirty years retired. But thank you anyway. People forget there's a difference."

*People forget,* Sable thought, following him into a living room where every surface gleamed, *is usually the first thing they say when they're managing a conversation.*

She'd done her homework on the drive up from the city—four hours of coastal highway with June's research notes spread across the passenger seat like a second navigator. Dane Colter had been thirty-four years old, four years on the force, when Vivienne Marsh walked out of her studio at Gullwrack Landing on the night of October 9th, 1994, and never came back. He'd led the search himself. He'd interviewed everyone. And he'd had an alibi so clean it practically squeaked: he'd been at his daughter Lena's birthday party, twelve witnesses, cake in photographs, the whole domestic tableau.

The alibi had never been questioned. Not once. Not by the state investigators who'd reviewed the file, not by the two journalists who'd written features on the anniversary, not by the armchair sleuths of the internet's first true-crime forums. *Sheriff Colter was at his daughter's birthday party.* Case closed before it was open.

Which was, of course, precisely why Sable had driven four hours to sit in his ironed house and ask him questions.

"Can I get you coffee? Odette's blend, if you know the boarding house. Best on the coast."

"I'd love some," Sable said, though she'd already had two cups and her hands were doing a fine tremor she'd have to edit out of any video. "And actually—if it's all right—I'd like to record. For the episode."

Something moved behind his eyes. It was very fast, very small, and very well controlled—a curtain twitching shut in a house where curtains didn't twitch. If she hadn't spent five years watching people lie for a living, she'd have missed it entirely.

"Course," he said easily. "Nothing to hide. Never was."

*Never was.* She filed the phrase away. Innocent people said *I didn't do it.* Careful people said *there's nothing to hide.* Only rehearsed people reached for the past tense of their own innocence.

* * *

They sat across from each other in matching armchairs, his recorder running on the table between them like a small black bird neither of them wanted to startle. Sable had learned long ago that the best interviews weren't interrogations—they were invitations. You didn't ask people what they did. You asked them what they remembered, and then you listened to the places where memory and record disagreed.

"Tell me about that night," she said. "October ninth, 1994. Where were you when you heard Vivienne Marsh was missing?"

"Lena's birthday," he said, without a beat of hesitation. "My daughter turned eight that day. We had the party at the fire hall—community thing, half the town came. Cake, pinata, the works. I didn't hear about Vivienne until near midnight, when Deputy Ruiz called me. By then she'd been gone—" he counted it off on his fingers, an old habit, a man who'd said this many times, "—call it six hours."

"Six hours is a long time in a missing person case."

"It is," he agreed. "But nobody knew she was missing until her neighbor saw the studio dark and got worried. Small town. We're not quick to intrude."

"You went straight to the studio?"

"Went straight to the studio." He nodded toward a shelf of framed photographs—Colter in uniform at various ages, Colter shaking hands with governors, Colter receiving a plaque. "Her easel was set up. Paintbrushes still wet, if you can believe it. A cup of tea on the sill, half drunk. Boat gone from her mooring. Looked like she'd stepped out for air and the air kept her."

"That's poetic, Sheriff."

"My wife says I'm not poetic." The ghost of a smile. "I say things plain."

"Plainly, then: wet paint, warm tea, missing boat. What did that tell you?"

He considered her for a moment, and Sable had the distinct sensation of being measured—not as a threat, exactly, but as weather. A front coming in off the water that a man who'd lived on the coast his whole life knew how to read.

"Told me she meant to come back," he said. "That's what it told me. People don't take their boat out mid-tea unless they're coming right back or never coming back at all. And everything else said coming back."

"And yet she didn't."

"And yet she didn't." He spread his hands, palms up—an open gesture, a man showing he held nothing. "We searched nine days. Divers, dogs, helicopters when the weather let us. Found her boat washed up on the north spit, no damage worth mentioning. No body. No note. Nothing."

"The official theory was drowning?"

"Official theory was accident. Woman goes out alone at night, October water, current off the spit—it happens. Tragic, but it happens." He said it smoothly, the way a man recites a prayer he's said so often the words have worn smooth as river stones. "Her family didn't buy it. Her patron didn't buy it. Half the town didn't buy it. But buying a theory and proving another one are different animals, Miss Delacroix, and we never caught the other animal."

Sable sipped her coffee. Let the silence stretch. Silence was a crowbar; most people couldn't leave it alone.

Dane Colter could. He sat in his silence like a man in his own living room, which—he reminded himself silently—he was.

So she changed tactics.

"Tell me about Aldous Wren."

The change was almost invisible. Almost. His shoulders didn't move, his face didn't move—but something in the room's temperature shifted, the way a house cools when a door opens somewhere far away.

"What about him?"

"He was her patron. He funded the studio, bought most of her work, had her under contract. That's a lot of control over one artist."

"Aldous Wren was a generous man who liked art," Colter said. "This town ran on Wren money for forty years. The library, the clinic, the seawall after the '89 storm. You want to ask about Aldous Wren, ask what he built, not what you read in some magazine."

"I haven't read anything in a magazine," Sable said mildly. "I've read the police file. Your file. And I noticed something interesting, Sheriff—you interviewed Aldous Wren twice in the week after Vivienne disappeared. Both interviews are logged. Neither interview has notes attached."

For the first time, Dane Colter looked away from her. He looked at the window, at the ironed hedges, at the flag standing at attention outside.

"Paperwork gets lost," he said. "Thirty years, paper gets lost."

"Both times?"

"Both times." He turned back, and now his face was fully composed again, the curtain drawn, the house sealed. "Look. I'll tell you what I told every reporter who ever sat in that chair. Aldous Wren had an alibi too—that night, dinner party at the estate, twenty guests, catered. Rich man's alibi, maybe, but alibis don't care about tax brackets. And Vivienne Marsh wasn't murdered, because there's no body, and she wasn't kidnapped, because there was never a ransom, and she wasn't hiding, because a woman doesn't hide for thirty years without spending money, and she never spent a cent. Occam's razor, Miss Delacroix. The sea took her. The sea takes everybody eventually."

It was a good speech. It was, Sable thought, a *great* speech—and that was the problem. She'd listened to hundreds of hours of this man on tape, depositions, press conferences, anniversary interviews, and the speech had never once varied. Not the phrasing. Not the order. *Occam's razor. The sea takes everybody eventually.* Thirty years of grief and scrutiny, and the man had a script.

Grief improvised. Scripts were written.

"Your daughter's birthday," she said, gently now, watching his hands. "That must have been hard—her party interrupted like that."

"Ruiz called at midnight. Party was long over." A pause, half a heartbeat too long. "Kids were asleep."

"Mm." She smiled and closed her notebook. "One more thing, and then I'll let you get back to your Saturday. The night Vivienne disappeared—did anyone check the ferry log?"

The question landed in the room like a dropped glass.

It was a small thing, a throwaway thing, a thing she'd pulled from June's auction records only that morning: the Gullwrack ferry, the little foot-passenger boat that crossed the bay to Marrow Bay twice a day, run for decades by a quiet man named Emile—Odette's brother, though Sable hadn't known that yet when she wrote the question down. The ferry logs from October 1994 were referenced in the file. Referenced. Never produced. *Log unavailable, water damage,* someone had written, in handwriting Sable now recognized as Colter's own.

"Ferry log," Colter repeated.

"Vivienne's boat washed up on the north spit. But the north spit faces the crossing. If she rowed partway and transferred—or if someone met her out there—" Sable shrugged, keeping it light, keeping it hypothetical. "Seems like the kind of thing a thorough investigator would look at."

"We looked at everything," Colter said. His voice was still level. His hands, folded in his lap, had gone very still—stiller than relaxed, stiller than calm. Still as a man holding a door shut from the inside. "Everything there was to look at."

"Of course." She stood, offered her hand. "Thank you, Sheriff. You've been very generous."

He walked her to the door. On the porch, in the flat silver light of the coast, he paused with his hand on the frame and said, quietly, almost kindly:

"You seem like a smart girl. Smart girls find stories everywhere. But some stories are just sad, and digging in them only makes mud." He looked out at the bay, at the gray line where water met sky. "Vivienne Marsh is dead. Whoever loved her should be allowed to grieve. Whoever killed her—" and here, for just a moment, something flickered across his face that wasn't guilt exactly, but was its close cousin, something tired and old and heavy, "—if there even was such a person—is probably dead too. Let it rest."

"I'll consider it," Sable said, which was what she always said, and which never once meant what it sounded like.

* * *

She didn't start the car right away. She sat in the driver's seat with her recorder in her lap and played the interview back, headphones on, eyes closed, listening the way June had taught her—not to the words, but underneath them.

There. At minute nineteen, when she'd said *ferry log*: a sound so faint she'd missed it live. A creak. The creak of an armchair, leather or old wood, bearing sudden weight—as if a man had leaned forward, or gripped the arms, or both.

And at minute eleven, when she'd mentioned Aldous Wren's unannotated interviews: a breath, drawn in through the nose, slow and controlled, the breath of a man counting to ten.

And threaded through all of it, the speech itself, word-perfect, thirty years polished—and one deviation. One single deviation, at the very end, when he'd said *whoever loved her should be allowed to grieve.*

Not *whoever mourned her.* Not *her family.* *Whoever loved her.*

Present tense. As if, somewhere in Dane Colter's ironed house and ordered heart, someone was still alive to love her.

Sable sat with that for a long moment while the gulls argued overhead and the bay glittered its flat silver glitter, and the thrill that had been singing in her blood since the twin envelopes arrived—the hunter's high, the scent-of-the-trail hum—curdled slowly into something colder.

Because here was the thing she'd been circling since she'd read the file on the highway: Dane Colter's alibi wasn't just clean. It was *load-bearing*. Take it away and the whole structure wobbled—the timeline, the theory, the tidy tragedy of a woman taken by the sea. An alibi that solid, that unexamined, that *rehearsed*, wasn't the absence of suspicion.

It was architecture. Somebody had built it. Somebody had paid for it, or begged for it, or bled for it—and somebody else had agreed to stand inside it for thirty years and never once let the walls show a crack.

And Colter wasn't frightened of her. That was the strangest part, the part she kept turning over like a stone. A guilty man cornered by a podcaster gets defensive, hostile, lawyered-up. Dane Colter had been none of those things. He'd been *sad*. He'd looked at her on that porch with something close to pity and told her to let it rest, the way you'd tell a child not to poke a sleeping dog—not because the dog would hurt the child, but because waking it would hurt everyone.

As if he wasn't protecting himself.

As if he was protecting *everyone*.

Sable started the car. On the seat beside her, June's notes fluttered, and she smoothed them flat with one hand, her eye catching again on the line she'd starred that morning: *Ferry operator, Gullwrack–Marrow Bay crossing, 1978–2003: E. Fontaine.*

Fontaine. The same name as the woman who ran the boarding house in Marrow Bay, the woman whose coffee Colter served so casually, the woman Sable was due to meet tomorrow.

Small towns, she thought, pulling onto the coast road. Small towns keep their secrets the way the sea keeps its drowned—

close, and warm, and shared.

She drove toward Marrow Bay, and behind her, in a house where nothing was ever out of place, Dane Colter watched her taillights disappear down the coast road for a long time before he went inside and picked up the phone.
