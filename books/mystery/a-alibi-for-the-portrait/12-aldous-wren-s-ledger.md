## 12. Aldous Wren's Ledger

The Wren estate archives smelled the way old money smells when it starts to rot: cedar polish laid over damp, over mildew, over something sweeter and less forgivable underneath.

"You're sure we're allowed in here?" Theo whispered, though he'd already crossed the threshold, already had his phone flashlight out, already looked like a man who'd been sneaking into places since birth.

"Public records annex," I said. "Technically."

"Technically is my favorite kind of allowed."

We had driven forty minutes inland from Marrow Bay to Ashcombe, where the Wren family had kept its seat for four generations—a gray stone pile called Harrowgate that the county had seized in pieces over the decades until all that remained of Aldous Wren's empire was a shuttered house, a trust fund that had evaporated somewhere around Julian's third divorce, and a records annex the historical society maintained out of a mixture of civic duty and morbid fascination.

The archivist on duty was a small woman named Mrs. Pell, approximately one hundred and ten years old, with reading glasses on a chain and the unblinking patience of someone who had watched a thousand researchers arrive certain they'd find something and leave with nothing.

"Aldous Wren," she repeated, when I gave her the name. She said it the way you'd say the name of a stain you'd given up trying to scrub out. "Financial papers are in the east room. Boxes fourteen through thirty-one. Mind the dust. It's older than both of us put together, and it bites."

* * *

The east room was long and low-ceilinged, lit by windows the color of weak tea. Boxes lined metal shelving in no particular order I could discern—Mrs. Pell's filing system appeared to be *wherever they fit*. Theo took the left wall; I took the right; and for two hours the only sounds were paper rustling, Theo muttering inventory lists under his breath like a man praying in an unusual faith, and once, distantly, Mrs. Pell sneezing with the force of a small artillery piece.

It was box twenty-six that changed everything, though I didn't know it yet. Box twenty-six held household accounts from 1993 to 1996—groceries, gardeners, the electric bill for a house that must have glowed like a lighthouse from the outside. Aldous Wren had been the kind of man who recorded everything. Every tin of sardines. Every tip to the ferryman.

Every payment to Sheriff Dane Colter.

I found the first one on a Tuesday in March 1994, three weeks before Vivienne Marsh disappeared. *D.C.—retainer—$2,000.* Then again in April. Again in May. The week after Vivienne vanished, the entry read simply: *D.C.—concluded—$15,000.*

I sat very still on a crate labeled MISC. GARDEN and let my heart do something complicated against my ribs.

"Theo," I said. My voice came out strange, flattened, like sound underwater.

He was beside me in four steps. He read over my shoulder, and I felt him go quiet in the particular way he went quiet—not calm, but coiled. Theo's silences had gears in them.

"That's not an alibi," he said finally. "That's a payroll."

"And look." I turned pages with fingers that wanted to shake and didn't quite dare. "*Dr. Halloway, coroner—consultation fee.* Dated the day after the search was called off. And this—" My throat closed around it. "*Marrow Bay Maternity Home—care of resident—monthly.*"

"Maternity home," Theo repeated.

"Marrow Bay Maternity Home." I knew the building. Everyone who grew up in Marrow Bay knew the building—the big white house on Cypress Lane that had been a private clinic, then a boarding school, then condos. I'd ridden my bike past it a thousand times. Some of the foster kids used to say it was haunted. Foster kids say everything is haunted; it saves time explaining why we flinch.

Theo pulled his laptop out and started photographing pages, methodical as a surgeon. I kept reading.

The maternity entries began in January 1994 and ran through October. Monthly payments, $800 each, always in cash-notation, always initialed *A.W.* In September there was a lump sum: *final care, delivery, placement—$5,000.*

Delivery. Placement.

I did the arithmetic without meaning to, the way you do arithmetic you've spent your whole life refusing to do. My birthday was June 12th. Nine months before June 12th was September.

September 1994.

The page didn't say my name. It didn't have to. The floor of the room tilted—not literally, Mrs. Pell would have objected to structural damage—but some load-bearing wall inside me quietly gave way, and everything I'd built my life on slid a few degrees sideways.

"Sable." Theo's hand was on my shoulder. When had he crouched down next to me? "Sable, breathe. You've gone the color of the boxes."

"I'm fine."

"You're the opposite of fine. You're doing the thing where you narrate yourself as fine so hard that reality gets embarrassed and leaves the room."

Despite everything—despite the ledger open on my knees like a confession written in a dead man's handwriting—I laughed. Just a little. Theo could always do that to me, even at the bottom of a well. Especially at the bottom of a well.

"It's me," I said. "Theo. The last entry. Nine months before—" I couldn't say *my birth* out loud yet. Saying it made it furniture in the world instead of a splinter behind my eyes. "Vivienne hid a pregnant girl in her studio. June told us that. A pregnant teenager. And Aldous Wren was paying a maternity home in Marrow Bay the same year, the same town, and the payments stop—" I pressed my palm flat against the page, as if I could hold the numbers still. "He didn't just buy the sheriff. He bought the whole story. Maybe including mine."

Theo sat back on his heels. For a long moment he didn't say anything, which from Theo was practically a eulogy.

"Okay," he said at last, gently, the way you talk to someone standing on ice. "Okay. We don't know anything yet. This is a ledger, not a birth certificate. Rich men paid for lots of things. Half of them were probably terrible, but terrible isn't specific."

"He initialed it, Theo. *A.W.* Final care, delivery, placement. That's not charity. That's logistics."

"Then we get specific. We document, we verify, we cross-reference. That's the job. That's what we're good at." He paused. "And Sable? Whatever this turns out to be—you don't have to be the podcaster right now. You can just be a person finding out something enormous. Both things are allowed. Simultaneously, even. I checked."

I loved him for that. I loved both of them—him and June—for having assembled themselves around me years ago without either of us knowing why, like iron filings arranging themselves around a magnet nobody could see.

"Keep photographing," I said. "Everything. The sheriff entries, the coroner, the maternity home. All of it."

"What are you going to do?"

I looked down at the ledger. Aldous Wren's handwriting was beautiful—of course it was; men like him always had beautiful handwriting, calligraphy over cruelty—and his initials marched down the margin like little soldiers standing guard over everything he'd purchased.

"I'm going to finish reading every page," I said, "because if he wrote down sardines, he wrote down everything else too. And then I'm going home."

"Home-home? Marrow Bay?"

"No." I closed the ledger carefully, the way you close a door on a sleeping animal. "June. I need June."

* * *

We worked until the tea-colored light went amber and then went out altogether, and Mrs. Pell came in with a lamp and an expression suggesting we had perhaps five minutes before she turned into a pumpkin and took the archives with her.

But those hours gave us the rest of it. The coroner, Dr. Halloway, had received four payments across eighteen months—enough to sign off on whatever verdict the case required, which had been, officially, *presumed drowned, remains unrecovered.* The harbor master got $500 the week after the disappearance, presumably for remembering things incorrectly. Even the editor of the *Marrow Bay Courier* had a line item, modest and damning: *advertising consideration—$1,200 quarterly,* running from the spring of 1994 straight through 1997, tapering off only when the story finally died of neglect.

Aldous Wren hadn't covered up a crime. He'd subscribed to silence the way other men subscribed to newspapers—regularly, reliably, and with his initials in the margin.

Near the end of the box, tucked loose between two bound volumes, Theo found a single folded sheet that wasn't accounting at all. It was a note, undated, in that same elegant hand:

*The studio matter is resolved. The girl has been placed. V. will not return—she understands what remains for her here if she does. Burn this.*

He hadn't burned it. Men who record their sardines do not burn their sins; sins are the only inventory they truly value.

*The girl has been placed.*

I read it eleven times. Theo read it twice and then deliberately stopped looking at me, which was its own kind of kindness—he was giving me a room to fall apart in without witnesses.

Because here was the shape of it now, unavoidable, drawn in a dead man's ink: Vivienne Marsh had hidden a pregnant teenager in her studio. Aldous Wren had found out. He had removed the girl—*placed* her, the way you place a piece of furniture or a bet—and he had made sure Vivienne understood the price of objecting. Three weeks later, Vivienne Marsh rowed out into the dark and ceased, officially, to exist.

And the girl. The placed girl. Delivered in September, in a maternity home ten miles from the foster system that eventually handed a baby named Sable to whoever was holding out their arms that particular month.

My origin and the crime weren't parallel stories. They were the same story. I had been walking around inside the case file my entire life, wearing it, breathing it, and I'd only ever thought I chose it.

"Hey." Theo's voice, soft. "Whatever you're deciding right now—decide it slowly."

"I'm not deciding anything. I'm just—" I folded the note along its original creases, precisely, because precision was the only thing holding me together at the seams. "Thirty years ago somebody decided everything for me. Where I'd live. What my name would be. Whether I existed loudly or quietly. I'm done being decided about. But I'm also—" My voice cracked, and I let it, because Theo had earned honesty and I owed him at least that much. "I'm scared, Theo. Not of the story. Of the answer."

He nodded slowly. "Then here's what I know. June's known you nine years. She digitized half the state's auction records for you at two in the morning without being asked. Whatever the answer is, it doesn't retroactively change what she is to you. People aren't math, Sable. They don't work backward."

"People are exactly math," I said, wiping my face with the heel of my hand. "That's the problem. Somebody did this equation in 1994 and I'm the remainder."

"Then we carry the one." He shouldered his bag. "Come on. Mrs. Pell is giving us the lamp-stare, and I, for one, fear her."

* * *

Mrs. Pell walked us out through the darkened hall of Harrowgate herself, lamp held high, keys jingling at her waist like a tiny alarm system. At the door she stopped and looked at me for a long moment over the top of her glasses.

"You found what you came for," she said. It wasn't a question.

"Some of it."

"Mm." She locked the door behind us with the ceremony of a woman closing a crypt. "I catalogued these papers myself, you know. Thirty years ago, when the estate was settled. I read every page." Her eyes, pale and old and terribly clear, rested on mine. "There was a young researcher came through here maybe fifteen years back. A girl. Asked sharp questions about the Marsh woman. I told her what I tell everyone: the truth in this county is like the water table. Everybody drinks from it. Nobody wants to know what's upstream."

She turned and shuffled back toward the door.

"Miss Halloran," she added, without turning around—she'd read my name off the visitor log, of course; women like Mrs. Pell read everything—"the box you want next is twenty-seven. Household staff. If you're the kind who doesn't sleep anyway."

Then she was gone, and the great door shut, and Theo and I stood in the parking lot under a sky going bruise-colored at the edges.

"Box twenty-seven," Theo said.

"Tomorrow."

"Tonight?"

"Tonight I drive to June's." I unlocked the car and stood there with my hand on the roof, the cold metal biting my palm, grounding me. "Before I read one more word written by that man, I need to ask her one question. Just one. And I need to ask it looking at her face."

Theo got in without arguing, which I understood to mean he already knew the question.

Everyone was going to know the question soon enough. The only variable—the only thing left in the whole wide case file that I couldn't photograph, cross-reference, or archive—was whether the answer would break something I couldn't rebuild.

I drove west, toward the coast, toward the lights of Marrow Bay coming on one by one across the water, and the ledger rode in the bag between us like a passenger who knew exactly where we were going.
