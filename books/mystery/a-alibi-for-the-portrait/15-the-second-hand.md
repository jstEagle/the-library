## 15. The Second Hand

Lena Colter had been following people for eleven years, and in all that time she had learned exactly three things worth passing on: never park where you'd have to explain yourself, always carry cash for parking meters because digital trails are tattletales, and if a job starts to feel wrong, it's already been wrong for weeks.

The job watching Sable Delacroix had felt wrong since roughly the second month.

She sat now in her sedan on the cliff road above Marrow Bay, engine off, thermos of terrible coffee balanced on her knee, and watched the lights of the Saltgull Boarding House glow through the fog. Third night in a row. The woman she'd been hired to watch was inside that house, and Lena was beginning to suspect the woman knew it.

That was the part that bothered her. Not the danger—she'd been shot at twice in her career, once by a man whose wife she'd been photographing and once by an actual raccoon situation gone sideways in Duluth. No, what bothered her was the *competence*. Sable Delacroix moved like someone who had grown up being watched and had decided, somewhere along the way, to be very good at noticing.

Lena checked her phone. The monthly deposit had landed that morning, same as it had for nine years—$2,400 from a shell account called Meridian Holdings, no name attached, no instructions beyond the original letter that had started it all:

*Watch the girl from the studio. Report anything unusual. Never make contact.*

Nine years of reports filed to a post office box. Nine years of "subject appears healthy," "subject has taken new apartment," "subject cried at a bus stop advertisement for dog food, cause unknown." She'd written that one at 2 a.m. and hated herself for how tender it sounded, but there it was. You couldn't watch a person for nearly a decade without developing opinions about them. Without, God help her, developing something like affection.

And now the reports were going nowhere useful, the money kept arriving anyway, and the girl from the studio had come home to Marrow Bay asking questions about Vivienne Marsh—the case Lena's father had spent thirty years pretending not to dream about.

Her father. There was the other thing. The thing sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone.

Dane Colter had taught her to drive on these exact roads. He'd taught her to shoot, to read tire tracks, to sit still for four hours without checking her phone. He'd also taught her, without ever saying it aloud, that some questions in Marrow Bay were like poking a sleeping dog that belonged to someone bigger than you. When she'd told him, three weeks ago over meatloaf, that she had a contract involving the Marsh case, his fork had stopped halfway to his mouth for exactly two seconds before continuing its journey as though nothing happened.

Two seconds. Her father could lie to grand juries with a steadier hand than that.

So when Theo Okafor—the producer with the gentle voice and the eyes of a man who catalogued everything—had approached her that afternoon outside the boarding house, all polite concern and one devastating sentence ("You've been parked on Harbor Street four times this week; we thought you might be lonely"), Lena had made the rookie mistake of hesitating half a beat too long.

Which is why she was now walking into a trap of her own design, or rather, out of one.

* * *

The plan, such as it was, had been Theo's, and it offended Lena professionally even as she admired its simplicity.

"They'll follow you if you lead them somewhere quiet," he'd said, sitting across from her at the diner booth with his hands folded like a man about to discuss a church raffle rather than stage a stakeout reversal. "Somewhere with one road in. June knows a place."

"The old oyster sheds past Gull Point," June had said. She was a small woman with reading glasses on a chain and the particular calm of someone who had organized forty years of county records and was not impressed by chaos. "Nobody goes out there since the harvest collapsed. One road. Fog comes in off the water around eight."

"And then what?" Lena had asked. "You confront me? I'm armed, I'm licensed, and I'm genuinely not the person you should be worried about."

"We don't want your gun," Theo said. "We want your invoice."

June had looked at her over those glasses. "Someone has been paying you for nine years to watch her. We'd like to know who."

And that—that was the thing, wasn't it. So would Lena.

So here she was, leading her own surveillance by driving conspicuously slowly out Gull Point Road with her high beams flicking twice every quarter mile, which was either a signal to any second tail that she'd been made, or just a nervous habit, depending on how honest she wanted to be with herself.

The fog came in right on schedule, thick as wool. The oyster sheds rose out of it gray and skeletal, their tin roofs singing softly under the mist. She parked, killed the engine, and got out, leaving her keys in the ignition—a professional courtesy, a signal that she wasn't planning a quick escape—and walked toward the largest shed, where a single lantern glowed behind fogged glass.

Inside, the smell was brine and old wood and, unexpectedly, coffee. Real coffee. Someone had brought a thermos that didn't taste like punishment.

Sable Delacroix stood by a workbench with her arms crossed. Up close, away from telephoto lenses, she was younger than her voice suggested and more tired. There was a bruise-colored shadow under each eye, and she held herself the way people do when they've stopped sleeping properly but haven't admitted it yet.

"Ms. Colter," Sable said.

"You know my name."

"I know your license plate, your insurance company, and that you take your coffee with an amount of cream that should legally be considered a latte." A pause. "Also that you cried at the bus stop ad. The dog food one."

Lena felt heat climb her neck. "That report was—" *Tender*, she almost said. "Internal."

"Nine years," Sable said, and her voice cracked slightly on the number, which Lena hadn't expected. She'd prepared for anger. Anger she could handle. This was worse. This was a woman who had found out she'd been haunted by kindness and didn't know what to do with it. "Nine years, somebody paid to have me watched, and I find out about it from a PI who's been sitting outside my window writing little reports about my emotional state."

"For what it's worth, most of them said you seemed okay."

"Most of them."

"The dog food one was an outlier."

Something flickered across Sable's face—not quite a laugh, but the ghost of one, circling. Behind her, near the lantern, June sat on an upturned crate with a notebook, and Theo leaned against the doorframe doing his best impression of a man who hadn't rigged the entire evening.

"Sit down, Ms. Colter," June said gently. "The fog's going to hold for an hour, and we have questions, and you, I think, have answers you've been wanting to give somebody for a long time."

* * *

Here is what Lena gave them, in the end, because the alternative was carrying it alone another year:

She gave them the original letter. She'd kept it, of course—she kept everything, it was the family disease—slipped under her office door eight years ago, wait, no, nine, typed on a machine that typed slightly crooked, instructing her to watch "the girl from the studio" and file monthly reports to the Meridian box. No signature. Payment arranged through a lawyer who claimed, plausibly and uselessly, to represent a client he'd never met.

She gave them the timeline. The payments began nine years ago—which meant whoever hired her had started watching Sable long before Sable ever mentioned Vivienne Marsh on air. Before the podcast existed. Before the letters.

"That's the part that matters," Theo said quietly, and Lena watched him understand it in real time, the way he seemed to understand everything a half-second before everyone else. "Whoever it is wasn't reacting to your investigation. They were protecting you from something. Or protecting something from you."

"Or both," June murmured.

Lena gave them the one detail she'd never put in a report, because it had felt too strange, too small: the first payment had arrived the same week her father retired. She'd noticed the coincidence, rolled it around for years, never let it become a thought. Tonight, saying it aloud in a cold oyster shed while the fog pressed against the windows, she finally let it.

"My dad was sheriff when Vivienne Marsh disappeared," she said. "He had an alibi nobody ever questioned. And nine years ago, the week he retires, somebody starts paying me to keep an eye on a woman I've never heard of, described only as 'the girl from the studio.'" She laughed, and it came out wrong, brittle. "I used to tell myself it was coincidence. I'm very good at telling myself things. It's basically the family business."

Sable had gone very still. "Lena. Did you know? All this time—did you know what your father did?"

And here was the question Lena had driven out here to avoid, and it caught her square in the chest anyway.

"No," she said. "I don't know. That's the truth, and I hate it. I know he lied to me about *something*—two seconds of frozen fork doesn't lie. But I don't know if he helped bury a body or helped bury a secret or just spent thirty years being scared of Aldous Wren like everybody else in this town." She wiped her face with the heel of her hand, furious at herself for the wetness. "I took this meeting because I want to know. Even if it breaks something. *Especially* if it breaks something. I'm so tired of keeping the town's secrets. I didn't sign up for that. I signed up to find cheating husbands and lost dogs."

"The lost dogs must be nice," Theo offered, and the gentleness of it, the sheer unguarded warmth, undid her a little. She nodded and couldn't speak for a moment.

"They're the best part of the job," she managed finally. "Everybody cries when you find the dog. Happy crying. There's not enough happy crying in this line of work."

* * *

They talked until the lantern burned low. Lena told them about the shell company, the crooked-typed letter, the lawyer named Pell who wore cologne like a warning label. She told them the deposits had continued even after Sable arrived in Marrow Bay—which meant whoever was paying either didn't know Sable had come home, or knew perfectly well and wanted eyes on her anyway.

"Or," June said, setting down her pen with great care, "the payments aren't from the person who hired her originally. Accounts can change hands."

Theo straightened off the doorframe. "You think someone took over the arrangement."

"I think," June said, "that a person who fakes her death and spends thirty years watching over a child would want to know the child was safe. And I think a person clever enough to paint an alibi in oils might be clever enough to fund one too."

The silence that followed had a different texture than the earlier ones. Hopeful. Terrified. Both at once, braided tight.

Sable spoke slowly, testing each word. "You're saying my stalker might be Vivienne Marsh."

"I'm saying," June replied, "that not everything that follows you is hunting you. Some things are herding you. Toward the truth. Toward the island. Toward whatever they need you to find." She looked at Lena. "Your reports. The ones from the last few months. Have the instructions changed?"

Lena blinked. She pulled out her phone, scrolled back through months of correspondence with the Meridian box—she communicated via mailed letters, mostly, though lately a second phone had appeared in the arrangement, prepaid, untraceable, with texts that arrived like weather.

And there it was. Three weeks ago, right around the time Sable's third letter had arrived, the tone had shifted. The old instructions—*observe, report, never contact*—had been joined by something new, sent in that same slightly crooked typeface reproduced as images:

*When she asks about the ferry, make sure she finds the answer.*

"When she asks about the ferry," Lena read aloud, feeling the words land in the room like stones dropped into still water. "I don't—I haven't done anything with that. I didn't know what it meant."

But Sable had gone white. "Emile," she whispered. "Odette's brother. The ferryman. He confessed—he confessed to killing Vivienne, but it didn't fit, none of it fit—" She pressed both hands flat against the workbench, steadying herself against a world tilting. "Someone wants me to look at Emile. Someone wants me to pull that thread."

"Or wants you to pull it in the right direction," Theo said. "There's a difference between pointing at a thread and pointing away from the rest of the sweater."

Outside, the fog had begun to thin, the way it does all at once, like a curtain deciding. Through the shed's broken window, the water below Gull Point showed black and patient, and far out—so far it might have been a star fallen into the sea—a single light blinked where no light should be, out past the shipping lane, in the direction of Gullwrack Island.

Lena saw it. So did Sable. Neither of them said anything, but something passed between them anyway—an understanding, fragile and provisional, between the watcher and the watched.

"So," Sable said at last, turning to Lena with an expression that was equal parts exhaustion and decision. "You've spent nine years being paid to protect me. How would you feel about doing it for free?"

Lena laughed—really laughed, for the first time in weeks, the sound surprising them both.

"Honey," she said, "I've been doing it for free for months. The checks were just gas money." She looked out at the distant blinking light, and the stone in her chest shifted into something almost like purpose. "Now. Somebody want to tell me why there's a light on an island that's supposed to be empty? Because I've got a boat guy, a thermos, and a sudden burning need to know who's been signing my paychecks."

June closed her notebook with a soft, decisive snap.

"Then tomorrow," she said, "we go to Gullwrack."
