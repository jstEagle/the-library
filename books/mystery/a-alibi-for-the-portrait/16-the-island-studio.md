## 16. The Island Studio

The ferry to Gullwrack Island ran twice a day, and only if the weather agreed.

Sable learned this from a hand-lettered sign at the Marrow Bay dock, where the morning fog was peeling off the water in long gray ribbons like the sea deciding what it wanted to keep. The ferryman—a broad-shouldered woman named Petra with forearms like dock rope—looked Sable up and down when she asked for a ticket.

"Gullwrack?" Petra said. "Nobody goes to Gullwrack."

"That's usually why I go places."

Petra almost smiled. Almost. "Round trip's fourteen dollars. But I'll tell you now, if the wind comes around northeast, I'm not waiting for stragglers. You miss the boat, you're sleeping with the gulls."

"I've slept worse places," Sable said, which was true, though she didn't elaborate. There was a whole episode of her podcast about that particular foster home, and she wasn't going to pitch it to a stranger on a dock at seven in the morning.

She paid, boarded, and sat on the stern bench where she could watch Marrow Bay shrink behind her. The town looked different from the water—smaller, softer, the way wounds do once they've scabbed over. Miss Odette's boarding house stood pale against the hillside, its porch swing hanging motionless. Last night Odette had pressed a thermos of coffee into her hands and said, "Gullwrack, is it," in a voice that wasn't a question, and then had packed her a lunch as if Sable were twelve years old and headed on a field trip. Two egg salad sandwiches, an apple, and a note that read only: *Come back before dark, child.*

It should have felt patronizing. Instead it had made Sable's throat tight for reasons she couldn't afford to examine before crossing open water.

* * *

The crossing took forty minutes. Lena's trail had taken three weeks.

It had been Theo who cracked it, actually—because of course it was Theo, who could find a paper trail the way bloodhounds found lost toddlers, with patience and slobbering enthusiasm. The monthly payments keeping watch on "the girl from the studio" hadn't come from any account connected to Dane Colter or Aldous Wren's estate. They'd come from a trust: the Marsh Charitable Remainder Trust, established 1995, one year after Vivienne Marsh walked into the bay and never came out.

"A dead woman funding a private investigator," Theo had said, spinning his chair in wide, delighted circles. "Do you understand what this means? Either we're being haunted by the most organized ghost in coastal history, or—"

"Or she never died," June had finished quietly, from her corner of the office, not looking up from the auction records she'd been annotating for a month. June, who grew up in Marrow Bay. June, who knew things she'd been rationing out like wartime sugar.

The trust had gone dormant six years ago—the payments stopped, the account drained down to nothing but paperwork. But dormant accounts leave shadows. A final disbursement, Theo found, went to a marine supply company in Marlowe Harbor: paint, primer, two rolls of tar paper, a woodstove flue kit. Delivered to the Gullwrack dock.

Somebody had been building something on an island nobody visited.

"You don't have to go alone," Theo had said last night over the phone, his voice doing the thing it did when he was worried and trying to sound like he wasn't. "I can be on a train by morning. June too. We'll make it a whole thing. Crew outing. Island adventure."

"And leave the outlet sniffing around the office unattended? They're already calling sources, Theo." Sable had looked out the boarding house window at the black water. "Besides, if there's really someone living out there, showing up with a film crew is how you get a door slammed in your face. Or worse. This has to be just me. One nervous woman with a notebook. Nobody's ever been threatened by that."

"Speak for yourself. You're terrifying before coffee."

She'd laughed, and then neither of them had said anything for a moment, and then Theo had said, "Check in every hour. Not because I think you can't handle it. Because I need to know you're okay, and I've stopped pretending that's a professional requirement."

That was the thing about the family you built yourself, Sable thought as Gullwrack rose green and gray out of the fog. They didn't ask permission to love you. They just did it, inconveniently, at all hours.

* * *

Gullwrack was smaller than it looked from the water—a humped shoulder of granite and spruce, maybe two miles end to end, with a ruined lighthouse at the northern tip and a scatter of old fishing cottages along the southern cove. Petra dropped her at a rotted wooden dock, handed her a laminated card reading EMERGENCY: VHF CHANNEL 16, and said, "Sunset's 7:42. I mean it about the wind."

Then the boat was gone, and Sable was alone on an island with the loudest silence she'd ever heard.

It wasn't truly silent, of course. It was full of sound—gulls arguing, surf worrying the rocks below the cliff path, wind moving through spruce with a sound like the ocean breathing through a pillow. But no engines, no voices, no hum of anything human. She stood on the dock for a full minute just letting it press against her ears.

Then she started walking.

The cottages along the cove were mostly ruins—roofless, windowless, their doorways grown through with bayberry. She checked them anyway, half expecting to find nothing and half dreading it. In the third one, a gull had nested inside an old cast-iron stove and regarded her with the flat outrage of a homeowner. "Sorry," she told it. "Wrong house."

But the fourth cottage—

The fourth cottage was different.

It sat apart from the others, tucked into a fold of the hillside where the spruce crowded close, as if the trees themselves had been asked to keep a secret. From the path you'd walk right past it; the roofline was low and the walls were weathered gray, same as every other ruin. But the door was new. That was the first thing. A fresh cedar door, still honey-colored, fitted into a frame that had been rebuilt within the last few years. And someone had painted it—not white, not gray, but a deep blue-green, the exact color of the sea in the photograph of Vivienne Marsh's last portrait. The color Sable had stared at so many times she saw it when she closed her eyes.

Her heart started doing something complicated.

"Sable Delacroix," she said out loud, to steady herself. "You are a professional. You have covered cults, cold cases, and one extremely litigious psychic. You can knock on a door."

She knocked.

Nothing. Wind. A gull somewhere, laughing at her.

She tried the handle. Locked. Of course locked. She stepped back and looked at the cottage properly, journalist-brain engaging even as some younger, hungrier part of her brain whispered *look look look*. Smoke stain above a vent pipe—recent, the black soft-edged, not years old. A rain barrel positioned under the eaves, full. And the garden.

The garden was the thing that undid her.

Behind the cottage, in a clearing hacked out of the spruce, someone had planted rows. Beans, peas, something leafy she couldn't name, all of it thriving, all of it tended—staked, weeded, mulched with seaweed the way coastal people did. You didn't keep a garden like that by visiting. You kept it by staying. By choosing, season after season, to stay.

And beside the garden, leaning against the cottage wall under a sheet of plastic, were canvases.

Six of them, facing the wall. Sable stood there a long time, her hand hovering, feeling like a burglar and a pilgrim at once. Then she lifted the edge of the plastic and turned the top canvas around.

It was the cove. Her cove, the one she'd just walked through—but painted the way she'd never seen it, the light caught mid-morning, the water holding the sky the way a cupped hand holds water. And in the lower corner, small and unmistakable, a monogram: *VM*.

Vivienne Marsh had signed paintings thirty years after drowning.

Sable sat down right there on the wet grass, because her legs had opinions of their own, and put her face in her hands, and laughed—one shaky, disbelieving sound that came out closer to a sob than she'd intended.

"She's alive," she said to the beans. To the gulls. To nobody. "Oh my God. She's actually alive."

* * *

She gave herself five minutes. She was, after all, a professional.

Then she got up and circled the cottage again, slower now, cataloguing. The woodpile: split, stacked, dry—enough to last a winter. The vent pipe: yes, definitely recent smoke. A clothesline between two spruces held a shirt, a pair of trousers, a man's wool sweater gone soft with age—and, pinned at the end, a woman's cardigan, faded blue, mended at both elbows with careful, uneven stitches. Mended by hand. Mended many times.

Someone lived here. Someone lived here *now*—the smoke, the garden, the laundry. Someone had been here within days.

But the door was locked, and the island was empty, and the question rearranged itself in Sable's chest from *is she alive* to *where is she*, and the second question had teeth. Because the payment trail had gone dead six years ago. Because the trust had been drained. Because whoever funded Lena Colter's surveillance of Sable herself had money and patience and a reason to watch a podcaster dig into a thirty-year-old drowning—and if that person was Vivienne Marsh, then Vivienne had known Sable was coming long before Sable knew it herself.

The sketch. The third letter, hand-delivered: a drawing of Sable asleep, done recently, done well. She'd assumed a threat. Sitting on the cold grass of Gullwrack, she let herself consider, for the first time, that it might have been something else entirely. Something more like a mother watching a child sleep. Something more like thirty years of guarding.

"Okay," she said aloud. Her voice sounded strange in all that quiet. "You waited here. You watched me my whole life. And now I'm standing in your garden eating Miss Odette's egg salad sandwich, and you're—" She gestured at the empty island. "Where?"

She ate one sandwich. It was excellent. Odette would be insufferable about it.

* * *

She searched the rest of the afternoon, methodically, the way June had taught her—grid patterns, photograph everything, touch nothing you can't put back. The other cottages yielded rusted pots, a child's shoe fossilized in salt, and one enormous spider she declined to evict. The lighthouse at the north point was a hollow shell of granite, its lamp room open to the sky, its spiral stair climbable if you didn't mind trusting ninety-year-old iron.

On the third landing, wedged behind a fallen section of railing, she found a tin box.

Inside: a coil of good rope. A storm lantern, oiled and functional. And a bundle of letters tied with string, addressed in a slanted, confident hand to *E.* at the Marrow Bay post office.

Sable's breath went thin. Emile. Odette's brother. The ferryman who confessed to a murder he didn't commit, who had spent thirty years carrying someone else's secret like a stone in his chest.

She shouldn't read them. She knew she shouldn't read them. She was a journalist; these were private letters; there were rules.

She read one.

*E.—The easel arrived, thank you, you impossible man. Tell Odette the winter squash was beyond generous and I will repay her in jam. Tell her also that I know she was the one who sent the quilt, whatever she claims about "finding it washed ashore." Nothing washes ashore folded in tissue paper. —V.*

Dated March, four years ago.

Sable closed her eyes. The picture assembled itself without her permission: Odette packing lunches and lying about quilts. Emile rowing supplies across the bay for decades, confessing to murder to bury the truth deeper. June pulling auction records with steady hands and knowing eyes. Theo finding the trust. Lena Colter taking money for years to watch a foster kid grow up safe—or to report back that she wasn't safe, Sable supposed, depending on how you read it. An entire constellation of people, scattered across thirty years, all holding their small pieces of one woman's survival, none of them ever telling each other, all of them somehow, impossibly, ending up in orbit around Sable Delacroix and her microphone.

*Her own found family of collaborators, each holding a fragment of the old alibi.* She'd written that line in her notes weeks ago as a hypothesis. Standing in the lighthouse with a dead woman's mail in her hands, it stopped being a hypothesis and became a fact with weight and warmth and terrifying implications.

They hadn't assembled around the case. The case had assembled around *her*.

"Oh," she said softly, to the empty lamp room, to the wheeling gulls, to whoever might be listening from wherever they watched. "Oh, you absolute—"

The light shifted.

Not dramatically. Just the sun sliding behind a cloud, the way it does. But Sable turned toward the south cove anyway, some animal instinct older than journalism, and saw—at the very edge of visibility, where the treeline met the shadow of the hill—a figure.

Small. Still. Watching.

For one heartbeat neither of them moved. The distance was too far to see a face, but Sable didn't need a face. She knew the set of those shoulders from a hundred photographs. She knew the way this woman stood from the portrait that wore her own likeness—head tilted slightly, weight on one hip, the posture of someone who had spent a lifetime looking closely at things and being looked at in return.

The figure raised one hand. Not a wave. Just—an acknowledgment. *I see you. I've always seen you.*

Then the cloud passed, the light changed, and the figure stepped back into the trees and was gone.

Sable stood on the lighthouse stairs with the letter bundle clutched to her chest, shaking, laughing, crying, all three at once and none of them winning.

"Wait," she called, uselessly, across the whole green island. "Wait—I have questions. I have *so many questions*—"

The island declined to answer. But the smoke stain on the cottage vent pipe answered for it. The mended cardigan answered for it. The garden, staked and weeded and patient as a promise, answered for it.

She wasn't running. She was waiting. Thirty years of waiting, and now the waiting was nearly over, and tomorrow—tomorrow Sable would walk up that hill and knock until the blue-green door opened, and everything would change, and she had exactly one night to figure out whether she wanted it to.

She looked down at the letters in her hands, then out at the darkening water, where the ferry would come at 7:42 sharp or not at all.

Miss Odette had said *come back before dark, child.*

Sable had a sudden, vertiginous suspicion that everyone in this story had been saying that to her, in one way or another, her entire life.

She went down the stairs carefully, holding the rail, holding the letters, holding—as best she could—her whole trembling, reassembling heart.
