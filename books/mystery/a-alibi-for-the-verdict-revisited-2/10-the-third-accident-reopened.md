## 10. The Third Accident, Reopened

The rain had not stopped since Tuesday. It came down the Grand Meridian's atrium in grey ropes, striking the glass roof with a sound like applause from a very distant, very bored audience, and by Thursday morning the whole hotel smelled of wet stone and old brass—the smell, Beatrix Oyelaran had always thought, of secrets kept too long in a damp cellar.

She stood at her desk behind the concierge station with the third file open before her, and she did not touch it for a long while.

The third accident. Everyone at the Meridian knew the shape of it the way they knew the shape of the staircase—without ever having to look. Nine years ago, Herr Dietrich Falk, the hotel's chief engineer, had fallen from the maintenance gantry above the boiler room on the night of the fortieth anniversary dinner. A wet rung. A missing rail. A man who had complained twice that month about the state of the gantry and been promised, both times, that repairs were scheduled. The coroner had called it industrial negligence and let it go at that; the hotel had paid a settlement to a daughter in Hamburg who signed the papers without ever crossing the Channel to see where her father died.

Beatrix had been twenty-six then, three years into her apprenticeship under Edmund Voss, and she remembered the night with the terrible clarity of things witnessed young. She remembered carrying Falk's coffee up to the gantry level herself because he'd asked—*a kindness, Fräulein Oyelaran, just this once*—and she remembered setting it down on the workbench beside the ladder, and she remembered, most of all, that when she came back down, Edmund Voss had been standing at the foot of the stairs with his hands folded behind his back, watching the dark above the boiler room with an expression she had taken, at the time, for grief.

Now she took it for something else.

The file was thin. That was the first thing wrong with it. Hotel deaths generated paper the way storms generated lightning—incident reports, witness statements, insurance correspondence, photographs—and yet the folder the night manager had given her, after a hesitation she chose not to interpret aloud, contained eleven pages. Eleven pages for a man falling four storeys onto a concrete floor.

She began, as she always began, with what did not fit.

* * *

The first anomaly announced itself quietly, the way anomalies do when you have spent two decades learning their voices. The incident report was timed 11:47 p.m. The anniversary dinner that year had ended at midnight—she knew because she had stood at the ballroom doors until the last guest left, counting coats, and the count had finished at five past twelve. Which meant the report describing Falk's fall had been written *before* the fall could have been discovered, unless someone had found him within thirteen minutes of his death and said nothing until morning.

She turned the page. The discovery time was listed as 7:20 a.m., logged by a porter named Anselm Krebs, long since retired to a village near Salzburg. So the report had been backdated—or forward-dated—or written by someone who believed the fall would happen at quarter to midnight and wanted the paperwork ready.

People who expected accidents wrote reports in advance. People who expected accidents also, occasionally, arranged them.

The second anomaly was the coffee.

She had carried it up herself. Two cups—one for Falk, one she'd drunk herself in the corridor outside, because the night had been long and the ballroom air had been thick with champagne and she was twenty-six and tired. She remembered the taste of it still: burnt, over-strong, the way the night kitchen made it after ten. What she did not remember—what she had never once thought to question in nine years—was whether anyone else had gone up those stairs between her descent and Falk's fall.

The statement from the night porter mentioned a figure. One line, buried in the middle of Krebs's rambling account: *Saw Mr. Voss come through the service corridor around half eleven, said he was checking the boiler pressure ahead of tomorrow.* And then nothing. No follow-up. No one had asked Voss what he had found at the boiler, or why the pressure log for that night—the log Falk himself maintained, religiously, every four hours—showed a gap between 10:00 p.m. and 2:00 a.m. Four hours. The exact window of a man's death.

Beatrix closed the file and sat very still, listening to the rain.

There was a discipline to this work that Edmund Voss had taught her himself, in the early years, leaning over her shoulder at this same desk: *Never build the cathedral first, Beatrix. Lay one stone. Then ask if the ground will bear another.* She laid the stones now, one by one, in the notebook she kept locked in the concierge drawer—a notebook whose existence he did not know about, or so she told herself, knowing even as she told it that she could no longer be sure of anything he knew.

Stone one: the report predated the discovery. Stone two: the pressure log gap. Stone three: Voss in the corridor at half eleven, unexplained, unasked-after. Stone four—

Stone four arrived at noon, wearing a raincoat and smelling of train smoke.

* * *

Anselm Krebs had a voice like gravel poured slowly, and a cottage near Salzburg with geraniums in the windows and a view of mountains that had watched him grow old without comment. He answered the telephone on the second ring, and when Beatrix identified herself—the Grand Meridian, the concierge's desk—he made a sound that might have been pleasure or might have been indigestion.

"Herr Krebs. Forgive the intrusion. I'm reviewing some older files, and your name comes up in the Falk matter."

A silence. Long enough that she checked the connection.

"The Falk matter," he repeated. "Nine years, and now it's a matter again."

"You remember it well."

"I remember everything," Krebs said, and there was something underneath the words, something that sounded almost like relief, like a man setting down a sack he had carried so long his shoulders had grown around its shape. "That's the trouble with porters, Fräulein. Nobody thinks we see anything. We're furniture. But furniture hears."

"What do you remember?"

Another silence, but a different one—deliberate, gathering itself.

"I remember I wrote in my statement that I saw Mr. Voss in the service corridor at half eleven. And I remember that when the statement came back to me to sign, that line was gone."

The rain filled the pause on her end. On his, a clock ticked somewhere, patient as a metronome.

"Gone," she said.

"Struck out. Neatly. Someone had retyped the page—I could tell, the ribbon was fresher, the letters sharper. Everything else word for word, except that one line. And a space where it had been, if you held the page to the light." He coughed, a dry, terminal sound. "I signed it. I want you to know that. I signed it because Mr. Voss brought it to me himself, and he stood in my little room by the loading dock while I read it, and he smiled at me, and he said, 'You've had a long night, Anselm. Memory plays tricks after midnight.' And I looked at his smile and I understood that my memory was going to play tricks whether it wanted to or not."

Beatrix wrote the date, the hour, the words. Her hand was steady. She noted, distantly, that this surprised her.

"Why tell me now?" she asked. "After nine years?"

"Because of the letters," Krebs said.

The clock ticked. Somewhere in the cottage a kettle began its low climb toward whistling.

"What letters?"

"There's a girl comes round. Young one, used to be chamber staff here—before your time, maybe during. She's been asking after the old ones. Me, and the second cook, and poor Frau Lindqvist who died in the spring. Asking what we remember about the anniversaries. About the dinners." His voice dropped, though there was no one in the cottage but himself and the geraniums. "And she said—I didn't believe her, Fräulein, but she said—that the man who wrote the confession last week, the one in all the papers, couldn't have done it. Because the man who wrote the confession was with *her* that night. All night. And she's finally ready to say so, if someone asks her properly."

Beatrix felt the floor of the investigation shift beneath her, the way the Meridian's marble sometimes seemed to shift when the boilers surged below.

"Her name," Beatrix said.

"She'll give it herself. She said to say—" Krebs paused, and when he resumed, his voice had changed, gone careful, the way men's voices go when they repeat something they don't fully understand. "She said to say: *the auditor keeps the books, but the maid kept the keys.*"

The line went quiet except for the kettle, which had begun, at last, to sing.

* * *

She walked back into the Grand Meridian at four o'clock, wet to the knees and thinking so hard that she took the service stairs instead of the lift without noticing, which was how she came to be passing the night auditor's office at an hour when Miriam Sattler should have been asleep.

The door was open a hand's width. Lamplight lay across the corridor floor in a yellow wedge, and inside, at the small desk beneath the single bulb, Miriam sat with her back to the door, hunched over something that was not a ledger.

Beatrix had learned, in the weeks since the letters, to read Miriam Sattler the way she read weather. The woman was a study in deliberate dullness—grey cardigan, grey hair pinned without ambition, spectacles that caught the light and gave nothing back. She had worked nights at the Meridian for eleven years, invisible as plumbing, accurate as gravity. Guests forgot her faces within minutes of checking out. But tonight, bent over the desk, her shoulders had none of the accountant's calm. They were drawn up around her ears like a woman expecting a blow.

On the desk before her lay a photograph, old and curling at the corners. And beside the photograph, a page torn from a ledger, its edges browned, a scorch mark eating one corner like a bite taken by fire.

Beatrix knocked.

Miriam moved with a speed that would have shamed a woman half her age—the photograph vanished into a drawer, the scorched page folded once, twice, and pressed flat beneath a ledger as the door swung wide. By the time Beatrix stepped into the lamplight, Miriam was the picture of tedium, pen in hand, columns before her.

"Fräulein Oyelaran." Not a question. Miriam never asked questions; she stated facts and waited to see what you did with them. "You're dripping on my floor."

"I've been telephoning Salzburg."

"Long distance." Miriam adjusted her spectacles. "The Falk file."

It was not a guess. Beatrix let the silence stretch, watching the older woman watch her, and understood that they had arrived at some border neither had named. Eleven years of nodding in corridors. Eleven years of *good evening* and *cold tonight* and nothing else—and now here they stood on either side of a desk with a burned page under a ledger and nine years of dead men between them.

"Miriam," Beatrix said carefully, "how long have you worked at the Meridian?"

"Eleven years as auditor." The pen tapped once against the blotter. "Before that, I travelled. Before that—" the tap stopped— "I was chamber staff. Another hotel. Another city. It's all in my file, if files interest you. They seem to be interesting you a great deal lately."

"A porter in Salzburg sends me regards."

Something crossed Miriam's face then, quick as a fish under ice—there and gone, unreadable, but *felt*, the way you feel a draft from a door opened somewhere far off in a great house. "Krebs," she said. "He was always kind to the maids. Gave us his cigarettes at Christmas. Never lit them, mind—just liked being the sort of man who gives."

"He remembers a line struck from his statement. He remembers who brought the statement for signing."

"Yes," Miriam said. "So do I."

The lamp hummed. Down the corridor, the building settled around its pipes, breathing the slow breath of old hotels.

"Then you'll understand," Beatrix said, "why I have to ask what happened to Dietrich Falk."

Miriam rose. It was a slow business, joints protesting, and she came around the desk and closed the office door with a click that sounded, in the small room, like a lock engaging. Then she lifted the ledger away and unfolded the scorched page and laid it flat between them, and Beatrix saw that it was a pressure log—Falk's own hand in the entries, round and confident—and that the final entry, dated the night of the fortieth anniversary, read:

*11:30 — Pressure nominal. Gantry rail STILL NOT REPAIRED. Third request. If it isn't fixed by Friday I go to the owners myself.*

Below it, in different ink, different hand, smaller and neater: *Received and acknowledged. — E.V.*

"He went to the owners himself," Miriam said quietly. "Or he meant to. There were papers, Fräulein. Papers about the anniversary fund, about money that walked out of the hotel's accounts dressed as renovations. Falk kept the boiler logs and the maintenance invoices, and maintenance invoices are very honest documents—they say what was bought and what was never bought at all. He'd found the gap. He was going to close it." She touched the scorch mark with one fingertip, gently, the way one touches a scar. "This page was in his coat pocket when they found him. Someone tried to burn it in the boiler room and it didn't take—not all of it. The rest of the batch did. This survived because it was folded, and because whoever gathered the ashes missed the pocket."

"And no one ever—"

"No one ever looked. The coroner got a clean coat. The family got a cheque. And Edmund Voss got—" she paused, choosing with visible care— "got to keep his ledgers balanced."

Beatrix stared at the neat initials. *E.V.* She had seen that hand ten thousand times—on guest correspondence, on staff rosters, on the note that had come up to her desk three weeks ago promising a fourth death. She had known the man's handwriting longer than she had known her own signature's worth.

"The confession," she said. "The man who confessed to the first death. You know he didn't do it."

"I know where he was," Miriam said. "And I know why he confessed anyway, and that's a sadder story than yours. But yes. He didn't do it. None of them did it—the porter who fell, the cook who drank, the engineer who dropped. Accidents, all three. Beautifully staged, impeccably documented, and every one of them signed off by a head concierge whose hands have never once, in thirty years, been dirty." She straightened, and for a moment the dullness fell away entirely, and what looked out from behind the spectacles was fierce and exhausted and terribly awake. "You're reopening the third accident, Fräulein. Good. But understand what you're doing. Falk wasn't killed because of what he knew. He was killed because of what he was going to *say*. And there's a difference, and the difference has a schedule attached. Your letters promise a fourth death. Ask yourself: what does the fourth person know that's worth killing for *now*, nine years later, on the fiftieth anniversary?"

Beatrix thought of the second letter, the promise, the elegant threat. She thought of the anniversary dinner six days away, the toast, the four hundred guests.

"I don't know yet," she said.

"Well," said Miriam Sattler, folding the scorched page along its old creases and holding it out to her, "when you find out, you may want to move quickly. Because whoever wrote that letter has already chosen. And in my experience—" she pushed her spectacles up, and her hand, for the first time, was not quite steady— "in my experience, the Meridian's anniversaries keep their appointments."

Beatrix took the page. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

At the door she paused, one hand on the frame, and asked the question she had been carrying since Salzburg, since the kettle sang, since *the maid kept the keys*.

"Miriam. The photograph on your desk. Who was in it?"

The lamp hummed. The pipes breathed. And the night auditor of the Grand Meridian Hotel looked at her for a long, long moment with the expression of a woman deciding, at last, which side of a wall she wished to live on.

"Come back tomorrow night," she said. "Bring the Falk file. And Fräulein—" the spectacles flashed, giving nothing back— "don't tell Edmund where you're spending your evenings. Not yet. Because the fourth name on his list, if I've read these nine years correctly—"

She stopped. Outside, the rain redoubled against the high glass, drumming like fingers, like footsteps, like a countdown.

"—is mine."
