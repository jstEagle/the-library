## 15. Voss's Clean Hands

The rain had settled into that grey, patient drizzle that made the Grand Meridian's windows look like frosted glass, and Beatrix Oyelaran stood behind her desk watching Inspector Voss cross the lobby as though he were walking through a photograph of himself.

He moved the way he always moved: unhurried, immaculate, his coat brushed free of weather before it could think about clinging to him. His umbrella was furled and dry despite the downpour outside, which meant either he had been dropped at the awning by a car whose driver had waited with an umbrella of his own, or he had walked from somewhere close enough to matter. Both possibilities told her something. Everything about Voss told her something. That was the trouble. He was a man composed entirely of signals, and none of them ever quite resolved into a message.

"Madame Oyelaran." He inclined his head at the desk, precise as a metronome. "A moment, if you can spare it."

"For you, Inspector, I can spare several."

"I imagine you can." A thin smile, there and gone like a fish under ice. "You've become very good at sparing things lately. Time. Suspicion. Judgment."

She led him to the alcove off the lobby where the hotel kept its small library — leather chairs gone soft at the elbows, a globe with countries on it that no longer existed. It was the room where she conducted conversations she did not want overheard by the front desk, which meant, these days, nearly all of them.

Voss sat without being asked, crossing one leg over the other, and placed his hands flat on his knees. She noticed his hands because she had been trained to notice hands; they were clean in a way that went past grooming. No ink under the nails, no callus from pen or pistol, no tremor. Hands that had never done anything. Or hands so well kept that whatever they had done had left no receipt.

"You received my note," he said.

"I did."

"And yet when I arrived this morning, I found your night auditor had already pulled the guest registers for the week of Ashcombe's death. Before my request was even logged." He let that sit between them like a stone set carefully on a table. "Curious timing."

"Miriam is thorough."

"Miriam," he repeated, tasting the name. "Yes. The auditor. Twenty-two years in the building, isn't that right? And no one remembers hiring her."

"People forget things, Inspector. It's why hotels keep ledgers."

"It's why *I* keep files." He unfolded himself, reached into his breast pocket, and produced not a notebook but a single sheet of paper, folded once, which he laid on the low table between them without opening it. "I'm going to tell you a story, Madame Oyelaran, and then I'm going to leave that paper, and you're going to decide what kind of person you are. I find people are usually one of two kinds. The kind who want the truth, and the kind who want their truth. You strike me as the first kind. It's why I've never suspected you."

"How generous."

"It isn't generosity. It's arithmetic." He leaned back. "Three deaths in this hotel across twenty years, all ruled accident, all touched by the same small circle of staff. Ashcombe. The valet, Pell. The sous-chef, Renard. And now two letters arrive on one morning — one confessing to a murder you personally proved was a murder, and one promising a fourth death before the anniversary toast. You've spent three weeks pulling threads. So have I. The difference is that my hands are clean, and yours—" he glanced, almost gently, at the folded paper, "—yours are about to get very dirty indeed."

Beatrix kept her face still. Behind her ribs, her heart performed its work with the discipline of long practice, but her mind was running ahead of him, laying out the map of everything she knew and everything she suspected and asking where Voss fit. He had been the junior inspector on the Ashcombe case twenty years ago. He had signed off on the verdict of accidental death that she had spent six months dismantling. He had reappeared three weeks ago, the day after the confession letter arrived, with the smoothness of a man returning to a stage he'd never really left.

"The story," she said. "You promised me a story."

"So I did." He looked at the rain on the window. "Twenty years ago there was a chambermaid here named Elena Vasquez. Good worker, quiet, ambitious in the way quiet people are ambitious — sideways, patiently. She saw something she shouldn't have. Something involving a man who mattered to this hotel, and a sum of money that did not belong to him, and a woman who died that same winter in a room on the fourth floor. Elena wrote it all down. Names, dates, amounts. She intended to sell it, or trade it, or simply hold it over the man's head — I've never been able to determine which, and it hardly matters. What matters is that the man found out. And what matters more is that Elena didn't die. She ran. And the ledger she wrote — the physical object — vanished."

"And the man?"

"The man is still alive. Still respected. Still beloved, if you can believe it." Voss turned his gaze from the window to her, and for the first time she saw something underneath the polish — not warmth, exactly, but a kind of banked urgency, a fire burning in a stove built to conceal it. "I was twenty-six years old, Madame Oyelaran. Junior man on the Ashcombe file. My superior told me the case was closed and handed me the report to initial, and I initialed it, because I was twenty-six and he was thirty years my senior and the hotel's owner had dined with the chief constable the week before. I have spent twenty years being ashamed of those initials. Do you understand? I am not here to obstruct you. I am the only man in this city who has been trying to finish what you started, longer than you've known it needed finishing."

It was a good speech. Beatrix had heard good speeches before; she had given some herself, behind this very desk, to guests who needed soothing and to liars who needed cornering. A good speech had architecture. It anticipated objections. It gave you just enough of itself to make you want the rest.

"What was the name," she said quietly, "of the man who mattered to the hotel?"

Voss smiled again, and this time it stayed a moment longer. "You know I can't give you that for free. Not yet. First, the paper."

* * *

She waited until he had gone before she opened it.

She took it up to her room on the third floor, locked the door, drew the curtain against the grey afternoon, and unfolded the single sheet under the lamp. It was a photocopy — Voss struck her as a man who never surrendered an original — grainy, slightly skewed, as though photographed in haste on a surface not entirely flat.

It was a page from a personnel ledger. Her own handwriting looked back at her.

*Oyelaran, B. — concierge trainee, sponsored by H. Delacroix, head concierge. Recommended without reservation.*

Dated nineteen years ago. Beneath it, in a second hand she knew almost as well as her own — the small, upright, unhurried script that had corrected her first attempts at the guest book, that had written *well done* on her first successful arrangement of a difficult anniversary — a second line:

*Confirmed. She sees everything. Keep her close.*

Henri Delacroix. Her mentor. The man whose office she had searched, whose ledger she had found hidden in the walls, whose twenty years of kindness she had spent three weeks learning to read as camouflage. Seeing his handwriting was no shock. She had braced herself for that particular wound weeks ago, and the brace held.

What shook her was the sentence beneath it. *Keep her close.*

Because there were two ways to read it, and both of them were true, and only one of them was survivable. Either Henri had written it as a predator writes — marking her early, folding her into the circle of protected witnesses, keeping the girl who *saw everything* where he could watch her — or he had written it as a mentor, honestly, proudly, and Voss had taken a page out of context and turned a compliment into a noose.

That was the thing about clean hands. They got that way by touching nothing. By letting other people carry the papers, sign the reports, pull the triggers, make the mistakes. Voss had initialed a false verdict at twenty-six and had spent twenty years making sure that everyone else's guilt was more legible than his own. And now he had come to her with a story about a runaway chambermaid and a vanished ledger — a story that matched, detail for detail, what she had already dug out of the walls of Henri's office — and offered it as though it were a gift.

Unless it wasn't a gift. Unless it was a test, or bait, or a mirror angled to show her what he wanted her to see.

She sat for a long time with the photocopy in her lap while the light failed and the rain kept its grey patience against the glass. Then she did what she always did with information that frightened her: she arranged it.

Fact: Voss knew about Elena Vasquez. Fact: so did she, since chapter and verse of the same story lived in Henri's hidden ledger. Fact: Voss had requested the guest registers for the week of Ashcombe's death — and Miriam had pulled them first. Fact: Miriam, the night auditor, the woman no one remembered hiring, who worked alone in a basement office among the ledgers of twenty years, who flinched — Beatrix had seen it, a flicker like a struck match — whenever the fourth floor was mentioned.

Elena Vasquez had run. Elena Vasquez had hidden. Where does a woman hide best? In plain sight, in the one place her hunter would never look twice — inside the machine itself, wearing a uniform, doing invisible work. A chambermaid becomes a night auditor. The woman who recorded rooms becomes the woman who records the records. Twenty-two years in the building, and no one remembers hiring her, because the person who hired her had arranged it so that no one would need to remember.

And Voss had come within a day of requesting documents that would put Miriam's name in front of the constabulary. Documents that would show a chambermaid's name adjacent to an auditor's signature, twenty years apart, in the same hand.

He hadn't requested them. He had waited. He had told Beatrix about the registers instead, casually, like a man mentioning weather — and watched whether she would warn her.

Which meant Voss knew. Knew who Miriam was, or at least suspected. And had chosen, this morning, to say nothing to her directly — to bring the story to Beatrix instead, with a poisoned page attached, and watch which way she jumped.

Her hands were clean, he'd said. She looked down at the photocopy, at her own young handwriting, at Henri's *keep her close*, and understood that Voss's cleanliness was the dirtiest thing in the building. Clean hands were hands that had never had to choose. Hers had chosen, were choosing now, would go on choosing until the toast was raised or the fourth glass poured.

There was a knock at her door. Soft. Twice.

She knew the knock. Everyone in the hotel knocked differently — porters knocked with authority, guests with apology, management with entitlement. This knock was the knock of someone accustomed to working at hours when knocking loudly could be fatal to sleep, or to secrets.

She opened the door. The corridor lamp threw a long yellow stripe across the floorboards, and standing just outside the stripe, half in shadow, was Miriam — the night auditor, off shift, out of the basement, holding a small tin box against her chest with both hands as though it were warm and alive and might bolt.

"I saw him leave," Miriam said. Her voice was low and roughened, a voice used to speaking to machines rather than people. "Voss. I watched him from the service stair. He came to give you something, and now you're standing there with it in your hand, and I need to know, Madame Oyelaran — I need to know right now — whether you're going to do what he wants, or what's right. Because they aren't the same thing. They haven't been the same thing for twenty years."

Beatrix stepped back from the doorway, and the corridor light followed the other woman in, and for the first time Beatrix saw her fully — not the grey uniform, not the bent posture of a woman practicing invisibility, but the face beneath, older than its years and wide awake, the face of someone who had been holding her breath for two decades.

"Come in," Beatrix said, and closed the door on the corridor, on the lamp, on the whole listening hotel. "And start from the beginning. The true beginning. I've had enough stories today that don't fit."

Miriam set the tin box on the writing desk. Her hands, when she lifted them away, were shaking — the first unclean thing Beatrix had seen all day, and the most honest.

"It starts," Miriam said, "with a page I burned. And the reason I burned it is the reason Voss will kill us both if we give him the chance."
