## 16. Miriam Comes In From the Dark

The night auditor's office sat at the end of a corridor the guests never saw, past the linen room and the service elevator that groaned like an old man climbing stairs. Beatrix had walked that corridor a thousand times in her twenty years at the Grand Meridian, and a thousand times she had passed the frosted glass door with its brass lettering—M. HALE, NIGHT AUDIT—and a thousand times she had knocked and been admitted and found nothing but a woman with reading glasses and a calculator and a polite, closed face.

Tonight the door was unlocked before she touched it.

"Come in, Miss Oyelaran," said the voice from within. "You've been standing outside my door for eleven seconds. I counted."

Beatrix stepped into a room lit by a single green-shaded lamp, the kind bankers had used a century ago, and by the blue-white glow of a ledger screen. The air smelled of cold coffee and carbon paper and, faintly, of something burned. Miriam Hale sat behind a desk that was less a desk than a fortification—stacks of folios, a typewriter, three decades of the hotel's arithmetic arranged in battlements around her. She had removed her reading glasses. That was the first wrong thing. Miriam Hale without her glasses was like a lighthouse without its lamp.

"You knew I'd come," Beatrix said.

"I knew someone would come eventually. I hoped it would take longer." Miriam gestured to the chair opposite her desk, the one Beatrix had sat in perhaps six times in two decades, always for some small administrative matter—a lost key, a disputed folio, the annual reconciliation of the petty cash. "Sit down, Beatrix. What I have to show you will look better sitting down."

Beatrix sat. The chair was hard. Everything in this room was hard; softness did not survive the night shift.

"You wrote the confession," Beatrix said. "The letter. *I take full responsibility for the death of Gerald Ashcombe.* That was you."

"It was." Miriam said it the way she said everything—flatly, precisely, as though reading a figure from a column. "You'll want to know why. I'll tell you. But first you need to understand that I am not a murderer, and that the confession was true in every particular except the one that matters. I did take full responsibility. I have been taking it for nineteen years. I simply never put it in writing before, because putting it in writing felt like an ending, and I wasn't finished."

"Finished with what?"

Miriam reached beneath the desk. What she lifted out was a shoebox, the kind that had once held good leather boots, its lid soft with age. She set it on the desk between them and did not open it.

"Nineteen years ago," Miriam said, "I was a chambermaid on the fourth floor. You were a girl at the front desk learning the trade from a man everyone in this building would walk into traffic for. And Gerald Ashcombe was a guest in 412 who saw something he should not have seen, and told someone he should not have told, and on the third of November he fell—or was dropped—from the fire stair between the fourth and third floors, and the police called it an accident, and the hotel breathed a great sigh of relief, and everyone went back to sleep."

"Except you."

"Except me." Miriam's hands were steady on the shoebox. Her knuckles, Beatrix noticed, were mapped with old scars, the fine white seams of a life spent handling things—linen, ledgers, perhaps other things. "Because I saw the stairwell too, Beatrix. I saw it before they cleaned it. And I kept a piece of it."

She opened the box.

Inside, on a bed of yellowed tissue paper, lay a page of hotel stationery. It was scorched at one corner, its edges browned as though it had been held too near a flame—or pulled out of one. Across its face, in a hand Beatrix recognized with a lurch she felt in her teeth, ran figures and annotations in pencil. The handwriting was Ambrose Voss's. She had watched that handwriting sign her first employment contract. She had watched it write her a note of congratulations when she made junior concierge, when she made senior, when she was named to the desk in her own right. She knew the way he crossed his sevens the way she knew her own heartbeat.

"What is it?" she asked, though she was already reading.

"A page from a ledger," Miriam said. "The real ledger. Not the one in the accounting office—the one that recorded what actually moved through this hotel in the years when certain guests paid not in money but in silence. Ashcombe found it because he was a curious man and a light sleeper, and because in 1987 the wall safe in 412 had a loose back panel, and behind the loose panel the hotel's previous owner had hidden his true accounts. Ashcombe photographed three pages. He sent one to a journalist. He kept one in his room. And the third—" Miriam touched the scorched corner with one finger, almost tenderly— "he burned, or tried to, in the ashtray by the window, and then thought better of it and folded what was left into his passport, because he was the kind of man who trusted paper more than people."

"The stairwell," Beatrix said. Her voice came out strange, distant, as though someone else were using it. "You said you saw the stairwell. Before they cleaned it."

"I was coming up the service stair with fresh towels. Fourth floor, quarter past eleven at night. I heard voices—two men. I did not see their faces. I heard the sound." Miriam's eyes went to the green lamp, not to Beatrix. It was easier, Beatrix understood, to tell it to the lamp. "You know what a body sounds like when it goes down a stairwell. I had hoped never to know. Then the door at the top opened and I did what any sensible chambermaid would do. I stepped into the linen closet and I held my breath and I watched the shoes. Two pairs of shoes came down the service corridor. One pair was expensive leather, well kept, slightly worn at the outer heel. The other pair was rubber-soled. Service shoes. The kind the hotel issues to its own staff."

Beatrix's mouth had gone dry. "You never told the police."

"I was twenty-two years old and the daughter of an immigrant cleaning woman, and the man whose handwriting is on that page was already the most beloved employee in the building, and the police had decided it was an accident before my towels had cooled." Miriam finally looked at her, and her eyes behind their nakedness were the eyes of someone who had been awake for nineteen years. "I told no one. I took the page—Ashcombe's passport was among his effects, waiting to be returned to his widow, and I lifted the folded page out of it before the passport left the building, and I have kept it for nineteen years, and I have watched, Beatrix. I have watched what happens to people who remember."

"The night auditor," Beatrix said slowly. "The transfer. Everyone thought you'd left. There was a farewell cake in the staff room. There was a card."

"I signed the card myself. I was in the back of the room." A ghost of something that was almost a smile crossed Miriam's face and was gone. "The hotel needed a night auditor with an eye for detail and no family and no life outside these walls. I applied under my mother's maiden name. Hale. It fit me. I have spent nineteen years reconciling the Grand Meridian's accounts at three o'clock in the morning, and every figure that has ever passed through this building has passed across my desk, and I have watched three people die who should not have died, and I have waited for someone on the day staff to be half as observant as they all believe they are."

"The letters," Beatrix said. "The confession came to me. The threat came to me. *A fourth before the toast.* Why me? Why not the police?"

"Because the police are what got us all here." Miriam closed the shoebox. "And because you, Beatrix Oyelaran, are the only person in this hotel who proved that a death was murder and would not let it go. I needed to know what you were made of. So I sent you a confession that didn't fit, and I watched what you did with it."

"You used me as a test."

"I used you as a hope," Miriam said, and for the first time her voice cracked, just slightly, like ice on a pond in early spring. "There is a difference. Though I'll grant it's a difference only the person being used can feel."

* * *

The lamp hummed. Somewhere far above them, in the world where guests slept behind numbered doors, a telephone rang twice and stopped. Beatrix turned the scorched page over in her hands. The pencil figures swam. Room numbers. Dates. Initials she did not recognize and two she did—one of them, in the margin, circled twice, was *A.V.*

"He wasn't just a witness," she said. "Ashcombe. He was blackmailing him. Voss."

"Ashcombe was blackmailing the previous owner. Voss was the owner's protégé even then—did you know that? Everyone thinks Ambrose rose from the concierge desk. He rose from the owner's right hand. He knew where every body was buried because he had helped dig the holes, in the accounting sense if not the literal one." Miriam folded her hands on the shoebox. "When the owner died and Ambrose took over the day-to-day, the ledger was supposed to have been destroyed. It wasn't. It went into the walls—literally, Beatrix, into the walls of the old service corridor behind the fourth-floor linen room, in a lead-lined box, because the old owner was a superstitious man who believed paper could be unburned by enemies and wanted it preserved as insurance against Ambrose. Ambrose has spent two decades looking for it. And every person who has ever come close to finding it, or remembering too loudly about the night Ashcombe fell, has had an accident."

"The gardener," Beatrix said. "Tomas. The fall from the roof. The second one."

"Tomas found a loose section of plaster in that corridor. He told me so himself, the week before he died. He was going to look behind it on his day off." Miriam's voice did not waver, but her hands, on the shoebox, had gone white at the knuckles. "And Elena Vasquez, the archivist, who pulled the 1987 staff rosters last month and noticed that a chambermaid named Miriam Ostrowski resigned the same week a guest named Ashcombe died—Elena made a bargain, I'm told. I don't know the terms. I know she is alive, which is more than Tomas managed, so whatever she traded, it bought something."

"And the third. The third accident." Beatrix set the page down. "You reopened it. In your letter you said the third death was murder too. Who?"

"Mr. Aldous Finch," Miriam said. "The insurance adjuster who came in the spring, asking quiet questions about the Ashcombe settlement, because his firm had been paid off nineteen years ago with a signature that did not match the widow's hand. He stayed four nights. On the last night he told the day porter he'd found what he came for and was leaving on the morning train. He did not leave on the morning train. He was found in the steam room at six a.m., and it was called a heart, because Mr. Finch had a heart condition, and because a heart is the most convenient organ a man can die of."

Beatrix closed her eyes. She saw the board in her own room, the threads and pins, the shape she had been circling for two weeks while telling herself it could not be true, must not be true, that the man who had taught her everything—the man who had taught her that a concierge sees everything and owes the truth to everyone—could not be the author of all this.

"He raised me," she said. It came out smaller than she intended. "He chose me. Out of two hundred applicants. He said I had the eyes for it."

"He chose you," Miriam said, "because you were the best of them. That was never in question. He chose you because the best of them would be the least suspected, and the most loyal, and because a hotel's conscience should ideally be someone who loves him." Miriam stood, and came around the desk, and for a moment she stood over Beatrix with the green lamp behind her, and she looked, suddenly, exactly like what she was—not a night auditor, not a chambermaid, but a witness, the last one, nineteen years in the dark. "He is going to kill again, Beatrix. Before the toast. He told someone he would, and that someone told me, because that someone is frightened enough now to break. The fourth death is scheduled for tomorrow night, in front of two hundred guests, at the anniversary dinner, where Ambrose Voss will stand at the podium with a glass in his hand and twenty years of love in the room, and no one—no one—will see it coming except the two of us."

"Who?" Beatrix stood. Her legs felt borrowed. "Who is the fourth?"

Miriam looked at her for a long moment. The lamp hummed. The service elevator groaned somewhere in the building's bones, climbing toward a floor where it had no business being at this hour.

"That," Miriam said, "is the part I couldn't put in a letter. Because the fourth name is a name that will break this hotel in half. And because I only have it secondhand, from a source that Ambrose is already hunting—and if he finds that source before tomorrow night, we lose everything, including the ledger, including the page in your hand, including any chance of counting his confessions properly."

"The source," Beatrix said. "Where?"

"Where no one ever looks," Miriam said, reaching for her glasses and putting them back on, and with them, somehow, her armor. "In plain sight. In a uniform. Doing a job so boring that the whole building looks straight through it. Beatrix—the fourth name is on tomorrow night's seating chart. And the seat has just been changed."

She turned her ledger screen toward Beatrix. There, in the banquet manifest, one name had been moved that afternoon—moved, the audit log showed, from table nine to the head table, to the chair directly at Ambrose Voss's right hand. A place of honor. A place, Beatrix thought with a cold that started in her spine and spread outward, with a clear view of the podium, and of the toast, and of every glass raised in the room.

The name beside the changed seat was one Beatrix had known her whole career.

And the change order, the log said, had been entered at 4:47 that afternoon—from the head concierge's desk.
