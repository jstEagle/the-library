## 2. Thirty Years of Fares

The ledger smelled like a church basement—damp paper, old glue, the faint ghost of cigarette smoke from a decade when dispatchers were allowed to smoke at their desks. August Pemberton sat in the archive room at 3 a.m., the radio quiet for once, and spread three decades of night logs across a folding table like a hand of cards they'd been dealt all at once.

The pattern was almost insultingly simple once you saw it. Every pickup from the Voss mausoleum road—the gravel spur that dead-ended at the cemetery's iron gate—fell on the same four dates each year: the second Tuesday of March, the last Friday of June, the first Sunday after harvest, and December 26th. Four fares a year, thirty years running. Always between 11:40 p.m. and 12:15 a.m. Always cash, logged as "account settlement." And always signed off with the same looping initials in the margin: *E.M.*

Edmund Marsh. The dispatcher who had vanished in 1998, leaving his coat on the back of August's chair like a man stepping out for coffee.

August ran a finger down the column of addresses where those fares terminated. For the first fifteen years, the drop-off was a P.O. box number that meant nothing. Then, starting in 1994, the logs grew specific: *Lakeshore Rd., blue cottage, north shore.* August knew that stretch of road. Everyone in Meridian Falls knew it as the place where the county's money went to rot—summer houses owned by people who summered elsewhere now.

The blue cottage had burned its porch rails gray and stood shuttered behind a wall of honeysuckle. August had driven past it a thousand times without seeing it, which was, they understood now, precisely the point.

* * *

They went out on their own shift's end, when the sky over the lake was the color of a bruise healing. The taxi company's oldest sedan—a Crown Victoria with a heater that only worked if you begged it—carried them up Lakeshore Road on tires that whispered through frost-silvered grass.

The cottage sat back from the water like something pretending to be asleep. But sleep was the wrong word. August had spent seven years listening to the town breathe through a radio handset, and they knew the difference between an empty house and a waiting one. This house had been *waiting*. The honeysuckle had been cut back recently—clean cuts, within the season. A path was worn through the frost-killed grass to the side door, dark with use.

Inside, dust lay thick on everything except the kitchen table, which was bare and clean, and the armchair by the wood stove, which held the shape of a body the way old cushions do—hollowed at the seat, worn smooth on the arms where hands had gripped and pushed themselves up, ten thousand times, for years.

On the mantel: a photograph of Harlan Voss at forty, the official one from the courthouse, but defaced gently, almost lovingly, with a mustache drawn in ballpoint. Beside it, a stack of paperback westerns with cracked spines. A hearing aid battery packet, half empty. A calendar on the wall with four dates circled each month in the same careful hand that had signed the dispatch logs—and then, three weeks ago, nothing. The circles stopped mid-month, like a sentence cut off before its verb.

In the bedroom, the bed was stripped. In the bathroom, the medicine cabinet held bottles with a pharmacy label August recognized because they filled the company account there: *V. HARLAN*, the name flipped like everything else about him. One bottle was still full. Two were empty. The last refill date was twenty-two days ago.

Twenty-two days. August did the arithmetic standing very still, the way you do when the floor might give. Three weeks ago, someone had walked out of this house carrying a dead founder of Meridian Falls, loaded him into a cab—one of *their* cabs, on one of *their* logs—and driven him back to the mausoleum to put him where the town already believed he was.

The stove ash told the rest. Someone had burned papers here, recently; among the char, August found a corner of heavy cream stationery with an embossed *V* and, legible in the fire's mercy, three words: *...payment will cease...*

Blackmail. Or accounting. With men like Voss, August suspected, the two wore the same suit.

* * *

Back at the depot, the morning crew was arriving, and August made the mistake of looking like someone with questions.

Ruth Calloway got there first. She always did. She came in carrying two coffees and a paper bag of cinnamon rolls the size of fists, and she set them on August's desk like a woman laying down trump cards.

"You look terrible," she said. "Eat something."

"Ruth." August kept their voice level, dispatcher-flat. "Who drove the cemetery runs?"

The depot went quiet in the particular way rooms do when everyone had already agreed on the silence. Danny Okafor, twenty-six years driving, suddenly found the fare meter fascinating. Marisol Vega began wiping down a dashboard that was already clean.

"Company history," Ruth said, settling onto the edge of the desk, close enough that August could smell her perfume—lily of the valley, the same scent she'd worn the night August's mother left, when Ruth had stayed until dawn teaching a grieving teenager the radio codes. "Every outfit has some. Ours is older than most."

"The logs say—"

"The logs are internal documents, sweetheart. Internal means they stay internal." Her voice never rose. That was the thing about Ruth; she could gut a fish without raising her voice. "You found something in the archive. Fine. Archive it again."

"Danny," August said, turning. "You've been on nights since '96. You must have—"

"I drive where I'm dispatched," Danny said, not looking up. "That's the job. That's always been the job."

Marisol said nothing at all, but her eyes flicked to August's—once, quickly, a look like a door opening and shutting—and then she was out the bay doors to her cab, engine turning over too fast, too eager to be gone.

Ruth waited until the others cleared out. Then she took August's cold hands in her warm ones, and her voice softened into something worse than threat: tenderness.

"When your mama ran off, who fed you? When you flunked out and came back with your tail down, who gave you the night desk? This company raised you, August. Company history isn't in the ledgers. It's in the soup." She squeezed once, released. "Some fares don't get talked about. Not because they're dirty. Because they're *kept.* There's a difference, and one day, if you're smart, somebody'll explain it to you."

She left the cinnamon rolls. August couldn't touch them.

* * *

That afternoon, August pulled the maintenance records—the real ones, kept in a locked drawer Edmund Marsh's key still opened, because nobody had ever changed the locks on a dead man's desk. Cab 7, the long-wheelbase sedan, had been serviced eleven times in thirty years for "suspension wear, heavy cargo." Cab 7's winter tires were ordered every October regardless of mileage. Cab 7's fuel log showed a recurring route: depot, cemetery spur, Lakeshore Road, depot. Four times a year. Thirty years.

And then, twenty-two days ago, one final entry, handwritten in Ruth's unmistakable schoolteacher script:

*Cab 7 — special run — settled in full.*

August sat in the archive room as the light went amber and then blue outside the windows, and understood, with the slow cold certainty of ice forming over a lake, what the company was. Not a cab business with a secret. A secret with a cab business wrapped around it, the way a body is wrapped around its skeleton. Every warm lie, every pot of soup, every Christmas bonus and birthday cake in the break room had been paid for out of the same account as the silence.

The people who fed them on bad nights had been feeding a corpse across town for thirty years. And three weeks ago, one of them had fed it its last ride.

The phone on the archive desk rang. August picked it up automatically, dispatcher reflex, before remembering the line was supposed to be dead since the switchboard upgrade.

No dial tone. No static. Just breathing, and then a voice August knew better than their own mother's—gentle, tired, and afraid:

"Don't go to the lake house again, August. Please. Some graves you leave dug."

Then the line clicked empty, and August sat holding the receiver, knowing exactly whose hands had taught them to hold it.
