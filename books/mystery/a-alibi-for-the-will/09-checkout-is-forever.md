## 9. Checkout Is Forever

The night porter's desk at the Grand Meridian had a drawer that locked, and in thirty years Oscar Halloran had never once used the lock. Locks invited questions. Questions invited attention. Attention was the one thing a person could not check out of, once it registered under their name.

Tonight, Oscar unlocked the drawer.

Inside lay the camera—a small silver thing bought secondhand from the pawnshop on Corliss Street three winters ago, ostensibly for photographing lost-and-found items so guests could be told, with documentary authority, exactly what shape their forgotten umbrella had been in when it left the hotel's care. Nobody had ever asked to see those photographs. Nobody ever would. It was the perfect instrument: a tool whose very purpose was to make things official, and whose owner was too forgettable to suspect.

Oscar loaded the film by feel beneath the desk, hands steady in the way that hands become steady after decades of carrying other people's luggage without dropping it, without comment, without being remembered as anything more than *the help did it*. The lobby around them was performing its nightly transformation—the great chandelier dimmed to embers, the marble floor giving back the last light like a pond at dusk, the grandfather clock in the alcove ticking with the patience of something that had outlasted every guest who'd ever consulted it.

Three volumes of registry sat in the safe room off the linen closet. Oscar had already mapped which ones mattered: 1984 through 1991, the years when the columns stopped describing rooms and started describing something else entirely. Payments dressed as occupancy. Favors recorded as amenities. And threaded through it all, in a hand that grew shakier with each passing autumn, the initials M.V.

*Miriam Vale.*

Their mother's name, written in their mother's handwriting, hidden in plain sight for thirty-five years in a book that hundreds of guests had signed without ever reading.

Oscar waited until the night cleaning crew finished the mezzanine—11:40, always 11:40, Mrs. Okafor's vacuum rising and falling through the floors above like slow breathing—and then took the service corridor to the linen closet, let themselves into the safe room, and set the first ledger open under the small brass lamp.

The pages smelled of dust and old iron and, faintly, of lavender sachet, because Miriam had kept the linens once, and some part of her hands was still in everything she'd touched. Oscar turned the leaves carefully, as if they were skin.

*Room 214 — Oct. 3, 1989 — one night — paid in full.*

There was no guest registered for Room 214 that October. There was no guest for any of the entries. But October 3rd, 1989, had been the night of the council meeting where the lake trust was voted into perpetuity. Oscar knew this now the way they knew the weight of every key on the board: completely, and with a sickness underneath.

The camera clicked. Once, twice, a third time. Each flash no brighter than a struck match, each sound swallowed by the hum of the ice machines two floors down. Oscar worked methodically, page by page, laying each leaf flat with cotton gloves borrowed from the housekeeping stock, photographing not just the entries but the margins, the watermarks, the faint indentations left by whatever had been pressed against the paper before it. Thirty years of noticing had taught them that documents confessed more in their margins than in their lines.

By 2:15 in the morning, Volume VI was done. Oscar began Volume VII—autumn of '89, the heart of it—and felt the familiar tightening behind the sternum that was not quite fear and not quite grief but some alloy of both that had no name in any language Oscar spoke.

They turned to the section covering September.

The page was there. They photographed it.

They turned again.

The next leaf came over clean and blank-edged, and for one disorienting moment Oscar thought they'd skipped ahead—but no. The stub was there. A pale rectangle of remaining paper at the spine, cut close and precise, the ghost of a page that had been removed with a blade rather than torn out in haste. Tearing is passion. Tearing is panic. This was neither.

Someone had taken a razor to the binding of the Grand Meridian's guest registry with the same unhurried care that a surgeon brings to an incision, or a concierge to the trimming of a loose thread on a guest's lapel before they step into view of the lobby.

Oscar turned the next stub. Another. Three pages gone in total, and when they checked the dates bracketing the gap—the last entry before, the first entry after—the missing span covered the last week of September and the first weeks of October, 1989.

The week Miriam Vale stopped signing her name.

The week Augustus Vane died—or didn't.

Oscar stood very still in the safe room, the lamp buzzing faintly, the cotton gloves suddenly absurd against their bare feeling. Someone had gotten here first. Not recently—the adhesive residue along the stubs had yellowed, aged, settled into the paper the way stains settle into a tablecloth that has been washed many times since. This had happened years ago. Decades, perhaps. Someone had razored out these pages long before the will was contested, long before Vivian Ashe arrived asking her pointed questions, long before anyone thought the registries would matter to anyone but the hotel.

Which meant someone had known, all along, that these pages mattered.

And someone else—someone who had learned only recently what the ledgers contained—might come back for the rest.

Oscar finished the photographs anyway. Method over dread; it was the whole of their creed. Then they relocked the safe room, returned the gloves, wiped the lamp's brass stem with a handkerchief out of pure habit, and stood for a moment in the dark of the linen closet listening to the building breathe.

Thirty years, Oscar had thought of this hotel as a body they knew better than their own. Now it felt like a body that had swallowed something, long ago, and had been digesting it ever since.

* * *

Morning came gray and smelling of rain that hadn't started yet. Oscar was restocking the front desk pens—they noticed things like pen levels; it was how they noticed everything else—when the police cruiser rolled past the hotel windows going not toward the station but toward the depot end of Corliss Street, moving slowly, the way cruisers move when they are looking for somewhere to stop rather than somewhere to go.

Oscar watched it go and felt the morning tilt.

At 9:20, Deputy Marsh came into the lobby, hat in his hands, wearing the expression of a man about to deliver news he wished belonged to someone else's town. He didn't ask for Aldous Vane. He didn't ask for the manager. He asked, quietly, for whoever had been on shift Sunday night.

"That'd be me," Oscar said. "It's usually me."

"You know Danny Reyes? Porter, works evenings?"

"Everyone here works evenings," Oscar said. "Danny more than most."

Marsh nodded slowly, turning his hat a quarter-turn. "His bicycle's down at the bus depot. Been there since sometime Sunday night, by the look of it. Chained to the rack, but the bag's still in the basket—his change of shirt, a paperback, half a sandwich. Bus schedule's posted right there, and there's a ticket booth window nobody's staffed since June." He paused. "You seen him?"

"I saw him Saturday," Oscar said. "He was upset about something. He wanted money. He said he'd seen something, the night Mr. Vane died—the elder Mr. Vane. He mentioned a trunk."

"A trunk," Marsh repeated, without surprise, and Oscar understood that Danny had already told the deputy this, days ago, and that the telling had gone nowhere, or worse than nowhere.

"He quit yesterday," Oscar added. "No notice. Left his uniform folded on the shelf in the porter's room."

"Folded," Marsh said. "Neat?"

"Very."

The deputy looked at the register on the counter, at the chandelier, at nothing in particular. "Kid's nineteen. Kids leave town all the time. But they don't usually leave the bike." He put his hat back on. "If he contacts you, Mr. Halloran—you'd let me know."

"Of course," Oscar said, and meant it, and heard in their own voice the smoothness that had served them for thirty years, the voice that made lies unnecessary because it made truth sound like less than it was.

When Marsh had gone, Oscar stood at the desk and let the lobby assemble itself around the fact. A boy of nineteen had seen a trunk going down into the cellar the night the founder died, had carried that seeing around for a month like a stone in his shoe, had finally tried to run—and had vanished so cleanly that he'd left behind the one thing a runaway never leaves. His escape. His wheels. His own two feet, essentially, chained to a rack at the depot while the morning fog came off the lake and nobody claimed him.

*Folded,* Oscar thought. *He folded his uniform.*

Danny Reyes had been raised right. He'd been raised by someone who taught him you don't leave a mess behind, you don't leave your name smeared across a place you're quitting, you fold what you're leaving and you go. And then he had gone—or been made to look as though he'd gone—with a neatness that matched the razor cuts in the ledger's spine.

Tearing is passion. Folding is intention.

Oscar looked down at the register lying open on the counter, at the blank line waiting for the next name, and understood, with the clarity that comes not like lightning but like cold water rising, what kind of town Merrow's Bend actually was. Not a town with a secret. A town that was a secret, wearing streets and steeples and a summer festival like clothes.

They had erased Miriam Vale from the pages. Now they were erasing Danny Reyes from the streets. And between those two erasures lay thirty-five years of practice.

* * *

That afternoon, Oscar did something they had never done in three decades of employment. They called in sick.

The word felt foreign in their mouth, spoken into the manager's answering machine—*this is Halloran, I've caught something, I won't be in tonight*—and it was true in a way the manager couldn't have imagined. They had caught something. It had been catching them for days.

Oscar took the photographs to the pharmacy on Bridge Street, the one with the curtained development window, and handed the film roll across the counter to old Petrossian, who had developed every photograph in Merrow's Bend since before color film was trustworthy and who had never once, in all those years, looked at a customer as though he saw them.

"Two copies," Oscar said. "One set I'll pick up Thursday."

Petrossian nodded, wrote the ticket, slid it across. "Forty-eight hours. Rain coming, might be fifty."

"Take seventy-two if you need."

"It's not the rain," Petrossian said, tucking the film away. "It's that I do it right."

Oscar almost smiled. Almost. Outside, the first drops were starting to spot the pavement, and the lake beyond the breakwater had gone the color of hammered tin.

They walked home the long way, past the depot, because Oscar was the sort of person who needed to see things with their own eyes even when—especially when—the seeing hurt. The bicycle was still chained to the rack, sagging now under a skin of rain, the basket's contents gone and tagged into evidence somewhere, the frame gleaming wetly under the depot's dead security light. A paper notice fluttered under the handlebars, plastic-sleeved against the weather. HAVE YOU SEEN THIS YOUNG MAN?

The photograph they'd chosen showed Danny grinning, mid-laugh, in his porter's uniform, one hand raised in a wave that the camera had caught at its apex. He looked, in the picture, like someone the world had not yet gotten around to disappointing.

Oscar stood in the rain a long time, longer than was sensible, letting it soak through their coat. Around them the town went about its evening: lights coming on in the windows of the founding families' houses up on the bluff, warm and golden and orderly, the judge's place, the doctor's widow's place, the Vane house at the crown of it all with its lights burning brightest, as though brightness were the same thing as innocence.

*He keeps her in the cold room.* No—not yet. That sentence hadn't surfaced yet. What Oscar knew, standing in the rain, was smaller and colder: *I am going to trade myself for her* was still weeks away from being found, sewn inside an apron in an attic Oscar hadn't yet thought to search. What Oscar knew was only this: three pages razored out. One boy vanished. One mother erased. And a pattern so consistent, so practiced, that it could only be called a tradition.

Miriam Vale had been twenty-six when she disappeared. Danny Reyes was nineteen. Both of them employees of the Grand Meridian. Both of them people whose absence would be explained within a day—she ran off, he ran off, people run off, it's a resort town, transience is the weather here. The hotel itself provided the alibi. *Checkout is forever*, the old porters used to joke in the smoking room, back when smoking rooms existed and jokes could still be told about the guests. Oscar had laughed along, thirty years ago, young and invisible and grateful to be included in any laughter at all.

They weren't laughing now.

Oscar walked home through the rain and climbed the stairs to their rooms above the dry cleaner's on Tanner Street, and sat down at the kitchen table with the lamp on, and did not turn on the radio, and did not make tea. They sat with their hands flat on the table, watching them, waiting for them to shake.

They didn't shake. That was the thing. Thirty years of steadiness, and the hands refused, even now, to give the fear its due. So Oscar let the fear go somewhere else instead—down, below the sternum, into the same cellar where they kept everything—and what rose in its place was not calm exactly, but something with edges.

Resolve, if you wanted the polite word.

They got out a pencil and a sheet of the hotel's own cream stationery, because irony deserved a witness, and began to write down everything they knew in the order they'd learned it, the way they'd write up a lost item: description, location, date last seen. The ledger entries. The council dates. M.V. The razored pages. The trunk Danny saw. The doctor who signed the certificate and left. The bicycle at the depot.

When they finished, the list filled one side of the page, and it looked so small. Thirty-five years of a town's sin, and it fit on one sheet of cream paper with room left over for the watermark.

Oscar read it twice. Then they folded it—not neatly, not the way Danny had folded his uniform, but tight, angry, small—and slipped it into the lining of their coat, where it lay against their ribs like a second heartbeat.

Then they picked up the telephone and dialed the number Vivian Ashe had left at the desk, written in her quick slanted hand on a card that smelled faintly of another city.

She answered on the second ring.

"They've started cutting pages out of the books," Oscar said, without greeting. "And the boy who saw the trunk is gone. His bicycle's at the depot and he isn't."

A pause on the line, long enough for the rain to fill with static. Then Vivian Ashe said, in a voice stripped of all its executor's polish:

"Come to my room. Bring everything you have. And Mr. Halloran—" another pause, shorter, sharper, "—from tonight on, stop being invisible. Whatever it costs."

Oscar looked at the list folded against their chest, at the rain running down the dark glass, at the reflection of their own face superimposed over the lit windows of the bluff across the water—there and not there, present and unrecorded, the way they had been for thirty years.

"All right," they said. "But we work fast. Checkout here isn't what it used to be."

Outside, the storm was gathering itself over the lake, and down in the town, in a house at the crown of the bluff, someone had just begun, very calmly, to burn paper.
