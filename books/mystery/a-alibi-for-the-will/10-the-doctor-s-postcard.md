## 10. The Doctor's Postcard

The postcard arrived on a Tuesday, which was fitting, because Tuesdays were when the Grand Meridian received its dead letters—returns, misdeliveries, correspondence addressed to guests who had checked out decades ago and never updated their forwarding addresses. Oscar had a drawer for them. A shallow wooden drawer lined with felt, like a reliquary, holding forty years of abandoned mail: love letters never claimed, tax notices for men long buried, a birthday card that had taken eleven years to find its recipient and arrived two days after her funeral.

Oscar sorted the Tuesday mail the way they did everything else—with the unhurried precision of someone who had learned that speed attracts attention and attention, in Merrow's Bend, had always been a currency better hoarded than spent.

The postcard was face-down in the stack. A picture of Lake Willoughby in autumn, the kind sold in drugstore racks, the colors slightly too saturated to be believed. The stamp was old but valid. The handwriting on the address side was a doctor's handwriting—tight, slanted, the letters of a man who had signed his name ten thousand times until the name wore smooth as river stone.

*Dr. E. Halloway, Birch Cottage, Harrow Lane, Cold Spring.*

Oscar stood very still in the back office with the postcard in their hand, and outside the window the lake did its gray morning shimmer, indifferent, and somewhere upstairs a guest ran a bath, and the pipes sang through the walls the way they always sang, and Oscar thought: *he's still alive.*

Dr. Edwin Halloway. The name Vivian Ashe had spoken three nights ago in the empty dining room, her voice low, her finger resting on a photocopied death certificate like it might bite her. *Signed by a doctor who left town in 1990. No forwarding address filed with the county. No license renewal. He just—evaporated.* She had said it the way people say things they have already begun to fear.

And now here was his handwriting, traveling the ordinary mails, asking—for the card was addressed to the hotel, not to any guest—to be remembered by the building he had once served.

Oscar turned the card over. There was no message. Just the address, the stamp, and in the lower corner, small, almost shy, a single line in that worn-smooth script:

*I remember the cold. Please tell them I remember the cold.*

* * *

Vivian Ashe arrived at four o'clock, which meant she arrived at 3:52 and sat in her car in the lot for eight minutes, because Oscar had watched her do it twice before and understood it was not hesitation but ritual—a woman giving herself time to become the version of herself the moment required. She came through the front doors with her coat still buttoned and her eyes already moving, cataloguing, and Oscar met her at the desk before she could ask.

"We need to talk somewhere without ears," Oscar said.

"Is there such a place in this hotel?"

"There is one place," Oscar said, "where even I don't listen."

They took the service stairs down past the laundry, past the boiler room with its patient industrial breathing, to the old wine cellar—or what had been the wine cellar before Prohibition emptied it and the decades filled it with furniture nobody wanted: stacked chairs from a banquet hall renovation in '74, a taxidermied pike glaring from a glass case, rolls of carpet wrapped in plastic like shrouds. The stone walls sweated cold. A single bulb burned behind a wire cage. It was the one room in the Grand Meridian where sound went to die, absorbed into stone and dust, and Oscar had chosen it precisely because thirty years of listening had taught them the geography of silence.

Oscar laid the postcard on an upturned crate between them.

Vivian read it twice. Her gloved finger hovered over the last line without touching it, the way one hovers over a wound.

"He remembers the cold," she said. "My father has been dead thirty-five years, Mr. Halloran, and this man is sending postcards about being cold."

"Not about being cold." Oscar heard their own voice come out flat and careful, the voice they used for delivering bad news to families who hadn't yet understood the phone call was that kind of phone call. "About remembering it. There's a difference. A man who is cold complains. A man who *remembers* the cold—" They stopped. The cellar air tasted of iron and old rain.

"Of what?" Vivian pressed. "Finish the sentence."

"A man who remembers the cold is a man who was there when something happened that he can't say out loud anymore."

Vivian took off one glove, slowly, finger by finger, and picked up the postcard with her bare hand as if skin against paper might yield some truth the eyes could not. "Cold Spring is two hours north. We could drive up tonight."

"We could drive up tomorrow," Oscar said. "Tonight I need to check the desk. And Mrs. Okonkwo in 214 watches the parking lot like it owes her money. If we leave together after dark, by morning the whole town will know, and Judge Crane will know first."

Something moved across Vivian's face—not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the acknowledgment of a shared grammar. "You think like a conspirator, Mr. Halloran."

"I think like a concierge," Oscar said. "It's the same skill set. Only the loyalties differ."

* * *

They drove north the next morning under a sky the color of unpolished pewter, the lake falling away behind them in the rearview mirror like a page turning. Vivian drove. Oscar navigated, though navigation was hardly necessary—the route to Cold Spring was one road through country that grew progressively more surrendered to October: fields gone to straw, barns with their mouths open, birches dropping their coins of yellow leaf onto the asphalt so the car seemed to travel on scattered light.

Neither spoke much for the first hour. There is a particular silence that forms between two people who are both afraid of the same thing and have not yet agreed to name it, and it filled the car like a third passenger.

It was Vivian who broke it, finally, at the top of a long hill where the valley opened below them, all smoke-colored woods and a river stitching through.

"When I was a girl," she said, "my father used to take me to the hotel for Sunday dinner. He'd order the trout and I'd have the ice cream, and Augustus Vane would come to our table and shake my hand like I was a person who mattered. Everyone loved him. That was the thing no one ever says about monsters—they're usually beloved first." She kept her eyes on the road. "I've spent my whole adult life assuming my father died of a heart attack in a town that worshipped the man whose estate I'm now untangling. What if the certificate was wrong? What if everything was wrong?"

"Then you'll want to sit down when we get there," Oscar said quietly. "Because I've been reading those ledgers for two weeks now, Ms. Ashe, and I will tell you what I told no one: nothing in this town adds up. Not the rooms. Not the dates. Not the grief. This whole place has been balancing its books against something in the dark for longer than either of us has been alive."

The words hung in the car. Vivian glanced sideways at them—at Oscar—and for a moment the executor's armor slipped and Oscar saw underneath it not an investigator but a daughter, tired in a way that sleep doesn't touch.

"You could have told me sooner," she said.

"Yes," Oscar admitted. "But telling things sooner is how people stop being invisible, and I've been invisible for thirty years. It's a hard habit to break." They looked out at the birches. "I'm breaking it anyway. Slowly."

* * *

Birch Cottage sat at the end of Harrow Lane like something the village had forgotten to demolish: a low stone house with a slate roof furred with moss, a garden gone gloriously wild with late asters, and a woodpile stacked with the obsessive geometry of a man who had once held steady hands. A brass plate beside the door still read *E. HALLOWAY, M.D.*, green with verdigris, the letters soft-edged as something drowned.

A woman answered the door—sixtyish, sturdy, wearing a cardigan the color of oatmeal. Her name was Mrs. Pell, she explained, and she was the caregiver, and yes, visitors were allowed, though she asked them kindly to keep the visit short because Dr. Halloway had what she called his "sundowning" and afternoons were best.

"He was a doctor here in Merrow's Bend," Vivian said. "Before he retired."

Mrs. Pell's face did something complicated and brief—the flicker of a woman who has heard stories and decided which ones to carry and which to leave at the gate. "He doesn't talk about the old days," she said. "Except sometimes. Come in, then. Mind the cat."

The cottage interior smelled of beeswax and chamomile and, beneath it, faintly, of camphor—the smell of a medical bag long since put away but never fully gone. Dr. Halloway sat in an armchair by the window, wrapped in a tartan blanket despite the mild day, a small man shrunk inside his own history. His hands lay on the blanket, and Oscar looked at those hands and thought of all the signatures they had produced, all the certificates, all the times they had written *herein lies* over truths that were not lying there at all.

"Edwin," Mrs. Pell said gently, crouching to his ear. "You have visitors. From Merrow's Bend."

The old man's head turned. His eyes were pale blue, watery, and utterly without guile—which was somehow worse than cunning would have been. He looked at Vivian first, and something in his face contracted, the way a face contracts around a name it isn't supposed to know.

"You have his chin," Halloway said. His voice was dry paper. "You have Marcus Ashe's chin exactly."

Vivian went white. "You knew my father?"

"Knew him." The old man smiled, and the smile was terrible in its gentleness. "Signed him too. Lovely signature. I do lovely signatures. That was always the trouble with me—I do *lovely* signatures." He laughed, a small cracked sound, and then the laugh guttered out and his eyes drifted to the window, to the asters burning purple in the garden, and he was somewhere else entirely.

Mrs. Pell made apologetic motions toward the kitchen. Vivian followed her, and Oscar heard the low murmur of their voices—*he has good days, he has days when he won't come out from under the blanket*—while Oscar stayed by the armchair and did what Oscar had always done best: waited, invisibly, for a secret to forget it was being kept.

It took eleven minutes. Oscar counted.

The sun shifted. A cloud crossed it, and the room dimmed, and in the dimness Dr. Halloway's hand crept out from under the blanket and found the arm of his chair and gripped it, and his lips began to move.

Oscar leaned close. Close enough to smell camphor and tea and the sweet dust of great age.

"They carried him down," the old man whispered. "Past the kitchen. Past the maids' stairs. She was there—that girl, the Vale girl, with the tray—and he wasn't cold yet. Do you understand? He *wasn't cold yet*, and we carried him down anyway, and she saw us, and God forgive me, God forgive all of us, we let her see us because stopping would have been worse than letting her see—"

His voice climbed, thinned, frayed. His grip whitened on the chair arm.

"—and it was so warm in that room, why was it so warm, a dead man should be cold, and he wasn't cold yet, he wasn't cold yet, he wasn't—"

"Edwin." Mrs. Pell was suddenly there, kneeling, her hands over his, her voice a low tide pulling him back. "Edwin, love, you're home. You're home in Cold Spring. Nobody's carrying anybody anywhere."

The old man subsided. His breathing slowed. His eyes found Oscar's, and for one lucid, appalling second, the senility lifted like fog off water and Oscar saw the man underneath—the man who had known exactly what he was doing, every signature, every date, every lie—and that man looked at Oscar with an expression of pure, exhausted pleading.

"You're the one who remembers everything," Halloway whispered. "I can tell. You have the look. Don't remember this one, son. Some things rot faster in memory than in the ground."

Then the fog rolled back over him, and he smiled at the window, and asked Mrs. Pell whether it was going to snow.

* * *

They walked out through the wild garden because neither of them could bear to sit in the car yet. The asters nodded in a wind coming down off the northern hills, and somewhere a pheasant called its rough two-note question, and Vivian Ashe stood among the dying flowers with her arms crossed tight against her body as if holding herself together at the seams.

"He wasn't cold yet," she said. She said it the way you test ice—one word at a time, weight shifting forward, listening for the crack. "A man who's just died of a heart attack cools at roughly one degree an hour. That's forensic basics. If Halloway remembers him as warm—if he remembers being *disturbed* by how warm—then either the death happened minutes before they moved him..."

"Or it hadn't happened at all," Oscar finished.

The wind moved through the asters. Far off, the pheasant called again.

"Preserved," Vivian said. The word came out of her reluctantly, dragged like a splinter. "People don't preserve bodies, Mr. Halloran. Not in 1989. Not in a lakeside town. Unless—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Unless the body is worth more than the death. Unless a dead founder who might not be dead is worth more than a founder who's honestly in the ground. The trust. The will. Everything hinges on Augustus Vane being *dead*. Legally, irrevocably dead. But if he wasn't—if he was only *made* dead on paper—"

"Then someone has spent thirty-five years keeping a corpse on ice," Oscar said, "and calling it a grave."

Vivian turned and looked at them, and in her face Oscar watched the horror arrive and rearrange everything—the death certificate, the sealed coffin, the ledger, the vanished maid, the whole tidy architecture of Merrow's Bend's grief—watched it all tilt and show itself as scaffolding around a lie.

"The stones in the coffin," she breathed. "River stones. Someone needed weight. Someone needed the coffin to feel full." Her hand rose to her mouth. "Oh God. The luggage tag. Whoever put that tag in the grave wanted it found eventually. Wanted the grave opened. That's why the will fight started—that's why *now*—"

"Someone is unwinding it," Oscar agreed softly. "Piece by piece. On purpose."

They stood in the garden while the light failed, two people holding the same terrible arithmetic, and Oscar felt the old familiar dread transmute into something harder and more useful. For thirty years Oscar had collected other people's secrets the way a tide collects glass—smoothed, anonymous, beautiful in their harmlessness. This was not harmless. This was a secret with a heartbeat, or the absence of one, waiting somewhere under Merrow's Bend in the dark and the cold.

Back at the car, Vivian paused with her hand on the door and looked north, toward home, toward the lake already visible as a bright seam between the hills.

"Halloway said they carried him down," she said. "*Down.* Past the kitchen. Past the maids' stairs." Her eyes came around to Oscar's, wide and certain and afraid. "Mr. Halloran. Where does one go *down*, in that hotel?"

Oscar did not answer immediately. Because Oscar knew the building's bones the way a surgeon knows anatomy—every corridor, every stair, every wall that had been added over older walls—and there was one place, one sealed and unlisted place, that the blueprints pretended did not exist. A place the oldest staff never mentioned. A place where, in another century, the hotel had cut its winter ice from the lake and stored summer's death against the heat.

"The pre-Prohibition ice cellar," Oscar said. "Under the west wing. Sealed since before the war."

The wind came down off the hills and shook the asters, and far away across two counties the lake waited, gray and patient, keeping whatever it kept.

They drove home in the gathering dark, and neither of them turned on the radio, and the headlights carved their small tunnel through the black, and behind them in a cottage garden an old man who did lovely signatures slept in his chair, dreaming, perhaps, of a warm room and a long staircase and the weight of a man who wasn't cold yet.
