## 17. Rehearsal

The diner closed at nine on Sundays, but on this Sunday the CLOSED sign went up at seven, and Pilar Ruiz flipped the deadbolt with the ceremony of a woman locking a vault. Then she turned around and surveyed what her sister had assembled in the back room like a general surveying an army that had never fought a war together.

Ansel Sandoval stood by the coat rack holding his own jacket like evidence he wasn't sure was admissible.

"Sit," Pilar said, pointing at a chair that had been dragged from the kitchen and set at the head of a long table of mismatched folding tables pushed together under the fluorescent hum. "You're the guest of honor. That's the whole point."

"I don't think I've ever been a guest of honor."

"We know," said Dr. Adaeze Okafor, retired county coroner, thirty-one years of death certificates behind her and a glass of red wine already in hand. She patted the chair beside her. "It shows. Sit anyway. Practice makes it look natural."

The back room smelled of coffee gone bitter on the warmer, of lemon polish, of the fryer grease that lived in the walls of every diner in America like a resident ghost. Someone had taped paper place cards at six settings. Someone—Danny, Ansel suspected, judging by the block letters pressed so hard they'd torn through in places—had written names: MARISOL. DR. O. MRS. VOSS. DANNY. ANSEL. And at the far end, facing the door, a card that read only HIM.

Miriam Voss arrived last, silver-haired and upright, wearing pearls to a rehearsal in a diner back room because, she explained, if one must pretend to attend one's own anniversary gala, one should dress for it. She had brought a casserole no one had asked for. It sat steaming in the center of the fake table between the salt shaker and the prop wine bottle Pilar had filled with grape juice, because, as Marisol put it, "Nobody rehearses a murder trap drunk."

Marisol stood at the whiteboard she'd wheeled in from somewhere—Ansel genuinely could not imagine where—and uncapped a marker.

"Okay. Ground rules." She wrote GROUND RULES as though the room might otherwise forget them mid-sentence. "One: this is practice, not performance. We stop whenever anyone says stop. Two: nobody plays themselves better than they actually are, except Danny, who is playing a grown man, which is its own kind of acting. Three—" she capped the marker, pointed it at Ansel, "—he does not get to leave when it gets uncomfortable. That's the whole exercise."

"That's entrapment," Ansel said.

"That's family," said Pilar, from the doorway, arms crossed.

The word landed in the room and sat there, warm and enormous, and Ansel looked at his place card to avoid looking at any of their faces.

* * *

They began, as Marisol insisted, with the exits.

"Stand up. Walk me through it. You're seated where?"

Ansel stood. The back room rearranged itself in his mind without effort—that was the thing about his memory, it never required effort, only permission, and he had spent forty years denying it permission to matter. "I'm at the head-adjacent seat, left of Mrs. Voss. Door behind me and to the right, twelve feet, swings out into the hall, second door past the restrooms leads to the kitchen, kitchen has the loading exit you showed me Tuesday. Windows—" he glanced up, "—painted shut, all four. Fire code violation, by the way."

"Noted," Marisol said dryly. "Continue."

"The ballroom proper has three egresses: main doors off the lobby, service corridor by the band platform, and the terrace doors past the memorial table. The terrace locks from the inside but the latch is old; I tested a photograph of the hardware and it's the same model I saw fail at the Dellwood Hotel in 2016. If I'm cornered, I go terrace, down the service stair, into the loading bay."

"And Lark knows all this too," Danny said quietly, from the end of the table. "Because he's read everything you've ever filed. Maybe he's walked the ballroom himself."

Silence took its seat at the table like a seventh guest.

"Yeah," Ansel said. "That's the problem. My exits are his exits. Which means my exits are theater. Which means—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "Which means the real plan isn't escape. It's containment. I keep him talking until the signal."

"The dropped glass," Miriam said. She turned her wineglass slowly by the stem. "At Edwin's funeral, a waiter dropped a tray during the eulogy and everyone laughed nervously, and I remember thinking how strange it was, that a man could be lowered into the ground and a tray could clatter, and both things would be true in the same minute." She set the glass down with a click. "So we drop a glass. Fine. But Ansel, dear—you'll be standing next to him when it drops. Are you prepared for what happens in the four seconds before Marisol's people reach you?"

"No," Ansel said honestly.

"Good," said Dr. Okafor. "Honesty. First useful thing anyone's said tonight. Now sit down and let's run the dinner."

* * *

Danny played Edmund Lark the way water plays a rock—patiently, finding every seam.

He'd asked for five minutes alone with the letters before they started, and Ansel had watched him read, this sixteen-year-old with a healed wrist and a witness's terrible gift for detail, running his thumb along the handwriting as though the pen strokes were braille. Now Danny stood at the far end of the room, hands clasped behind his back, and when he spoke, his voice came out lower than his years, smoothed and courteous, with a warmth in it that made the hair on Ansel's arms rise.

"Mr. Sandoval. What a pleasure to finally meet you in person." Danny extended an imaginary hand. "I've admired your work for a very long time."

Ansel shook the empty air. "Mr.—?"

"Lark. Edmund Lark. Though I suppose you knew that." Danny smiled the way the letters smiled, in their curving, patient script. "You write beautifully, did you know? All those reports. So precise. *The decedent's left shoe was found upright beneath the dock ladder, laces tied.* I've read that sentence two hundred times."

"You've read my reports," Ansel said, keeping his voice level, playing the scene as planned.

"I've read *you*, Mr. Sandoval. There's a difference." Danny tilted his head, and it was uncanny, the tilt, exactly the tilt of a man who had spent fifteen years watching from the quiet side of a records office. "Do you know what I admire most? You never took credit. Three deaths, three denials signed, three funerals you attended from the back row. A lesser man would have wanted a name in the paper. You wanted nothing. That's how I knew you'd understand."

"Understand what?"

Danny's voice dropped further, gentle as a hand on the shoulder. "What it costs. To see everything and be seen by no one."

And Ansel—Ansel who had rehearsed this, who had memorized the layout of a ballroom in nine minutes flat, who could recite the fire-code violations of four counties—felt the floor of the scene open under him.

Because Danny wasn't quoting the letters anymore. He was quoting something older. Something from a page that had arrived inside a locked apartment, torn from a diary a twelve-year-old boy had kept in a house where being right was the same as being hated.

*I counted the spelling words again. Forty-seven. Miss Pell said I was showing off. I wasn't showing off. I just can't not know them.*

"Stop," Ansel said.

The word came out smaller than he intended. The room obeyed instantly—Danny dropping the voice like setting down something fragile, Marisol half-rising, Pilar's hand appearing on Ansel's shoulder before he'd registered crossing the room.

"I said stop," Ansel repeated, and heard the crack in it, and hated the crack, and heard it anyway.

"Rule one," Marisol said quietly. "We stop. We stopped. Sit down, Ansel."

"I'm fine."

"You're not, and that's allowed." She steered him back to his chair. "Water."

Pilar put water in front of him. Dr. Okafor refilled her own wine and made no comment about it, which from her was a kindness. Miriam Voss reached across the fake tablecloth and laid her age-spotted hand over Ansel's knuckles and left it there, saying nothing, asking nothing, the way people do when they have buried a husband and learned that silence can be a held door.

"He used my childhood," Ansel said finally, to the water glass. "In there. Just now. Not even on purpose—Danny was doing the voice, and the voice knew things, and my brain went—" He exhaled. "There's a page missing from my life. He has it. He's had it for months. And every time I try to build a wall around that fact, he's already standing on the other side of it, taking notes on the wall."

"Then stop building walls," Dr. Okafor said.

Ansel looked at her.

"You're an investigator. Walls are your native architecture—I've read enough of your reports secondhand through Marisol to know you file human beings like exhibits." She said it without cruelty, the way coroners say true things. "But this man's entire method is breach. Letters through locked doors. Pages from burned diaries. He wins every time you fortify, because fortifying is what he expects memory to do. Memory builds archives. Archives have walls. Walls have doors." She sipped her wine. "So don't archive tomorrow night. Be a person. Persons are unpredictable. Persons flinch and recover in ways no file predicts. Let him study a man who's changed since the last report he read."

"The last report he read," Ansel repeated slowly.

"Is months old," Marisol finished, catching it. "Everything he knows about you ends at the man who worked alone."

"And the man sitting here," Pilar said from the doorway, "learned to accept a casserole. That's growth no archivist has on record."

A ragged little laugh went around the table. Even Ansel produced one, thin but real.

* * *

They ran it again. And again.

By the third pass, Danny's Lark had cornered Ansel by the imaginary memorial table—the corner where, in two days' time, the real confrontation would happen or wouldn't—and delivered the verdict speech, the one they'd reconstructed from letter fragments and Danny's memory of the benefactor's cadence: *Every accident was a lesson, Mr. Sandoval. Every denial you signed was tuition. I merely collected.*

And this time Ansel didn't stop. This time he stood in the flinch and let it move through him like weather, and answered:

"Then you know what I remember about the Harrow boathouse. Tell me the color of the rope."

Danny blinked—out of character for half a second, a sixteen-year-old surfacing—and then, because Danny was Danny, he improvised: "Green."

"Wrong," Ansel said softly. "It was gray. Weathered gray, frayed at the cleat, and that detail was never released, which means whoever sent that confession read the file, and whoever reads files in this county works in a very small office, and I will be shaking your hand across a memorial table in front of forty witnesses while I say so." He let the pause stretch. "Your turn, Mr. Lark."

The back room broke into applause that embarrassed everyone involved, most of all Ansel, who discovered that being applauded felt like sunlight on skin accustomed to shade—pleasant and slightly burning.

"Again?" Danny asked, breathless, alive in a way Ansel hadn't seen from him since the hospital.

"One more," Marisol said. "Then everyone goes home and sleeps, because tomorrow night we do it with crystal instead of grape juice."

* * *

It was nearly midnight when the others filtered out—Okafor first, then Miriam with her emptied casserole dish embraced like a shield, then Danny, who paused at the door and looked back at Ansel with an expression too old for his face.

"For what it's worth," Danny said, "the voice was easier than I thought it'd be. Because I've heard it in my head for two years anyway. Every time I almost told somebody and didn't." His jaw tightened. "Tomorrow I tell everybody. Loudly. In a tux."

After he left, Pilar pressed a foil-wrapped plate of pie into Ansel's hands—"You'll eat it or you'll throw it away, but you'll take it"—and Marisol walked him out through the dark dining room, past chairs upended on tables, past the window where the streetlight laid its orange stripe across the linoleum.

"At the third rehearsal," she said, not looking at him, "when Danny quoted the diary page. You stopped. Good. Stopping is a skill." She unlocked the door and held it, cold air shouldering in. "But here's what I need you to hear before tomorrow. When it goes wrong in there—and it will go wrong somewhere, it always does—you will want to do what you've done your whole life. Go quiet. Go inward. File yourself away under *casualty* and handle it alone."

She stepped closer. Under the buzzing sign above the diner, her face was half-lit, half-shadow, a working detective's face.

"Don't," she said. "Look for one of us. Any of us. Okafor by the bar. Miriam at the head table. Me at the service corridor. Danny wherever Danny damn well pleases, probably somewhere he shouldn't be. We will be there, Sandoval. That's not the plan. That's the premise."

Ansel stood in the cold with his foil-wrapped pie and his photographic mind, which had already stored this moment—her face, the stripe of light, the exact temperature of the air—in whatever chamber of it keeps the things that matter, whether he permits them to or not.

"Premise accepted," he said.

He was halfway down the block when his phone buzzed. A text from an unknown number, and he knew—he knew with the sick certainty of a man whose memory catalogued everything, including the shape of every threat that had come before—that he should not look at it until morning, with witnesses, with Marisol.

He looked at it.

One line, in that patient, curving hand rendered in type:

*Enjoyed the rehearsal. You've gotten better at being watched. See you at the fourth toast — E.L.*

Below it, a photograph: the back room of the diner, taken from outside the window, sometime in the last three hours. Six paper place cards visible on the long table.

Seven settings, if you counted the seventh chair, pulled up close, empty, waiting.

Someone had been watching the rehearsal the whole time.
