## 8. The Ledger in Plain Sight

The rain came at dusk, as it often did in Saltmere Bay, arriving without ceremony and settling in for the night like a relative with no other plans. Beatrix Hartwell watched it streak the archive window from her desk, where she had spent four hours doing nothing that would look like work to anyone passing by. That was the point. She had learned, over the past week, to arrange herself into an attitude of boredom—shoulders loose, tea going cold beside her, a random file open on top of the desk—while her mind worked somewhere else entirely.

It was working now, down in the tide.

She had Eleanor Vane's almanacs spread across the long table in the back room, six of them, spanning 1994 through 2001, their cloth covers faded to the color of old rope. The town library had deaccessioned them years ago—a small mercy of municipal indifference—and they'd sat in Beatrix's collection ever since, catalogued but never truly read. Tide tables were tide tables. The sea rose, the sea fell, and the numbers said so twice a day, forever, in columns so regular they might have been printed by a machine rather than a person.

Except they hadn't been. Not all of them.

Beatrix turned to the 1998 volume, opened flat under a brass weight shaped like an anchor, and looked again at the marginalia she'd found three nights ago—the notation that had started this whole unraveling. In the margin beside the entry for August 14th, in faded blue-black ink, someone had written: *cf. Ps. 107:23–30.*

*They that go down to the sea in ships...*

A scripture reference. In a tide almanac. Eleanor Vane had been many things according to the town's collective memory—formidable, exacting, generous in the way that made you feel the debt—but she had not been devout. No one remembered her in the pews of St. Brendan's. And yet here was psalm notation, tucked against the tides of the very week Cordelia Vane walked out of the world.

Beatrix had spent two days telling herself it was coincidence. Devotional habit, a stray thought, nothing. But tonight, with the rain sealing her inside the archive like wax around a letter, she stopped lying to herself and did what she was built to do.

She went through everything.

The first thing she noticed, once she committed to looking, was how *few* the annotations were. Across six years of daily tide entries—more than four thousand lines of heights and times—there were only eleven marks. Eleven verses, penciled or inked in margins, each one beside a specific date. If they were devotional, they were the most selective devotion she had ever seen. Nobody prayed eleven times in six years, always beside particular tides, and never once in between.

Nobody wrote scripture next to a tide unless the tide meant something.

She copied the dates onto a fresh sheet of paper in her narrow, upright hand:

- March 3, 1995 — *Gen. 7:11*
- June 19, 1996 — *Ex. 14:21*
- September 2, 1996 — *Jonah 1:15*
- April 11, 1997 — *Ps. 69:1–2*
- August 14, 1998 — *Ps. 107:23–30*
- November 30, 1998 — *Josh. 3:16*
- February 22, 1999 — *Ex. 14:22*
- July 8, 1999 — *Matt. 8:24*
- October 17, 2000 — *Acts 27:41*
- January 5, 2001 — *Jonah 2:3*
- May 26, 2001 — *Rev. 21:1*

She stared at the list until the letters swam. Every verse was water. Genesis, the flood. Exodus, the parting sea. Jonah, swallowed. Revelation, *and there was no more sea.* Whoever had chosen these verses hadn't been praying—they'd been *indexing*. Each one was a chapter heading in a story about water, arranged beside specific days, and if there was one thing Beatrix knew about the Vanes, it was that nothing in their possession was decorative.

So she asked the question any archivist would ask: what did these dates have in common?

She pulled the actual tide data for each annotated day and laid the numbers side by side. High water times, low water times, heights above chart datum. For a while the columns resisted her, offering only noise. Then she noticed the low-water heights. Most days in Saltmere Bay, low tide bottomed out between 0.8 and 1.4 meters above datum—an ordinary breathing of the bay. But on the annotated dates, the figures dropped lower. 0.6. 0.5. On August 14th, 1998: **0.3 meters**. A spring tide. An extreme low, the kind that exposed the mudflats below the old pier and let a person walk out beneath its pilings on bare ground.

Every single annotated date was an unusually low tide. Not the lowest of each year—she checked; some springs ran deeper—but consistently, deliberately low. Eleven days over six years when the sea pulled far back from the land.

Eleven appointments.

Her pulse picked up, a small quick drumming she could feel behind her ears. She stood, paced the length of the back room, stood again. This was the feeling she lived for and pretended not to—the moment a pattern surfaced from silt, cold and clean, like something the tide itself had delivered. She pressed her palms flat against the table and made herself slow down. Patterns could be wished into existence. She'd seen amateur researchers drown in coincidences of their own manufacture. Test it. Test it against something independent.

The confession letter. It was locked in the fireproof cabinet across the room, but she didn't need to open it; she had transcribed it twice and memorized the relevant passage. *What was done was done at the water's edge, when the water was gone and the ground showed.* When the water was gone. The letter's author had described a scene possible only at extreme low tide—beneath the pier, on exposed flats, in darkness or near-darkness. And the letter had been postmarked the day after Eleanor's funeral, which meant its author had waited decades and then chosen *that* moment, as though the funeral were a key turning in a lock.

Eleanor's almanac marked eleven low tides with verses about water. The letter described something done at a vanished tide. The dates clustered before and after August 1998, densest in the year Cordelia disappeared.

It wasn't proof. But it was architecture. And architecture meant a builder.

Beatrix sat back down and began the real work: converting tides into numbers. Because if these dates were entries in something—if Eleanor, or whoever held the pen, had been keeping a record disguised as piety—then the record needed a system. Dates alone proved nothing. Dates plus values might prove everything.

She tried the obvious first: the low-water height itself. 0.3, 0.6, 0.5... She wrote the sequence out and squinted at it. Too small a range, too arbitrary. Then high-water times, converted to minutes past midnight. Nothing. Then the interval between high and low water on each date. She computed them carefully, checking her arithmetic twice, and wrote the results in a column:

March 3, 1995 — 6 hours 12 minutes
June 19, 1996 — 6 hours 25
September 2, 1996 — 6 hours 07
April 11, 1997 — 6 hours 31
August 14, 1998 — 6 hours 18
November 30, 1998 — 6 hours 44
February 22, 1999 — 6 hours 09
July 8, 1999 — 6 hours 37
October 17, 2000 — 6 hours 22
January 5, 2001 — 6 hours 15
May 26, 2001 — 6 hours 29

Minutes past the hour: 12, 25, 07, 31, 18, 44, 09, 37, 22, 15, 29.

She looked at those numbers for a long time. They weren't random—random was what she'd expected, and random has a texture she knew well, the texture of noise. These had a different grain. Twelve, twenty-five, seven... Her eyes kept snagging on the range. All between one and forty-four. All within the span of—

She reached for the gravestone inscriptions. She'd photographed every stone in the Vane plot at St. Brendan's years ago, part of her original survey of the case, and the files were alphabetized in the cabinet under V. She pulled the folder and spread the photographs across the table beside the almanacs, and there it was, the thing she'd catalogued a hundred times without ever *reading*: the epitaphs. Every Vane grave carried a verse. *The Lord gave, and the Lord hath taken away.* *I will ransom them from the power of the grave.* *In my Father's house are many mansions.* Eleven graves. Eleven verses. Eleven annotated tides.

The same book, written twice—once in stone, once in water.

Her hands were not quite steady as she matched them up. The order of the graves by date of death gave her a sequence; the order of the annotated tides gave her another; and where the sequences intersected, numbers emerged. The third grave in the plot bore Job 19:25—chapter nineteen, verse twenty-five. Beside it in her column of tide-derived figures: 19 and 25 appeared together, split across two adjacent entries. The seventh grave carried Psalm 46:10, and there in the tide intervals, forty-six minutes' difference between two computed values, ten appearing as a day-count between annotated dates. It was not clean. It was not elegant. It was the kind of code made by someone who wanted it findable only by a person willing to do exactly what Beatrix was doing—cross-referencing scripture against hydrology against cemetery photography at one in the morning while rain hammered the windows.

But it was *there*. Underneath the town's oldest grief, underneath the mourning veil Eleanor Vane had worn for a quarter century, there was a ledger. Dates, payments perhaps, or meetings, or deliveries—she couldn't yet say what the numbers recorded—but a ledger nonetheless, kept in the only cipher Eleanor would have trusted: one that looked like grief and God to everyone else, and like arithmetic to almost no one.

"Almost," Beatrix said aloud, to the empty room, and heard how strange her voice sounded after hours of silence.

Because that was the word that mattered. *Almost no one.* A code is built for two parties: the one who writes and the one who must read. Somewhere, if this ledger was what it seemed, there had been a second person who could decode it. Gerald Pike, perhaps—the estate lawyer whose name surfaced in every probate document she owned like a watermark. Or someone else. Someone who might still be alive, still in Saltmere Bay, still able to read the water.

She gathered her papers into a single folder, squared its edges, and slid it into the cabinet with the letter. Then she stood a moment with her hand on the cool metal door, listening to the rain, letting the exhilaration drain slowly out of her body the way the tide drained off the flats—leaving things exposed that usually stayed hidden.

That was when she saw it.

The back room had two entrances: the door to the front office, and the outside stair used for deliveries, which she kept bolted from within. She had bolted it tonight. She always bolted it. But the brass slide-bolt, which she checked nightly with the same compulsive ritual she applied to everything, was sitting in the open position—not thrown back violently, not forced, just *open*, resting in its channel as neatly as if she'd forgotten it.

She had not forgotten it. Beatrix Hartwell forgot nothing; forgetting was a luxury her life had never afforded. She retraced her evening in her mind with archival precision: arrived at six, made tea, worked at the front desk until nine, moved to the back room at nine-fifteen, checked both bolts at nine-twenty as she always did. The bolt had been closed at nine-twenty. It was open now, at nearly one in the morning, and she had not left the building since.

Unless someone else had.

She crossed the room quietly, absurdly aware of the sound of her own feet on the floorboards, and examined the bolt. No scratches. No disturbance in the fine salt dust she'd stopped wiping from the sill months ago because she liked knowing when anything touched it. The dust was undisturbed too—which meant whoever had opened it had either been careful enough to leave no trace, or had opened it from knowledge rather than force, or...

Or she had misremembered. The thought arrived uninvited, wearing the reasonable face of a friend. *You check a dozen bolts and locks and latches a day. You're tired. You've been running on coffee and revelation for a week.*

"No," she said softly. "I don't misremember."

But standing there in the lamplight with the rain coming down and a decoded ledger humming in the folder behind her, she felt the peculiar vertigo of a woman who has just realized her obsession has weight—that it presses on other people, that it moves through the world, that it can enter rooms. For seven years the Cordelia Vane case had been hers in the way a fossil is hers: safely dead, beautifully preserved, entirely inert. Tonight it had moved. Something in the town's sealed past had shifted, and the shift had reached as far as a brass bolt on a delivery door.

She threw the bolt closed, tested it twice, and turned off the lamp. In the sudden dark, the rain sounded louder, and beneath it—she couldn't be sure, she told herself she couldn't be sure—she thought she heard, faint and brief, a footstep on the gravel outside, retreating toward the lane.

She stood in the blackness until her eyes adjusted, until the shapes of shelves and cabinets reassembled themselves around her, until she was certain the sound had been the sea, or a branch, or nothing at all.

Then she took the folder out of the cabinet again, tucked it inside her coat against her chest, and let herself out the front, locking the door behind her with hands that were perfectly steady and perfectly cold.

Whatever the ledger recorded, she would read all of it. But tomorrow she would do it somewhere with more than one exit—and the first person she needed to talk to, whether she wanted to or not, was Harriet Okafor, who had kept house for Eleanor Vane for thirty years, and who had once told Beatrix, laughing over the garden fence, that *Mrs. Vane never wrote anything down, dear, except what she wanted found.*

* * *

The walk home took eleven minutes along the cliff path, and Beatrix spent all of them replaying the bolt. The streetlamps of Saltmere Bay were sparse and amber, spaced like afterthoughts, and the rain had thinned to a drifting mist that beaded on her coat and caught the light. The town slept the way it always slept—deeply, trustingly, on top of its secret. Lights burned in Harriet's kitchen window as Beatrix came down Harbor Lane, and she slowed, tempted, exhausted, wanting nothing so much as a warm room and someone to tell her she wasn't imagining things.

Not tonight. Tomorrow. With the ledger half-built and the bolt unexplained, she needed sleep more than comfort, and she needed to approach Harriet with a question already sharpened, because Harriet Okafor missed nothing and answered everything at her own pace, in her own season, like a woman who had spent thirty years learning that words, once given away, could not be recalled.

Beatrix climbed the stairs to her rooms above the chandlery, set the folder on the kitchen table, and weighted it with the iron horseshoe she used as a paperweight—a joke of a gift from her mother, bought the summer before everything. She put the kettle on. She did not turn on the overhead light; the stove's blue ring and the harbor lights through the window were illumination enough.

And it was there, in that dimness, waiting for the water to boil, that she allowed herself the thing she'd been refusing all night: the full shape of what she'd found. Not the mechanics—the mechanics were safe, satisfying, a puzzle with edges—but the meaning. Eleven annotated tides bracketing the worst year in the town's history. A code built from scripture and seawater. A confession letter timed to a funeral. And now a bolt standing open in a locked room, as if the past had gotten up from its chair and begun, quietly, to walk around.

*Someone knows I'm reading it,* she thought. *Or someone thinks I might.*

The kettle began its low climb toward whistling. Beatrix wrapped her hands around a mug she didn't fill, and watched the fog move across the harbor, and understood—with the clarity that always came to her at the edge of exhaustion—that the exhilaration and the fear were not two feelings but one, the way a tide is one thing whether it's leaving or returning.

Outside, far down the lane, a car door closed. Softly. The way a person closes a door when they don't want to be counted.

Beatrix turned off the burner, sat down at the table with the folder beneath her palm, and did not sleep.
