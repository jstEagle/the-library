## 9. Harriet's Warning

Harriet Okafor had been awake since four, which was nothing new. Sleep had become a country she visited less and less as the years went on, a place with stricter border controls every winter. She lay in her narrow bed above the empty shopfront on Pell Street and listened to the gulls argue over the harbor's leavings, and she thought about the girl downstairs.

Not a girl anymore, of course. Beatrix Hartwell was thirty-four years old and had lived in the rooms above Harriet's house for seven years, paying her rent on the first of every month in cash envelopes left under the ceramic owl by the door, eating dinner alone at least five nights out of seven, and building—brick by patient brick, page by catalogued page—the archive that was going to get somebody killed.

Harriet had known it would come to this. She had known it the way you know weather is coming: not from any single sign but from the whole pressure of the air, the ache in old joints, the way the light goes strange before a storm. She had watched Beatrix for seven years the way one watches a slow tide creep up a seawall—not alarmed at first, then curious, then quietly, thoroughly afraid.

She got up at half past five because lying still had stopped helping. She put the kettle on. She took down the blue enamel pot she used only for this—one purpose, one pot, a private ceremony—and measured out the loose rooibos that reminded her of her mother's kitchen in Lagos, though her mother had drunk it sweet enough to stand a spoon in and Harriet took hers bitter as penance.

Then she stood at her kitchen window and looked across the yard at the lit window of Beatrix's flat, where a lamp had been burning since before dawn, and made the decision she had been circling for three days like a moth around a bulb.

She would tell her.

God help her, she would tell her some of it.

* * *

The trouble with telling, Harriet knew, was that telling was never just telling. Telling was choosing which silence to end, and every silence she kept had been bought and paid for. She was seventy-one years old and she had spent forty-two of those years in the employ or the orbit of the Vane family, and a person did not spend four decades inside a house like Vane House without learning that the walls were load-bearing with secrets. You didn't pull one out to look at it. You learned its weight, you walked around it, and if you were smart—if you wanted to keep your job, your references, your standing in a town where the Vanes' name opened doors and closed mouths—you carried your knowledge the way you carried a full tray through a crowded room: carefully, close to the body, eyes forward, never spilling.

She had been twenty-nine when Eleanor Vane hired her. Young enough to be flattered, old enough to be useful, Black enough in Saltmere Bay in 1982 to be grateful for steady work and expected to be silent in it. She had polished silver that Cordelia would later hide in odd places, had laid out Eleanor's mourning clothes twice before she was forty, had learned to read Mrs. Vane's moods from the angle of her cane against the hall table. And she had been there, in the summer of 1998, in the long hot weeks when Cordelia Vane stopped coming down to breakfast and the house filled with a hush so complete that Harriet could hear the ice melting in the pitcher on the sideboard.

She had seen things that summer. Small things. A trunk carried down to the pier at dusk by two men who moved like men carrying something heavier than luggage. Eleanor Vane standing at the seawall in her good coat in July, watching the water with an expression Harriet had never found a word for and had spent twenty-six years not looking for one. Gerald Pike's car parked below the boathouse at hours when lawyers have no business being anywhere.

And afterward, when the town tore itself apart looking for the heiress and the police came with their questions and their dredges, Harriet had said what she'd been told to say and nothing else, and a month later there had been an envelope in her pay packet thicker than any month's wages justified, and Eleanor Vane had looked at her across the morning room and said, "You have a daughter in Accra who wants for nothing, I hope," and that had been that. That had been twenty-six years of that.

Loyalty, Harriet thought now, rinsing the pot though it was clean, is a word people use when they mean fear wearing its Sunday clothes. She had told herself for decades that she'd kept silent out of loyalty. Out of discretion. Out of the professional pride of a woman who knew how to keep a great house running and understood that a household's secrets were part of its maintenance, like the boiler, like the roof. But she had banked the money. She had sent it to Accra. She had built a life on top of the silence the way the pier was built on top of the water, pilings driven down into something dark, and she had never once, in all these years, gone back at low tide to look at what held her up.

Until the letters started arriving. Until the funeral. Until the girl upstairs started pulling threads.

* * *

At seven, Harriet crossed the yard with a covered dish of jollof-spiced rice and smoked fish she had no plausible reason to have made at six in the morning except as pretext, because in Saltmere Bay you did not knock on a neighbor's door to deliver a warning; you knocked to deliver food, and the warning rode along in the basket like a stowaway.

Beatrix answered on the second knock, wrapped in a gray cardigan, hair pinned up with whatever had been nearest—a pencil, Harriet noted, a pencil through the bun, which meant the girl had been working since before dawn and hadn't planned to see anyone. Her eyes had the particular brightness Harriet had learned to dread over the past week: the brightness of a mind running too fast on too little sleep, chasing something down a dark corridor with a candle.

"Mrs. Okafor. You didn't have to—"

"Rice doesn't keep," Harriet said, sweeping past her into the kitchen, which was a liberty she took precisely once a year and had decided this was the day for it. The flat smelled of cold coffee and paper. Papers everywhere—that was new, that was worse. They fanned across the table like a hand of terrible cards, and Harriet's eye, trained by forty years of household management to inventory a room in three seconds, caught the tide almanac open in the center, weighted at both pages with teacups, and caught also the fresh ink of marginal notes in a small precise hand that was not Eleanor Vane's.

So. The almanacs. Harriet set the dish on the stove out of habit, straightening her back with a small crack, and turned to face the girl.

"You've been in Eleanor's books," she said.

It was not a question, and Beatrix didn't treat it as one. She stood very still, the way people do when they've been caught at something and are calculating whether denial is worth the effort. Then, because she was fundamentally honest in a way that both endeared her to Harriet and would someday be the death of her, she said, "You know about the almanacs."

"I dusted them for thirty years." Harriet lowered herself into the kitchen chair that Beatrix pulled out for her, accepting it because her knees accepted things whether she willed them or not. "Mrs. Vane wrote in the margins of everything. Grocery lists, hymnals, the TV Guide. If a piece of paper sat still long enough in that house, she annotated it." She folded her hands on the table, over the grain of wood she could feel through her palms, and made herself look at Beatrix directly. "I'm going to ask you something, and I want you to answer me plain, because I'm an old woman and I haven't time for the dance. What did the letter say?"

The change in the girl's face was immediate and total—the color leaving it not all at once but in stages, like a tide going out, until what remained was pale and watchful. "What letter?"

"Child." Harriet said it gently. "I kept the Vane house for three decades. I know what a guilty face looks like from every angle, including my own in the mirror. There's a letter. It came after the funeral. And whatever's in it has you reading dead women's tide tables at five in the morning." She paused. "And it has you sleeping with the door locked, which you never used to do, because I can hear the bolt from my kitchen and I've heard it every night this week."

The clock on Beatrix's shelf ticked. Somewhere outside a dog was barking at the sea, the way dogs in Saltmere Bay always barked at the sea, as if the sea owed them money.

"It named things," Beatrix said finally, and her voice was low and level, the voice of someone who has decided that if she must fall she will fall in order. "Details from the investigation that were never released. Sealed evidence. Things only—" She stopped herself, but Harriet finished it for her, silently, in the old fluency of the house: *things only the killer could know.* "And the postmark was dated the day after Eleanor's funeral. Which is impossible, unless—"

"Unless it was arranged to be sent on his death," Harriet said. "Whoever he was."

Beatrix stared at her. "You think it was a man."

"I think Gerald Pike died in March," Harriet said, "and I think Eleanor Vane was buried eleven days ago, and I think between those two facts there is a story, and I am here to tell you to stop digging for it." She heard her own voice go hard, harder than she intended, the voice she'd used on scullery maids and once, memorably, on a journalist who'd tried to slip a camera past her at Cordelia's memorial service. "That's why I brought the rice. That's why I'm sitting in your kitchen at seven in the morning. Stop."

* * *

But the girl had that quality Harriet had seen in her from the first week—the quality that had made Harriet take her in as a lodger in the first place, though she'd told herself at the time it was about the rent. Beatrix Hartwell did not stop. She was not defiant about it; defiance was loud and Beatrix was quiet as dust. She simply absorbed a command the way sand absorbs water, and remained, and continued, and you woke one morning to find she had grown straight through your instruction like grass through pavement.

"Why?" Beatrix asked now. Not *no*, Harriet noticed. Not argument. Just *why*, asked with a terrible gentle patience, leaning forward with her hands wrapped around a mug of coffee gone cold hours ago. "Why should I stop, Mrs. Okafor? Why does it frighten you?"

Because I know what they do, Harriet thought. Because I have taken their money for my silence and I know exactly what the silence costs and what the speaking would cost, and the difference between those two prices is the rest of my life. Because in 1998 I watched a trunk go down that pier and I have spent twenty-six years telling myself it was furniture, it was linens, it was anything, and I cannot carry one more night of knowing what I know while also carrying the weight of what happens to you.

What she said was: "Because Eleanor burned her papers."

The words came out of her before she had fully decided to release them, the way a cork comes out of a bottle pressed too long. Beatrix went motionless. Even her breathing seemed to pause.

"Three weeks before she died," Harriet continued, staring at the table because she could not stare at the girl, "she had me build a fire in the study grate. In June. The house was warm as an oven and she had me lay a fire, and then she sent me out and I stood in the corridor and I heard paper after paper go in. Hours, child. It went on for hours. And when she rang for me to clear away, the grate was ash to the bricks, and she was sitting in her chair with her hands folded like a woman who has finished a long chore, and she said to me—" Harriet's throat tightened around it, the sentence she had carried like a stone in her shoe for months. "She said, 'That's the estate settled, Harriet. Let them dig through *that*.'"

Silence. The dog outside had given up. Down in the harbor a boat engine coughed and caught, the sound carrying across the water with terrible clarity in the early air.

"She knew," Beatrix said softly. "She knew someone would come looking."

"Eleanor Vane knew everything," Harriet said. "That was the whole of her. She wasn't a cruel woman, whatever the town says—cruelty takes passion and she had none, not for people. She was a careful woman. She ran that family like a ledger, and when a line in the ledger threatened the balance, she struck it through and burned the page." Harriet lifted her eyes at last. "And you are reading the margins of her arithmetic, and asking questions in a town where questions have a way of getting answered by people who don't like them. Ambrose Vane has already been to see you, hasn't he."

It wasn't a question either. Beatrix's silence confirmed it, and Harriet felt the old fear shift in her chest like ballast.

"He came asking whether I'd received anything of Eleanor's," Beatrix admitted. "He was very charming about it."

"Ambrose is always charming. His grandmother taught him that charm is cheaper than honesty and holds better in salt air." Harriet pushed herself up from the chair, joints protesting, and went to the window. From here she could see the harbor, gray-blue in the early light, and the old pier extending into it like a finger pointing at something it refused to name. Forty-two years of looking at that pier. Twenty-six years of not looking at it. "I liked you from the start," she said, to the glass. "Quiet girl, paid on time, kept to herself. I told myself you were just another lodger. But I knew. I knew when you asked me in your first month here—do you remember?—you asked me, so casually, over the garden fence, whether I'd ever worked for the Vanes. And I lied to you. I said briefly, ages ago, nothing worth remembering."

Beatrix said nothing. Behind her, Harriet could hear the faint sound of her sitting down slowly, as if her legs had reconsidered their employment.

"And every year since, you've asked a little more, and I've lied a little more, and we've built this whole arrangement on my lies the way the town's built on its pretty story about a runaway heiress." Harriet turned. The light from the window fell across her and she let it, wanting the girl to see her face plainly, all of it, the age and the fear and the thing underneath the fear that might, if you squinted, be love—for this strange solitary tenant, yes, but more than that: for the truth itself, starved all these years, thin as a winter bird. "I'm telling you to stop because stopping is what keeps people alive in this town. I'm telling you Eleanor burned her papers because I need you to understand what kind of woman could make even her ashes lie. And I'm telling you—" and here her voice failed her for a moment, and she steadied it with the memory of her mother's kitchen, sweet tea and loud singing and a world where silence wasn't currency, "—I'm telling you because if something happens to you in that archive of yours, I will have to live with knowing I saw it coming and said nothing. Again."

The word *again* hung in the kitchen like smoke.

Beatrix rose. For a moment Harriet thought the girl would reach for her hand, and braced herself against pity, which she could not afford. But Beatrix only stood there, pencil still skewering her bun, cardigan buttoned wrong, looking at Harriet with an expression of such naked, unguarded hunger for the truth that Harriet felt it like a draft under a door.

"The trunk," Beatrix said quietly.

Harriet's heart stopped, started again, wrong. "What?"

"On the pier. In 1998. You saw it—I can see it in your face, Mrs. Okafor, I've been reading faces in archives for seven years and yours is the clearest document in this town." Beatrix's voice stayed soft, stayed almost tender, which was somehow worse than if she'd shouted. "Two men carried it down at dusk. You've known for twenty-six years. Haven't you."

The kitchen was very warm suddenly, or very cold; Harriet couldn't have said which. She gathered her composure the way she had once gathered scattered silverware off a dropped tray, piece by piece, smiling at nothing. "You should eat the rice before it clots," she said, moving toward the door. "And you should burn that letter, and put Eleanor's books back on the shelf, and find yourself a case with a body in it, because bodies at least stay buried."

At the threshold she paused, one hand on the frame, and did not turn around.

"There's a page missing from the second almanac," she said. "August 1998. Torn out clean. Eleanor tore it herself—I found the stub when I was dusting, days after Cordelia went into the water, and I asked her about it, and she looked at me the way she looked at the help when the help had seen too much, and she said, 'Some tides aren't worth recording, Harriet.'" 

She stepped out into the yard, into the brightening morning, and behind her she heard Beatrix's chair scrape back from the table—heard the girl already moving, already reaching for the almanac, already refusing, bless her stubborn heart, to stop.

Harriet walked home slowly across the wet grass, an old woman carrying a dish that had never been touched, and told herself that she had done what she could.

She told herself that all the way to her door.

It was only when she was inside, bolting it—when had she started bolting it?—that she allowed herself to admit the other thing, the thing she had circled all morning and never landed on: that she had not warned Beatrix off at all. That a warning says *danger ahead*. What she had given the girl was a map. The missing page. The torn stub. The date.

Some tides aren't worth recording.

Harriet Okafor put the kettle on again, though she had no thirst, and listened to it climb toward boiling, and understood that she had just chosen a side—quietly, in a kitchen, over cold rice—in a war she had spent twenty-six years pretending wasn't happening.

Upstairs, through the wall, she could hear the scratch of a pencil moving fast across paper.
