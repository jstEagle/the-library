## 10. Chapter and Verse

The rain came in off the bay around four in the morning, and Beatrix heard it before she was fully awake—a soft, insistent ticking against the window of her attic room, like someone patiently tapping out a message in code. She lay still for a moment in the gray half-light, listening, and thought: *everything is a code if you're willing to sit with it long enough.*

She had fallen asleep at her desk again, cheek against the open tide almanac, a pen still curled in her right hand. The ink had transferred to her cheek; she could feel it when she pressed her fingers there, a small dark smudge she'd carry through the day like a bruise she'd earned. On the page beneath her face, Eleanor Vane's marginalia waited—those small, precise annotations in faded pencil, dates and tides and numbers that had meant nothing for thirty years and now meant everything.

Beatrix sat up, stretched until her spine crackled, and looked at the wall.

She had covered it overnight. Every ledger entry she'd decoded from the almanacs was pinned there on an index card, arranged chronologically: *March 14, 1971 — 4s 2d. June 2, 1973 — 11s. September 9, 1976 — 7s 6d.* Dozens of them, spanning three decades, each one a transaction Eleanor Vane had recorded in the only cipher she could trust—the sea itself. The amounts were modest, irregular, almost insultingly small for the scale of what they seemed to buy. Silence, presumably. Loyalty. The slow, patient purchase of a town's willingness not to look too closely at its own founding family.

But numbers alone were just noise. A ledger needed structure. It needed somewhere to *live*.

And Eleanor Vane, Beatrix thought as she pulled on a sweater and descended the narrow stairs into the cold, had spent her whole life telling people exactly where that was. She'd simply done it in the language of the dead.

* * *

The Saltmere Bay churchyard sat on the bluff above the harbor, a quarter mile from the archive, and Beatrix had walked past its iron gate perhaps a thousand times without ever truly entering. That was the discipline of her life: she catalogued everything and touched nothing. The graveyard was part of the town's furniture, like the war memorial and the fish-and-chip shop and the pier with its rotting stanchions. You didn't examine furniture. You sat on it, you leaned against it, you forgot it was there.

This morning she pushed the gate open and heard it groan on hinges that hadn't been oiled since before Cordelia Vane disappeared.

The Vane plot occupied the highest ground in the churchyard, naturally—the family had claimed the view of their own harbor even in death. Six granite headstones in two rows, lichen-furred, the older ones leaning eastward as if craning toward the water. Beatrix stood at the low iron rail that enclosed them and took out her notebook, and for a long moment she simply read.

*Edmund Vane, 1901–1968. "The rock of our house."*

*Margaret Vane, 1905–1959. "A light upon the waters."*

*Alistair Vane, 1928–1990. "He kept the faith."*

*Eleanor Vane, 1930–2024. "She watched over all."*

*Cordelia Vane, 1974–1998. "The sea keeps what it loves."*

And one more, smaller, set apart at the edge of the plot—an infant, unnamed, 1962. *"Suffer the little children."*

Beatrix's breath fogged in the cold air. She read them again. And again. And on the fourth pass, something in her chest went tight and electric, because she wasn't reading epitaphs anymore. She was reading a key.

*"The rock of our house."* Rock. In the almanac's marginalia, Eleanor had used a small triangular symbol beside certain entries—a symbol Beatrix had logged weeks ago as meaningless ornamentation. But triangles were how surveyors marked elevation. How sailors marked fixed points. *Rock.* Fixed ground.

*"A light upon the waters."* Light. There had been a notation in the margins shaped like a tiny star or asterisk, recurring on eleven separate dates across the decades.

*"He kept the faith."* Faith. And there—Beatrix's hand was shaking now, badly enough that she had to brace the notebook against the rail—there was a third symbol, a small cross-like mark, appearing beside exactly those entries whose amounts ended in sixpence. She'd assumed it was shorthand for currency. It wasn't currency. It was a *category*. It was a word.

Eleanor hadn't written a ledger in numbers alone. She'd written it in scripture—or rather, in the secular scripture of her own family's stones. Each epitaph was a heading. Each symbol in the margin told you which grave the entry belonged under. And the verse inscriptions—

Beatrix flipped back through her notes, through pages of transcriptions, until she found what she was looking for: the full text carved beneath each name. Not just the epitaph lines but the verses beneath them, the little scriptural fragments the Vanes had chosen for themselves, the kind of thing no one ever read twice.

Under Edmund: *"Set thine house in order, for thou shalt die and not live."*

Under Margaret: *"They that go down to the sea in ships."*

Under Alistair: *"Well done, thou good and faithful servant."*

Under the infant: *"Of such is the kingdom of heaven."*

Dates. They were all dates. Beatrix knew this because she'd spent three years building a reference library of nothing else—every date of significance in the Vane family history, cross-indexed, alphabetized, color-coded. Edmund Vane had died on the ninth of November. *"Set thine house in order"*—the ordering of the house, the settling of the estate. Margaret had died at sea, or near enough; her body had been recovered off the point on the second of June. *"They that go down to the sea."* Alistair had died on the feast of some saint or other—Beatrix would have to check which—but *"faithful servant"* rang of stewardship, of accounts rendered.

Each verse was a date wearing a disguise. And each ledger entry, tagged by symbol, pointed to a grave—and therefore to a date—and therefore to an *event*.

She worked standing at the rail in the rain for nearly two hours, oblivious to the wet soaking through her sweater, matching entry to stone to date. By the time the church bell struck nine, the architecture of it had risen whole in her notebook: a payment schedule spanning thirty years, organized by the dead, indexed by the dead, hidden in the most public place in Saltmere Bay. Anyone could have walked in here and read it. Everyone had walked in here and read it, and seen nothing but grief.

That was the genius of it, and the obscenity. Eleanor Vane had built her conspiracy a cathedral and then invited the whole town to admire it.

* * *

By noon the rain had cleared and Beatrix had colonized her usual corner table at the Kettle & Anchor, the café on the harbor road where the owner had long ago stopped trying to move her from the seat by the window. Her notebook lay open between her teacup and a scone going stale, and she was deep in the arithmetic when the bell above the door chimed and Milo Ferreira came in out of the cold, school bag slung over one shoulder, hair dripping.

"You look," he said, dropping into the chair opposite her without asking, "like you haven't slept."

"I slept fine."

"Your cheek has pen on it. Also you're talking to a notebook."

Beatrix touched her face, remembered the smudge, and decided not to dignify it. Milo was seventeen, a volunteer at the records office two afternoons a week, and the closest thing she had to a colleague—which was to say, the only person in Saltmere Bay who asked questions about her work and then stayed to hear the answers. He ordered hot chocolate he was slightly too old for and leaned over her notebook with the unselfconscious curiosity of someone who hadn't yet learned that curiosity was dangerous.

"Gravestones?" he said.

"Gravestones." She turned the notebook toward him. "Tell me what you see."

Milo studied it the way he studied everything—thoroughly, silently, his brow furrowing. "Payments," he said finally. "Same names coming up over and over. Okafor—that's your landlady, isn't it? Harriet Okafor?"

Beatrix went very still. "You know Harriet?"

"Everyone knows Harriet. She does the flowers for the church." He tapped the page. "There's a payment here every year on the same week in April. Going back to 1988. And these—" his finger moved down the column, "—these ones stop in '98. All of them. Everything after September 1998 is different. Smaller. Fewer names."

September 1998. The month the sea was supposed to have taken Cordelia Vane.

"Milo," Beatrix said carefully, "when you're at the records office. The burial registers. The gravestone inscription books. Has anyone ever asked to see them? Recently, I mean. Before me."

He shrugged. "Tourists, sometimes. Genealogy people. Why?"

"No reason." She closed the notebook, and the motion was perhaps too quick, because Milo's eyes narrowed with the particular shrewdness of teenagers who are lied to constantly and have developed an ear for it. "Eat your chocolate."

But her mind was already moving past him, out the fogged window, across the harbor to the churchyard on the bluff. Because here was the thing she hadn't said aloud, the thing that had been gathering weight in her chest all morning like storm pressure: the payments stopped changing in September 1998, but they didn't stop. Whatever happened on the night Cordelia Vane vanished, it hadn't ended the arrangement. It had *restructured* it. Smaller sums. Fewer recipients. A conspiracy tightening its belt, closing ranks, paying only those who still needed paying.

And one name appeared in the post-1998 entries that had never appeared before. Not a name, precisely—a symbol. The triangle. *The rock of our house.* Edmund Vane's stone. Except Edmund had been dead for thirty years by then, and dead men don't draw salaries.

Which meant the triangle didn't point to Edmund at all. It pointed to whatever Edmund represented. The house itself. The estate. Or—

Or whoever held the estate's keys. And in Saltmere Bay, for forty years, the man who held the estate's keys, who drafted its wills, who probated its deaths, who signed its every legal breath, was Gerald Pike, solicitor, of Pike & Vane.

Beatrix stared at the rain-washed harbor and felt the shape of the thing turning over in her mind like a body shifting under a sheet.

* * *

She went back to the churchyard at dusk, when the light went long and amber and the stones threw shadows like sundial hands. She wanted to check one last correspondence, one she'd been avoiding all day because she already knew what it would show, and knowing it would make the whole structure real in a way that couldn't be undone.

Cordelia's stone. *"The sea keeps what it loves."*

No verse beneath it. No scripture. Just that line, elegant and terrible, chosen—Beatrix had always assumed—by a grieving mother.

But Eleanor Vane never did anything without a system. Beatrix crouched at the base of the stone, ran her fingers along the lower edge where the moss grew thickest, and found what she was looking for: a date. Small, incised, easy to miss beneath twenty-six years of lichen. Not a death date. An installation date—the day the stone was set.

The seventeenth of March, 1999.

Six months after the disappearance. Which was normal; stones took time. But she checked her ledger cards anyway, heart hammering, and there it was: an entry dated the seventeenth of March, 1999. Tagged with the star. *A light upon the waters.* Margaret's symbol. Margaret's stone. Margaret's date.

Except Margaret had been dead since 1959, and this payment was made forty years after her burial, on the exact day her granddaughter's headstone was installed.

It wasn't a coincidence. Nothing in Eleanor's system was a coincidence. The star didn't mean Margaret—it meant *the sea*, the way Margaret's verse meant the sea, the way the whole family mythologized the water that made them rich. Payments tagged with the star were payments for the sea's silence. For the story of drowning. For the fiction everyone in Saltmere Bay had agreed to believe because the truth was unthinkable.

And the largest single entry in the entire ledger—larger than any annual payment, larger than anything before or since—was tagged with the star, and dated the nineteenth of September, 1998.

The night Cordelia Vane walked down to the ferry and, according to thirty years of consensus, sailed away forever.

Beatrix knelt there in the fading light with her hand flat against the cold granite, and the churchyard settled around her—not peaceful, the way graveyards were supposed to be, but *architectural*, load-bearing, every stone a pillar in a structure built to hold up a lie. She had spent years thinking of the case as a hole in the record, an absence, a girl-shaped gap in the town's memory. Now she understood it was the opposite. It was a building. Someone had designed it, funded it, maintained it, paid its contractors on schedule for decades.

And buildings, unlike absences, could be entered. Could be mapped. Could be brought down.

She stood, knees stiff with cold, and that was when she noticed the fresh flowers.

Someone had left white chrysanthemums at the base of Cordelia's stone—fresh, stems cut that morning, rain-beads still clinging to the petals. Beatrix looked at them for a long moment. Then she looked at the churchyard gate, at the lane beyond it, at the empty street running down toward the harbor where the lamps were just flickering on.

Fresh flowers meant a visitor. Today. Perhaps within the hour.

She walked home fast, taking the cliff path in the dark, and only let herself breathe once she was inside with the door bolted. Upstairs, she pinned a new card to the center of her wall, between the first entry and the last:

*19 Sept 1998 — ★ — the largest payment of all.*

Then she wrote beneath it, in letters that shook only slightly:

*Gerald Pike knows what the star bought. Find out why Harriet Okafor's name is in this book—and why she's never once mentioned it.*
