## 12. Episode Ninety-One

The microphone was a birthday gift from Harriet, three years ago now, and it still had the faint smell of the cardboard it had arrived in. Beatrix kept it on the second shelf of her archive, between the box labeled VANE, E. — FINANCIAL and the one labeled VANE, C. — WITNESS STATEMENTS, because she liked the absurdity of it: a piece of modern technology wedged into a mausoleum of paper. Tonight she took it down, blew a speck of dust from the pop filter, and set it on the desk beside the tide almanac with the cracked leather spine.

She had been recording *The Alphabet of the Dead* for four years. Ninety episodes. She knew the number the way other people knew their own phone number—by heart, without thinking, without feeling. Ninety episodes of careful, bloodless narration. Ninety episodes in which she had laid out the facts of Cordelia Vane's disappearance like a pathologist lays out organs: labeled, weighed, and drained of everything that had once made them alive. The town listened the way a town listens to a story it already knows the ending to—politely, half asleep, the way you listen to rain on a roof you've stopped hearing.

Episode ninety-one would not be careful. Episode ninety-one would be a hook dropped into dark water, and Beatrix would be both the fisherman and the bait.

She sat down in the old office chair that sighed under her weight and pulled the almanac toward her. The marginalia crawled along its edges in two hands—one she now knew was Eleanor Vane's, tight and vertical, a hand that pressed hard enough to emboss the paper; and one that made her stomach turn every time she looked at it, because it was loose, looping, familiar in the way a scar is familiar. Her mother's handwriting. Her mother's hand, in Eleanor Vane's tide tables, in the margins of the case Beatrix had spent seven years cataloguing from the outside, believing herself a stranger to it.

She hadn't told anyone about that yet. Not Harriet. Not Milo. Not the empty air of her office, which had lately felt less like solitude and more like surveillance.

*Later,* she told herself, the way she had been telling herself for six days. *First the bait. Then the wound.*

She switched on the microphone. The red light came on like a small, patient eye.

* * *

"Good evening, Saltmere Bay. This is Beatrix Hartwell, and this is Episode Ninety-One of *The Alphabet of the Dead*."

Her voice came out steadier than she felt. She had learned that trick years ago—the trick of sounding calm into a machine, of letting the microphone believe in you even when you didn't. It was a form of acting, and she was, if nothing else, a woman who had spent her whole life performing the role of someone unremarkable.

"For ninety episodes, we have told this story the way it was given to us. A heiress walks out of Vane House on the last night of August, 1998, to catch the evening ferry to the mainland. She never arrives. Her handbag is found on the ferry dock, upright, dry, positioned—positioned, I want you to hear that word again—*positioned* on the bench as though set down by someone with time to spare. The sea is blamed. The sea, as always, is blamed. And for twenty-seven years, Saltmere Bay has told itself a story about the ocean's appetite, because it is easier to fear the water than to look at the people standing on the shore."

She paused. In the silence she could hear the building around her: the tick of the radiator, the wind worrying the loose flashing above the records office window, and—she was almost sure—nothing else. No footstep in the hall. No car in the lot. She had been listening for those sounds all week, ever since she'd noticed the archive boxes shifted a quarter-inch out of her own alignment, and the listening had changed the texture of every hour. She had lived alone so long that solitude had been a coat she wore without thinking. Now it was a coat with someone possibly standing behind her.

"But tonight," she continued, "I'm not going to tell you the story we were given. Tonight I want to tell you about a document."

She let that sit. Then, deliberately, she reached across the desk and let her fingers rest on the almanac—not to open it, not to show anything, just to touch it, so that the faint rasp of her skin on cracked leather would be audible on the recording. A sound with no explanation. An invitation to lean in.

"Those of you who've listened since Episode Twelve will remember the tide almanacs donated to the town archive from the Vane estate—eleven volumes, 1987 through 1998, annotated in the matriarch Eleanor Vane's own hand. I described them then as 'a record of a woman who trusted the sea's arithmetic more than she trusted any person in her family.' I stand by that. But I said something else in that episode that I now need to correct. I said the margins were empty of meaning. That the notations—dates, verse references, small numerals—were the idle marks of a pious woman cross-referencing her Bible against the moon."

She stopped the recording there, the first time. She always stopped. She had learned in ninety episodes that the pause was where the listener leaned forward, and she had never before used that lean as a weapon. Her heart was going hard enough that she wondered if the microphone would pick it up, and then she decided she hoped it would. Let the listener hear a heartbeat. Let him hear that she was not made of paper.

"I need to correct it," she said again, when the red light returned, "because the margins are not idle. The dates in those margins correspond to low tides. The tides correspond to numbers. And the numbers—" she let the sentence die, let it hang in the dark of the recording the way a body hangs in dark water, "—the numbers, I believe, are a ledger. A private ledger of payments. Payments made, over a period of at least nine years, to a person or persons unknown, in amounts that begin at two thousand dollars and end at amounts I am not yet prepared to say aloud."

This was a lie, and she said it fluently. She was perfectly prepared to say the amounts aloud. She wanted him to think she wasn't—wanted him to think there was still a door she hadn't opened, so that he would come to close it himself.

"Now, I know how this sounds. I know what some of you are thinking—that the archivist has finally spent too many winters alone with dead women's papers. I've heard the joke. *Beatrix and her ghosts.* It's a good joke. I've laughed at it myself, at the counter of the Saltmere Diner, in the tone of a woman who agrees she is harmless." Her mouth went dry and she reached for the tea, gone cold, and drank it anyway. "But here is what I will say, and then I will say no more tonight."

She looked at the window. The glass had gone black with the hour, and in it her own reflection sat ghosted over the parking lot's single sodium lamp—a thin woman at a desk, holding a dead man's secret like a lit match.

"Three weeks ago, this archive received a letter. I will not describe the letter. I will not say when it was postmarked, though those of you with sharp memories and sharper calendars may already be doing arithmetic involving a certain funeral. I will say only this: whoever wrote it knew things that were never in any newspaper. Things that were sealed. Things that could only be known by someone who stood, in August of 1998, on the pier at Vane Point, in the dark, watching."

The radiator ticked. Somewhere down the street a dog began to bark, three sharp notes, and stopped.

"For twenty-seven years, this town has asked what the sea took. I am no longer asking that question. I am asking a better one. I am asking what the sea was *given*."

She let the silence run for a full five seconds—she counted them, thumb ticking against forefinger—and then she closed with the formula she had used ninety times, the small liturgy that had become the only ritual in her life besides alphabetizing: "This has been Episode Ninety-One of *The Alphabet of the Dead*. I am Beatrix Hartwell, and the dead of Saltmere Bay are alphabetized, cross-referenced, and patient. Good night."

She stopped the recording. The red light went out, and the office seemed to exhale.

* * *

She almost didn't upload it.

That was the truth of the hour after—the truth she would remember later, in the mud and the moonlight and the aftermath, when people asked her whether she had understood what she was doing. She had understood. But understanding is not the same as being unafraid, and for forty minutes she sat with her finger hovering over the trackpad while the file sat rendered on her screen, ninety-one minutes of her voice and her gamble, waiting.

Because she knew what she was inviting. She had spent the last week reconstructing, piece by piece, the shape of the thing that had been in her office—the boxes shifted, the drawer that stuck differently now, the faint smell of cigarette smoke that didn't belong to her or to Harriet or to anyone she'd invited in. Someone had been here. Someone had read her shelves the way she read everyone else's papers, and had left no note, which was itself a note: *I can, and I will, whenever I choose.*

If she uploaded the episode, she was no longer the archivist. She was the lure. And lures, she knew—she had grown up on a coast, she knew the water—lures get bitten and lures get swallowed, and sometimes the fisherman's line goes taut and sometimes the rod goes into the water after it, and the fisherman with it.

Her mother's handwriting sat in the margin of the open almanac, looping and patient. *Low water, St. Erasmus 3:41, verse as before.* As before. As if it were a habit. As if her mother had sat in some room—this room? a kitchen? Eleanor Vane's parlor?—year after year, writing down the arithmetic of payments, and had never once, in all those years, told her daughter anything at all.

Beatrix looked at that handwriting and felt the fear rearrange itself into something with an edge. It was not courage, exactly. It was closer to anger that had finally found a place to stand. Her whole life had been organized around a principle she had never said aloud: *keep small, keep quiet, keep the doors of yourself closed so nothing can get in and nothing can be taken out.* Her mother had kept her small for a reason. Her mother had been protecting her—from the Vanes, from whatever this was, from the reach of people who shifted boxes in the night. And the protection had cost her the only thing Beatrix had ever actually wanted, which was the truth about her own life.

Well. The protection had failed. Someone had already been in the office. The small life had already been entered, searched, and left standing. So the choice was not between safety and risk. The choice was between being hunted and being hunted *on her own terms*.

She clicked upload.

The progress bar filled in four seconds, which struck her as obscene—all those years of the case, all those decades of the town's silence, dispatched into the world in the time it took to pour a cup of tea. The episode went live at 9:47 on a Thursday night, into the phones of a town of four thousand people, most of whom would listen in their kitchens while doing dishes, half attending, the way they always had.

She gave them an hour. She washed her one mug, straightened the boxes on the second shelf—VANE, E. — FINANCIAL; VANE, C. — WITNESS STATEMENTS; the microphone back in its place, because even now, especially now, she could not bear a misaligned shelf—and she watched the window and the lot and the dark shape of the harbor beyond, where the mast lights rocked in their long slow pendulum.

At 10:52, her phone lit up.

Milo: *did you just say PIER on the podcast?? beatrix. BEATRIX. call me back.*

At 10:53, a comment appeared on the episode's page, from an account with no picture and a string of numbers for a name: *Careful, miss. Tide tables are for sailors.*

She read it twice. Her first feeling was not fear. Her first feeling was a cold, bright, almost joyful certainty: *You listened. You're listening right now.* The bait was in the water and something had already come up from the deep to mouth it.

At 11:15, Harriet knocked—three soft raps, the landlady's knock, the one that meant *I know you're awake, I can see your light.* Beatrix opened the door to find the old woman in her coat, though it was thirty feet between their doors, holding her keys in her fist like a weapon she'd forgotten how to use.

"I heard it," Harriet said. Her voice was low and cracked. "Everyone's heard it. The whole town's going to be talking by morning." She looked past Beatrix into the office, at the almanac lying open on the desk, and something in her face folded. "Girl, what did you find?"

"A ledger," Beatrix said. She was tired of the shape of the lie, tired of carrying it alone up all these stairs. "Payments, Harriet. Twenty-seven years of payments. And I think I know who was paid, and I think I know why, and I think whoever it is just told me to be careful."

Harriet came in without being asked and stood over the almanac the way people stand over graves. For a long moment she said nothing. Then, very quietly, in the voice of a woman setting down a weight she had carried since 1998: "I need to tell you something about that pier. And you're not going to like what it costs you to hear it."

Beatrix's phone lit up on the desk between them. She glanced down, expecting Milo again, expecting the numberless account, expecting the town.

It was neither. It was an envelope icon. A new email, timestamped 11:17, from an address that had been created, according to the header, four minutes ago.

The subject line read: *The tide gives up what it takes.*

She hadn't even said that phrase on the episode. She had saved it. She had kept it in her mouth like a stone, for the next one, for the one after that—she had told no one, written it nowhere, kept it in no file.

She opened it. The message was one line long.

*You left something out of Episode Ninety-One, Miss Hartwell. So will I. But not for long.*
