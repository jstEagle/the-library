## 14. Cordelia's Summer

The box arrived on a Tuesday, which Beatrix would later think was fitting—Tuesdays were the days the archive received its donations, its dead men's papers and widows' photographs, the sediment of other people's lives settling quietly onto her shelves.

It came from a solicitor's office in Portsmouth, forwarded twice, wrapped in brown paper gone soft at the corners as though it had been handled by many reluctant hands. The return label read *Estate of E. Vane — per instructions*. Inside, cushioned in tissue that had yellowed to the color of weak tea, lay a diary bound in oxblood leather, its spine cracked into three pale ridges like old scars, and beneath it a single sheet of Eleanor Vane's stationery bearing four typed lines:

*These belonged to my daughter. I could never bring myself to destroy them. Perhaps someone should finally know her.*

Beatrix sat very still for a long moment after reading it. In twenty years of archiving this case, she had learned to distrust every artifact associated with Eleanor Vane—even, perhaps especially, the ones that looked like contrition. But the diary itself was another matter. The diary was Cordelia's own hand, unmediated, unedited, the only voice in the entire archive that had never been rehearsed for an audience.

She pulled on her white cotton gloves out of habit, then stopped. The gloves were for preservation. What she wanted now was contact.

She read until the light went amber in the windows and Harriet knocked twice on the stairwell door—their old signal that supper existed if she wanted it—and Beatrix heard herself call back, "Later," in a voice she didn't recognize, hoarse and young.

* * *

The diary began in June of 1998, eleven weeks before Cordelia Vane walked out of Saltmere Bay and into legend.

*June 3rd.* Grandmother has decided I am to be presented at the Regatta Gala like a prize marrow at the county fair. Ambrose is to escort me. I told her I'd rather be exhibited at the fish market; at least there the buyers are honest about what they intend to do with you.

Beatrix laughed—a startled, solitary sound in the empty archive. She had spent two decades assembling Cordelia Vane from other people's memories: the grieving fiancé who spoke of her in past-perfect platitudes, the schoolfriends who remembered a girl "quiet but lovely," the newspaper photographs where she smiled the tight smile of someone being photographed. None of them had mentioned that she was funny. None of them had dared.

*June 9th.* Gerald Pike came to tea today. He has the gift of making everyone feel he is their friend while committing nothing to paper. Grandmother adores him. I have begun keeping this book locked in my trunk because I no longer trust anyone in this house not to read what I write—not out of malice, exactly, but out of ownership. They believe they own me the way they own the house: something to be maintained, insured against loss, and eventually passed on.

*June 14th.* Swam at the point before anyone was up. The water was so cold it felt like being born. I thought: I could swim out and keep going, and they would say afterward how happy I seemed. That thought frightened me more than the water did.

Beatrix set the diary down. Her hands were not quite steady. She had catalogued hundreds of documents about Cordelia Vane's final summer—the tide charts, the ferry manifests, the statements taken in the weeks after her disappearance—but she had never once considered that the girl might have been unhappy in the ordinary, suffocating way that girls in gilded cages are unhappy. The case file had made her a mystery. The diary made her a twenty-three-year-old woman who swam too far out on purpose and scared herself.

She made tea in the archive's back room, the little electric kettle she wasn't strictly supposed to have, and carried the mug back to her desk, and kept reading.

* * *

*July 2nd.* Saw Mr. Whitcombe at the bank today about moving my trust. He was very kind and very useless, which I am learning are usually the same thing. Everything routes through Grandmother. Everything always has. I signed papers when I was nineteen without reading them because reading them would have been "ungrateful." I am done being grateful.

*July 5th.* Ambrose cornered me on the terrace. He wanted to know why I'd been asking questions about the estate. I said I was curious. He said curiosity in a woman of my position was "a charming flaw" and then he laughed, and I understood that he knows. Or suspects. Either way, the clock is running.

So it was true. Beatrix had found fragments of this over the years—a withdrawn petition here, a memo from a bank there—but the shape had never been clear. Now it assembled itself with terrible simplicity: Cordelia Vane had been trying to take herself out of the estate. Not add to it. Exit it entirely.

*July 11th.* Told Grandmother plainly tonight. I said I intended to dissolve the trust, sell my share, and leave. She didn't shout. She went very still, the way the house goes still, and then she said, "You don't know what you are, Cordelia. You're a Vane. Vanes don't leave." And I said, "Then I'll be the first," and left the room before she could see my hands shaking.

I meant it. God help me, I meant every word.

Beatrix read that entry three times. Outside, the harbor lights came on one by one, small gold coins dropped along the black water. Somewhere down the street a dog barked at nothing, and a car passed, and the ordinary world continued, indifferent to the fact that a dead girl had just spoken to her across twenty-six years with more clarity than any living witness ever had.

Vanes don't leave. It was almost funny. Because the official story—the story the town had been telling itself since September of 1998—was precisely that she had left. Run off, drowned, vanished; the verb changed depending on who was telling it, but the grammar stayed the same. Cordelia Vane leaves. The town had needed her to leave. It had built its whole mythology on the leaving.

And yet here, in ink, eleven weeks early: *I'll be the first.*

Unless, Beatrix thought, setting down her cold tea, someone had decided that the first Vane to leave would also be the last.

* * *

*July 19th.* Found the tide almanacs in the library, Grandfather's old ones, full of his marginal scribbles. Dates and figures in the margins—tide heights, I think, or times. There's a system to them I can't crack. I asked Gerald about them once and he went smooth as glass and said they were "just an old man's arithmetic." Nobody's arithmetic looks like that. I'm going to work it out. If the family keeps its secrets in numbers, I'll learn the numbers.

Beatrix's breath caught.

She knew those almanacs. They sat forty feet from her desk, donated to the archive in 2004 among Eleanor Vane's papers, their margins dense with cramped annotations that Beatrix had catalogued years ago as *miscellaneous tidal calculations, likely ornamental*. Ornamental. She had filed a cipher under *ornamental*, and Cordelia Vane had spotted it in an afternoon.

The girl had been smarter than all of them. Smarter than the family, smarter than the lawyer, smarter—Beatrix admitted it with a wince—than the archivist who'd had the same books in her possession for two decades and seen only weather records.

*July 24th.* Worked out part of it! The dates aren't random—they're low tides, the extreme ones, the spring tides when the water drops far enough to walk under the pier. Grandfather marked them like appointments. Under the pier. Why would a man mark under-the-pier tides like appointments unless something happens there worth scheduling?

I watched the pier tonight from the dunes. Nothing. But I felt watched myself, which is probably imagination. Probably.

Beatrix looked up at the ceiling as if the answer might be written there. The old man's arithmetic. Eleanor's marginalia—or was it? She had assumed the hand in the almanacs was Eleanor's because the donation had come from Eleanor's estate. But she had never verified it. Archivists verify. She had been careless once, twenty years ago, and the carelessness had calcified into fact.

She wrote a note to herself on a scrap card: *Whose handwriting? Verify against known samples.* Then she underlined it twice, and then, feeling slightly foolish, a third time.

* * *

*August 8th.* Gerald proposed a solution today, of all people. He took me walking on the headland—he does that, walks you until you feel you owe him your confessions—and he said, delicately, that if I truly wished to exit the estate, there were "arrangements" that could be made. That disappearance, in certain legal circumstances, could accomplish what divorce accomplishes for marriages. He said it so gently. Like offering me a shawl against the cold.

I didn't say yes. I didn't say no. I want to say I was horrified, but the truth is I stood there looking at the water and felt something terrible and bright open in my chest, like a door.

They want me gone? Fine. Let them arrange it. Let them build the machinery. And when I step off that ferry in Southampton, I will sign whatever they put in front of me, and the estate can rot in probate, and none of them—not Grandmother, not Ambrose, not one vulture cousin—will get a penny of mine while I breathe free air somewhere they can't find me.

Is that wicked? It doesn't feel wicked. It feels like the first plan I've ever made that belongs entirely to me.

Beatrix pressed her palm flat against the page, careful of the ink, careful of everything, wanting somehow to hold the moment still. So there it was—the architecture of the whole catastrophe, sketched in a dead girl's confident hand. The staging hadn't been done *to* Cordelia. It had been negotiated *with* her. A vanishing arranged as a business transaction: the heiress disappears, the will voids, the estate enters probate, and Cordelia reappears elsewhere, legally severed, financially free, invisible at last.

It was elegant. It was monstrous. And it explained everything the case file had never been able to explain—why there was no body, no struggle, no note; why the ferry manifest showed no Cordelia Vane; why the family's grief had always had that faintly theatrical quality, grief performed for an audience that might someday demand proof.

But the diary kept going. August kept going. And Beatrix already knew, from twenty years of reading endings, that plans made in August do not survive into September.

*August 21st.* Something is wrong. Details keep shifting. My trunk was moved—I'm sure of it, the lock faces the wrong way now. Gerald has stopped returning my calls. When I asked Grandmother directly whether the "arrangement" was still proceeding, she smiled and said, "Everything proceeds exactly as it should, darling," and the way she said *exactly* made my skin crawl.

I have copied the important pages of this diary and hidden them. If anything happens to me, whoever finds this: I did not run away. Whatever they say, whatever letters appear, whatever signature turns up on some document—I did not run away. I was going to. But not like this. Not on their schedule, not in their darkness.

I changed my mind. I told Gerald yesterday I wanted out of the arrangement. He looked at me for a long time and said, "That may be difficult at this stage."

Difficult. At this stage.

What stage? What have they built while I was packing?

Beatrix sat motionless as the archive cooled around her. The radiator ticked. Somewhere in the stacks a pipe knocked, the building's old bones settling. She realized she had been holding her breath and let it out slowly.

*I changed my mind.*

Four words, and the entire case inverted. Every reconstruction she had ever built—the staging theory she'd been assembling from ledger fragments and tide tables and gravestone verses—assumed Cordelia was a willing participant right up until the end. But the diary said otherwise. The diary said the machinery had been started, and the girl had tried to stop it, and the men and women operating that machinery had discovered that a living Cordelia, a *changed-her-mind* Cordelia, was worth more dead than alive.

Not because they hated her. Because the estate required her absence, and her absence had been scheduled, and schedules—in Eleanor Vane's world—were not subject to revision.

*September 4th.* Last entry. The ink is different here, hurried, the pen pressed hard enough to emboss the next three pages.

Tonight is the new moon. Low water at 11:40. Gerald says the crossing must happen at slack tide, that everything is prepared, that I am to wear dark clothes and carry nothing identifiable. I said I wanted to tell one person goodbye and he said no one can know, not even—

The sentence stops. There is a long blank space, and then, smaller, in the margin, angled sideways as though written in the dark or in a hurry or both:

If I am afraid, I am allowed to be afraid. Fear is just the body telling the truth.

That was the last thing Cordelia Vane ever wrote.

Beatrix closed the diary with exaggerated gentleness, the way you close a door on a sleeping child. The archive was fully dark now except for her desk lamp, its circle of light holding her and the book and nothing else. She became aware of her own breathing, of the enormous quiet of the building, of the harbor beyond the windows lying flat and black under a sky with no moon in it—no moon tonight either, she realized. She checked the almanac on her shelf without meaning to. Low water at 12:07.

Some habits of attention, once acquired, cannot be put down.

She thought about the last entry. *Carry nothing identifiable.* And yet a shoe with a monogrammed heel had waited under the pier for twenty-six years, according to the ledger coordinates she'd decoded, waiting for a storm generous enough to give it back. Identifiable after all. The sea kept better records than any archive.

And she thought about the blank space after *not even—*. Not even whom? A name unfinished, a confidence unmade. Cordelia had wanted to tell one person goodbye. Who did a girl like that turn to, in a house full of owners? A grandmother who called her a possession? A cousin who laughed at her curiosity? A lawyer who offered her a shawl against the cold?

Or someone lower in the house. Someone paid to dust the rooms and press the linens and see everything and say nothing. Someone who, decades later, still lived above the chandlery and knocked twice on Beatrix's stairwell door to tell her supper was ready.

"No," Beatrix said aloud, to the empty room, and hated how much she wanted it not to be true.

Harriet Okafor had been the Vane housekeeper in the summer of 1998. Beatrix had known that from her second week in Saltmere Bay. She had simply never let herself ask the obvious question, because Harriet was the closest thing to warmth her carefully small life contained, and some archives you keep sealed on purpose.

She gathered the diary, locked it in the fireproof cabinet, and turned the key, and stood with her hand flat on the cool metal door a moment longer than necessary.

Tomorrow, she told herself. Tomorrow she would go back to the ledger, back to the coordinates, back to the methodical work of turning paper into evidence. Tomorrow she would walk out to the old pier at low water and stand where Cordelia Vane had stood on a moonless night in September, wearing dark clothes, carrying nothing identifiable, waiting for a boat that the record insisted never came.

But tonight she allowed herself one minute—one full sixty seconds, timed on her watch—to sit in the dark and mourn a girl she had never met, who had planned her freedom so carefully and been so nearly clever enough to survive it.

Then she turned off the lamp, climbed the stairs to her rooms above the archive, and did not sleep.

Outside, the tide was going out, pulling back from the flats below the old pier, exposing inch by inch the seabed that had held its secret for twenty-six years—and tomorrow, Beatrix promised the dark, she would go and see what else it was willing to surrender.
