## 17. Mother's Handwriting

The rain came in off the bay around four o'clock, a soft gray curtain that turned the archive windows into mirrors and made the whole room feel like the inside of a sealed jar. Beatrix had always loved this hour best—the last light before she switched on the desk lamp, when Saltmere Bay seemed to hold its breath between the working day and the long dark of the coast. Tonight, though, she barely noticed it. She had Eleanor Vane's tide almanacs spread across three tables, and she was no longer reading them.

She was listening to them.

It had started that morning, innocently enough, with the 1994 volume—the year Cordelia turned nineteen, the year before everything went wrong. Beatrix had been cross-referencing the marginalia against the letter's phrasing for the fourth time, hunting for the pattern she knew was there, the way you know a word is on the tip of your tongue. The annotations were dense in places, sparse in others: small notations beside certain dates, tiny figures worked out in the margins like homework—*+3, −7, half ebb*—and occasionally a single word underlined twice.

*Wait.*

That was the word that stopped her. Not because of what it meant, but because of how it had been written. The *w* began with a little upward flick, a hesitation, as though the hand had lifted off the page mid-stroke. The *t* was crossed with a long, impatient slash that overshot both shoulders of the letter.

Beatrix sat very still in the lamplight, her tea going cold at her elbow, and felt something move through her chest that had no name yet—a cold, rising thing, like water finding a crack in a hull.

She knew that handwriting.

Not remembered it. Knew it, the way you know the sound of your own front door. Her mother had written grocery lists in that hand. Birthday cards. The labels on the boxes they'd packed when they moved from place to place in those restless years before Saltmere Bay—*KITCHEN, MARGARET'S BOOKS, DON'T DROP.* The *t*'s slashed too long. The *w*'s that flinched upward, as if even the alphabet had been nervous around them.

"No," Beatrix said aloud, to the empty room, to the rain, to nobody.

Her mother's name had been Margaret Hartwell. She had been a quiet woman who did quiet work—bookkeeping, mostly, for businesses that preferred their accounts unexamined—and she had died seven years ago with her affairs in such perfect order that Beatrix had sometimes wondered, in her darker hours, whether the tidiness itself had been a kind of confession. But her mother had never been to Vane House. Had never, as far as Beatrix knew, crossed paths with the Vanes at all. She'd come to Saltmere Bay in 1989 as a widow with a nine-year-old daughter and taken rooms above the chandlery, and she'd lived at the edge of the town's attention the way driftwood lives at the edge of the tide—present, unremarkable, washed by whatever came ashore.

And yet here, in the margins of Eleanor Vane's private tide almanac, donated to the archive only after Eleanor's death, was that flinching *w*. That slashing *t*.

Beatrix told herself it could be coincidence. Hands are hands; people learn penmanship from the same copybooks; a whole generation of schoolgirls had been drilled until their letters marched in lockstep. She said this to herself the way you say *the rope will hold* while standing on a ledge. Then she pulled the 1995 volume from its acid-free sleeve, and the 1996, and laid them open side by side beneath the lamp, and began the slow, terrible work of comparison.

* * *

By eight o'clock she had filled six pages of her notebook with transcriptions, and the coincidence had died quietly somewhere around page two.

It wasn't just the letterforms. It was the habits underneath them—the private architecture of a hand. Her mother had always written her sevens with a bar through them, a bookkeeper's tic, and there they were in the margin of the March 1996 almanac: *7 days after the spring ebb — 7/III.* Her mother had drawn her ampersands like a treble clef, an affectation she'd picked up, absurdly, from a music teacher in Ipswich, and there one was, tucked beside a payment notation Beatrix couldn't yet decode: *G & E — settled.* Her mother had pressed hard when she wrote numbers and lightly when she wrote words, so that on the reverse side of thin pages the digits stood up in relief like braille. Beatrix ran her fingertips across the back of the almanac page and felt them. Felt her mother's pressure, fossilized in paper, twenty-eight years old.

She got up once to open a window because the room had gone airless, and stood there breathing the wet salt wind until her hands stopped shaking. Then she closed the window and went back to work, because that was what she did—that was who she was, the woman who filed things, who alphabetized grief, who put the world in order so it couldn't hurt her. Only tonight the order was the wound. Tonight the archive had turned around and looked back at her.

The marginalia fell into two hands, she realized now. One was Eleanor Vane's—she'd seen enough of the matriarch's correspondence to know the imperious, downward-slanting script of a woman who signed things rather than wrote them. The other hand appeared only beside certain entries: the ones involving sums. Dates converted to tides, tides to figures, figures to initials. *G & E.* Gerald and Eleanor. And threaded through all of it, in that third voice she had refused to name until she could refuse no longer—

Margaret.

Her mother hadn't merely known about the ledger. Her mother had helped build it. Had done the arithmetic of concealment in the margins of a dead woman's almanac, in the same hand that had written *BEATRIX'S ROOM — DON'T DROP* on the boxes of their one shared life.

Beatrix thought of the confession letter, postmarked the day after Eleanor's funeral. *Whoever kept these books wanted them found,* she had written in her notes, weeks ago, in the flush of discovery. She had imagined the sender as some guilty servant, some estranged heir, some shadowy figure at the perimeter of the Vane empire. She had built an entire architecture of suspicion, and every beam of it had rested on the assumption that the conspiracy was distant from her—that she was the clean instrument applied to a dirty machine.

Now the machine had her mother's fingerprints all over it.

* * *

She didn't sleep. Around midnight she made herself toast she didn't eat and carried the 1998 almanac—the crucial one, the one whose final entries predated Cordelia's disappearance by eleven days—to the window seat, where the rain had thinned to a fine sea mist and the harbor lights smeared themselves across black water.

The last annotated entry was dated the ninth of August, 1998. Cordelia Vane walked out of the world on the twentieth.

In the margin beside the ninth of August, in her mother's hand, in pencil pressed hard enough to emboss the next three pages:

*Last entry. If anything happens to me, B. is not to be told. Promise me, E. She's a child. Let her keep her childhood.*

B. Beatrix. *B. is not to be told.*

The words swam, doubled, resolved. Beatrix read them five times, ten, mouthing them like a prayer said backward. *E.* Eleanor. Her mother had gone to Eleanor Vane—not the other way around, or not only—and had *asked* for something. Had bargained. *Let her keep her childhood.* As if Beatrix were a debt being negotiated. As if her innocence were a line item in a ledger, entered against some future date, accruing interest for twenty-five years until tonight, when the account came due in a cold archive room with the rain coming off the bay.

She tried to reconstruct it and couldn't; the facts wouldn't assemble. Her mother had been a bookkeeper. Bookkeepers don't consort with the richest family on the coast. Unless—unless the quiet work had never been quiet. Unless the businesses that preferred their accounts unexamined had included, somewhere in those restless years, a family that needed its accounts very much unexamined indeed. Unless Margaret Hartwell had been doing more than balancing columns for the Vanes. Unless she had been keeping secrets for them. Keeping them the way she kept everything: neatly, privately, in a hand that flinched upward on the *w*.

And then the other thought arrived, the one she'd been holding at arm's length since page two, and it came in with the force of the tide under a pier:

*Why?*

People keep secrets for money. People keep secrets for fear. But her mother had written *let her keep her childhood*—had begged, you could hear the begging in the pencil-pressure—and you did not beg for a stranger. You begged for someone you would burn the world to protect. Which meant either that Margaret Hartwell had loved Cordelia Vane's ghost enough to bury the truth of her, or—

Or the secret being buried had been Beatrix herself. Or something touching Beatrix. Something her mother believed a child could not survive knowing.

Beatrix set the almanac down on the windowsill with exaggerated care, the way you handle a thing when you're afraid your hands have stopped obeying you. Outside, the mist had thickened again. A boat light moved far out on the water, patient and anonymous, going nowhere she could see.

*You're not invisible,* Harriet Okafor had told her once, months ago, in one of the landlady's rare moments of unguarded kindness. *You've just been hiding where nobody looks.* Beatrix had taken it as a compliment. Now it curdled. Hiding where nobody looks was a skill, and skills are taught. Who had taught her to make herself small, to file herself away, to live a deliberately narrow life in a town where her own mother had apparently helped bury a heiress? She had thought she chose Saltmere Bay. Seven years ago, grieving, rootless, she had told everyone—including herself—that she'd chosen it for the archive, for the case, for the intellectual puzzle of Cordelia Vane.

But her mother had moved them here in 1989, the year before Cordelia vanished. Had raised her daughter in the very town where the secret slept under the pier. And Beatrix had grown up swimming in those waters, walking that beach, collecting shells below the old pilings without ever once being told *stay away from the pier*, because children don't need telling, do they, unless—

Unless someone wanted her close. Unless someone wanted her near enough to watch, far enough not to see.

"Mother," she said to the dark window, and her breath fogged it, and the fog erased the harbor lights one by one, "what did you do?"

The glass gave no answer. Glass never does. That was the whole trouble with archives, Beatrix thought, with a laugh that came out wrong, cracked down the middle: they answer every question except the ones you actually asked.

* * *

Dawn found her still at the window seat, the almanac in her lap, having passed through exhaustion into a strange clear plateau beyond it. She had spent the small hours doing the only thing that steadied her: documenting. Photographing every marginal page. Transcribing every notation into her notebook in her own careful print—as unlike her mother's hand as she could make it, though she caught herself, twice, drawing barred sevens, and stopped, and stared at her own fingers as if they belonged to a stranger.

Because that was the deepest cut of it, the one she kept returning to like a tongue to a broken tooth: the handwriting wasn't foreign. It was *inherited*. She had her mother's barred sevens. She had the treble-clef ampersand, though she'd assumed she'd invented it. Somewhere in the years of grocery lists and box labels, Margaret Hartwell's hand had flowed down into her daughter's the way silt flows downstream, silent and total, and Beatrix understood now that she had been carrying the evidence of her mother's complicity in her own muscles since childhood. Every form she'd filled out. Every card she'd signed. Twenty-five years of unwitting forgery.

At half past six she heard Harriet moving downstairs—the familiar percussion of the kettle, the radio murmuring the marine forecast—and for a moment she almost went down. Almost knocked on the kitchen door and said *Harriet, you kept house at Vane House, look at this, tell me I'm mad.* But Harriet's face rose in her memory, that particular guardedness the older woman wore whenever the Vanes came up, and Beatrix heard her own voice from weeks ago asking casual questions and Harriet answering them with the flat courtesy of a locked door. No. Not yet. Not until she knew what she was holding. Trust, she was learning, was also a ledger, and she had overdrawn too many accounts already.

Instead she packed the almanacs away with ritual slowness—gloves, sleeves, acid-free folders, the whole liturgy of preservation—and it struck her, sliding the 1998 volume into its box, that she was performing last rites. That whatever these documents had been to her—clues, puzzles, the raw material of a podcast and a purpose—they were something else now. They were her mother's diary. Her mother's confession, written in installments across a decade, addressed to no one, hidden in plain sight in a public archive, waiting for the one reader in the world who would recognize the hand.

*If anything happens to me, B. is not to be told.*

Something had happened to her mother, then. Not death—death was ordinary, death was the hospital in 2017 and the tidy files afterward. Something earlier. Something that had made a bookkeeper go to the most powerful woman on the coast and ask for her child's ignorance as a favor. Beatrix turned the phrase over and over. *If anything happens to me.* People write that when they're afraid. When they think they might be made to disappear, or silenced, or—her mind shied from it—when they've already done something that could bring the disappearing down on their own heads.

Had her mother been in danger from the Vanes? Protected by them? Both? Was there a difference?

She locked the archive at seven-fifteen, standing a moment in the doorway while the morning fog burned off the harbor and the gulls resumed their ancient arguments. The town looked exactly as it always looked: whitewashed and weathered, the fishing boats knocking gently at their moorings, Mrs. Trelawney sweeping her step with the aggression of a woman defending territory. An ordinary Tuesday in Saltmere Bay. Beneath it, Beatrix knew now, lay the ledger, and the grave, and her mother's penciled plea, and twenty-five years of a lie so large the whole town stood on it without noticing the ground was hollow.

She walked home along the seawall, and halfway there she became aware of a sensation she couldn't immediately place, and then could: she was being watched. Not the diffuse attention of a small town, which she'd long ago learned to wear like weather. Something specific. She turned on the promenade, casually, scanning the row of shops—the bakery, the chandlery, the shuttered travel agency—and saw nothing but the ordinary morning population going about its business. A man in a green waxed jacket studying the noticeboard outside the post office. Two teenagers loitering by the railings with the studied idleness of youth. A figure at the far end of the seawall, too distant to read, standing motionless beside the railing as if waiting for someone.

She walked on. The feeling stayed.

Back at Harriet's, she climbed to her room and took from the bottom of her wardrobe the tin box she'd brought to Saltmere Bay seven years ago and never opened: her mother's effects, the residue no one else had wanted. Letters of administration. A funeral bill. A photograph of Margaret at roughly Beatrix's current age, squinting into sun on this same seawall, looking—Beatrix saw it now for the first time—not peaceful, but vigilant. Watching the water. Watching the pier, gray and skeletal in the background of the shot, as if checking on something stored there.

And at the bottom of the tin, folded into quarters, a single sheet of lined paper in her mother's hand. A list. Beatrix unfolded it with numb fingers.

Six items. Groceries, the first three—milk, bread, tea. The fourth item was a name: *G. Pike.* The fifth was a sum: £2,000. The sixth, underlined twice in that long impatient slash, was a single word.

*Pier.*

Downstairs, faintly, the telephone began to ring.
