## 22. Milo Follows Someone

Milo Quist had learned three things about surveillance from the true-crime podcasts he wasn't supposed to be listening to during his shift at the Saltmere Bay chandlery: one, always have a reason to be where you are; two, never look directly at the person you're watching; and three, most people, even guilty people, are so absorbed in their own business that they forget the world contains witnesses.

He was seventeen, he had a bicycle with a basket, and he had a job that required him to stand in the doorway of the chandlery every afternoon at four o'clock to flip the OPEN sign to CLOSED—which meant he had an unobstructed view of the narrow lane that ran past Beatrix Hartwell's archive office, the converted net-loft with the green door and the brass plaque that read SALT BAY ARCHIVES in letters so small you'd think she was hiding it.

She probably was. That was the thing about Miss Hartwell. She wanted to be invisible, and mostly the town let her be, the way you let a lighthouse be invisible by simply not looking at it. But lately people were looking. Milo had noticed because noticing things was what he did—quietly, from doorways, the way his grandmother said he'd been doing since he could walk. Harriet Okafor called him her little periscope, which he pretended to find embarrassing and secretly treasured.

The man had appeared on Tuesday.

Milo clocked him the way you'd clock weather—a change in pressure, nothing dramatic. A gray sedan parked at the far end of the lane, engine off, no one getting out. Wednesday it was back, or one like it, same color, same rental-company sticker on the rear window half peeled away as if someone had tried to remove it. Thursday, the man got out.

He was maybe fifty, dressed like a fisherman pretending not to be one—oilskin jacket too clean, boots too new, the kind of outfit you bought at a marine supply catalog instead of earning through decades of salt. He carried a camera with a long lens, held low against his thigh, casual, the way tourists carry them. Except tourists photograph the harbor. The gulls. The boats with their pretty names. This man stood across the lane from the green door for eleven minutes—Milo counted, checking the chandlery's wall clock between customers buying rope hooks and tide charts—and photographed the office of Beatrix Hartwell from six angles, including two that framed the upstairs window where Milo knew she kept her filing cabinets, because Harriet had shown him once when he helped carry up a box of donated newspapers.

Then the man photographed the chandlery itself.

That was when Milo's stomach went cold and interesting, the way it did before a storm. Because there was only one reason to photograph the chandlery, and that was that the chandlery could see the office. Which meant the man wasn't just documenting the building. He was documenting who could see the building. Who might see *him*.

Milo flipped the sign to CLOSED at four, locked up, and rode his bike home the long way, past the lane, pedaling slowly enough to look like a kid dawdling, fast enough that the gray sedan's driver—if the sedan still had a driver—wouldn't mark him. The car was gone. But the man had been there three days running, and Milo would have bet his entire savings—forty-one dollars and a commemorative coin from the 1998 regatta, which felt suddenly relevant—that he'd be back.

* * *

He told Harriet over dinner, because Harriet was the kind of person you told things to, and also because he'd promised himself, after the fire at the records office, that he wouldn't keep things from her anymore. She'd been quiet for a long moment, her spoon resting in her soup, looking at some middle distance that Milo suspected contained the year 1998.

"You're sure it was a camera," she said. "Not a phone."

"A camera. With a lens like—" he made a cylinder with his hands. "You don't carry one of those unless you need distance. Unless you don't want to be close."

Harriet stirred her soup without eating it. "Your grandmother's instincts," she said finally, which was high praise delivered in the flattest possible voice. "But Milo. Listen to me. You do not follow this man. You do not approach him. You do not become a story."

"I'm not going to *approach* him—"

"Because the last person who poked at this town's secrets—" She stopped herself. Set down the spoon. Her hands, Milo noticed, weren't quite steady. "Miss Hartwell is dealing with enough. Don't add yourself to her worries."

Which was, of course, exactly the wrong thing to say to a seventeen-year-old boy who had already decided, somewhere around the second spoonful of soup, that he was absolutely going to follow the man.

Not recklessly. He'd thought it through. That was the difference between what he planned and what Harriet feared—he'd thought it through the way Miss Hartwell thought things through, in steps, in contingencies. Rule one: always have a reason to be where you are. And Milo had the best reason in Saltmere Bay. He delivered. Groceries to the houses on the headland, parts to the boatyard, packages to anyone who ordered online and didn't want to drive to the depot in Marsh Crossing. A boy on a bicycle with a basket was furniture. A boy on a bicycle with a basket was *invisible*, and if there was one thing Milo had learned from watching Miss Hartwell build her whole careful life, it was that invisibility was a skill, and skills could be borrowed.

* * *

The man came back on Saturday morning, and this time Milo was ready.

He saw the sedan first from the top of Harbor Street, where he'd stopped to "adjust his chain"—a performance he'd rehearsed, crouching beside the bike with a rag, eyes down, attention apparently consumed by gears. The sedan was in its spot at the end of the lane. At 9:40, right on schedule—the man had come at 9:40 Tuesday, 9:45 Wednesday; they were creatures of habit, all of them, the watchers and the watched—the driver's door opened and the too-clean fisherman stepped out.

Milo gave him a thirty-second head start, then walked his bike along the opposite sidewalk, past the bakery, past the shuttered ice cream stand, and cut through the alley behind the chandlery into the warren of lanes behind the harbor front. He came out by the fuel dock, where he had a delivery—two cases of engine oil for the *Puffin*, a lobster boat whose captain paid him in cash and conversation—and while he waited for old Deke to finish arguing with the pump, he watched the man in the oilskin jacket work his way along the waterline.

The man wasn't photographing the office today. Today he was photographing the pier.

The old pier. The one that jutted out past the boatyard like a broken finger, fenced off with sagging chain-link since before Milo was born, its pilings black and furred with barnacles, the planks gone soft as bread. Kids dared each other onto it every summer and every summer someone's foot went through. It was, as far as Milo knew, the single most boring landmark in Saltmere Bay—a ruin everyone had agreed to stop seeing forty years ago.

And this man stood at the fence line for twenty minutes, camera up, shooting the pier from the shore, then walking the shoreline to shoot it from the north, then the south, then crouching—actually crouching—to frame something low, something near the waterline, something that would only matter at low tide.

Milo signed for the oil, wheeled his bike to the top of the boatyard ramp, and let himself look directly at the man for the first time. Just once. Two seconds. Face, build, hands.

Fiftyish. Tall, heavy through the shoulders but soft in the way of men who worked desks. Gray-blond hair under a canvas cap. And the hands—this was the detail Milo filed away with the care of someone who'd grown up around boats and the men who loved them—those hands had never hauled a line in their life. Soft palms, manicured nails, and a watch that cost more than Deke's truck.

The man felt the look. His head started to turn.

Milo was already moving, walking the bike casually toward the chandlery, heart hammering in a way that felt less like fear than like being *alive*, truly alive, for the first time since the night the records office burned and he'd stood in the crowd smelling smoke and understanding that the story he'd been listening to on headphones was happening to real people, to his people, to Miss Hartwell with her green door and her alphabetized dead.

The man didn't follow. Of course he didn't. You didn't chase the furniture.

* * *

He should have stopped there. He knows that now. He tells himself, later, in the version of events he rehearses for Detective Reyes, that the plan was always just to get the plate—but the truth is that when the sedan pulled away from the lane at 11:15, heading north out of town on the coast road, Milo was already on his bike, three hundred feet behind, telling himself this was reconnaissance, this was podcast episode ninety-two material, this was *helping*.

The coast road north of Saltmere Bay is two lanes of cracked asphalt stitched between hedgerows and the occasional gap-toothed field. There's nowhere to hide on it, which is why Milo stayed far back and why he nearly lost the sedan twice—once behind a hay truck doing fifteen with its hazards on, once at the junction where the road forks toward Marsh Crossing or climbs toward the headland estates. The sedan went left, toward Marsh Crossing, and Milo made a decision he'd replay a thousand times: he took the shortcut.

Everyone in Saltmere Bay knew the shortcut—the farm track that cut across the peninsula and came out on the coast road just past the fork, saving a mile and a half. What nobody used it for was racing a car on a bicycle with a basket full of nothing. Milo stood on the pedals. The track was rutted and his teeth rattled and somewhere around the halfway point his front wheel found a pothole and the world went sideways in a slow, terrible arc, and he came down hard on his left forearm and hip in a tangle of spokes and brambles, lying in the dirt of the farm track listening to his own breath come back.

When he got up—arm scraped, pride hemorrhaging, bike rideable but complaining—the shortcut had given him everything and nothing. He reached the coast road in time to see the gray sedan a quarter mile ahead, slowing, pulling onto the gravel shoulder near the old Vane boathouse.

Milo stopped. Dismounted. Walked the bike into the hedge shadow at the track's mouth, and watched.

The boathouse had been empty since before he was born—everyone said the Vanes kept it out of stubbornness, like the pier, like the whole salt-rotted empire. Its doors faced the water, its windows were boarded, and its gravel apron was the only place on this stretch of road where a car could pull off without sliding into a ditch. The man in the oilskin jacket got out, camera in hand, and photographed the boathouse. Front. Side. The padlocked doors. Then he walked down the slipway to the waterline and stood there a long time, longer than any photographer needed, just looking—at the water, at the mudflats exposed by the ebb, at the distant black finger of the old pier two miles south.

Then he took out his phone, made a call, and spoke for perhaps ninety seconds. Milo couldn't hear a word of it. But he could see the man's posture change as he listened to the reply—the shoulders drawing in, the head bowing slightly, the weight shifting from foot to foot like a man receiving instructions he didn't like. When the call ended, the man stood motionless for a full minute, staring at the mudflats.

Then he got back in the car, turned around, and drove south, back toward town, passing within forty feet of the hedge where Milo stood with his heart in his throat and his phone already raised—not to photograph the man, he hadn't been quick enough, the tinted glass ate the face—but angled down at the road, at the rear of the sedan as it went by.

The shutter sound was very loud in the hedge shadow.

The plate was half obscured by road grime, the way plates always are in coastal towns, salt spray and mud doing their patient work. But the photo caught it at an angle, and Milo, reviewing it with shaking thumbs right there among the brambles, could make out enough: the plate was from out of county, and the last three characters were legible—**K-7-9**—with the leading characters smeared into gray suggestion. Rental sticker on the trunk. Same as the other car. Or the same car. It didn't matter which. What mattered was that a stranger with soft hands and a catalog wardrobe had spent his Saturday photographing a ruined boathouse owned by a family that had buried its matriarch a month ago, and had ended his work standing at the edge of the mudflats below the old pier, making a call, and looking afraid.

Guilty men didn't look afraid, Milo thought, pedaling home the long safe way with his arm stinging and his mind roaring. Men who had gotten away with it for twenty-seven years didn't stand on a slipway and flinch at a phone call.

Men who were being *sent* looked like that.

* * *

Harriet met him at the door, took one look at his arm, and marched him to the kitchen sink without a word, where she cleaned the scrapes with a thoroughness that was its own kind of lecture. He told her everything while she worked—the sedan, the schedule, the pier, the boathouse, the call, the plate. She didn't interrupt once. When he finished, she dried her hands and sat down across from him, and Milo saw something in her face he'd never seen before: not the warning look, not the fear. Something older and harder. Decision.

"Show me the photograph," she said.

He showed her. She studied the partial plate for a long time, then the timestamp, then the background—the boathouse doors, the slipway, the mud.

"Harbor office keeps a log of vehicles using the north lot," she said slowly, thinking aloud. "Has for years, since the county made them. If this car came through town more than once..." She trailed off, and Milo watched her arrive at the same place he'd arrived at in the hedge: that the answer might be sitting in a ledger somewhere, waiting, the way all the answers in this town seemed to wait in ledgers.

"We take it to Miss Hartwell," Milo said. "Tonight."

Harriet looked at him for a long moment. Outside, the gulls were making their evening argument over the harbor, and the light was going gold and then copper over the water, and somewhere out there beneath the old pier the tide was turning, beginning its long patient withdrawal from the flats.

"Yes," Harriet said at last, and her voice was quiet and steady and final. "We take it to Miss Hartwell." She stood, and paused at the window, looking north toward the coast road, toward the boathouse neither of them could see. "And Milo—when we tell her, we tell her everything. Including the part where you fell off your bike chasing a murderer's errand boy. She'll want the whole shape of it. She always does."

"She's going to be angry I followed him."

"No," Harriet said, and there was something almost like a smile in it, tired and fierce at once. "She's going to be angry that we didn't tell her sooner. Go wash your face. We leave in ten minutes."

Milo went, and as he scrubbed the farm-track grit from his forearms, he thought about the man on the slipway, bowing under the weight of a phone call, staring at the mud. Whoever was giving the orders—the man with the soft hands was only the messenger, and messengers could be traced backward, plate by partial plate, log entry by log entry, all the way up the chain to whoever in Saltmere Bay still had something to bury.

By the time he came downstairs, Harriet had her coat on and the photograph printed, and the two of them stepped out together into the blue evening, heading for the green door with the small brass letters—two more witnesses the town had forgotten to count on.
