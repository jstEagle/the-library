## 38. Her Mother's Page

The archive was quiet in the way Beatrix had once loved and now only tolerated—the hush of paper sleeping in its drawers, the tick of the radiator, the sea audible even here, a low percussion beneath everything Saltmere Bay did. She had not slept. The trial's paperwork sat in a box on her desk like something dead she hadn't yet decided how to bury: deposition transcripts, the ledger in its evidence photocopy, Detective Reyes's card with a handwritten note that said *When you're ready.* She was not ready. She suspected she would never be ready for what she had saved for last.

Her mother's page.

It had been in the almanac all along—Eleanor Vane's tide tables, donated to the archive years ago in a crate of household books, their margins crowded with annotations Beatrix had spent months decoding as if they were Eleanor's own hand. Dates converted to tides, tides to numbers, numbers to payments. A ledger hidden in the grammar of the sea. And threaded through it, in a second hand she had refused to recognize until she could no longer refuse, the smaller, slanted script of Margaret Hartwell.

Her mother had been here. In these margins. In this conspiracy. Not at the edges of it, as Beatrix had first told herself—not a secretary copying letters, not an innocent clerk—but inside it, writing dates beside sums, writing *confirmed* beside initials, writing, on one terrible page near the end, something else entirely.

Beatrix had photographed every annotated page weeks ago, during the long unraveling, but she had not read this one closely. She had told herself she was saving it. The truth was she had been afraid of it, the way you are afraid of a room in your own house where something once happened.

Now she laid it flat under the lamp.

* * *

The page was dated March 2003—five years after Cordelia Vane went into the water, four years before Margaret Hartwell packed her daughter off to an aunt in Leeds and stayed behind to die slowly of something the doctors named only vaguely, something Beatrix had always privately blamed on grief.

The marginalia began conventionally enough. *E. paid G. quarterly — see 1999–2002 entries. H. delivered cash to pier office, Tuesdays.* Her mother's initials, H., appearing in the ledger's architecture like a signature on a confession. Beatrix read it twice, three times, letting each line do its small violence. Her mother had carried the money. Her mother had known about the payments—to Gerald Pike, to someone listed only as *O.*, whom Beatrix now understood must have been Harriet Okafor, whose silence had been purchased in envelopes for twenty-five years.

But it was the final note—the one written lower on the page, cramped into the margin's last inch as though the writer had run out of both space and courage—that stopped Beatrix's breath.

She read it aloud, because silence made it worse, and her voice sounded strange in the empty archive, younger than she felt.

*"If B. ever finds this — I am sorry. I did it so they would never look at you. A Vane takes what it wants and calls it legacy. You were seven when I understood what they'd done, and I chose you over the truth. I would choose you again. God forgive me, I would choose you again."*

*B.* Beatrix. Even in the margins of a criminal ledger, her mother had been thinking of her. Had built the whole concealment around her, the way a shell is built around a soft thing.

Beatrix sat down slowly on the drafting stool, because her legs had opinions of their own.

So that was the shape of it. Not cowardice alone, though there was cowardice in it—she could see that now without flinching, could hold both truths at once the way she held two documents side by side and compared them. Her mother had known, by 2003 at latest, that Cordelia Vane had not run away to the ferry and a new life; she had known the staging had gone wrong, that a girl was dead under the pier, that Eleanor Vane and Gerald Pike were paying to keep the sea's mouth shut. And Margaret Hartwell—a young archivist then, newly arrived, already cataloguing the town's records—had discovered it in exactly the way her daughter would discover it twenty years later: in the paper trail, in the margins, in the arithmetic of tides.

And she had buried it. Not out of loyalty to the Vanes. Out of terror of them.

*A Vane takes what it wants and calls it legacy.*

Beatrix thought of Ambrose Vane in his funeral suit, of the polished menace of his visit to her office, of the way he had said *of Eleanor's* as if the possessive were a weapon. She thought of the fire at the records office, of Milo coughing smoke on the steps, of the letter stolen from her home while she bought groceries. The family's reach was not metaphor. It had burned actual buildings. It had frightened an old woman into twenty-five years of envelopes.

And her mother had looked at all of that, and then had looked at her seven-year-old daughter—or the daughter she would have—and had made the calculation mothers make in the dark, the ones that don't survive daylight but get made anyway.

*I chose you over the truth.*

"You should have told me," Beatrix said to the page. Her voice cracked on *me*, which embarrassed her, and she let it. "You should have trusted me with it."

But even as she said it, she knew the answer. Her mother had died when Beatrix was nineteen, too soon for any of it to be safe to carry. And before that—what, exactly? A childhood spent in Saltmere Bay, summers on the shingle beach, her mother pointing out tide pools and saying, *the sea keeps everything, Bea, remember that, the sea keeps everything,* and young Beatrix thinking it was poetry. It had been a warning. It had been a woman rehearsing a confession she could never deliver, teaching her daughter the one fact that might someday save her: that the bay remembers.

Beatrix pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. The lamp hummed. Somewhere outside, gulls argued over something the storm had turned up.

* * *

She didn't go home that evening. She worked through the marginalia properly now, all of it, page by page, building the map of her mother's complicity and her mother's protection as carefully as she had once built the case itself. There was more than the apology. There was, tucked between two tide entries from 2004, a list in Margaret's hand: names, and beside each name a single word.

*Eleanor — knows everything.*
*Gerald — does the paying.*
*Okafor — saw the trunk. Paid.*
*Ambrose — suspects nothing. Keep it so.*

And at the bottom, underlined twice:

*B. — knows nothing. KEEP IT SO.*

Her whole childhood, annotated. Her ignorance, maintained like a garden. Every summer her mother had steered her away from the Vane end of the beach; every time the disappearance came up at school, her mother had changed the subject with a fluency Beatrix had mistaken for delicacy. When Beatrix, at seventeen, had announced she wanted to study archival science, her mother's face had done something complicated—pride and dread sharing the same expression—and she had said, *Paper is honest, Bea. People aren't. Stick with the paper.*

Stick with the paper. As if the paper weren't the most dangerous thing in Saltmere Bay. As if her mother hadn't known that someday the honest paper would lead her daughter straight back to the pier.

Unless—Beatrix turned the thought over slowly—it was meant to. Unless some part of Margaret Hartwell, the part that wrote *I am sorry* in a margin she must have known was evidence, had been leaving a door open all along. Not a confession to the police; her mother had never had the courage for that, or perhaps had judged, correctly, that a lone archivist's word against the Vanes' money would accomplish nothing except orphaning a child. But a thread. A loose thread left deliberately in the weave, for a daughter clever enough to pull it when the world was finally safe enough to allow pulling.

Or perhaps Beatrix was flattering herself. Perhaps her mother had simply been careless at the end, tired, dying, writing truths into margins because the living couldn't hear them anymore.

She would never know which. That was the tax on secrets kept too long: even the truth, recovered, arrives missing pieces. Forgiveness, she was learning, was not a verdict you reached. It was an archive you opened, knowing some folders would stay empty forever.

* * *

Harriet Okafor found her there past midnight.

Beatrix heard the key in the archive's side door—Harriet still had one, from her landlady's practical dominion over half the block—and looked up to find the old woman standing in the doorway in her coat, silver hair uncombed, holding a covered dish she clearly hadn't planned on offering.

"I saw your light," Harriet said. "It's midnight, child."

"I know."

"Reyes said you'd want to be alone. I told her alone is the last thing anybody in this town needs right now." She set the dish on the worktable—jollof rice, by the smell, the dish Harriet made when words failed her—and lowered herself onto the chair across from Beatrix with the careful descent of knees that had filed their complaints decades ago. Her eyes went to the almanac, open under the lamp, and something moved through her face like weather. "That's hers. Your mum's writing."

"Yes."

"I'd know it anywhere. She used to bring the ledgers to the house for Eleanor to check. Sat at our kitchen table doing sums." Harriet's hands folded in her lap. "I liked her. That was the worst part, afterward. Liking people you've lied alongside."

Beatrix closed the almanac gently, as if the page might bruise. "Did my mother ever talk to you? About any of it?"

"Once. Near the end, when she was ill." Harriet's voice dropped, the way voices do in this town when they approach 1998, as if the year were a sickroom. "She asked me if I thought a person could love their child and still ruin them. I said I didn't know. She said, 'Neither do I, but I'm about to find out.'" The old woman's eyes were wet and furious about being wet. "I thought she meant she was dying. Now I think she meant you."

The radiator ticked. Beatrix looked at the covered dish, at the steam ghosting up from its edge, at this woman who had taken Vane money for twenty-five years and had finally, in a police interview room, given it all back with interest in the form of testimony.

"She protected me from them," Beatrix said. "And it cost Cordelia Vane her name for twenty-five years. Both things are true. I keep trying to make one of them smaller and they won't fit."

"No," Harriet agreed. "They won't. That's what complicity is. It doesn't shrink to fit your better self." She reached across and put her dark, spotted hand over Beatrix's, briefly, firmly. "Your mum wasn't Eleanor. Remember that when you're judging her. Eleanor did it for power. Gerald did it for blood and money. Your mum did it because she was one small woman in a town owned by people who burn buildings. She picked the only fight she could win, and the prize was you."

"And lost Cordelia."

"And lost Cordelia," Harriet said. "There's no clean hands in Saltmere Bay, child. There's just hands, and what they held, and how long they held on."

* * *

Beatrix walked home along the front at nearly one in the morning, because she needed the cold and the sound of the water. The storm two weeks ago had rearranged the shingle; the seafront smelled of exposed weed and old wood, and the pier stood black against the lesser black of the sky, its pilings naked at low tide, the flats gleaming where the mud had finally, grudgingly, given up its locket and its rotted shoe and its twenty-five-year secret.

She stopped at the rail above the stanchion where they had found the hollow. The tide was coming back in, filling the hole Cordelia had occupied all this time, erasing the excavation marks, smoothing everything toward forgetting again. But it wouldn't forget now. There were police tape remnants snagged on a piling, fluttering like shed skin. There was a courtroom calendar with Martin Hale's name on it. There was a town waking up every morning to the fact that its founding story was a lie, and arguing about it in the bakery, and in the churchyard, and in the letters page of the *Saltmere Bay Sentinel*.

And somewhere under all of it, her mother's handwriting, keeping its appointment for twenty years.

*I chose you over the truth. I would choose you again.*

Beatrix realized she was crying only when a tear hit the cold iron of the railing. She let it happen. She had spent her whole adult life mistaking control for peace, order for safety, a small alphabetized life for a life. Her mother had made the same mistake in reverse—had mistaken silence for safety, ignorance for innocence—and the bill had come due for both of them, and for Cordelia, who had never gotten to make any choices at all after that June night.

"I forgive you," she said to the water, testing the words, weighing them like coins. "Mostly. Some days. Ask me again tomorrow."

The sea said nothing, but the sea never promised anything either. That was the difference between it and her mother. The sea kept everything openly, without pretending otherwise.

She turned back toward the town. Lights still burned in Harriet's kitchen window. On the bench by the seawall, improbably, Milo sat hunched in a hoodie with his phone's glow on his face—he was sixteen, he kept vigil over things now, he had earned it—and he raised a hand when he saw her, casual, unafraid, as if a woman walking home at 1 a.m. past a crime scene were simply how Saltmere Bay worked now.

"Milo," she said. "It's midnight-something."

"Couldn't sleep." He shrugged. "Reyes says the tide's supposed to be extra low Thursday. She wants me to show her where I found the plate photo angle. From the shore, I mean. Official-like." His grin was quick and crooked. "Says I've got a good eye. Says maybe I should think about archives."

"Archives," Beatrix repeated, and found the word had changed weight in her mouth. Lighter. Possible. "It's mostly dust, you know."

"Yeah," Milo said. "But it's dust that tells the truth eventually. That's kind of cool."

She walked on, up the hill toward the house Harriet rented her, past the darkened shopfronts, past the noticeboard where someone had already pinned a flyer for a memorial service—*For Cordelia Vane, at last*—and she understood, with the tired clarity of the very late hour, that the town was going to keep her. Not the invisible archivist, the woman who filed other people's tragedies and went home alone. Someone else. Someone whose mother's handwriting was in the margins of the town's oldest wound, someone who had pulled the thread, someone the town now argued about over breakfast with something adjacent to pride.

Her mother had hidden her to keep her safe. The hiding was over. What remained to be seen—what she would have to decide, in the blank notebook waiting on her desk—was who she wanted to be now that everyone was looking.

At her door, she paused, keys in hand, and looked back down at the black water.

Thursday. Extra low tide. Reyes on the flats with a teenager and a flashlight, reading the sea's ledger one more time.

Beatrix let herself in, put the kettle on, and reached for the phone.
