## 39. Belonging

The town of Saltmere Bay did not know how to look at her anymore.

Beatrix felt it first at the bakery on Harbor Street, three days after the arrest. Mrs. Calloway, who had sold her a morning bun every Saturday for seven years without once asking her name, set the bun in a paper bag and slid it across the counter and then—instead of turning back to her ovens—stood there with flour on her forearms and said, "You'll be wanting to know we always thought there was something wrong about that family."

It was not an apology. It was not quite an offering either. It was the sound a town makes when the story it has told itself for twenty-six years is taken away and it must learn to speak without it. Beatrix took the bag and said thank you, and Mrs. Calloway nodded too many times, and Beatrix walked out into the pale October light understanding that she had become, overnight, the most visible person in Saltmere Bay—the woman who had unmade its founding myth, and who now had to live among the people whose myth it had been.

She had spent seven years perfecting invisibility. She had built herself out of routines: the same table at the library, the same seat on the seawall, the same walk home along the cliff path timed to avoid the dog-walkers and the school run. Invisibility had been armor. It had also been a wound she'd dressed as a choice. Now the armor was gone—stripped from her by a confession letter, a coded ledger, a shoe rising out of the mud like a hand reaching up—and what was underneath turned out to be a person people wanted to talk to.

It was disorientating. It was, if she was honest with herself, which she was trying harder to be these days, a little wonderful.

* * *

Harriet Okafor found her on the seawall on Thursday, carrying two mugs of tea wrapped in dish towels, and sat down beside her without asking permission, which was how Harriet did everything.

"You're famous," Harriet said. "Terrible thing to happen to a private person."

"I'm notorious," Beatrix corrected. "Famous implies they like me."

"They like you fine. They just don't know how to say so." Harriet blew on her tea. Below them the tide was coming in over the flats where, eleven days ago, Martin Hale had knelt in the mud with his hands open and his face undone, and Detective Ilsa Reyes had read him his rights while gulls screamed overhead like witnesses. "Mrs. Calloway gave me a free scone yesterday. First free thing that bakery's given anyone since the war. That's what grief looks like in this town, Bea. Baked goods."

Beatrix laughed—a real laugh, surprising in its own throat—and Harriet looked pleased with herself, the way she always did when she made Beatrix laugh, as though it were a skill she'd practiced.

They drank their tea. The water filled in the channels between the sandbars, patient as anything.

"I saw him," Harriet said finally. "Martin. Through the glass at the county lockup, before they moved him. He asked for me." Her voice went flat and careful. "I didn't go. Twenty-six years I carried what I saw on that pier, and I paid my silence like rent every month, and I am done paying. But I didn't sleep last night anyway. You don't stop being someone's neighbor just because they turned out to be—" She stopped. Started again. "I keep thinking about his mother. Gerald's wife. She never knew. She died never knowing her husband had another son, or what that son did. Whole lives folded inside other lives, and nobody opens them till everybody's dead."

"That's why I archive," Beatrix said. "Somebody has to open them while there's still somebody left to answer."

Harriet looked at her sideways, and there was something new in her face—not the guardedness of the landlady who had warned Beatrix off twice, not the guilt of the woman who had confessed her own complicity in Harriet's kitchen with shaking hands, but something softer and more frightened and more fond. "Your mother would have been proud of you," she said. "And furious with you. Both at once. That's how love works in this town."

"She hid the truth to protect me," Beatrix said. "From the Vanes. From all of it."

"And you dug it up anyway."

"And I dug it up anyway."

Harriet clinked her mug against Beatrix's, a small ceramic toast. "Then I suppose we're both terrible at being protected." She stood, brushed off her skirt, and paused. "Sunday. My house. I'm making the fish stew my mother made, the one I haven't cooked since before my husband died, because cooking it alone felt like talking to an empty chair. Milo's coming. Bring nothing but yourself. And Bea—" she pointed the dish towel at her like a teacher, "—if you say you have work to do, I will come to your office and move your files myself."

For seven years no one had ordered Beatrix Hartwell to dinner. She found that her eyes were stinging, and blamed the wind off the water, which was convenient and only half a lie.

* * *

Milo arrived at the archive on Saturday afternoon with a cardboard box under one arm and the particular swagger of a sixteen-year-old who has recently been mentioned by name in a detective's incident report.

"Partial plate," he announced, setting the box on her desk without ceremony. "I made prints. Two copies. One's for you, one's for evidence, except Reyes already has hers, so this one's technically redundant, but I thought the file should be complete." He said *the file* the way other boys said *my team*, and Beatrix understood that somewhere in the past month, without her noticing, the Cordelia Vane case had stopped being hers alone.

The box contained photographs—Milo's own, printed at the pharmacy, of the harbor, the pier, the courthouse steps on the day of Hale's arraignment. He had an eye. That was the thing that kept startling her. The photographs were not snapshots; they were compositions, the pier's stanchions falling into shadow, the crowd outside the courthouse arranged by accident into something like a Renaissance frieze, Hale's face caught between the deputies, small and gray and ordinary.

"You should keep shooting," she said.

"It's just a hobby."

"So was archiving, once." She spread the prints across her desk. "Milo. What you did—following that car, getting the plate, staying safe while you did it—that wasn't luck. That was work. Careful work. You helped close a case that beat everyone else for twenty-six years."

He tried to look casual about it and failed completely; the grin broke through like sun through cloud. "Reyes said I could ride along sometime. When I'm seventeen. She said, and I quote, 'the paperwork is a nightmare but you've earned a nightmare.'"

"She's warming to you."

"She's terrifying," Milo said reverently. "I love her."

Beatrix laughed again. Twice in one week. The town was ruining her.

He lingered at the door on his way out, and his swagger dropped away, and he was suddenly just a kid asking a question he'd clearly been carrying around. "Do you think it's weird? That I liked it? The following part. The figuring-it-out part. Everyone at school keeps saying I should be freaked out, and I'm not, I just—" he searched for it, "—it felt like mattering."

Beatrix thought of herself at sixteen, invisible in a different town, cataloguing everything because cataloguing was the only way to touch the world without being touched by it. She thought of the years it had taken her to understand that mattering and belonging were not things you waited to be given.

"No," she said. "It's not weird. Just make sure you matter *to* people, not just *at* mysteries. That's the trap. I lived in it a long time."

He nodded slowly, filing it away—she could see him filing it—and left, and the archive felt warmer after he'd gone, which made no sense and complete sense.

* * *

Detective Ilsa Reyes came on a Tuesday, unannounced, with a manila folder and two coffees from the gas station, which was the best coffee in Saltmere Bay because the bar at the hotel had closed and standards had fallen accordingly.

"The estate's frozen," she said, dropping into the chair across from Beatrix's desk without waiting to be invited. Reyes did not wait for invitations; Beatrix had decided this was one of her better qualities. "Probate court's untangling the Vane holdings. Ambrose retained counsel, then fired counsel, then retained counsel again. His lawyer says he's cooperating fully, which means he's terrified. The ledger did what ledgers do—it made everything arithmetic. Dates, payments, signatures. Juries can't argue with arithmetic."

"And Cordelia?"

"Cordelia gets a headstone now. A real one, with a date of death. The county's handling it." Reyes opened the folder and slid a photograph across the desk: a granite marker, blank, awaiting its engraving. "There's a question, though. About the inscription. The family's all disgraced or scattered, so the town council asked the—well." She cleared her throat, and Beatrix realized with a jolt that the detective, who had faced down a murderer on the tidal flats and a hostile press corps in the county seat, was embarrassed. "They asked if you'd write it. The epitaph. Since you're the one who—since you knew her best, in the end. Better than her own family ever did."

Beatrix stared at the blank stone in the photograph. Somewhere in her reconstructed reading of Cordelia's diaries there was a line, a girl's handwriting full of plans and contempt and hunger for a life larger than Saltmere Bay: *I will not be a name on their stone. I will be a name of my own.* She had died before she could manage it. But names could be given back. That was, Beatrix thought, the whole point of archives.

"I'll need a few days," she said.

"Take a week." Reyes stood, then hesitated, hands in her jacket pockets, and Beatrix recognized the posture—someone deciding whether to cross a professional line. "For what it's worth. I've worked cases where the town fought us tooth and nail. Doors slammed, witnesses coached, evidence walked into the sea. Saltmere Bay didn't do that. Saltmere Bay sent me a teenager with photographs and a landlady with a thirty-year-old memory and a baker who wouldn't shut up. This town wanted the truth. It just needed someone to ask out loud." She shrugged. "Anyway. The stew on Sunday. Harriet invited me. I'm told attendance is mandatory."

"It is," Beatrix said. "I checked."

* * *

Sunday smelled of saffron and fish and woodsmoke, and Harriet's kitchen was too small for four people and exactly the right size. Milo claimed the corner seat and narrated the arraignment for the benefit of Reyes, who had been there and corrected him twice, gently, enjoying it. Harriet moved between stove and table like weather. And Beatrix sat with a bowl in her hands and realized, somewhere between the second helping and Milo's impression of Ambrose Vane's lawyer ("he says 'my client' the way other people say 'help'"), that she had not thought about her office, or her files, or the case, for nearly three hours.

Three hours. A personal record since the letter had arrived.

Afterward they washed dishes together in Harriet's narrow sink, four sets of hands, an assembly line that required cooperation and produced bickering, and Beatrix dried the bowls and thought: *this*. Not the dramatic belonging of the funeral or the trial or the headline. This—elbows bumping, steam on the window, Harriet telling Reyes she used too much soap, Milo stealing a last piece of bread. Belonging, it turned out, was not a door that opened. It was a thing you built, badly and daily, out of Sundays.

Walking home along the cliff path in the dark, the sea below her doing its ancient work, Beatrix let herself feel the strangeness of it: that she had come to Saltmere Bay to hide inside a dead girl's mystery, and the mystery had instead handed her back to the living. Her mother had hidden the truth to protect her. The truth had protected her better. The truth had given her Harriet's honesty, Milo's loyalty, Reyes's respect, a town learning awkwardly to say her name.

At her gate she paused. The light in her office window was off, as she'd left it. But the envelope taped to the door was new.

Not a threat. She knew threats now; she knew their weight and their handwriting. This was cream-colored, personal, addressed in a careful elderly hand: *Miss Hartwell — regarding the stone. If you have a moment. E.M., Gull Cottage.*

E.M. Edith Marsh, the retired schoolteacher, ninety-one years old, who had taught half the town—including, Beatrix remembered suddenly, a young Cordelia Vane, class of 1996.

Beatrix stood in the dark with the envelope in her hands, and above her the stars wheeled over the bay, indifferent and beautiful, and she felt the old familiar pull—the pull of a story not yet finished, a voice not yet heard, a detail waiting in someone's memory like a shell waiting in the tide. Tomorrow she would shelve the alphabetized file. Tomorrow she would start the blank notebook. But tonight—

Tonight there was one more letter to open, and for the first time in seven years, Beatrix Hartwell was not afraid of what it might contain. She was curious. Curious, and known, and home.

She carried it inside, and put the kettle on, and began to read.
