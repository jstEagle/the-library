## 10. A Room With Two Locks

The dust told on them before anything else did.

Marisol Vane had grown up in that study. She knew its silences the way other people knew their mothers' voices — the particular hush of the ledgers on the north shelf, the way the afternoon light came through the leaded window and laid itself across the desk like a cat too dignified to be petted. And she knew the dust. Her grandfather had been a man who believed dust was a form of honesty; he'd refused to let the housekeeper touch his study more than once a season, saying, *A room should show you where it's been touched.* So when Marisol pushed open the door on a Tuesday morning and saw the bookshelves wearing fingerprints like accusations — clean swaths through twenty years of gray — she stood very still in the doorway and let the cold walk up her spine one vertebra at a time.

Someone had searched Elias Vane's study.

Not ransacked it. That was the unsettling part. Nothing was broken, nothing thrown, nothing overturned. The intruder had been careful, reverent almost, lifting each book and setting it back with its spine aligned to the shelf edge. But they hadn't known about the dust. They hadn't known that her grandfather kept a tin of peppermints in the third drawer and that the drawer now sat two millimeters shallower than it should, or that the blotter had been turned ninety degrees, its ink stains rotated like a compass needle pointing somewhere wrong.

Marisol closed the door behind her and leaned against it.

*Think,* she told herself, in her grandfather's voice, which was insufferable but effective. *Who comes into a dead man's house looking for paper?*

The answer arrived with unpleasant promptness: whoever had arranged for an insurance audit to exhume a coffin that had no business being exhumed. Whoever had drowned Rui Baptista last spring and called it a slip on wet rocks. Whoever ran this town from behind a face so respectable that questioning it would get you pitied rather than arrested.

She crossed to the desk and knelt, running her fingers along the underside until she found the loose panel her grandfather had shown her when she was nine years old, back when secrets were games instead of inheritance. The cavity was empty. Of course it was empty. It had been empty for years — because whatever he'd hidden there, he'd taken with him.

"Hello, Marisol."

She came up off the floor so fast she cracked her head on the desk's knee hole, which was, she reflected through the stars, exactly the sort of undignified end her grandfather would have found hilarious. Isandro Hartwell stood in the doorway of the study, hands visible at his sides, wearing the expression of a man who has just realized he has startled someone holding, potentially, a fire poker.

"I knocked," he said. "Twice."

"The front bell doesn't work. It hasn't worked since 1998." She straightened, rubbing her head, and took stock of him: the insurance man with the memory like a vault door, standing in her grandfather's study uninvited, his eyes already moving over the shelves with that unnerving stillness of his — the look of someone who read rooms the way other people skimed newspapers, once, completely, forever. "And yet here you are. In my house. Should I assume you're here to sell me additional coverage? Because I have to tell you, your company's record with our family is not inspiring."

"I'm not here on behalf of the company."

"No," she said. "I didn't think so. The company thinks the exhumation was vandalism. Someone filed a complaint Tuesday morning. Before the coffin was even reinterred properly." She watched his face do something small and controlled — confirmation landing, being filed away. "You know who filed it."

"I have a suspicion."

"You have a photographic memory and a suspicious mind. It's practically a superpower. Shame about the complete lack of evidence." She gestured at the shelves. "Well? You've been staring at my grandfather's books for a full minute. Tell me what you see, Mr. Hartwell. Impress me."

He hesitated. Then, quietly, as if the walls might be taking notes: "Somebody searched this room. Recently. Within the last three days. They went through every volume on the north wall — history, law, hymnals especially. They checked behind the maps. They opened the desk drawers and put everything back within a centimeter of true. They were looking for a document, and they didn't find it, and they left the way they came, which means they had a key or a lockpick or—" he paused "—or permission."

"Permission," Marisol repeated.

"There are no scratch marks on any of the locks. And the window latch is painted shut under a fresh coat. Someone let them in, or someone in this house is lying to you."

The cold came back, walking up her spine again, but this time it brought anger along as company. "My mother lets no one in. The housekeeper has been with us thirty years and steals nothing but small change from her own purse by accident. Which leaves," she said, "the granddaughter who spends most of her time in Lisbon and has an alibi made entirely of train tickets."

"Or," Isandro said, "the granddaughter who was here Sunday."

The silence stretched between them like a held breath.

Marisol had planned, on the boat ride over from the mainland — no, that wasn't right, there was no boat, there was only the long drive through the cork oaks — she had planned to handle Isandro Hartwell the way she handled all dangerous things: quickly, charmingly, and with maximum misdirection. She had a whole repertoire. But standing in her grandfather's dust-disturbed study, watching this man refuse to flinch, she felt the plan crumple in her hand like a note passed under a dinner table.

"Yes," she said. "I was here Sunday. I come when I can. I sit in his chair and I talk to him like he can hear me, and if you repeat that I will deny it with such conviction that you'll doubt your own memory, and we both know how rare that is." She lifted her chin. "So. Two people searching a dead man's study. One of them broke nothing and found nothing. The other one is you. What were you looking for, Mr. Hartwell?"

"A ledger," he said simply. "Half of one. Water-damaged. Written in Elias Vane's hand. I found it in the chapel on Hymn Row Nine, behind the loose stone under the ninth pew, and then I lost it again, because the county archive burned down Monday night and someone decided that anything connected to it should burn too."

The words went through her like a fishhook finding flesh.

She thought of the music box on her dresser in Lisbon. She thought of the letter folded inside the false bottom, in handwriting she'd know anywhere, addressed to *my Marisol, when you are ready*, which she had carried unopened across two countries and eleven years because some part of her had always understood that ready was a country she might never reach.

She thought: *he visited me. He really visited me. All those summers, the old man in the gray coat who smelled of river water and pipe smoke, who taught me hymns on the chapel piano and never once explained why.*

"Sit down," Marisol said.

"I'd rather—"

"Sit down, Mr. Hartwell. You look like a man who hasn't slept, and I'm about to say something I've never said to anyone, and I'd prefer you hear it sitting, so that when you fall off the chair I can feel superior."

To his credit, he sat. On the edge, like a bird ready to fly, but he sat.

* * *

She made tea first, because her mother had raised her to believe that no revelation should be delivered without something warm in the listener's hands — it gave them somewhere to put their fear. The kitchen of Vane House still smelled faintly of cinnamon and old copper, and Marisol moved through it on autopilot while her mind ran ahead of her, cataloguing everything she'd ever noticed and never let herself understand.

When she came back, Isandro was examining the framed photograph on the mantel: Elias Vane at fifty, stern as a ship's figurehead, with a small girl on his shoulders whose grin suggested she had recently done something forbidden.

"That's you," he said.

"That's me. I had just released all the chickens from their coop. He told my mother he'd caught me doing it, but actually he'd held the ladder." She set the tea down. "He liked accomplices, my grandfather. He collected them. Gravediggers. Housekeepers. Granddaughters." She wrapped her own hands around her cup. "Tell me about the half-ledger."

He told her. He told it plainly, without performance, which she was beginning to suspect was the only mode he had: the coffin full of river stones, the cipher in the tool marks, the hymn numbers, the names and sums, Rui Baptista drowning last spring with staging marks on the rocks. The archive fire. Chief Cordeiro's smooth, patient smile. He recited the decoded fragments from memory, and Marisol felt the hair rise on her arms, because he didn't pause, didn't grope, didn't approximate — he delivered the dead men's names like a priest reading a parish roll.

"You really do keep everything," she said.

"Everything," he agreed, and for the first time since she'd met him, he looked tired of it. "Do you know what it's like, carrying a list of murders in your head? There's no putting it down. No drawer to close. I used to think it was a gift."

"And now?"

"Now I think it's a witness box." He looked at her over the rim of his cup. "You said you had something to tell me."

Marisol set down her tea. Walked to the door. Checked the hallway — empty, her mother at the market, the housekeeper singing to the radio two floors up — and came back.

"When I was seven," she said, "an old man started appearing in my summers. Gray coat. Pipe. Smelled like the river. My mother said he was a family friend. He taught me hymns — always the same ones, always in order, and if I got the order wrong he'd tap my knuckles with the hymnal, gently, like he was keeping time." She smiled despite herself. "He also taught me to pick locks, cheat at cards, and identify forged signatures by the pressure of the pen. I assumed all grandfathers' friends did this."

Isandro had gone very still. "Your grandfather faked his death. You're telling me he visited you after."

"I'm telling you that for eleven years, a dead man climbed through my window and taught me music." She let that land. "And the summer I turned eighteen, he gave me a music box. Rosewood, brass pins inside the lid — it plays a hymn when you wind it, number nine, naturally, the man had a sense of humor like a crypt. And he said: *One day a stranger will come asking questions with better answers than yours. When he does, give him the box.*" She met Isandro's eyes. "Then he said, *and Marisol — check the dust.*"

"The dust," Isandro repeated.

"He knew someone would search this study eventually. He wanted me to know it happened — wanted me to understand that the searchers were real, that the danger was real, that I shouldn't dismiss the stranger when he came." She laughed, short and bitter. "I thought it was an old man's poetry. Then I opened this door this morning and saw the fingerprints in the dust, and I heard his voice so clearly I nearly answered it."

Isandro set his cup down with exaggerated care, as though the saucer might detonate. "The music box. Where is it?"

"Lisbon. In my apartment. Behind a false panel I built myself, because apparently paranoia is hereditary." She tilted her head. "But I'll tell you what I remember about it, since you collect things. Brass pins inside the lid, arranged in rows. I counted them once as a girl — forty-one pins, in five uneven rows. I asked him what they were for and he said, *they're the second key, little one. A lock with two keys keeps honest both the people who hold them.*"

Isandro's breath went out slowly. "A lock with two keys. The cipher in the coffin lid is keyed to hymn numbers — the substitution grid. But a substitution cipher alone is breakable. Add a transposition keyed to the pin pattern, and neither half works without the other." He was staring past her at the middle distance, and she could nearly see the machinery turning. "He split the key. Gave half to the coffin and half to a child. Because a child grows up loyal to no one but him."

"Loyal to *him*," Marisol corrected softly. "That was the trouble with my grandfather. He loved you so thoroughly you couldn't tell where the affection ended and the assignment began." She looked down at her hands. "I spent years angry at him for dying. Now I find out he didn't even do that properly."

For a moment neither of them spoke. Outside, wind moved through the cork oaks with a sound like slow applause.

"Marisol." He said her name carefully, the way you set down something heavy. "The archive burned Monday night. Tomás Ferro — the gravedigger — I spoke to him four days ago, and now I can't reach him. Cordeiro offered me a promotion yesterday with a smile like a closed door. Every person who touches this gets closer to the fire, and you just told me you've been carrying the second key for eleven years without knowing it. If Sampaio's people—" he stopped himself, but too late.

"Sampaio," Marisol said. "Aurelio Sampaio. Yes. I know the name. Everyone knows the name; he endowed the library." She watched Isandro's face and understood, suddenly, how alone he had been — carrying the whole burning thing in his skull, unable to write it down, unable to say it aloud, trusting no one because everyone was either bought or frightened. She recognized the condition. She'd inherited it along with the cheekbones.

"Here's what I propose," she said. "We stop testing each other. It's exhausting, and we're both bad at pretending. You have half a key and a perfect memory. I have the other half and a music box in Lisbon and a grandfather who trained me for exactly this conversation without either of us knowing it. Alone, we're two people the town will bury politely. Together, we're a problem."

"Together," Isandro said, "we're also a target with twice the surface area."

"Everything worth being is." She stood and extended her hand, formal as a treaty. "Rules, Mr. Hartwell. One: no more entering my house unannounced, or I will set the dogs on you, and by dogs I mean myself with a fire poker. Two: we write nothing down. Ever. Your head and mine are the only safe in this country. Three:" she paused, and found the next rule waiting in her chest, fully formed, slightly terrifying, "three: when this is over — however it ends — we tell each other the truth. All of it. Even the parts about the chickens."

He took her hand. His grip was cool and steady, and something in her chest — the part that had spent eleven years guarding a locked box of grief — turned over like a page.

"Agreed," he said. "Although I should warn you, rule three may be difficult. I've noticed you lie beautifully."

"I've noticed you don't lie at all, which is far more suspicious." She released his hand and walked to the window, where the light was going amber over the hills — over the vineyards and orchards that had been stolen twice, once by ink and once by silence. "We leave for Lisbon tomorrow, first ferry. You'll want to see the box."

"Actually," Isandro said, and there was something new in his voice, something taut as wire, "there's something you should see first. Tonight, if you're willing. It's about your grandfather — about where he lived, those twenty years. I worked it out from the tide tables and the ledger coordinates, and I need someone to tell me I'm not mad."

Marisol turned from the window. Somewhere below them, in the dark under the town, she imagined the river moving the way it always moved, patient, keeping everything.

"Mr. Hartwell," she said, reaching for her coat, "you are absolutely mad. Fortunately, so am I. Let's go."
