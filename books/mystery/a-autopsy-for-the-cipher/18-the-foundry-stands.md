## 18. The Foundry Stands

The ledger's coordinates had been written in a dead man's shorthand: *where the river remembers the fire.* Isandro had spent two evenings over the map of Vale do Ouro before the phrase surrendered its meaning. The old foundry. Of course. Every cipher in Elias Vane's hand circled back to the foundry eventually — the place where the town's fortune had been smelted, and where, apparently, its conscience had been buried.

"You realize," Marisol said from the passenger seat, her boots on his dashboard in a way that suggested she considered the car communal property, "that we are driving at night, on an unmarked road, toward a flooded ruin, following instructions left by a man who faked his own funeral."

"When you put it that way—"

"I'm saying I approve," she said. "I just want it noted for the record. If there is a record. Which there won't be, because we're about to become two more entries in my grandfather's very long list of people who died doing his errands."

"Your grandfather didn't die doing errands. He died writing the truth."

"He died in a flooded basement with ink-stained fingers and no one to bring him soup." Her voice was light, but Isandro heard the grief underneath it, worn smooth now like a river stone. "The truth doesn't keep you warm, Isandro. Believe me. I've been sleeping next to family secrets for thirty years and I still own exactly one good blanket."

He glanced at her — the profile sharp against the dark windshield, the small defiant smile she wore like armor — and felt the familiar treacherous warmth in his chest. He was beginning to suspect it would not be going away.

* * *

The road ended where the map promised it would end, which was to say abruptly, in a curtain of blackberry bramble and a rusted chain across two leaning posts. Beyond it, the land sloped down toward the river, and there — half-swallowed by ivy and twenty years of forgetting — stood the foundry.

It was larger than Isandro had imagined. A cathedral of brick and iron, its great arched windows blind with grime, its chimney leaning against the sky like a drunk against a lamppost. The floodplain around it was silvered with standing water, and the building itself sat in a shallow bowl that the river had claimed decades ago and never quite given back.

"The ledger said the water level drops in autumn," Marisol murmured, reading the decoded fragment again by flashlight. "*'When the eel grass shows its teeth, walk the third arch.'*" She looked up. "It's October."

"So we walk the third arch."

They picked their way down through the reeds, flashlights hooded with their palms. The air smelled of cold iron and river mud and something older underneath — coal ash, maybe, or memory. At the third archway, a low door stood ajar, its hinges long since eaten to lace. Inside, the darkness was total.

Isandro stopped at the threshold. His photographic memory laid the scene out before him even as his eyes adjusted: the flooded floor, black and mirror-still; the catwalk above, sagging but intact; and there, bolted to the far wall, a wooden cabinet with a brass plate screwed above it.

The same brass plate as the coffin lid.

"Elias," Marisol breathed beside him. "You absolute dramatic old man."

There was a rowboat. Of course there was a rowboat — Vane had thought of everything, right down to the oars wrapped in oilcloth and hung from a nail. They pushed off into the flooded hall, the water so calm their lights seemed to travel beneath them into some drowned twin of the world. Overhead, the iron ribs of the roof vanished into shadow. Somewhere, water dripped with the patience of a metronome.

"It's beautiful," Marisol said quietly. "In a horrifying way."

"That's the family motto, isn't it?"

She laughed — a real laugh, startled out of her — and the sound went up into the dark rafters and came back changed, softer. "Careful, Mr. Hartwell. Keep making me laugh in a haunted foundry and I'll start thinking you're the one."

The cabinet stood on a raised platform of brick, dry above the waterline, and it opened without resistance. Inside, wrapped in waxed canvas and tied with twine gone brittle as biscuit, lay a bundle of notebooks, a tin box, and — folded with terrible neatness — a man's coat.

Marisol lifted the coat out and held it a long moment. Wool, gray, mended at one elbow with thread that did not match. "His," she said. "This is his. My mother kept a photograph — he wore this at the dedication of the school. I used to think she was imagining things when she said he'd have hated being buried in the fancy suit they put him in." Her voice caught. "He wasn't buried at all."

"No," Isandro said gently. "He was here. Working."

They carried the bundle up to the catwalk, where a rusted workbench offered a surface, and unwrapped it by flashlight. The notebooks were dense with Vane's small, precise hand — the same hand Isandro knew from the coffin cipher, grown shakier toward the end but never less determined. Dates. Names. Sums. Confessions dictated with the thoroughness of a man who knew he might be interrupted at any moment and intended to leave nothing to chance.

And then the tin box, and inside the tin box, what neither of them had dared quite hope for.

Photographs. Dozens of them. Forged wills photographed page by page, signatures enlarged, compared side by side with genuine specimens. Correspondence. A bank draft with Aurelio Sampaio's name on it, dated three weeks after Elias Vane's funeral — paid, per the notation in the margin, *"for services rendered to the estate, i.e., for burying me twice."*

Marisol made a sound that was half laugh, half sob. "He photographed everything. He documented his own murder-in-advance."

"Not murder-in-advance." Isandro turned a page with care. "Insurance. He knew that if anyone ever came looking, they'd come to destroy. So he built a second archive. Paper proof, to go with the cipher in my head." He looked up at her, and the weight of it settled onto him fully for the first time. "Marisol. This is enough. This would stand in any court in the country."

"Yes," she said. "Which is why we have perhaps an hour before someone comes to make sure it never sees one."

* * *

She had noticed it first — or rather, her grandfather had noticed it first, twenty years ahead of her, because the last notebook contained a final entry that read less like testimony than like a will:

*If you are reading this, then you have come by boat in the dark, which means you are either my granddaughter or a fool, and I pray you are both. Know this: Aurelio will not let paper sit. He burned the probate office rather than let it speak. When he learns the foundry stands, he will send men with kerosene. Count on it as you'd count on rain.*

"There's a watch post," Marisol said, sweeping her light along the catwalk. "Look — he kept watch. That window faces the road." She crossed to it, wiped the grime with her sleeve, and peered out. Then she went very still.

"What?"

"Lanterns." Her voice had dropped flat and cold. "Two of them. Coming down the hill. Not fishermen — fishermen don't carry jerry cans."

Isandro joined her. Below, in the blue hour before true dark, two figures moved along the embankment with the unhurried confidence of men on familiar errands. One of them set something down by the bramble line. Even at this distance, the gesture was legible: a man setting down fuel.

The dread that moved through Isandro was strangely calm, almost administrative. *So this is how it ends,* some clerk in his mind noted. *Drowned in a foundry with a dead founder's laundry.*

Then Marisol took his hand, and the clerk lost his train of thought entirely.

"He wrote that we'd stop running," she said. "Last page. Listen — I memorized it too, don't look so surprised, photographic memory runs in families whether or not it's polite to mention." She recited, quiet and fierce: "*'To whoever finishes this: do not run with the truth. Truths carried while running get dropped. Stand somewhere and let them see you hold it.'*"

Below, a match flared — a tiny orange star in the gathering dark.

"They're going to burn it," Isandro said. "With us in it, if we're unlucky, and without checking either way."

"Then we have a decision to make, and we should make it quickly." She turned to face him, and in the flashlight's glow her eyes were bright and absolutely certain. "We can take the notebooks and run — save ourselves, maybe lose half of it in the river, spend the rest of our lives looking over our shoulders while Sampaio's lawyers pick apart whatever washes up. Or—" she nodded at the photographs, the drafts, the coat, the whole patient archive of a dead man's courage, "—we stand somewhere and let them see us hold it."

"The Founders' Hall," Isandro said slowly. The idea arriving whole, the way his best ones always did. "Tomorrow night. The anniversary ceremony. Every family in the valley under one roof, including Cordeiro, including Sampaio. We don't run with it. We *read* it."

"That is either the bravest thing I've ever heard or the stupidest."

"In my experience those arrive together."

She grinned — quick, wild, alive — and kissed him, brief and hard, tasting of river air and resolve. "For luck," she said, in a tone that suggested luck could mind its own business.

Below, the smell of kerosene reached the catwalk.

They worked fast after that. The notebooks went into the waxed canvas, the canvas into Isandro's satchel, the satchel strapped tight across his chest. The photographs Marisol divided between her coat pockets, muttering an inventory under her breath like a rosary. The brass plate from the coffin — recovered from the evidence lockup in a moment of corruption Isandro chose not to examine too closely — she tucked into her waistcoat, over her heart.

At the doorway to the rowboat, Isandro paused and looked back once at the great dark hall, at the workbench where an old man had spent his exile writing himself back into existence, at the black water holding all of it safe until tonight.

"Thank you," he said aloud, to the room, to the man, to the dark.

"Don't thank him yet," Marisol said, pushing the boat off. "Thank him tomorrow, in front of everyone."

Behind them, through the third arch, the first tongue of flame licked up the old brick — and found, as fires always do in places built to withstand fire, only stone and stubbornness. The foundry would burn slowly, reluctantly, complaining the whole way down, like everything else in Vale do Ouro that refused to die quietly.

They rowed into the current, away from the orange glow, toward the lights of the town — toward the hall, the ceremony, the assembled descendants of four forged wills who had no idea what was coming to dinner with them tomorrow night.

"Hold it steady," Marisol said, watching the shore grow closer. "Tomorrow we stop running."

"Tonight," Isandro corrected, and pulled for the bank.
