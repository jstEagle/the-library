## 19. High Water

The foundry smelled of iron and old rain, the way a church smells of incense and old prayer — as if the building itself had been keeping a vigil all these years. Water dripped somewhere in the dark with the patience of a metronome. Our flashlight beams crossed and uncrossed over the flooded lower chamber, where Elias Vane had spent his last two decades writing down every crime this town had committed against itself.

"Cozy," Marisol said, her voice echoing off brick. "Grandfather always did have a flair for real estate."

"He lived here," I said quietly. "Twenty years. In the dark, under the town, watching everyone walk over his grave that wasn't a grave."

She didn't answer right away. Her light found the table first — a carpenter's bench, dry only because it sat on a raised stone platform above the waterline — and on it, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with string gone soft as cloth, the second half of the ledger. Beside it, a tin cup. A pair of spectacles folded with their arms crossed, like a man who expected to come back.

Marisol touched the spectacles and made a small sound I pretended not to hear.

"We should take everything," she said. "All of it."

"The ledger's what matters."

"The ledger is *paper*, Isandro." She looked at me, and in the flashlight glow her face was all angles and stubbornness. "The spectacles are my grandfather."

So we took both. That decision — sentiment over sense — is the only reason I'm alive to write any of this down, though at the time it felt like the opposite of wisdom. It usually does.

* * *

We were on the platform, the oilcloth open between us, when we heard the sound.

Not footsteps. Footsteps would have been merciful. This was water — a great swallowing rush of it, somewhere above us, followed by a metallic groan like the building clearing its throat.

"They've opened the sluice gates," I said. The words came out flat, factual, the voice I used for reading actuarial tables aloud. "The old millrace feeds straight into this chamber. Sampaio would know. He drafted the deed that decommissioned the foundry."

"You can tell that from a *sound*?"

"I can tell it from the fact that someone just tried to drown us, yes."

The water was coming fast now. We could see it in the flashlight beams — a black sheet sliding across the lower floor, climbing the brick, carrying twenty years of silt and rust ahead of it like an honor guard. The chamber was built like a bowl. Everything below the platform would be under four feet of water within minutes. Everything on the platform would follow shortly after, because whoever designed the floodgates had clearly never been asked to design an exit strategy.

Marisol grabbed the ledger. "The boat. Tomás kept a skiff moored at the—"

"The mooring is on the far side of the chamber. Which will be underwater. Which means swimming, which means—" I looked at the ledger, at its swollen, water-battered pages, already fragile as autumn leaves. "It won't survive the swim."

"Then we hold it over our heads and we *swim well*."

"Marisol."

"Don't you *Marisol* me in that insurance voice. This is the evidence. This is the whole case. Without it you're a man shouting numbers in a town square."

She was right. That was the terrible arithmetic of it, and no one has ever accused me of being bad at arithmetic. A perfect memory proves nothing — Sampaio had told me so himself, calm as Sunday, and he'd been right too. The paper was the case. The paper was Elias Vane's hand, his ink, his confession, the physical weight of twenty years of truth.

And the water was at our ankles.

I did the thing I do. The thing that has made me strange at parties and invaluable at work and, apparently, useful in drowning chambers. I opened the ledger to the final pages and I began to read.

"What are you doing?" Marisol shouted over the roar.

"Reading."

"*Now?*"

"There is no later!" And there wasn't. There was only this: the last entries in Elias Vane's long confession, written in a hand that had grown shakier over the years but never less precise. I fixed them. Page by page, line by line, the way I fix everything whether I want to or not — the date Aurelio Sampaio forged the fourth will; the sum paid to Chief Cordeiro's father to lose a file in 1987; the name of the man who had helped Vane fake his death, a ferryman dead eleven years now, betrayed by his own silence; and then the final entry, dated three days before Vane actually died, which read:

*I have said everything. Let the water keep what it wants. The rest lives in whoever reads this.*

He'd known. Of course he'd known. He'd spent twenty years underground preparing for exactly this moment, and he'd left the door open for a stranger with a head full of other people's paperwork.

The water hit the platform. Cold shot up through my boots, and the ledger pages fluttered like something trying to take flight.

"Isandro." Marisol's hand closed around my collar. "Tell me you're done."

I looked at the page. I looked one more time — the habit of a lifetime, the blessing and curse of it — and I closed my eyes and saw it anyway, whole, permanent, printed behind my eyelids in ink that would never run.

"I'm done."

"Then *swim*."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about fighting your way out of a flooding room while holding a century-old ledger over your head: it is not dignified.

The water took the platform thirty seconds after we left it. We swam for the far wall, where Tomás Ferro — bless his suspicious, gravedigging heart — had once mentioned a maintenance ladder bolted into the brick, back when he'd been telling me ghost stories about the foundry to see if I'd flinch. I hadn't flinched. I'd memorized the story. And now the story was saving my life, which I intend to mention at his memorial, assuming either of us lives to attend one.

The current wanted us for the sluice gate. I could feel it pulling at my legs, patient and enormous, the way the town itself had always pulled — gently, inevitably, toward forgetting. My lungs were doing arithmetic of their own. Somewhere behind me I heard the ledger's oilcloth wrapping tear free and go spinning into the dark, and I heard Marisol make a sound like grief and fury sharing a coat.

"Let it go!" I shouted.

"It's *letting go of me*, you idiot!"

We reached the ladder. She went first because she argued faster, and I came up behind her with my arms burning and the spectacles — God knows how — still hooked in my breast pocket, digging into my chest like a small cold hand. At the top, the hatch. Rusted shut. Marisol hit it with her shoulder once, twice, and on the third try it gave with a shriek that I suspect woke half the town, and we spilled out onto the riverbank in the rain, gasping, filthy, alive.

Behind us, water poured from the foundry's broken windows like the building crying after holding it in for twenty years.

We lay on the mud and breathed. Rain fell on my face, and I let it, because it was the first honest water I'd felt all night.

"It's gone," Marisol said finally. "The ledger. All of it. Twenty years of his handwriting, and it's at the bottom of a millrace."

"No," I said.

She turned her head. Mud on her cheek, hair plastered flat, eyes furious and bright, and I thought — absurdly, inappropriately, in the middle of everything — that I had never seen anyone look so much like her grandfather.

"No?"

"It's not gone." I sat up. My hands were shaking, so I pressed them flat against the ground until they stopped. "Every page. Every entry. Every name, every sum, every date, including the ones in the half we found in the chapel and the fragments from the coffin lid. It's all here." I tapped my temple. "Sampaio was right about one thing. A memory proves nothing in a courtroom. But a memory isn't for courtrooms, Marisol. It's for *town squares*."

She stared at me. Then she started laughing — wet, ragged, slightly unhinged laughter, the kind that arrives when terror and relief share a taxi — and pulled the spectacles from my pocket before I could stop her.

"You absolute lunatic," she said. "You memorized a drowned man's entire confession while drowning."

"It seemed efficient."

"And if you'd been wrong? If you'd missed a page?"

"I don't miss pages." I said it without pride. It was simply true, the way my eye color is true. "That's the whole tragedy of me."

She looked at the spectacles in her hands, at the foundry drowning behind us, at me. And then she did something I didn't expect. She reached out and took my shaking hand and held it, mud and all, and didn't say anything kind or comforting at all, which is how I knew she meant it.

"They'll say we burned it," she said eventually. "Or that there never was a ledger. Sampaio will stand up tomorrow and call us vandals and thieves, and half the town will believe him because believing him is easier."

"Yes."

"So what's your plan, insurance man? You going to file a claim?"

I looked out at the river, black and moving, carrying Elias Vane's handwriting out toward the sea. Twenty years of exile, and the water got it in the end — just like he wrote. *Let the water keep what it wants.* But the water couldn't keep the rest. The rest was sitting on a muddy bank holding a dead man's granddaughter's hand.

"The anniversary ceremony," I said. "Founders' Hall. Tomorrow morning. Every family in town, the chief, the mayor, Sampaio at the head table wearing his saint's face." I stood up, and my knees complained, and I ignored them. "They're expecting a eulogy."

Marisol rose beside me, wringing out her sleeve, and smiled the smile I'd first seen across the gate at Vane House — sharp-tongued, amused, dangerous.

"Well," she said. "Isn't it lucky we've got one prepared."
