## 5. Paid in Full

Detective Inspector Ruth Okonkwo had a theory about death certificates, and it was this: the more expensive the handwriting on them, the less you should trust what they said.

Edmund Vale's certificate sat on the passenger seat of her unmarked Vauxhall as she drove up the hill toward Vale House, and it was written in handwriting so expensive it practically had its own coat of arms. *Cardiac event, witnessed by household staff. Certified by Dr. Halloway.* Neat, confident, signed with a flourish that suggested Dr. Halloway charged extra for flourishes.

Ruth did not like it.

She didn't dislike it for any reason she could put in a report. A man of sixty-eight, portly, fond of his own cooking, dies in his sleep three weeks after his doctor told him to lay off the butter — that happened every day in every town in England. But there were small things. The household's account of the evening had been rehearsed-smooth, like a hymn sung too many times. The widow had declined an autopsy with a graciousness that Ruth could only describe as *expedited*. And the funeral had been held eleven days after the death, which was fast for a man of Vale's standing, though not unheard of.

Small things. Ruth had built twenty-two years of policing out of small things, the way other people built walls out of bricks.

Vale House rose ahead of her, all pale stone and tall windows, looking down on the town with the expression of a man who has never once been refused. She parked at the foot of the steps, gathered her folder, and rang the bell.

The door was opened by a butler named Pell — she'd checked the staff list twice on the drive over; names mattered, names always mattered — a long, grey man who looked as though grief had been issued to him along with his livery.

"Detective Inspector Okonkwo," she said. "I telephoned yesterday."

"Madam is expecting you." He said it the way he might have said *the fish is expecting you*, without judgment, without warmth, and stood aside.

* * *

Mrs. Vera Vale received her in the morning room, a bright space that managed to be both luxurious and faintly joyless, like a hotel that had won awards. Vera Vale herself was perhaps fifty-five, silver-blonde, dressed in black so well-cut it made mourning look like a career move. Her handshake was firm and dry.

"Inspector. I confess I'm surprised. I understood the matter was closed."

"Routine follow-up, ma'am." Ruth settled into the offered chair and opened her folder. "We like to be thorough when a gentleman of Mr. Vale's prominence passes. It helps the paperwork sleep at night."

Something flickered across the widow's face — amusement, Ruth thought, quickly folded away. "And does your paperwork suffer from insomnia?"

"Chronically." Ruth smiled and let the smile do its work while she watched. "Tell me about the last evening. Take your time. Details are my favorite food."

Mrs. Vale told it again: dinner at eight, the lamb Edmund had insisted on despite Dr. Halloway's warnings, brandy in the study afterward, Edmund retiring at half past ten saying he felt "perfectly splendid," and Mrs. Danvers — no, that wasn't right, the housekeeper was a Mrs. Coleridge — finding him at seven the next morning, cold, peaceful, one hand hanging off the bed as if reaching for something.

"Reaching for what?" Ruth asked.

"I couldn't say. Perhaps the bell pull. Edmund hated being alone with anything, even sleep."

It was a good answer. It was almost too good — shaped, rounded, finished. Ruth wrote it down anyway. Good answers were still answers.

"And the brandy. His usual?"

"His usual," said Mrs. Vale.

"Which was?"

A pause. Small, but Ruth caught it the way a cat catches the movement of a curtain. "He kept a particular bottle. A gift from a friend. I'm afraid I don't recall the label."

"You don't drink it yourself?"

"I take sherry," said Mrs. Vale, with the finality of a woman closing a drawer.

* * *

The staff gave her the same story in the same order, which was itself interesting. In Ruth's experience, honest households told stories like weather — each witness adding their own clouds. This one told it like scripture. The cook, Mrs. Coleridge, wept a little and meant it, Ruth judged; the housemaid, a nervous girl called Tilly, wept a great deal and Ruth suspected only some of it was for Mr. Vale. The gardener hadn't seen him in a week. Pell confirmed everything and volunteered nothing, standing at parade rest as though the interview were a formality between nations.

"Did Mr. Vale receive any unusual correspondence lately?" Ruth asked the room at large. "Letters, packages, visitors?"

Silence, of the kind that has furniture in it.

"He was a benefactor," said Pell at last. "Cathedral, hospital, the school. Letters came constantly. People wanting things."

"People always want things," Ruth agreed. "Anyone specific? Anyone he saw privately?"

Pell's eyes went, very briefly, somewhere over her shoulder — toward the window, the drive, the town beyond — and came back. "Not that I'm aware, madam."

*Madam*. Even to a police inspector. Ruth decided she liked Pell the way one likes a locked door: with professional respect and a growing certainty about what's behind it.

* * *

She found the archive by accident, which was to say she'd asked directions to the lavatory and taken a wrong turn through a corridor that smelled of paper and cold stone, and come upon a door standing open onto a room that looked like the inside of a very tidy mind.

Shelves to the ceiling. Boxes labeled in a small, precise hand. A desk under the window, and behind the desk a thin man in a cardigan the colour of weak tea, sorting envelopes into piles with the concentration of a monk and the posture of a question mark.

"Oh," he said, standing so quickly he knocked a pen to the floor. "Sorry — I mean, hello. Can I help you? This is the archive. I'm the archivist. Cyrus Montrose."

"Detective Inspector Okonkwo." She showed her warrant card, mostly because watching people read warrant cards told her things. "I'm speaking with the household about Mr. Vale."

"Yes. Of course. Terrible business." He bent for the pen, missed it, bent again. "Though of course it wasn't — terrible, I mean. It was peaceful, they say. Expected."

"Expected deaths are my favorite kind," said Ruth. "They require no investigation whatsoever, and yet here I am."

He laughed — a short, startled sound, as if laughter had caught him without his coat on — and then looked mortified at having laughed. Ruth filed that away too. Grief made people strange, but guilt made them strange in patterns.

"Mr. Vale was a great friend to the cathedral," Montrose said, recovering. "He donated his papers. His correspondence, financial records, that sort of thing. I've been cataloguing them."

"May I see?"

His hands went flat on the desk. Just for a moment — a stillness like a rabbit's — and then he was moving again, briskly, gesturing her in. "Of course. They're — most of it is quite ordinary. Charity letters. Dinner invitations. There's a surprising quantity of menus."

"Men love a menu," said Ruth solemnly, and stepped past him into the room.

The boxes were arranged with military precision. She lifted a few papers at random: invitations to a harvest supper, a letter from the school thanking Vale for a new organ fund, a bill from a wine merchant that would have fed her street for a month. Ordinary. Catalogued. Too catalogued, maybe — everything smoothed flat, everything in its place, nothing left loose the way real lives leave things loose.

"You knew him well?" she asked, not looking up.

"Not well. We spoke sometimes. About books." A pause. "He liked to be useful. To institutions, I mean. Not — people are harder to be useful to."

"That they are." She set down the menu she was holding — pheasant, naturally — and turned. "One last thing, Mr. Montrose. Did Mr. Vale ever mention anyone who might wish him harm?"

The archivist went very carefully blank. It was a practiced blankness, she thought — the blankness of a man who had spent years making sure his face said nothing at all, deployed now slightly too early and slightly too hard.

"No," he said. "No one. Everyone loved him. That's rather the point of men like Edmund Vale."

*Everyone loved him.* Ruth nodded, thanked him, and walked back down the corridor smelling of old paper, thinking: *that's exactly what worries me.*

* * *

In the car she ate a cheese sandwich and made notes, because notes were how she prayed.

Cause of death: cardiac event. No autopsy. Family declined. Doctor signing off was Halloway — she'd look into Halloway, his age, his eyesight, his relationship with the Vale chequebook. Household accounts consistent to the point of liturgy. Widow composed. Butler locked. Archivist nervous.

The archivist interested her most, which annoyed her, because there was no reason he should. A shy man flustered by a police inspector was a dog bites man story. But it wasn't the flustering. It was the moment before it — the flat hands, the rabbit-stillness — and then the smoothness after, the way he'd produced *everyone loved him* like a coin from behind her ear.

People who hid things badly were usually hiding small things. People who hid things *well* were hiding large ones, and Montrose hid well until he forgot to. Which meant whatever he was hiding, he wasn't used to being asked about it. Which meant it was old. Old secrets got polished smooth with handling.

She started the engine. On the seat beside her, the death certificate gleamed its expensive handwriting up at her, and beneath it she'd clipped a copy of the funeral notice — *deeply mourned by his wife Vera and all whose lives he touched* — and below that, in her own blunt pencil, a list:

*Halloway. Brandy bottle. Why 11 days? Who visits a dead man's papers first?*

That last one she'd underlined twice. Because here was the thing she kept turning over like a stone in her pocket: the papers had gone to the cathedral within days of the funeral. Donated, catalogued, shelved. A grieving widow moving that fast was either very efficient or very eager, and either way, someone had wanted Edmund Vale's paper life locked safely inside a building where nobody but one nervous little archivist would ever look at it again.

Or — she corrected herself, pulling out onto the road — where nobody would look at it except whoever knew how to find what was hidden in it.

She drove back toward the station through streets she'd known for six years, past the cathedral rising grey and patient above the rooftops, its memorial plaques catching the afternoon light. Half the town's grandest names were carved on those walls, she thought idly. Benefactors. Pillars. Men whose generosity was the largest fact about them.

Her phone buzzed against the dashboard. The lab, confirming receipt of the samples she'd quietly requested from the body before burial — blood and tissue, held in storage pending her signature. Routine. Thoroughness helping the paperwork sleep.

Except that as she pulled into the station car park, the phone buzzed again, and this time it was the pathologist's office, and the voice on the other end was apologetic and puzzled in equal measure, and it said words that made Ruth sit very still in the parked car with the engine ticking:

"...the preliminary tox screen on the Vale sample, Inspector. There's digitalis compound in the blood. Nothing dramatic — nothing you'd necessarily catch if you weren't looking. But it's there. And it shouldn't be."

Ruth looked up through the windscreen at the cathedral, grey and patient above the town, and reached for her notebook.

"Well then," she said softly. "Let's go and disturb somebody's sleep."
