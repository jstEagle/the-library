## 18. Why Fell Killed

Detective Inspector Ruth Okonkwo had a theory about paperwork, and it was this: nobody lies in ink unless they're hiding something worth lying about. People lie to their spouses over breakfast. They lie to their doctors about how much they drink. But they lie in documents only when the truth would cost them everything.

Which was why she'd spent three days at the county records office, elbow-deep in boxes that smelled of dust and other people's secrets, looking for a man who had died ten years ago.

"Tea?" asked the junior archivist, a round young man named Pelly who seemed to regard her presence as the most exciting event of his professional life.

"Please," she said, and did not look up from the death certificate.

*Daniel Harrow. Age 41. Drowned. Body recovered from the Millbrook weir, identified by dental records.* Signed by a coroner whose name she'd already checked — retired now, living in Whitby, and when she'd phoned him he'd been pleasant and vague in equal measure. *Dental records, yes, that's how we did it back then. Terrible business. The family was devastated.*

Except there hadn't been much family. A sister, who had signed the cremation forms and then, according to every record Okonkwo could find, never visited the grave. Not once in ten years. Not on anniversaries, not on birthdays.

People visit graves, Okonkwo thought. Even people who hated the deceased. Especially people who hated the deceased. You go to make sure they're still in there.

She turned the certificate over, as if Daniel Harrow's death might have something written on its back. It didn't. Just the watermark of the county council and a coffee ring left by some clerk a decade dead to this office.

Pelly returned with tea in a mug shaped like Big Ben. "Found what you were after?"

"Not yet." She sipped. It was terrible tea. She drank it anyway; you didn't get good tea and good evidence in the same building, that was just physics. "I want everything on Daniel Harrow. Birth, school, employment, tax, the lot."

"That'll take a while."

"I've got a while." She pulled her chair closer to the boxes. "And another pot of tea wouldn't hurt anyone."

* * *

It took until Thursday, and what emerged was a portrait of a man assembled entirely from paper, which is to say a portrait with all the warmth of an insurance claim.

Daniel Harrow: born 1958 in Sheffield, father a steelworker, mother died young. Left school at sixteen. Worked warehouses, worked docks, worked — here the record went briefly strange — as a bookkeeper for a shipping firm despite no documented training in bookkeeping. Then nothing. Then the weir, the dental records, the cremation, the silence.

Okonkwo sat back and looked at the gap the way she'd been taught to look at gaps, which was not as absence but as architecture. Something had been built in that space between the dock work and the drowning. Someone had taught a warehouse labourer to keep books. Someone had given him teeth that could be identified from records — because here was the thing that had kept her up half the night, chewing it like gristle:

The dental records matched. Perfectly. A man with no money, no history of dentistry beyond whatever the NHS scraped out of him, had a set of records on file precise enough to confirm identity after a drowning.

You only have dental records like that if someone paid for them. And you only pay for someone's dental records if you're planning to use them to identify a body.

Or to disappear one.

She thought about Edmund Vale, wealthy benefactor, generous to the cathedral, dead three weeks now in his own locked crypt with a heart that had allegedly simply stopped. She thought about the ledger Cyrus Montrose had shown her — or rather, the pages of it Montrose had *not* shown her, the four entries cut away with a blade so sharp it might have been surgery. One of those missing names, Montrose had finally admitted, corresponded to a man who had died mysteriously a decade ago.

Montrose had said it reluctantly, in the archive, with candlelight making him look like a painting of a man rather than a man. He'd held things back. She knew he had. There was a whole geography of evasion behind his eyes, and she was learning to read its map.

But he'd given her the thread, and Ruth Okonkwo had built a career on pulling threads until either the sweater unravelled or somebody asked her to stop.

Daniel Harrow had drowned. Daniel Harrow had excellent dental records. Daniel Harrow had a sister who never visited his grave.

And Marcus Fell, head verger of St. Aldhelm's Cathedral, a man beloved by every parishioner, remembered every name, every birthday, every grief — Marcus Fell had come from nowhere. That was the phrase in his own file, the one the diocese kept on its employees. *Previous employment: various. References: available upon request.*

Nobody had ever requested.

* * *

"You're staring," said Sergeant Adeyemi, sliding into the chair across from her at the café near the station. He ordered a flat white without asking, which was one of the reasons she tolerated him.

"I'm thinking."

"You're staring at a photograph of the head verger of a cathedral like he owes you money."

"He might owe me a murder." She turned the photograph around. Fell at the cathedral fete, holding a tray of scones, smiling with the particular patience of a man who has smiled at strangers for thirty years. "Tell me what you see."

Adeyemi considered it over the rim of his cup. "A nice man?"

"Look again."

"A nice man who's tired of being photographed."

"Try: a man with no past." She laid her printouts on the table between them, fanned like a losing hand. "Marcus Fell appears in the historical record eleven years ago. Before that, nothing. No school records under that name, no employment, no National Insurance trail before age fifty-two. It's like he was issued fully formed from the diocesan office."

"People fall through cracks."

"People don't fall through *every* crack." She tapped the death certificate. "Daniel Harrow fell through exactly one crack, and it was filled with water, and ten years later a man with Harrow's hands — I checked the verger's personnel file photo against the dock worker's ID card, calluses don't lie, Adeyemi — is greeting widows at the cathedral door."

Adeyemi put down his cup. The humour drained out of his face, replaced by the careful blankness he wore when a case stopped being interesting and started being real. "You think the verger is a dead man."

"I think the verger is a man who bought a death. And I think Edmund Vale sold it to him." She gathered the papers. "And I think Vale started selling it *again*. Recently. To the same customer."

"Blackmail."

"Blackmail." She said the word quietly, though the café was loud enough to swallow it. "Ten years of tithes and donations and quiet service, and then Vale needs money — or leverage, or just likes the taste of it — and he goes back to the well. Except this time the well has a family. This time the well has a life worth defending with more than money."

Adeyemi was quiet for a moment. "You sound like you feel sorry for him."

"I feel sorry for everyone," Okonkwo said. "It's a professional hazard. Sympathy doesn't change the charges." But even as she said it, she felt the shape of the thing pressing against her chest, uncomfortable and true: somewhere in this town was a man who had drowned himself on purpose, climbed out of the river as someone new, and spent a decade being genuinely, painstakingly good at kindness. And when the old debt came calling, he had paid it the only way left to him.

She'd seen monsters. Monsters enjoyed it. She had a feeling Marcus Fell had hated every minute, right up until the moment he didn't stop.

* * *

Canon Aldous had died fourteen months ago. Heart failure, in his sleep, in the canonry. Open and shut, signed off by a GP who had since moved to Spain.

Okonkwo pulled the file anyway, because open-and-shut deaths kept arriving in this case like uninvited guests, and because she had learned that in St. Aldhelm's parish, convenient timing was less a coincidence than a local industry.

The will was straightforward. Books to the cathedral library. A modest sum to a niece in York. And one curious bequest: *to my colleague C. Montrose, my desk, and whatever papers he finds therein, to do with as his conscience directs.*

His conscience. Okonkwo wrote the word in her notebook and underlined it twice. People didn't put phrases like that in wills by accident. That was a message. That was a man saying, *I know what I'm leaving you, and I know what it will cost you, and I'm sorry, and I trust you anyway.*

So what had Aldous known?

She went back to the timeline, the one taped inside her notebook cover, growing more crowded by the day. Fourteen months ago: Aldous dies. Three weeks ago: Vale dies in his crypt. Two days later: an anonymous confession letter arrives at the archive, postmarked the day after the funeral. Since then: a fire in the parish registry, the sexton Tom Brack found unconscious on the crypt steps, four entries razored out of a ledger that had sat untouched in a strongbox for years.

Someone was cleaning house. Someone with access, with knowledge, with steady hands and a reason to be in the cathedral at all hours without raising an eyebrow.

And here was the arithmetic that made her stomach tighten: Aldous dies fourteen months ago. The ledger sits in Vale's strongbox. Who else knew Aldous had discovered the cuts? Who else had both the proximity to Aldous and the desperation about the ledger?

She thought about the confession letters. Both written in a disguised hand — but disguise, Montrose had told her, showing her the samples with the reluctance of a man handing over a piece of himself, disguise has habits underneath. The letterforms leaned the way a teacher's letterforms lean. The penmanship of someone trained by the same hand.

Trained by Aldous.

Cyrus Montrose had been trained by Aldous. That much was in the archive's own records, plain as a hymn number.

But so, she realized, sitting very still in the records office while Pelly hovered hopefully with more terrible tea — so had half the cathedral's staff, if you counted confirmation classes and choir instruction and thirty years of a verger's patient tutelage under a canon who liked to teach. Aldous had been a teacher the way lighthouses are lamps. Everyone in that building had passed through his light.

Including, she would have bet her pension, a man named Daniel Harrow, sometime in the first year of his new life, learning to write his new name in a hand that wasn't quite his own.

* * *

Friday evening she walked to the cathedral, not on official business — she told herself it wasn't official business — but because the light was going gold over the rooftops and she wanted to see the place the way the killer saw it. Not as a crime scene. As a home.

The evening service had just ended. Parishioners spilled down the steps in twos and threes, and at the door stood Marcus Fell, shaking hands, murmuring condolences to a woman in black, lifting a child onto his shoulder with the easy competence of a man children trusted instinctively.

Okonkwo hung back by the lychgate and watched.

He was good at it. That was the thing that struck her, watching him work the crowd like a host at his own eternal dinner party. He wasn't performing. Or rather — she corrected herself — he was performing, but he'd been performing so long the performance had grown roots. Somewhere along the way, Daniel Harrow's borrowed smile had become Marcus Fell's actual one. The name was fake. The care was real.

He caught her eye across the churchyard. Of course he did; a verger notices everyone, that was the job description. He inclined his head — courteous, unhurried — and returned to the widow in black.

Not guilty, she thought. Guilty men avoid the police officer's gaze or hold it too long. He did neither. He acknowledged her the way you acknowledge weather.

She turned to go, and nearly walked into Cyrus Montrose, who was emerging from the shadow of the lychgate with the startled air of a man who had been standing there considerably longer than coincidence required.

"Inspector."

"Mr. Montrose." She studied him. He looked worse than last week — thinner, greyer, with the specific exhaustion of a man who has been sleeping badly beside a secret. "Evening walk?"

"Something like that."

"In the churchyard. At dusk. Near the verger."

His eyes flicked toward the cathedral door, where Fell was locking up, and something crossed his face too fast for her to catalogue. "I could ask you the same question."

"You could. I'd give you a better answer than you'd give me." She let that sit. Then, because she was tired, and because the evening was beautiful, and because she suspected — had suspected for days — that she and this careful, frightened archivist were digging toward the same grave from opposite ends, she said: "Daniel Harrow."

Montrose went very still. Stillness, in Okonkwo's experience, was the loudest sound a person could make.

"Drowned," she continued, conversationally. "Ten years ago. Excellent dental records. No grave visitors. And a pair of hands I've seen folding altar linens every Sunday since." She watched him. "You knew. Didn't you. When you showed me the ledger with four pages cut out — you knew whose entry was missing."

The bells began the quarter-hour above them. Montrose waited them out, and when he spoke, his voice was low and roughened. "I suspected. Now I'm certain. And Inspector — there's something else. About Canon Aldous. About why he really died."

"I'm listening."

"He didn't die in his sleep." Montrose glanced once more at the darkening cathedral, at the small lit window where the verger was still moving, putting away hymnals, closing up a building he loved. "He called me the week before. Said he'd found something in the ledger. Said names had been taken out — cut — and that he knew whose hand held the knife. He said he was going to tell Vale it had to stop." Montrose's jaw tightened. "Then he died. And then Vale died. And now the confessor wants me to bring the ledger to a meeting, or the next fire takes the archive — and everything in it. Including me."

Okonkwo felt the case turn over in her chest like a key finding its lock. "When is this meeting?"

"Tomorrow night," Cyrus Montrose said. "In the crypt."
