## 21. What the Law Can't Touch

The courtroom smelled of lemon polish and old wool, and Cyrus Montrose spent the better part of the morning discovering that a witness box is simply a confessional with worse lighting.

He had testified before—once, years ago, in another life under another name, about a flood-damaged deed and nothing more—but never like this. Never with Marcus Fell sitting three meters away in a grey suit, hands folded, wearing the patient expression of a man who had greeted ten thousand parishioners by name and could not now remember which of those names were real.

"State your occupation for the court," said the prosecutor.

"Cathedral archivist," Cyrus said. The word *cathedral* steadied him. He had learned, over these strange months, that when his voice wanted to shake, he could anchor it to stone.

"And how did you come to be involved in the investigation of Edmund Vale's death?"

"I received a letter."

The letter itself was in evidence now, sealed in a plastic sleeve that made it look less like a confession and more like an insect pinned for study. Cyrus had read it so many times he could recite it in the dark: *Edmund Vale was murdered. The proof sleeps where he meant to sleep. The crypt keeps what the crypt keeps.*

"Did you know the handwriting?"

Cyrus let the pause stretch exactly as long as it needed to. This was the arithmetic he had done a hundred times in the weeks since Fell's arrest, subtracting everything dangerous from everything true and testifying only to the remainder.

"It resembled the penmanship I was taught as a young man," he said. "By Canon Aldous. The accused studied under him as well."

The prosecutor nodded, satisfied. That was all she needed from him on that point—that the letters came from someone inside Aldous's small school of careful hands. She did not need to know that Cyrus had recognized, in the second letter's flourishes, a habit of his own that Aldous had drilled into both of them: the way the crossbar of a *t* dipped slightly left, like a man glancing over his shoulder even in ink.

She did not need to know that for one terrible week, Cyrus had wondered if the hand was his own, written in some fugue of guilt he couldn't remember. He had sat up two nights with a magnifying lamp comparing his own correspondence against the confession before concluding that no—he wrote his *t*s like a man facing forward. It had been Fell who learned to flinch.

"And the ledger," the prosecutor continued. "The one recovered from the crypt. You examined it?"

"I did."

"What did it contain?"

Here it was. The hinge. Cyrus looked at the defense table, at Fell's solicitor—a sharp, decent woman named Pryce who had already signaled, through channels Cyrus didn't want to think too hard about, that her client would plead to manslaughter if the Crown's case stayed narrow—and then at the gallery, where Vera Vale sat in black that had gone soft at the elbows from wear rather than age.

"The ledger contained financial records," Cyrus said. "Payments received by Mr. Vale. Names, dates, amounts."

"Names of whom?"

"People who had purchased documents from him. Passports, certificates, records of baptism. New histories." He kept his voice level, almost bored, the way one describes weather. "Mr. Vale was, in plain terms, a forger of lives. A very good one."

A murmur went through the gallery. Somewhere near the back, Tom Brack—the sexton, recovered now but walking with a cane and a new caution about stairwells—made a sound that might have been a cough or might have been a laugh.

"And did you identify any of these names?"

"No," Cyrus said.

It was not precisely a lie. He had identified four of them, cut from the strongbox ledger by Fell's own blade, and he had identified them in the only place they still existed: in his memory, which he intended to take to whatever grave he eventually occupied, under whichever name the stone bore.

The prosecutor moved on. She asked about the night in the crypt, and Cyrus gave her the parts that mattered: how he had lured Fell with a fabricated cipher, how Fell had come with a can of lamp oil and a box of matches, how the door had locked behind them because Cyrus had asked Tom Brack to hold the key until dawn. He told it plainly, without triumph. There was no triumph in it. There had been a moment—Fell lunging for the lantern, both of them going down among the coffins, water from a burst pipe sloshing ankle-deep between the tombs of people who had mostly been invented—when Cyrus had understood that he might die in the dark next to the man he'd trapped, and that the ledger would burn anyway, and that some part of him had planned for exactly that contingency and felt no shame about it.

He did not tell the jury that part either.

* * *

The verdict came on a Thursday, grey and drizzling, the kind of day the cathedral wore best. Guilty of murder. Fell received fourteen years, and when the sentence was read he did not weep or rage; he simply turned, as the bailiffs took his arms, and found Cyrus in the gallery, and mouthed something across the room.

Cyrus read it easily. He had spent months reading mouths and margins and the silences between entries.

*Thank you.*

That was the horror of it, and the truth of it. Fell had mouthed *thank you*, because whatever else the trial had stripped away, it had not stripped the thing they shared: the knowledge that half the benefactor plaques downstairs belonged to people who had been nobody once, and had paid generously to stop being nobody, and deserved to keep their purchase. Fell had killed to protect a life built from nothing. Cyrus had burned to protect forty others built the same way. They were the same animal in different cages, and only one of the cages had bars.

Cyrus walked home through the rain without an umbrella and let it soak him, which felt appropriate, and also felt like something Aldous would have called *theatrical self-punishment, my boy, at least have the decency to enjoy it.*

* * *

Okonkwo came to the archive on a Monday.

Cyrus heard her on the stairs—that unhurried tread, the one that had haunted him for months, the tread of a woman who believed that every door would eventually open if you knocked politely enough times. By the time she reached the archive door he had arranged himself at his desk with a conservation report open in front of him, performing the role of a man with nothing to hide, which was easier now than it had ever been, because there was very nearly nothing left to hide.

"Detective Inspector," he said. "Come to arrest me for arson? I should warn you the archive has excellent fire suppression. I installed it myself."

"I've come to return something." She set a document box on his desk. Evidence label, case number, the whole bureaucratic liturgy. "Exhibit Forty-Seven. Recovered from the crypt strongbox. Released following conviction."

The ledger. Or what remained of it.

Cyrus looked at the box the way one looks at a sleeping animal that may or may not be rabid. "I assumed the Crown would retain it."

"The Crown retained copies of the pages relevant to the murder." Okonkwo pulled out the chair across from him and sat, uninvited, in the manner of someone who had earned the right to sit anywhere she pleased. "Fourteen pages. Fell's entry, Vale's notes on Fell, the correspondence about the blackmail. Everything else—" she tapped the box— "is yours."

"Mine."

"You're the archivist. It's a historical document pertaining to the cathedral. Seems like your jurisdiction." Her face gave away nothing, which Cyrus had learned was itself a kind of eloquence. "Of course, if you'd prefer, I could take it back to the station and log it into general evidence storage, where it would sit in a basement with a leaking pipe and eventually be catalogued by an intern. Those basements flood, you know. Twice last winter."

Cyrus looked at her for a long moment.

"Detective Inspector," he said carefully, "are you offering me deniability?"

"I'm offering you a box." She stood. "What you do with boxes in your own archive is between you and God, and I understand He doesn't keep minutes down here."

She was nearly at the door when Cyrus spoke, and he hated how quiet his voice came out.

"Why?"

Okonkwo stopped. For a while she didn't turn around, and Cyrus watched her shoulders do something complicated.

"Because I spent four months pulling threads in this town," she said finally, "and every thread led to somebody whose only crime was surviving. A teacher who fled a violent husband and became a different teacher. A man who took his dead brother's name because his own was on a debtors' list that would have followed him past the grave. Your Canon Aldous, whoever he actually was." She turned then, and her eyes were tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. "I became a detective because I believe the truth matters. I stayed one because I learned the truth isn't the same thing as justice, and confusing them gets people hurt. Fourteen years for Fell. That's justice. That's what the law can touch." She nodded at the box. "The rest of it is above my pay grade. And below my conscience."

Then she left, her tread unhurried on the stairs, and Cyrus sat alone with Exhibit Forty-Seven and the sound of the rain.

* * *

He waited three days. Not out of drama—though he suspected Aldous would have accused him of exactly that—but because burning a thing properly required preparation, and preparation required thought, and thought required company he didn't have.

On the third evening, after the cathedral had emptied and the verger's replacement (a nervous young man named Pellow who knew no one's real name and greeted everyone identically, which Cyrus found restful) had locked the west doors, Cyrus carried the box down to the incinerator room behind the boiler house, where the sextons burned broken candle stubs and ruined vestments and other holy debris that could not simply be thrown away.

Tom Brack was already there.

The old man sat on an upturned crate beside the incinerator hatch, a thermos of tea balanced on his knee, as though this were the most natural appointment in the world. Which, Cyrus supposed, it was. Brack had held the crypt's true key for thirty years. Brack had lain unconscious on those steps while Fell walked over him. Brack, of all people, had earned a vote.

"I told Okonkwo you'd do it tonight," Brack said. "She said Thursday, more likely. You're a Thursday sort of man."

"She's known all along, hasn't she. About the missing entries. About—" Cyrus gestured vaguely at everything: the plaques, the parish records, the whole paper architecture of borrowed lives.

Brack poured tea into the thermos cup and handed it over. "Ruth Okonkwo has known since the fire in the registry office. Why d'you think she never charged you? Man burns a registry, he wants the paper gone. You wanted the paper *saved*. Only one person in this parish has been destroying records, son, and he's eating prison food now." He sipped. "Course, she'll deny knowing anything if anyone asks. So will I. So will you. That's generally how knowing things works around here."

Cyrus sat down on the second crate. The tea was terrible and scalding and exactly right.

They opened the box together. The remaining pages lay in their evidence sleeve, and Cyrus lifted them out with the cotton gloves he used for sixteenth-century vellum, because it seemed important that the last thing these pages received was reverence. Forty-one names. Forty-one people who had woken one morning as someone unbearable and gone to sleep as someone survivable. A schoolmistress. A churchwarden. Two doctors. A woman who had given the cathedral its beautiful east window and whose original name, Cyrus knew, was attached to a warrant in a country she would never see again.

"Any regrets?" Brack asked.

Cyrus thought about it honestly, because Brack deserved honesty. "One. That I can't give them back the fear. Every one of them has spent years waiting for a knock on the door. Burning this won't un-wait them."

"No," Brack agreed. "But it means the knock never comes. That's not nothing." He nodded at the hatch. "Go on, then. I'll mind the fire."

Cyrus fed the pages in one at a time. It felt nothing like destruction. It felt like burial—the proper kind, the kind with rites. Each page curled and brightened and went to ash, and he watched each one go, giving each name its moment, a private funeral service for forty-one resurrections. When the last page caught, he saw for an instant the ink glow red before it blackened, all those careful Aldous-taught letters burning in their own handwriting, and he said aloud, to the fire, to the mentor who had taught him script and secrets and how to disappear:

"You should have told me. We could have decided together."

The fire crackled, noncommittal. Aldous had always been better at silence than answers.

When it was done, Brack handed him the last of the tea, and they sat in the warmth like two men who had just finished a long and disagreeable chore, which in every sense that mattered, they had.

"Feel lighter?" Brack asked.

"Heavier, actually."

"Aye. That's how you know it was the right weight." The old man levered himself up off the crate with the cane, wincing, and paused at the door. "Vera's coming to the archive tomorrow. Wants to talk about donating Edmund's papers. Officially, like. You'll want to be..." He waved a hand. "Whatever it is you are."

Cyrus looked at the ash cooling in the incinerator, at the empty evidence box, at the gloves folded neatly on his knee.

"Yes," he said. "I suppose I'll want to figure that out."

Outside, the rain had stopped, and through the boiler house window he could see the cathedral rising black and certain against the clearing sky, its stones holding their secrets the way they always had—not hidden, exactly, just carried, the way foundations carry everything above them without complaint.

Tomorrow, Vera. Tomorrow, the estate, and the question of what he would sign, and what name he would sign it with.

Tonight, for the first time in longer than he could measure, Cyrus Montrose walked home carrying nothing at all.
