## 1. The Date That Wouldn't Die

The Voss house had been dead longer than most of the people Gideon Fairweather buried.

It stood at the crook of Delft Street where the pavement gave up and turned to gravel, its porch roof sagging like a tired eyelid, and for eleven years it had done nothing but hold its breath. Now an estate company from two counties over had pried it open, tagged everything with little yellow stickers, and left the leavings in cardboard boxes on the front lawn under a tarp that flapped all night like something trying to get out.

Gideon had not planned to come. They told themselves they were there as a courtesy — the coroner of Harrow's Bend was often asked to walk through houses before estate sales, to confirm nothing human remained in them, which was a strange way to put a job but an accurate one. In a town this old, people sometimes died in rooms no one entered for years, and someone had to be the last person to know them.

So: professional courtesy. That was the story Gideon carried up the gravel drive on the first Saturday of October, past the hand-lettered sign — ESTATE SALE, EVERYTHING MUST GO — and into a house that smelled of cold dust and the ghost of lavender water, the way all long-shut houses in Harrow's Bend smelled, as if the town itself had once been perfumed and had since sweated it out.

The sale had already happened. What remained were the boxes nobody wanted, and the estate company had called Gideon because the alternative was the dump, and the dump man refused anything with paper in it anymore. Paper, he said, attracted trouble. He hadn't meant it the way it would come to mean.

* * *

Gideon worked the way they did everything: methodically, gloved, without sentiment until sentiment arrived uninvited.

The first box held kitchen things. A flour sifter rusted into a single piece. A jar of buttons sorted by color into a mason jar, blues on top, whites sunk below, a little drowned civilization. The second box held photographs, and this was where the morning began to tilt. Gideon knew the faces the way everyone in Harrow's Bend knew them — by their absence. The Aldermere mill picnic, 1987, half the town squinting into the sun, and in the back row a young woman with dark hair cut blunt at the jaw, her smile doing all the work of a person who had decided to be happy whether or not it came naturally.

Lena Voss. Everyone in town could still draw her face from memory, though most of them would claim they couldn't. Twenty-two years old when she went into the reservoir. Twenty-two forever, because that was what the water did — it kept people at their best age, their last age, and let the living grow old around them.

Gideon set the photograph down with more care than the task required and noticed their own hands were steady, which was the thing about coroner's hands. They stayed steady so the rest of you didn't have to.

The third box was heavier than it should have been. Books, mostly — mildewed paperbacks, a Bible with a pressed fern gone brown as tobacco between Romans and Corinthians — and beneath them, wrapped in a dish towel gone gray, a diary.

Not a diary, exactly. A ledger-style notebook, the kind sold at the hardware store for tracking feed costs, five dollars and change, marbled cover cracked along the spine. Someone had written *Property of Lena Voss* inside the front cover in a hand that slanted hard to the right, urgent even in ownership, as if the name might otherwise escape the page.

Gideon sat down on the porch steps with it, because some part of them — the part that had spent twenty years reading the last written words of strangers — understood that you didn't stand up for certain kinds of reading. You sat down. You made yourself small before it.

* * *

The early entries were ordinary, and that ordinariness was its own kind of ache. Lena Voss wrote about the mill whistle waking her at six. About a pie crust that wouldn't cooperate. About a boy named Danny who took her bowling and talked about himself for two hours "like a man paying rent on my attention." She wrote about her mother's cough getting worse. She wrote about the reservoir in June, how green the water went in summer, how the town pretended it was a lake, a recreation spot, when everyone knew what it actually was: a grave with a scenic overlook.

Gideon read with the professional calm they wore like a coat, the calm that had gotten them through four hundred death certificates and every awkward silence at every funeral potluck. Dates, weather, small grievances. A life in miniature, ticking along.

Then the entries changed.

August 3rd: *Saw R.A.'s car on Delft again tonight. Third time this week. If I'm imagining it, I'm imagining it very consistently.*

August 9th: *Asked Dad if anyone from the mill office had come around asking about me. He got that look. The look like a door closing in a house I live in.*

August 14th: *I found the list. I wasn't supposed to find the list.*

And then — nothing. No entry dated August 15th, the day Lena Voss walked out of recorded history. Her shoes were found on the dam. Her coat was folded on the rail, neat as a napkin. The reservoir was dragged for nine days. Sheriff's department ruled it accidental drowning; the body was never recovered, which the report called *not unusual for the reservoir*, citing the cold currents and the intake pipes, and the town accepted this the way it accepted everything the Aldermere mill asked it to accept: quietly, gratefully, with its eyes on its own paycheck.

Gideon knew all this. It was in the file they'd inherited with the office, a thin folder gone soft at the corners, one of those cases that lived in a filing cabinet the way a splinter lives in a palm — mostly forgotten until pressure found it.

But here was the thing. Here was the thing that made Gideon Fairweather, forty-one years old, sit down harder on the porch steps of a dead woman's house and feel the first crack run through twenty years of professional calm.

The diary did not stop on August 14th.

There were more entries. Eleven more days of them.

* * *

Gideon checked the dates three times. Checked them against the case file memory, against the newspaper clippings tucked in the box — the *Harrow's Bend Courier*, August 16th, RESERVOIR SEARCH ENTERS THIRD DAY; August 21st, FAMILY HOLDS MEMORIAL AS HOPE FADES. The memorial service had been held on a Sunday. The whole town had stood at the water's edge and sung *Shall We Gather at the River*, which Gideon had always thought was either the most comforting or cruelest hymn choice available, depending on what you believed about the reservoir.

And on the day after the memorial — while the town was still passing casseroles to the Voss house, while the sheriff's report was being typed up in careful bureaucratic language — Lena Voss's handwriting had continued.

August 22nd: *They sang at the river today. I could hear it from the tree line. Mom's voice carries even when she isn't carrying. I almost walked down. God help me, I almost walked down.*

August 24th: *The clerk says stay gone thirty days minimum. Says they'll watch the roads till then. Says a girl who drowns shouldn't be seen buying gas.*

August 27th: *New name learning me. It fits worse than my old one but it fits.*

The handwriting was the same throughout — that hard rightward slant, the same cramped g's, the same way she crossed her t's with a single impatient stroke. Gideon had handled enough questioned documents in training to know what forgery looked like: hesitation, tremor, overcorrection. This had none. This was a hand moving at speed, a hand that knew where it was going, a hand writing in the dark of wherever Lena Voss had gone after she died.

Which meant one of two things, and Gideon's mind laid them out on the table like organs on a scale, weighing each.

Either someone had continued the diary after her death — a hoaxer, a grieving sister, a cruel jokester with a steady hand and a sick sense of timing, writing fake entries *while the town searched the water for her body* —

Or Lena Voss had never been in the water at all.

* * *

The sun had moved. Gideon noticed this distantly, the way you notice your own heartbeat. The boxes sat around them on the porch, tagged and worthless, and somewhere down the street a dog was barking at nothing, and the mill whistle blew the noon hour, and Harrow's Bend went on being Harrow's Bend, a town built like a sentence that always ended the same way.

Gideon turned to the final entry.

It was shorter than the others. The handwriting had changed — not the hand itself, but its pressure. The pen had been pressed so hard into the page that the letters had torn through in places, tiny wounds in the paper, and the words crowded each other like people pushing toward a door.

September 2nd:

*He knows I know about the ledger.*

That was all. Four lines' worth of blank space followed, then the notebook simply ended, the remaining pages still crisp and white, waiting for entries that never came. As if the hand that had been writing had been interrupted mid-thought. As if the pen had been taken from it.

Gideon read the line aloud without meaning to. Their voice sounded wrong in the empty yard, too loud, a living sound in a dead place.

*He knows I know about the ledger.*

A ledger. Not the diary — a ledger. Something else, something named, something worth knowing about and worth killing to keep unknown. And a *he*. Unnamed, unexplained, dropped into the last sentence of a dead girl's book like a stone into a well, falling and falling with no bottom to land in.

Gideon sat with it. This was the discipline, the only discipline they had left: sit with the thing until it stopped being a shock and started being evidence. Feelings first, facts second, feelings third. They catalogued their own pulse the way they'd catalogue a wound — elevated, steady, survivable.

But the crack was there now, running through the calm, and Gideon could feel the whole coat of professionalism hanging off them differently, seams showing.

Because here was what twenty years of corpses had taught them: the dead are tidy. However violent the ending, whatever chaos preceded it, the dead themselves settle. They stop moving. They become dates, causes, names in a column. Gideon had built a whole life on that tidiness — on the belief that Harrow's Bend's dead stayed put, stayed quiet, stayed *finished*, and that their job was just to write the period at the end of each sentence.

This diary was not a period. This diary was a comma that had waited twenty-two years in a cardboard box to keep going.

Lena Voss had written eleven days past her own drowning. She had written through her own memorial service. She had heard her mother sing at the river and had not walked down to her. And then she had written one final terrified sentence naming a thing called a ledger, and stopped.

Gideon closed the notebook carefully, the way you close a door on a sleeping person, and held it in both hands and understood, with a dread that arrived not as a flood but as a slow cold rise of water, that they were holding the only document in Harrow's Bend that proved the dead could talk.

The question was who had wanted them silent badly enough to drown them twice — once in the water, and once in the record.

Gideon stood, tucked the diary inside their coat, and looked down Delft Street toward the reservoir road, where the autumn light lay flat and silver as a coin on a dead man's eye.

Tomorrow, they decided, they would walk the cemetery. Every grave by name. Starting with the Voss plot — and the question of what, exactly, was buried under Lena Voss's headstone.

If anything was.
