## 2. Every Grave by Name

Sleep did not come for Gideon Fairweather that night, and so at dawn they did what their mother had taught them to do with a mind that would not quiet: they put on their boots and went to stand among the dead.

Harrow's Bend Cemetery sat on the shoulder of Alder Hill like a congregation that had been waiting a very long time for a sermon. The fog came up off the reservoir in the early hours and pooled between the headstones, knee-deep and slow-moving, so that the graves seemed to float rather than rest. Gideon walked the gravel path from the iron gate without hurrying, breath smoking in the cold, hands jammed in the pockets of the coat they'd grabbed off the hook without looking — their father's old coat, still smelling faintly of pipe smoke and mothballs after all these years.

The diary was in their satchel. They had not meant to bring it. It had simply ended up there, the way guilty things end up in one's possession, pressed against their hip like a second heartbeat.

*He knows I know about the ledger.*

Eleven days past her disappearance. Eleven days of entries in a dead girl's hand, and then nothing, as if whoever had written them had finally run out of air.

Gideon had spent half the night telling themselves it was grief. Lena Voss's mother, Ruthie, had kept writing after the drowning — everyone knew that, or thought they knew it; grief did strange arithmetic in small towns. A mother finishing her daughter's diary, filling it with invented days because she could not bear to leave the pages empty. It happened. Gideon had seen stranger things in twenty years of weighing hearts and measuring livers.

But the handwriting hadn't changed. That was the thing that wouldn't let go of them. If Ruthie had taken up the pen, the hand would have shifted — older, heavier, the letters of a woman in her fifties instead of a girl of nineteen. Gideon had seen enough hands in enough suicide notes to know how grief rewrote a person down to their penmanship. This hand never wavered. Same slant. Same little loop on the *d* that looked like a sail catching wind. Eleven days, and the same nineteen-year-old hand, steady as a metronome, right up until it stopped.

Gideon stopped walking.

They were standing, without having decided to be standing there, at the top of the rise where the oldest stones began — the Voss-era section, the town called it, though no one said *era* out loud. Just a stretch of ground where the granite went soft-edged and the names went hard to read, and where the grass grew in that particular reluctant way grass grows over ground nobody visits on purpose.

The fog moved through the stones like something reading them.

"All right," Gideon said aloud, to no one, to all of them. "Let's do this properly."

Because that was the discipline, wasn't it? That was the whole trade. When a body told you a story you didn't want to hear, you didn't argue with it. You took the inventory. You named every wound, every mark, every absence, and you wrote it down in your own neat coroner's hand, and you let the facts be louder than your fear.

So: the inventory.

* * *

Lena Voss. Drowned June 14th, 1989, age nineteen. Found three days later tangled in the fish weir below the dam spillway, waterlogged past saving, lungs full of reservoir. Ruled accidental. No witnesses. Gideon knew the file by heart now — had pulled it twice more since midnight, once just to hold it, which was not a thing a professional should admit even to themselves.

Her stone stood third from the path, modest granite, the letters already going shallow. *LENA MARIE VOSS. BELOVED DAUGHTER.* Below the dates, someone had cut a small line of verse that the weather had mostly eaten; only the last word survived, and it was *water*, which felt less like consolation than like a threat.

Gideon crouched, touched the cold of it, and let the memory of the file unspool.

Then they stood, and turned left, and walked eleven rows north.

Daniel Okafor. Drowned August 2nd, 1986, age thirty-four. Mill foreman, second shift. Found in the millrace on a Sunday morning when the town was in church, which meant the finding fell to the one man who wasn't — old Amos Trelawney, who'd been half blind and fully drunk and swore until his dying day that Danny Okafor hadn't gone into the race by accident, that his boots were sitting dry on the bank, laced, side by side, like a man who'd stepped out of them to go swimming in his work clothes. Ruled accidental anyway. Amos died two winters later of what the paper called natural causes, and what Amos himself, in his last lucid week, had called *being tired*.

Gideon stood at Okafor's grave a long time. The stone listed his name and nothing else. No beloved daughter, no verse. The mill had paid for this marker — Gideon remembered that now, remembered the line item in some old budget, *memorial expenses*, filed under goodwill. The company had bought him a rock and the town had bought the story, and both purchases held.

Two rows further.

Sylvie Marsh. Drowned May 19th, 1991, age twenty-six. Schoolteacher. New to Harrow's Bend, eleven months in residence, engaged — briefly, quietly — to a man whose family name made the engagement brief and quiet. Found floating near the boat launch with her coat buttoned wrong. Ruled accidental. Her people came up from two counties away for the funeral and left before the flowers wilted, and after that nobody tended the stone, and moss took the surname first, then the first name, until Sylvie Marsh became, functionally, a weathered rectangle with an attitude of mystery.

And then the fourth. The one Gideon had been walking toward the whole time without letting themselves know it.

Thomas Fairweather.

Their father.

Drowned October 3rd, 1993, age forty-one. Coroner of Harrow's Bend, same office Gideon held now, same desk, same squeaky drawer where the scalpels lived. Found below the dam in November ice-water, six weeks missing, identified by dental records and a wedding ring. Ruled accidental. *Slipped on the spillway apron,* the file said. *Intoxication a contributing factor.*

Their father had not drunk. Their father had smelled of coffee and peppermint every morning of Gideon's childhood, and had poured his one glass of holiday sherry back into the bottle when he thought no one saw, because he liked the ritual of it and not the taste.

Gideon had signed off on the re-examination of that ruling themselves, years ago, when they took the office. Had pulled the file, read it top to bottom, found nothing but clean paperwork and a tidy conclusion, and closed it again. Because the alternative was to believe that their own predecessor — their own *father* — had died the way these others died, and Gideon had not been ready, at twenty-nine, to inherit that.

They were not sure they were ready at forty-three either. But readiness, they were learning, was a luxury the dead did not extend.

Four drownings. One decade. All ruled accidental. All within sight of Aldermere water — the millrace, the spillway, the launch, the weir. And now a fifth name, a girl whose grave held no girl, whose diary refused to stop breathing.

Gideon stood among the four stones and felt the shape of it settle over them like the fog itself: not a pattern, exactly. Patterns were things you could show people. This was more like a silence with edges.

* * *

Sheriff Dane Pruitt found them there at half past eight, which surprised Gideon not at all. Pruitt walked the cemetery most mornings — had done it since his wife passed, though he'd deny it under oath — and his truck was as much a fixture of the hill as the gate.

"You look like hell, Fairweather." He came up the path with two cups of gas-station coffee and handed one over without asking, which was the closest thing to tenderness the man permitted himself in public.

"Long night."

"Ruthie Voss's boxes." Pruitt said it flatly, watching the fog. "Word travels."

"Word always travels here faster than the truth does."

Pruitt grunted, which might have been agreement. He was sixty-some, built like the doorframe of an old church, with a mustache going gray in patches and eyes that missed very little and admitted even less. He had been a deputy the year Lena drowned. He had been a deputy for all four of them, Gideon realized suddenly. Four accidental drownings, and the same lawman's shadow across every file.

"I need to show you something," Gideon said, and took out the diary.

They watched him read. Watched the mustache go still. Watched him turn the pages with a care that contradicted everything his face was doing, which was nothing at all. Pruitt had a face like a locked filing cabinet, and Gideon had spent twenty years learning to read the locks.

"Where'd you get this?"

"Estate sale. Ruthie's house sold last month. Everything went in boxes."

"And you're saying this is Lena's hand."

"It is Lena's hand."

"Eleven days after she drowned."

"After she *disappeared*. Three days before they found a body."

Pruitt closed the diary. He did it gently, the way you close a door on a sickroom, and handed it back, and looked out over the graves with an expression Gideon had seen on a hundred faces in this town — the expression of a man deciding how much reality he could afford this fiscal year.

"Grief does strange things," he said.

"That's what I told myself. For about four hours."

"Ruthie lost her only child. People keep writing for them. Keep them alive on paper." He shrugged, enormous shoulders rising and falling like terrain. "I've seen it. You've seen it."

"The handwriting doesn't change, Dane. Not once. Not a tremor. I've read ten thousand hands. Dead men's hands, living men's hands, liars' hands. This is one person, one hand, continuing past the date her body entered the water."

"Or the date somebody *thought* it entered the water." Pruitt said it quietly, and then, visibly, wished he hadn't, because it was the first honest thing either of them had offered, and honesty between the two of them had always been contraband.

Gideon went very still. "Say that again."

"I said—" He shook his head. Coffee sloshed in his cup. "I'm saying a grieving woman can lose track of days. I'm saying leave it alone, Gideon. That's what I'm saying."

"Four drownings in ten years, Dane. All accidental. All in Aldermere water. My father among them." Gideon heard their own voice come out level, which cost them something. "You were at every scene. So tell me I'm wrong. Tell me it's coincidence, tell me it's bad luck, tell me anything with your actual mouth and I'll take it home and sleep on it."

For a moment — one long moment, fog curling between them — Gideon believed Pruitt might. Believed something in the man was leaning toward the light the way a plant leans, helplessly, after years in the dark.

Then the moment passed, the way moments did in Harrow's Bend. Pruitt drained his coffee, crushed the cup one-handed, and put the crushed cup in his coat pocket, because Dane Pruitt littered nowhere and confessed nowhere.

"I'll tell you what I told the state boys in 'ninety-one," he said. "Small towns drown people. Always have. Water's close, folks drink, folks slip. It's sad and it's true and it doesn't need a conspiracy to explain it." He turned to go, then paused, and without looking back added, low: "That diary. Don't file it. Don't photograph it. And don't go digging up anything that's resting."

"Resting," Gideon repeated.

"Everybody in this ground is resting, Fairweather. Let them." He walked down the hill toward his truck, and the fog closed behind him like a curtain drawn by a careful hand.

* * *

Gideon stayed another hour.

They walked all four graves again, slower, and this time they spoke each name aloud, the full inventory, coroner's discipline, voice carrying flat and clear across the stones: *Lena Marie Voss. Daniel Okafor. Sylvie Marsh. Thomas Fairweather.* The fog took the names and gave back nothing.

That was the thing they understood, standing there with numb fingers around a dead girl's diary: it wasn't only the Aldermeres. It couldn't be. Three generations of one family couldn't make a whole town look away on their own. The town helped. The town *wanted* it. Wanted the files closed and the rulings clean and the dead tidy in their rows, because a tidy dead was the difference between a place you could live and a place you had to flee. Every person in Harrow's Bend had a stake in the accident. The mill fed them. The story clothed them. And anyone who unpicked the seam risked finding their own name stitched somewhere they didn't remember sewing it.

Gideon thought of their mother, changing her name, arriving here with a child and no history. Thought of the squeaky drawer, the scalpels, the father who smelled of peppermint. Thought: *what did it cost us? What did we pay?*

They didn't have the answer yet. But they had a question with a body attached to it — a body that had never been in the ground where the town had buried its version of events.

By noon, Gideon was back at the office, pulling the exhumation forms from the bottom drawer, filling them out in their neatest hand.

If Lena Voss wasn't in her grave, Gideon intended to find out what was.
