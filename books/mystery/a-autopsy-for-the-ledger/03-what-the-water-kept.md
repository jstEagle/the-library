## 3. What the Water Kept

The exhumation order took nine days to arrive and cost Gideon three favors, one of which they would never be able to repay. Judge Ellery had signed it with a fountain pen that scratched like a nervous animal, and he'd looked at Gideon over his reading glasses for a long moment before saying, "You're sure about this, Fairweather? That girl's been in the ground twenty years."

"Has she," Gideon said, and the judge had not asked again.

Now, on a gray morning in late October, when the fog came off the reservoir and lay over Harrow's Bend like something drowned itself, Gideon stood at the edge of an open grave in the old section of Millbrook Cemetery and watched two men from the county work a backhoe with the delicacy of men defusing ordnance.

* * *

*June 14th. Mrs. Aldermere stopped me at the mill gate today. She smiled at me the way you smile at a dog you intend to have put down — kindly, patiently, already decided. She said, "You're a clever girl, Lena. Clever girls see too much." I said I didn't know what she meant. She said, "No. You don't." And then she touched my arm, just once, just above the wrist, and her hand was cold as reservoir water.*

*I haven't slept since.*

* * *

The casket came up at 9:40 in the morning.

Gideon knew coffins the way other people knew faces. They had lowered enough of them, sealed enough of them, signed enough certificates to know what a coffin should weigh after twenty years in wet ground — heavier, always, water being patient and thorough, seeping through the best gasket ever machined. The two county men grunted as the straps took the load, and Gideon heard the weight of it, did the arithmetic without meaning to, and felt the first cold thread of vertigo unwind behind their sternum.

It was too light.

A casket holding a body, even a body reduced by two decades of reservoir-adjacent soil chemistry, should have come up like a burden. This came up like a box of laundry. The straps went slack between the men's hands and one of them laughed, embarrassed, thinking he'd lost his grip, and then both of them stood very still on the lip of the hole, looking down at the thing swinging gently on its chains, and nobody laughed anymore.

They set it on the grass beside the tented grave. The cemetery sexton, old Mr. Halloway, who had dug this hole himself twenty years ago and wept at the funeral like everyone else in town, crossed himself twice and stepped back behind the backhoe as if distance could make him uninvolved.

Gideon knelt and worked the latches with gloved hands. The seals were intact. The gasket was intact. Whatever was inside had been inside since the day it was closed.

Inside was river stone.

Not gravel, not fill dirt, not the neat deception of sandbags. River stones — smooth, gray-brown, hand-sized, the kind that collect along the shallows below the dam where the current slows and drops everything it can't carry. Dozens of them. Hundreds. Someone had spent hours gathering them, carrying them, filling the casket by lamplight or daylight or whatever light there was, laying them in rows almost tenderly, and on top of them, folded once, a dress. Lena Voss's blue dress, the one from the photograph in the missing-persons file, laid over the stones like a sheet over furniture in a house where no one lives anymore.

The stones were dry.

Twenty years in the ground, and they were dry. The seal had held because it had been made to hold — made carefully, made by someone who understood that someday, someone might check. Gideon sat back on their heels in the wet grass and stared into the casket and felt the world tilt a degree off its axis and keep tilting.

She was never in the water.

She was never in the ground.

Lena Voss had walked out of that casket and out of this town, and someone — more than one someone; those stones weighed as much as a body, and grief is heavy but not that heavy — had helped her do it, and then every person who stood at this graveside had wept over rocks.

Including, Gideon realized with a slow, sickening turn, themselves. They had been fourteen. They remembered standing at the edge of this crowd in a black coat too big for them, watching their mother hold Aunt Cora's hand, watching the whole town mourn a girl the water supposedly took. Their mother had cried. Gideon remembered that most clearly: their mother crying, quietly, into her glove, and Cora whispering something to her that made her stop.

* * *

*May 30th. I saw the ledger today. I wasn't supposed to. Mr. Pruitt sent me to the clerk's office to fetch a form and the old book was open on the desk while Mr. Fenwick went to lunch, and I only looked because my name was on the page in front of me. Not my whole name. Just "V., L." and beside it a date and the word "pending."*

*Pending. Like I'm a bill. Like I'm weather.*

*I put the book back exactly how it was. My hands shook so badly I dropped the form twice in the hall. I keep telling myself it means nothing. Clerks write things. Towns keep records.*

*But the entry above mine says "resolved." A man named Tomas Reyes. Resolved. And Tomas Reyes has been gone eleven years, and everyone says he ran off to Ohio, and his mother still sets a place for him at Sunday dinner because she doesn't believe it either.*

* * *

By noon the news had traveled the way news travels in Harrow's Bend, which is to say silently, completely, and with malice aforethought. By one o'clock, three people had called the coroner's office to ask whether the disturbance at the cemetery was "really necessary." By two, a man from the Aldermere mill office — not Whit himself, never Whit himself, but a man with a mill badge and a mill haircut — had appeared in Gideon's doorway without knocking and asked, pleasantly, whether there'd been some kind of administrative error.

"No error," Gideon said. "The casket was full of stones."

The man smiled the way Mrs. Aldermere must have smiled at a clever girl at a mill gate. "Rivers move things around. Groundwater does strange work in old sections." He left a card on the desk that Gideon did not pick up until after he'd gone, and the card was blank except for a phone number, and Gideon burned it in the sink.

That evening they sat in the office with the diary open under the good lamp, reading backward now instead of forward, because the direction of the story had changed. It wasn't a record of a drowning anymore. It was a record of a woman learning she was already dead on paper, and deciding to run before the paper became true.

* * *

*June 2nd. I asked my father about Tomas Reyes tonight. He wouldn't look at me. He said, "Some people leave, Lena." I said, "Did he leave, or was he resolved?" and my father went white as flour and told me never to say that word in this house again.*

*So it's a word they all know. It's a word they pass around like a dish at supper, everyone taking their portion of silence.*

*The mill employs half this town. The other half sells to it, marries into it, buries its dead. There is nowhere here that isn't Aldermere ground. Even the church pews were donated. Even the cemetery fence. I checked. There's a little brass plate: A GIFT OF THE ALDERMERE FAMILY. They gave us the fence around our own graves. I suppose that's a kindness. I suppose that's a warning.*

* * *

*June 11th. Three days ago I found the second diary page torn out. Not removed cleanly — torn, hurriedly, by someone going through my room. My father swears it wasn't him. I believe him, which frightens me more, because if it wasn't him, then someone else has a key.*

*Today a car followed me home from the library. Dark sedan, mill plates. It parked across from our house for two hours. When I waved at it, it drove away slowly, the way you drive when you want someone to know you're leaving but not why.*

*I am done pretending this is a town. It's a machine, and the machine has noticed a loose thread, and machines don't warn threads. They cut them.*

*There's a woman at the clinic who owes my mother. She says she can get papers. She says there's a road out past the reservoir that the state patrols don't watch after dark. She says if I go, I go all the way, and I never write, and I never call, and everyone I love becomes a stranger I used to know.*

*I used to think dying was the worst thing. Now I understand there are worse things. There's being erased politely. There's a word in a ledger where your name should be. There's a brass plate on a fence.*

*If anyone reads this: I did not drown. Remember that. Whatever they say happened at the reservoir, I did not drown. I chose the smaller death, the one you can walk away from.*

*He knows I know about the ledger.*

* * *

Gideon closed the diary and sat in the dark of the office for a long time. Outside, the fog had come up from the reservoir again, pressing against the windows like something with lungs.

The case wasn't cold. It had never been cold. It was alive, breathing somewhere out beyond the tree line, wearing a name and a uniform and a pair of shaking hands, and every day it stayed alive was a day the machine could find the thread and finish cutting it.

Gideon picked up the phone, dialed the hospice where Aunt Cora lay sleeping, and asked to speak with whoever had the night shift.

The voice that answered was warm, professional, unfamiliar.

"This is Marisol," it said. "How can I help?"

And beneath the warmth, faint as a song through a wall, Gideon thought they heard the water.
