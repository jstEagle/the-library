## 2. Alphabetical Order of the Dead

The call came at 6:47 in the morning, which was wrong in itself. Dana Okafor did not call at dawn unless something had gone past the edge of what her department could hold.

"Edmund." Her voice had the flatness she used when she didn't trust herself to shape it any other way. "We pulled a man out of the reservoir last night. I need you down here."

"Insurance?"

"Worse." A pause long enough for me to hear a gull somewhere behind her, screaming its one note over the water. "It's Paul Ashcombe."

I sat with the phone against my ear and let that name settle into me like a stone dropped into a well I'd thought was dry. Paul Ashcombe. Thirty-one years old. Son of Vivian Ashcombe. Son—

Son of Lena.

"I'll be there in twenty minutes," I said.

I was there in fourteen.

* * *

Marrow's Bend in October wears its beauty like a widow wears jewelry—correctly, and without joy. The reservoir lay flat and pewter-colored under a sky the color of old newspaper, and the crime scene tape snapped and settled, snapped and settled, keeping time with nothing. Okafor met me at the tape line, and I saw before she said anything that this was not a consultation she wanted to give me. It was one she needed to survive.

"You underwrote his policy," she said as we walked the gravel toward the boathouse ramp. "Three months ago. Term life, half a million, beneficiary listed as the estate. That makes it yours by every rule we've got."

"Who found him?"

"Kayakers. Six-forty yesterday evening. He was floating face-down about forty yards off the east bank, near the old swimming dock." She stopped walking. She is a tall woman, Dana Okafor, and she has a way of stopping that makes the ground itself seem to have made the decision. "Edmund, before you see him—I need you to look at the clothes first. Not the body. The clothes."

That should have struck me as strange. It didn't, because nothing about the morning had been ordinary enough to make strangeness register. We came around the corrugated wall of the evidence tent, and there they were: four items laid out on a folding table beneath a work lamp, photographed twice over, each on its own sheet of brown paper like specimens in an entomologist's drawer.

A gray wool overcoat. A white dress shirt, monogrammed *E.T.* at the cuff. A navy silk tie with a diagonal stripe of burgundy—the tie my father gave me when I passed my adjuster's examination, the tie I wore to every settlement meeting for twenty years because I believed it was lucky. And a pair of black leather gloves, size medium, the left one creased across the palm where mine always creased.

My coat. My shirt. My tie. My gloves.

Except they were not mine, because mine were hanging in my closet eleven miles away, and I had looked at them last night while I ate my dinner standing up, the way I do, cataloguing without meaning to: coat on the third hook, shirt freshly laundered, tie knotted loosely over the closet rod, gloves on the shelf above. All present. All accounted for.

These were copies. Or these were the originals and my closet held ghosts. Either way, someone had taken the contents of Edmund Thornbury's wardrobe and dressed a dead man in them, item by item, and then arranged those items on a table in an order that made my stomach drop through the floorboards of the world.

Coat. Gloves. Shirt. Tie.

C, G, S, T.

Alphabetical.

"They're staged," I heard myself say. My voice sounded like it belonged to a younger man, one who hadn't learned yet what staging meant. "Not discarded. Arranged. Whoever put them here wanted them read, not found."

Okafor nodded slowly. "We figured that much. Read how?"

I didn't answer right away, because answering required me to admit what I was seeing, and admitting it would drag thirty years of sediment up into the light. But Dana was watching me with those patient, unblinking eyes, and I understood that she had already guessed more than she was saying. She had called me at dawn. She had asked me to look at the clothes first. She knew whose they were. Of course she knew; the monogram was stitched into the cuff in thread I could count from three feet away.

"C, G, S, T," I said finally. "Coat, gloves, shirt, tie. Alphabetical order. Left to right, top row first." I pointed without touching. "But it's not just alphabetical. It's alphabetical by *category*. If he'd simply emptied a closet, the order would be arbitrary or chronological. This is curated. This is a filing system."

"A filing system," Okafor repeated.

"I keep files," I said. "Twenty-two years of claim files on this town. Every death, every fire, every flood, every accident that paid out and every one that didn't. It's how I work. I index everything." I looked at the tie, at the burgundy stripe, and felt the memory press against the inside of my skull like a hand against frosted glass. "Someone is indexing back."

* * *

They let me view the body at nine o'clock, after the county examiner had done her preliminary work and covered everything but the face. Paul Ashcombe had his mother's jaw. That was the first thing, and it hit me harder than the water in his lungs ever could—Lena's jaw, Lena's stubborn chin, translated thirty years forward onto a face that had never once smiled at me. I had seen photographs of him growing up, the way I'd seen photographs of everyone in Marrow's Bend, filed and cross-referenced in the archive behind my eyes. School pictures. A wedding announcement from a marriage that lasted two years. A fishing license renewal, age twenty-nine, weight one-ninety.

He weighed less now. Drowned men lose their argument with gravity quickly.

"Time of death between six and ten p.m. Tuesday," the examiner said. "Water in the lungs consistent with drowning. Contusions on the right temple, antemortem or perimortem—we can't say yet whether he went in conscious. No defensive wounds on the hands." She paused. "There's something else. You'll want to hear it from me before you hear it from a rumor."

"Go on."

"We recovered fibers from his airway. Wool fibers, gray wool, matching the overcoat on your table. They were aspirated—breathed in while he was still alive and breathing." She let that sit. "He drowned wearing your coat, Mr. Thornbury. Or someone put it on him very close to the moment he went into the water."

I stood very still. This is a skill I have practiced all my life, stillness, because a photographic memory is only useful if the body holding it doesn't shake. I stood still and I let the facts file themselves: gray wool, aspirated, antemortem or perimortem, east bank, old swimming dock.

The old swimming dock.

Thirty years ago there had been a dock there too, before the county rebuilt it, and on a night in late April of 1994 there had been a woman on that dock, and I had stood on the bank in the cold with my notebook open and written down four statements, one after another, each one clean as a hymn, and I had believed them—or I had decided to believe them, which in the ledger of a life amounts to the same entry—and I had ruled the death accidental, and the town had exhaled, and the Ashcombe name had stayed whole, and I had gone home and slept, God help me, I had slept.

Lena Ashcombe. Filed under A for Accident.

"Mr. Thornbury?" The examiner was looking at me oddly.

"Paul's mother died in this reservoir," I said. "In 1994. I handled the claim."

"I know," she said quietly. "Dana told me."

Of course she had. Dana Okafor owed me her career—twenty years ago I had vouched for a young deputy everyone else wanted to fire, and she had never forgotten it, and I had never once mentioned it, and now she was watching me stand over a dead man wearing my clothes with the particular gentleness people use on someone they suspect is already guilty of something.

* * *

She walked me out along the east bank afterward, away from the techs, until the tape and the gulls and the hum of the generator were behind us and there was only the water, flat and patient, keeping everything it had ever been given.

"I'm asking you formally," she said. "Consulting on the policy angle. Beneficiary review, financial motive, the standard sweep. Nothing more than that." Then, softer: "And unofficially, I'm asking you to tell me if there's something I should know. About the family. About '94. About why somebody would dress a corpse in your wardrobe and lay it out like a card catalogue."

The wind came off the reservoir and moved through the bare trees with a sound like paper being turned.

"There are things I should know," I said carefully, "that I don't know yet. When I know them, you'll hear them from me."

It was not a lie. It was also not the truth, and we both knew the architecture of the sentence, and she let it stand because that is what small towns run on—not lies exactly, but sentences built to bear weight without bearing witness.

"One more thing," she said as we turned back. "The staging. You said it was a filing system. Alphabetical. Is there—" she hesitated, and I watched a woman who had faced down armed men in the parking lot of the VFW search for a way to phrase a question, "—is there a reason someone would use *your* system? Your order?"

Because it is my order. That was the answer, and it went through me like cold water through cloth. Twenty-two years of claims indexed by category, alphabetized within category, cross-referenced by date. Coat, gloves, shirt, tie. C, G, S, T. Anyone who had spent time in my office, anyone who had ever watched me work, anyone who had studied me the way I had studied this town—studied it the way a lepidopterist studies moths, pinning each one neatly, labeling each one, certain that the pins were harmless—

Anyone who had ever been a moth.

"I don't know," I said.

* * *

That night I could not sleep, so I did what I always do instead: I opened the archive. Not the steel cabinets in my office—the other one, the one behind my eyes, the one no one can subpoena. I lay in the dark and I walked the aisles of it, shelf by shelf, and I pulled the folder marked ASHCOMBE, LENA — CLAIM NO. 4471, and I began, as I have done ten thousand times with ten thousand files, to read.

Every page was there. Every page was perfect. The date of the report. The weather that night—light rain beginning at nine-forty, temperature forty-one degrees. The four statements, word for word, because I remember word for word, I have always remembered word for word, it is the one gift and curse I was issued at birth and have never learned to set down. Vivian Ashcombe, at home with her husband Gerald, playing bridge, two guests to confirm it. Gerald Ashcombe, corroborating Vivian, adding that Lena had seemed "low lately, talking about the water." The marina owner, Roy Pell, closing up, seeing her car in the lot at eight-fifteen, seeing nothing after. The young deputy, first on scene, statement taken at the dock itself.

Four alibis. Four clean pages. A flawless report. Too flawless—I had thought that even then, some small clerk in the back office of my conscience had stamped it and shelved it and said nothing—and now, lying in the dark with a dead man's face floating behind my eyelids, wearing my coat, breathing my coat, I understood that the flawlessness was itself the signature. Real reports have ragged edges. Mine had none, because I had sanded them, or because someone had handed me wood already smooth.

And then I reached page nine.

Page nine of Claim No. 4471. I knew there was a page nine. I could feel the shape of it the way you feel a missing tooth—not the tooth, but the absence, the gap the tongue keeps returning to. In my memory the file ran eight pages and then resumed at page ten, seamless, unremarked, as though the ninth had never existed. And here was the thing, the thing that made me sit up in the dark with my heart going like a fist on a door:

I could not remember what had been on it.

I remembered the color of the ink. Black, my usual black, the Parker pen I bought in 1991 and used until 2009. I remembered the weight of the clipboard on my knee. I remembered rain starting, the smell of the water, the deputy's flashlight beam jittering across the dock boards. I remembered writing. I could feel the motion of the writing in my hand, the specific fatigue in the wrist.

But the words themselves were gone. Not faded. Gone—cleanly excised, the way a surgeon removes a tumor and leaves the surrounding tissue untouched. In fifty-one years, my memory had never once failed me. It had been my instrument, my weapon, my identity. Edmund Thornbury, the man who forgets nothing.

Someone had cut a page out of me.

I sat in the dark until the birds started, and I thought about a coat, a pair of gloves, a shirt, a tie, laid out in careful alphabetical order on a folding table beside a reservoir that had already taken one Ashcombe and kept it. And I understood, with the calm certainty of a man reading his own obituary, that the arrangement was not a taunt and not a riddle.

It was a letter. Addressed to me. And whoever wrote it knew that I was the only person in Marrow's Bend who could read it—and knew, further, that there was a page missing from my file that I could not account for, and that sooner or later I would come looking for it.

The phone rang at 6:47 the next morning. Dana Okafor again.

"Edmund," she said. "Vivian Ashcombe is asking for you. By name. She says—" I heard paper turn, her reading from her notes, needing the exact words, "—she says, 'Tell him it's time to open the cabinet.'"

Outside my window, the town of Marrow's Bend lay quiet under a thinning fog, every house in its place, every secret in its drawer, alphabetized and waiting.
