## 6. Thirty Years of Alibis

The statements were still in my head. That was the trouble with a memory like mine—nothing ever went to the county archive to gather dust and mildew. Everything stayed where I'd put it thirty years ago, in perfect condition, waiting.

I sat in my office on the Monday after Paul Ashcombe surfaced, after Sheriff Okafor released me from her interview room with a nod that was neither trust nor suspicion but something worse: courtesy. And I did what I had done ten thousand times before. I opened the cabinet in my mind labeled *Lena Ashcombe — October 14, 1994* and read the four statements back to myself as though they were fresh ink.

**Statement One: Vivian Ashcombe.** At home, Bridge Street, playing cards with her sister-in-law until eleven, then alone with a book. Heard nothing. Saw nothing. Signed at 9:40 p.m., October 15th.

**Statement Two: Gerald Ashcombe.** At the marina office doing inventory until midnight. Saw Lena's car leave around nine. Assumed she was going for her usual walk by the water. "She liked the water," he'd said, and I'd written it down without asking how he knew what she liked.

**Statement Three: Hollis Greer**, owner of the Bend Marina. Closed at eight, drove home past the reservoir at nine-fifteen, saw no one, noticed Lena's sedan in the gravel lot—"that old gray Buick, you couldn't miss it"—and kept driving because it was cold and his wife had supper on.

**Statement Four: Deputy Warren Pike**, twenty-six years old, patrolling Route 9 between ten and midnight. Passed the reservoir twice. Saw nothing unusual. Lights off at the boathouse. Road empty both directions.

Four statements. Four alibis. Four signatures. In 1994 I had laid them side by side like cards in a winning hand and declared the hand good. No contradictions. No gaps worth chasing. A woman walked into cold water on a warm October night and drowned, and the town exhaled, and I filed it under A for Accident.

Now, with Paul's body wearing my clothes arranged like punctuation, I read the same four statements and saw something I had refused to see at twenty-nine years old.

They didn't just agree. They *rhymed*.

Vivian's card game ended at eleven; Gerald's inventory ran to midnight; Hollis passed at nine-fifteen; Pike swept the road twice. Each statement covered a different hour of the evening, each one sealed a gap the others left open, and not one of them overlapped enough to contradict another. It was beautiful. It was airtight. It was the kind of completeness you almost never get from four separate human beings recalling a single night, because human beings are sloppy clocks. These four had been set to the same time.

Or someone had set them.

I took my coat off the hook—the new coat, bought two winters ago, because the old gray one was gone—and drove into town to ask the questions I should have asked when I was young enough to believe I was asking them for the first time.

* * *

Vivian Ashcombe received me in the sunroom of the house on Bridge Street, the same house, the same wicker chairs, the same smell of lemon polish and cut chrysanthemums. She was eighty-one now and looked carved rather than aged, all planes and decision. She poured tea without asking whether I wanted any.

"Mr. Thornbury." She said my name the way you'd read an account number. "You've been busy."

"A woman's son is dead, Mrs. Ashcombe."

"My grandson," she corrected, "though he hardly qualified. He came here three months ago wanting things out of my files. Money. Papers. He had his mother's temper and none of her grace." She set the pot down with a click. "You're here about 1994. I can see it in your face. You have the face of a man rereading something."

"I'm here about your statement. October fifteenth, 1994. You were playing cards with your sister-in-law."

"Until eleven."

"You remember the hour precisely."

"I remember everything precisely, Mr. Thornbury. It's a family affliction." She smiled, and there was something in the smile that made the hair on my arms rise—not menace, exactly. Recognition. "You wrote the letter, didn't you?"

"We've been through this. I don't know what letter you mean."

"Mm." She stirred her tea though there was nothing in it to stir. "Then let me help you with your other question, since you're too polite to ask it directly. Yes, I was playing cards. Yes, until eleven. And yes, Mr. Thornbury, if you go find my sister-in-law—and you will, because you are thorough the way a dog is thorough—you will find she remembers it too. Every hand. Every hour." She lifted her cup. "That must be very convenient for you. Or very frightening. I've never been sure which."

"Where were you at midnight, Mrs. Ashcombe?"

"In bed. Asleep. Alone, since you're wondering." She drank. "My daughter-in-law went to the water that night. Everyone knew she liked the water. She liked it the way lonely people like things—too much, and at the wrong hours." Her eyes came up and held mine, level and dry. "Is there anything else? Only, grief makes me tired, and I've been grieving for thirty years, so you can imagine the accumulation."

I stood. At the door I turned, because there was one more thing, the thing that had been itching under my skin since the reservoir.

"When I interviewed you in 1994, you told me Lena left the house at nine. But your statement says you were playing cards until eleven and never saw her leave. Someone told you I knew she'd gone out. Who?"

For the first time, something moved behind the carved face. Not fear. Something older and colder than fear—the look of a woman watching a bill come due that she'd believed forgiven.

"Good afternoon, Mr. Thornbury," she said.

* * *

Gerald Ashcombe kept the family's second house on the lake road, a sprawling shingled place gone soft at the eaves, and he answered the door himself in a cardigan the color of oatmeal, looking like a man who had spent thirty years expecting this exact knock and rehearsing nothing.

"Edmund." He stepped back to let me in, which surprised me. Guilty men make you stand on porches. "I figured you'd come. After the boy."

We sat in a den full of mounted fish and dead fireplaces. Gerald was seventy-four and his hands shook, but his voice, when he finally used it, was steady in the way of a man reading from a script he'd memorized so long ago it had become scripture.

"I was at the marina office. Inventory. Until midnight."

"That's what you told me in 1994."

"It's what happened in 1994."

"Hollis Greer closed at eight, Gerald. The office would have been dark. Cold. What were you counting?"

He looked at his hands. Then, unexpectedly, he laughed—a small, terrible sound, like a hinge giving way.

"You know what nobody ever understood about you, Edmund? Everybody in this town thought you were the safest man alive, because you remembered everything. But I always thought you were the most dangerous. Because you remember everything *except what you choose not to*." He raised his eyes. "You sat right there—no, you stood, you never sat in my house—you stood in that doorway and you took my statement and you wrote it down word for word, and you never once asked me why a man doing inventory in a locked building could tell you what time Lena Ashcombe's car pulled out of her driveway. Two miles away. In the dark."

The floor seemed to tilt a degree. I kept my feet.

"How did you know, Gerald?"

He was quiet a long moment. Outside, a crow worked at something in the gutter with obscene patience.

"I saw her car," he said finally. "From the marina lot. Headlights coming down the lake road toward the water. Nine o'clock or near it. And I thought—that's odd, she's going to the water again. And then I thought nothing else about it for thirty years, because thinking else about it would have cost me everything." His hands stilled in his lap, deliberately, the way a man steadies a rifle. "Ask your next question, investigator. I can see it sitting in your throat."

"Who paid you?"

"There it is." He nodded slowly, almost with respect. "Not whether. Who. You always did skip to the interesting part." He stood, went to the mantel, and touched one of the mounted trout with one finger, gently, like a man touching a headstone. "I'm not answering that today. But I'll tell you something for free, because I'm tired and you're the only person alive who might understand it. Every year on the fourteenth of October, money leaves my account and goes somewhere I never chose. Thirty years. Same day. Like rent." He turned. "Like a subscription to silence. And every year I pay it, and every year I think: someday the man who remembers everything is going to do the arithmetic."

He walked me to the door. On the porch, in the gray light, he said, quietly:

"The account number's in your head already, isn't it? From some filing, some paper, some moment you photographed without knowing why." He almost smiled. "Go home and read yourself, Edmund. You're the best witness either of us has."

* * *

Hollis Greer was easier and somehow worse. He still ran the marina, half of it, the other half sold to cover what he called "a bad decade," and he talked the way he'd always talked, in long unhurried loops, hands moving like a man casting line.

"Gray Buick, yeah. Parked in the gravel lot, nine-fifteen, maybe nine-twenty. I remember because I checked my watch against the dashboard clock, which ran fast, and I always liked being able to say I knew the real time." He grinned, gap-toothed, sixty-eight years old and unbothered. "Funny thing, though."

"What's funny, Hollis?"

"Well—it's funny now, anyway. Back then it wasn't anything." He leaned on the counter, on the same counter, I realized, where he'd given his statement while I stood where I was standing. "When you interviewed me, you asked what I saw. I told you: the Buick, the dark lot, the boathouse lights off. And you wrote it all down and thanked me, and I went home feeling like I'd done my civic duty." He scratched his jaw. "But here's the thing I never said, because you never asked. There was a second car. Pulled in maybe ten minutes after I got there. I didn't note it because it wasn't strange—people park at that lot all the time, fishermen, kids, whatever. But I've thought about it lately, with the boy drowning and all, and I keep trying to remember what it looked like, and you know what? I can't. Color, shape, nothing. Gone clean out of my head."

"Describe the night," I said. "Everything."

And he did—the cold, the smell of diesel, the loons on the black water, the frost starting on the windshield—and it was all vivid and useless, a wall of detail built around one missing brick. I listened with my whole mind photographing him, and when he finished I said:

"You told me the boathouse lights were off."

"Yep."

"But you also told me you only slowed down. Didn't stop."

"That's right."

"Then how did you know the lights were off, Hollis? At nine-fifteen, from a moving car, in the dark?"

The grin died by degrees. He looked at me the way men look at a scale that has begun tipping the wrong way.

"Huh," he said softly. "Thirty years, and nobody caught that. Not even you." He wiped his hands on a rag that was cleaner than his conscience. "I stopped, Mr. Thornbury. Just for a minute. Just to look. And there was somebody out on the dock—I want to say two somebodies—but it was dark, and I minded my own business, because that's the religion of this town, we're all very devout about it." He folded the rag. "You want to know the worst part? When you took my statement, I started to say it. Started to. And you looked at me—just a look, friendly, patient, like you were saving me from a mistake—and I shut up. And I've always wondered, since. Did you save me? Or did you file me?"

I had no answer. I drove away with his question riding shotgun.

* * *

Judge Warren Pike received me in chambers, which was a courtesy, and offered coffee, which was a warning. He was fifty-six now, silver at the temples, a man who had traded a deputy's uniform for robes the way other men trade up in cars. We had known each other twenty years. He had never once been easy with me, and I had never once known why.

"Edmund." He gestured to the chair across the desk. "I heard you were consulting for Okafor. That's a strange fit."

"Why?"

"Because you're either the best witness she has or the best suspect." He said it mildly, sipping, watching me over the rim. "Possibly both. Sit down."

I sat. "Your statement, October 1994. You were on patrol, Route 9, passed the reservoir twice between ten and midnight. Saw nothing."

"That's the record."

"I'm not asking for the record. I'm asking for the night."

Pike set his cup down and was quiet long enough that the clock on his shelf became the loudest thing in the county. Then he laughed, short and without humor.

"You know, I've waited half my life for you to ask me a question like that. Do you know why? Because in 1994 you were the most frightening man I'd ever met. Twenty-nine years old, and you'd look at you—look at a witness—and I swear to God it was like being X-rayed. Nobody lied to you. Nobody *could*. So when you took my statement, I gave you the truest false statement of my life, and you wrote it down, and I walked out of that room thinking: either I'm smarter than I thought, or he wanted it to be true." He laced his fingers. "Which was it, Edmund? Because I've wondered for thirty years."

"Where were you standing, Warren?"

The question landed. I watched it land. His face did something complicated—the flinch of a man whose old wound has finally been touched by the right doctor.

"You never asked me that," he said. "In ninety-four. You asked what I saw, and I said nothing, and you accepted it. But you never asked where I was *standing*." He rose, went to the window, looked out at the courthouse lawn as if the answer might be planted there. "Route 9 has that pull-off above the reservoir. The overlook. You can see the whole water from there. The dock. The boathouse. Everything." His reflection in the glass looked older than he did. "I was parked there both times. Ten-forty and eleven-fifty. Engine off. Watching."

"Watching what?"

"I don't know what I saw," he said, turning, and his voice had the careful flatness of a judge delivering instructions he wishes he didn't have to give. "It was dark. There were shapes. And I was twenty-six years old and the Ashcombe name meant something in this county, and a young deputy learns very quickly which calls to hurry and which ones to drive past slow." He came back to the desk and stood over me. "Here's what I'll give you, and it's more than I've given anyone. If you dig—and you will dig, you're constitutionally incapable of not digging—there's a piece of this you haven't found yet. Something missing from the picture. Not a person who was there." He paused. "A person everyone agrees was never born."

I felt the words go through me like cold water through cloth.

"What does that mean?"

"It means," Warren Pike said, picking up his gavel and turning it over in his hands like a man remembering a former life, "that when you finally figure out what you certified in 1994, Edmund, you're going to wish your memory wasn't so goddamn perfect. Some things are filed away for a reason. Even by you." He set the gavel down. "Especially by you."

I walked out of the courthouse into a wind that smelled like coming rain, and I stood on the steps and did what I had spent my whole life doing: I read myself. Statement by statement, hour by hour, the night of October 14th, 1994, laid out in my head like evidence on a table. Four alibis, each covering the others' seams. A marina owner who stopped and was saved from saying so. A deputy parked at the overlook, watching shapes. A brother-in-law paying rent on the fourteenth of October. A matriarch who remembered everything precisely, like me, like—

Like a family affliction.

And underneath it all, the sentence I had signed with my own confident hand thirty years ago, the finding I had typed and notarized and filed under A: *Accidental drowning. No evidence of foul play.*

Every alibi held. Too perfectly. And there was only one man alive who could have assembled four imperfect human recollections into one flawless architecture, who could have taken those statements, weighed them, and pronounced them complete.

Me.

I hadn't just recorded the lie. Somewhere in the deep vault of my perfect memory, in a page I could no longer reach, I had *built* it.

I got in my car, and my hands were steady, and that frightened me more than shaking would have. Because steady hands were the hands of an investigator, and investigators follow evidence, and the evidence was leading somewhere I had forbidden it to go thirty years ago—to a page nine I suddenly, desperately needed to read, in a file I suddenly couldn't trust myself to have written.

There was only one copy. It was in my own archive. And for the first time in twenty years of cataloguing this town's secrets, I was afraid to open my own cabinet.
