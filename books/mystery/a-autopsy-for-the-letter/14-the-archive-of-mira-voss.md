## 14. The Archive of Mira Voss

The county records annex sat forty miles from Marrow's Bend, in a converted feed store on the edge of Halloway, and it looked like every building Edmund had ever trusted: brick, unremarkable, its windows too high to see through from the street. He had driven past it twice before parking. Not because he was lost. Because his memory kept offering him the route and he kept refusing it, the way a man refuses a hand he suspects has been holding something behind its back.

He had not slept. Page nine lay folded in his breast pocket like a splinter working toward the heart—*I saw her go in. I said nothing.*—and above it, in the same pocket, Ruth Calloway's third letter, the birth certificate photocopied twice over until the ink ghosted: **Mira Ashcombe**, born 1987, committed to the care of the county in 1995, signed out eleven months later by a caseworker whose name Edmund had photographed twenty years ago without knowing why. *D. Voss.* He could still see the signature's downstroke, the way the V hooked leftward like a man flinching.

An archivist. Of course. He should have known the moment the clothes came out of the reservoir in alphabetical order. Only two kinds of people arranged grief that way—the guilty and the cataloguers—and increasingly he understood those were not separate categories.

The door was unlocked. Inside, the annex smelled of dust and toner and the particular cold sweetness of old paper, a smell that hit him with the force of recognition, because it was the smell of his own office, of his own life, distilled. Fluorescent lights hummed over rows of steel shelving. At a desk near the back, beneath a window that let in a bar of gray morning, a woman sat sorting documents into folders with movements so economical they seemed rehearsed across decades rather than years.

She did not look up when the bell over the door chimed. "We open at ten," she said. "But if you're here about the Hollis estate, I can pull that now."

"I'm not here about the Hollis estate."

She finished the folder she was holding, squared its edges against the desk, and set it in a tray marked with a date three days out. Then she looked up.

Edmund had prepared himself for many faces—a stranger's face, a hostile one, one that might carry Lena Ashcombe's jaw or her father-in-law's heavy brow. What he had not prepared for was a face that carried nothing at all. It was pleasant, symmetrical, forgettable by design. Except for the eyes. The eyes were doing what his own eyes did in mirrors: watching him the way an auditor watches a ledger, line by line, looking for the entry that doesn't balance.

"You're Edmund Thornbury," she said. Not a question. She pronounced all four syllables of his surname carefully, as though she'd only ever seen it written.

"And you're Mira Voss."

"Officially." She rose. She was small, mid-thirties, wearing a cardigan the color of oatmeal, and she moved around the desk the way he imagined she moved around everything—along established routes, touching nothing unnecessarily. "You're a long way from your archive, Mr. Thornbury."

"How do you know about my archive?"

"Everyone in records knows about your archive." A faint almost-smile. "Twenty years of claims files cross-indexed against death certificates, probate records, sheriff's reports. You've requested copies from this annex forty-one times since 2004. I've pulled every one of them." She tilted her head. "Did you think the requests disappeared? That paper goes into a void?"

He hadn't thought about it at all, which was itself a kind of confession. For twenty years he had treated other people's records as raw material, inert, waiting for his eye. It had never occurred to him that somewhere a person pulled each folder, read each page, and formed conclusions.

"What did you conclude?" he asked.

"That you were building something." She walked past him toward the shelves, and he followed because there was nowhere else to be; the room was hers the way the reservoir had been hers. "A map of the town. Every secret filed under the name of the person keeping it. I used to wonder what you'd do with it when it was finished." She stopped at a shelf, ran one finger along a row of box labels without reading them—she knew exactly where everything was. "Then Paul started coming around, asking questions with money behind them, and I stopped wondering what you'd do with it. I started wondering what it would do to you."

"Mira—"

"Ms. Voss," she said mildly. "Mira Ashcombe died in 1995. On paper. Paper is very obedient that way—you may have noticed."

* * *

She made tea. He didn't want tea but he accepted it, because refusing would have been a move in a game and he wanted, desperately, to believe they were not playing a game. She poured from an electric kettle into two mismatched mugs and set his on a coaster, precisely centered, and the gesture undid him more than any accusation could have. His own desk had coasters. His own mugs were mismatched. He had told himself for thirty years that order was how he served the truth. Watching her, he understood it was how they had both survived things that should have scattered them like chaff.

"Your brother," Edmund said. "Paul."

"What about him."

"The clothes in the reservoir. The gray overcoat, the shirt, the tie. They were mine. They were staged."

"Was the staging alphabetical?" She sipped her tea. "Or numerical? I've read the sheriff's report summary—it leaked, the way everything leaks eventually—and the inventory wasn't clear."

"Why does that matter to you?"

"Because alphabetical means a mind. Numerical means a filing system. They're different animals, Mr. Thornbury. One arranges to be understood. The other arranges to be *found*—by a specific person, using a specific key." She set the mug down. "Only one person alive holds the key to those claim numbers."

The fluorescent light buzzed. Somewhere in the back of the annex a dehumidifier clicked on, breathing.

"You're telling me you didn't do it," Edmund said slowly, "while telling me exactly how it was done."

"I'm telling you what the evidence says. Isn't that what investigators do?" Her voice never rose. It was the flattest, most controlled voice he had ever heard outside his own head, and hearing it from another mouth was like catching his reflection in a shop window and not recognizing the coat. "You underwrote Paul's policy. You consulted on the case. Your garments were on the body. By every professional standard, you are the most interesting object in this story." She looked at him steadily. "And yet you drove forty miles to sit in my reading room and drink bad tea. Why?"

Because page nine was burning a hole in his chest. Because the judge had fled town leaving red ink behind. Because Gerald had broken like cheap drywall and named an account opened in 1994 under a name that rearranged into hers. Because every road he had walked for a month ended at this desk, in this room, in front of this calm, precise woman who sorted the dead for a living.

"Because I need to know what happened to my report," he said. "Page nine."

Something moved behind her eyes—not surprise, but the settling of a weight long anticipated, like a shelf accepting a book it was built for.

"Tell me about page nine," she said.

* * *

So he did—or as much of it as he could bear. He told her about 1994, about the drowning ruled accidental, about four alibis taken and certified, about a report so clean it had always troubled some wordless part of him, like a floor swept too thoroughly. He told her about discovering the missing page last month, and the vertigo of reaching for a memory that had never once refused him and finding a smooth blank wall where the words should have been.

"I don't lose things," he said. "It's the one true thing about me. Everything else is negotiable, but my memory doesn't lose things. So either I destroyed that page—" his voice caught on the possibility— "or someone took it. And if someone took it, they had to know it existed. And the only person who could know it existed is me."

"Unless," Mira said, "the page was never lost at all. Unless it was *held*."

"Held."

"In escrow." She turned her mug a quarter-turn on its coaster, aligning the handle to the right. "That's what archives are, Mr. Thornbury. Guilt in escrow. Every deed, every certificate, every sealed record—somebody's worst day, preserved at exact temperature and humidity until someone comes to claim it." She met his eyes. "Someone has been holding yours for thirty years. And mailing it back to you one piece at a time. The socks to Ruth Calloway. The letters. The photograph of page nine." She paused. "The clothes."

"You know who's sending them."

"I know who has access to a county records annex," she said. "I know who can pull a 1994 caseworker file, a 1995 deletion order, a claim index going back to before either of us could drive. I know who spent three months being blackmailed by a man who thought a sealed estate was a bank vault." Her composure held, but underneath it he heard it now—the hum, the same hum he carried, grief compressed until it resonated. "And I know that whoever it is chose you, specifically, to receive the correspondence. Not the sheriff. Not the newspapers. You. Which means the letters aren't evidence, Mr. Thornbury. Evidence goes to authorities. Letters go to addressees."

The tea had gone cold in his hands. He set it down on the coaster, aligned it without thinking, and hated himself a little for the comfort the alignment gave.

"You filed my mother under A for Accident," Mira said.

The sentence landed in the quiet room like a stone dropped down a well. He waited for the splash.

"She went into the water on the fourth of October, 1994, and within six weeks there was a file with a number—4471—and inside it a conclusion, and the conclusion began with the letter A. Accident. Approved. Authorized payout. Closed." She recited it the way he recited policy terms, flat and total. "My mother became a filing decision. My brother and I became line items in a disbursement schedule. And the man who made that decision got to keep every copy, build his little map, live his tidy alphabetized life—while I was signed out of the county's care by a caseworker whose signature you photographed and never once wondered about." She stood, walked to the shelf, and drew out a single folder without consulting any index. "Do you know what I do here, Mr. Thornbury? All day? I correct indexes. Deeds recorded under wrong names. Estates misfiled by clerks who couldn't be bothered. Children entered as wards when they had living kin. Somebody has to do it. The truth doesn't stay sorted on its own."

She held out the folder. He didn't take it.

"Is that what you're doing?" he asked quietly. "Correcting an index?"

"I'm correcting *your* index." Her voice finally cracked—not broke, but showed the seam, one hairline fracture running through all that precision. "You put her under A. Someone is moving her to L. Lena. Where she should have been from the beginning. Under her own name. With her own page nine."

"Lena asked me to bury it." The words came out of him before he had decided to say them, torn loose by the fracture in her voice. "Whatever's on that page—whatever I can't remember—I'm starting to understand that she asked. That the accident was a mercy she ordered. That I falsified the report because a dying woman begged me to protect her children." He heard his own breath shake. "I did it *for* you. Both of you. The insurance money raised you. If the town had known—if Vivian had been charged—there would have been no payout, no group home with a trust behind it, no—"

"No mother," Mira said. "No funeral anyone attended. No grave with her real name spelled correctly—have you seen the stone, Mr. Thornbury? They misspelled it. *Lenna.* Thirty years, and no one corrected the index." She set the folder on the desk between them, square to the edge. "You keep saying you did it for us. But you did it for yourself, too. Don't pretend you don't know that. A clean report. A closed file. A town that owed you silence forever. You built a career out of knowing where everyone's bodies were buried, and the first one you buried was your own."

The dehumidifier clicked off. In the silence Edmund felt thirty years settle onto his shoulders like wet wool.

"Yes," he said. "Both things are true. That's the arithmetic nobody teaches you—that mercy and cowardice can occupy the same page." He looked at the folder. "Is that hers? Is that Lena's file?"

"It's a copy of everything the county ever held on Mira Ashcombe. Birth, ward status, discharge, deletion order." She watched him. "Every document except one. The one that matters. The one that explains why the deletion order exists, who signed it, and what my mother actually wrote in the last weeks of her life." She slid it an inch closer to him. "I could give it to you. Or I could hold it. In escrow. The way everything in this story gets held."

"By whom?" he asked. "Who's holding it, Mira? Is it you? Are you the correspondent? Did you dress a drowned man in my clothes and arrange him like a filing error?"

For the first time, she smiled, and the smile was the loneliest thing he had ever seen—recognition and warning in equal measure, two archivists acknowledging each other across a reading table.

"You have a photographic memory," she said. "So tell me. When you reconstruct the scene at the reservoir—the position of the body, the order of the garments, the tide marks on the stones—does it look like a confession? Or does it look like a *transmittal letter*? Something sent by someone who needed the recipient to do the reading?" She gathered her own mug, his untouched one, carried them to the sink. "I neither confirm nor deny anything, Mr. Thornbury. That's what the record is for. Go home. Read what's in the folder. And when you get to the last page—the discharge summary—look at the date. Then ask yourself what Paul found that made him dangerous enough to end up in the water wearing your coat."

He opened the folder with hands that were not quite steady. Deed of wardship. School transfers. Medical screenings, initialed in a tired hand. And at the back, the discharge summary, dated March 1996—eleven months after commitment, far earlier than any standard placement—

and signed out not to a foster family, not to an institution,

but to a private guardian, name redacted by a strip of paper applied so long ago it had yellowed into the page, with a countersignature beneath it that Edmund's memory seized and held and would not release:

*V.A.*

Vivian Ashcombe had signed her granddaughter out of the county's care—and then erased her from every record in the town.

Behind him, calmly drying the mugs, Mira Voss said, "Now you know why I correct indexes, Mr. Thornbury. Some deletions aren't mercy. Some are just the powerful tidying up."
