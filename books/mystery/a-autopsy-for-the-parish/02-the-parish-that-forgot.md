## 2. The Parish That Forgot

The train stopped giving up on the landscape somewhere past Ely. Ulyana watched the fen unroll outside the window — flat black earth, drainage ditches like veins, a sky so wide it seemed to press down on the fields like a lid. She'd grown up in towns with hills. Towns where you could stand on something and see what was coming. Here, she thought, you'd never see anything coming. You'd just hear it, late, across the water.

She had recorded two episodes of *The Long Silence* on the journey down. In the first she'd read both letters aloud, her voice doing the thing it did when she wanted listeners to lean in — slowing, dropping, letting the silences between sentences do the accusing. In the second she'd interviewed a graphologist over the phone, a brisk woman in Leeds who confirmed what Ulyana already knew: the confession and the threat were written by the same hand. Same pressure, same slant, same small hesitation before the letter *y*, as if the writer were always about to change his mind about something.

"One person," the graphologist had said. "Or two people who learned to write from the same teacher. But that's rare, after a certain age. Handwriting is a confession in itself, you know. It's the one thing we can't lie with."

Ulyana had thanked her, hung up, and sat with the thought for a long time while the fens slid past. *The one thing we can't lie with.* She'd spent nine years interviewing people about the worst days of their lives, and she had learned that everyone lied — the grieving, the guilty, the police, the priests. They lied kindly and cruelly and out of habit. But ink didn't. Ink just sat there on the page, being itself.

The letters sat in her bag now, in two plastic sleeves, side by side. She had not shown them to anyone but her producer, and her producer had said, in the gentle voice she used for Ulyana's worst ideas: *You're going anyway, aren't you.*

"I'm going anyway," Ulyana said to the empty carriage, and the fen outside the glass said nothing back, which felt appropriate.

*

Marsh Aldhelm announced itself the way dying towns do: a sign with too many coats of paint, then a chapel converted into an antique shop, then a square that had once been a market and was now a car park with ambitions. The village sat on a slight rise — the only rise for miles — and on the rise stood St. Aldhelm's, its flint tower visible from the causeway road, from the drainage ditches, from everywhere. It dominated the way a lighthouse dominates, or a gallows. You couldn't look at the village without looking at the church, and Ulyana suspected that was the point. Someone had built it there on purpose, four hundred years before anyone now living was born, so that every eye in the parish would be drawn upward and held.

She parked the rental beside the war memorial and got out into air that smelled of cold water and woodsmoke.

The square was warm in the way of small places. A woman sweeping her doorstep nodded at her. Two men outside the pub — *The Eel and Ember*, a sign that had probably been funny once — lifted their chins in greeting. A boy on a bicycle rang his bell at her for no reason and grinned. Ulyana had done this enough times to know the choreography: arrive as a stranger and the village performs hospitality, because that's what villages are for. Strangers are weather. You comment on them and they pass.

She let herself be weather for an hour. She bought a thermos of coffee from the post office, which was also a shop, which was also, according to a handwritten sign, the key collection point for the village hall. The woman behind the counter — sixty-ish, reading glasses on a chain, a name badge that said BREN — told her the history of the shop unprompted, told her about the flood of '98, told her which of the two pubs was better (neither), and asked, with genuine interest, what had brought Ulyana out this far.

"Research," Ulyana said. "For a project. I'm interested in St. Aldhelm's — the church. Its history."

"Old as the hills," Bren said happily. "Tower's fourteenth century. There's a leaflet."

"And more recently? I understand something happened here in the seventies. 1974. Three parishioners went missing."

The change was not dramatic. That was what Ulyana noticed first, and what she would keep noticing for the rest of the day: the change was not dramatic at all. Bren's face didn't close like a door. It closed like a shop — the lights still on, the shelves still stocked, everything in its place, but nobody home. Her smile stayed exactly where it was, a smile preserved in amber, and her hands kept folding the newspaper on the counter, and her voice, when it came, was perfectly pleasant.

"Before my time, love. You'd want the vicar. Reverend Cole. He's in most mornings."

"Of course. Do you remember it at all? People talked about it, surely—"

"Two forty for the coffee," Bren said. "And the leaflet's free."

*

The pub was warmer. The pub was always warmer. Ulyana took a corner table with a view of the door and ordered soup she didn't want, and for forty minutes she did the thing she was actually good at, the thing that no amount of institutional training could teach: she made herself small and kind and interested, and she let people talk.

The landlord talked. The landlord's wife talked. A retired farmer named Cyril, who had adopted her table on the grounds that she was listening, talked about drainage rates for twenty minutes and it was, she'd admit later, not uninteresting. Everyone in Marsh Aldhelm was friendly. Everyone in Marsh Aldhelm had time for a stranger. The village was a warm bath and Ulyana sat in it and let it lap against her, and every so often she'd say, lightly, the way you'd mention the weather:

"Strange thing, though — three people just vanishing. In a place this small. Everyone must have known them."

And the bath would go still.

Not cold. Still. The landlord would find a glass that needed polishing. Cyril would remember an urgent appointment with the bottom of his pint. The landlord's wife would say, brightly, "That was the year of the big storm, wasn't it? Terrible winter," and the conversation would be carried, gently, by four willing hands, and set down somewhere else entirely, and the subject would be gone like a body in a fen — no splash, no trace, just water closing over water.

By the third attempt Ulyana had stopped being a person asking questions and started being an instrument measuring a phenomenon, and what the instrument registered frightened her more than hostility would have. Hostility has a temperature. Hostility is information. This was something else. This was a village-wide performance, unrehearsed and yet perfectly synchronized, as if the entire population of Marsh Aldhelm had been issued the same script fifty years ago and had rehearsed it so long they no longer knew they were acting.

*Before my time. You'd want the vicar. Terrible winter.*

She tried once more, with a woman at the bar who looked old enough — mid-eighties, maybe, a face like a walnut, eyes that were still quick. Ulyana bought her a sherry and made her laugh twice, and then said, softly, "My grandmother's era, you and me. Tell me — 1974. What do you remember?"

The woman looked at her. And for one half of one second — Ulyana would replay this half-second perhaps a thousand times over the coming weeks — something moved behind the walnut face. Not fear exactly. Grief. Grief with a lid on it, pressed down so hard and so long that the lid had become part of the face.

Then the lid came down, and the woman said, "I remember the storm," and picked up her sherry, and turned to the window, and the interview was over.

*

Reverend Idris Cole received her in the parish office at four o'clock, because she had telephoned at two and he had said, in a voice like warm milk, "Of course, of course, come by any time."

She'd expected the office of a rural vicar to be cluttered and cozy, and it was — bookshelves, a radiator that ticked, a framed print of some Pre-Raphaelite saint, a tin of shortbread offered within the first thirty seconds. But Ulyana had spent nine years reading rooms the way other people read books, and the room read wrong. The desk was too tidy. The visitor's chair was positioned at exactly the right distance — not too close, not too far — and angled at exactly the right degree, friendly but not intimate. The shortbread was offered at the correct moment. Everything about Reverend Idris Cole was correct, and correctness, in Ulyana's experience, was what fear looked like when it had had fifty years to practice.

He was perhaps fifty-five, soft-spoken, with the kind of face that made you want to confess things to him, which she supposed was a professional asset. He wore his collar like a man who had never once thought about taking it off.

"Miss Galloway." He pronounced it correctly — she'd never told him the stress was on the first syllable — "I listened to your podcast, actually. The one about the ferry disaster. My sister was on the Isle of Wight then. It stayed with me." He smiled. "I didn't realize you were coming to *us*, though."

"I only decided recently," Ulyana said, which was true, and watched him. Nothing moved in his face. Either he hadn't heard the letters existed, or he'd had practice.

"And what is it the podcast wants with St. Aldhelm's?" He said the word *podcast* with the gentle precision of a man handling an unfamiliar tool, and she almost liked him for it.

"The church itself, mostly. Its history. I'm interested in how parishes remember." She let that sit for a moment. "Or don't."

"Ah." He poured tea — he'd made tea without asking, another correct move — and handed her a cup. "Memory is a strange thing in a place like this. Small communities, they hold on to things, and they let go of things, and it's not always the things you'd expect. The flood of '98, everyone remembers. The harvest of '61, my predecessor could tell you every sheaf. But other years—" he spread his hands, an ecclesiastical shrug, "—they go under. Like the fen. Things sink here, Miss Galloway. The ground doesn't keep everything. It can't. It would be unbearable if it did."

"That's beautifully put," Ulyana said. "Did you write it down somewhere?"

Something flickered — the first flicker, the first honest thing — and was gone. "I give a sermon on it most Novembers. Remembrance season." He smiled again. "You said 1974 on the phone."

"I did."

"May I ask why?"

"Three parishioners disappeared that year," Ulyana said. "Walter Pask. Margaret and Dennis Croft. Never found. There was an inquest. And then, apparently, there wasn't. I'd like to understand what the parish understood about it."

The ticking of the radiator filled the room. Outside, a rook called from the tower, once, twice, like a question nobody would answer.

"You'd want the diocesan records," Cole said. "I keep the current registers — baptisms, marriages, burials, the ordinary business of souls. The historical material went to the diocesan archive years ago. Standard practice." He said it smoothly, but Ulyana had heard the half-second pause before *standard practice*, a pause the size of a held breath. "And I should say — I've been here eleven years. Whatever happened in 1974, it happened to a parish I never knew, under men I never met. I can't tell you what they understood. I'm not sure they understood anything themselves. Grief does strange things to a community. Sometimes the only mercy is to let it sleep."

"Is that what this is?" Ulyana asked, gesturing with her teacup at the window, the square, the whole warm, sealed, performing village beyond it. "Mercy?"

Cole looked at her for a long moment. And she saw it again, the thing under the thing, the grief with the lid on — the same expression the walnut-faced woman had worn, the same expression Bren's amber smile had been built to cover. It was in him too. Whatever the script was, the vicar had learned it from the same teachers as everyone else, and underneath the script he was carrying something that weighed as much as any of it.

"Miss Galloway," he said quietly, "may I give you some advice, as one professional to another?"

"Please."

"Whatever you're making — and I'm sure it will be very good — be careful what you wake up in a place like this. The dead here are not restless. They were *settled*. Settled deliberately, at some cost, by people who believed they were doing the only thing that could be done." He set down his own cup, and his hands were perfectly steady, which was its own kind of tell. "You'll find the churchyard overgrown. The east end especially. The groundskeeper is a man called Sorrell, if you need anything — he's been here longer than anyone. Longer than he'd like, I think." A small, tired smile. "Now. I have a funeral committee at five, and you'll want to see the church before the light goes. Do look at the roof. It's fifteenth century. We're very proud of it."

It was a dismissal, delivered with such courtesy that it took her a moment to recognize it as one. She stood. He stood. At the door she paused.

"Reverend — one more thing. The three who disappeared. Pask and the Crofts. Are they in your burial register?"

The pause this time was longer. Long enough to count.

"Missing persons are not buried, Miss Galloway," he said. "That's rather the difficulty with them."

"Of course," she said. "Silly of me."

But as she walked out across the square, the light going bronze behind the tower, she found herself turning the sentence over. *That's rather the difficulty with them.* It had been said lightly, a vicar's small joke, and yet it had the texture of something else — of a sentence he'd had to say before, or had heard said, or had spent eleven years not saying to anyone.

*

She went to the churchyard because it was there, and because the light was dying and she wanted to see the place with her own eyes before the town's version of it finished closing over her like water.

St. Aldhelm's churchyard was a wilderness of leaning stones, blackened flint walls, grass grown to the knee in the older sections. Rooks owned the yew trees and commented on her progress. She walked the paths slowly, reading names the way she read everything — as text, as evidence, as a story someone had tried to tell in the only alphabet death allowed them.

The east end was worse than overgrown. It was abandoned. The grass there stood waist-high, the stones furred with moss, several of them fallen face-down into the earth as if the ground were pulling them under. She waded in anyway, because she was who she was, and because Reverend Cole had told her about it, which meant he had wanted her to see it, which meant — her mind was already running three steps ahead, the way it did, the way it always had — that the east end mattered.

She was pushing aside a curtain of dead bracken when she saw the stone.

It stood apart from the others, newer than its neighbors, cleaner — cleaned, she thought, deliberately, recently, by hand. A single slab, modest, with three names cut into it in a plain, careful script:

WALTER PASK · MARGARET CROFT · DENNIS CROFT

And beneath the names, a date.

Not 1974.

Ulyana stood very still in the rising dark, the rooks going silent above her one by one, and read the date again, and again, feeling the thrill come up through her like cold water through a floor — the thrill she had been waiting nine years to feel and had promised herself she'd never trust.

Someone had buried the missing. Someone had cut their names in stone. And someone had chosen a date that was a lie — a date decades after the last anyone saw them alive — and had set the stone here in the forgotten corner of a churchyard where the whole village had agreed, in perfect unison, to stop looking.

From the tower behind her, the rooks lifted all at once, a black exhalation against the last of the light.

Somewhere across the grass, a gate creaked. Footsteps, unhurried, coming toward her through the overgrowth.

"Miss Galloway," said a voice she didn't know, "you shouldn't be standing on that grave."
