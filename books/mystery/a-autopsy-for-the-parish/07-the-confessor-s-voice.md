## 7. The Confessor's Voice

The phone rang at 3:14 in the morning, and I knew before I answered it that I would.

Not because I'm brave. Because I'd spent the last two days sleeping with the hotel room's deadbolt thrown and the chain on and my laptop open to the photographs of the burial ledger, and because some animal part of my brain had already understood that the letters weren't finished with me. You don't get two envelopes like that — one confessing, one threatening — and then get to sleep like a person.

The screen said UNKNOWN NUMBER. No caller ID, no voicemail badge, nothing.

I sat up. The room was the kind of dark that fenland does well — no city glow, no streetlight, just the black water of the Broads somewhere out there holding all its secrets under a skin of mist. The phone rang a third time. A fourth.

On the fifth, I answered.

"Ulyana Galloway."

Silence. But not empty silence — the silence of someone breathing carefully, someone who has rehearsed this and is now discovering that rehearsal is useless. I could hear a faint mechanical wheeze underneath the breathing, rhythmic, like a small pump working. I'd heard that sound before, in a hospital corridor in Norwich, two years ago, when I'd recorded an episode beside my mother's bed. An oxygen concentrator.

"I've listened to all of them," the voice said. "Every episode. Forty-one."

The voice itself was the worst thing about it. It was a ruined voice — gravel dragged through gravel, a voice that had been broken and then broken again, the way voices get after radiation burns the throat or a tube has been down it too many times. Each word cost him something. I could hear the cost.

"Who is this?"

"You know who I am." A wet sound that might have been a laugh. "You've been reading my letters."

I turned on the lamp. My hands were steady, which surprised me. My mouth had gone dry as chalk.

"Then you know I have questions."

"And I know you have a deadline." The wheeze rose and fell. "All Souls' Day. Nine days."

"Which is why I'd like to start with the part where you promise to kill someone."

Another silence, longer. When he spoke again, the gravel had shifted, and underneath it I could hear something older — a fenland accent, flattened vowels, the speech of a man who had never left the marshes except perhaps once, and regretted it.

"You think I wrote that letter because I want to hurt someone."

"You wrote it to make me work faster. I understand leverage. I've made forty-one episodes about people who use it."

"No." The word came out cracked down the middle. "I wrote it because I'm the only one left who remembers how it all fits together, and I am running out of time faster than you are running out of days. The deadline isn't for you, Miss Galloway. It's for me. I need to know it's done before I go. I need to know someone read it. All of it."

I pulled the notepad across the bedspread, though I knew I wouldn't need it. Some conversations you keep whole.

"Then tell me who you are."

A pause. The oxygen pump cycled.

"I was the sexton's apprentice at St. Aldhelm's. Nineteen seventy-four. I was seventeen years old." He said it flatly, like reading a headstone. "My name was on the parish roll for eleven years after that, and then it wasn't, and nobody asked why. That's how it worked there. Things stopped being on lists and nobody asked why."

"Three people stopped being on lists in 1974," I said. "Margaret Ellery. Ronald Petch. Susan Vance."

"You've been to the churchyard." Not a question. "Then you found the stone."

"The stone with their names, dated 1991."

"Dated wrong," he said. "Everything there is dated wrong. That's the first thing you have to understand about Marsh Aldhelm. The dates are the lie. All of them. The ledgers, the registers, the plaques, the donations. The parish kept two calendars, Miss Galloway. The one in the light and the one in the dark. I kept the dark one."

My skin prickled. The ledger. The hymn numbers, the plot coordinates, the donation amounts — the repeating pattern I'd photographed while Reverend Cole watched me with eyes like a man watching a flood rise.

"You buried them," I said. "The three of them. You helped."

"I dug the hole." No hesitation. No flinch in the ruined voice. "I was seventeen and he told me to dig, and I dug, because he was the sexton and my father's friend and because at seventeen you dig when you're told. Three of them, in the old chantry footings, where the ground was already disturbed and no one would look. I carried Margaret Ellery myself. She was lighter than you'd think." The pump wheezed. "She'd been dead four days by then. They kept her in the boiler room under the vestry until the search died down. I know because I stoked that boiler. I was the one who kept it hot."

I pressed my knuckles against my mouth. The hotel room was very cold, or I was.

"Why are you telling me this? Why not the police? Why not — God, why not just write it all down and send it to me? You've sent letters. You know where I sleep."

"Because paper can be dismissed. A dying man's letters, no signature, no corroboration — you'd be a crank with a theory and I'd be a ghost. And because the police—" the laugh again, that wet broken sound, "—the police were the whole problem, Miss Galloway. In 1974 a constable came asking. One constable, young, earnest. He was transferred to Norfolk within the week. Earnest men get transferred. That's how it worked. That's how it always worked."

"And who made it work? Who told you to dig?"

The silence stretched so long I checked whether the call had dropped. The timer kept climbing. 3:22. 3:23.

"I can't say the name."

"You can. You've confessed to three murders to a stranger on the phone at three in the morning. The name is nothing."

"It's not nothing." The gravel tore. "It's the only thing. It's the whole machine. If I say it and you're careless, it dies with me and goes in the ground again for another fifty years, and some other child gets taken off a register, and some other ledger gets a new entry. I have one chance to do this right. I've had fifty years to learn how careful you have to be."

"Then help me be careful. Give me something I can verify."

He breathed. The pump cycled. Outside, somewhere over the black water, a curlew called — that rising, grieving note the fens make at night, the sound that got into my bones the first day I arrived and hasn't left since.

"Read what we were paid," he said.

"What?"

"The ledger. You've seen it. You photographed it — don't look surprised, the vicar told the council within the hour, and the council has ears I can still reach." A pause, and something almost like apology. "I'm sorry about that. I needed to know if you were real. You are. So: read it. Not the burials. The payments. Every hymn number, every plot, every donation, back to 1968. It's not a burial ledger. It never was. It's an account book. We were paid, Miss Galloway. The sexton, the vicar, the council, the organist, me. Small sums at first. Then not small. Fifty years of payments for fifty years of silence, all written down in the only book nobody ever reads because nobody ever reads a burial ledger. Read what we were paid, and you'll know who paid us, and you'll know why the dates are all wrong, and you'll know why three people had to stop being on lists."

"And the fourth?" I made my voice flat, professional, though my heart was slamming. "The fourth victim. All Souls' Day. Was he paid too?"

The change in his breathing was immediate — a catch, a stutter in the pump's rhythm.

"There is no fourth victim."

"You wrote—"

"I wrote what would make you move. I'm dying, Miss Galloway. Pancreatic, second time around, and this time it wins. I have maybe six weeks and I've already spent two of them watching you walk around my churchyard. I don't have time for you to be thorough. I need you terrified, and I need you fast, and the only lever I had left was a threat." The gravel dropped low. "I have done one unforgivable thing in my life with a shovel, and I will not do another. No one dies. But if you don't finish reading that ledger before the bells ring in All Souls', then everything I buried — the paper, the proof, the names — gets buried again, this time permanently, because there are people still alive who will make sure of it, and they are not dying, and they are not sorry."

I sat with that. The fear didn't leave — it just changed shape, from fear of him to fear of everything he'd just implied. *There are people still alive.* The letters had been bait, but the bait had been built on something real, and the something real had teeth.

"You said 'we.' You keep saying 'we.' We were paid. We buried. How many?"

"Enough to fill a pew," he said. "Most of them are dead now. That's the mercy of it. Time does what courage couldn't." A long, rattling breath. "One isn't."

My mouth went dry all over again.

"One isn't dead," I repeated.

"One is very much alive. One is celebrated. One is on television." And then, before I could get a word out, his voice changed — hardened, hurried, the voice of a man who has said one sentence too many. "Read the payments. Start with the flower fund. Start with 1968. And Miss Galloway—"

"Yes."

"Trust the detective. Not the vicar. Not the bishop. The detective." A pause, and then, quieter, almost inaudible under the pump: "I'm sorry about your window. And your sleep. And the letters. I chose the only door that was still open, and it was ugly, and I'm not asking you to forgive it. I'm asking you to finish it."

The line went dead.

I sat there in the lamplight with the phone in my hand and the fen pressed black against the window, and I did the thing I always do when a source hangs up on me: I wrote down everything, immediately, in order, before the recording in my head could degrade. The pump. The gravel. *I carried Margaret Ellery myself.* The boiler room under the vestry. *Read what we were paid.* The flower fund. 1968. *Trust the detective.*

Then I sat with the other thing, the thing underneath, the thing that had been coiling in me since the first envelope and now had a name.

He had killed three people. Or helped. At seventeen, under orders — and some part of me, the part that had grown up on my grandmother's stories of occupied villages, understood exactly how a seventeen-year-old ends up holding a shovel. And he was dying, alone, in a room with an oxygen machine, spending his last weeks not on absolution but on *accounting* — on getting the books balanced before the final entry. He wasn't asking for mercy. He was asking for a witness.

And I was it. God help me, I wanted to be it. That was the shameful part, the part I'd admit to no one, not even Ashe: when he said *read the payments*, something in me lit up like a struck match. Forty-one episodes of closed doors and sealed files and grief that had nowhere to go, and now a dying man was handing me the master key and telling me to hurry.

I got up. I put the kettle on because my hands needed a job, and while it heated I opened the photographs of the ledger on my laptop and scrolled back to the earliest pages. 1968. The entries were small, almost shy. Flower fund donations. Roof repair funds. A gift of £4 to the organist at Easter. A gift of £7 to the sexton at Michaelmas. Hymn numbers in the margin — 168, 92, 403 — and plot coordinates that didn't match any burials I'd found in the churchyard.

I got a legal pad and started a table. Hymn number against plot against amount against date. By the fourth cup of tea the pattern was crawling off the page at me: every donation clustered around dates when, according to the parish registers I'd photographed in the office, a child's name had quietly vanished from the Sunday school roll. Not transferred. Not grown out of. *Vanished* — the ink of the entry lifted, the margins scraped, the way old registers look when someone has been at them with a blade.

The flower fund. 1968. A gift of £4.

I looked at that number for a long time. Four pounds to bury a child's name.

The sky over the fen was going grey at the edges when I finally let myself think about the sentence he'd dropped and fled from. *One is very much alive. One is on television.* I ran the parish notables through my head — the vicar, Cole, too young and too nervous; the churchwardens, elderly and frightened; the archdeacon, a cipher. And then the one I'd been not-thinking about since the letters arrived, because he was too large, too beloved, too impossible: the diocese's celebrated reformer, the man on the television every Advent, the bishop whose face was on the poster in the hotel lobby not forty feet from my door.

No. Not yet. Not on four hours' sleep and a dying man's riddles. Evidence first. The payments first, all of them, back to 1968 — and I needed the 1974 inquest file, the one nobody in this town would even admit existed, and for that I needed the police.

I picked up my phone and pulled up the number DS Rowan Ashe had pressed into my hand outside the churchyard, along with a look I was still deciding how to read.

It rang twice.

"Ashe."

"It's Galloway. I need to see you. Now. And you need to bring the 1974 file."

A pause. In the background, a radio, a chair creaking — he was already at his desk, or still at it.

"Ms. Galloway," he said, and his voice had changed, gone careful, "I was about to call you. There's something you need to know about that file."

* * *

I met him an hour later in the car park behind the Fisherman's Return, because he wouldn't use the station phone and wouldn't come to the hotel, and when he got out of his car he was holding a folder the colour of old bread and his face was the colour of the folder.

"The inquest file on Ellery, Petch, and Vance," he said, "was opened in 1974 and sealed by order of the diocese before the coroner's findings were ever published. I requested it three days ago. This morning my superintendent told me to stop." He looked at the folder, then at me. "The seal order is signed. You'll want to see the signature."

He handed it to me, open to the last page.

The signature was a young man's signature, confident, looping, already half-formed into the hand that would one day sign pronouncements and appear beneath photographs on hotel lobby posters.

Bishop Ambrose Fenn. Then a curate, aged twenty-six.

The bells of St. Aldhelm's began to ring the hour across the cold grey water, and every stroke of them sounded, suddenly, like a countdown.

*Read what we were paid,* the dying man had said. I looked at the signature, and then at the ledger photographs glowing on my phone, and understood that I had been reading the payments all along — I just hadn't known yet who had been paying.
