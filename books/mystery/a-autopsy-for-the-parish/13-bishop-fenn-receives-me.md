## 13. Bishop Fenn Receives Me

The summons came on a Tuesday morning, delivered not by post but by a young man in a grey suit who stood outside my hotel in the drizzle holding an envelope of heavy cream paper like it might detonate.

"From Bishop Fenn's office," he said. "He'd be honoured if you'd call at the cathedral close. Thursday, at your convenience."

"At my convenience," I repeated.

"Eleven o'clock," he said, and got into a car that had been idling across the square long enough to be noticed by everyone including me.

I turned the envelope over twice before opening it. Inside, a single card, handwritten in a hand so controlled it looked typeset: *Miss Galloway — I have followed your work with admiration and some sorrow. I believe we are both seeking the same thing. Come and tell me what you have found. +Ambrose Fenn.*

*I believe we are both seeking the same thing.* I read that sentence perhaps forty times over two days, and each time it did something different to me. It was either an olive branch or a noose being measured out politely, coil by coil, and the terrible thing was that I could not tell which, and the more terrible thing was that part of me didn't care. After three weeks in Marsh Aldhelm — after closed doors and averted eyes and Reverend Cole's smile like a drawn curtain — here at last was someone willing to say the year 1974 out loud.

That frightened me most of all. The whole town had spent fifty years perfecting its silence, and this man wanted to talk.

* * *

The cathedral close at Wexcombe was everything Marsh Aldhelm was not: mown lawns, restored limestone, a porter's lodge with brass fittings polished by devotion or budget. St. Aldhelm's in the fen crouched in its churchyard like something waiting; the cathedral stood upright and certain, its spire stitched into the low sky, its grounds raked, its gravel crisp underfoot. Money lived here. Old money, older guilt, whichever funded the stonemasons.

A verger met me at the west door and walked me through the nave without a word, his footsteps swallowed by stone. Candles burned in ranks along the side chapels. A cleaning crew worked quietly near the font, and a woman knelt alone in the Lady Chapel with her coat still buttoned, praying or resting, impossible to say. It was beautiful in the way hospitals are clean — as a policy, as a statement.

Bishop Fenn received me in a first-floor room overlooking the close, a study arranged for effect: books worn rather than displayed, a desk too old to be fashionable, two chairs set at an angle that suggested conversation rather than interrogation. He rose when I entered, and I understood immediately why the diocese called him their reformer and why the national press photographed him at refugee centres and food banks with his sleeves rolled like a working man's.

He was tall, silver-haired, with a face built for grief — deep lines around the mouth, hooded eyes that seemed permanently braced against bad news. When he took my hand his grip was dry and warm and exactly firm enough.

"Miss Galloway. Thank you for coming." His voice was low, slightly roughened, a voice made for reading names of the dead. "You'll forgive me for summoning you like a schoolmistress. I find that invitations get lost in the post these days."

"I find they get found," I said, "when someone wants them to be."

Something moved behind his eyes — appreciation, I thought, though with this man every expression came pre-laundered. "Sit, please. Tea? My chaplain makes it far too strong, which is how I like it and how nobody else does."

He poured himself, which surprised me. Bishops, I have since learned, pour for themselves only when they want you to watch their hands.

* * *

For ten minutes he talked about nothing — the weather, the state of the fen roads, a restoration project on the cathedral's south transept. He was good at nothing. That was the point. He let me settle, let me catalogue him, let me think I had him measured while he measured me back with the patience of a man who had been listening to people confess for forty years.

Then he set down his cup and said, gently, "You've been in Marsh Aldhelm nearly a month. And in all that time, I don't believe anyone has told you a single true thing."

"No," I said.

"And yet you keep finding things." He smiled, and the smile was sorrowful, and the sorrow was genuine, and none of it meant what it appeared to mean. "The headstone. Three names on one stone, dated 1991, buried together like a secret instead of a tragedy. You found that in your second week. I know because the parish council telephoned me in a panic, and I told them — I said, *let her look.*"

"You told them to let me look."

"I did." He folded his hands. "Because Miss Galloway, whatever you may have concluded about this diocese — and I suspect your conclusions are unflattering — I have spent twenty years trying to drag this institution into the light. Safeguarding reforms. The independent review. I sat survivors down in this very room and listened until dawn, and then I fought my own colleagues to make sure what they told me wasn't buried again." He paused, and his voice dropped into something rawer. "And every time, the same answer comes back from the past: *we handled it. We handled it in 1974. We handled it in '68. We handled it the way institutions handle things,* which is to say we didn't handle it at all, we entombed it."

He said *entombed* looking directly at me, and I felt the word go through me like a needle finding wire.

"That's a remarkable speech," I said. "Rehearsed?"

"Practised," he admitted, and laughed — a real laugh, brief and self-deprecating, and I hated myself for liking it. "Twenty years of practice. But Miss Galloway, practise doesn't make it false. Here is what I propose." He leaned forward. "Full cooperation. Every archive, every file, every sealed record the diocese holds — open to you. Exclusive access. Nobody else, no other journalist, gets what you get. And in return—"

"There it is," I said.

"In return," he continued smoothly, as though I hadn't spoken, "you do what you do best. You tell it properly. Not the feverish version, not the version where a dying man's letters turn a market town into a lynch mob. The true version. Careful. Sourced. With context." He spread his hands. "You want the truth. So do I. The difference between us, I suspect, is that I already know most of it, and knowing it has cost me twenty years of sleep."

"The letters," I said. "The confession. The threat against a fourth victim. You know about those."

"I know about everything that happens in my diocese," he said mildly. "Including, I'm told, that you've been photographing burial ledgers in parish offices. Including that Detective Sergeant Ashe has been asking questions in Norwich. Including—" and here he paused, delicately, the way a surgeon pauses before showing you the scan, "—that whoever wrote to you knows things only a handful of living people could know."

"Which is why I'm surprised you're offering me access instead of lawyers."

"Lawyers are how the last fifty years happened," Fenn said. "Lawyers are the silence, Miss Galloway. I am trying to end the silence." He rose, went to the window, and stood with his back half-turned, silhouetted against the pale sky — a pose I recognised from a hundred photographs, the reformer at his window, bearing the institution's weight alone. "May I tell you what I remember of 1974? Since everyone else in this story has forgotten so conveniently."

I said nothing. He took it as permission.

"I was a curate then. Twenty-six years old, wet behind the ears, posted to a deanery I didn't understand. I knew there were rumours — there are always rumours — and I knew that three people left one winter and were never spoken of again. I asked questions. Once." His shoulders moved, a small shrug of shame performed so well I almost believed it had never been rehearsed. "I was told by my betters that the matter was in hand, that I was young, that faith required trust. And I believed them. That is my sin, Miss Galloway. Not what was done — I didn't do it, whatever your dying sexton's apprentice has told you — but that I let wiser, worse men tell me it was settled, and I went away and built a career on the foundation of their settling."

He turned back to face me, and his eyes were wet, and I want to state plainly, for the record of my own conscience, that I believed every word he said and trusted none of it, and that both of those things can be true at once, and that this is precisely how men like Ambrose Fenn survive.

"So yes," he said softly. "Open the archives. Name the dead. Say 1974 aloud in the square at Marsh Aldhelm if you must. Only — let me help you aim. Because there are versions of the truth that destroy the guilty, and versions that merely destroy the innocent, and the people who wrote those letters to you are not interested in the difference."

* * *

We spoke for another hour. He answered questions I hadn't asked yet, anticipated others, and deflected three — the deflections so smooth that I only catalogued them on the drive home. He gave me the name of the archivist who held the sealed inquest file (Daniel Okafor, he said, a good man, thorough, recently very anxious about something — and watched my face as he said it, and I gave him nothing, and I think he saw that I gave him nothing). He offered me the diocesan safeguarding reports from 2003, redacted, he admitted, but redacted by lawyers, not by him. He pressed a card into my hand with a private number on it and said, "Any hour. Truly."

At the door he stopped me with a light touch on my arm.

"One more thing," he said. "Thomas Reeve."

I kept my face still. Somewhere below us the cathedral bells began the quarter-hour, and the vibration came up through the floorboards like a pulse.

"He was the sexton's boy, back then. Sweet lad. Carried the candles at funerals because his hands were steady." Fenn's voice gentled further. "If he is the man writing to you — and I say *if* because I would hate to see a sick old man prosecuted for the crime of wanting to be heard — then understand what he is, Miss Galloway. He is not your villain. He is the institution's residue. Everything this place failed to carry, it set down on boys like Thomas Reeve, and they carried it fifty years, and now it is killing them." He released my arm. "Whatever he has told you, ask yourself who taught him that burying things was his job."

It was, I will grant him, a magnificent performance. It was also, I realised somewhere on the stairs, a confession wearing vestments — because I had never said Reeve's name to anyone in the diocese, and neither had Ashe, and the hospice in Norwich had been discreet.

Fenn knew. Which meant either the confessor had written to him too — or Fenn had been reading the ledger all along.

* * *

Outside, the drizzle had thickened into proper rain. I sat in the rental car in the close for a long time with the engine off, watching water crawl down the windscreen, doing the arithmetic of the interview.

He had offered me everything. Archives, access, exclusivity, cooperation — the exact instruments I had come to Wexcombe begging for. And every gift had arrived with a string so fine I couldn't see it, only feel it, the way you feel a hair across your lips. Exclusive access meant exclusive control: whatever I published, I published through his door, on his timetable, framed by his contrition. *Let me help you aim*, he'd said — and aiming, I now understood, was how you made sure the bullet went where you wanted.

Worse than the offer was the effect of it. Driving back through the fens with the wipers going, I caught myself constructing the generous version of him — the young curate, the bad orders, the twenty years of fighting — and I had to pull over onto a verge and sit with it, because that was the trick, that was always the trick: he didn't need me to believe him. He only needed me to hesitate. One honest doubt, lodged in me like shrapnel, and every sentence I broadcast afterward would carry it. *Even Ulyana Galloway isn't sure.* Doubt is cheaper than silence and works just as well.

I had gone in feeling like an investigator. I came out feeling laundered — wrung, rinsed, hung out to dry in his weather.

My phone buzzed against the passenger seat. A text from a number I didn't recognise, and my stomach dropped before I read it, because the last unknown number had known my room number and quoted psalms.

But this one was four words, from Daniel Okafor:

**'Meet me. Tomorrow. Please.'**

Behind me, miles back in Wexcombe, the cathedral bells finished the hour. Ahead, the fen road ran straight and black through the rain toward Marsh Aldhelm, where the lights of St. Aldhelm's were just coming on — one by one, like something counting.
