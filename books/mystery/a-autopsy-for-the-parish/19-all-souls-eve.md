## 19. All Souls' Eve

The Requiem began at seven, and the whole of Marsh Aldhelm came to be seen grieving.

I stood at the back of St. Aldhelm's nave in a black coat I'd bought in Norwich that afternoon, because my other clothes had burned with my rental car, and watched Bishop Ambrose Fenn process down the aisle like a man being crowned rather than a man burying the past. Television lights glowed on scaffolds near the transept — he'd invited them himself. *A Requiem for the Missing*, the diocesan press release called it. Fifty years late, and filmed in high definition.

He was magnificent. That was the thing I kept forgetting to hold against him. Tall, silver at the temples, a voice built for stone vaults, he read the names of the three vanished parishioners — Vera Kettleby, Ronald Pask, Margaret Ashwin — as though each one were a wound of his own. Half the congregation wept. The cameras loved him. Somewhere in the upper reaches of the tower, the bells waited for ten o'clock, when they would ring in All Souls' Day, and Thomas Reeve's deadline would expire, and either Daniel Okafor would die or he wouldn't.

I hadn't heard from Reeve since All Hallows' Eve. His phone went to a hospice switchboard. His room, according to a nurse who owed me a favor from an old episode, was empty except for the oxygen equipment he'd signed out against medical advice. A dying man had crawled out of his own care and into the dark somewhere, carrying fifty years of guilt and a ledger's worth of secrets, and I couldn't decide whether that made me safer or more afraid.

Ashe found me at the back, dressed in a suit that fit him badly, which meant it wasn't his.

"You look like a detective," I murmured.

"I feel like a man committing career suicide in a nice building." His eyes tracked Fenn without appearing to. "Superintendent's here. Third pew. He didn't come to pray."

"Neither did half these people."

"No." Ashe's jaw tightened. "I've been through the crypt access twice today. There are two ways down — the stair behind the organ loft, and the memorial chamber door off the north aisle. The chamber door's been resealed. Fresh mortar. Someone wanted it shut."

"When?"

"Mortar's still soft. Days, not weeks." He turned his head slightly, voice dropping. "And there's this. I pulled the Requiem's run sheet from the verger's office. Fenn scheduled the church closed to visitors from six o'clock. No tours, no side-chapel access. He cleared the building."

"For a televised service."

"For a service where nobody wanders downstairs." Ashe looked at me then, and whatever he saw in my face made him exhale slowly. "You're going down there tonight, aren't you. During the service."

"The last ledger entry is dated tomorrow," I said. "All Souls'. If Okafor is the final line, this is the only window. Everyone who matters is up here, being seen to grieve."

"And if you're wrong?"

"Then I've interrupted a crypt during a Requiem and I'll apologize to the bishop personally."

He almost smiled. Almost. "I'm coming with you. And before you argue — I've already lost the job. The suspension letter came this morning. Diocesan office made one call." He said it flatly, without self-pity, and something in my chest twisted. "So as far as I'm concerned, I'm just a member of the public attending a service. Who happens to be armed."

"Fenn will have people."

"Fenn has verger and two cameramen. I'll take the verger."

* * *

We moved during the Sanctus.

It was the only cover the building offered — every voice in the parish lifted together, the organ swelling under the vaulting, all eyes forward on the bishop at the altar. We slipped out through the choir vestry, past cassocks hanging like shed skins, and Ashe put his hand flat against the door behind the organ loft and pushed.

The stair was narrow, medieval, lit by a single bulb on a timer that had given up sometime in the previous century. Our phone torches cut cones through the dark. The air changed as we descended — colder, wetter, tasting of lime and old stone and something else underneath, something organic and patient. I had been in crypts before. I knew what they smelled like. This one smelled like it was holding its breath.

The passage opened into the undercroft, and my torch found the wall.

Not the crypt proper — the older part, the part on the 1730s plan marked *chantry, disused*. It was walled. Brick over ancient stone, mortared recently enough that the joints still shone faintly damp, and set into the brickwork at eye level, incongruous as a joke told at a funeral, a slab of polished Purbeck marble with an incised cross. The memorial stone. The one the final ledger entries mapped, plot coordinates that weren't graves at all but measurements — distances from the chantry corners, triangulating to a point directly behind this wall.

Fifty years of documents. Three bodies. Sealed behind a wall in the middle of a church where people prayed every Sunday for half a century, six feet from the truth.

"Ashe," I said quietly.

"I see it." He ran his palm along the mortar seam. "This section's newer than the rest. Look — the brick color changes here. This bit's been opened and reclosed. Recently." He glanced back at me. "Days."

My mouth had gone dry. "Okafor found the suppressed autopsy report. He told me yesterday. He said the trio didn't drown — they were suffocated. He was coming to the crypt to find the original inquest materials. He texted me at four o'clock: *Behind the stone. They never even moved them.* Then nothing."

Ashe drew his baton, then thought better of it and unclipped his torch instead, holding it like a club. "Stand back from the door line."

There was no door line. There was a gap in the brickwork at the far corner, hidden behind a rank of stacked chairs, wide enough for a man to pass if he turned sideways. Fresh scuff marks on the floor led toward it. And caught on a rough brick edge, glinting in my torchlight: a thread of navy wool.

Daniel Okafor wore a navy cardigan. I'd seen it in the photograph on the diocesan website, the archivist smiling beside a cart of boxed files.

We went through sideways, into the dark.

* * *

The chantry beyond was a low vaulted cell, maybe five meters square, its original altar niche bricked up centuries ago. Candle stubs littered the floor — old ones, wax run into the cracks of the flagstones, decades of them. Someone had been coming here for years. Coming here to sit with what he'd done, or what he'd helped do, or what he'd been paid to forget.

Daniel Okafor lay against the far wall, bound at wrists and ankles with plastic ties, gagged with his own scarf. His eyes found my torch and went wide, and he made a sound against the gag — not a scream, something worse, a sound of *finally*.

"Daniel." I knelt, working at the ties. "Look at me. You're alive. You're fine. Where is he—"

"He's not here," said a voice behind us, calm as a weather report. "But he was very helpful."

Ambrose Fenn stood in the doorway with a hurricane lamp raised in one hand, and the light did extraordinary things to his face. It should have made him monstrous. Instead it made him look like every painting of every saint I'd ever stared at in a provincial church — sorrowful, luminous, patient. In his other hand, held delicately between finger and thumb like a communion wafer, was a single page, brown with age.

The ledger's oldest page. 1968. The first entry. The first payment.

"Bishop," Ashe said, and stepped between Fenn and me, torch raised.

"Detective Sergeant Ashe." Fenn smiled gently. "Or former Detective Sergeant Ashe, as of nine o'clock this morning. Do come in. Mind the candles — some of them are older than both of us."

Behind me, Okafor thrashed against the ties. I got the wrist ones loose; blood rushed back into his hands and he tore the gag down himself, gasping. "He was waiting," Okafor rasped. "When I broke through — he was sitting here. In the dark. Like he'd been waiting for years."

"Perhaps I had," Fenn said. He stepped fully into the cell and set the lamp on a ledge, and the shadows rearranged themselves around him like courtiers. "Ulyana Galloway. Sit down, please. Both of you. There's no need for drama, and there's nowhere to go — the stair is the only way out, and I locked it behind me. Not to trap you. To give us privacy. What I have to say shouldn't be overheard."

"We're recording," I lied.

"No," he said kindly, "you're not. Your phone died at the top of the stairs — signal jammer, very small, very modern, in my coat pocket. And Mr. Ashe's radio stopped working the moment he entered the undercroft. I did my homework too. It seemed polite."

Ashe's jaw worked. But he didn't reach for anything, because there was nothing to reach for, and Fenn watched him understand that with the mild satisfaction of a chess player watching an opponent count remaining pieces.

Okafor had gotten his feet free and dragged himself upright against the wall, breathing hard. "The bodies," he said. "They're here. Behind the east wall. I could see the edge of a coffin board through the breach."

"They're here," Fenn agreed. "Vera. Ronald. Margaret. I was twenty-six years old when they died. I want you to know that I remember their faces better than I remember my mother's." He said it without a flicker of performance, and that was the most frightening thing yet — that he might actually believe his own grief. "That doesn't change what I'm about to offer you."

He lifted the page. 1968. I could read the entry upside down from three meters away, because I had decoded it twice over: *Roof repairs, £40, R.P.* Ronald Pask. The first hush payment. The first lie in a ledger of lies.

"Do you know what this is?" Fenn asked me.

"It's the beginning."

"It's the *beginning*," he repeated, savoring it. "One page. One signature. Mine." He let that land. "Yes. I signed it. I was the council secretary. I was ambitious and frightened and the reverend of that day — a monster, by the way, a genuine monster, everything Edith Marlowe remembers and worse — explained to me what would happen to the parish, to the school, to forty families, if those three spoke to the police. And I believed him. God forgive me, I believed him, and then I helped make sure no one ever had to find out whether I was right or wrong."

"So you suffocated them yourself?" Ashe said flatly.

"I was *present*," Fenn said, and for one instant the saint's face cracked and something older looked out of it, something tired and cold. "That is not the same thing. That has never been the same thing. Ask any soldier." He smoothed the page against his thigh. "Thomas Reeve did the digging. Thomas Reeve kept the ledger. Thomas Reeve has spent fifty years dying by inches in that bell tower, and now he is dying quickly, and he has decided to spend his last weeks burning down the Church that sheltered him. The letters, Miss Galloway. The threats. The deadline. All of it — his design. I merely... declined to stop him."

"You're going to blame him," I said. "For all of it."

"I'm going to *attribute* it to him," Fenn corrected, "and history will do the rest. A dying sexton's apprentice, consumed by guilt, kills three people in 1974 on his own initiative, seals them in the crypt, blackmails the parish for decades — the ledger supports it, the handwriting supports it, and the poor wretch cannot contradict it because he'll be dead or demented by the time the trial concludes. Meanwhile the diocese, wounded but transparent, commissions a safeguarding review, publishes everything, weeps on camera—" he gestured vaguely upward, toward the television lights, "—and is *reformed*. Truly reformed. That is the story. That is the story the country will be told tomorrow morning."

"And the actual story burns," Ashe said.

Fenn produced, from inside his coat, a slim steel lighter. Old-fashioned. Engraved. He didn't open it. He simply held it, the way a priest holds the paten.

"Here is my offer," he said, turning to me, and his voice dropped into the register he used for blessings. "You walk out of here tonight. You leave the country for a month — I'll arrange it, a fellowship, a writing residency, whatever fiction suits you. When you return, I give you the archive. All of it. Every document in this wall, authenticated, plus my personal papers, plus full access to the sealed inquest. You write the book. Not the podcast — the *book*. The definitive account of Marsh Aldhelm, told with the cooperation of the one man still alive who can answer every question. You'll have what no journalist alive has ever had: the monster's own testimony, freely given, on the record."

"And the catch."

"The catch," Fenn said, "is that the book tells the truth as I've described it. Reeve killed them. Reeve extorted the parish. The institution erred, grieved, and reformed. You will be the most celebrated true-crime writer in Europe within eighteen months. Or—" the lighter turned once in his fingers, catching the lamplight, "—you interfere, and in ninety minutes the bells ring, the congregation disperses, and this wall comes down anyway — I have men coming at half past nine — and everything in it becomes ash in a brazier before midnight. And you will have witnessed nothing, proved nothing, and Daniel Okafor here will tell whatever story keeps him employed, won't you, Daniel."

Okafor spat at him. It missed. Fenn didn't even blink.

"You're offering me the story of a lifetime," I said.

"I'm offering you the *only* version of the story that will ever exist," Fenn said. "That is not a threat, Miss Galloway. It is arithmetic. Evidence burns. I don't. Choose."

The lamp flame stood perfectly still. Somewhere above us, muffled through meters of stone, the choir had begun the Agnus Dei, and the sound came down like water through rock — *Lamb of God, who taketh away the sins of the world* — and Ambrose Fenn closed his eyes briefly, as though the music moved him, as though perhaps it genuinely did.

I looked at Okafor, pressed against the wall among fifty years of candle stubs. At Ashe, coiled and helpless. At the page in the bishop's hand — one signature, 1968, the first entry in a ledger of debts that ended, dated tomorrow, with a blank line waiting for a name.

"Before I answer," I said, "one question. The fourth letter. The threat against Daniel. Was that Reeve's bait — or yours?"

Fenn smiled, and opened the lighter, and the small flame stood up between us like a second candle.

"What a perceptive question," he said. "Unfortunately—"

Above us, impossibly early, a single bell struck.

Once. Deep. Wrong.

Fenn's head snapped upward, and for the first time since I'd met him, the Bishop of the diocese looked afraid.

"That's not the schedule," he said. "Who's in my tower?"
