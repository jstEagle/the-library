## 20. The Bells Ring In

The crypt smelled of candle wax and wet stone and fifty years of held breath.

Ulyana stood at the foot of the chantry steps with her phone in her hand, the live feed running, the red dot pulsing like a small stubborn heart. Above her, muffled through three feet of medieval flint, the Requiem had begun — she could feel it more than hear it, the congregation's murmur seeping down through the floor of St. Aldhelm's like water through a fen bank.

Fenn stood by the walled-off chantry arch, and he was not what she had braced for.

She had expected a monster caught in the act. What she got was a man in full vestments, white and gold, holding the ledger's oldest page the way a priest holds a host — delicately, reverently, between thumb and forefinger. A brass lighter sat closed in his other palm. Behind him, taped at wrists and ankles to an old votive rack, Daniel Okafor was conscious and watching her with eyes that were doing arithmetic: *how long, how far, who else*.

"Miss Nakamura," Fenn said. His voice carried in the crypt the way voices carry in places built for prayer — effortlessly, as if the stone itself were listening. "You're late. The Gloria started four minutes ago."

"I don't sing along."

"No." He smiled, and it was a good smile, and that was the whole horror of him. "You never did. That's why I asked you here. Or rather — why I let you find your way here. You'd have come regardless. You're not a woman who can leave a ledger half-read."

"The page," Ulyana said. "Put it down."

"It's one page of several hundred." He turned it slightly, letting the candlelight through the paper so she could see the faded columns — hymn numbers, plot coordinates, sums in a dead sexton's careful hand. "Do you know what this entry is? November 1968. The first payment. Eleven pounds, recorded as *repairs to the south porch*. Eleven pounds to keep a mother quiet about what her son told her happened in the vestry." He looked at the page almost tenderly. "It's the parish's birth certificate, Miss Nakamura. Everything Marsh Aldhelm is, grew from this."

"And everything it buried is behind that wall."

"Yes." No flinch. No denial. That was the thing people would never believe afterward, she thought — that he never once denied it. "Three of them. Forty-one years of correspondence, inquest papers, council minutes. I offered the diocese a clean church. I gave them one. Do you know what I've done since? Reform after reform. Safeguarding boards. Apologies read aloud in cathedrals across two counties. Every good thing I have built stands on the decision that this—" he lifted the page a fraction higher "—stays ash."

"You're going to burn it and blame Thomas Reeve."

"I'm going to burn it because it should be burned, and yes, Thomas Reeve will be blamed, which will be easy, because Thomas Reeve killed three people and confessed to it in his own handwriting." Fenn's tone didn't rise at all. "He wanted justice outside the law. I'm offering you something better than either. Walk out now. Keep recording — I don't care. In six months I'll be retired, the diocese will settle quietly with Okafor's family, and you'll have the only interview I will ever give about 1974. The story of your life, Miss Nakamura. The one they'll teach in journalism schools."

The red dot pulsed on her phone. Somewhere above, the choir swelled toward the Sanctus.

"You rehearsed that," she said.

"Every word. For years."

"That's the saddest thing I've ever heard."

Something moved behind his eyes then — the first hairline crack in all that composure. "You think grief is a performance?"

"I think you've been performing it so long you can't tell anymore." She took a step forward, deliberately slow, keeping his attention on her face and off the dark mouth of the tower stair behind her. "Here's my counteroffer, Bishop. You put down the page and the lighter, we untie Daniel, and you say it all again on camera. On the record. Your reforms might even survive it. Some of them were real. Let them be the sentence instead of the alibi."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the bells ring in, and everything comes down."

Fenn tilted his head, genuinely curious. "What do you mean, *everything comes down*?"

And Ulyana, who had spent three weeks learning to read a dead man's code, heard the answer arrive before she could stop it — heard it in the small change of sound overhead, in the groan of old timber that no one in the congregation above would notice but that she had been listening for since she slipped away from her pew during the Kyrie.

Someone was in the bell tower.

Someone was climbing.

* * *

Reeve had sworn he wouldn't try anything. *I'm bait, not a butcher*, he'd said in the bell tower, oxygen tank gleaming beside him like a tin god. But he'd also said: *someone else has been reading the ledger*, and Ulyana had spent the last day wondering which of them was lying, and now the tower stair was creaking under a weight that moved too slowly and too stubbornly to be anyone but a dying man descending ninety steps on will alone.

She kept talking. It was the only stall she had.

"Tell me about the boy," she said. "The one who sang like an angel. Edith Marlowe couldn't finish the sentence. Can you?"

Fenn's lighter-hand tightened. "Edith Marlowe should have stayed in the choir loft where she belonged."

"His name was Stephen Voss," Ulyana said. "Born 1959. Removed from the parish register in March 1971 — entry coded to hymn 234, plot 41, donation eleven shillings. He'd have been twelve." She watched the page tremble, just barely, in Fenn's fingers. "There are nine more like him in the ledger. Nine children, erased line by line, and every erasure paid for out of the flower fund. You want to talk about what the parish grew from? It grew from that."

"You know nothing about those years—"

"I know the autopsy." She hadn't meant to play that card yet; it left her anyway. "Okafor found the suppressed report. They didn't drown, Bishop. Alice Pargeter, Ronald Tebbit, Margaret Soames — suffocated. Held down. By men strong enough and calm enough to do it twice more after the first one stopped moving." Her voice cracked on the last words and she let it. "Your calm. I finally understand where it comes from. You've been holding things down since 1974."

For one naked instant, Ambrose Fenn looked his age — seventy-nine years old, alone in a crypt with a dying man's evidence and a living woman's microphone, the gold on his vestments gone the color of tallow in the candlelight.

Then the first bell struck.

* * *

It was not the Requiem bell. The Requiem bell tolled slow and single, and this was nothing like it — this was the old mechanism, the pre-war wheel-and-stock that the parish had abandoned decades ago, and when it released it went off like a gunshot, a full-throated bronze roar that shook grit from the vaulting and made the candles gutter sideways.

Above, the congregation's singing stopped mid-phrase.

Fenn spun toward the tower stair. "Who—"

"Thomas Reeve," Ulyana said. "Ninety steps. Fourteen minutes. He told me he'd crawl them if he had to." She raised her phone higher, red dot steady. "He also told me about the mechanism. The old headstock's rotted through — he reported it himself in 1998 and they never fixed it, because fixing things wasn't what the maintenance budget was actually for, was it? When the great bell swings free, the whole frame torques against the east wall. The east wall of the tower is the east wall of the chantry."

Understanding crossed Fenn's face — and with it, for the first time, fear.

The second bell struck. Plaster sifted down like snow. From the church above came the sound of three hundred people rising to their feet at once, chairs scraping, a priest's voice calling for calm, and beneath it all the deep irregular tolling of a bell rung by hands that had no strength left except exactly enough.

"She can't ring them properly," Fenn said, half to himself, backing toward the chantry wall, toward the page and the lighter. "The mechanism needs—"

"Four pulls," Ulyana said. "Reeve counted them out for me. Pull four releases the brake entirely."

The third bell struck, and the wall behind Fenn breathed dust.

He looked at the page in his hand. He looked at the lighter. And Ulyana saw the decision move through him like weather — saw him weigh fifty years of architecture against one sheet of paper, saw him choose, with the terrible efficiency that had organized his entire life, to save the fire rather than the church.

He flicked the lighter.

Ulyana lunged. She was not fast enough and she knew it even as she moved; the flame caught the corner of the page and the old paper bloomed orange, and Fenn drew back his arm to hurl it into the candle rack—

—and the fourth pull came.

The great bell did not strike so much as detonate. The whole tower seemed to inhale, and then the east wall answered — a crack like the world's spine giving way, a seam of daylight opening where there had been solid flint for five hundred years, and the chantry wall came down in a slow, thunderous avalanche of stone and lime and centuries.

Through the gap, lit by the falling plaster and the guttering candles, Ulyana saw it: the walled-off chantry laid open at last. Three shapes wrapped in lead sheeting, side by side, undisturbed. And behind them, stacked to the ceiling, boxes. Files. Bundles tied with parish string. Fifty years of the Long Silence, breathing its first breath of air.

The burning page left Fenn's hand.

Ashe came out of the dark of the north stair like something thrown — she never learned afterward how long he'd been there, or on whose instruction — and hit Fenn at the waist. The two of them went down across the fallen stone. The page fluttered, still burning, and landed face-up on the wet crypt floor, where the flame found damp lime and died to a black-edged curl, one corner gone, the rest intact. *Repairs to the south porch. Eleven pounds.* Surviving itself.

Fenn fought. He was stronger than his age suggested and completely without hesitation, and for ten seconds it was ugly and close — until Ashe got an arm across his throat and said, close to his ear, in a voice Ulyana had never heard him use: "Bishop Ambrose Fenn, I am arresting you for the murders of Alice Pargeter, Ronald Tebbit, and Margaret Soames, and for the murder of—" he stopped, corrected himself, "—and for the false imprisonment of Daniel Okafor. Anything you say will be taken down. And Bishop — it *will* be taken down. All of it."

Fenn went still. Not defeated — calculating, even now, even pinned in the rubble. Then his eyes found Ulyana's phone, and the red dot, and whatever he calculated came up short, and something in him finally, finally broke. He began to laugh. It was the worst sound in the crypt, worse than the wall coming down — a rich, warm, practiced laugh, the laugh of a man who had comforted thousands of grieving families, echoing off the stones while the dust settled around him like ash.

Above, the congregation was pouring down the nave stairs. Ulyana heard gasps, someone praying aloud, someone screaming for a doctor, and through it all the great bell swinging itself empty and wild overhead, ringing in All Souls' with no hand on the rope at all.

She knelt by Okafor and cut the tape off his wrists.

"They're here," he whispered. His voice was wrecked. He was staring past her at the opened wall, at the leaded shapes. "They were here the whole time. Under the floor we stood on. Every Sunday."

"I know," Ulyana said.

"Everyone sang over them."

"I know." She helped him sit up. "Not anymore."

* * *

They brought Reeve down on a stretcher, though he argued about it the whole way, wheezing, one hand still hooked around the tower rail as if the mechanism might need him again. At the foot of the stairs he made them stop, and Ulyana crouched beside him, and he looked past her at the breach in the wall with an expression she would carry for the rest of her life — not triumph, not relief. Accounting. A sexton checking his figures at last.

"Pull four," he rasped.

"Pull four," she agreed.

"Ledger balances." His eyes closed. "Get the names right, girl. All of them. Even his." A breath. "*Especially* his."

Medics lifted him. Ashe stood over Fenn, who had stopped laughing and was watching the light pour through the broken wall onto the three leaded shapes, his face at last perfectly legible: a man looking at the thing he had spent fifty years standing on.

Ulyana picked up the scorched page from the floor. One corner burned away; the rest of it trembling slightly in her hand. She turned to the crowd filling the crypt stairs — Edith Marlowe among them, upright and furious and alive, the whole parish pressing down into the earth to see what they had spent their lives not seeing.

She held up the page, and the phone, and her voice, when it came, did not shake at all.

"This is Ulyana Galloway," she said, "and the silence ends tonight."

Behind her, unroped and unbidden, the great bell swung once more — a single clear note rolling up through the broken wall, out through the open tower, over the fen — and the town of Marsh Aldhelm, gathered in the dark below, began at last to remember.
