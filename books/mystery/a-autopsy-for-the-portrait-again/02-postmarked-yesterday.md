## 2. Postmarked Yesterday

The morning after Canon Merrick's funeral, Felix Reyes did what Felix Reyes always did after a funeral: they went to work.

Not the funeral itself—Felix had attended, of course, standing at the back of the nave beneath the tower with the other staff, hands folded, face arranged into the mild blankness of a man who grieves appropriately and no more. Canon Merrick had been a decent man, in the way that institutions produce decent men: punctual, generous with his small kindnesses, careful of his reputation. He had sung in the choir as a boy here, been ordained here, served here for forty years until the river took him. The whole of St. Aldhelm's had turned out to bury him. The Dean had wept beautifully. The organist had played something by Parry that made even the vergers blow their noses.

And Felix had stood in the back row and thought: *you didn't drown yourself, Canon Merrick, and I don't believe for a moment you drowned by accident.*

It was not a thought Felix shared. It was never a thought Felix shared. That was the whole architecture of Felix's life, load-bearing and invisible: the archivist of St. Aldhelm's Cathedral, a thin, quiet person of indeterminate middle age who smelled of dust and binding adhesive, whose name most of the congregation could not have produced under oath. Felix had spent eleven years cultivating that anonymity the way a gardener cultivates moss—in patience, in shadow, with a certain tenderness. Invisibility was not a wound. Invisibility was a wall, and behind the wall Felix kept a locked drawer, and in the locked drawer were the files.

The files were not official. They were not even, strictly, supposed to exist. They were Felix's own careful transcriptions, photographs, and marginalia concerning the deaths that had washed through St. Aldhelm's parish over the years wearing the convenient clothing of accident. The verger who fell from the tower scaffold in 2016, though the scaffold inspection log showed the guardrail bolts freshly tightened two days before. The churchwarden whose "heart attack" at the wheel came four days after she'd told the PCC she'd found irregularities in the restoration accounts. The drowning of a visiting bell-ringer in the mill leat, where the water was knee-deep and the mud was the kind that holds you upright. Three cases in eleven years, each closed by the police with the tidy finality of a ledger line, each reopened—quietly, alone, at night—by Felix.

Felix had never gone to the police. That was the discipline. Felix wrote the answers down and locked them away, and if occasionally, anonymously, a photocopied page had found its way to a particular detective constable with a note in block capitals—*CHECK THE SCAFFOLD INSPECTION LOG, OCTOBER 14*—well. The wind blew things about. Cathedrals were drafty places.

So on the morning after the funeral, Felix came down the crypt stairs at seven-forty with a thermos of coffee and the particular heavy contentment of a person returning to a familiar silence, and found the envelope on the mat.

It had not been there the night before. Felix was certain of it, in the way that Felix was certain of most things—certainly enough to bet on. Felix locked the crypt every evening at six, and the outer door to the archive stair was bolted from within, and the mat inside that door was bare when Felix left at six-oh-three, because Felix always checked, the way other people checked the stove. The mat this morning held a single envelope, cream-colored, heavy stock, addressed in fountain-pen ink.

*F. Reyes, Cathedral Archivist.*

Not *The Archivist.* Not *To the Archive.* F. Reyes. Felix's name, spelled correctly, which was more than the payroll office managed.

Felix stood over it for a long moment, coffee cooling in one hand, and felt the small animal part of the brain—the part that had kept the species alive—begin to whisper. Then Felix set down the thermos, put on the white cotton gloves kept on the hook by the door for handling fragile documents, and picked up the envelope by its corner like the artifact it already was.

The postmark was the first wrong thing.

Felix turned it to the light. The franking was smudged, as frankings always were, but legible enough: *ST. ALDHelmS, 14 OCT.* And Felix knew the date the way Felix knew all dates that mattered, because dates were the skeleton key of the archive: the fourteenth of October was the day before Canon Merrick's body had been found. The day before two dog-walkers had seen his cassock snagged on the tide gate below the flats and called it in. Merrick had gone into the water on the night of the thirteenth—or so the police believed, or so they had decided to believe—and the fourteenth was the day the river gave him back.

Which meant this letter had been posted before anyone knew Canon Merrick was dead.

Felix carried it to the long oak table beneath the one high, barred window that gave the crypt its grey, submarine light, and sat, and did not open it. Not yet. First, the ritual. Felix photographed the envelope from six angles—the front, the back, the flap, the postmark under raking light from the desk lamp—then measured it with the brass rule, then weighed it in the palm. Cream laid paper, 120 grams at least, the kind of stock the cathedral office itself used for correspondence with the bishop. The flap had been sealed with a plain stick of glue, no wax, no monogram. The address was written in a hand that made Felix's neck prickle, because it was not a stranger's hand.

It was not a hand Felix could name, either. That was the maddening thing. Eleven years among documents teaches an archivist to read handwriting the way a huntsman reads ground—every hand has its gait, its habits, the little stumbles and flourishes that identify it as surely as a fingerprint. This hand was a careful one, an old-fashioned one, the letters upright and slightly narrow, the crossbars of the t's drawn long and level like ruled lines. The capital F of *Felix* had a crossbar too, which was unusual. And somewhere under the care of it, under the deliberate evenness, Felix caught a flicker of something familiar—a slight backward drag on the downstrokes, as though the writer were left-handed and forcing the pen against its nature.

Felix knew a left-handed writer who had trained himself right-handed. Felix had catalogued three of his sermons. But the thought slid away when Felix reached for it, the way a fish slides away in dark water, and Felix set it aside and opened the letter with the bone folder, slitting the top cleanly, preserving the envelope entire.

One page. Same cream stock. Same careful hand, though here the evenness began to break down halfway through, the letters tightening, the ink pooling where the pen had paused too long.

*Mr. Reyes,*

*You will have questions about the death of Canon Merrick. I am writing to tell you that I am responsible for it.*

*I did not mean what happened, but I did it, and I will not pretend otherwise, and I will not go to the police because they would not believe me and I have reasons of my own for keeping my name out of it. I am telling you because I know you will understand. I know about the tower. I know about the churchwarden. I know you keep your answers in the crypt, in the locked drawer, and that you have already begun to ask questions about the Canon, and I am asking you—begging you—to stop.*

*You will want to know why I am confessing to you and not to the police. It is because of what happened in 1994. The Canon and I were both there, and the water has kept it all this time, and now it is giving it back, and the Canon wanted to talk, and I could not let him.*

*I am sorry. Not for what I did to him. For what I am about to do to you, if you do not stop.*

*There is no signature because you already know who I am.*

Felix read it four times. Then Felix took off the gloves, laid them flat on the table, and sat very still in the grey light while the crypt's familiar silence rearranged itself around the letter into something else entirely.

The first thing, the thing that made the skin crawl, was the postmark. A confession posted before the murder was discovered. Which meant one of three things: the writer had killed Merrick and posted the letter in the hours before the body surfaced, in some ritual of guilt or triumph; or the writer had posted it in advance, knowing when the body would be found, which implied the drowning had been scheduled like a service; or the postmark was false, and someone wanted Felix to believe one of the first two things. Every possibility was worse than the last.

The second thing was the drawer.

*You keep your answers in the crypt, in the locked drawer.* Felix looked at the drawer now, at the far end of the table, brass lock gleaming dully. Three keys existed. One hung on Felix's ring. One was in the safe in the cathedral office, sealed in an envelope marked *ARCHIVE SPARE.* And the third—Felix had always assumed the third had been lost decades ago, before Felix's time, a gap in the key register that Felix had noted with archival disapproval and done nothing about. Someone had that key. Or someone had a lockpick and patience. Either way, the letter's author knew what was in the drawer, which meant they had opened it, which meant they had stood in this room, at this table, and read Felix's secret files, and put everything back so neatly that Felix—who noticed everything—noticed nothing.

The third thing was the tower. The churchwarden. Those were Felix's cases. Not the police's—Felix's. Felix had never told a living soul. The anonymous notes to the detective constable had been typed. The knowledge in this letter had only one possible source, and the source was Felix's own locked drawer, and the drawer had been opened, and the world had quietly tilted on its axis.

Felix got up, walked to the drawer, unlocked it, and counted. Everything was there. The 2016 file. The churchwarden file. The bell-ringer. The thin new folder, barely started, labeled only *M—, October.* Someone had read them and returned them. Someone had read them and learned Felix's method, Felix's habits, Felix's whole clandestine architecture—and had then written to Felix not as a stranger but as a colleague. *I know you will understand.*

And that was the fourth thing, the thing Felix kept circling back to as the morning light crawled down the barred window and the cathedral above began its small noises—the boiler, the doors, the first tourists of the day shuffling across the nave floor like weather. *It is because of what happened in 1994.* A date. A specific, deliberate date, dropped into a murder confession like a coin into a well. Felix had spent eleven years among the cathedral's records and knew exactly what 1994 meant at St. Aldhelm's, because every archive has its holes, and 1994 had the largest: the year a choirboy named Thomas Penhale walked out of choir practice on the fourth of July and was never seen again. The year the tide went out and did not give him back. The year the police searched the flats for nine days and the diocese closed the file and everyone agreed, in that particular ecclesiastical tone, that the boy had run away.

Felix had read the file years ago, out of professional habit, and noted the holes in it the way one notes water stains on a ceiling—without expectation, without urgency. Pages missing from the choir attendance register. A witness statement that ended mid-sentence. A case closed with a speed that had always felt less like resolution than like a hand laid gently but firmly over a mouth.

*The water has kept it all this time, and now it is giving it back.*

Felix looked at the sentence for a long time. Then Felix looked at the letter's final line. *There is no signature because you already know who I am.*

But Felix didn't. That was the humiliation of it, sitting there in the cold with the letter spread out like a specimen. The hand was almost familiar. The backward drag of the downstrokes was almost familiar. Felix's whole life was made of almosts—almost noticed, almost suspected, almost known—and Felix had built the almosts into a fortress. Now someone had walked through the wall as if it were fog, and they knew Felix's name, and Felix's secrets, and Felix's method, and they were telling Felix, with terrible courtesy, to stop.

Felix did the thing Felix always did with fear, which was to file it. The letter went into a polyethylene sleeve, the sleeve into a new folder, the folder into the safe behind the false row of nineteenth-century parish magazines. The photographs went onto the encrypted drive. The envelope's measurements went into the log Felix kept of everything, because the log was the one habit that had never once betrayed Felix.

Then Felix sat back down at the table and allowed the thought that had been waiting all morning, patient as the tide.

The letter knew Felix had begun asking questions about Merrick. Not guessed—*knew*. *You have already begun to ask questions about the Canon.* But Felix's questions had been private, silent, conducted entirely inside Felix's own skull and the cathedral's records: the anomaly of where Merrick's body was found, downstream of where he should have entered the water given the tide that night; the fact that a man of seventy-three who swam every morning at the lido had somehow drowned in three feet of silted water; the small, wrong detail that Merrick's boots were on his feet but laced wrong, laced by another hand, the way one dresses a body. Felix had told no one. Felix had written nothing down yet. The M— folder in the drawer contained three pages of notes, and Felix had written them *yesterday morning*, before the funeral, and locked them away.

Which meant the writer had read them within the last thirty hours. Which meant the writer had been in the crypt since the funeral. Which meant—

Felix looked up at the high barred window, at the thin grey light, and understood that the silence of the crypt, the silence Felix had loved for eleven years, had never been silence at all. It had simply been a silence in which Felix was the only one talking. The walls had ears and had always had ears, and now the walls had picked up a pen.

Above, muffled through stone and floor, the cathedral bell began to ring the quarter. Felix sat with the letter's last line echoing, *you already know who I am*, and turned the phrase over like a coin worn smooth. Who did Felix know? Who, in this cathedral of rehearsed grief and beautiful music, had a left-handed hand trained rightward, access to a key or the patience to do without one, and a reason to know the year 1994 the way one knows a wound?

The bells finished. In the new quiet, Felix heard footsteps on the crypt stair—unhurried, heavy, descending—and Felix closed the safe with a sound like a swallowed word, and put on the face of a man who has been doing nothing at all, as the door opened and Sergeant Imogen Vale stood in it, holding her hat in one hand and something in her eyes that Felix had seen before, on other people, at other funerals.

"Mr. Reyes," she said. "I need to show you something the tide brought in."
