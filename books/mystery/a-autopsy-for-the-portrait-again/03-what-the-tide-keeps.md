## 3. What the Tide Keeps

The tide had been going out all morning, and Felix Reyes felt it in the building.

That was nonsense, of course—St. Aldhelm's stood a mile and a half inland from the flats, its foundations sunk deep in clay that had never once tasted salt water—but the cathedral had its own weather, and Felix had learned over eleven years to read it the way sailors read clouds. The stone breathed differently when something was coming. The air in the crypt went still and attentive, like a dog lifting its head before the doorbell rings. This morning, descending the worn steps with a tray of tea gone cold because they'd forgotten to drink it, Felix felt the whole building holding its breath.

They told themselves it was the barometer. They told themselves a great many things.

The crypt was Felix's kingdom, such as it was: two long vaulted chambers beneath the north transept where the diocese kept everything it could not bear to throw away and could not be bothered to organize. Ledgers from 1740 to the present. Burial registers with water-stained covers. Boxes of vestment buttons, broken processional lanterns, the minutes of committees dead longer than their members. To anyone else it was a tomb of paper. To Felix it was the only honest room in the building, because paper did not know how to lie—it only knew how to wait for someone who could read.

Felix set the tea down on the worktable beside the Merrick file and did not look at it. That was becoming a discipline too, this not-looking. For three days they had made themselves leave it alone, and for three days it had sat there radiating the particular gravity of an unanswered question, pulling at the edges of every other task until Felix's neat systems began to fray at the corners.

Canon Alistair Merrick. Drowned. Found on the tidal flats below the old sea wall on the morning of the fourteenth, face down in six inches of water, his shoes dry.

*His shoes were dry.* Felix had read the coroner's summary twice—the sergeant had let them see it, which was itself a small mystery—and the detail kept surfacing like a body. A man walks a mile across wet mudflats in the dark, slips, drowns in a tide pool, and comes out of it with dry shoes? Either he'd flown there, or someone had carried him, or he had died somewhere else entirely and been moved after the rain stopped. Three possibilities, and the police had closed the file on the least interesting one. *Misadventure. Alcohol may have been a contributing factor.* Merrick had taken a single sherry at evensong supper; Felix had poured it.

Felix had known Merrick for nine years. Nine years of Tuesday conversations in the archive while the canon hunted through hymnal revisions he would never finish, complaining about the organist's tempo and the dean's ambitions and the slow rot of the sea wall. Merrick was fussy, vain about his Greek, secretly generous—he'd paid the heating bill for the choir school annex out of his own stipend for two years and sworn Felix to silence about it, which was how Felix knew, because Felix kept everyone's secrets whether asked or not. A man like that did not wander drunk onto the flats at midnight. A man like that did not drown in six inches of water with dry shoes.

And now there was the letter.

It had arrived yesterday—yesterday, the day *after* the funeral, though the postmark predated the discovery of the body by nearly a week—and it sat upstairs in Felix's desk drawer under a false bottom that didn't exist yet but would by nightfall, because Felix was beginning to understand that some documents needed hiding not from the world but from themselves. Felix had read it eleven times. It confessed to killing Merrick in language so precise, so rich in detail no stranger could possess—the sherry, the Tuesday visits, even the exact shelf where Merrick liked to sit—that it could only have been written by the killer or by Merrick himself. And Merrick was in consecrated ground behind the cloister wall, and the handwriting, half-familiar as a voice heard through a wall, belonged to neither.

Felix had spent three days not looking at it, and had failed every day, and this morning had come down here instead to do something useful: to pull the burial registers and check whether the cathedral had ever, in its long memory, lost anyone else to the flats.

Because that was the thing Felix hadn't written down anywhere, the thing that lived only in the archive's oldest habit of mind. St. Aldhelm's had a relationship with the tide. Twice a century, roughly, the sea gave something back—a coffin displaced from the eroded churchyard of the drowned medieval village a mile east, a boundary stone, once, horribly, a cow. The flats kept what the town gave them and returned it on their own schedule, without apology. Felix had catalogued three such returns in the parish records. All three had been treated as curiosities.

Felix was reaching for the 1994 volume—they weren't sure why 1994; the year had simply been itching at them since the letter, some half-heard association, a name attached to a date—when the crypt door opened above and light fell down the stairs in a long trapezoid, and a shadow came down it wearing police-issue boots.

Felix knew the tread already. Sergeant Imogen Vale had visited the archive four times in the past two years, ostensibly to consult parish records, actually, Felix suspected, to watch Felix consult parish records. She had the specific patience of an officer who has decided a person is interesting and is willing to spend months confirming it. Her questions always arrived slightly sideways, aimed at what you hadn't said rather than what you had.

"Archivist." She stood at the bottom of the stairs, hat under her arm, rain still beaded on her shoulders. She had walked from the station. "Got a minute?"

"I've got several hundred," Felix said. "They're all spoken for, but I can lend you one."

Vale didn't smile. She never smiled, exactly; she performed a small muscular event near her mouth that acknowledged humor without endorsing it. She crossed the crypt slowly, her gaze doing its usual inventory—the ledgers, the worktable, the file folder lying squared to the table's edge with corners aligned to a precision no casual person achieved. Felix watched her notice the alignment. Watched her file it away.

"You've heard," she said.

"Heard what?"

"That's the third time you've answered a question with a question, Sergeant-to-Sergeant, and I'm counting."

"Archivist-to-sergeant," Felix corrected mildly. "Heard what?"

Vale pulled out the second chair—the visitor's chair, the one Felix kept for researchers and clergy and, twice a year, police—and sat down without being invited, which was also new. She laid her hat on her knee. Up close she looked like a woman who had slept badly and dressed anyway, collar straight, eyes bruised underneath.

"The erosion," she said. "East of the old sea wall. We've had a metre of the bank go since the January storms. Yesterday morning a dog walker found—" She stopped. Started again, more carefully, the way people do when they are choosing words not for clarity but for weight. "Remains. Partial. The tide's been working on them."

Felix's hands, which had been resting flat on the table, went very still. Stillness was the tell, Felix knew; a truly unbothered person would have kept sorting papers. But the body does what it does, and Felix's body had understood before the mind caught up, the way it had understood the morning the letter arrived, standing in the doorway holding an envelope that weighed nothing and everything.

"Whose?" Felix asked.

"We don't know whose." Vale's eyes held theirs, steady, unblinking, and Felix realized she was watching not for shock but for recognition. "Not officially. Forensics will take weeks. But the clothing survived better than the—than the rest did. A cassock. Choir cassock, child-sized. And a surplice fragment with the cathedral mark embroidered at the hem. And this." She reached into her coat pocket and set something on the table between them, gently, the way one sets down an egg.

A small brass badge, green with age, its pin still closed. Felix leaned forward and read the stamped letters without touching it. *ST. ALDHLEM'S CATHEDRAL CHOIR — HEAD CHORISTER.*

The crypt's silence changed quality. It had been the silence of old paper; now it was the silence of a held breath, and Felix understood suddenly why the building had felt like this all morning, why the stone had seemed to lean inward. Something had come back. The flats had opened their ledger and entered a line.

"There was a boy," Felix said. The words came out level, which surprised them. "In ninety-four. I haven't looked him up yet—I was reaching for the register when you came down. But there was a boy. Everyone who's been here longer than me goes quiet around that year. I assumed it was a budget crisis."

"Thomas Wrenn." Vale said the name the way you set a stone on a grave. "Eleven years old. Head chorister, spring term 1994. Reported missing on the fourth of June. Last seen at choir practice, Wednesday evening, by the verger on duty. The search went on for nine days. Dogs, divers, helicopters. Then it stopped." She paused. "Then it stopped very thoroughly."

"What do you mean, thoroughly?"

"I mean the file is thin, Mr. Reyes. Suspiciously thin. I pulled it last night after the identification call came in—preliminary, off the clothing and the dental records we managed to trace, and I'll deny saying that—and there are pages missing. Actual razored stubs in the binder. And a closing memo signed by a superintendent who retired six months later to a house in Spain that I have trouble squaring with his pension." Vale turned the chorister's badge a quarter-turn with one finger, not looking at it. "Thirty years ago somebody wanted that case shut, and somebody with rank agreed to shut it. And now the sea has reopened it, and I'm the poor devil holding the file."

Felix looked at the badge. Eleven years old. Head chorister. Someone had sewn that surplice hem, someone had pinned that badge to a small chest on some Sunday morning while parents beamed from the nave, and then the tide had taken him and kept him for thirty years within walking distance of the tower where he'd sung.

"Why are you telling me this?" Felix asked quietly. "This isn't archive business. This isn't even really your case yet, if forensics is weeks out."

"No," Vale agreed. "It isn't."

She let the word sit. Felix had learned that Vale deployed silence the way other officers deployed shouting, and that the correct response was to refuse to fill it. They waited. Somewhere overhead a door boomed; the noon volunteers arriving, footsteps crossing the transept, the ordinary machinery of the cathedral grinding on above a room where a dead boy's badge lay on a worktable between a policeman and an archivist.

"You wrote to us," Vale said at last. "Three times, over two years. Anonymous letters. The Hartley fall in the bell tower—your note pointed us to the maintenance log showing the scaffold inspection was falsified. The Verger Kempley business—you sent us the page from the visitors' book nobody else thought mattered. And Mrs. Ansel, the drowning upriver, you got us the pharmacy receipts." She recited them without notes, each detail placed like a coin on a counter. "Every time, the tip was right. Every time, it cracked a case we'd called accidental. And every time, the handwriting was disguised, the paper was ordinary, and the sender was never traced." She looked up. "I'm not here to arrest you, Mr. Reyes. If I were, I'd have brought someone bigger than me. I'm here because yesterday a dead boy came out of the mud a mile from your cathedral, and this morning I find you pulling the 1994 register before anyone's asked you to, and I want to know how much you already know."

"I didn't know the boy's name until thirty seconds ago."

"But you knew there was a boy."

Felix hesitated. This was the hinge, and they felt it flex under their hand. There was a version of this conversation in which Felix showed Vale the letter—the confession postmarked before the death, the impossible knowledge, the half-familiar hand. There was another version in which Felix said nothing, because the letter named no one but its writer, and its writer had addressed it to Felix alone, *to the Archivist, St. Aldhelm's*, in an envelope that had passed through the cathedral's own internal mail tray, meaning someone inside these walls had posted it, meaning the killer or the confessor or whoever stood behind either was close enough to touch the same brass letterplate Felix touched every morning.

And there was a third consideration, colder than both: if Felix handed the letter to the police, it became evidence, and evidence became public, and whatever careful invisible apparatus Felix had built over eleven years—the anonymous letters, the nudged investigations, the whole quiet practice of reading the dead through their paperwork—would be dragged into the light and examined by people who would not understand it and might well call it obstruction. Or worse, obsession.

"I knew the flats keep things," Felix said finally. "I knew Merrick's death didn't fit, and I'll say that to your face because you'll have worked it out yourself by Friday. Dry shoes, Sergeant. He didn't walk there." Vale's expression didn't change, but something behind it shifted, a curtain moving in a window. "And I knew that when a place like this loses someone, the loss gets curated. Filed. Tidied. I've spent eleven years learning how this institution tidies things. So when a letter arrived yesterday confessing to a murder committed by someone else's hand—" Felix stopped. Closed their mouth. Too far. Too fast.

Vale leaned forward slowly. "Say that again."

"A letter arrived," Felix said carefully, "that I am not ready to discuss."

"Withhold evidence in a suspicious death and I can make you ready."

"It's not evidence yet. It's a letter to me. And you haven't charged me with anything, Sergeant, because you can't, because I haven't broken a law you can name, and because—" Felix met her eyes, and let a little of the truth show through, deliberately, the way one shows a credential. "Because you don't want to arrest me. You want what I know. And what I know isn't finished knowing itself yet. Give me a week."

"A week." Vale sat back. Outside, faintly, the noon bells began, and the vibration came down through the stone into the crypt floor and up through the chair into Felix's spine, twelve strokes of bronze certainty falling on top of a room full of doubt. "You realize how this looks from my side of the table. An archivist who solves murders by post. A confession letter nobody's seen. A canon with dry shoes. And now a chorister out of the mud, thirty years late, ten minutes' walk from your office." She shook her head slowly. "In twenty-two years of policing I have never met anyone who attracts dead bodies the way you do, Mr. Reyes. Either you're the luckiest innocent I've ever encountered, or you're the best-hidden guilty man in Dorset, or—" she stood, took her hat off her knee, settled it on her head with two precise movements, "—or you're exactly what you appear to be, which frightens me considerably more."

"And what do I appear to be?"

"Someone the dead trust." She picked up the badge, weighed it in her palm, and set it back down on the table, deliberately, in front of Felix. "Evidence log says it stays with me. My conscience says a head chorister's badge belongs to the cathedral that gave it to him, and to whoever loved him, and I've got a week before anyone official asks where it went. Keep it somewhere safe. You're good at safe."

She was halfway up the stairs when Felix spoke, and the question left them before judgment could catch it:

"Sergeant. The boy. Thomas Wrenn. Did anyone ever confess? Back then. Anyone at all."

Vale stopped on the step, one hand on the cold stone wall, and did not turn around.

"Someone wrote to the superintendent in July of '94," she said. "A letter claiming to have seen something on the flats the night the boy disappeared. It's referenced in the file index. The letter itself is one of the pages that's missing." She resumed climbing. "Whoever wrote it knew enough to be dangerous. Whoever removed it knew enough to be frightened. In my experience those are rarely the same person, Mr. Reyes—and the space between them is where people like you and me get hurt."

Her boots faded up the stairwell. The door closed. The bells finished their twelfth stroke, and the silence came back down into the crypt like water filling a hole.

Felix sat for a long time with the badge in front of them, not touching it. Then, slowly, they drew the 1994 register toward them across the table—the choir attendance register, bound in blue buckram, its spine sun-faded to the color of shallow water—and opened it to the summer term.

The pages were there. April, May, the first week of June. Neat columns of names in the choirmaster's upright hand, absences noted in red, the whole living texture of thirty children's Tuesdays and Thursdays preserved in ink. Felix ran a finger down the first-of-June entries and found Thomas Wrenn present, punctual, marked with the small cross that denoted head chorister.

Felix turned the page to the second week of June.

The page was gone. Not torn—razored, cut close against the binding with a steady hand, leaving only a pale ghost of stub and, along its edge, the faintest offset shadow of ink where the excised page had lain pressed against its neighbor for thirty years. Felix turned to the next. Gone. The next. Gone. Six pages in all, the entire month of June 1994 lifted out of the record as cleanly as a tooth from a jaw.

Except.

At the bottom of the last remaining page, in the margin where the choirmaster sometimes penciled reminders—*cassocks to laundry, Dean's visit Friday*—someone had added a single line in a different hand, in a different ink, an iron-gall brown gone rusty with age, in writing that Felix's eye recognized the way the ear recognizes a footstep on a familiar stair.

*The water keeps what we give it.*

Felix sat motionless in the ringing silence, the dead boy's badge gleaming on the table, and understood that the tide had not merely given back a body.

It had given back a witness.

And that handwriting—the same half-familiar hand as the letter waiting upstairs in a drawer that needed a false bottom by nightfall—belonged to someone who had been here, in this building, in this month, thirty years ago, and had just begun, after three decades of silence, to speak.

Felix reached for the telephone list taped inside the cupboard door, and their finger stopped, almost without their permission, on a name they had circled in pencil three days ago and never explained to themselves: the elderly organist, Teddy Loxley, whose service record stretched back further than anyone's, and who had played the funeral music for Canon Merrick with hands that shook.

First, though—first, before anyone else in this tidying institution learned what the tide had done—Felix photographed the razored stubs, all six, and locked the register in the map cabinet, and pocketed the badge, feeling its small cold weight like a debt transferred.

The past wasn't surfacing. The past was *testifying*. And someone, somewhere above these stairs, was about to learn that the archive had started taking notes.
