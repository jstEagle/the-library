## 9. Thirty Years of Tides

The crypt at St. Aldhelm's kept its own weather. Felix Reyes had learned that in their first winter below ground, when the damp crept up the limestone walls in pale continents and the air took on the mineral smell of the sea, though the sea was a mile away and had been retreating from the town for two hundred years. Tonight the weather was cold and dry, the good kind, the kind that made paper lie still and cooperative on the reading table.

Felix spread out the tide tables like a hand of cards they intended to win with.

They were photocopies—Felix did not trust themselves with originals anymore, not after what had happened to the 1994 attendance register, its pages razored out with a steadiness that still haunted them—but they were faithful ones, pulled from the harbourmaster's office that afternoon under the pretext of researching "historical flood markers for the archive." Sergeant Vale would have seen through the pretext in a heartbeat. The harbourmaster's clerk, a young man with earbuds and no curiosity whatsoever, had not.

August 1994. The month Thomas Ellery—no, Felix corrected themself; the boy's name was being kept out of even their own thoughts now, reduced to initials, to *the choirboy*, as though anonymity were a kind of protection they could extend retroactively across thirty years—the month the choirboy went into the water.

The official account was a small, tidy document. It always amazed Felix how much tidiness could be packed into an official account, like a suitcase packed by someone who had done it many times. The boy had gone missing on the fourteenth of August, a Sunday, after morning service. He had last been seen near the tidal flats east of the harbour wall. The police conclusion, reached within seventy-two hours and never revisited: he had wandered onto the flats at low water, been cut off by the returning tide, and drowned. Body unrecovered. Case closed pending recovery, and recovery, for three decades, had politely declined to happen.

Until the erosion began this spring, and the flats started giving back what they'd been asked to keep.

Felix uncapped their pen and wrote at the top of a fresh sheet of paper, in their small vertical hand:

*If he drowned on the flats on 14 August 1994, the tide must permit it.*

It sounded obvious written down. Most truths did. That was the whole trick of documents, Felix had found—they made the obvious visible, and then made you complicit in it, because once you had seen it you could not unsee it, and seeing things was dangerous work in a town that preferred its pasts smooth.

* * *

The tide tables for August 1994 were printed in the old style, dense columns of figures, high water and low water given to the minute. Felix transcribed the relevant week onto graph paper, drawing the curve of each day's tides by hand, because there was something about drawing a curve by hand that made the body understand it before the mind did. High water on the fourteenth had come at 11:47 in the morning and again at 12:09 past midnight. Low water at 5:52 in the evening—a low tide of only 1.8 metres above chart datum, which meant the flats had drained far and wide that evening, exposing the full grey acreage of mud and channel.

So far, so consistent. A boy could indeed have walked out onto those flats after five o'clock and been caught by a tide that turned just before six and came back fast, as August springs do. The story held its shape.

But stories are shaped by what they leave out, and Felix had learned to read the margins.

They pulled the second set of papers across the table: the weather logs. These had been harder to get—the Met Office regional archive had required an email, a wait, and a small fee, but the archivist there, a fellow creature of the stacks, had thrown in the barometric readings without being asked, the way one archivist does favours for another across institutional lines. Felix laid the sheets beside the tide curves.

The fourteenth of August 1994 had been, according to every instrument in the region, a day of strong south-westerly winds gusting to forty knots, with rain beginning mid-afternoon and a barometer falling all day ahead of a front that arrived that night. The wind had been blowing *onshore*, straight up the estuary, piling water into the basin.

Felix sat back and looked at the two sets of numbers side by side, and felt the particular cold clarity come down over them like a lens focusing—the feeling they lived for and feared in equal measure, the moment when the documents stopped being documents and became testimony.

Because here was the thing about a falling barometer and a forty-knot onshore wind: they didn't just bring the tide in. They brought it in *early* and *high*. Every coastal archivist knew it; every harbourmaster knew it; every fisherman's widow in St. Aldhelm's knew it. Surge. On the fourteenth of August 1994, with that pressure drop and that wind, the evening high water would not have come at the predicted time. It would have come perhaps forty minutes early, and half a metre higher than the tables promised.

Which meant the safe window on the flats—that generous low-water window between 5:52 and whenever the returning sea made the channels treacherous—had not been generous at all. It had been narrow. Perhaps ninety minutes of genuinely passable ground, and then a tide that came back like something summoned.

And here was the second thing, the thing that made Felix's pen hover motionless above the paper: the boy had been seen, per the witness statements Felix had coaxed out of the diocesan file weeks ago, *near the flats* at around four o'clock. Four o'clock. An hour and fifty minutes before predicted low water. If he had gone onto the flats at four, with the tide still draining and the wind rising and the rain starting, he would have been walking into mud that was still, in the deeper channels, chest-deep slurry. A child does not stroll out onto that. A child gets stuck, panics, drowns in the mud itself—and is found, if he is found, close to where he stuck.

Or he never went onto the flats at four at all. Or the sighting was wrong. Or the sighting was *made*.

Felix wrote, slowly: *The official story requires him to go out late (after 6 pm) to drown in the returning tide. But the only witness places him there at 4 pm, when the flats were impassable. The story cannot be true in both versions.*

There was a third possibility, and it had been sitting in Felix's chest since the remains surfaced, cold and patient: that the boy had died somewhere else entirely, somewhere with no tide at all, and had been carried to the flats later, when the sea could be enlisted as an accomplice.

* * *

Felix worked until the crypt clock—Victorian, temperamental, ten minutes slow since 1987—read past midnight. Then they did the arithmetic that mattered.

If someone had wanted to move a body to the flats and let the tide take custody of it, when could they have done it? Not on the fourteenth. The fourteenth was wind, rain, darkness, a searching town, torches along the seawall, the coastguard out. No one carries a heavy burden across open mudflat with half the parish looking.

But the fifteenth—the fifteenth was the gift. Felix checked the tables twice, then a third time, because this was the load-bearing wall of the whole reconstruction and it had to hold. Low water on Monday the fifteenth had come at 6:41 in the morning. Dawn that August, with the front passed through and the sky scoured clean, had broken around 5:40. Which meant that between roughly six and seven-thirty that Monday morning, the flats lay exposed in flat early light, empty, the town still asleep off its Sunday, the search parties not yet reassembled, the mud firm enough after two days of drainage to bear a man's weight along the old cockle path.

A man carrying something heavy could have crossed to the deep channel at the far edge—the one the locals called the Gut, where the water pooled black even at low water—and laid his burden down in the silt, and been back over the seawall before the first searcher yawned. And the returning tide on the fifteenth, modest and ordinary, would have covered everything, and kept covering it, and kept its mouth shut for thirty years.

Felix looked at what they had drawn: two curves, two days, and a narrow bright corridor of possibility on the morning of the fifteenth. The church's story needed the boy on the flats on Sunday evening. The evidence—the impassable mud, the surge, the timing—said Sunday evening was impossible. The only version of events that fit the physics was a version in which the flats received him *after* he was already dead.

He was moved. The words settled in Felix with no triumph at all. This was the part no one warned you about, the part that made Felix keep their gift behind locked doors: that proof arrives cold, and stays cold, and you carry it in your chest like a stone you can't put down. Somewhere in this town, thirty years ago, someone had carried a boy's body across the cockle path in the grey light of a Monday morning, and the sea had agreed to help, and the church—the church whose registers Felix kept, whose stones Felix loved—had written the fiction into its minutes and its prayers and its tidy grief, and had called it an accident ever since.

Concealment, then. Not accident. And concealment on this scale—with razored register pages, with alibis polished like brass, with a canon who had watched something in 1994 and gone silent for three decades—was not the work of one frightened person. It was the work of an institution doing what institutions do best: curating.

Felix thought of Merrick's funeral, the coffin going down into the same earth that held everyone who had ever kept St. Aldhelm's secrets, and felt the shape of the next question arrive before they dared write it.

Because the reconstruction raised one final problem, and it was a problem with a date attached. If the boy was placed in the Gut on the morning of the fifteenth, then whoever placed him had needed to cross the flats—and the only dry way on and off that stretch, the way the cockle pickers used, ran through the tide gate at the seawall. And tide gates, Felix happened to know, because Felix knew every ledger this cathedral and its parish had ever produced, were logged. The gatekeeper's book for August 1994 existed. Felix had seen it indexed years ago and never had reason to care.

They had reason now.

Felix gathered the graphs, squared the edges, locked them in the iron cabinet with the key that hung against their sternum, and climbed the crypt stairs into the dark nave, where the moon through the east window laid a wash of coloured light across the floor like something spilled.

Tomorrow, the gatekeeper's log. Tomorrow, a list of names of everyone who crossed the flats on the morning the sea was asked to keep quiet.

And as Felix stood a moment in the empty nave, they became aware of a sound overhead—faint, rhythmic, unmistakable to anyone who had spent years listening to this building breathe: someone playing the organ, very softly, in the middle of the night. Not a hymn. Not anything Felix recognized. Just a few notes, repeated, like a man practising a confession he couldn't yet bring himself to speak.
