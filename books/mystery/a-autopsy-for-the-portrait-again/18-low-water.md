## 18. Low Water

The tide table said the sea would be at its lowest at 4:17 in the morning, and Felix Reyes had learned long ago that the sea kept its appointments better than any witness ever had.

They left the cathedral at three, taking the service door by the vestry because the main doors groaned on their hinges and the night porter slept with one ear tuned to that particular complaint. The town was dead in the way small towns are dead before dawn—not empty but held, like a breath. Streetlamps burned orange over wet cobbles. A gull argued with something unseen above the fish quay. Felix walked with their collar up and their hands in their pockets, and inside those pockets, curled against their knuckles, was a torch and the half-burned diary fragment wrapped in a plastic sleeve, because some things you do not leave behind even when you know them by heart.

*The water remembers.* Teddy Loxley had said it, weeping into his tea, and Merrick had written it, or something like it, in the scorched margin of his last entry: *the flats keep what we cannot carry*. Felix had spent two nights with tide tables and the almanac in the crypt's reference shelf, cross-checking dates the way they once cross-checked burial fees against parish registers—looking for the seam where the record and the truth diverged.

The seam was tonight. The spring tide of this week matched, almost to the hour, the spring tide of thirty years ago—the lowest water the coast would see until autumn. Whatever the flats had taken in 1994, whatever they had been made to hold, they would give it back now or not for months. And Felix could not wait for months. Not after the archive. Not after finding their notes gone from the locked drawer, replaced by a single sheet of paper bearing one sentence in a hand too careful to be natural: *You have read enough.*

Felix had read enough. That was precisely the problem. They knew what they were looking for—or believed they did—and knowing it meant there was no longer any version of the story in which they walked away clean. Merrick had tried to walk away clean for thirty years, and look what it had bought him: a lungful of cold seawater and a funeral attended by every person who might have saved him if any of them had asked the right question early enough.

Felix intended to ask the right questions. Out loud. In front of everyone. But first they needed the proof, and the proof, if it existed, was somewhere between the tide gate and the old oyster beds, under a meter of retreating water.

* * *

The flats at low water were another country.

Felix had seen them a hundred times from the sea wall—a brown-grey plain stitched with channels, smelling of salt and rot and ancient mud—but never like this, never from within, never with the water pulled back so far that the horizon seemed to have misplaced itself. The mud gave underfoot with a sound like slow applause, sucking at each boot, releasing it with reluctance. Channels ran through the plain like veins, brimming with a few inches of glassy water that reflected nothing but the bruised pre-dawn sky. Here and there the ribs of an old boat stood up out of the silt, black and skeletal, a whale that had died standing up.

The erosion scar was easy to find even in the dark; Sergeant Vale had described it precisely, in the flat professional voice she used when she didn't trust her own feelings. A slump in the clay bank near the second channel, where winter storms had peeled back decades of sediment like skin off a fruit. That was where the boy had come back—what remained of him, thirty years compressed into a few pounds of bone and a belt buckle the coroner's office still had not officially released to anyone.

Felix stood at the edge of the scar and let themselves feel it for exactly as long as they could afford, which was not long. A child had come here, in 1994, alive. A child had left here, in 1994, in a sack carried by someone who loved him or feared him or both. And the church had closed around the fact like a fist, and the sea had kept the receipt.

"Where," Felix said aloud, to no one, to the boy, "would he put the rest?"

Because that was the thought that had woken them at midnight and refused to let them sleep. Merrick had seen someone carry a heavy sack toward the flats. One sack. But Merrick's diary—the burned fragment, the pages Teddy must have torn out and tried to destroy and then, in the end, couldn't—had been incomplete in a way that felt deliberate rather than accidental. Merrick had been an archivist of his own guilt. He would not have trusted the whole confession to paper he kept in his rooms. He would have split it. Hidden half where only the tide could find it, timed to a tide he calculated years in advance, because Canon Edmund Merrick had spent his life among records and understood better than anyone that the safest vault is the one your enemy doesn't know exists.

And he had drowned within sight of these flats. Or been drowned. The distinction mattered less than Felix had once thought.

Felix switched on the torch and began to walk the line of the old channel, following logic rather than hope. If you were a man in a cassock carrying something you could not be found with, you would not lug it across open mud. You would use the tide gate—the same gate whose key log Colin Ashe had signed, hours before his body surfaced, in a hand steady as a printed line. You would come down the maintenance path, through the gate, and you would go only as far as you had to. Deep enough that casual eyes wouldn't find it. Shallow enough that you could reach it again—if you ever dared.

Which meant the thing Felix wanted was close. Close to the gate. Close to the clay.

The sky was going from bruise to ash in the east. Felix worked the edge of the channel with the torch beam, boots sinking, breath smoking, telling themselves that the cold in their spine was only the wind coming off the water. Twice they found things that stopped their heart and released it: a rusted fuel drum, a tangle of modern netting, a stone shaped almost like a skull. The flats were generous with false promises.

Then, forty meters from the tide gate, wedged in the root-plate of a fallen groyne post where the scouring current had dug a hollow, the torch caught leather.

Not modern leather. Not the grey, bloated hide of something recently lost. This was dark, cracked, furred with algae, the brass buckle green with thirty years of patience. A satchel. A clergyman's satchel—Felix knew the shape instantly, had catalogued three identical ones in the cathedral's stores, the kind issued to ordinands in the eighties, stiff-sided, strap across the top, embossed with a cross that time had nearly erased.

Felix knelt in the mud and did not touch it for a long moment. Their breath came fast and shallow. Somewhere behind them a curlew cried, one note, repeated, like a question nobody would answer.

*If I take this,* Felix thought, *there is no archivist anymore. There is only the person who took it.*

They took it.

The leather came free of the mud with a sound like a kiss. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth tied with waxed twine—Merrick had done this properly, had done it to last—was a bundle: a second diary, slimmer than the burned one, its cover swollen with damp but its pages, protected by the oilcloth, legible. And beneath it, folded into quarters, a single loose page in Merrick's upright, unhesitating hand.

Felix trained the torch on the page and read.

It was dated the fourteenth of September, 1994, and it was not a fragment. It was the whole of it—the entry Merrick had begun in the book that later burned, finished here, sealed here, weighted here, in the place where his silence had been buried alongside a child. Felix read it twice, three times, the words blurring and sharpening, and each reading made the world colder.

Merrick named the day. Named the storm. Named the boy—his real name, the name the register had been razored to erase. And then, in sentences that shook only at the very end, he named what he had seen from the vestry window at dusk: Daniel Ashe, fourteen years old, white-faced, hauling a sack bigger than himself down the maintenance path while his father stood watch at the tide gate. And he named what he had done about it, which was nothing, which was worse than nothing, which was thirty years of nothing wearing the costume of kindness—because the Asches were grieving in their own way, he wrote, because the boy's death had been an accident, a terrible childish accident, and what good would it do to feed a fourteen-year-old to the system? So he had watched them bury the evidence of a child's death in the grave of a child's disappearance, and he had kept the record, because someone had to keep the record, because God forgive him, he could not bring himself to be the one who spoke.

And then the last lines, added in different ink, dated three weeks ago:

*I told him today that I could no longer carry it. I told him I had written everything down and given it to the one person in this cathedral who reads the dead properly. He looked at me the way his father must have looked at me thirty years ago, and I understood that I had waited too long to be safe, and so had he.*

Felix lowered the page. Their hands were shaking, and the shaking had nothing to do with the cold.

Daniel Ashe. The facilities manager. The boy's closest friend, grown into the man who kept the cathedral's locks and keys and tide gates—who had inherited his father's vigilance along with his father's keys, and who had decided, three weeks ago, that Canon Merrick's conscience was a threat requiring permanent solution.

The curlew cried again. And then stopped.

Felix became aware of the silence the way you become aware of a hand on the back of your neck. The flats had been full of small sounds all morning—water ticking in the channels, mud sighing, birds arguing over the oyster beds—and now, in the space behind them, between the dunes and the sea wall, there was nothing. A hole in the world's noise, shaped exactly like a person standing still.

Felix did not turn around. Turning around was a confession of fear, and fear, right now, was a luxury itemized on someone else's invoice. Instead they wrapped the page back in its oilcloth, zipped the satchel shut, and slung it across their body, tight, the way you carry something you intend to defend.

"You can come down," Felix said, loud enough to carry across the mud. "The tide's coming back either way. We may as well both get wet."

Nothing moved. Then, at the crest of the dunes, silhouetted against the lightening sky, a figure detached itself from the marram grass and stood looking down at them. Too far to read a face. Near enough to make the distance feel like a formality.

Felix's heart slammed against their ribs, and they made themselves breathe slowly, the way they did when a ledger finally balanced. The figure began, unhurried, to descend the dune.

Felix checked the sky. Ash turning to pearl. The tide would turn within the hour, and the channels would fill first, cutting the flats into islands, and the path back to the tide gate ran directly past the place where the figure would meet them.

Felix tightened the strap across their chest, put the torch away, and started walking—not toward the gate, but along the channel's edge, choosing the soft ground, the ground they knew, buying minutes with mud.

Behind them, unhurried, footsteps followed.
