## 2. Salt and Wool

The phone rang at 6:14 in the morning, and Edmund Iversen knew before he answered it that the day had already gone wrong. He knew it the way he knew weather coming off the water—some pressure shift too subtle to name, registered in the body before the mind could argue.

He had slept badly, which was itself an anomaly. For eleven years he had kept a sleep so regular it bordered on liturgical: lights out at ten, awake at five-thirty, coffee black, the walk down Harbor Street while the gulls were still deciding whether to bother with the day. Grayhaven Bay rewarded routine. It was a town that had made its peace with repetition, that measured its life in tides and seasons and the slow erosion of the seawall, and Edmund had arranged himself to fit that rhythm the way a stone fits a wall—load-bearing, unnoticed.

"Dr. Iversen." Deputy Colfax's voice, too fast. "We've got a body at Miller's Tide Flats. Fisherwoman out early found her. The chief wants you."

"Of course." He was already reaching for his coat. "Preserve the scene. Nobody walks above the waterline until I've had a look at the sand."

"There's—" Colfax hesitated, and that hesitation was the second wrong thing. Colfax did not hesitate. Colfax was twenty-six and had the untroubled certainty of a man who had never yet seen anything the sea could do that genuinely surprised him. "There's something you're going to want to see yourself, Doctor."

Edmund drove the coast road with the windows cracked, salt air moving through the cab of his car like a slow tide of its own. The flats were fifteen minutes out, past the cannery ruins and the leaning fence posts that marked the old Miller property, and he used the time the way he used all time: cataloguing. A woman, Colfax had said. Found by a fisherwoman, which meant she'd been visible from the waterline, which meant she'd washed in on the night tide. The spring tides had been building all week toward the equinox. Whatever the sea took out, it was starting to give back.

He had said her name before he ever saw her. He didn't know her name yet, of course. But he said the words *Jane Doe, Miller's Tide Flats* aloud in the car, quietly, the way a priest might rehearse a rite, because that was what he did. That was the ritual. Every body that came to his table got its name spoken before the first incision—even the ones who arrived as John and Jane, even the ones whose names would never be more than a placeholder. *You are a person,* the ritual said. *You were a person, and I will not forget it between now and the moment I learn what the world called you.*

He had invented the ritual eleven years ago, in the worst month of his life, and he had never told anyone about it. That was the arrangement he'd made with himself: he could keep his gift, his terrible fluency in the language of the dead, so long as he kept it in the dark. So long as no one ever learned how much he saw, how much he understood, how many deaths he had quietly corrected while the official record slept on in its comfortable errors.

Three corrections, in all these years. A fall. A fire. A drowning. He kept the notes in a locked drawer beneath his desk, written in a shorthand of his own devising, and he had never once regretted the silence—no, that wasn't true, he regretted it constantly, the way a man regrets a stone in his shoe he has decided to stop mentioning—but he had never once acted on the regret. The corrections were his private penance and his private pride. Invisibility was the price and also the protection.

He parked behind the sheriff's cruiser and walked out onto the flats.

The morning was the kind of gray that made the world feel underwater even above the waterline. Low cloud, no wind, the tide far out and gleaming like poured pewter, the mudflats stitched with channels of standing water that held the sky upside down. Two deputies stood at the margin of the scene, and beyond them, half a dozen gulls stood in the shallows with the patient insolence of creditors.

The woman lay on her back at the waterline, one arm flung above her head, the other resting at her side, and the sea had arranged her with a kind of terrible courtesy—straightened her limbs, smoothed her hair back from her face, presented her.

She was perhaps forty. Brown hair darkened with water, skin gone the pale gray-blue of the flats themselves. And she was wearing—

Edmund stopped walking.

He did not decide to stop. His body simply declined to continue, the way a man's body declines a stair in the dark that isn't there.

The woman was wearing a gray cardigan. Lambswool, cable-knit, buttons of dark horn, the left cuff slightly frayed where he had worried it with his thumb for years without noticing he was doing it. Beneath the cardigan's open collar, wound once around her neck and hanging loose down her chest, sodden and heavy with seawater, a scarf. Charcoal wool with a single thin stripe of oxblood red at each end.

His cardigan. His scarf.

He knew them the way he knew his own hands. He had hung that cardigan on the second hook of his closet—second hook, because the first held the coat he wore to work—no more than thirty-six hours ago. The scarf lived in the basket on the closet's top shelf, folded, because he had never learned to fold a scarf without it looking like an apology.

"Doctor?" Colfax was beside him. "You okay? You went white."

"I'm fine." His voice came out level, and he marveled at it from a great distance, the way one marvels at a machine performing flawlessly in a room that is on fire. "Give me a moment before anyone comes closer."

He crouched at the margin of the scene, not touching, and let his eyes do what his eyes did.

The water had been kind to her, but not kind enough. He could see what the sea had tried to smooth: the asymmetry of the lividity, still faintly visible through the pallor, pooling wrong for a simple drowning. Petechial hemorrhaging in the eyes when he looked closely—no, he'd need the light, the magnifier, but he thought he saw it, the fine red stippling that spoke of pressure, of restriction, of a hand or a ligature or something pressed and held. And there, at the collar of the cardigan, where the wool had ridden up: a mark on the throat. Faint. Vertical. The kind of mark a body makes when something has been pressed against the front of the neck hard enough to bruise the skin against the hyoid beneath.

The cardigan's buttons were fastened wrong. The second button through the third hole. He had owned that cardigan eleven years and had never once, not in the fogs of flu or the half-sleep of a night call, buttoned it wrong. Whoever had dressed her had done it in the dark, or in haste, or with hands that shook—or with hands that didn't care, because the buttons were never the point.

The point was the cardigan. The point was the scarf. The point was that anyone who saw this body would see Edmund Iversen's clothes on a drowned woman, and would understand, without a single word being spoken, exactly what was being said to them.

"Colfax." He kept his voice at the register he used for difficult families—low, unhurried, warm at the edges. "Has anyone searched the area above the waterline? The dunes, the grass margin?"

"Two of the boys are walking it now. Why?"

"Because drowned women don't dress themselves, and whoever put her in the water may have left something behind." He rose. His knees ached. He was fifty-four years old and his knees ached and the world had tilted three degrees off its axis and no one else could feel it. "I'll need her clothing bagged separately at the morgue. Every garment. And I want photographs of the buttons before anything's removed. The buttons, Colfax. Before anything."

"The buttons," Colfax repeated, and wrote it down, because Edmund Iversen's odd requests had a way of turning out to matter, and everyone in the Grayhaven sheriff's office knew it even if none of them had ever asked why.

* * *

They found the key at 7:40.

Edmund was standing at the edge of the dune grass, reading the sand—reading the way the grass was bent, reading the shallow depression where something heavy had rested, reading the absence of footprints that itself was a kind of print, because the tide had come in over the dunes' seaward face and smoothed everything, which meant the body had been placed after the tide turned, which meant the killer had known the tide table, had *used* the tide table, the way Edmund himself would have—when one of the deputies called out from the line of beach rye above the flats.

"Doctor! You'll want to see this."

The key lay in the grass, half-buried, glinting dully. A brass key on a plain ring. Edmund looked down at it and felt the morning, which had been tilting, now come apart entirely.

He knew the key. He had cut it himself at the hardware store on Delancey Street nine years ago, after the winter his pipes froze and he'd been locked out of his own house for six hours in a wet coat. It was the spare. It lived in the third drawer of his desk at the morgue, wrapped in a chamois cloth, because Edmund Iversen did not hide keys under doormats or in flowerpots like an amateur in a television drama. The third drawer. The chamois. He could see it there now in his mind's eye with photographic completeness, and the drawer in his memory was empty.

"Bag it," he said. "Don't touch it. Bag it, log it, and I want to know whose prints are on it."

"Whose prints do you think are on it?" The deputy said it lightly, the way men say things they have already half-decided are jokes, and Edmund heard the future in the question—heard the trial, the testimony, the prosecutor's finger, the jury turning in their seats like a single slow animal.

"Unknown," he said. "That's what evidence is for."

But his own hand, he noticed, was not entirely steady, and he put it in his coat pocket where no one could see it, and he stood over the key in the grass and thought: *Someone has been inside my life.*

Not inside his house. That was the smaller violation, the one he could almost bear. Inside the *record* of him. Someone knew where he kept a spare key that no living person was supposed to know about. Someone had opened his closet, taken the second-hook cardigan and the basket-scarf, and understood—not guessed, *understood*—that those objects were a signature. Someone had studied him with the same patience he had studied the dead, and had learned to read him the way he read bodies: by the things that never make it into any official account.

He thought of the three corrections. The fall. The fire. The drowning. Eleven years of notes in a locked drawer, written in his private shorthand, seen by no one. And he thought, with a cold that had nothing to do with the wind coming off the water: *What if I am wrong about that? What if someone has seen them? What if someone has been reading me for years, the way I read the dead, and I never once felt the touch?*

"Doctor Iversen?" Colfax again, at his shoulder, notebook out. "The chief's asking—do you know this woman? Recognize the clothing?"

*Do you know this woman.* As though the question were innocent. As though the cardigan were a coincidence, the key a coincidence, the whole morning a run of bad luck rather than a sentence being constructed, word by word, out of the fabric of his own life.

"No," Edmund said. "I don't know her."

It was true. It was also, he understood with terrible clarity, the last easy question anyone was ever going to ask him.

* * *

The body came to his table at 9:15, because there was no one else in Grayhaven Bay to receive it, and because refusing would have been the loudest confession he could possibly make. He performed the external examination with his hands steady and his mind moving at the speed of panic beneath the calm like a riptide under smooth water.

Female, approximately forty years of age. Well-nourished. No identification. The ligature mark on the anterior neck—vertical, narrow, consistent with a firm manual pressure or a soft cord, not a rope. Petechiae in the conjunctivae. Fluid in the lungs, yes, but not the pattern of a living drowning; there was too little of the struggle, too much of the stillness. She had been unconscious, or nearly, when the water took her. Drowned, perhaps, in the technical sense. But killed first. Killed by hands, and then given to the sea like a letter being mailed.

He spoke her name into the quiet of the morgue, as he always did. "Jane Doe," he said. "You are a person. I will not forget you."

And then, because the ritual demanded honesty and he had never lied to the dead, he added, barely above a whisper: "And I think someone means for me to join you. Or to hang for you. I don't yet know which."

The cardigan and the scarf lay in their evidence bags on the counter beside him, and he looked at them for a long time. His own wool, his own weave, a thread pulled loose at the left cuff that he had never gotten around to mending. He thought about the closet. He thought about the third drawer and the chamois cloth. He thought about the four—no. He stopped himself. He would not begin counting the people who might know things, because counting was the first step toward suspicion, and suspicion was a fire that, once lit in a house like his, burned everything including the walls.

The phone rang at 11:02. The sheriff's office. A witness had come forward, the sheriff said—a woman, an artist, who had been sketching the flats that morning and had seen something. Seen *someone.* A figure in the dunes before dawn, watching the water. And the description she'd given—

The sheriff paused, and Edmund recognized the pause. It was the same pause Colfax had used at 6:14 in the morning. It was the sound of a town beginning to look at him.

"She said the man was wearing a long dark coat," the sheriff said. "Doctor—like the one you've got on right now."

Edmund looked down at his coat. The coat he wore every day of his life. The coat that hung on the first hook of his closet, beside the empty second hook where the cardigan should have been.

"I'll come in," he said, "and give my statement," and he heard his own voice do the thing it did so well—stay level, stay warm at the edges, stay the voice of a man with nothing to hide—and for the first time in eleven years, Edmund Iversen understood that the invisibility he had built his whole life inside of was not shelter at all.

It had been a blindfold. And someone had just removed it.
