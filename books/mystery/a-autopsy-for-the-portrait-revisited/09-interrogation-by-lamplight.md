## 9. Interrogation by Lamplight

Adaeze Okafor had conducted her first interrogation in a room that smelled of burnt coffee and fear, and she had learned something in that room she had never unlearned: nobody lied well under a good lamp. Not because the light revealed them—lies were not written on faces, whatever the television said—but because a good lamp made the silence loud. It gave the silence edges. People filled it. And in the filling, they told you everything.

The Grayhaven Bay police station had no good lamps. It had fluorescent panels that buzzed at a frequency just below conscious annoyance, the kind of institutional lighting that flattened every face into the same tired gray. Okafor had brought her own. It sat now on the table between her and Edmund Iversen—a brass banker's lamp, secondhand, with a green glass shade and a filament that threw warm amber light in a tight circle, leaving the rest of the room to the fluorescents. She had bought it three years ago in Providence, at an estate sale, from a widow who told her it had belonged to a probate lawyer. *He said the truth liked to sit in the circle,* the widow had said. Okafor hadn't believed it then. She was starting to.

Edmund Iversen sat in the amber circle like a man who had been seated for a portrait he hadn't agreed to sit for. His hands were folded on the table—she noted the fold itself, deliberate, the posture of a man managing his own body—and his face was the face of every coroner she'd ever met: patient, unremarkable, a face built for waiting rooms. Grayhaven had a population of eleven thousand and a coroner who looked like a substitute teacher. She had read his file on the drive up from Portland. Forty-six years old. Never married. Twenty-two years in the post. No disciplinary actions, no commendations, no complaints. A man who had made himself so smooth a part of the town's machinery that the machinery had forgotten he was a man at all.

And yet.

She opened the folder in front of her and did not look at him yet. Let the paper speak first. Paper was honest in a way faces weren't, and she had learned to let it do the opening moves.

"Dr. Iversen," she said. "Coroner. Twenty-two years."

"Yes." His voice was quiet, level, a voice calibrated to neither invite nor repel.

"Before that, residency in Portland, pathology. Before that, medical school at Dartmouth. Good record. Unremarkable." She looked up. "That word keeps coming up in your file. Unremarkable."

"I've never been accused of flamboyance."

"No," she agreed. "You haven't." She turned a page. "On the fourth of March, Ruth Calloway was found at Miller's Tide Flats. Drowned. Your preliminary finding was consistent with drowning—no dispute there. Time of death estimated between eleven p.m. and two a.m. based on water temperature and lividity. You signed the report at six-forty the following evening."

"That's correct."

"Here's what I'm having trouble with." She laid a photograph on the table, inside the lamp's circle, and turned it to face him. The gray cardigan. The scarf. "The clothing. Your cardigan, your scarf—confirmed by your own statement, and the weave and wear patterns are consistent with items from your wardrobe. Found on a drowned woman. And this." She set down a small evidence bag. The spare house key caught the lamplight. "Your key. Found on the flats, forty feet from the body, half-buried in sand. Which means, Dr. Iversen, that between the time this woman went into the water and the time you signed your report, you had—what? Thirty-six hours? And in that time, someone dressed a corpse in your clothes, planted your key, and left you holding a report with your own signature on it."

"I'm aware of how it looks."

"I don't think you are." She leaned back. "Because if you were aware of how it looks, you'd have recused yourself from the autopsy. You didn't. You performed it yourself. On a body dressed in your own clothing. And then you wrote a report so clean, so thorough, so *unremarkable*, that it reads like a man trying very hard to be exactly what everyone expects him to be."

The silence filled the room. She let it. The lamp hummed faintly, a sound like a held breath.

"I performed the autopsy," Iversen said at last, "because it's my jurisdiction and my duty, and because recusing myself would have looked worse."

"Would it?"

"Wouldn't it?"

Okafor almost smiled. He was better than she'd expected. Not good—nobody was good—but better. He answered questions with questions when he needed a beat. He kept his hands still. He gave her nothing to read in his cadence, which meant he had thought about cadence, which meant he had thought about being read, which was in itself a kind of confession.

She turned another page. "Let's talk about your files."

Something happened then. It was small—she almost missed it, and she was watching for it. His folded hands tightened. Not a flinch. A *settlement*, like a house settling on its foundation, like something shifting a quarter-inch into a position it intended to hold.

"What files?" he said.

"The ones in your basement." She said it flatly, without triumph, because triumph was a tool and this wasn't the moment for it. "I have a warrant. I executed it this morning at seven-fifteen while you were at the station. Four banker's boxes. Handwritten case notes going back to your first year in Grayhaven. Photographs. Correspondence. And—" she paused, and this pause she allowed herself to enjoy, just slightly— "three folders marked with colored tabs. Blue, red, green."

The lamp hummed. Somewhere down the hall, a phone rang twice and stopped.

"Those are my working notes," Iversen said. "Every coroner keeps notes."

"Every coroner keeps *notes*." She pulled a photocopy from the folder and set it in the circle. "This is your note on Daniel Marsh, 2011. Found dead at the base of the stairwell at the Cannery Row apartments. Ruled accidental by the responding officers. Your note says—and I'm quoting—*'Fracture pattern of the right radius inconsistent with a fall backward down stairs. Defensive posture. Someone was holding his wrist.'* And then, below that, in the same handwriting: *'Spoke with the sister. She knows who. She won't say. Not enough to reopen. File it away.'*" Okafor looked at him. "File it away. You found evidence of a homicide, Dr. Iversen. You found a witness. And you *filed it away*."

His jaw moved once, a small motion, like a man swallowing a stone. "I had a fracture pattern and a scared woman. That's not a case. That's a rumor with a bone attached."

"You could have pushed."

"On what? My word against the responding officers' report? I'm the coroner. I'm not the police. If I'd pushed and lost, I'd have lost my access, my credibility, my ability to ever—" He stopped.

"Your ability to ever what?"

The silence came back, and this time it had weight, and she watched him carry it. In the amber light his face was still composed, but the composition had changed; it was no longer a portrait now but a held position, a plank bridge over deep water.

"Ability to keep looking," he said finally.

"Looking at what?"

"At deaths."

Okafor set down the photocopy and took her time with the next one. She had spent the previous night with his basement boxes, alone in the evidence room with the boxes and the bad coffee and the growing, cold understanding that she was not looking at the files of a coroner but at the private archive of a man who had been conducting a one-person war against mislabeled death for two decades. Fall. Fire. Drowning. Three cases with colored tabs, three cases where his notes went on for pages, three cases where his conclusions diverged from the official record and then—nothing. No report filed. No correction requested. Just the knowledge, folded away like letters never sent.

"The red tab," she said. "Elena Voss, 2014. House fire on Kestrel Lane. Ruled accidental—faulty wiring. Your notes run to eleven pages. You documented pour patterns inconsistent with an electrical origin. You documented that the smoke detector had been disabled, its battery removed, and you noted—quote—*'Removed cleanly, recently. Not deterioration. Intent.'* And then your notes end with a single line: *'Widow saw something. Too frightened. Left my card. She never called.'*" Okafor looked up. "Did she ever call, Dr. Iversen?"

"I wouldn't know. That's a police matter."

"You kept her name in your private files for ten years."

"I keep everyone's name."

"Do you?" She turned the page. "Then let's do the green tab, and you can tell me about keeping names. Thomas Iversen. 1998."

This time the silence had teeth. She heard him breathe—once, slowly, the breath of a man putting something down carefully before picking something else up.

"My father," Iversen said.

"Your father. Found in his boat off the north jetty. Drowned. Ruled accidental—rogue wave, according to the harbor report. Your notes—" she held up the photocopy, but she didn't read from it; she had read it enough times to know it by heart, and she wanted him to know she knew it by heart— "your notes say the water in his lungs was inconsistent with the wave pattern that night. That his hands were unmarked when a man who fought a rogue wave for his life would have torn them on the gunwales. That your father was a strong swimmer. And then the last line: *'He was afraid of someone. I was seventeen. I didn't know who. I still don't.'*" She set the page down. "You've been carrying your father's death in a green folder for twenty-seven years."

The lamp hummed. Down the hall, the fluorescents buzzed their one flat note, and Okafor thought: this is the moment. Not the moment he breaks—she didn't expect him to break, and if he broke she'd be suspicious—but the moment the cost came above the waterline. Every interrogation had it. The moment the secret stopped being a strategy and became a weight, and the weight showed on the face like weather.

"I keep meticulous files," Iversen said, "because the dead don't get to correct their own records. Someone has to. That's all this is."

"That's not all this is."

"Detective—"

"Here's what I think." She closed the folder and folded her hands, mirroring his, and she saw him notice the mirroring and something flicker behind his eyes—not anger, not fear. Recognition. The look of a man meeting his own methods turned back on him. "I think you've spent twenty-seven years solving murders in secret. I think you've corrected the record in your own private archive because you didn't trust the public one, and you didn't trust it because the public record failed you first, when you were seventeen, when your father drowned and nobody asked the questions you now know to ask. I think Ruth Calloway is wearing your cardigan because someone has read these boxes—these exact boxes—or knows them intimately, and is staging deaths the way *you* would stage them, the way *you* see them, and is doing it to make the town look at you. And I think the reason you performed the autopsy yourself instead of recusing yourself is that you needed to see the body before anyone else did, because you recognized something in it." She leaned forward into the lamplight. "Am I wrong?"

Edmund Iversen looked at her for a long moment. In the amber circle his eyes were the color of wet slate, and behind the composure she saw it—the thing she'd been hunting since Portland, the thing that had made her take a case three hours up a gray coast in the off-season. Not guilt. Not innocence. *Recognition.* He was looking at her the way a man looks at a door he'd hoped would stay closed.

"You're a very good detective," he said.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer I have tonight."

"It's not." She stood, gathered the folder, and let the pause stretch until the fluorescents seemed to shout. Then, at the door, she stopped. "One more thing, Dr. Iversen. The blue tab. The drowning case. Nina Aldous, 2019, ruled accidental, your notes say homicide, and your notes end with a question you never answered: *'Why the tide flats? Why there?'*" She turned in the doorway. "Ruth Calloway washed up at Miller's Tide Flats. The same flats. Whoever is doing this didn't just read your files. They're answering your questions. They're finishing your sentences." She let that sit in the humming air between them. "Sleep on that. And Dr. Iversen—when the next one comes, and it will come, because a man who stages a tableau doesn't stop at the first panel—call me before you call anyone else. Not because I trust you." She switched off the banker's lamp, and the amber circle died, and his face went gray with all the rest of the light. "Because whoever this is knows you better than anyone alive. And I'd very much like to meet the only person who understands you."

She was at the elevator, coat on, when the desk phone rang behind her and the night dispatcher's voice carried down the corridor, thin and wrong, the way bad news always sounds before you understand it:

"*—second body, out past the flats—Okafor? You're going to want to see what he's wearing.*"
