## 18. Bait

The press room at the Grayhaven Bay municipal building had been a storage closet until three days ago. Edmund knew this because he had once signed for a delivery of body bags that was temporarily shelved there, back when the town's dead were fewer and the shelves were emptier. Now it held a podium, a folding table with a water pitcher no one would drink from, and eleven chairs arranged in rows for a press corps that consisted of the *Grayhaven Courier*'s editor, two stringers from Portland, and a woman who livestreamed city council meetings under the name TidelineTrudy.

Okafor stood at the window of the adjoining office, watching the rain stitch silver lines down the glass. She had dressed for the occasion in a charcoal suit that made her look like what she was: an instrument of the state, precise and unsentimental. But her hands, folded behind her back, betrayed a tension Edmund had learned to read the way he read bodies—the slight whitening at the knuckles, the thumb pressing against the index finger as if testing its own pulse.

"Say it again," she said, without turning.

"The office of the coroner, in consultation with the district attorney, has determined that the 2011 death of Iris Renner warrants formal reopening." Edmund recited it flatly, the way he read names aloud before autopsy—stripped of feeling, stripped of anything a listener could use against him. "New evidence has come to light regarding the circumstances of the drowning."

"Slower on 'new evidence.' Let them chew on it."

"You want him to hear it."

Okafor turned then. In the fluorescent light her face was unreadable in the ordinary way, but Edmund had spent three weeks learning her particular grammar, and he saw what others wouldn't: the fatigue pooled beneath her eyes like standing water, the set of her jaw that meant she'd already argued this with herself and lost.

"I want everyone to hear it," she said. "That's what makes it bait instead of a trap. A trap is something you spring on one man. Bait is something you lay out in the open and trust the world to do your work for you." She crossed to the table where a folder sat, manila, unremarkable, its tab labeled in Okafor's blocky hand: RENNER, I. — REOPENING. Inside was nothing. Edmund knew because he'd watched her put nothing into it. The folder was a promise shaped like paper.

"Silas Renner spent thirty years as a courthouse clerk," she went on. "He knows how announcements work. He'll know that a reopening means subpoenas, means interviews, means someone will eventually come knocking on his door asking where he was on the equinox tide four years ago. If he's going to finish whatever he's building out at Miller's Flats, he has to finish it before the machinery starts turning. We're not giving him a deadline. We're making him feel one."

"And if he doesn't bite?"

"Then we've publicly reopened a case that deserves reopening, and I've done my job badly in a way that costs me nothing but pride." Her mouth twitched—not quite a smile. "You should try doing your job badly sometime, Dr. Iversen. It's very freeing."

The joke landed somewhere below his ribs and stayed there, aching. Because that was the thing about the announcement they were staging: it wasn't only bait for Silas Renner. It was bait for Edmund too. The moment he stood at that podium and said the words *new evidence*, he would be testifying to the existence of evidence that existed nowhere but in his private notebooks—in the marginalia of a career spent correcting the record in secret. Okafor knew it. She had chosen, in her uneasy partnership, not to say it aloud. That silence between them was either mercy or leverage, and Edmund had stopped trying to determine which.

He thought of the second victim, Hollis Brandt, laid out on his table wearing his father's watch—the watch that had never been catalogued, never photographed, never mentioned anywhere except in a conversation Edmund had once had with himself over an open drawer. *They're performing your biography back at you.* And now he would stand in front of cameras and perform a piece of his own biography back at the man who had studied it so lovingly. Two readers, trading pages.

"When," he said.

"Tomorrow, ten a.m. Low tide is at 4:17. Equinox tide comes in fast out at the flats—Renner will know that better than anyone. If he moves, he moves in that window." Okafor picked up the empty folder and held it a moment, weighing nothing. "Get some sleep, Doctor. Tomorrow you become visible."

* * *

Mara Voss arrived at the seawall at dusk, when the harbor lights came on one by one like a sentence being spelled out slowly. Edmund was already there—he had been there an hour, watching the water practice its old patience against the pilings—and he heard her footsteps on the wet boards before he saw her, and something in his chest turned over with an ease that frightened him more than any of it did. Three weeks ago he had not known her. Now her absence had weight; her presence rearranged the air.

She stopped beside him, close enough that their shoulders nearly touched, and looked out at the black water. She was carrying her sketchbook, rolled and tied, the way she always carried it, and he understood without being told that she had brought it as armor—that if the conversation became unbearable she could unroll it and hide inside the drawings.

"Okafor called me," she said.

"I asked her not to. I'm sorry."

"Don't apologize. Just tell me why." She turned to face him, and in the sodium light her eyes were the color of the harbor at low tide, all the color drained out of them and something harder left behind. "She wants me at the press conference. Standing behind you. Visible. She says—" Mara's voice caught, steadied. "She says if Silas sees me standing with you, he'll understand that his script isn't working. That I'm not his witness anymore. That I chose."

"That's what she says."

"And what do you say?"

Edmund looked at the water. He had rehearsed this conversation a dozen times in the last two days, and every rehearsal ended the same way: with him telling her to stay home, lock her door, let him carry the risk alone, because that was the shape his whole life had taken—carrying things alone, quietly, invisibly. But the rehearsals had all felt like lies. So he said the true thing instead.

"I say it's the most dangerous place in Grayhaven Bay tomorrow morning, and I have spent three weeks being unable to protect you from a man who studies people the way I study bodies, and I am tired of pretending that keeping you away from me keeps you safe. It didn't work before. You found the body. You drew his face without knowing it. You were in this before I ever learned your name." He turned to her. "So yes. Stand with me. Not because it's good strategy—though Okafor's right, it is—but because I'm done letting other people write you into their scenes."

Mara was quiet for a long moment. Out on the water, a trawler's running lights moved toward the bar, small and steady.

"Do you know what I keep thinking about?" she said finally. "The morning I found her. Before I understood what I was looking at. I was sketching the flats and the light was doing that thing it does out there, turning everything to pewter, and I remember thinking it was the most honest landscape I'd ever drawn. Nothing hiding. Everything the tide took, the tide showed you." Her hand found his on the railing. Her fingers were cold from the walk and ink-stained at the cuticles. "And then I turned around and there was a body in your cardigan, and I learned that honesty is just a word people use for the moment before they understand what they're seeing."

"Mara—"

"Let me finish. I've been afraid since that morning. Afraid of him, afraid of the police, afraid of you a little, if I'm honest—afraid of what my testimony could do to a man whose hands I've now held." She lifted their joined hands slightly, evidence. "But I'm done being afraid in the passive tense. If I stand behind you tomorrow, I'm not bait. I'm a witness who decided which side of the record she's on. There's a difference, and I need you to see it, because if you look at me tomorrow like I'm something you're sacrificing, I won't be able to hold still."

"I don't look at you like that."

"No," she agreed softly. "You look at me like I'm a name you're saying aloud. Like I matter enough to be spoken." She squeezed his hand once and let go, and the loss of her warmth was immediate and physical. "Ten o'clock. I'll be there."

She walked back along the seawall toward the stairs, and halfway there she stopped, unrolled the sketchbook against the railing, tore out a single page, and left it weighted under a stone. Edmund waited until she'd gone before he retrieved it.

It was the flats at dawn, pewter light, everything honest. And in the foreground, small but unmistakable, two figures standing close together on the waterline—one holding a camera, one holding nothing at all, both facing the sea as if it owed them an answer.

* * *

The press conference lasted nine minutes. Edmund spoke for six of them.

He had expected the room to feel like a stage; instead it felt like an autopsy theater, all those faces angled down at him with professional hunger, waiting for him to open something up. Okafor stood at his left. Mara stood at his right, half a step behind, her hands folded, her chin level, and the presence of her was a live wire running through the whole performance—every reporter clocked her within seconds, and he heard the whisper move down the row like wind through grass: *that's the witness, that's the witness from the flats.*

He read the statement Okafor had drafted and he had amended, and when he reached the line about new evidence he heard his own voice do the thing it always did with names—slow down, give each word its full weight, refuse to let it pass unspoken. *The death of Iris Renner, initially ruled accidental drowning, will be formally reopened effective today.*

In the third row, TidelineTrudy raised her phone. Somewhere near the back, a door opened and closed—softly, apologetically, the sound of someone leaving who did not want to be remembered leaving. Edmund noted it the way he noted everything now: a detail filed against the hour of its occurrence. The door had opened at 10:04. The tide would turn at 4:17.

Questions came. He answered none of them well, by design—*the nature of the evidence is pending, the investigation is active, I can't speak to persons of interest*—and Okafor stepped in with the practiced flatness of a woman who had ended a hundred press conferences by saying less than she began with. Then it was over, and the room emptied, and the three of them stood in the sudden quiet among abandoned chairs.

"He was here," Edmund said.

Okafor didn't ask how he knew. She had stopped asking, weeks ago, how he knew things. "Back row?"

"Back row. Left side. He left at 10:04, right after I said her name." Edmund's mouth was dry. "He wanted to hear it said aloud. That was the point, wasn't it? Not the reopening—the saying. He's spent four years needing someone to say her name in public, and I just did it, and now the clock is running."

Okafor was already moving, pulling her phone, signaling the two plainclothes officers stationed outside. "Units to Miller's Flats, non-emergency approach, I don't want sirens spooking him. Get me eyes on the dunes road and the parking pull-off." She looked back at Edmund, and for one unguarded instant the institutional calm dropped and he saw underneath it: a woman about to bet a witness, a coroner, and her own career on the behavior of a seventy-year-old man with a grievance the size of a daughter. "If he goes early, we have six hours of daylight to get ahead of him. Go home, Doctor. Charge your phone. Stay reachable."

"And Mara?"

"Stays with the officer I'm assigning to her, in the apartment she does not leave until dark." Okafor's eyes flicked to Mara, and something passed between the two women—Edmund couldn't parse it, some negotiation conducted entirely in posture. "You did well in there. Both of you."

They walked out together into a morning that had cleared hard and bright, the kind of coastal light that made everything look exposed, every surface confessing its details. Mara's escort was waiting at the curb, a young officer with an earnest face. She paused with her hand on the car door and looked back at Edmund across the plaza, and she didn't smile and she didn't wave; she just looked at him the way she'd looked at the flats that first morning, honestly, taking his measure, committing him to memory the way she committed everything to memory before she drew it.

Then the door shut, and the car pulled away, and Edmund stood alone in the bright square with his phone heavy in his pocket and six hours stretching ahead of him like a held breath.

At 11:52, his phone rang. Okafor's number.

He answered before the second ring. "Tell me."

Her voice was tight, controlled, and underneath the control something moving very fast. "The officer we assigned to Ms. Voss just radioed in. When they got to her building, her door was open." A pause, the length of a heartbeat, the length of a tide turning. "There's no sign of struggle, Doctor. There's a package on the table, unwrapped. And pinned to it—" 

The line crackled with wind; Okafor had started walking, fast.

"—pinned to it is a page torn from a sketchbook. One of hers. And written across it, in handwriting I've now seen on three separate notes—"

"Okafor." His voice came out strange to him, level and cold, the voice he used over the dead. "What's written on it."

"*The tableau needs a witness,*" she read. "*Bring the file. Come alone. You know where.*"

Edmund closed his eyes. Behind them, unbidden, rose the image Mara had drawn and left for him under a stone on the seawall: two figures on the waterline, facing the sea, waiting for it to answer.

The equinox tide would turn at 4:17.
