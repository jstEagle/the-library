## 1. The Twin Letters

The crypt of St. Brannoc's smelled of cold stone and older things — candle tallow gone rancid in its sockets, the mineral breath of water that had been seeping through the same hairline crack since before anyone now living had been born. Yara Okonkwo knelt on the flagstones with a clipboard balanced against her knee, her torch propped between two stacked crates, and catalogued the dead.

Not people. Not anymore. The sealed inventory of the crypt was a ledger of objects: vestments rolled in brown paper and tied with string gone brittle as biscuit, processional banners folded into coffers that had once held communion silver, boxes of hymnals with their spines furred grey by damp. Thirty years she had worked among them, first as an assistant to old Archivist Penhale, then, after his heart gave out in the north transept, as keeper of the archive herself. She knew this room the way other people knew their own kitchens. She knew which flagstone rocked underfoot and which gave back a hollow note when struck, though she had never told anyone about the hollow one, not even Penhale, who had died without telling her what lay beneath it.

Outside, above the vaulted ceiling, the cathedral carried on its Tuesday: a school group clattering through the nave, the organist running scales that dripped down through the stone like slow rain. Down here there was only her breathing, the scratch of her pen, and the sea — always the sea, a low percussion through the walls, for St. Brannoc's stood close enough to the harbour that the tide kept time beneath it like a second heart.

She was writing *coffer 14, six albs, moth damage to hems* when the letter arrived.

It did not arrive so much as announce itself. She heard footsteps on the crypt stair — the wrong rhythm, too quick, too light — and then Mrs. Tregenna from the parish office leaned into the torchlight, breathless, holding two envelopes as if they were something that might bite.

"Post came early," she said. "There's one for you. Well. Two." She squinted at them. "Same handwriting, I'd say."

Yara set down her pen. "For me? People don't write to me."

"They wrote to you." Mrs. Tregenna laid them on the crate lid as though setting down eggs. "Both postmarked yesterday. Both local, by the look of the franking. Odd, isn't it — two letters, same day, same hand." She lingered, hungry for gossip, but Yara only smiled the small closed smile that had kept colleagues at a polite distance for three decades, and Mrs. Tregenna retreated up the stairs, her footsteps fading into the organ's scales.

Yara looked at the envelopes for a long moment before touching them.

Both addressed to *Miss Y. Okonkwo, Archivist, St. Brannoc's Cathedral*. No street address needed; the town was small enough that the cathedral was its own postcode. The handwriting was careful, upright, a hand trained in some older school — every letter formed with a draughtsman's patience. The ink was black. The paper was good stock, heavy, cream-coloured, nothing like the flimsy aerogramme weight of ordinary post.

She turned them over. Sealed with plain adhesive, no wax, no monogram. Nothing to trace. Of course nothing to trace. She had spent years learning how evidence behaved, how it hid itself, how it waited. Whoever had written these letters understood evidence too.

She opened the lighter one first. It felt right, somehow — the way you lift the smaller stone before the larger.

Inside, a single sheet, typed. Not handwritten after all; the envelope alone bore the pen. The type was clean, from a machine or a printer, unremarkable:

*Miss Okonkwo —*

*You keep accounts. So do I.*

*The tide will give up what it took. Be at the sea wall when it does, or the next entry in your ledger will be written in someone else's blood.*

*— A friend of unfinished business*

Yara read it twice. Then a third time, slowly, the way she read anything that mattered, letting each sentence settle like sediment.

*You keep accounts.*

No one knew about the ledger.

The thought arrived not as surprise but as a kind of cold recognition, a door opening onto a room she had always known existed. For eleven years — since the night she had sat up with Penhale's files and noticed that the verger who fell from the bell tower had climbed it, according to his own diary, with a fear of heights so severe he'd once taken the outside steps of his own cottage on his knees — she had kept a private record. A green cloth-bound notebook, bought from the stationer's on Fore Street, unremarkable, forgettable. In it she wrote the cases the coroner had closed and she could not: the fall, the fire, the drowning, the quiet overdose of the woman who had been about to give evidence in a land dispute. Fourteen entries now. Fourteen accidents that were not accidents, each one annotated with what she had found and what she believed and what she could never prove. She kept it locked in the bottom drawer of her desk, beneath the false bottom she had built herself from a drawer liner and four brass screws, and she had told no living soul. Not because she feared ridicule — she had long ago made peace with being the archivist who asked uncomfortable questions — but because the ledger was hers, the one thing in her life that belonged entirely to her, the shape her grief and her stubbornness had made together.

And someone had just told her they knew it existed.

She set the first letter down and picked up the second. Her hands were steady. That surprised her a little; she would have expected them to shake. But steadiness was what the work had taught her. You did not tremble over documents. You read them.

This envelope was heavier. Inside was another typed sheet — and folded within it, a photograph.

The photograph was old, its colours shifted toward that particular bruised amber of prints from the nineties. It showed the choir of St. Brannoc's lined up on the cathedral steps, robed in white surplices, perhaps thirty boys arranged by height. Summer light. Someone's thumb had blunted the corner. And in the front row, third from the left, a small boy stood slightly apart from the others, his head tilted, his smile crooked, as if he had been laughing at something a half-second before the shutter clicked and had not quite finished.

On the reverse, in that same patient draughtsman's hand, someone had written a date: *July 1994*. And beneath it, in different ink, darker, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper:

*I drowned him.*

Yara sat down on the crate. The torch beam wavered as her knee brushed it, and for a moment the shadows swung across the crypt walls like something turning over in its sleep.

Elias Wren.

She had not heard the name spoken aloud in years, but she knew it the way she knew all the names in her ledger — not as a case number but as a boy. She had been twenty-six in 1994, newly arrived, already haunting the archive, and she remembered the summer the choir lost one of its own. The drowning at Porthmellin Cove. Ruled accidental: a strong ebb tide, a boy who swam beyond his depth. There had been a service, candles, a plaque that had later been quietly removed during the reordering of the north aisle — she had catalogued it herself, wrapped in newspaper, boxed away with other things the parish preferred not to look at. She remembered Dean Crane's sermon at the memorial, his voice breaking beautifully at exactly the right moment, and she remembered thinking, even then, even at twenty-six, that the grief had been too well performed, like a soloist hitting a high note he had rehearsed.

But she had never investigated Elias Wren. He was before the ledger. Before the verger in the tower. His file, if it still existed, lived in the county records office, closed and gathering dust, and she had walked past that dust for thirty years because there had always been other deaths, nearer ones, ones with loose threads she could actually pull.

Now the thread had been pulled for her. Delivered to her hands in a cream envelope, postmarked yesterday, by someone who knew her name, her office, her habits — and her secret.

She looked again at the photograph. Third from the left. The crooked smile. Somewhere under the mud of the harbour, under thirty years of tide, was whatever remained of that boy, and somewhere in this building — she felt it with the certainty of long practice, the archivist's instinct for what a collection is hiding — was the reason he had died.

The letters had been posted the same day. Same hand on both envelopes, but the messages inside were different animals. One confessed. One threatened. Or — she turned the idea over, examining it from all sides, the way she examined a disputed document — perhaps neither was quite what it pretended. A confession that named no method, no place, no witness. A threat that promised proof rather than punishment. *The tide will give up what it took.* As if the writer wanted her to find something. As if the writer were not warning her off but summoning her in.

Her eyes went around the crypt, the crates, the coffers, the dark mouths of the storage niches where the parish stored what it could not bear to throw away. Thirty years of donations and discards and deliberate forgetting, all of it passing through her hands, all of it catalogued in her neat archival script — except the things no one had ever told her were there.

Above her, faintly, through three feet of stone, the organ finished its scales and began, softly, a hymn she almost recognised. Something about light descending. The congregation singing to a God who presumably kept the truest ledger of all.

Yara gathered the letters, the photograph, and slid them into the flat leather folder she carried everywhere, the one that looked like it held conservation reports. Then she took out her keys, crossed to the far wall, and unlocked the shallow cabinet where she kept the records of the 1994 reordering. If a plaque had been removed, a file would exist. If a file existed, a name would be in it. Names led to places; places led to dates; dates led, eventually, to the truth, which was the only thing she had ever worshipped.

Before she opened the cabinet, she paused. The crypt was silent except for the sea. And yet she felt it — the sensation she had learned to trust more than any document — of being watched. Not by ghosts. By someone patient, someone who had chosen her deliberately, who had read her secret ledger page by page and decided she was the instrument the law had failed to provide.

*I drowned him,* the ink had said, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper.

She opened the cabinet. Inside, behind the folders of faculty notices, a slim manila file marked in faded pencil: *WREN, E. — memorial, removal of.*

She drew it out. The paper inside was thin and yellowed, and at the top of the first sheet, in the registrar's tidy hand, was a line that stopped her breath:

*Approved for removal at the request of the Dean. See attached correspondence — donor of replacement portrait wishes to remain anonymous.*

Attached correspondence. Three pages. And on the last one, a signature she would spend the rest of this story trying to forget.

Yara stood very still in the cold, listening to the tide knock softly beneath the floor, and understood, with the calm certainty that had carried her through fourteen impossible cases, that she had not received two letters at all.

She had received a summons.
