## 2. What the Tide Took

The archive room smelled of cold stone and old vellum, and Yara Okonkwo had always found it comforting — until now. Now the two letters lay on her desk like a pair of opened oysters, their shells cracked, their contents already working their way into her blood.

She locked them in the bottom drawer of the filing cabinet, beneath the false bottom she'd installed herself three years ago, and turned instead to the ledger. Not the real one — the real one never left the flat on Marlowe Street — but her working notes, the photocopies and clippings and half-formed theories that would eventually be transcribed in her small, slanted hand. She pulled the folder she'd begun the night before, after the letters arrived, after she'd sat in the dark of the archive until her tea went cold and the cathedral bells rang two o'clock.

*Elias Wren. Drowned. August 1994.*

The parish records were thin. That was the first thing she noticed, and the first thing that bothered her. St. Brannoc's kept meticulous accounts — every candle purchased, every roof tile replaced, every christening and funeral and choir rehearsal logged in a fair hand — but the year of Elias Wren's death had gaps. Not erasures, exactly. Nothing so crude. Just a softness, a blurring, as though someone had run a damp thumb across the ink of that summer until the details bled into one another.

The burial register told her what she needed first: Elias Wren, aged twelve, interred 14 August 1994, in the north-east corner of the churchyard, where the ground was poorest and the yew trees threw their deepest shade. No plot number. No next of kin listed beyond a mother's name — *Sylvie Wren* — in handwriting that grew unsteady halfway through, as if the registrar's own hand had begun to shake.

Yara went out into the churchyard that afternoon, when the light had gone the colour of weak tea and the gulls were wheeling and crying over the harbour wall. The north-east corner was exactly as she'd expected: neglected, half-swallowed by bramble, the stones there leaning like tired dancers. She found his marker on her third pass — a small slate slab, the kind the parish had stopped using decades ago, cheaper than granite, softer, quicker to wear. The inscription was nearly gone. She had to kneel and brush away a season's worth of leaf-mould with her fingers to read it.

*ELIAS WREN. 1982–1994. A LITTLE WHILE, AND THE STORM IS PAST.*

She stayed kneeling for a long time. The ground was damp and it soaked through the knees of her trousers, and she found she didn't care.

There was a particular grief, she thought, in a stone that was already forgetting. Thirty years, and the sea air had done what the parish's silence had begun: smoothed him away, softened his name until it was barely a rumour in the rock. The boys who had sung beside him were men in their forties now, with mortgages and receding hairlines and children of their own, and she would have wagered anything that not one of them had stood here since the funeral. The parish had not merely forgotten Elias Wren. It had *tidied* him. Filed him under accident, under tragedy, under acts of God, and closed the book with a relieved little sigh.

She knew that motion. She had seen it before, in the other cases in her ledger — the fall from the belfry stairs in 2009, the fire at the rectory in 2013, the hit-and-run the police had never quite managed to trace. Death, in a parish, was an inconvenience to be managed. And the managers were always the same: the people with keys, with authority, with the trust of the congregation like a coat of armour that no one ever thought to look beneath.

Yara stood, brushed the leaf-mould from her knees, and made a promise to the small worn stone that she would not have made aloud to any living soul.

*I'll find out what happened to you.*

* * *

Tom Pellowe lived in a bungalow on the far side of the harbour, in a street where every house had a view of the water and every garden had a wind-bent shrub doing its best against the salt gales. He answered the door before she'd finished knocking, which told her he'd seen her coming — a woman in a dark coat, walking with purpose, asking at the corner shop for the old verger's address. In a parish this size, that was all it took.

"Miss Okonkwo." He said her name the way people said the names of weather systems. "The archivist. I heard you'd been asking after the old records."

"I have." She smiled, and kept the smile small, unthreatening, the smile of a woman interested in candle inventories. "May I come in, Mr. Pellowe? It's about the Wren boy."

Something moved behind his eyes — not guilt, she noted, filing it carefully, not exactly. Something older and more complicated than guilt. Recognition, perhaps. The look of a man who has been waiting thirty years for a knock on the door and is almost relieved when it finally comes.

"Aye," he said. "I supposed someone would, eventually. Come in, then. Mind the cat."

The bungalow was warm and close and smelled of toast and coal smoke. The cat, an enormous grey tom, regarded her from the windowsill with the flat indifference of his kind. Tom Pellowe settled into an armchair by the window with the careful movements of a man whose joints had opinions, and looked at her with an expression she could not immediately read — patience, or its performance.

"Ask," he said. "You've come to ask, so ask."

"Tell me about the night Elias died."

He didn't flinch. That was the first surprise. She had expected a flinch, a deflection, the small evasions of a man with something to hide. Instead he folded his hands in his lap and looked out at the water, and spoke in the level voice of a man reciting something he had recited many times before.

"It was the second week of August. Hot, that summer — the hottest I can remember, before or since. The tide was out far that night, further than usual. Spring tide, they call it. The boy was last seen going down toward the sea wall around nine o'clock, after evensong. His body came up two days later, past the point, caught in the kelp."

"You were the verger that year."

"I was. Twenty-six years a verger, before my hips gave out." He said it without pride, as though stating a fact about the building. "I locked up the cathedral that night. Ten o'clock, as always. I remember it because the clock in the vestry had been running fast all that week and I'd been meaning to have it seen to."

"You remember the time precisely."

"I remember *everything* precisely." He turned and looked at her then, and his eyes were pale and steady as the horizon. "That's what you're really asking, isn't it, Miss Okonkwo. You're asking why my memory's so tidy. Everyone else from that night remembers it in pieces — a hot evening, a boy gone missing, a search with torches along the wall. But me, I remember it like a photograph. Ten o'clock, lock-up, the clock running fast. Ten-fifteen, home. Ten-twenty, my wife will tell you, though she's dead now and can't tell you anything."

"Why is that, Mr. Pellowe?"

He was quiet for a long moment. The cat jumped down from the sill, stretched, and left the room with the deliberate dignity of a creature declining to be present for what followed.

"Because I was told to remember it," he said finally. "Because three days after the inquest, the Dean called me into his study — Aldous Crane, God rest him, though I'm not sure God did — and he said to me, *Tom, there's going to be questions, and when there are, it'll be a comfort to have the times straight.* And he gave me the times. Like a man giving change. Ten o'clock, ten-fifteen, ten-twenty. And I took them, because he was the Dean, and because I was a coward, and because I told myself that a boy who went swimming at night on a spring tide was nobody's fault but his own."

Yara kept her breathing even. Her heart was not cooperating.

"And do you believe that? Now?"

"I believe a boy who was afraid of the water," Tom Pellowe said, "does not go swimming at night alone."

The room seemed to tilt very slightly. She heard the sea, faint and constant, beyond the window. She thought of the small slate stone under the yews, and the inscription wearing away like a breath on glass.

"Afraid of the water," she repeated.

"Elias Wren couldn't swim. I taught half the choir to swim, down at the harbour pool, in the summers. He'd sit on the steps with his feet in the water and laugh at us, and he'd never come in past his knees. Never. Not once in two years." Pellowe's hands had tightened in his lap, knuckles pale. "And when he was found, he was found with his shoes on. Who swims in his shoes, Miss Okonkwo?"

"Did you tell the inquest this?"

"I told them he was a careful boy. They wrote down that he was a lively boy. There's a difference, and no one wanted to hear it." He looked at her again, and now she could read him fully: thirty years of a weight carried in silence, and the terrible relief of finally setting it down, even into the hands of a stranger. "The parish decided what happened that summer, Miss Okonkwo. The parish decided, and the rest of us agreed to it, because agreeing was easier. That's how a thing like that works. It's not a lie anyone tells. It's a lie everyone accepts."

She wrote nothing down. She didn't need to; the words had already gone into the permanent record of her, the one no one could confiscate.

"Mr. Pellowe," she said, standing, "the letters I received—"

"I know nothing about letters." But he said it quickly, too quickly, and something in the set of his shoulders told her that he knew *exactly* what letters she meant, and that the knowledge frightened him.

She let it go. She thanked him for the tea he hadn't offered, and let herself out, and the cat watched her from the hallway with its flat green eyes as though it, too, had decided what had happened in August of 1994 and saw no reason to reopen the case.

* * *

That night she walked out along the sea wall, because she could not help herself, because the letters had promised her the tide and she wanted to stand where the promise would be kept. The spring ebb was three weeks away yet — she'd checked the almanac, twice, with the particular obsessiveness she brought to dates — but the water was already pulling back, exposing the black ribs of the mudflats, the tangle of kelp and old rope and things the sea had held and grown tired of.

Somewhere out there, thirty years ago, a boy who could not swim had gone into the water with his shoes on.

She thought about the confession letter, its careful, educated hand. *I drowned him.* Two words that had sat in her drawer all day like a coal, still warm. And the second letter, the threat, the promise: *the tide will give up what it took.* As though the writer knew exactly what the tide had been given, and where, and when. As though the tide were not the murderer but the accomplice — the great indifferent keeper of secrets, holding its evidence in the silt for three decades, waiting for someone to ask the right question at the right hour of the right moon.

The wind came off the water and it smelled of salt and weed and old, old things. Below the wall, in the blue hour, the mudflats gleamed like hammered pewter, and she found herself scanning them — for what? A rusted tin. A pair of small shoes. The proof the letters had promised, lying in the open air at last, patient as a stone under a yew tree.

She turned back toward the cathedral, its tower black against the last of the light, and the thought arrived with the certainty of a bell striking: the tide had already given up its secret, once. It had given up the body, two days after Elias went into the water. And the parish had taken that body, and buried it in the poor ground, and written *lively boy* where *careful boy* should have been, and closed the book.

But the tide kept other things. The tide kept everything, eventually. Shoes. Photographs. Truths.

And somewhere in that cathedral — she was sure of it now, sure the way she was sure of the tide tables — somewhere in the stone and the glass and the gilded dark, the parish had kept a secret of its own. Something Elias had owned. Something someone had gone to great and deliberate trouble to preserve.

She climbed the steps to the north door, and her hand paused on the iron ring, and she looked back once at the water, silver-grey and endless, giving nothing up tonight.

Three weeks to the spring ebb. She had three weeks to learn what the tide had been holding.

Behind her, in the dark of the archive, the ledger waited for its next entry.
