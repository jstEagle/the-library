## 3. A Portrait Overpainted

The conservation room at St. Brannoc's occupied the north transept's upper gallery, a space the cathedral had surrendered to dust and daylight sometime in the 1970s and never asked to have back. Light came through a roundels window in discs of amber and sea-green, moving across the floor with the slow patience of something that had all the time in the world. Yara Okonkwo had always liked the room for that quality — the sense that here, at least, nothing was in a hurry. This morning, she resented it. Her hands wanted to hurry.

She had carried the letters with her for two days now, moving them between drawer and satchel like contraband, and last night she had finally done what the second letter demanded, in its cold, typewritten courtesy: *Begin where he hangs.* She had taken the crypt inventory ledger from its locked drawer, run her finger down the list of donated artworks, and found what she already half-knew she would find.

*Item 41. Portrait in oils, Aldous Crane, Dean of St. Brannoc's 1981–1996. Artist: the sitter. Donated to the cathedral by the sitter, 1996, on the occasion of his retirement. Condition: excellent.*

He had painted himself. And then he had given himself to the church, to hang in the corridor outside the chapter house, where generations of choristers had filed past him twice daily on their way to evensong. Yara had walked past that painting for eleven years. She had straightened its frame once, after a flood season left it hanging a degree askew, and she could still remember the tacky give of the varnish under her thumb, like skin.

*Begin where he hangs.*

She had taken the portrait down at six this morning, before the cleaners arrived, carrying it up the spiral stair with the awkward reverence of a woman carrying a coffin lid. It was larger than she remembered — Crane at three-quarter length, seated in his dean's chair, one hand resting on a closed book, his face turned toward the light with an expression of mild, reasonable authority. The face of a man who had never once been contradicted. The congregation had loved him. The town had loved him. In the photograph that hung in the vestry — Crane blessing the new sea wall in 1992 — he looked like a man carved from the same stone as his cathedral.

Yara set the portrait on the trestle easel, squared it against the light, and stood back.

"Right," she said aloud, to the empty room, because the room was empty and because saying it aloud made it a procedure rather than a fear. "Let's have a look at you."

* * *

She had learned X-ray fluorescence the way she learned most things — badly at first, then obsessively, then well enough to be dangerous. The diocese, in one of its periodic fits of stewardship, had purchased a portable XRF unit four years ago to survey the gilding on the rood screen, and the unit now lived in a padded case beneath the conservation room's sink, consulted roughly twice a year and otherwise keeping company with the cleaning fluids. Yara had borrowed it twice before: once to confirm that a memorial brass was Victorian forgery, once to settle a bet with herself about the age of a reliquary. Both times the machine had told her the truth, in its flat, element-by-element way, and both times the truth had been small.

She did not think this truth would be small.

The unit hummed as she ran the wand across the canvas, its readout painting numbers into the air between her and the painting. Lead white throughout — expected, ubiquitous, the skeleton of every oil painting of that era. Vermilion in the face and hands. Prussian blue in the cassock's shadows. She worked methodically, top to bottom, left to right, cataloguing, and for the first twenty minutes the portrait was exactly what it appeared to be: a competent, unremarkable likeness by a painter who had studied seriously but painted rarely.

Then the wand crossed the region below the seated figure's left knee, where the dean's chair leg dissolved into a shadowed floor, and the readout changed.

The mercury spike was wrong. Too much vermilion for a floor shadow. And beneath it — she moved the wand back, slower, breathing through her mouth the way she did when the ledger's pages got too close to something living — beneath the lead white of the overlying paint, the X-ray fluorescence was reading a *different* composition. A different layer. Zinc white, where the surface showed lead. A distribution of dense pigment that didn't belong to chair legs or floors.

Yara set down the wand. Her hands were not entirely steady, and she watched them not being steady with a kind of clinical detachment, as if they belonged to someone else — some other archivist, in some other cathedral, discovering some other buried thing.

The confession letter lay in her satchel. She had read it perhaps forty times in two days, and there was a passage she had told herself was metaphor, because the alternative was unbearable. She took it out now, unfolded it along its worn creases, and read it standing, in the moving discs of colored light.

*You will know the place when you find it, because I told you what hangs there. The dean gave the church his own face. But faces are the easiest things to paint over. Ask the canvas what it was before it was him.*

*Ask the canvas.*

She looked up at Aldous Crane — at his mild, reasonable, beloved face — and felt the floor of the room tilt very slightly, the way the stone floor of the crypt sometimes seemed to tilt if you stood in it too long.

* * *

The radiography took the rest of the morning and most of her nerve. The cathedral's old X-ray unit — a hospital surplus machine, donated a decade ago and used, so far as Yara knew, exactly once, to examine a cracked rood — still worked, if you coaxed it and did not ask it to be quick. She dragged it to the gallery, rigged the lead aprons over the windows, and ran the first plate at half past ten, standing behind the protective screen with her heart going like a chorister's foot on the processional stair.

The first exposure was a failure. The second was underexposed. The third —

The third came up in the developer bath like something surfacing.

She had expected an underdrawing. Pentimenti, perhaps — the honest ghosts of a painter's revisions, a hand moved, a chair redrawn. What rose out of the chemical bath instead was a figure.

A boy. Smaller than the dean by half, positioned where the shadowed floor had been, as if the painter had first composed a picture of two people — a seated man and a standing child — and then had decided, or been made to decide, or had needed, desperately, to unmake it. The boy's face was the clearest thing in the radiograph: an oval, a fringe of hair, features rendered with a delicacy the rest of the buried figure had been denied. The painter had not sketched the face. He had *finished* it. Finished it, and then covered it — covered it with the same care a man uses to close a door on a sleeping child.

Yara stood over the developing tray for a long time. The fixer's vinegar smell filled the room. Outside, somewhere below, the Tuesday cleaning crew moved through the nave with their vacuum cleaner's distant hymn, and the cathedral went on being the cathedral, vast and indifferent and full of light.

The boy's face was not a study. It was not a practice piece. It was not anonymous.

She had spent eleven years among the cathedral's photographs — the parish albums, the choir commissions, the faded glossies of Sunday schools and harvest festivals — and she knew the faces of St. Brannoc's children the way a lighthouse keeper knows the faces of the sea. She knew this one. The set of the jaw. The cowlick that no comb had ever fully defeated. The slight asymmetry of the eyes that made him look, in every photograph, as though he were about to ask a question he wasn't sure he was allowed to ask.

Elias Wren. Choirboy, aged eleven. Drowned in the ebb below the sea wall on the fourteenth of September, 1994. Ruled accidental. Buried in the churchyard's north corner, where the light doesn't reach until afternoon.

The parish had chosen to forget him, and the parish had been helped to forget him, and now here he was — thirty years drowned, thirty years painted over — looking up out of the developer bath with his question still unasked.

Yara lifted the radiograph with both hands, let it drain, and clipped it to the line above the sink. Then she took out her private ledger — the one no one knew existed, the one the second letter had quoted by page number — and turned to a fresh page, and her pen hovered there for a long moment before she wrote:

*Item 41 is not a portrait of Dean Crane. It is a portrait of Elias Wren, wearing Crane as a shroud.*

She wrote the date. She wrote the exposure numbers. She wrote, in her smallest, most controlled hand, the sentence that made it real: *The confessor described this canvas before I found it. He has seen beneath the paint. Which means either he painted it, or he watched it being painted.*

She sat back. The colored light had moved; a disc of sea-green now lay across the portrait's surface, across Crane's painted knee, across the shadowed floor where a boy stood invisible, thirty years deep.

* * *

She was rehanging the portrait at dusk — she could not leave it down; its absence would be noticed, and she was not yet ready to be noticed — when the question arrived that she had been holding at arm's length all day.

The letter-writer knew. Not suspected — *knew*. Knew the canvas, knew the overpainting, knew the exact language of the confession's instruction. Knew her ledger by page number. The letters were not the work of a guilty conscience unburdening itself after three decades; guilt does not audit. The letters were the work of someone managing her — feeding her the thread and watching to see whether she would pull it, patient as a fisherman at the ebb.

Which meant the letters were bait. Which meant whoever wrote them wanted the portrait opened the way she had opened it — wanted Elias Wren dragged up out of the lead white and the zinc and the thirty years of varnish — and had chosen her, specifically, the archivist with the private ledger, to do the dragging.

She tightened the last screw of the hanging plate and stepped back. In the corridor's failing light, Aldous Crane looked down the passage toward the chapter house, mild and reasonable and beloved, and beneath him, invisible, a boy stood waiting with his unfinished question.

Yara pressed her palm flat against the wall beside the frame — against the plaster, the stone, the whole accumulated weight of the building — and made herself a promise in the language of her ledger: she would find out who painted the boy, and who painted him over, and she would write their name in the record if it was the last entry she ever made.

Behind her, in the darkened gallery above, the radiograph hung drying on its line — the boy's face lifted clear of the paint at last, pale and patient as a drowned thing risen to the surface, waiting for the tide of morning light to show him to the room.

And in her satchel, folded against the confession, the second letter's final line seemed, for the first time, less like a threat than an instruction she had finally learned to read:

*The tide will give up what it took. So will the canvas. So, Mrs. Okonkwo, will you.*
