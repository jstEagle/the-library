## 6. Thirty Years of Silence

The name came to her the way names always did in St. Brannoc's — sideways, half-heard, carried on someone else's grief.

Yara had spent two evenings with the parish burial registers and the diocesan payroll ledgers, cross-referencing the staff of 1994 against the rosters she'd photographed from the archive. The verger, Pellowe. The sacristan, dead these fifteen years. The dean himself. And one more: *M. Voss, organist*, whose salary payments stopped in October 1994, mid-quarter, without explanation. No resignation letter. No forwarding address. In a cathedral that recorded everything — every candle purchased, every bolt of cloth — Miriam Voss had simply been erased from the ledger like a smudged note.

People who vanish from records don't usually vanish by accident.

It took Yara three days and two favors owed to find her. A retired music teacher in Penzance remembered "the Voss woman" — brilliant hands, terrible nerves, left suddenly for a nursing order in Somerset. From there it was a hospice in Wells, and a phone call Yara made from the phone box outside the bakery rather than her office, because she no longer trusted her office walls.

"She receives visitors," the sister on duty said, "but not many. Tuesdays are best. She likes to sit by the window in the afternoon. She says the light there is like the light over the nave."

*She still measures light by the cathedral,* Yara thought, and something in her chest went tight and cold.

She took the train on Tuesday. She told herself it was routine — an interview, another thread pulled loose from the woven alibi of that August night thirty years ago. But as the Somerset hills rolled past, wet and green under a low sky, she admitted what she was really doing. She was going to meet the only other person alive who had kept Elias Wren's death folded away inside them, all this time, the way Yara kept her ledger: close, secret, and heavy.

* * *

The hospice smelled of lavender and disinfectant, the two scents fighting a long stale war in the corridor. Rain ticked against the windows. Yara signed the visitors' book — her real name, though she hesitated over it; lately her name felt like something she should stop leaving lying around — and followed a nurse down a corridor of closed doors to a room at the end where the window was wide open despite the weather.

Miriam Voss was smaller than Yara had imagined, folded into a wingback chair under three blankets, her hands resting on the armrests like birds that had forgotten how to fly. They were still beautiful hands. Yara could see it even now — the long fingers, the precise knuckles, the hands of someone who had once coaxed fugues out of a pipe organ while boys' voices climbed above her.

"You're the archivist," Miriam said, before Yara had finished crossing the room. Her voice was dry as old paper but steady. "From St. Brannoc's. I wondered when you'd come."

Yara stopped. "I haven't written ahead."

"No." Miriam smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of someone who had been waiting a long time for something they dreaded. "But you've been asking questions. Tom Pellowe telephoned me after you visited him. And before him—" she paused, and her eyes, pale and watery, fixed on Yara with terrible attention, "—before him there was a letter."

The rain filled the silence.

"A letter," Yara said carefully.

"Three years ago now. Or four. The seasons blur in here." Miriam lifted one hand, an effortful thing, and gestured to the drawer of the small table beside her chair. "You may read it. I've kept it for exactly this reason, I think. Though I didn't know what the reason was until you walked through that door."

Yara opened the drawer. Inside lay a single envelope, yellowed at the edges, addressed in a hand she knew — she had stared at that hand for hours, magnified under her desk lamp, tracing the peculiar hooked *f* and the way the writer crossed their *t*s like small wounds. The same hand that had written *the tide will give up what it took*. The same hand, she was now almost certain, that knew the page numbers of her private ledger.

She drew out the letter with gloved fingers — habit, now, more than caution — and read.

*Mrs. Voss. You were paid once for your silence. I am not asking for money back. I am asking whether thirty years is enough for a woman's conscience to wake. If it is, write to the archivist at St. Brannoc's. She keeps accounts. She will know what yours adds up to.*

No signature. But at the bottom, in smaller script, a single line:

*Elias played the harmonium better than you ever let him admit.*

Yara looked up. Miriam was watching her face, reading it the way she must once have read a conductor's baton.

"So," the old woman said softly. "You're the archivist who keeps accounts."

* * *

They sat a long while before either spoke again. The nurse brought tea and Miriam waved it away; Yara held her own cup without drinking, letting the warmth bleed into her palms. Outside the window, rain moved across the levels in grey curtains, and somewhere below a thrush was singing anyway, stubbornly, into the wet.

"I saw them," Miriam said at last. It came out flat, rehearsed, the way things come out after decades of being said silently. "That night. The twenty-third of August, 1994. I'd stayed late — there was a recital coming, and the pedal board on the organ was misbehaving, sticking on the low C. I was in the loft until nearly ten. The church was empty. I thought it was empty."

Her hands tightened on the blanket.

"I heard the vestry door. Voices. One was the Dean's — you know a voice after thirty years in a building, you know it the way you know your own pulse. Crane. And the other—" she stopped, and her throat worked, and Yara understood that the next word had been waiting inside her for thirty years like a stone swallowed whole.

"Elias," Yara said quietly, giving it to her.

Miriam nodded. Once. "Elias. He sounded frightened. Not crying — frightened the way animals are frightened, quick and quiet. They went down toward the crypt stairs. I should have gone down. I have gone down that stairway in my mind ten thousand times, and every time I make myself go down, and every time I arrive too late or too much of a coward, and I don't know which it is anymore." She turned her face to the window. "Instead I locked the organ loft and I went home. And two days later the boy was in the water under the sea wall, and the Dean stood at his funeral and called it God's inscrutable will, and I sat in my loft above him with my hands in my lap."

"And then he paid you."

"He came to my cottage on the Thursday. Brought a bottle of sherry and an envelope. Three hundred pounds — it was a fortune then, or it felt like one. He said the church couldn't afford scandal, that the boy's mother had suffered enough, that my imagination would only wound her further if I started seeing things that weren't there." Miriam's mouth twisted. "He was very good. That was the horror of Aldous Crane — he was good. He believed his own kindnesses. He handed me that envelope with tears standing in his eyes, and some part of me wanted to be comforted by him. I took the money. I gave notice the same week. I never touched an organ again — not properly. My hands shook too badly. Isn't that a joke? He silenced me so thoroughly I silenced myself."

Yara set down her untouched tea. "Why tell me now?"

"Because I'm dying, dear, and the dead have nothing left to protect." Miriam laughed, a small dry sound. "And because of the letter. Because someone finally asked. Thirty years I waited for a policeman to ask, and none ever did — they'd already written *accidental drowning* in their tidy book and closed it. Then a stranger writes to me and asks about my conscience, and tells me where to bring it." Her eyes came back to Yara's. "You keep a ledger. He said so. Is it true?"

The question hung between them. Yara thought of the notebook hidden behind the false spine of the 1911 inventory, thirty years of accidents that weren't, each entry dated, each one closed except the ones that couldn't be. She had never spoken of it aloud. To speak of it felt like opening a vein.

"It's true," she said.

Miriam exhaled — relief, or grief, or both. "Good. Then write this down. Write that Miriam Voss, organist, saw Dean Aldous Crane take Elias Wren by the arm toward the crypt stair at approximately ten o'clock on the night of the twenty-third of August, 1994, and did nothing. Write both halves. The sin and the silence. If you're going to keep accounts, keep mine honestly."

"I will," Yara said. "I promise."

"One more thing." Miriam leaned forward, and for a moment the dying fell away and Yara saw the woman who had ruled a choir loft, sharp-eyed and formidable. "That letter-writer. He knows things. Things only someone inside would know — he mentioned the harmonium, he mentioned the low C, which I never told a living soul. Whoever he is, he was near that church that night, or he's talked to everyone who was. And he wrote to me *before* he wrote to you, which means you weren't his first choice. You were his second. Ask yourself why. Ask yourself what he wanted first, and why he settled for you."

The thrush outside had gone quiet. The rain thickened against the glass.

"There's something else," Miriam said, and now her voice dropped, and her hands began, very faintly, to tremble — the tremor she'd blamed on the organ all these years. "When Crane gave me the money, he said something I've never understood. He said, *'It isn't the first time the sea has been kind to this parish.'*" Her pale eyes held Yara's. "Kind. As if the drowning were a favor. As if the water had done the church a service. I've turned that sentence over for thirty years and it has never once lain flat."

Yara felt the floor of the conversation shift beneath her, the way mudflats shift when the tide turns. *Not the first time.* She thought of her ledger — of the other entries, the other accidents, the other years. Of how many pages might connect to that one sentence, spoken in a dead man's voice by a dying woman's memory.

"Mrs. Voss," she said slowly, "before Elias — were there others? Accidents. Children. Anyone the parish lost to the water, or to falls, or to quiet unluckiness?"

Miriam was silent for a long moment. When she answered, her voice had gone very small.

"I don't know," she said. "But there's someone who would. Father Anselm Rook — Crane's curate, back then. He kept the parish register, the burials, the memorials. He catalogued everything, that man. Obsessive about it. If anyone knows what the sea has been kind to—" she paused, and something flickered across her face, doubt or warning, "—it's him. He'll be pleased to see you, I expect. He always did love an earnest visitor."

Outside, the rain was easing. Yara gathered her coat, her gloves, the copied letter, and stood to go. At the door she looked back: the small figure in the wingback chair, the open window, the light going grey and gold over the wet fields — nave-light, Miriam had called it, and Yara understood now that the old woman had been sitting in her cathedral all along, these thirty years, alone in the loft, listening.

"Thank you," Yara said.

"Don't thank me." Miriam closed her eyes. "Finish it. That's the only thanks that weighs anything."

On the train back, as the Somerset levels slid past in the fading light, Yara opened her notebook and wrote the date, and beneath it: *Rook.* She underlined it twice, and found her pen pressing hard enough to nearly tear the page — because she had already begun to notice what Miriam, mercifully, had not: that the handwriting in the confessor's letters, with its hooked *f* and its wounded little crosses, was a hand she had seen before today.

In the memorial book of St. Brannoc's Cathedral. Signed, thirty years running, beside every anniversary the parish chose to remember.

*Anselm Rook, curate.*
