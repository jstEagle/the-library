## 8. Low Water

The last letter came on the morning of the equinox tide, and it was written in the same careful hand as all the others — but this one smelled of salt.

Yara stood at her office window with it held to the grey light, as though the paper might give up more than ink. *The spring ebb will pull the sea back past the old wall,* it read. *What it took, it will return. Come at dawn. Bring your ledger. Bring your courage. You will want both.*

Below, the harbour lay flat and pewter-coloured, holding its breath. She had lived in this town long enough to read its tides the way other people read weather — the way the water withdrew from the sea wall in great reluctant sheets, baring mud that had not seen air since the last spring moon. Thirty years of springs, she thought. Thirty years of ebbs. And only now did someone promise the sea would speak.

She looked at the ledger on her desk, closed, its leather spine worn soft as an ear. *Bring your courage.* The writer knew her too well. Knew the ledger, knew its pages, knew the number of a page where Elias Wren's name sat in her small, stubborn handwriting: *1994. Drowning. Ruled accidental. Not believed.*

She put the letter in her bag beside the others and went down into the cold.

* * *

Rook was waiting at the foot of the sea wall when she arrived, his collar dark against the wind, his hands folded like a man at a graveside. The tide had gone out further than she had ever seen it go, leaving the mudflats exposed in vast brown terraces, glistening, veined with channels of trapped seawater that caught the early light and held it in thin silver threads. The air smelled of iodine and rot and something older — the smell of ground that remembered being seabed before there was a wall, before there was a cathedral, before there was anyone to drown.

"You came," Rook said.

"You knew I would." She picked her way down the stone steps cut into the wall's base, steps slick with weed, each one furred green and treacherous. "You've been arranging this for months. The letters. The bait. You don't stop halfway."

"I stop nowhere," he said quietly. "That's rather the trouble with me."

They walked out onto the flats. Each step sank them ankle-deep; the mud pulled at their boots with a wet, sucking sound, like the ground breathing. Gulls wheeled overhead, screaming their thin complaints. Somewhere behind them the town was waking — a door, a dog, a bell practising for matins — but out here the world was reduced to mud and sky and the long grey line of the wall, and Yara felt the peculiar exposure of it, the sense of being watched by everything at once.

"Where?" she asked.

Rook consulted something he carried folded in his palm — not a map, she saw, but a photograph, old and foxed at the corners. He showed it to her without explanation. It was the sea wall from the seaward side, decades ago, and standing at its base, half-turned toward the camera, a young man in a cassock with his hand raised as if in blessing or in warning. She recognized the face from the portrait upstairs, from the plaques, from thirty years of parish reverence.

Dean Aldous Crane. At the water's edge.

"Where did you get this?"

"The same place you'll find what we're looking for," Rook said. "Miriam Voss kept a box. She kept everything. She told me once that keeping things was the only prayer she trusted." He turned eastward, toward a cluster of rocks the locals called the Coffin Teeth, black and jagged where they broke the flatness of the mud. "There. Where the channel bends. He wouldn't have gone far. Men who do terrible things are lazy about distance."

They walked. The light strengthened by degrees, laying gold along every rivulet until the flats looked stitched with wire. Yara's breath smoked. Her hands, in her pockets, were closed around the ledger as if it could anchor her — and perhaps it could; it was the only honest thing she had carried in years.

"Elias played here," Rook said as they went. "Did you know? Tom Pellowe told me — after enough years and enough sherry — that the boy used to come down at low tide to dig for coins. Victorian pennies, bits of clay pipe. The verger used to watch him from the wall and never once told him to come away from the water." His voice frayed slightly. "Everyone watched. No one came away."

The channel bent. And there, half-buried where the mud had slumped against the rocks, Yara saw it: a corner of metal, rusted the colour of dried blood, catching the sun.

They dug with their hands. The cold of the mud climbed her wrists, under her sleeves, into her bones; the smell of it was profound and mineral, the smell of the deep floor of the world. The box came free with a groan of corroded hinges — a biscuit tin once, painted with ships, now a ruin of orange scale — and inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, they found the shoes.

Small shoes. Black leather, cracked, laces still tied in the double knot a child uses because no one has taught him otherwise. A choirboy's shoes, polished once upon a time by careful hands, scuffed at the toes from running.

Yara knelt in the mud and could not move. The grief she had been carrying for months — for a boy she had never met, whose name the parish had sanded smooth out of its memory — rose in her throat like floodwater. Beneath the shoes lay a second bundle, and inside it, dry despite three decades, a photograph: Crane at the water's edge, the same image Rook held, but this one inscribed on the reverse in a hand she now knew as well as her own. *Forgive me what I keep.*

"He brought them back," she whispered. "He took the shoes off the body and then he couldn't—"

"They weighed on him," Rook said. "Guilt is a tide too. It goes out and it comes back, and each spring it reaches a little higher up the wall." He looked east, toward the pale line where sea would return. "We should go. The water turns in forty minutes."

But Yara was still kneeling, the photograph in her muddy hands, when she heard the sound.

A footstep on stone. Then another — slow, deliberate, the tread of a man who had decided, finally, to stop hiding.

She turned.

Dean Aldous Crane stood at the base of the sea wall above them, upright in his long coat, his white hair moving in the wind. He must have followed them from the town — or known, as Rook had known, exactly where the ebb would lead. His face was composed, courteous, the face from the portraits, the face from the plaques. Only his eyes were wrong. They were fixed on the small shoes in the open tin, and they held the expression of a man looking at a wound he had spent thirty years pretending was healed.

"You should not be here," Crane said. His voice carried across the flats, unhurried, almost gentle. "Neither of you. This ground is not safe when the tide turns. It takes people. It always has." A pause, and then, softer, terrible in its plainness: "It took Elias Wren. I know, because I was here."

The gulls screamed. The mud held them all.

Yara rose slowly, the photograph pressed to her chest, mud to her elbows, her heart going like a fist against a locked door. Beside her, Rook had gone very still — the stillness of a man who had set a trap and caught something larger than he intended.

"I wrote to you," Crane said, descending one step, two, his shadow long across the shining mud. "All those letters. Confession is a discipline, Miss Okonkwo. One learns it slowly, in the dark, alone. But words spoken only to God curdle. They need a witness. They need—" his gaze dropped again to the shoes, and his composure cracked, just once, like ice underfoot, "—they need someone who keeps accounts. I have read your work, archivist. The deaths the police mislabeled. The ones you refused to let lie. I chose you the way one chooses a confessor. Carefully. Prayerfully."

"You killed a child," Yara said. The words came out low and steady, surprising her with their calm. "And you painted over his face and hung it where everyone could admire you."

Crane closed his eyes. When he opened them, he said, "Come to the crypt tonight. Both of you. I will tell you everything — all of it, in order, properly, as it deserves to be told. Not here." He glanced east, where the sea was already beginning to turn, a thin white lace of water creeping up the distant channel. "Not with the water coming. It took him once. I will not stand on this ground while it decides whether to take witnesses too."

He turned and climbed the steps, coat snapping in the wind, and did not look back.

The first finger of the returning tide touched Yara's boot, shockingly cold. Above them, on the wall, the Dean's figure grew smaller against the brightening sky — a man walking back toward the cathedral that had sheltered him for thirty years, toward the crypt beneath its stones, toward whatever waited in him that was worse than confession.

Rook exhaled, a long shaking breath. "He'll run," he said. "Or he'll destroy it. Whatever he keeps down there—"

"No," Yara said. She looked down at the shoes, at the small tied laces, and closed the rusted lid with a sound like a coffin shutting. "Men like him don't run. They rehearse." She tucked the tin under her arm and started for the steps, the tide rising fast around her ankles, patient as everything else in this town had ever been. "Tonight, Father. In the crypt. We finish the autopsy."

Behind them, the sea came home over the place where the box had lain, smoothing the mud to a mirror, keeping its new silence well.
