## 10. Where the Light Falls

The restoration took eleven days, and Yara Okonkwo spent every one of them holding her breath.

She worked in the north vestry, where the light came through leaded glass in pale, watery sheets — light that changed with the tide, she'd learned, brightening as the estuary emptied and dimming when it returned. Solvent first, applied with cotton swabs no larger than her fingertip, dissolving Aldous Crane's varnish in slow, amber curls. Then the overpainting itself, layer by layer, the Dean's solemn face lifting away like a mask, like a skin, until the boy beneath emerged in increments so small she sometimes doubted he was there at all.

He was there. He had always been there.

Elias Wren looked out at her from the canvas with the particular gravity of a child who has sat still past the point of comfort — the painter had caught that, at least, before he caught everything else. One sock fallen to the ankle. A hymnal clutched against a surplice gone soft at the collar. And the eyes: brown, nearly black, holding the particular patience of a boy accustomed to waiting — for a solo, for a bus, for an adult to remember he existed. The portrait had been painted three weeks before he drowned. Yara knew this now the way she knew her own heartbeat. Miriam Voss had told her, in the hospice, in a voice thinned to thread: *He painted Elias that autumn. Said the boy had a face like a psalm.*

On the twelfth day, the last of Crane's pigment came away from the boy's cheek, and Yara sat back on her stool and wept — not the clean grief of a funeral but the ragged, unglamorous weeping of someone who has carried a thing too long in the wrong place. The vestry smelled of turpentine and old stone and the salt that lived in the walls of St. Brannoc's the way sin lives in a family: quietly, permanently, acknowledged by no one.

She let the canvas rest for a full day before she varnished it. Some things deserved the dignity of patience.

* * *

Aldous Crane's resignation letter was read aloud at the chapter meeting on a Tuesday, in a voice carefully modulated to suggest ill health. *After thirty-one years of service, and with the infirmities of age upon me...* Yara sat at the end of the table with her hands folded and watched the faces of the men and women who had trusted him for three decades. None of them knew yet. The letter was a masterpiece of evasion — she had to give him that. He had spent a lifetime painting over things; he was, in the end, only doing it once more.

She had made her choice in the crypt, among the stone saints with their eroded faces, and she did not regret it. That was the strange part. She had expected regret to arrive like a summons. Instead there was only this: a clear, cold certainty, the same certainty she felt when a ledger entry finally closed.

He would not stand in a dock. The evidence — the shoes, the photograph, the satchel, the confession she had recorded but would never play for a court — would remain hers, sealed, a verdict rendered in a jurisdiction of one. But he would not be permitted the mercy of being forgotten, either. She had told him so in the crypt, her voice low, his breath rattling in the dark. *You will walk out of this cathedral every Sunday for whatever years God grants you, and every person in those pews will know exactly what you are. Your name will be in the record. Not the police record. Ours.*

He had wept then. Old men weep easily, she had thought, and then corrected herself: no. *Guilty* men weep easily. She had seen enough of both to know the difference.

The record was the parish's own — the cathedral archive, the memorial book, the oral history project that Tom Pellowe, retired verger and reluctant witness, had agreed to sit for with the grim relief of a man setting down a sack he'd carried since 1994. Yara had written the entry herself, in the careful hand she reserved for permanence. *Elias Wren, chorister, drowned 14 October 1994. Death ruled accidental. Was not.* Seven words did the work of a trial.

* * *

They hung the portrait on the first Sunday of Advent, in the south transept, where the morning light fell longest.

Yara had chosen the spot deliberately. It was the wall where the Crane portrait had hung for thirty years, revered, dusted, pointed out to visitors as an example of the dean's *remarkable benefaction* — he had painted it himself, a modest man's one vanity. Now the boy hung there instead, and the congregation filed past him in their winter coats, and Yara stood at the edge of the transept and watched them stop.

They stopped. That was the thing. People who had hurried toward their pews for decades found themselves arrested by a child's fallen sock, a hymnal held like a shield. An elderly woman Yara didn't recognize pressed her fingers to her lips. A young father lifted his daughter so she could see the boy's face at her own height, and the girl studied it with the unflinching frankness of children, and said, clearly, in the hush: *He looks like he's waiting for someone.* And the father said, *Yes. He was. We're late.* And Yara turned away before anyone could see her face.

The plaque beneath it read:

**ELIAS WREN, 1981–1994**
*Chorister of this cathedral. Remembered.*

No explanation. No accusation. The truth did not need a caption; it needed only light, and the light came every morning now, moving across the boy's face as the tide turned below the sea wall, giving him back hour by hour to a parish that had chosen, for thirty years, not to look.

Crane was there that Sunday. She saw him from across the nave — an old man in a good coat, seated at the end of a back pew, alone, the way lepers and penitents have always sat. He did not come forward to look at the portrait. He looked at his hands. And when the choir began the processional — children's voices, the same vault of stone that had held Elias's voice thirty years ago — Yara saw Aldous Crane's shoulders begin, very slightly, to shake, and she understood that this, too, was a kind of sentence, and that it would be served in weekly installments for the rest of his life, in full view of everyone, with no remission.

She felt no triumph. She had expected that, too. What she felt was closer to the stillness after a tide turns — the moment when the water hangs, neither coming nor going, and the whole estuary seems to hold its breath.

* * *

That night, the cathedral emptied, she climbed to her office with the ledger under her arm and a cup of tea going cold beside the lamp.

The ledger was nearly full. Thirty years of entries in her own hand — the falls ruled accidental that weren't, the fires with tidy origins, the quiet deaths the coroner signed off on and the parish signed off on and everyone signed off on because signing off was what parishes did. She had solved eleven of them. She had never once told a soul. The book had been her confessional, her courtroom, her country of one.

She turned to the final page. The entry for Elias Wren was there, begun weeks ago, the facts laid out in her precise script: the letters, the satchel, the shoes in the tin box, the photograph at the water's edge, Miriam's testimony, Rook's confession of the letters, Crane's confession of the crime. Everything a detective would need. Everything a court would never see.

She read it through once. Then she dipped her pen and, beneath it all, wrote:

*14 December. Case closed — not solved. There is a difference, and I have finally learned it. A solution gives the law its due. A closure gives the dead theirs. I have chosen the second, and I will answer for the choice to no one but the boy himself, and he has waited thirty years, so he can wait a little longer for my explanation, if he wants one.*

*I did not give Aldous Crane to the police. I gave him to the parish. I do not know which sentence is heavier. I suspect he does.*

*Elias: they can see you now. Forgive us the delay.*

She blotted the page, closed the ledger, and sat for a long moment with her hand flat on the cover, feeling the weight of it — not the weight of paper and board, but of every name inside, every debt, every small justice she had rendered in the dark and told no one about.

The lamp flickered. Outside, the tide was on the turn; she could hear it, that low exhalation the estuary made when it gave up the mudflats, the sound she had once heard as menace and now heard, she realized, as something almost like relief. The tide gives up what it took. The letter had promised that, and for once a promise had been kept.

She was reaching for the lamp switch when she saw it — a white envelope on the mat inside her door, where no envelope had been an hour ago. No stamp. No postmark. Hand-delivered, tonight, into a locked building.

Her name on the front in a hand she did not recognize — not Rook's careful italic, not Crane's faded copperplate. Something new. Something patient.

She opened it. One line, in ink still faintly shining:

*You kept the ledger for thirty years, Miss Okonkwo. I kept one too. Shall we compare entries?*

Yara stood very still in the lamplight, the paper trembling only slightly in her hand, and heard, beneath the sound of the tide, the unmistakable creak of the vestry stair — someone descending, slowly, into the dark of the cathedral, as if they had every right to be there.

As if, at last, they had come to collect.
