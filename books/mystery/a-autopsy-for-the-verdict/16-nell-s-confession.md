## 16. Nell's Confession

I had rehearsed this conversation for seven years, which is a long time to rehearse anything, and in all that rehearsal I never once imagined I'd be doing it in the back room of the Harrow's Bend Public Library, sitting across from the woman whose filing system I'd memorized like scripture, while Marisol Quist stood guard at the door with her arms crossed and the expression of a woman who has catalogued every lie in town and is deciding where to shelve this one.

"You're Delia Marsh," Jonah said.

It wasn't a question, but I let it hang there anyway, because I'd earned the pause. Seven years of being nobody earns you a pause.

"I'm whoever Ruth Voss needed me to be," I said. "For a while that was a nurse. Before that, for one week in 2017, I was a dead girl."

Jonah didn't flinch. I'll give her that. Most people flinch when you hand them your own obituary in conversation. But then, most people hadn't spent seven years alphabetizing a town's sins into manila folders. She just sat there with her hands flat on the table — she does that, I'd watched her do it through windows and across courtrooms, that lawyer's trick of making her hands into paperweights so they can't betray her — and she said:

"Say your name."

And that was the moment I understood I'd been wrong about everything, because I had planned for anger, for denial, for her reaching for the phone to call Pike or Calloway or God, and I had not planned for her to ask me for my name like it was the only document in the room that mattered.

"Nell," I said. "Nell Voss."

* * *

You should understand something about dying people: they get honest at the end, but they get honest on their own schedule, and if you're not sitting next to them when the honesty arrives, you miss it entirely.

Ruth Voss hired me in March of 2017 as a live-in nurse, which required her to believe she needed nursing and me to believe I could pass a background check, and both of us were wrong in useful ways. The background check came back clean because Delia Marsh had been born eleven weeks earlier in a county records office two states over, and I had spent those eleven weeks learning to sign a new signature, walk with a new gait, answer to a new name without the half-second delay that convicts spies in movies. What the check couldn't see was that I already knew how to care for a dying woman, because I'd done my residency in grief. My little sister was dead. My mother was pretending otherwise. And I had a car accident certificate from the state saying I was too.

The first month, Ruth talked about the weather. The second month, Ruth talked about her pills. The third month, Ruth looked at me over her breakfast tray and said, "You have Ada's way of standing in doorways," and I knew the game was up, and I also knew — this is the strange part — that I wasn't afraid. I was tired. Seven years of being no one makes you hungry to be someone, even someone caught.

"I don't know what you mean," I said, because old habits.

"Sit down, Delia," she said, and my name in her mouth sounded like a costume she was politely asking me to take off. "Or should I say the other thing? Because I've had seven years to think about what happened to my girls, and I've decided I'd rather die knowing than die comfortable."

So I sat down. And I told her I was sorry. And she said, "Not yet you aren't. Wait until you hear what I'm sorry for."

* * *

Here is what Ruth told me, over that winter, in pieces, the way you hand someone broken glass — carefully, and a little at a time, and always bleeding a bit:

She told me about the Harrow Trust. Not the pretty version they print in the centennial pamphlet, the one where old Amos Harrow's fortune "endowed the town in perpetuity." The real version, where the trust became a washing machine sometime in the nineties, and the men who fed money into it — dirty money, development money, money that needed to come out clean and did, year after year, in grants and land deals and "preservation funds" — learned that a town of four thousand people will believe any document with a seal on it.

She told me Ada found the ledgers. Ada, who worked summers in the county clerk's office, who read everything, who used to read cereal boxes aloud at breakfast because silence bored her. Ada found the ledgers and did what Ada always did with information, which was hold it up to the light and look at it from every side before deciding what to do, and in the three weeks she spent deciding, someone noticed her looking.

She told me about the night at the river. Headlights. Two cars. Her daughter running toward the road, not away from it — toward, because Ada had arranged to meet someone, because Ada still believed, at seventeen, that powerful men could be shamed into behaving like ordinary ones.

She told me she took the settlement. That's the part that made me put down the tea I wasn't drinking. There was a settlement, quiet, sealed, structured like an insurance payout, and Ruth Voss signed it because her surviving daughter was sixteen and grieving and she thought — God help her, she thought — that silence was protection. That if she kept her mouth shut, the machine would leave one Voss girl alive instead of zero.

"And then you died anyway," Ruth said to me, "and I understood that the deal was never a deal. It was a leash. They let me live the way you let a dog live, as long as it stays in the yard." She looked at her own hands, which by then shook even when they weren't holding anything. "I stayed in the yard for seven years, Nell. I'm telling you this so you can do what I couldn't."

"What's that?"

"Get out of the yard," she said, "and burn it down."

* * *

"So you wrote the letter," Jonah said. We were back in the present, back in the library, and her voice had gone very careful, the way voices go when they're carrying something heavy across ice. "The confession. Signed with Ada's name. Postmarked the day after Ruth's funeral."

"I forged the postmark," I agreed. "That was the easy part. The hard part was the typewriter."

Something moved behind her eyes when I said that. Recognition, and under it, dread.

"You recognized the font," I said. "Your font. Your 1978 Smith-Corona, the one you bought secondhand your first year of law school, the one you typed every early draft of every filing on before you could afford a computer. I know because I've read your files, Ms. Okonkwo. All of them. I've had seven years and a library card, and Marisol Quist keeps better archives than the county does."

Marisol, at the door, said nothing, which from Marisol was a full paragraph.

"Why mine?" Jonah asked. And here was the thing — she wasn't angry yet. She was working. I could see the machinery going, the same machinery that had caught the codicil, the dissolved notary stamp, the identity that didn't exist before 2017. She was assembling me out of evidence, and I realized with a kind of vertigo that I had built myself to be assembled. Every clue I'd left was a sentence in a language only she could parse. Filing codes only she used. A postmark timed to a funeral only she would attend professionally. A confession addressed, in every way that mattered, to the keeper of the files.

"Because I needed it to be you," I said. "Because I tried everyone else first."

* * *

Let me tell you about the seven years between my death and my confession, because Jonah would want it documented and because, frankly, I need to say it out loud once to someone who isn't a ghost.

I tried the state police. I wrote them a letter — a plain one, my own handwriting, no theatrics — laying out the trust, the ledgers, the names. It went into a file. I know because I requested the file later, under FOIA, using a name I'd invented for the purpose. Closed: insufficient corroboration.

I tried a reporter in the state capital. He was interested until he wasn't; his editor killed it after two phone calls from people whose numbers I later found in Silas Brandt's calendar. I know because I copied Brandt's calendar. I was Delia Marsh by then, invisible, and invisible people learn that the whole world leaves its secrets face-up on desks.

I tried a victims' advocacy group, a law school clinic, a podcast, God forgive me, a true-crime podcast. Every door opened onto the same room: a room where a dead girl's word weighed nothing against a judge's, a developer's, a deputy's, and where the living witness was a mother who wouldn't speak and a sister who officially didn't exist.

And through all of it, one name kept surfacing. Not Calloway's, not Brandt's, not Pike's. Yours. Jonah Okonkwo, estate attorney, who arrived the week Ada died, who filed the probate on the settlement, who handled the paperwork on the sealed statement — the statement Dr. Hale dictated and someone asked her to lose, and she lost it, and she has hated herself in a very organized way ever since, which I know because hating yourself in an organized way is a thing I recognize like a family resemblance.

"You were the wall," I told her, there in the library. "Every document that mattered went through you and came out buried. And I hated you for years. From a distance. Under a different name. I hated you the way you hate the lock on the door when you're the one locked out."

"But?" she said.

"But then I got close enough to watch you work. Ruth's estate planning, three years ago. You sat in that living room and you told her — I was in the kitchen, making tea, nobody tells the nurse anything, that's the whole point of nurses — you told her, 'A will is a promise the law agrees to keep. So we're going to write one the law can't wriggle out of.' And I stood there holding a teapot thinking: she means it. She actually believes the documents are supposed to mean something. And I thought, oh no. Oh no, I've been hating the one person in this town who might actually open the file if someone forced her hand hard enough."

"So you engineered it."

"I engineered it," I said. "The codicil — Ruth signed it a week before she died, properly witnessed, and yes, the notary stamp was from a firm dissolved in 1998, because I bought a box of defunct firm supplies at an estate sale in 2019 and I am not ashamed, or rather, I am ashamed of exactly as much as I need to be and no more. The letter. The postmark. The typewriter — I bought yours' twin at a flea market in Ohio and practiced for four months until Marisol here couldn't tell the difference, and Marisol can tell the difference between anything."

"She can," Marisol said, from the door, in the tone of a woman confirming a citation.

"The point," I said, "was never to fool a court. Courts are Calloway's home turf; I wasn't going to win there. The point was to fool *you*. To make the past arrive at your office with perfect timing and your own fingerprints on it, so that you would have to choose — really choose, at three in the morning, alone, the way I chose — whether you were the wall or the door."

Jonah was quiet for a long moment. Outside, somewhere, a truck went past, and the library windows hummed the way old glass hums.

"It worked," she said finally, and there was something in her voice I hadn't rehearsed for either. Not forgiveness. Not quite. More like the sound a person makes when they find out their prison had a door in it the whole time and someone else had been standing outside it, knocking, for seven years.

* * *

"Don't mistake me for a good person," I said, because I could feel the moment softening and I couldn't afford soft, not yet. "I forged documents. I impersonated a nurse — I gave a dying woman's medication schedule to a stranger's standards, and if I'd gotten a dosage wrong, that would have been on me. I let my own mother go to her grave — " my voice did something I hadn't authorized, cracked sideways, and I waited it out — "I let my mother be buried without a daughter, because showing up as Nell Voss felt more dangerous than missing it. I did cruel arithmetic every single day for seven years and I always rounded in favor of the plan. So whatever you're feeling right now, whatever generous thing is trying to happen in your chest — save it. Check it against the record first. You of all people should know better than to accept testimony at face value."

"That's the difference between us," Jonah said.

"Is it."

"You want me to distrust you. I want to verify you. Same instinct, different posture." She pulled a folder from her bag — of course she had a folder, she probably slept with folders — and laid it on the table between us. "Hale is dead. Staged as suicide, typewriter missing. Which means whoever cleaned him up knows about the original report, which means Pike talked, which means the clock is running. If I'm going to do this — if I'm going to stand up at the centennial assembly and read the actual coroner's findings to the faces that paid to bury them — I need the rest of it. All of it. Ruth's account, your documentation, the ledgers Ada found. Everything, verified, cross-referenced, airtight. Because Calloway will have lawyers in that room, and lawyers eat uncorroborated grief for breakfast."

"And if I give you everything," I said slowly, "what happens to the part where you lost the statement? Where you filed the settlement, sealed, and never asked why a dead girl's case settled like a fender-bender? Because I have that documented too, Jonah. I told you — I rounded in favor of the plan. The plan includes you answering for your seven years the same as I'm answering for mine."

For the first time since I'd known her — and I had known her longer than she knew, years longer, watching, waiting, typing her name into plans — Jonah Okonkwo smiled. It was a small smile, tired, and it did not reach anywhere near her eyes, but it was real.

"Good," she said. "Because I've already drafted my resignation. It's dated tomorrow."

She slid the folder across the table, and inside it I saw pages in her own cramped hand, and at the top of the first page, a heading that stopped my breath:

**What Actually Happened — per R. Voss, dec., corroborated in part.**

"Now," she said, uncapping her pen, "start at the beginning. And don't round anything in anyone's favor. Not even mine."

* * *

I started at the beginning. The ledgers. The headlights. The second car. Ada running toward the road with her whole stupid brave heart, believing the world worked like the civics textbook she'd annotated in red ink.

And as I talked, I watched the last lawyer in Harrow's Bend take down the testimony of a dead woman's daughter, and I understood that this — this table, this library, this ugly necessary hour — was the closest thing to a courtroom I was ever going to get, and that it was enough. It was more than enough.

It was, at last, on the record.
