## 1. The Reservoir Gives Up Its Dead

The rain had been falling on Hollowmere for eleven days, and by the twelfth it had decided, in the way of this town, to fall forever.

Yara Vane heard it before anything else that morning — the drum of it on the mortuary's slate roof, the gutters swallowing it, the way it made the whole building sound like a ship at anchor in some gray, patient sea. They stood at the window of their office above the examination suite with a mug of coffee going cold in their hand and watched the reservoir road disappear into water-smoke. Somewhere down there, at the bottom of a hundred and forty feet of cold black water, the reservoir had been holding on to something. And now it had let go.

The call had come at 5:40 a.m. from Deputy Ilsa Brand, whose voice on the phone had been careful in a way Yara had learned to read over four years of working with her. Not excited. Not grim, exactly. *Careful.* The way a person is careful when they've recognized something they wish they hadn't.

"Crane crew was doing the autumn dredge by the north spillway," Ilsa had said. "Brought up a body in the grab bucket. It's — Yara, you should just come look."

"Male or female?"

A pause on the line, long enough that Yara had counted the seconds against the rain. "You should just come look," Ilsa said again, and hung up.

Now the body was on Yara's table, and Yara understood the pause.

It was a woman. That was not the problem. The problem was that Yara knew her.

Not in the way one knows a stranger's face from a missing-persons poster — though Yara had that knowledge too, had pinned that particular poster to the corkboard behind their desk three years ago and never taken it down, because Hollowmere did not lose people often and when it did, the losing stayed with you. Marisol Quade. Twenty-six years old when she vanished. Dark hair, a crescent scar through her left eyebrow from a childhood fall off the quarry wall, a small tattoo of a wren on the inside of her right wrist — her grandmother's name for her, she'd told the interviewing deputy at the time, because she'd been born in the first week of spring and her grandmother said wrens sang the winter out.

The woman on the table had the scar. The tattoo was there too, blurred but unmistakable under Yara's magnifier, the wren's wing still holding its shape after however long the water had kept her.

"Okay," Yara said quietly, to the body, to the room, to the rain. "Okay. Hello, Marisol."

The overhead lamps hummed. The ventilation system breathed its low mechanical breath. Yara pulled on fresh gloves, adjusted the mask, and began the work that had been theirs alone in this town for four years — the work they were, if they were honest, quietly proud of. Coroner in a place like Hollowmere saw maybe forty deaths a year, and most of them were kind: old hearts stopping in warm beds, roads made treacherous by fog. Yara had built a reputation on being thorough anyway, on treating every body like a mystery worth respecting. *You're the last doctor anyone gets,* their mentor had said once. *Make the record worth reading.*

So Yara made the record worth reading. External examination first, methodical, top to toe, narrating into the recorder because the recorder deserved the same care as the page.

"Subject is an adult female, apparent age mid-twenties to early thirties. Hair dark brown, approximately shoulder length, saturated. Skin —" Yara paused, tilted the lamp. "Skin shows significant maceration consistent with prolonged immersion. Wrinkling of the hands and feet, degloving of the soles. Adipocere formation beginning in the facial and abdominal soft tissue."

There it was. That was the phrase. Yara said it and heard it and then, a half-second later, actually *heard* it, the way a musician hears a wrong note a beat after playing it.

Adipocere. Grave wax. It formed in cold, wet, low-oxygen environments, and it took *time* — months at minimum, in ideal conditions. A year was not unusual. The reservoir was cold, certainly; the deep water of Hollowmere stayed at four degrees Celsius year-round, a fact every child in the town learned alongside the names of the founding families. Cold water preserved. Cold water slowed everything down.

But it did not slow it down this much.

Yara set down the magnifier. Took off one glove, put it back on. Took a breath that fogged the inside of the mask.

"Time of immersion," they said to the recorder, and their voice sounded different to them, flatter, "pending. Preliminary estimate —"

And there they stopped, because the preliminary estimate was a number they did not want to say, and the number they did not want to say was in direct contradiction to the file that sat upstairs in their office, the file they knew by heart.

Marisol Quade had been missing for three years, two months, and eleven days. Yara knew this the way they knew their own blood type. They had signed the initial missing-persons intake themselves, had photographed her bedroom in her aunt's house on Fen Row, had catalogued her effects — the wren pendant found on the reservoir path, her shoes, neatly placed, her coat folded on a bench as if she'd gone swimming in November on purpose. The town had decided it was suicide, mostly. The reservoir was where Hollowmere went to die when it wanted to; that was the local wisdom, unkind and unexamined. Yara had never fully believed it. There had been no body, and Yara did not certify a death without a body, and so for three years Marisol Quade had existed in the town's records in a state Yara had privately found fitting: *missing.* Unresolved. Unfinished.

Now she was on Yara's table, and she was unfinished in an entirely different way.

Yara worked the next two hours with a care that bordered on ceremony. They took the samples they were required to take and several they were not: hair, nail clippings from both hands, vitreous fluid, a core of the silt that clung to the body's back like a second skin. They X-rayed. They photographed from every angle, the camera's flash turning the rain on the windows into brief white constellations. They noted the absence of ligature marks, the absence of defensive wounds, the single healed fracture of the right radius consistent with the childhood injury in the file. They weighed the organs with the little brass scale their predecessor had left behind, and they spoke to Marisol the whole time, softly, the way they did with all the dead, because it kept the hands steady and because the dead deserved to be told what was being done to them.

And the whole time, the wrong note kept sounding.

The decomposition did not match. That was the plain, unspeakable shape of it. The body showed the chemistry of a year, perhaps eighteen months in the water at the outside — adipocere in its early stages, soft tissues still partially intact, the hair still rooted, the fingernails still present. Not three years. Three years in even the coldest water left a different body than this. Yara had read the studies. Yara had, in a colder professional corner of their mind, once written a summary of those studies for a regional coroners' bulletin. *Yara Vane, "Immersion Timelines in Deep Cold-Water Recovery," Hollowmere District, 2021.* They had been proud of that bulletin. They had been proud of this file. They had been proud, if they were honest down to the bottom of themselves, of being the one person in Hollowmere who knew exactly what had happened to Marisol Quade, which was: nothing that could be proven, nothing that could be closed, nothing that could be filed away by anyone's certainty but Yara's own.

And now the body on the table was telling them a story that did not fit the file, and Yara trusted the body. That was the whole discipline, wasn't it. The body did not rehearse. The body did not remember wrong. The body only ever told you what had actually happened to it, in the only language it had.

The body said: *I was not down there three years.*

The file said: *Marisol Quade has been gone three years.*

Between those two sentences lay a gap, and Yara stood at the edge of it for a long time, one hand flat on the cold steel edge of the table, listening to the rain.

There were explanations. There were always explanations. Cold microclimates at depth, silt encapsulation, some quirk of the north spillway's currents that kept the body in a pocket of near-freezing stillness — Yara constructed each of these carefully, the way they'd construct any hypothesis, and each one collapsed under its own weight when they tested it. The dredge had brought her up from ninety feet, not a deep trench. The grab bucket operator, a talkative man named Oskar Pell, had already told Ilsa the body had been lying *on* the silt, not in it, half-buried at most. A body in a silt cocoon might slow. A body lying exposed in four-degree water did not slow by half.

Yara removed the mask. The room's cold air touched their face like a hand.

"Okay," they said. "Then who are you?"

The question hung in the lamplight. Because that was the other possibility, the one pressing up from underneath the first, and it was worse, and Yara made themselves look at it straight on, because that was the whole discipline: you looked at the worst thing first, and then you worked backward.

If the timeline said one year and the missing file said three, then either the file was wrong about when Marisol Quade disappeared — and Yara had been there, had walked Fen Row, had taken the photographs — or the body was wrong about being Marisol Quade.

The scar was right. The tattoo was right. The healed radius was right. The teeth — Yara had the dental comparison half-completed on the lightbox, the three-year-old charts faxed over from the practice in Corvaston where Marisol had gone as a teenager — the teeth were *right*, filling for filling, the distinctive rotated lower left incisor sitting exactly where the chart said a rotated incisor should sit. Yara had checked it twice. They checked it a third time, and it was right, and being right three times made it worse, not better, because now the identification was airtight and the timeline was broken and both things could not be true and both things *were* true, and Yara Vane stood in the gap between them and felt, for the first time in four years of competent, quiet, unremarkable service to a small gray town, the floor of their own confidence shift like silt.

The dead did not lie. But someone had arranged the dead.

The thought arrived whole, uninvited, and Yara rejected it, and then made themselves un-reject it. Because there was a version of this that was not error. There was a version of this where everything on the table was accurate — the scar, the teeth, the wren — and accurate because it had been *made* accurate, chosen, matched, a body selected and prepared and delivered to the one reservoir in the region with a dredge scheduled this autumn. Yara did not know what that version required of the world. They only knew that it required intention, and intention was a thing they had spent their whole career finding in bruises and fractures and the small dishonest geography of staged scenes, and they had never once found it in Hollowmere, and finding it now felt like discovering a trapdoor under the floor of your own house.

Yara wrote the preliminary report in longhand first, because longhand slowed them down and they wanted slowing. *Identity provisionally established as Marisol Quade, 29, of Fen Row, missing since 14 October. Cause of death: pending. Note: decomposition timeline inconsistent with reported date of disappearance. Further analysis required.*

They looked at the sentence a long time. *Inconsistent.* It was such a small, clean word for such a large, dirty crack.

Upstairs, the file cabinet gave its familiar complaint when Yara opened the drawer — the Quade file, thick with three years of nothing, sat where it had always sat, and Yara carried it to the desk and spread it under the lamp and read it again from the beginning as though it were a stranger's file, as though they had never touched it, hunting for the thing they must have missed.

Photographs of the bedroom. The wren pendant in its evidence bag. The statement from the aunt, Edith Quade, hands folded, voice steady, *she'd been low lately, coroner, she'd been low a long while.* Statements from neighbors, all of them sad, all of them brief, all of them agreeing. The town had agreed so completely, Yara remembered thinking at the time. Grief usually had rough edges. This grief had been sanded.

And at the back, sealed in its own evidence envelope with Yara's own signature across the flap, dated three years ago: *Personal effects — held pending resolution. Diary (1), leather, water-damaged, recovered from subject's bedroom desk.*

Yara had never opened it. There had been no death, so there had been no inquest, so there had been no reason, and the diary had sat in the dark of the cabinet like a seed. Yara held the envelope now and felt its weight, and outside the rain kept up its patient drumming on the slate, and down the hall on the steel table the body that was and was not Marisol Quade waited under the sheet with her one eyebrow scarred and her wren still flying on her wrist, telling the truth in a language only Yara could hear, and the truth did not fit the file, and the file was all Yara had.

They slit the envelope open.

The diary's cover was swollen with old damp, the leather gone the texture of a leaf. Yara turned to the first page with a conservator's care. The early entries were what they expected — a young woman's handwriting, slanted and quick, the ink faded, the dates running through that last October and stopping. Yara read the final dated entry from before the disappearance, three years old, and it said nothing remarkable at all, just a line about the reservoir path and the light on the water in late autumn and how she'd been walking it every day that week.

Then Yara turned the page.

And the next page.

And the dates kept going.

Yara sat very still in the lamplight with the rain falling on the roof of the building and the body lying in the room below, and read an entry dated the fourteenth of March — five months after Marisol Quade vanished — in the same slanted, quick, unmistakable hand.
