## 3. What the File Forgot

The courthouse on Calloway Street had grown a new wing since Astrid Okafor last stood inside it, all glass and municipal ambition, but the smell was the same—toner and old coffee and the faint institutional sweetness of floor wax laid over decades of fear. He carried the casebooks in a canvas tote against his chest like a man smuggling contraband, which, in a way, he was.

Dani Reyes found him in the lobby before he'd finished signing in. She came down the stairs two at a time, blazer over one arm, reading glasses pushed up into her dark hair, and for a moment she was twenty-six again, standing in his bookshop doorway with a borrowed copy of *In Cold Blood* and a head full of questions she didn't know how to ask yet.

"You're donating books," she said. "To the *prosecutor's office*."

"Old casebooks. Reference material. They were gathering dust."

"They were gathering dust for fifteen years, Astrid." She kissed his cheek anyway, quick and warm, and took the tote from him without asking. "Come upstairs. If I have to suffer through small talk, I'm at least going to make you do it near my window."

Her office was on the fourth floor, narrow, overheated, with a view of the parking structure and a corkboard that had once held case photos and now held her daughter's crayon drawings of horses. Astrid catalogued it all in the old way—the habit never died—before letting himself sit in the chair she kicked toward him.

"So," Dani said, dropping into her own seat. "A retired man walks forty minutes across town to donate fifteen-year-old casebooks. What's wrong?"

"Can't a man visit his favorite former protégé?"

"He can. But he doesn't. You hate this building. You told me once it smelled like 'justice going stale.'" She leaned back, arms crossed, and studied him with the particular patience she'd learned from him, which he had never taught her consciously and could not now object to. "What's wrong?"

He had rehearsed this. That was the humiliating part—he'd stood in front of his own bathroom mirror that morning, holding a paperback, practicing casualness. And here she was, dismantling it in four sentences.

"I read about the Harrow trial coming up," he said. "The drowning. Vann."

Something moved behind her eyes—not surprise exactly. Weary recognition. "Everyone reads about it. The Harrow family made sure of that, just not in the way they wanted."

"The fisherman who drowned in the marina basin. The official finding was accidental, wasn't it? Intoxication, night fishing, slipped from the pier."

"That's what the file says." Her voice flattened. She glanced at her door, closed, then lowered it further anyway. "That's what the file is *supposed* to say. Except someone in my own building disagrees loudly enough that we're going to trial over it. Marisol Quist saw a boat that night. Saw men on it. Saw them do something at the rail. She's the star witness, and the Harrows' lawyers have spent eight months trying to prove she's a drunk, a liar, or both."

"And you believe her."

"I believe the tide tables," Dani said. "I believe the water temperature. I believe that Elias Vann's blood alcohol was half what the first report claimed, and that the correction arrived eleven days late with no explanation." She stopped herself, jaw tightening, as if she'd said more than she meant to. Then, softer: "Why do you ask, Astrid?"

"Curiosity. Old habits." He gestured vaguely at the tote she'd set on her desk. "It's why I'm giving you those. Keep the mind limber."

She didn't laugh. She opened the top casebook instead, flipped through it with the absent speed of someone who already knew its contents, and said, "You know what I miss? Your marginalia. You used to write these little questions in the margins of everything. 'Who benefits.' 'Where's the second witness.' I still do it. My clerks think I'm insane."

The ache arrived on schedule, right on time, punctual as grief. He smiled and let it sit behind his teeth.

"What happened to the evidence log?" he asked. "For Vann. The chain-of-custody record."

"Why?"

"Humor me. Consider it the price of the donation."

Dani considered him for a long moment. He watched her decide—watched the exact moment the prosecutor in her won out over the protégé, because there were things she couldn't discuss, and because she trusted him with the boundary itself. That trust was a knife he was holding by the blade.

"Custody log's clean," she said finally. "Almost too clean. Every transfer initialed, every seal number accounted for. But—" she pulled a legal pad toward her, then seemed to think better of it, and spoke instead from memory, which told him she'd been staring at the document late at night, alone, "—there are gaps. Time gaps. The toxicology sample sat in intake for thirty-one hours with no initials. The scene photographs were logged in a batch dated two days after they were taken. Small things. Nothing a defense attorney would bother with. Nothing that proves anything."

"But enough that you noticed."

"I noticed." She rubbed her eyes under her glasses. "And when I asked about it, politely, upstairs, I was told the log was 'within standard variance.' Standard variance. Three separate times." She laughed, short and bitter. "You'd have hated this place, Astrid. It's all standard variance now."

He turned his head slowly toward the corkboard, the crayon horses, the window, buying himself a second to keep his face still. Because the letter was in his inside pocket—he'd carried it like a stone all week—and the letter knew things. Knew the re-dated photographs. Knew the thirty-one hours. Knew the corrected blood alcohol figure that had never appeared in any newspaper, because the correction had been buried in an amended filing that, as far as he could tell, almost no one had read.

Someone inside this building was feeding the letter writer. Or writing it.

"Dani," he said carefully. "When the trial opens—who has access to the exhibits? The physical file?"

"Anyone in records. The trial clerk. Me. Two paralegals." She shrugged. "It's a big building. Why do you—"

"A feeling." He stood, joints announcing themselves, and picked up his empty tote. "An old man's feeling. Indulge it: keep your own copies of everything. Paper, not the system. Something you can hold in your hand that nobody else has touched."

She stared at him. "That sounds like advice from someone expecting trouble."

"It's advice from someone who spent twenty years learning that files lie more often than people do." He kissed the top of her head on his way past, an old gesture she tolerated with a scowl she didn't mean. "Lunch soon. Bring the girl; I'll find her a horse book."

"Astrid." He paused at the door. She was looking at him with that expression again—the young one, the one from the bookshop doorway, the one that believed he was the best detective she'd ever met and didn't know the half of why. "Whatever your feeling is. If it turns into something. You'll tell me. Right? Not go off and do whatever it is you do."

*Whatever it is you do.* She'd said it before, over the years, half-teasing. He had always let the tease stand, let it be a joke between them, a small tax paid gladly for her company.

"I'll tell you," he said.

The lie went down smooth as water, and tasted worse.

* * *

Outside, the afternoon had gone the color of pewter, and the wind off the harbor carried the smell he'd been trying not to think about for three weeks: cold water, diesel, the marina. He walked instead of taking the bus, needing the motion, and by the time he reached Salt & Sparrow the light in the front windows was already on—Nia's doing, she lit it at dusk whether there were customers or not, a small act of defiance against the failing ledger.

Mrs. Pell was installed in the armchair by the poetry section, knitting something military-green and enormous. Theo was in back; Astrid could smell the day's last batch of cardamom buns warming under the lamp. Nia looked up from the returns cart, took one look at his face, and abandoned the cart mid-shelf.

"Well?" she said.

"I saw the evidence log. Secondhand."

"And?"

"Gaps where the letter says there are gaps." He hung his coat on the hook, moving slowly, thinking aloud because these three had earned thinking-aloud. "Thirty-one unlogged hours on the tox sample. Photographs dated late. A blood alcohol figure quietly corrected downward—which means someone wanted Vann drunker than he was. Someone wanted the drowning to stay an accident."

Theo emerged from the back wiping flour off his hands, drawn by gravity. Mrs. Pell's needles had gone silent.

"So the letter writer isn't guessing," Nia said. "They've *read* the file. Recently. Inside access."

"Or wrote parts of it," Astrid said.

The silence that followed had weight. Theo sat down heavily on the bottom step of the ladder. Mrs. Pell resumed knitting, but slower, her eyes on Astrid over the rims of her glasses.

"There's a trial clerk," Astrid continued. "Records staff. Paralegals. Half a building of suspects, and I can't ask a single question without explaining why I'm asking." He dropped into the chair behind the counter, suddenly tired in the marrow. "Twenty years I solved things from this room. Quietly. No badge, no name in anyone's notebook. And now some stranger has written my nickname on paper and mailed it to my shop, and every move I make toward them drags me closer to the light."

"Then don't move toward them," Theo said quietly. "Let it go. Let the courts handle it. Dani's handling it."

"Dani's being stonewalled by her own building, and she doesn't know why. And whoever wrote that letter promised a fourth death timed to the trial opening." He looked around at them—at Nia's steady dark eyes, Theo's flour-white hands, Mrs. Pell's needles clicking again now, deliberate, like a metronome for courage. "Three weeks until jury selection. If the letter keeps its schedule, I have three weeks to find a ghost who works inside the courthouse, using methods I can't explain to anyone with a badge."

Mrs. Pell snorted. "Nonsense. You've done harder puzzles with less tea."

Despite everything, Astrid felt his mouth twitch. Then his eye caught the returns cart, still parked where Nia had abandoned it, and atop the stack, spine cracked, lay the shop's last remaining copy of *The Waterworks* — the edition he'd sold exactly one of, three months ago, to a customer whose name he couldn't quite—

He went very still.

"Nia," he said slowly. "When did that come back in?"

She followed his gaze. Frowned. "Tuesday's returns bin. Why?"

Because he hadn't sold it to a customer. He'd sold it to a man who'd paid cash, worn gloves indoors, and asked whether the shop kept records of purchases.

Astrid lifted the book from the cart as if it might detonate, and opened the front cover to the flyleaf, where in pencil, in handwriting he did not recognize, someone had written a single line:

*You're getting warmer, Detective.*

* * *

That night, after closing, after Theo's buns and Mrs. Pell's third cup of tea and Nia's insistence on walking him the twelve feet to his door above the shop, Astrid sat at his kitchen table with the confession letter and the returned novel side by side under the lamp.

The capital letters in the letter. He'd marked them weeks ago—stray capitals, seemingly random, scattered through the florid prose like birds startled from a hedge. He'd assumed vanity, or madness.

Now he counted them against the pages of *The Waterworks*, and his breath went shallow.

Page numbers. Line numbers. The capitals weren't decoration. They were coordinates—and the map they drew wasn't the confession at all.

It was a schedule.
