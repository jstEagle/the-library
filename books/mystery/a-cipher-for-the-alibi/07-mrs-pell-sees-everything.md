## 7. Mrs. Pell Sees Everything

Edith Pell had buried a husband, a sister, and forty-one years of marriage to a man who thought she noticed nothing, and so she had made noticing her religion. She sat in the window chair of Okafor's Books every morning from eight until ten with the same order—black tea, one sugar, brought by Theo from the bakery next door in a chipped blue cup—and she watched the street the way other women watched their grandchildren: with total, uncritical devotion.

She had seen Astrid Okafor burn three notebooks in his back-room stove last spring. She had seen Nia cry twice and pretend both times it was dust. She had seen the young man who came on Tuesdays to buy true crime paperbacks and always read the endings first, flipping to the last chapter right there at the counter like a heathen. And eleven days ago, she had seen Gerald Finch.

She knew his name before anyone said it aloud, because Edith Pell had attended every session of the Harrow trial that her hips would allow, sitting in the third row of the public gallery with a crossword folded inside her handbag as camouflage. The clerk who called the court to order, who carried the evidence binders, who handed exhibits up to the bench with white-gloved hands—that was the man who had come into the bookshop on the fourth of March, a Tuesday, at quarter past two in the afternoon, when Astrid was at the bank and Nia was in the stockroom.

Edith remembered because she had been reading *The Moonstone* for the ninth time and he had interrupted her.

He had been a careful sort of man, this Finch. Soft-voiced. He'd asked after first editions in a tone that suggested he already knew what answer he wanted. Edith had shown him the glass case herself because Nia was busy—he'd insisted on that, actually, *don't trouble your colleague*, as if troubling Nia were somehow different from troubling an old woman—and he had lingered over the Wilkie Collins with his gloved fingers resting on the glass, not quite touching, the way men touch things they intend to own.

"First state," he'd murmured. "You don't see the correct binding color often."

And then he had done the thing Edith could not stop turning over, the thing that had woken her at four in the morning now for three nights running. While Nia rang up nothing—because he bought nothing, he'd found some flaw in the Collins, some rebacked spine, and declined it with a small disappointed smile—he had drifted toward the back of the shop. Toward the door behind the biography section. The locked door. The door with no sign, no handle worth mentioning, the door Astrid kept shut the way other men keep their hearts shut.

He had stood there perhaps ninety seconds. Edith had counted, because counting was what she did instead of sleeping. He hadn't tried the handle. That was the remarkable part. He had simply stood before it, head tilted, reading the door itself—the frame, the lock, the gap beneath—like a man appraising a painting he intended to steal later, once the gallery closed.

Then he'd turned, smiled at her, and said, "Lovely shop. It must be wonderful, knowing everything that's in here."

And left.

* * *

On Thursday morning she brought it up the way she brought up most things: sideways, over tea, watching Astrid's face with the patience of a woman who had spent decades extracting confessions from a husband who believed himself unreadable.

"That clerk from the Harrow trial came in March," she said. "The one with the gloves."

Astrid looked up from the ledger he was pretending to balance. "Did he buy something?"

"He tried to buy the Collins. Declined it. But that's not why I'm telling you." She set down her cup with a click that meant business. "He went to your back door, dear. Stood there. Didn't touch it. Just... took its measure."

The ledger stopped moving. Astrid's face did something complicated and quick—a shutter closing over a window—and Edith, who had raised two sons and outlived them both in the art of reading faces, saw underneath it. Not surprise. Recognition. The look of a man assembling a picture whose pieces had finally arrived.

"When exactly?" he said.

"March the fourth. Quarter past two. You were at the bank." She paused, savoring it slightly; being right was the only luxury left to her. "But here's the part you'll want more than that. Two weeks later, the city sent a building inspector. Health and safety, fire exits, all that theater. A thin man with a clipboard, very polite, very thorough. Except—" she leaned forward, "—he wasn't thorough about anything except your back room. He asked to see it specifically. Said the plans showed a storage area there. Nia let him in, because he had paperwork, because we're law-abiding, because none of us knew there was anything to find."

"How long was he in there?"

"Eleven minutes. I timed him too." She smoothed her skirt. "When he came out, he thanked us, and he had ink on his fingers. Blue ink. Your ledgers are written in blue ink, Astrid. I've watched you write in them for six years."

The silence in the shop had a texture to it. Somewhere in the stacks, the radiator ticked. Astrid set the ledger down flat, pressing it closed as if something might escape.

"Mrs. Pell," he said carefully, "why didn't you mention the inspector?"

"You didn't ask." She lifted her chin. "And if I told you every man who walked through that door and behaved oddly, you'd never balance that ledger. But a man who measures a locked door in March and returns in April with official paper and leaves with ink on his hands—" she tapped the counter once, sharply, "—that's not odd, dear. That's a plan."

* * *

She watched him go into the back room after that, and she did not follow, though every fiber of her wanted to. Instead she stationed herself at the window and watched the street, because if someone had been watching Astrid, then someone might be watching still, and Edith Pell had decided—somewhere between the fourth sleepless night and the fifth—that this shop and its broken people were hers now, and she defended what was hers.

It was nearly an hour before Astrid emerged. His face had gone the gray of old newsprint. He held a folder—not the ledgers, something thinner—and his hands were not entirely steady.

"The files are reordered," he said quietly, mostly to himself. "Not taken. Reordered. Someone read them and put them back almost perfectly. Almost." He looked at her, and for the first time since she'd known him, Astrid Okafor looked less like a detective than like a man standing in a house that had been entered while he slept. "Finch has been in my private case notes. Everything I know about how those deaths were really solved. Everything I never told the police."

"Then he knows you," Edith said simply. "Better than you know yourself, possibly. Men like that collect people, dear. They collect them the way you collect first editions—looking for flaws, deciding what they're worth, waiting for the price to be right."

Astrid laughed, but it was a hollow sound, and it died fast. He crossed to the window beside her, and together they watched the street: the baker's van, the schoolchildren, the ordinary world going about its business, unaware that somewhere in it a man in white gloves was holding pages of Astrid's secret life.

"I need to know everything else you've seen," he said. "Everything, Mrs. Pell. Every stranger, every detail, however small. Especially anything connected to the trial, or to the witness—Quist, her name is Marisol Quist."

Edith turned her teacup slowly on its saucer, buying a moment, because what she said next would change things and she knew it.

"There was a woman," she said. "Twice now. Dark hair, keeps her collar up, stands across the street by the lamppost where the light doesn't reach properly. First time was the night of the funeral. Second time was Tuesday." She met Astrid's eyes. "And yesterday, dear, a black car parked outside for forty minutes with a man in it who never once opened a newspaper, which in my experience is the surest sign of surveillance there is."

Astrid had gone very still.

"They're not just watching me," he said.

"No," Edith agreed. "They're watching everyone who matters to you. Which means, dear boy, that whatever you're about to do—you should do it quickly. Because I may be old, but I can count watchers, and this week there were three."

She picked up her tea, drank it cold without complaint, and settled deeper into her chair, taking up her post. Outside, across the street, by the lamppost where the light didn't reach, the pavement stood empty—for now.
