## 9. Thursday Night

**Astrid**

The rain came down in sheets over the old cannery district, and Astrid Okafor stood in the dark of a loading bay that smelled of salt and rust and forty years of fish, waiting for a man to come and kill someone.

Not Marisol. Not tonight. Marisol Quist was three miles away in a borrowed apartment above a laundromat, guarded by a woman who owed Astrid a favor and a dog that owed nobody anything. What waited in the cannery was a decoy: a single chair under a single work lamp, a coat draped over it, a wig of dark hair that Nia had sourced from the theater supply shop with the unshakable calm of a woman who had stopped asking questions somewhere around the second cipher.

The bait had been beautiful in its simplicity. A letter, aged with tea and handled with gloves, planted in the evidence review room where Gerald Finch's fingers wandered the way other men's wandered to cigarettes. *The witness will be moved alone on Thursday night. The cannery on Pellwater Road. Come for her before the prosecution can hide her.* Astrid had written it in a hand that wasn't his, in a cipher that was almost the dead man's — close enough that a clerk who'd studied the original for weeks would swallow it whole.

"You're sure he'll come himself?" Theo had asked that afternoon, flour still on his forearms, standing in the back room among the maps and the string and the photographs that had turned Astrid's sanctuary into a war room.

"He won't send anyone," Astrid had said. "He can't. Whoever he sends might read the file. Might talk. Finch has spent twenty years being the only man in the building who knows where all the bodies are filed. He trusts exactly one person, and that person is standing in this cannery right now."

Mrs. Pell had said nothing at all. She had only looked at him for a long moment — the look she gave the shop's accounts when she suspected the numbers were lying — and then she had said, "Wear the good coat. If you're going to be murdered in the rain, at least be murdered respectably."

Now the good coat was soaked through, and Astrid crouched behind a stack of pallets with his heart beating the way it hadn't beaten in years — not with fear, he realized, but with the terrible old familiarity of it. This was the country he had sworn off. The border crossing felt the same.

Headlights swept the broken windows at 11:47. A car he didn't recognize, government plates, parked with the precise, unhurried competence of a man who had never once parallel parked under pressure.

Gerald Finch stepped out with an umbrella he didn't open and a leather portfolio tucked under his arm like a hymnal. He was smaller than Astrid remembered from Mrs. Pell's description, grayer, a man built like a filing cabinet that had been left out in the weather. He walked to the work lamp, looked at the chair, and looked at the coat.

"Miss Quist," he said pleasantly, to the empty room. "Or whoever is kind enough to be standing in for her. You can come out. I've never been good with suspense. It's why I prefer paperwork — everything arrives in its proper order."

Astrid stepped out of the dark.

Finch didn't flinch. That was the first surprise. He adjusted his glasses, took in Astrid's face in the lamplight, and something like satisfaction moved through his expression, slow and oily as tide.

"Detective Okafor." The old rank, spoken like a key turning. "Or should I say the man who signs his letters with a dead fisherman's name. You know, when the decoy letter came through the review room, I thought: this is either a trap or a gift. And then I thought — it doesn't matter. Because either way, I'd finally meet you."

"You read the letter in a room where reading it was a crime."

"I *file* things in rooms where filing them is my job." Finch set the portfolio on the pallet beside him, unhurried. "Do you know what I've learned in twenty-two years at that courthouse? Cases don't collapse. They *drift*. A date moves. An initial appears. A report is re-dated by a week, and suddenly the timeline has a hole in it wide enough to drive the Harrow family's reputation through." He tapped the portfolio. "This is the file that would have ended Miss Quist tomorrow. Every discrepancy, every alteration — documented, cross-referenced, ready to be introduced as evidence of *her* unreliability. The witness whose testimony doesn't match the records. Juries hate that. Juries love a tidy clerk."

"You corrupted evidence to protect a family that let a man drown."

"The Harrows," Finch said, "protect *me*. There's a difference, and it's measured in pension." He smiled, and it was the smile of a man who had done the arithmetic a long time ago and found it came out in his favor. "You of all people should understand. You solved three deaths without a warrant, without a badge, without anyone's permission. Vigilante is just a word the losing side uses. I'm simply better capitalized."

He reached into his coat.

Astrid moved — old reflexes, older sins — and the two men collided against the pallets. The portfolio hit the wet concrete and burst open, pages fanning in the rain through the broken roof. Finch was stronger than he looked and desperate in the way accountants are desperate when the ledger finally balances against them. His hand came up with something small and black, and Astrid caught the wrist, felt the metal of it, cold and certain—

—and then the lights came on.

Every light. The cannery's dead sodium lamps stuttering awake one after another down the length of the building, and a voice that Astrid had heard argue motions and mentor interns and once, years ago, defend him to a review board that didn't deserve her:

"Gerald Finch. Drop it. *Now.*"

Dani Reyes stood at the loading bay door with her weapon drawn and six officers fanning out behind her, rain streaming off her coat, her eyes finding Astrid's over the barrel of her gun with an expression that was equal parts fury and something that might, in a kinder light, have been relief.

**Dani**

She had known, of course. She had known since the decoy letter surfaced in the review room with a postmark that was too neat and a cipher that was too clever, and she had driven to the bookshop that night and stood outside in the rain looking at the light in the back window, and she had not knocked. Because she had understood, standing there, that Astrid Okafor had set a trap and had not called her — and that the reason he hadn't called her was the same reason she'd never been able to find the file on how three separate deaths had quietly resolved themselves over the years, always just off the record, always just past the reach of subpoena.

She had called in the warrant herself. She had written it by hand. And she had told the tactical team nothing except the address and the time, because there were perhaps four people in the building she trusted, and she was looking at a fifth across forty feet of wet concrete with a gun in his hand and a dead man's cipher in his pocket.

"Hands," she said again, and Finch, remarkably, obeyed — slowly, theatrically, letting the officers take the small black object from his fingers: a recording device. Not a weapon. A confession machine. He'd come to document the vigilante, not to fight him.

"You heard all of it," Finch said, almost cheerful, as the cuffs went on. "Every word. The Harrows, the file, the re-dated report. Wonderful. Do you know what happens now, Prosecutor? Nothing. The Harrows own the building you work in. By Monday I'll be a clerical error."

"Then it's a good thing," Dani said, "that the evidence against you isn't clerical." She held up an evidence bag: the portfolio's scattered pages, already being collected by gloved hands. "The original tampered file. Your handwriting on three re-dating memos. And a warrant signed at six o'clock tonight based on a sworn statement from a witness who saw you access the Okafor case files during a building inspection in March." She let that land. "Mrs. Pell has excellent eyes, Mr. Finch. She remembers everyone who lingers."

Finch's composure finally cracked — not with fear, she noted, but with something worse: *recalculation*. She had watched that expression on better men. "The Harrows will—"

"The Harrows," Dani said, "are about to learn what it costs when the paper trail fights back. That's rather the point of me."

They marched him out through the rain. At the door he turned, one last time, and looked at Astrid — and it wasn't hatred in his face. It was the look of a man recognizing a colleague.

"They'll come for you now," Finch said. "Not me. I'm disposable. But you — you're a loose end with a history. Enjoy the bookshop while it's still standing."

The doors closed behind him.

Dani stood in the emptying cannery, the lamp still burning over the decoy chair, and faced the man who had been her teacher, her compass, and — she understood now — a ghost the whole time.

"The witness is safe," Astrid said. "She was never here."

"I know. I've known since Tuesday." She pulled a folded paper from her coat and held it out. "Subpoena. You're testifying. Under oath. About the tampering, about the file, about everything you found — and everything you did to find it."

Astrid looked at the paper for a long moment. Rain dripped from the broken roof onto the concrete, patient as a metronome.

"They'll take the shop," he said quietly. "My name. Everything I built to stay invisible."

"Yes," Dani said. "They will. That's what testimony *is*." She softened, just slightly, the intern he'd once mentored surfacing through the prosecutor. "You taught me that the truth is a thing you either carry into the light or bury so deep it rots. I'm asking you to carry it. One last time."

Astrid took the subpoena. Outside, the rain was easing, and somewhere down Pellwater Road, the lights of the bookshop were still on — three people who knew his whole history now, keeping a lamp lit for him.

"The Harrow family will come for me too," he said.

Dani almost smiled. "Then it's a good thing," she said, "you've got a found family with excellent eyes, a dog, and a bakery. I'll see you on the stand, Astrid. Wear the good coat."

She walked out into the wet dark, and Astrid stood alone in the lamplight, holding the paper that would end his shadow life — and finding, to his own surprise, that his hands were steady.
