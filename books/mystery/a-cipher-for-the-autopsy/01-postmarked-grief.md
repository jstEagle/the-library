## 1. Postmarked Grief

The rain came sideways off the harbor, the way it always did in March, and Sable Okafor stood at the edge of the cemetery with her umbrella collapsed and useless, watching them lower Ada Voss into the ground.

Nine mourners. She counted them the way she counted everything now, out of a habit thirty years deep and impossible to quit: the solicitor, Mr. Pell, with his ink-stained fingers and his umbrella held with the delicacy of a man who had never once been rained on; a woman in a gray coat Sable didn't recognize, standing too far back to be family; a young man from the coroner's office who wept openly and without embarrassment; and Dr. Elias Marsh, Ada's protégé, her chosen successor, her shadow for two decades. Marsh stood closest to the grave, his hands folded, his face composed into an expression of grief so precise it looked rehearsed. Maybe it was. Grief, performed long enough, becomes craft.

Sable had known Ada Voss for thirty-one years. Ada had taught her to read a body the way other people read a letter — patiently, assuming it was trying to tell her something. Ada had signed off on four thousand deaths in her career, and she used to say, over bad coffee in the morgue's break room, that the living lied so fluently the dead were the only honest witnesses left in the county.

Now the most honest witness Sable had ever known was in a box, and the rain was falling on nine people who had come to see it happen.

The minister said something about a life of service. The words scattered in the wind. Sable watched the casket and thought about the last time she'd seen Ada alive — eleven days before the heart attack, in Sable's own bookshop, standing in the mystery section with her finger tracing the spines.

"You've shelved Chandler wrong," Ada had said. "Farewell, My Lovely goes next to The Big Sleep. Chronology matters, Sable. Order matters."

"You drove forty minutes to critique my shelving?"

"I drove forty minutes because I needed to talk to you and I didn't want to do it on the phone." Ada had pulled a book off the shelf, turned it over in her hands, put it back. "There's something I have to fix. Something old. I'll need a few weeks."

"Fix what?"

Ada had looked at her then — that long, level look, the one that made generations of detectives and defense attorneys shift their weight — and said, "When I'm done, I'll tell you everything. I promise you that."

Eleven days later, Ada Voss was dead of a myocardial infarction in her kitchen, alone, a half-finished cup of tea on the counter. Natural causes. The ruling had come down from Dr. Elias Marsh himself, who had signed the certificate with a steady hand and wept at the funeral of a woman he'd loved like a mother.

Sable watched the casket descend and felt something she couldn't name yet. It sat in her chest like a swallowed stone.

* * *

The bookshop was losing four hundred dollars a month, which was a way of saying it was dying slowly, with dignity, the way Ada would have appreciated. Sable had opened it two years ago with her pension and her severance and the naive belief that a retired detective could sell used books to a town that got its reading off phones. Three weeks into retirement — real retirement, the kind where nobody called her at two in the morning about a body — and she was already doing arithmetic in the dark, standing behind the register with the shop's ledger open, moving numbers around like a woman rearranging furniture on a sinking ship.

The bell over the door rang at 6:47 in the evening.

She almost didn't answer it. The mail had already come — the mail always came at noon, and it was always bills — but the knock was followed by the wet slap of footsteps retreating down the sidewalk, and when she opened the door there was nothing but an envelope on the mat, rain-spotted, no courier in sight.

She picked it up and stood in the doorway a long time, letting the cold in.

The handwriting was Ada's. There was no question. Sable had seen that handwriting on thousands of case files, on birthday cards, on the note tucked into her graduation folder from the academy thirty years ago: *Order matters. So do you.* A slanted, economical script, the letters of a woman who wrote quickly because she always had somewhere to be — a body, a courtroom, a truth.

*Ms. Sable Okafor* was written across the front in that script. No return address. The postmark read: **March 14.**

The funeral had been March 13.

Sable stood in the doorway of her failing bookshop, rain coming in past her shoulders, and felt the stone in her chest turn over.

The dead do not mail letters.

She locked the door, turned the sign, and carried the envelope to the back table where she did her failing arithmetic. She put on reading glasses she still wasn't used to. She turned the envelope over twice. She checked the postmark again, as though it might have changed, as though grief might have made her illiterate. March 14. The day after they buried her. Which meant Ada had written it, addressed it, and posted it before she died — and the letter had sat in the system for eleven days, or it had been posted by someone else, or—

Or the dead do not mail letters, and one of them had done it anyway.

She opened it with a knife from the register, the way she'd opened a thousand pieces of evidence, and told herself her hands were steady. They were not, but they were slow, and slow was close enough.

The letter was two pages. Ada's handwriting, unsteady in places, as though written by a tired hand or a trembling one. Sable read it once at speed, the way Ada had taught her to read a scene — whole before parts — and then again, slowly, and by the third reading she was standing, though she had no memory of getting up.

*Sable,*

*If you are reading this, then I am dead, and you have done me the courtesy of coming to the funeral, which I know you would have done even if we hadn't spoken properly in a year. I'm sorry about that. I'm sorry about most things, but that one especially.*

*You will have been told I died of my heart. My heart was bad — I knew that better than anyone, I've held a hundred of them in my hands — but my heart did not kill me. I was murdered, Sable. I have known for some weeks that it was coming, and I chose not to run, because running would have left the lie standing, and the lie has been standing for thirty-six years, and I am tired.*

*In 1989 I falsified nine autopsy rulings. I need you to understand that I did not do it lightly, and I need you to understand that I did do it, and I will not insult you by pretending otherwise. Nine people went into the ground with the wrong deaths written on their certificates, and someone killed them, and I helped bury the truth because I was afraid, and because I was asked by someone I loved, and neither excuse will survive the light. It shouldn't. Excuses never do.*

*The truth is in the ledger everyone reads. That will mean something to you. It took me thirty years to understand what I was looking at, and you were always faster than me.*

*If I am dead, someone killed me to keep the ninth entry quiet, and they will not stop, because the estate is still worth killing for. Be careful. You were never careful, it was the thing I liked least about you and the thing that made you the best of them.*

*Order matters. Finish the sentence.*

*— A.V.*

Sable set the letter down on the ledger of the failing bookshop, on top of the arithmetic of her own small ruin, and stood in the lamplight while the rain worked at the windows.

*The ledger everyone reads.*

She knew it. Of course she knew it. Ada had donated her professional library to the shop two years ago, when the morgue was renovated and her name came off the door — forty years of texts, monographs, and first editions, and among them a copy of Gray's Anatomy, 1901 edition, that Ada had handled more than any other book Sable had ever seen her touch. Ada had read it the way monks read scripture. Margins full of pencil. Case numbers. Dates.

*The ledger everyone reads.* Not the county's ledger of deaths. Not the morgue's logbook. The book that had sat in plain sight on Sable's own shelves for two years, spine out, priced at forty dollars, dusted once a week by a woman who didn't know what she was dusting.

Sable walked out into the shop floor. The rain had thickened to a proper drumming, and the streetlamp outside threw a gray wedge of light across the mystery section, where Chandler stood in the wrong order because she'd never gotten around to fixing it, because Ada had died eleven days after telling her it mattered, because that was what death did — it left everything half-finished, every sentence unclosed, every order unmade.

She found the anatomy text where it had always been. Third shelf, between a water-damaged Dickens and a book on maritime law that nobody had ever bought. She took it down and felt the weight of it, the heft of a thing that had been in Ada's hands for half a century.

She didn't open it. Not yet. She stood there holding it and let herself feel the grief properly for the first time since the cemetery — the full, cold width of it, the fact of a world with no Ada in it — and then, underneath the grief, she felt the other thing arrive. The thing she'd sworn off three weeks ago. The thing that used to wake her at two in the morning and walk her through crime scenes and keep her company through thirty years of the dead's honest testimony.

Suspicion. Curled around the grief like a hand around a knife.

Ada had been murdered. Ada had known she would be. Ada had trusted her — not the police, not Marsh, not the county — *her*, the retired one, the failing one, the one who shelved Chandler wrong.

Sable carried the book back to the table, set it beside the letter, and turned to the lamp. Outside, the rain kept falling on the harbor, on the cemetery, on the town that had believed a clean certificate from a grieving man.

The dead did not mail letters.

But this one had, and Sable Okafor opened the anatomy text to the first penciled margin, and began to read.
