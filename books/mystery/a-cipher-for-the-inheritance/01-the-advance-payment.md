## 1. The Advance Payment

The rain came down on Meridian City like it had a grudge to settle, and Edmund Blackwood watched it through the dispatcher's window with the particular affection of a man who had made his peace with the weather years ago. Rain was good for business. Rain meant people who didn't want to walk, didn't want to wait, didn't want to be seen standing on corners looking like they were waiting for something they'd rather not name.

He ran the night desk at Kestrel & Sons Taxi — there were no sons, and the Kestrel in question had been dead eleven years, but the letterhead was paid up until 1987 and nobody saw a reason to change it — and between calls he read the city the way other men read newspapers. The pattern of fares told him things. A surgeon from St. Ambrose taking three rides to the same address in Dockside meant an affair or a malpractice suit, and by the frequency of the tips, Edmund could usually tell you which. A lawyer riding home at 2 a.m. with his collar undone and his briefcase lighter than when he left meant he'd lost. The city talked all night long. Most people just weren't listening.

At 11:40 on a Thursday, the radio was quiet and Edmund was annotating a map of the warehouse district with small pencil marks that would have looked like madness to anyone else and were, in fact, a perfectly reasonable theory about a series of "accidental" gas leaks, when the bell over the office door chimed.

The woman who came in was soaked despite an umbrella that had clearly lost its argument with the wind somewhere between her car and the door. Mid-thirties, good coat, better shoes, and the kind of exhaustion that sits behind the eyes rather than under them. She stood dripping on the linoleum and looked around the office — the corkboard of pinned maps, the wall of index cards in Edmund's cramped handwriting, the coffee pot that had achieved a state of being technically liquid — with the expression of someone recalculating a decision she'd already made.

"You're Blackwood," she said. Not a question. She'd done her homework.

"Depends who's asking," Edmund said, "and whether they're planning to sue me."

"Vivian Ashcombe." She set a wet envelope on the counter between them. "I need you to prevent a murder."

Edmund put down his pencil. This was not, strictly speaking, an unusual request. It was unusual mostly in its directness. People usually dressed it up — *I have concerns about my brother's new business partner*, *my aunt has been acting strange since she changed her will* — as though the word *murder* were a stain you could get out if you only used cold water and enough euphemism.

"Well," he said, "that's a new one for the ledger. Usually I'm hired after."

"After what?"

"After it's too late to matter." He nodded at the envelope. "May I?"

She pushed it toward him. Inside was cash — real cash, counted and banded, the kind of money that said *I don't trust banks this week* — and a photograph of an elderly man on the steps of a stone house, squinting into sunlight with the wary dignity of someone who'd never quite enjoyed having his picture taken.

"My uncle Gerald. Gerald Ashcombe. He's eighty-one. His will is being read Friday morning, and I have reason to believe someone means to make sure he doesn't reach it."

"What kind of reason?"

"The kind you don't get paid enough to hear secondhand." She peeled off one of the bands and set it on the counter. Triple your rate. That's the advance. The rest when he's alive on Saturday."

Edmund looked at the money, then at the photograph, then at Vivian Ashcombe's face, which was doing a poor job of concealing something underneath the determination — fear, maybe, or guilt, though he'd learned not to guess which without more data. He'd solved three deaths the police had filed under *accident*, and each time the family had thanked him by asking him never to speak of it again. Grief, he'd found, preferred its lies comfortable.

"Ashcombe," he said slowly. "Ashcombe Shipping? The Ashcombes with the wing of the hospital named after them?"

"There's only one family that rich in this city, Mr. Blackwood. We're like the river. There's no second one."

"And you're sure about the murder."

"I'm sure someone in my family wants my uncle dead before Friday, yes."

"That's a strong sentence to say out loud."

"It's a stronger one to live inside." Her voice cracked just slightly on the last word, and Edmund decided he believed her. Belief, in his experience, was less about evidence than about the specific way a person held their hands when they lied versus when they told the truth. Hers were flat on the counter, fingers spread, like a woman bracing against wind.

"All right," he said. "Standard procedure. I'll need his address, his schedule, his doctor's name, and a list of everyone who benefits from the will. And I'll start tonight."

"Tonight?" Something flickered across her face — relief, he thought, but fast, almost too fast, gone before he could weigh it.

"Murderers don't keep office hours. Neither do I." He took the photograph. Gerald Ashcombe stared up at him, eighty-one years old and entirely unaware he was about to become a client. "I'll call you tomorrow with an assessment. Where can I reach you?"

She gave him a number and left without another word, umbrella swinging, heels striking the pavement in a rhythm that faded into the rain. Edmund watched her go, then looked at the money on the counter for a long moment.

Triple rate. For prevention. From a family that rich.

In his experience, rich families didn't hire outsiders to stop murders. Rich families hired outsiders to clean them up afterward.

* * *

The Ashcombe house sat on Hollis Hill like a dowager at a party — old, gray, and daring anyone to comment. Edmund parked two blocks down, where a taxi wouldn't look like surveillance because taxis never look like anything; that was the whole beauty of them. The rain had thinned to a fine drifting mist that turned the streetlights into soft halos, and the house showed exactly one lit window on the third floor.

One window. An eighty-one-year-old man alone in a house that size, four days before his will was read.

Edmund sat with his notebook and did what he did best: he listened to the pattern. A delivery van had come and gone at 9:15. A black sedan with municipal plates had crawled past twice, slow, unhurried, the way cars move when their drivers are bored rather than watching — or pretending to be bored. The neighbor's dog, which should have been raising hell at a stranger on the street, was silent. Someone had been feeding that dog, or drugging it, and Edmund made a note either way.

At 1:20 a.m., he walked up to the front door under the pretense of a fare confirmation — dispatchers verified addresses, it was perfectly ordinary — and rang once.

Nothing.

He rang again. Nothing. But as he stepped back, he caught it: the smell. Faint, sweetish, wrong. Lilies. Funeral lilies, drifting from somewhere near the service entrance, where a wreath lay propped against the wall with its ribbon still curled in cellophane.

Flowers arrive *after*, Edmund thought. Flowers don't arrive early.

His cold dread arrived ahead of his understanding, the way it always did — the body knowing before the mind consented to know. He went back to the cab, picked up the phone, and called directory assistance, then the county records line, which kept late hours for death certificates because grief, too, doesn't keep office hours.

"Gerald Ashcombe," said the clerk, after a pause full of keyboard sounds. "Died Tuesday. Heart failure. Certified by attending physician, Dr. E. Marsh."

"Tuesday," Edmund repeated.

"Yes sir. Anything else?"

"No," Edmund said. "That's everything."

He hung up and sat in the ticking quiet of the cab while the rain wrote its endless shorthand on the roof. Tuesday. Three days ago. Vivian Ashcombe had stood in his office tonight, rain-wet and frightened, and paid him triple rate to prevent a murder that had already happened — and she had known. She must have known. Unless she hadn't, unless someone had kept it from her, unless—

Unless somebody wanted it that way. Wanted the case closed before it opened, wanted the will read Friday over a coffin instead of a living man, wanted a dispatcher chasing a ghost through the rain while the real work got done somewhere warm and dry.

Edmund opened his notebook and wrote, in his small precise hand:

*Gerald Ashcombe — dead Tuesday. Natural causes, allegedly. Client knew. Or didn't. Find out which.*

Then he started the engine and drove toward the one place in Meridian City where the dead still answered questions: the county morgue, where an orderly named Ferro owed him for a little matter involving his brother-in-law's taxi medallion, and where, at 2:40 in the morning, Edmund intended to ask exactly one question.

*Who signed the certificate — and does Dr. E. Marsh exist?*
