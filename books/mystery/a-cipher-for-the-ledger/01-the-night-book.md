## 1. The Night Book

The green ledger opened to a fresh page at nine o'clock sharp, the way it had for ten years, and Ada Oyelaran pressed her palm flat against it like a woman taking an oath.

*Tuesday. October. Shift begins 21:00.*

Her pen was a black ballpoint with a chewed cap — Teo's fault; he borrowed pens and returned them ruined — and she wrote in a hand so small and even that Ozzie once said it looked like typesetting. She took this as the compliment it was. In Marrow Creek, where everything important was decided in back rooms and everything unimportant was shouted across the diner counter, Ada's ledger was the only honest document in town. Every fare. Every time. Every name or no-name. Every odd call at three in the morning from a payphone by the shuttered cannery, logged in her neat columns because a record kept faithfully is a kind of promise: *this happened, I saw it, you can't unsay it later.*

She ruled the line under the date and sat back on her stool. The dispatch office of Marrow Creek Taxi occupied what had once been a coat-check closet at the old Rialto Hotel, twelve feet wide, smelling of burnt coffee and vinyl and the particular cold-metal sweetness of the radio base station. Above her head, forty green ledgers stood on metal shelving in strict chronological order, their spines soft with handling, going back further than she'd been alive. Behind her, the switchboard hummed its low note, the one she'd stopped hearing after her first week and only heard now when something was wrong.

Tonight it hummed steady. Good.

The window over the desk looked out on Depot Street, which at nine p.m. consisted of the lit window of Kowalczyk's Pharmacy, the dark window of the hardware store, and a streetlamp with a moth doing penance against it. Rain had come through around six and left the asphalt shining, so the whole street looked varnished, like something worth keeping.

Ada poured coffee from the pot Renata had left warming — Renata's coffee, thick as river water, sweetened past apology — and settled in.

* * *

The first fare came in at 9:17. Mrs. Ellery from the senior apartments, Tuesday being her bingo night, though there was no bingo on Tuesdays anymore and hadn't been since the hall closed, but Mrs. Ellery insisted on being driven to the building anyway, where she sat in the dark lobby for an hour "keeping the chairs company," as she put it.

"Dispatch, we got a pickup at Birchwood Apartments?" Ozzie's voice came through the radio gravelled and half-swallowed, the way it always did, as if he were transmitting from inside a gravel quarry or possibly from inside his own throat.

"Copy, car two. Birchwood, 21:17." Ada wrote it down. *Ozzie. Fare 4402. Birchwood → Community Hall.* Then, in the margin, in her smaller hand: *Mrs. E. Chairs.*

That margin habit was hers alone. Nobody had trained her into it; nobody had told her not to. Ten years ago, when she'd taken the night desk at nineteen — too young, too new in town, the girl with the accent nobody could place and the last name nobody recognized — she'd discovered that the fares themselves were only half the record. The other half was what clung to them. Who cried in the back seat. Who paid in exact change counted out coin by coin like a man defusing a bomb. Who asked the driver to wait five minutes and then sat in a parked car outside a house they used to live in until the porch light went off.

People told taxi drivers things they'd never tell a priest, because a priest might absolve them and a driver might repeat them, but a dispatcher — a dispatcher just writes it down and keeps it forever, and there is a strange comfort in that, being witnessed without being judged.

At least, that was how Ada thought of it. She had never examined too closely whether it was comfort for them or for her.

"Car two, your passenger wants the scenic route," Ozzie radioed ten minutes later. "I told her the scenic route costs the same as the regular route and takes eleven minutes longer, and she says good, she's in no hurry to be anywhere."

"Log it as regular," Ada said. "Scenic's free tonight."

"You're a saint, dispatch."

"I'm a bookkeeper."

Ozzie laughed, a short bark like a door slamming in a wind. He'd been driving cab in Marrow Creek for thirty-one years, longer than Ada had been alive by a decade and a half, and he drove the night shift the way old men walk — slowly, deliberately, knowing every crack in the sidewalk before their feet find it. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with forearms like dock rope and a mustache that predated the town's current zoning laws. He called her *kid* when he remembered she was technically his supervisor, and *dispatch* when he didn't feel like remembering anything at all.

He was also, though he would have denied it under oath, the closest thing she had to a father.

* * *

Renata came in at ten for her paperwork, filling the little office with the smell of lavender hand cream and cigarette smoke, an unholy combination she'd perfected over decades. She was small and round and silver-haired, with reading glasses pushed up into her hair and a rosary wound twice around the rearview mirror of car four, beads worn smooth as sea glass.

"Mija." She kissed the top of Ada's head as if Ada were still nineteen and new, which, in Renata's private cosmology, she perhaps always would be. "You're eating? There's tamales in the break room fridge. Don't let Ozzie take the pork ones, those are labeled."

"They're not labeled."

"They're labeled in my heart." Renata flipped through her trip sheet, initialing it, then paused. "You hear about the Havel dinner? Corliss called the house looking for you. Very gracious, very warm. You know how she is — she makes you feel like the most important person in Marrow Creek right up until you realize she made everyone else feel exactly the same way."

"What did she want?"

"To plan something. Anniversary business." Renata waved a hand, rings flashing. "October nonsense. You'll see." She squeezed Ada's shoulder on her way out, and the squeeze lingered a half-second longer than usual, the way it did when Renata wanted to say something and decided, as she often did, to pray about it instead.

Ada logged Renata's end-of-shift at 22:04 and noted, in the margin, *R. worried about something. Ask Sunday.*

Sunday was family dinner at Ozzie's — Ozzie, Renata, Teo, and Ada, four people bound together not by blood but by the specific gravity of night work, which pulls harder than most kinds of love. She'd been folding herself into that table for a decade. First as the orphaned newcomer who'd shown up with one suitcase and a certificate in records management and nowhere to be; then as the kid they fed; then, without anyone announcing the transition, as simply theirs.

Teo arrived at 22:30 for the late shift, ducking through the door with rain still beaded on his jacket, twenty-four years old and built like a question mark, all elbows and apology. He set a gas-station coffee on her desk without being asked — black, two sugars, the way she took it — and dropped into the chair beside the switchboard.

"Slow night?"

"It's Tuesday. Tuesdays are for the lonely and the sick." She sipped. "You'll get Mrs. Ellery home at eleven, probably the Delgado boy from the clinic at midnight, and nothing else till bar close."

"Cool." He said it flatly, but Ada caught the relief underneath it. Teo liked slow nights. Teo liked nights when nothing happened, when the radio stayed quiet and the worst event on the log was a raccoon in the dumpster behind the VFW. He'd been with the company three years, hired at twenty-one, and he still flinched at loud radios and sudden knocks, and none of them ever asked why, because everyone at Marrow Creek Taxi understood that people arrive carrying whatever they carry, and the job was to drive, not to dig.

Though Ada dug, in her way. She just did it on paper.

*Fare 4403, 23:00, Teo, Birchwood → Maple Street,* she wrote when Mrs. Ellery finally consented to leave her chairs. In the margin: *E. humming. Good night for her.*

* * *

The odd call came at 1:38 a.m., because odd calls keep banker's hours in reverse.

The switchboard rang twice — not the dispatch line, the old direct line, the one printed nowhere anymore — and Ada answered on the second ring out of pure reflex.

"Marrow Creek Taxi, night dispatch."

Silence. Not dead air — *occupied* silence, the kind with breathing in it, someone holding the receiver close and saying nothing, the way people do when they've dialed a number out of muscle memory and then forgotten why.

"Hello? Do you need a cab?"

A breath, almost a word, almost — and then the click, and the dial tone, and the low hum of the board resuming its single steady note.

Ada sat with the receiver in her hand for a moment. Then she pulled the ledger toward her and wrote:

*1:38 a.m. Direct line. No voice. Held approx. 8 sec. No callback attempted.*

She stared at the entry. It wasn't the first such call — the direct line collected ghosts, wrong numbers, drunks, kids daring each other — but there was something in the quality of that silence, some held-breath weight, that made her add, after a pause:

*Caller seemed to know the number.*

Then she felt foolish, crossed nothing out — she never crossed anything out; the ledger recorded even her own hesitations — and ruled the line.

Outside, the moth had won its argument with the streetlamp or lost it; either way the light was clear now, falling straight down onto wet asphalt. Teo was out on a fare. Ozzie had signed off at midnight with a grunt that meant *goodnight, kid* in fluent Ozzie. The office was just Ada and the ledgers and the hum.

This was the hour she loved best and trusted least. At this hour the order of the thing was visible: forty books on a shelf, each one a year of the town's secret life, each entry a small act of faith that writing something down mattered. She ran her finger along the spines sometimes, on slow nights, the way other people touched photographs. *Here's the flood year. Here's the year the mill cut shifts. Here's the year Danny Voss drowned,* and her finger always slowed at that spine, the oldest book she'd personally filled, though she couldn't have said why. Twelve years ago. She'd been seven months on the desk. She remembered almost nothing about that October except that everyone had agreed, quickly and completely, that it was a tragedy and an accident and a closed case, and that Sheriff Brandt himself had said so at the diner, standing, hat in hand, and the whole room had nodded like a field of wheat.

Everyone kind to her had said so. So she had believed it. That was how belonging worked, when you were nineteen and new: you believed what the kind people believed, because the alternative was being alone again with your suitcase.

The switchboard hummed. The coffee went cold in the pot. At 2:14 a.m. — she would remember the time later, would remember it with a violence she couldn't yet imagine — the radio crackled and Teo's voice came through, young and careful:

"Car three, dispatch. I'm dropping at the depot. Anything else out here?"

Ada glanced at the board. Nothing pending. The town lay under its wet shine, all its secrets filed away for the night.

"Nothing, car three. Come on in. Log out at 2:20."

"Copy. See you Sunday."

She wrote the final entries of the shift in her small even hand, totaled the fares, initialed the page, and closed the green book with the soft thump of a heart valve shutting. Forty volumes on the shelf behind her, and this one done, and tomorrow another, and somewhere in all that paper — though she didn't know it yet, wouldn't know it for six more days — a fare waited at 2:14 a.m. on a road by the reservoir, paid in cash, passenger unnamed, logged by her own faithful hand and never once questioned.

The mail came at seven. But that was tomorrow.

Ada turned off the lamp, and the ledgers stood in the dark like witnesses who never sleep.
