## 10. The Cipher Emerges

The kitchen at 4 a.m. was Ada's second dispatch desk. She'd learned that years ago — that some problems wouldn't be solved under the green lamp in the station office, where every creak of the floorboards sounded like Ozzie checking on her and every ring of the phone pulled her back into the river of fares. Some problems needed a table you could spread things across, a pot of coffee going bitter, and the particular silence of a house where nobody else had ever really lived.

She had both letters flat on the table now, weighted down with the salt shaker and the pepper mill, and around them she'd built a paper city: forty photocopied pages, one pulled from each year of her ledgers, chosen at random like control samples. If there was nothing in the letters but grief and cruelty, these would prove it. If there was something else —

*The rest is filed where you'd file it.*

She read it again. She'd read it maybe two hundred times since Tuesday, and each reading had given her the same chill, because it wasn't a threat exactly. It was an instruction. It assumed she had a system. It assumed the system was legible to someone other than her.

Nobody else had ever found her system legible. She'd shown Teo once, back when he was training, how she coded repeat customers by initial and zone, how she logged oddities with a small asterisk and a time-stamp notation. He'd lasted about ninety seconds before his eyes glazed over. *Ada, it's a taxi company, not the CIA.* She'd laughed and closed the book, and something in her had quietly folded shut, the way it always did.

But whoever wrote this letter hadn't glazed over.

She turned to the confession letter again. The handwriting was careful, upright, unhurried — ballpoint, medium pressure, no tremor. The phrasing:

*I have carried what happened to Danny Voss for twelve years. I was there. I did not act. The water took him and I let the water take the blame too.*

Twelve years. She'd written that number herself, hadn't she? Not in those words — but in the ledger's anniversary margin, every June fourteenth, she logged a small entry: *D.V. — 12 yrs today*, updating the count like a subscription renewal. She'd started doing it the third year, when she realized nobody else in town marked the date except the Voss family's lawyer, who sent a card to the reservoir authority asking them to check the fencing. It was a private ritual. A private, unshared, unwritten-anywhere-but-the-ledger ritual.

Her hands went cold around the coffee mug.

She set it down and pulled the June ledgers. 2013, 2014, 2015. There they were, three entries in her own slanted hand, spaced exactly twelve months apart, each one noting the date and the accumulating year-count. The letter writer said *twelve years*. This year's ledger said twelve. Coincidence was possible. Anyone in town could count to twelve; the anniversary committee had been counting loudly enough for a month.

But then there was the second letter. *Filed where you'd file it.* Filed. Not hidden, not buried, not thrown in the reservoir — filed. That was a records word. That was a dispatcher's word. Her word.

Ada pushed back from the table, walked to the window, looked out at the dead-end dark of Sycamore Street, and made herself breathe. Then she came back and began the work that would eat the rest of the night.

* * *

She started with structure, because structure was where she lived. Every letter has a skeleton — the order of its clauses, the weight of its pauses. She took a pencil and numbered the sentences of the confession:

1. I have carried what happened to Danny Voss for twelve years.
2. I was there.
3. I did not act.
4. The water took him and I let the water take the blame too.
5. I am sorry in the only way left to me.
6. Ask the night book. It does not lie.

She sat very still when she got to six. She hadn't noticed it before — or she had, and some protective part of her had skimmed past it the way you skim past a word you're not ready to read. *Ask the night book.* Nobody called it the night book. The drivers called it the green book, when they called it anything, usually with affectionate mockery — *what's the green book say, Ada?* — but the night book was hers alone, a name she'd given it in her head the first winter, when the station was so quiet she talked to the ledger to keep herself company. She had never said it aloud. She was almost certain she had never said it aloud.

Almost certain was doing a lot of work in that sentence.

She checked the second letter again. *The rest is filed where you'd file it.* Two letters, two references to her private language of record-keeping. One person knew the ledger as intimately as she did — or one person had decided to perform that intimacy, which was somehow worse.

Unless. Unless the intimacy wasn't performed. Unless it was borrowed.

She thought about the razored page. Page 214, the oldest book, the fare from the reservoir road at 2:14 a.m., gone. Someone had been editing her archive for a long time. Maybe the same someone was now editing it in the opposite direction — adding instead of subtracting, pointing instead of cutting.

Fine. If the letters spoke her language, she would answer in it. She would do what she did every night: log everything, code everything, and read the pattern until it confessed.

* * *

The method, when it came to her, came the way methods always did — sideways, while she was doing something mundane. She was alphabetizing the photocopies by year, purely to keep her hands busy, when she noticed that she'd unconsciously stacked them in her own filing order: zone first, then date. Zone Six, the reservoir zone, then Zone Two, downtown, then Zone Four, the Havel hill. And looking at the stack that way, she saw it — not the answer, but the shape of a door.

Every letter she'd ever received went through the mail drop at the station. Every memo from the owner — the reclusive holding company that paid the lease and never called — arrived the same way. Every work order, every insurance form, every driver's schedule change crossed her desk and got logged with a reference code: initials of sender, zone of relevance, sequential number. R.H.-Z4-0117. B.S.-Z1-0033. Ten years of them, thousands of them, a sediment of bureaucratic life laid down in her own hand.

What if the letters weren't just *about* the ledger? What if they were *keyed* to it?

She pulled the master index — the thin black notebook where she kept the reference codes, because even she couldn't hold ten years of codes in her head. She opened the confession letter beside it and forced herself to stop reading it as prose and start reading it as data.

Sentence lengths: 11 words, 3 words, 4 words, 15 words, 9 words, 7 words.

Hmm. Nothing. She tried initial letters of each sentence: I, I, I, T, I, A. Nothing. First letters of every word? That way lay madness and crossword puzzles.

Then she did the thing she should have done first, the thing she did every single shift without thinking: she stopped looking at the letter and started looking at the *times*. Because if there was one thing her ledger never lacked, it was times. Every entry had one. Times were the spine of the whole system.

The confession letter mentioned no times. But it mentioned *the water*, and the water meant Zone Six, and Zone Six had a history she'd catalogued in asterisked notations for a decade — the late-night pickups off Reservoir Road, the fishermen, the teenagers, the one ambulance run the summer the heat killed old Mr. Prewitt at the boat launch. She pulled every Zone Six entry from every ledger, forty pages' worth, and laid them out in chronological order across the kitchen table until the table looked like a card catalogue had crashed there.

And then, because her eye was trained and her hand was steady and it was five in the morning and she was beyond tired into that clear cold country where the mind stops pretending to be reasonable, she did the thing she'd done with the sentence numbers. She took the first letter of each Zone Six entry's coded passenger field.

Most entries had no passenger name — cash fares, unnamed. But the coded field wasn't empty even when the name was. She always entered a placeholder: the pickup zone initial, the hour, the sequence. Z6-0214-A. Z6-0338-B. Z6-2317-C. Alphabetical by sequence within each day, resetting daily. Her own invention. Her own private little taxonomy that no one had ever needed to learn because no one else had ever needed to read it.

She wrote the placeholder letters down the margin of her legal pad, one per entry, ten years of Zone Six fares:

A, C, D, B, F, E, H, G, J, I, L, K, N, M, O...

Pairs. The alphabet walking down the page in pairs, slightly out of order — A-C, D-B, F-E, H-G — transposed pairs, like fingers typing a step ahead of intention, like a mind that knew the sequence and kept nudging it.

She stared at it for a long time. The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere outside, the first gray suggestion of dawn stood behind the pines.

Transposed pairs. If you corrected the transpositions — if you sorted each pair alphabetically, which is what *she* would have done, which is what the system demanded — the column resolved:

A, B, C, D, E, F, G, H, I, J, K, L, M, N, O...

Sequential. Perfectly, impossibly sequential. Fifteen consecutive letters running through fifteen Zone Six entries scattered across ten years of ordinary nights. The odds of that happening by chance were the kind of odds you didn't calculate because calculating them would mean admitting the universe had played a joke this elaborate.

It wasn't chance. It was a message. Someone had planted a sequence in her ledger the way you'd plant a flag — slowly, patiently, one fare at a time, over a decade. Or — the colder thought, arriving right behind the first — someone had simply *read* the sequence that was already there, because her system generated sequences whether she intended it or not, and any sufficiently long random string contains patterns, and a patient reader could find one and claim it.

Which was it? Had the ledger been written to, or was it being read from?

Her hands were shaking now, and she noted that with clinical detachment, the way she'd note a driver reporting icy conditions. She made herself keep working.

If the letters were keyed to the ledger, the key should extend beyond Zone Six. She went back to the confession letter and this time counted differently — not words, but characters per line, the way the typed memos from the holding company were always formatted, sixty-two characters to a line, a quirk of their ancient typewriter ribbon that she'd logged once, years ago, in a fit of thoroughness, and never thought of again.

The confession letter's lines ran fifty-eight characters. Fifty-eight. She checked the second letter. Fifty-eight. She pulled the memo file — the thin folder of correspondence from Marrow Creek Holdings, the owner that never called — and measured. Sixty-two, sixty-two, sixty-two. Close. Same machine family, different setting, or a deliberate variation. Someone writing on equipment related to the company's, or imitating it, or —

Or someone who had worked at the station long enough to know its machines.

She sat down on the kitchen chair very carefully, the way you sit down when the floor has become unreliable.

* * *

By seven she had filled eleven legal-pad pages, and the pattern had grown teeth.

The Zone Six sequence was only the beginning. Once she knew to look, the correspondences surfaced everywhere, the way faces surface in wallpaper once someone points them out. The second letter's phrase *where you'd file it* matched, character for character in its vowels, the vowel-pattern of her most-used dispatch formula: *logged and filed, end of entry* — the closing notation she stamped on every completed incident report, four hundred and six times in ten years. Whoever wrote the letter had built its central phrase out of the bones of her own boilerplate.

Worse: the dates. She'd assumed the letters' arrival was random — a Tuesday mail drop, nothing special. But she cross-checked the postmark against her logs and found that both letters had arrived on days whose entries contained Zone Six activity. Not the anniversary. Not Danny's death date. Small, forgettable days — a lost wallet returned from the boat launch, a fisherman's early pickup — days when her pen had touched the reservoir and moved on. The letters hadn't come when the calendar said to come. They'd come when the *ledger* said to come.

Whoever was writing them was reading her in real time. Not from memory — from access. Current access. Recent access. The letters were being timed to her entries the way a duet is timed to a lead part.

Exhilaration hit her first, and she was ashamed of how pure it was — thirty seconds of actual joy, standing barefoot in her kitchen at seven in the morning with cold coffee and eleven pages of proof that the most secret part of her life, the part she'd never been able to explain to anyone, the grammar she'd invented to make ten thousand lonely nights mean something — that grammar *mattered*. Someone had studied it. Someone had taken it seriously enough to build a message inside it. For one shining moment she felt less like the woman who talked to ledgers and more like the keeper of a text worth decoding.

Then the fear arrived, and it did not leave.

Because flip it over. Flip the exhilaration over and look at its underside. If someone knew her system that well — knew the placeholder codes, knew the closing notation, knew the sixty-two-character ribbons and the Zone Six habits and the private name she'd never spoken aloud — then that someone had been watching her for years. Not her routes. Not her drivers. *Her.* The letters weren't a confession delivered to a stranger. They were a confession delivered to a specific instrument, tuned to her frequencies, and the tuning had taken a decade.

And the razored page. She'd been thinking of it as vandalism, as evidence tampering. Now she thought of it as punctuation. Someone was editing the text. Cutting some passages, composing others, timing deliveries to the entries. Her ledger wasn't a record anymore. It was a correspondence, and she was only now realizing she was one of the parties — possibly the lesser one.

There was one more thing. She saved it for last because she didn't want to look at it directly, the way you don't look directly at a shape in the water.

The Zone Six sequence — the fifteen consecutive letters — ended at entry O. Entry sixteen, the next Zone Six fare in chronological order, was the one from the oldest book. The one from the night Danny Voss died. The 2:14 a.m. pickup. Page 214. The razored page.

The sequence stopped exactly where the page was missing.

Either coincidence — and she no longer believed in coincidences that neat — or the missing page was the sixteenth letter of the message. P. Or the missing page was the point of the whole exercise, the destination the other fifteen letters had been walking toward, one decade-long step at a time.

Someone had removed the final piece of the cipher and mailed her the rest, daring her to notice the gap. *Ask the night book. It does not lie.* But the night book had been lied to, cut open, edited — by someone with keys, with patience, with ten years of access to the rack of green volumes behind her desk.

She thought of Ozzie's eyes flicking to the ledger rack yesterday over coffee, quick as a fish turning. She thought of Teo's face going pale at the mention of the letters. She thought of Renata's rosary clicking through her fingers in the break room, bead after bead after bead, like someone counting something that couldn't be counted on fingers.

One of them. It had to be one of them, or someone who moved among them. The knowledge was too domestic, too deep, too warm. Strangers didn't know what you named your ledger in your head.

Ada gathered the legal pads, squared their edges, and locked them in the metal box she used for the day's cash float. Then she stood at the sink washing the coffee mug she hadn't drunk from, watching the light come up hard and ordinary over Sycamore Street, and let herself say the conclusion out loud, because saying it aloud was the only way she trusted it:

"Whoever you are, you learned me." The tap dripped. "You've been learning me for years."

The phone rang. The landline, the station line forwarded to her on slow mornings, the number only drivers and the owner's holding company had reason to use at this hour.

She dried her hands and picked up on the third ring, dispatcher-voice automatic. "Marrow Creek Taxi."

A pause. Then a voice she knew — official, unhurried, wearing its authority like a pressed uniform.

"Miss Oyelaran. Sheriff Brandt. You got a minute?" A beat, and then, lightly, almost kindly, the way you'd mention weather: "I understand you've been getting some mail."
