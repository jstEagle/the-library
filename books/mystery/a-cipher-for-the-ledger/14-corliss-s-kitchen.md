## 14. Corliss's Kitchen

The invitation came on cream card stock, hand-addressed, delivered by a boy on a bicycle who didn't wait for a tip. *Mrs. C. Havel requests the pleasure of Ada Oyelaran's company, Thursday, seven o'clock, to discuss transport arrangements for the Anniversary Dinner.* And beneath the printed formality, in a different ink, a line added by hand: *I'm told you make the best coffee in Marrow Creek. Bring your own beans; mine are terrible.*

Ada read it four times in the dispatch office, the card balanced on her knee while the switchboard hummed its small electric patience. She should have felt flattered. Corliss Havel did not invite people; she *summoned* them, and the summons was itself a kind of currency in Marrow Creek — a coin you could spend for the rest of your life. Ozzie had eaten at that table twice in thirty years and still told the story like a war medal.

Instead, Ada felt the specific cold of a fisherman's line touching water.

Because Corliss Havel had no reason to know Ada made good coffee. And because the dinner was four days away, the transport plan already drafted, already approved — Ada had typed it herself, three copies, one for the committee, one for Brandt, one for the green ledger. There was nothing left to discuss.

Which meant Corliss wanted to look at her.

She went anyway. Of course she went. You didn't refuse the Havels; you attended, you smiled, you kept your hands visible, and you took notes afterward. Ada took notes in her head now, always, the ledger habit having spread from paper into her skull until her whole life ran in neat columns.

* * *

The Havel house stood on Knoll Street like a ship that had decided, long ago, to stop sailing and simply dominate the harbor. White clapboard, black shutters, a weathervane in the shape of a heron that hadn't turned in years — Ozzie swore it was rusted in place, which he considered a metaphor for the entire family. The porch light was on when Ada's cab — she'd driven herself, logged it, *personal, 6:52 p.m.* — pulled up the crushed-shell drive.

Corliss opened the door herself. No housekeeper, no nephew, no buffer. She was seventy-one and wore her age the way other women wore jewelry: deliberately, as a choice. Silver hair in a low twist, a cardigan the color of moss, reading glasses pushed up into her hair like a crown she'd grown tired of wearing.

"Ada Oyelaran." Her voice was warm brandy. "Ten years of night shifts and this is the first time you've stood on my porch. I feel like I've been entertaining a ghost."

"Most of my work is done while people sleep, Mrs. Havel. Ghosts are sort of the demographic."

Corliss laughed — a real laugh, head tipped back, and Ada hated how much she liked it. "Come in. And it's Corliss. Anyone who calls me Mrs. Havel in my own kitchen gets fed last."

The kitchen was the heart of the house, and it knew it. Copper pots hanging like a congregation, a farmhouse table scarred by a hundred years of elbows, a window over the sink looking out on the dark slope of the reservoir road — no. Ada corrected herself. Looking out on the *hills*. She was not going to stand in this woman's kitchen and see the reservoir in every window. That was the letters talking. That was the dread talking, dressing everything in shrouds.

"Beer, wine, or the iced tea I'm famous for?" Corliss asked.

"Tea. And I brought the beans, as instructed." Ada held up the paper bag. "Dark roast. I wasn't sure how serious you were."

"Deadly serious. Aldous drank that swill from the diner for forty years out of loyalty, and I drank it out of marriage, and the day I buried him I swore the next man who touched my coffee pot would be a professional." She said it lightly, but Ada caught the seam in it — the way the sentence about Aldous was finished too quickly, like a hem sewn in a hurry. "Sit. Talk to me while I ruin your beans."

So Ada sat at the scarred table while Corliss Havel ground her coffee by hand, an old brass grinder clamped to the counter, and asked questions that sounded like small talk and weren't.

"How long have you been with the company now? Ten years?"

"Ten in June."

"And you keep the books yourself? All of them?"

"Every fare. It's the job."

"Every fare." Corliss set the grinder down and looked at her over the counter, and for a half-second the warmth in her face was simply *attention*, pure and total and slightly terrifying, the way a lighthouse is terrifying to something small in the water. "I admire that. Most people can't be trusted to remember their own phone numbers. You've built a memory for a whole town." She poured the beans into the pot. "Do you ever throw anything away?"

The question landed with a weight that had no business belonging to it. Ada kept her voice easy. "Paper's cheap. Throwing things away is expensive, if you ever need them back."

"Mm." Corliss smiled at the coffee pot. "Aldous used to say the opposite. He said the past was a debt and the only good debt was a paid one. He was a closer-out, my husband. Closets, accounts, arguments. He could end a thing like snapping a thread." She brought the tea over — a sweating glass, lemon, mint from the windowsill — and sat down across from Ada, folding her hands. "But you didn't come here to hear an old widow talk about her dead husband."

"Actually," Ada said, "I sort of did."

Corliss's eyebrows rose, amused, inviting.

"The Anniversary Dinner. The Voss family. Twelve years." Ada turned her glass a quarter-turn on the table, watching the condensation ring. "You've organized it every year. Most people would have stopped by now. Grief anniversaries usually fade."

"Grief anniversaries." Corliss tasted the phrase. "Is that what you call them, in your ledger? Under G?"

"Under V. I file by family."

Something flickered across Corliss's face — so fast Ada would have missed it if she hadn't spent a decade reading faces through a radio, catching the tremor in a voice before the voice itself knew it was there. It was not guilt. It was something more like a card player watching the deck being cut.

"By family," Corliss repeated. "That's a lovely system. And a dangerous one, in a town like this, where every family has a chapter they'd rather you skipped."

"Mostly people skip their own chapters. I don't write them anyway. I just log what happened."

"What happened." Corliss rose, went to the stove, poured the coffee — she'd made it for herself, Ada realized, a ritual, a prop — and stood with her back turned, watching the dark stream fill the cup. "You know, when the committee asked you to handle the transport, I argued for you. Did they tell you? Brandt wanted one of his own men driving the Voss family around. I said absolutely not. I said, give it to the girl who never loses a fare, who never forgets a name, who has driven half this town home drunk and never once made them feel small about it in the morning." She turned around, cup in hand, and her smile was the sun coming out. "I said Ada Oyelaran is the most discreet person in Marrow Creek, and I've never even met her. Isn't that funny? I recommended a stranger on pure reputation."

"It's the job," Ada said again, and heard how thin it sounded the second time.

"It is the job." Corliss sat back down. She wrapped both hands around the coffee cup but didn't drink. "And here's the thing about a job like yours, Ada. Here's the thing I've learned at my age, which I offer free of charge, worth every penny." She leaned forward, and the kitchen light caught the reading glasses in her hair and made them glint. "A record is a mercy. That's what nobody tells you. People think keeping accounts is cold, but it isn't — it's *kind*, because a thing written down can finally be put down. Carried. Set on a shelf. The people who suffer are the ones who can't file. Who have to keep it all in the body, in the head, in the small hours." Her thumb moved along the cup's rim, a slow circle. "The rest is filed where you'd file it."

The kitchen went very quiet. The refrigerator hummed. Out in the dark, a car passed on Knoll Street, headlights sweeping the ceiling like slow searchlights.

Ada did not move. Every instinct she had — ten years of nights, ten years of keeping her face a closed door — told her not to move, not to blink, not to let one muscle report back to the woman across the table.

Because that was the sentence. *Verbatim.* The second letter, the one with no signature, no confession, just that single line in typewriter print: *the rest is filed where you'd file it.* She had read it perhaps two hundred times since it arrived. She had it memorized down to the punctuation. And Corliss Havel had just said it aloud, in her own kitchen, over her own coffee, as if it were a proverb her grandmother had stitched on a sampler.

"Sorry?" Ada said. "Where you'd file what?"

Corliss blinked at her — and then she laughed, waving her hand, and the whole moment dissolved like sugar in tea. "My phrase, not yours. Aldous's, really. Whenever something was settled — a debt, a dispute, some ugly business at the mill — he'd say, *that's filed where you'd file it*, meaning it was done, meaning don't dig it up. I say it at every committee meeting. Ask anyone. It's practically the Havel family motto." She finally drank her coffee and made a face of genuine pleasure. "Oh, that *is* good. You've ruined me for the diner swill forever."

"Family motto," Ada said, smiling. "Every family should have one."

"Most families' mottos would fit better on a warning label." Corliss was standing again, clearing plates that weren't dirty, moving through her kitchen with the easy authority of a woman who had run this town's heart for fifty years and saw no reason to stop. "Now. The dinner. Let's talk about the dinner, since I've kept you."

They talked about the dinner. Corliss wanted the Voss family's car to arrive at 6:40, before the guests, so they wouldn't have to walk through a gauntlet of well-wishers. She wanted a separate exit route planned, "in case Nadia finds the evening harder than she expects — grief is weather, Ada, it changes without notice." She wanted the drivers in dark jackets, no company logos, "because this is a family evening, not an advertisement." Every instruction was precise, generous, thoughtful. Every instruction was also, Ada noted, a way of controlling exactly who moved through that hall, and when, and who saw them.

At the door, Corliss pressed a jar of her famous preserves into Ada's hands — blackberry, her own cane — and held both of Ada's hands around it for a moment longer than the jar required.

"I'm glad we finally met," she said. "I feel like I know you already, you know. From your reputation. From your *records*." A small smile. "You take care of this town's nights, Ada. It matters more than people know. Somebody has to write down what actually happened, or the town writes it down wrong, and then we're all living in a lie with good penmanship."

"Is that a Havel motto too?"

"That one's mine." She released Ada's hands. "Drive safe going home. It's a dark road out there past the hills."

*Past the hills.* The reservoir road. Ada kept the smile on her face all the way down the porch steps, all the way across the shell drive, all the way through the ritual of starting the car, checking the mirror, logging herself — *personal, 8:41 p.m., destination home* — with fingers that were only slightly unsteady.

* * *

She didn't go home.

She drove to the dispatch office, let herself in with her own key, and sat at the switchboard with the second letter flat on the desk under the green lamp. She took out her notebook — not the ledger, the private one — and wrote the sentence twice, once as it appeared in the letter, once as Corliss had said it, and set them side by side.

*The rest is filed where you'd file it.*

*The rest is filed where you'd file it.*

Identical. Word for word. And Corliss's explanation — Aldous's phrase, the committee motto, *ask anyone* — was exactly the kind of explanation that couldn't be checked without asking the whole town, and the whole town would confirm it, because the whole town had been hearing Corliss Havel say charming things for fifty years. A perfect alibi built out of small talk. If the phrase was a motto, it proved nothing. If it wasn't — if it was a slip, a taunt, a woman testing how close the record-keeper had come — then Corliss Havel had just told her, over blackberry preserves and hand-ground coffee, *I know what's in your mail.*

Ada sat back. The lamp hummed. Somewhere out on Route 9, Ozzie's cab reported in, his voice gravel and static: *Dispatch, 12, clear at the junction, nothing moving.*

"Copy, 12," she said, and her voice was steady, and her hands were not.

Because here was the thing she kept circling, the thing with teeth: the letters had said *where you'd file it*, and Ada had assumed that meant her ledger, her system, her decade of green books. But Corliss's version — Aldous's version — meant something older and colder. *Filed* as in *finished*. *Filed* as in *closed, paid, buried*. A debt settled so completely that even the memory of the debt could be retired.

Two meanings. One sentence. And a woman who used it like a coin she'd carried so long she'd forgotten which pocket it came from.

Ada opened the private notebook to a fresh page and wrote, in her smallest, neatest hand, the column she'd been avoiding for a week:

*Corliss Havel. Knew the letter's phrasing. Controls the dinner. Controls the exits. Recommended me — why? Watched me all evening the way you watch weather.*

She looked at the column for a long time. Then, beneath it, she added the entry that frightened her most, because it was the one that made the least sense and therefore the most:

*She was kind to me. It didn't feel like a performance. That's what scares me.*

The radio crackled. Renata this time, calling in her last fare of the night, her soft accented voice warm as bread: *Dispatch, 7, dropping at Elm and Third, then heading in.*

"Copy, 7. Good night, Renata."

Ada closed the notebook. Outside, the town lay dark and orderly, every house filed where it belonged. And in four days, all of it — the Havels, the Vosses, the drivers, the sheriff, the whole careful architecture of Marrow Creek's good penmanship — would be gathered in one hall with their glasses raised.

She was going to need to know, before then, whether the woman who'd poured her coffee was the writer of the letters — or the thing the letters were written about.

There was one person who might know which. The one who'd gone pale at the mention of them, who'd begged her to let it go, who'd walked out of the room rather than say why.

Ada reached for the radio, then stopped. Some conversations you didn't have over the air.

She picked up the phone instead and dialed the number Teo had given her three years ago, *for emergencies*, and listened to it ring in the dark of the empty office, once, twice — praying he'd answer, and more afraid of what it would mean if he did.
