## 25. Renata Breaks

I didn't go to Renata's right away. I sat in the dispatch office for two hours after the handwriting analysis, the looped descenders of her memos fanned out across my desk like a hand of losing cards, and I tried to build another explanation out of them. A forgery. A coincidence. Twelve years of work orders written by someone who happened to curl their y-tails the same way a person curls them when they've spent forty years writing addresses on carbon slips.

There was no other explanation. There was only the drive across town, past the shuttered feed store, past the church where Danny Voss had never had a funeral because the Havels had paid for a memorial instead — quieter, cleaner, no body required — to the little yellow house on Delacroix Lane where Renata had lived since before I was hired, with its plastic-covered couch and its statue of the Virgin in the front window and its porch light that never went off, because Renata said a light left burning was a promise kept.

I knocked. I heard her slippers on the linoleum, slow, unhurried, the way she moved through every crisis she'd ever absorbed — Ozzie's rages, Teo's panic attacks, my own first winter here when I cried in the break room and she'd held my head against her shoulder like I was something worth keeping.

She opened the door already holding two cups of coffee.

"Sit down, mija," she said. "You look like you drove here angry."

"I drove here knowing," I said.

Something moved behind her eyes — not surprise. Relief, maybe, arriving late to its own funeral. She stepped back and let me in.

* * *

Her kitchen smelled of cinnamon and dish soap and the faint mineral smell of holy water. The rosary was on the table. Not put away — laid out, deliberate, each bead aligned along the wood grain like a sentence waiting to be read. And beside it, small and tarnished and impossible, a bus token.

Zone Six. The reservoir route. Discontinued eleven years ago, when the county rerouted the line after the drowning made the road famous in the wrong way.

I sat down across from it and didn't touch it. "You kept it."

"Twelve years." She set my coffee down and folded her hands around her own cup, and I watched her knuckles whiten around the ceramic the way they used to whiten around the wheel whenever we passed the reservoir turnoff, all these years, and I had thought it was grief, general and unassigned, the way drivers grieve roads. It wasn't general. It had an address. It had always had an address.

"You wrote the letters," I said.

"Yes."

Not *I don't know what you mean.* Not *you're tired, mija, drink your coffee.* Just yes, plain as a fare logged at 2:14 a.m., and hearing it out loud did something to the room — took the air out of it, or put too much in. I gripped my cup so my hands would have a job.

"Why?"

"Because I am dying of it, Ada." She said it without drama, the way she'd report a flat tire. "Twelve years is a long time to carry a thing in your chest. You get so you can't breathe around it anymore. You get so you pray and the prayers come out wrong — not asking forgiveness, just asking *please, somebody else say it first.*" Her thumb moved over the rim of her cup. "So I said it first. In pieces. To people who might be able to do something with the pieces."

"To me. To Brandt. To Nadia."

"To whoever God put in the path of the mail truck." Almost a smile. It died fast. "I couldn't say it whole. Whole was too heavy. But pieces — pieces I could carry one at a time to the mailbox."

"And the cipher. The phrasing. 'Filed where you'd file it.' You used my own system."

"I used the only language I trusted." She looked at me then, full-on, and her eyes were dry but red-rimmed, the eyes of a woman who has done her crying in private and budgeted for it. "You think I don't know what you built in those green books? Ten years I watched you write everything down — every drunk, every lie, every midnight confession passengers think the driver can't hear. You made this town honest on paper if nowhere else. I figured if anyone could read what I sent, it was you. If anyone deserved to be the one—" Her voice caught, repaired itself. "If anyone should have been told, it was the keeper of the record."

"The record was edited," I said. "Page 214 is gone. The tape was erased. Did you know that?"

"I knew some things were taken out." Her jaw tightened. "I didn't know who took them until recently. That's not my sin. My sin is what I saw and what I did with seeing it."

The clock over her stove ticked. Somewhere outside a dog barked twice and gave up. I realized I was leaning forward like a passenger toward a driver taking a curve too fast.

"Tell me," I said. "All of it. Start wherever you need to start."

* * *

She started with the rain.

"It rained that night like the sky had a grudge. October twelfth. I remember because it was my sister's birthday and I'd missed her call. I picked up Mrs. Havel from the country club — nine-forty, maybe quarter to ten. She'd been drinking but she carried it well, she always did, back straight, voice level, tipping too much the way guilty people tip." Renata's hands had gone still around her cup entirely now. "She asked me to take the long way home. She said she liked the water at night. I thought nothing of it. Rich women ask for pretty routes."

"But she wasn't going home."

"Not yet." Renata closed her eyes. "We came up the reservoir road and there was a car pulled off near the boat launch, hazards on, and there were two men standing by the rail arguing. One of them I knew right away — Mr. Aldous. He was our fare sometimes, he was good for the company, everyone kissed the ground he walked on. And the other one was younger. Soaked through. Drenched like he'd been in the water and climbed out, and he was shouting. Even through the rain I could hear him shouting about *papers,* about *what his father signed,* about how he was done being quiet. And Mr. Aldous had him by the arm, and Mrs. Havel—"

She stopped. Opened her eyes. Looked at the Virgin in the window as if checking whether she was still permitted to continue.

"Mrs. Havel got out of the car," she said. "In the rain. In her good coat. And she walked toward them and she said something I couldn't hear, and the boy turned to her, and then—" Renata's breath went ragged. "Then there was a shove. Or a slip. I have prayed twelve years to know which, mija, and God has not seen fit to tell me. The boy went back. Over the rail. Into the water. And the sound—" Her hand rose to her throat. "The sound the water made. Like a door closing."

"What did you do?"

"What any coward does." No self-pity in it; she stated it like a fact of geology. "I turned the car around. I drove her home by the short way and I never said a word and she paid me triple and looked at me — one long look, in the mirror, in the porch light — and I understood that look meant *you saw nothing.* And I have been seeing nothing ever since. Every Sunday. Every rosary. Every time Ozzie tells his story about the night the reservoir ate a boy, I sit there and I see nothing so hard I could scream."

"Danny Voss was alive," I said. My voice sounded far away to me, like dispatch audio with the gain turned down. "He climbed out. He was alive, and arguing, and then—"

"He was alive." She nodded slowly. "And three days later the sheriff said drowned, drunk, accident, case closed, and the town sighed with relief because the alternative was thinking badly of the family that fed half of us, and I told myself maybe I imagined the rain, maybe I imagined the boy, maybe God would handle it." She laughed — a terrible small sound. "God handles things slowly, Ada. So finally I started mailing pieces of it myself."

"The letters weren't a confession," I said slowly, working it through. "They were a summons. You weren't confessing — you were deputizing."

"I'm an old woman with a clean driving record and an accent people pretend not to notice. Who would listen to me?" She spread her hands. "But you — you with your books. And Nadia Voss, who has been sharpening her grief into a knife for twelve years. And even Sheriff Brandt — I sent him one too, God forgive me, to watch him flinch. He called me the next day, very kind, very concerned about my health, suggested I might be getting confused in my old age." Her mouth flattened. "Confused. After thirty-eight years of driving this town's nights without one lost fare."

I thought of Brandt's office, his warning, his knowledge of the letters. It fit now. All of it fit.

"There's something else," Renata said.

She stood, went to the sideboard, the old walnut one that smelled of lemon polish and decades, and opened the bottom drawer. When she turned back she was holding a key. Brass, old-fashioned, with a paper tag gone soft and yellow at the edges, tied with fishing line.

"This came to me eight months ago. No letter. Just this, in an envelope with no return address, the morning after Mr. Aldous's anniversary Mass." She set it on the table between us, next to the bus token, next to the rosary. "I never used it. I was too afraid. I prayed over it instead, which is what I do instead of being brave." She pushed it toward me with one finger, gently, the way you'd push a life raft toward someone in the water. "It's the boathouse. The Havel boathouse. I drove Mrs. Havel there once more, that spring, after — she had me wait in the car while she went inside for an hour, and when she came out her shoes were wet and she had dirt on her knees, and she never asked me to drive her anywhere private again."

My heart was doing something arrhythmic. "Why give it to me now? Why not burn it?"

"Because you came to my door knowing," she said simply. "Because the letters were me whispering *please* into the dark for a year, and you're the first person who walked toward the sound instead of away from it." She reached across the table and covered my hand with hers, warm and dry and trembling slightly, the hands that had steadied me through blizzards and breakdowns and my own worst nights. "I didn't kill that boy, Ada. I want you to hear it from my mouth looking at yours: I did not kill him. But I watched him die and I said nothing, and I wrote letters instead of testimony because I am a coward with good penmanship. Whatever happens next — whatever you find behind that lock — I will stand up in front of this whole town and say it out loud. At the dinner, if you need me to. In front of her. I'm done dying quietly."

For a moment neither of us moved. Then I turned my hand over and gripped hers, hard, the way you grip a rope.

"You're not a coward," I said. "You're the last witness standing."

"No, mija." She shook her head, and for the first time all night her composure cracked — not into tears but into something rawer, a nakedness. "I'm the second-to-last. Because whatever is in that boathouse, Corliss Havel has had twelve years to make sure it can't testify. And she knows her letters found their way home." Her grip tightened on mine. "Ada — the day I mailed the third letter, someone followed the mail truck. I saw the car. Dark sedan, county plates. Somebody out there knows exactly how much I've said, and to whom."

* * *

I drove back across town with the key in my jacket pocket, pressed against my ribs like a second heartbeat. The rain had started again — light, indifferent, the same weather twelve years late. At a stoplight on Delacroix I checked my mirror out of pure dispatcher habit.

A dark sedan idled half a block back, headlights off, engine running.

When the light changed, I turned left instead of right. It didn't follow. It just sat there, patient as a ledger, waiting to see where I'd file myself.

The boathouse key burned against my side the whole way home.
