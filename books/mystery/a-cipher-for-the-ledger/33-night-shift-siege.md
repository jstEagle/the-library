## 33. Night Shift Siege

The decision came to her at 4 a.m., three days before the dinner, in the particular silence that follows a bad phone call.

Nadia had called from the motel — the landline at the front desk patched through to the switchboard, because Nadia Voss did not trust cell phones and never had, a trait Ada was beginning to understand ran in the family like eye color. *Someone tried my door last night,* she said. No fear in it. Just fact, laid on the counter between them like a receipt. *Card slid along the frame. I keep a matchstick in the jamb. It was on the floor this morning.*

Ada sat with the receiver against her ear and looked down the length of the dispatch office — the green ledger rack against the far wall, forty volumes standing spine-out like books in a church pew, twelve years of Marrow Creek's nights filed in her own handwriting. Forty books. Every fare. Every zone code. Every reroute slip stapled where she'd stapled them, every notation in the margins where she'd written what the forms didn't have room for.

*They're not after you,* she said. *They're after the paper.*

*Then we should be somewhere the paper isn't,* Nadia said, and hung up before Ada could answer, which Ada was learning was simply how Nadia ended conversations — like cutting a rope rather than untying it.

Ada sat for a long time in the blue glow of the switchboard. The town clock struck four. Somewhere out on Route 9, Ozzie's cab ticked past with a fare he'd picked up at the hospital, and she knew that without looking because she always knew, the way she knew rain coming off the reservoir by the smell of the vents.

Forty books. She had spent ten years believing they were hers — her ritual, her order, her quiet cathedral of other people's secrets. But a ledger kept in the place where the crime happened wasn't a record. It was a hostage.

She stood up, took her coat off the hook, and called Renata.

* * *

Moving forty ledgers takes longer than you'd think, if you do it right.

Ada did it right. She didn't use boxes — boxes got noticed, carried through doorways, seen under streetlights. She used the canvas dispatch bags, the heavy ones with the shoulder straps that drivers used to carry coin rolls back when there were coin rolls, and she moved the books in batches of six over four nights, walking them out the back door of the station one armload at a time while Teo covered the board and logged phantom fares to explain any gap in the tape.

*What do I write?* Teo asked the first night, pen hovering, hands still not steady since the locker, since the tape, since everything.

Ada thought about it. Ten years of codes and shorthand, initials and zone numbers, a whole private language built so the truth could hide inside the routine — and now here she was, needing one more lie to protect the truth, and finding she hated the taste of it more than she could say.

*Write what actually happened,* she said. *Fare 5102. Pickup: dispatch office. Drop: the Voss motel. Passenger: A. Oyelaran. Freight: forty items, miscellaneous.*

Teo wrote it exactly like that, word for word, and when he finished he looked at the entry for a moment and something almost like a smile crossed his face. *That's the most honest thing anyone's ever put in one of those books.*

*Don't get used to it,* Ada said, and shouldered the bag.

She carried them to Nadia's room at the Blue Spruce Motel two at a time across those four nights, past the ice machine humming its one note, past the window where Nadia sat waiting with the lamp turned low, counting each volume onto the bed like a nurse counting instruments. By Thursday morning all forty were stacked in the closet behind Nadia's winter coat, wrapped in the motel's scratchy towels, and the rack in the dispatch office held nothing but air and the ghost-smell of old paper.

Ada left the empty rack exactly as it was. Spines out. Nothing behind them.

If they were going to come, let them come to an altar with no god in it.

* * *

They came on Friday night, during the dinner rehearsal, which told Ada everything about how well Corliss Havel still knew this town — strike when everyone who matters is watching something else.

Ada was at the community hall, running transport logistics for Corliss's committee, holding a clipboard she hadn't asked for and couldn't seem to put down. Corliss stood at the head table rehearsing her toast in a voice pitched for the back row, warm as a hearth and twice as controlled. *To memory,* she said to the empty chairs, *and to mercy, which is just memory that has learned forgiveness.* The caterers nodded as though this were normal dinner-table talk. Maybe in Marrow Creek it was.

Renata found Ada by the coffee urn. She didn't say anything. She just touched Ada's elbow — twice, quick, the signal they'd used for years when a driver needed the dispatcher's attention without the whole room hearing it — and walked toward the doors.

In the parking lot, under the buzzing sodium light, Renata said only: *Ozzie drove past the station. The back door's open. He says don't go alone.*

Ada's heart did not speed up. That was the strange part. Ten years of night shifts had trained something in her to go flat and clear at exactly the moments when a normal person would panic, and she felt that flatness settle over her now like a coat.

*Get Teo,* she said. *And call Nadia. Tell her to stay in the room with the chain on.*

*Ada—*

*Tell her the books are safe. That's the important part. Everything else is just mess.*

* * *

The dispatch office looked like it had been hit by weather.

Not ransacked — that was the first thing Ada registered, standing in the doorway with Teo beside her and Ozzie filling the frame behind them both, his big hands opening and closing at his sides. Weather. A storm had come through looking for something specific and left the general wreckage of its search behind. Drawers pulled and upended, their contents fanned across the floor in bright plastic drifts — pens, rubber bands, the tin of spare keys, the takeout menus taped inside the cabinet doors. The filing cabinet in the corner stood open, hanging folders gutted and strewn. The archive shelf in the basement, when Ozzie went down to check, had been swept clean of its dispatch tapes — every reel from 2011 to 2013, gone, though the tapes from other years hung untouched in their sleeves, which told Ada the visitors had known precisely which years mattered.

And the ledger rack. Someone had pulled every book off it and thrown them across the floor, forty hollow spines scattered like shed skins, and then — this was the detail Ada would turn over for days afterward — someone had gone through them anyway. All of them. Page by page, or fast enough to be thorough about the obvious dates, checking, checking, finding nothing, because there was nothing to find.

*They tore apart forty empty books,* Teo said. His voice had a thin, disbelieving edge. *For hours, maybe. They went through every single one.*

*No,* Ada said.

She crouched and picked up Volume 12 — the current book, the one with the honest entry about Fare 5102 sitting in it in Teo's handwriting. She turned pages until she found it. Still there. Of course still there; nobody reads a log entry about miscellaneous freight.

*I mean they weren't hours.* She set the book down gently. *Look at the dust on the windowsill. Look at the coffee cup.* There was a paper cup on the desk, half-full, cold, no lid. *They were here once, fast, and they were calm about it. This is Brandt's people or people who move like Brandt's people. They knew the rack would be the first place anyone would look, so they made sure it was the first place they looked too.* She stood, brushing dust from her knees. *Which means whoever sent them doesn't know the books are gone. Which means we have a little time before they figure out that tearing this place apart bought them nothing.*

Ozzie had gone very still by the door. He was looking at the room the way he looked at accident scenes on the highway — total attention, zero expression, the professional calm of a man who had scraped people off asphalt for thirty years and learned that screaming helps nobody.

*Your back door lock's been shimmed,* he said finally. *Old trick. Credit card, stiff one. Somebody taught me that trick in 1987 and I've never once used it on a friend.* He let that sit in the room. *You want to tell me who we're at war with, girl? Or you want me to keep pretending I don't already know?*

Ada looked at him. Twelve years of deflection lived between them — the war stories, the coffee, the eyes that always drifted to the ledger rack. And underneath it, she understood suddenly, all of it had been this: a man standing guard over a secret that was eating him alive, waiting for someone to give him permission to put it down.

*Tomorrow,* she said. *I'll tell you everything tomorrow, Ozzie. I promise. Tonight I need you to do one thing.* She handed him the clipboard list from the hall. *The dinner. Saturday. I need you driving the committee cars, and I need you sober, and I need you at that hall no matter what happens, even if — especially if — somebody tells you not to be.*

*Somebody will tell me not to be,* Ozzie said.

*I know,* Ada said. *That's how I'll know it matters.*

* * *

They cleaned up together, the three of them, working in the kind of silence that families fall into without deciding to — Teo restocking drawers, Renata sweeping, Ozzie fitting a new bolt to the back door, a real one, the kind you'd need a drill to beat. Ada re-shelved the empty volumes one by one, spine out, in perfect order, and found she was almost smiling as she did it.

Let them come again. Let them tear the room apart a second time and find the same beautiful nothing. The books were sleeping behind a winter coat at the Blue Spruce, wrapped in motel towels, and every hour that passed without the Havels knowing was an hour the truth got to breathe.

It was nearly midnight when the switchboard lit up.

She picked up on the first ring, expecting a fare, expecting Nadia, expecting Brandt's flat voice telling her to stay away from grief. Instead there was only breathing, and then a voice she knew — soft, accented, careful — though not the voice she'd expected it to be.

*Miss Oyelaran.* A pause, long enough that she heard a television playing somewhere behind it, low, some late movie. *This is Miles Havel.*

Ada sat down slowly at the board. *Mr. Havel.*

*My mother doesn't know I'm calling.* Another pause. In the background the TV murmured on, and Ada realized he'd probably had it on the whole time, the way lonely men do, for company. *I've been reading about myself in your ledger, I think. My sister told me — no. Never mind how I know. I'm calling because tonight two men came to my apartment asking whether I'd been in touch with anyone in Marrow Creek. They said they were insurance investigators.* A dry sound that might have been a laugh. *My rehab facility closed its file on me eight months ago. Nobody investigates anything about me anymore. Except apparently someone does.*

Ada's hand tightened on the receiver. *What did you tell them?*

*I told them what I tell everyone. That I don't know anything about Danny Voss except what the town decided, and the town decided it was an accident.* The TV laughed at something in the next room. *But here's the thing, Miss Oyelaran. Here's why I'm calling at midnight like a crazy man. Those men weren't insurance investigators. One of them had a sheriff's department haircut and the other one had my mother's manner. You know the manner. The one where she asks you a question and somehow you end up apologizing.*

*What do you want, Mr. Havel?*

A long silence. When he spoke again, the drawl of drink was gone from his voice and what was underneath sounded young and scared and tired of being both.

*Twelve years ago I checked into St. Alban's the same week Danny Voss drowned, and everyone in this family has treated that like a favor I did them instead of an alibi they needed. And I have spent twelve years not asking why my mother drove me there herself, personally, at two in the morning, when she could have sent the driver.* The pause stretched. *I'm coming to the dinner Saturday. I haven't been back since the funeral. And when I get there, Miss Oyelaran, I'd like to stand next to whoever in that room is finally going to tell the truth — because I'm done being the alibi. I was never the alibi. I was the decoy.*

The line clicked dead before Ada could answer.

She sat in the blue glow of the switchboard, the cleaned-up office quiet around her, the empty rack standing at attention against the wall, and outside the window the town lay dark and settled under its streetlights, rehearsing its toast, polishing its glasses, closing its ranks.

One day left.

She opened the current ledger, found Fare 5102, and beneath it, in her own hand, added a single new line:

*Passenger count revised. Two.*
