## 35. Anniversary Eve

By four o'clock the light had gone the color of old brass, and Marrow Creek was dressing itself the way it always did for the Havels — slowly, carefully, with everyone pretending they hadn't been asked.

Ada watched it happen from the dispatch window while she pretended to do inventory. Down the block, Denny Pruitt was on a ladder outside the feed store, stringing bunting in white and gold. The hardware store had polished its plate glass until the street reflected in it like a second, cleaner town. Somebody had swept the gutter in front of the First Mercantile twice; she'd seen the same man do it both times, working the broom like a rosary. Even the flag over the courthouse had been lowered and re-hung so it hung straighter, as if a crooked flag after twelve years would be an accusation.

The Voss-Havel Anniversary Dinner. Twelve years of generosity, twelve years of memory, one long table under canvas at the grange hall with place cards printed by the printer whose mortgage Corliss Havel had quietly bought down to nothing in '09. Ada knew that because it was in the ledger. Volume thirty-one, page 88: a fare from the bank to the printer's back door, cash, passenger unnamed, driver Ozzie, notation in her own hand — *heavy envelope.*

Everything was in the ledger. That had stopped feeling like comfort around the time she realized it also meant everything was evidence.

She logged the thought the way she logged everything now, in the fresh green book she kept on her knee like a shield: *4:12 p.m. — town dressing its wounds with ribbon.*

* * *

At five, Renata came in early for her shift, which meant she hadn't slept. Her hair was pinned with unusual care, and she was wearing the good cardigan, the navy one with the pearl buttons she saved for funerals and citizenship appointments.

"You're going tonight," Ada said. Not a question.

"I'm going." Renata set a foil-covered dish on the counter — pierogi, still warm, the smell of butter and dill filling the office like an apology. "Corliss asked me personally. Asked me to help serve." She smoothed the cardigan over her stomach, a gesture Ada had seen her make a thousand times, usually before saying something that cost her. "Twelve years I've driven that woman home from things. She likes a familiar face holding the door."

"Renata."

"I know what you're going to say."

"Do you?" Ada came around the counter. Up close she could see the tremor in Renata's hands, fine as a moth's wings. "Because I'm going to stand up in front of three hundred people tonight and read out of these books. And when I get to the part about the reservoir road, you're going to be standing next to her in your good cardigan, and I need to know —"

"What? What do you need to know, *detka*?" Renata's voice cracked open, gentle and terrible. "You need to know if I'll faint? I won't. You need to know if I'll run? Where would I run, in this town, from this?" She touched the ledger rack with two fingers, the way she touched the medal of St. Christopher hanging from her rearview mirror. "I have been dying for twelve years in small installments. Tonight the installments end. Whatever you read — I will stand there and I will let it be true out loud. That is all I have left to give anyone. Let it be true out loud."

She picked up her keys and went out to the car, and through the window Ada watched her sit behind the wheel for a full minute with her forehead against her knuckles before she started the engine.

* * *

Ozzie arrived at six with his dress shirt buttoned wrong, one collar point folded under, and didn't fix it when Ada pointed at it. He looked older than he had a week ago, or maybe he'd looked this old for years and only now stopped performing younger.

"Cars are gassed," he said. "All six. Spare's got a full tank too, in case somebody needs to leave fast." He said it looking at the wall, not at her.

"Somebody might."

"Yeah." He lowered himself onto the bench by the coat hooks, the one with the springs gone soft, and put his cap on his knee like a man visiting a grave. "I did a thing today you should know about."

Ada waited. With Ozzie you learned to wait; he talked the way wells filled, slow and from far down.

"Went to see Brandt this afternoon. Told him I'd been driving forty-one years and I could smell a setup from the cab of a moving vehicle, and his little arrangement — the pension, the quiet, the whole package — smelled like one." His jaw worked. "He told me I was an old man who didn't understand how things stayed standing. I told him things don't stay standing. They just haven't fallen yet."

"And?"

"And then I drove around for two hours because my hands were shaking too bad to come here first." He finally looked up. "I'm not going to tell you not to do it. I want you to know that. Forty-one years, and the one thing I never did was tell anybody not to do the right thing. I just made sure I wasn't in the room when they decided." He stood, put the cap back on, straightened the wrong-buttoned shirt with a tug like he was starting a mower. "Tonight I'm going to be in the room."

He went out. The bell over the door gave its small cheap ring, and Ada wrote in the green book: *6:04 p.m. — Ozzie, in the room.*

* * *

Teo came last, after dark, and he didn't come alone in any way that counted.

He'd shaved. He wore a tie. He stood in the doorway of the dispatch office holding a garment bag with his one good suit in it, and he looked like a man reporting for a sentencing he'd requested himself.

"I checked the sound system at the grange," he said. "Nadia's guy ran a line to the hall speakers off the portable deck. It works. I tested it with a cassette of my mom's church choir." He swallowed. "It'll carry to the back row."

"Teo."

"If Brandt's people cut power, there's a battery pack. If they take the deck, Nadia has a duplicate tape in her room. If they take *you*—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "If they take you, I take the deck and walk to the middle of the floor and press play myself. I've been practicing what I'd say. It's two sentences. I can remember two sentences."

Ada looked at him — twenty-six years old, hired at nineteen because he was the only applicant who could alphabetize without being asked, the kid she'd taught to log fares in her own shorthand, the trainee who'd erased a tape because a sheriff told him he was closing a wound — and felt something in her chest go very still and very clear.

"Two sentences," she said.

"'Marrow Creek,'" Teo recited, eyes shut, "'this is what happened while you were sleeping.'" He opened his eyes. "That's sentence one. Sentence two is just the date. October ninth. People keep asking for a date like it's a thing you can argue with."

* * *

The grange hall at nine o'clock was a lantern seen from across water.

Ada stood at the edge of the parking lot with the ledgers in two canvas book-bags, forty volumes weighing maybe sixty pounds, heavy enough that carrying them felt less like smuggling and more like hauling ballast. String lights ran along the eaves. Inside, through the tall windows, she could see the tables dressed in white, the folding chairs with gold ribbon on the backs, the banner over the stage — TWELVE YEARS OF GIVING — in letters that had been painted by hand, badly, by volunteers, which somehow made it worse.

Brandt had stationed officers at the doors. Two of them, visible, in uniform, flanking the entrance like the hall was a courtroom or a bank. A third stood by the fire exit smoking, and a fourth — she was nearly sure — sat in an unmarked sedan at the lot's edge with its engine running and its dome light off. Brandt himself was inside somewhere; she'd caught the gray brim of his hat moving past a window. He wasn't hiding the security. He wanted it seen. *Nothing happens here tonight,* the uniforms said. *This is a party.*

Ada stood in the cold and let herself look at it honestly, the way she logged a fare she didn't like: the truth of it, no codes.

They were expecting a fight. Brandt had built his whole evening around intercepting a fight — a leak to the press, a handoff to a state investigator, a dramatic arrival with documents. Every officer at every door was placed to stop paper from getting in or out.

Which meant the one thing none of them could stop was a woman with a book, standing up, using her voice.

She'd turned it over all day, turning it like a stone, checking underneath. Call the *Gazette*? The Gazette's editor played poker with Brandt. Call the county prosecutor? Corliss Havel had chaired the man's last fundraiser; Ada had the fare log to prove it. Any channel with a gatekeeper was a channel Corliss owned or rented. Twelve years of buying silence buys the post offices too.

But a room — a room full of people who had eaten the pierogi and drunk the wine and shaken the widow's hand and let their children swim at the reservoir — a room couldn't be un-heard. You couldn't confiscate three hundred memories. Nadia had said it first, weeks ago, brittle and beautiful in the motel room with the ledgers stacked on the bedspread: *"My brother drowned in front of this whole town and nobody saw it. So make them see it. Make it impossible to un-see."*

No press. No sheriff. No leak. Just the reading.

Her hands weren't shaking. She noticed that with a kind of distant surprise, the way you notice weather. For ten years her hands had shaken over late drivers and missed pickups and the small nightly fear of being alone in a building full of other people's secrets. Tonight they were steady as a ruled line.

She walked in through the front door, past the officers, past the sign-in table where a volunteer pressed a program into her hands, and she gave her name to the girl with the clipboard — "Ada Oyelaran, transport coordinator" — and watched the girl write it down without knowing she was logging the defendant's witness into the record.

* * *

Corliss was rehearsing.

Ada found her at the front of the hall near the stage, one hand resting on the podium, murmuring to a half-circle of committee women with their programs held like hymnals. She wore deep green silk and her mother's pearls, and her voice, even at rehearsal volume, had the trained warmth of a woman who had been the town's hearth for fifty years.

"—and so we remember not only what Aldous built, but what Danny—" a pause, delicately managed, eyes briefly down, "—what Danny's loss taught us all about holding one another close. Memory, and mercy. Those are the two words I ask you to carry home tonight. Memory, and mercy."

The committee women murmured approval. One dabbed her eye already, pre-grieving on schedule.

Corliss lifted her head and saw Ada across the hall, and her face did the thing it always did — opened like a door, warm light spilling out. "There's my dispatcher! Come here, child." She extended both hands, and Ada took them, and the pearls clicked softly as Corliss drew her close enough that the perfume — lavender and something older, camphor maybe, something kept in a closed drawer — settled over her like snowfall.

"Nervous?" Corliss asked, searching her face with those pale quick eyes. "Big night. Don't be nervous. All you have to do is stand where I point you and look competent, and heaven knows you always do." She patted Ada's cheek once, twice, the exact rhythm she'd used since Ada was twenty-two and new. Then, softer, almost tender, almost private: "And afterward, whatever anyone says, however the evening goes — remember what I told you. The rest is filed where you'd file it."

The hall went very quiet inside Ada's skull. The string lights hummed. Somewhere behind her a chair scraped.

"That's a strange thing to say," Ada heard herself answer, level as a dial tone.

"Is it?" Corliss smiled, released her hands, turned back to her podium. "It's just something an old woman says. Go check your cars, dear. Cold night for breakdowns."

Ada walked the length of the hall to the doors, past the officers, out into the dark, and only when she reached the book-bags did she let her breath go, fogging in the cold like a signal.

She'd rehearsed it too, all week, in the motel mirror with Nadia timing her: the order of entries, the pages flagged with ribbon, the places where her voice would need to slow down. But some part of her had still hoped, right up until the pearls clicked, that she'd walk in tonight and find a way to be wrong.

*Memory, and mercy.* Corliss had chosen her two words. Fine. Ada had hers.

They were dates, fare numbers, and page numbers, and she had them memorized.

* * *

Back at the station, the night shift assembled at eleven like a crew before a storm crossing. Ozzie in his wrong-buttoned shirt. Renata in her navy cardigan, dish returned empty, hands folded. Teo in his tie, the garment bag over his shoulder, the portable deck in a grocery sack at his feet. Nadia had driven in from the motel in a black dress that fit her like armor; she stood by the switchboard turning her mother's ring around and around on her finger.

Ada laid it out for them one last time, plain, no codes.

"I go in at seven with the books. Nobody else carries anything. At the toast, when Corliss raises her glass, I stand up. Teo, the moment I stand, you're at the sound table with the deck cued. If anyone touches you, you play it anyway. Ozzie — you're on the aisle, third row. If Brandt comes for me, you don't fight him. You just stand up. All six feet of you, forty-one years of this town trusting your face. Stand up and stay standing. Renata—"

Renata reached over and closed Ada's ledger gently with one hand, the way she'd close a hymnal. "I will be beside Corliss Havel when you read my name," she said. "And I will not look away from you. That is my whole assignment, and I have been rehearsing it for twelve years."

Nobody laughed. Nobody needed to.

Outside, the first guests were arriving at the grange — headlights swinging into the lot, doors thunking, voices bright with wine and occasion. The string lights glowed. The banner hung. The officers stood at their posts with their hats squared and their faces arranged into festival neutrality.

Ada picked up the book-bags, one in each hand, sixty pounds of ten years' truth, and felt the terrible calm settle over her like a coat that finally fit.

"One more entry," she said quietly — the third letter's promise, the writer's vow, *anniversary night* — and glanced at the clock above the switchboard. 11:47. Thirteen minutes to midnight, thirteen minutes to the day itself, twelve years exactly since a boy called for a cab that never came.

"Let's go give the town its entry."

She pushed open the door, and the cold rushed in, and somewhere across the dark lot the grange hall's doors swung wide to receive them — all of them, even the ones carrying the shatter.
