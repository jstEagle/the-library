## 37. Read Aloud

The Havel Grange Hall had been dressed for mercy. Garlands of dried wheat along the rafters, white cloths on every table, candles in jars that threw a soft amber light over three hundred people who had spent twelve years agreeing not to remember. Ada stood at the back with the green ledger pressed flat against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and she watched Corliss Havel work the room.

Corliss wore dove gray and pearls and the expression of a woman who had forgiven everyone everything, including themselves. She moved from table to table with both hands out — one for clasping, one for patting — and wherever she passed, faces softened. Sheriff Brandt stood near the doors in his dress uniform, two deputies flanking him, watching the crowd the way a man watches water rising around his boots.

Ada found Nadia Voss by the sound board at the hall's rear wall. Nadia was in black, her hair pinned up so severely it looked like armor, and she had positioned herself beside the auxiliary input with the casualness of someone who had spent an hour learning which cable did what.

"Corliss is on her third circuit," Nadia murmured without looking at her. "She's saving the toast for when the dessert plates clear. That's when they'll all be softest."

"Then that's when I go up." Ada's mouth was dry. She'd rehearsed this in the motel room forty times, ledger open on the bedspread, reading aloud to a woman who already knew every word. It hadn't helped. In the motel room there had been no pearls, no garlands, no three hundred neighbors holding wine glasses like small bright weapons.

Nadia's hand closed briefly on her wrist. "You don't have to be brave. You just have to be accurate. You're the most accurate person I've ever met."

"That's the nicest thing anyone's said to me, and it's terrifying."

Across the room, Ozzie sat at a corner table with Renata and Teo, the old family arranged together like survivors of something. Ozzie caught her eye and gave a single nod — slow, deliberate, the nod of a man who had chosen. Teo looked sick. Renata held a rosary wound through her fingers beneath the tablecloth, lips moving, and when Ada's gaze touched hers, Renata didn't look away. That was new. That was everything.

The dessert plates cleared. Corliss ascended the small stage with the unhurried grace of a woman who had been ascending things her whole life. She tapped the microphone once, smiled until the room hushed itself, and lifted her glass.

"Twelve years ago," she began, her voice warm as candlelight, "this town lost a boy, and I lost—" a pause, calibrated, "—a piece of my heart. The Voss family turned their grief into generosity, and Marrow Creek turned its sorrow into memory. So tonight we raise our glasses to Danny. To memory. And to mercy — because mercy is what holds a town together when the truth would tear it apart."

Glasses rose across the hall. Three hundred hands, three hundred small refusals to look down.

Ada walked toward the stage.

She felt the movement before she heard it — the ripple of heads turning, the parting of the crowd like water around a hull. Brandt saw her first. His jaw tightened, and he started forward from the door, but Ozzie was already standing, already moving, a big slow bear of a man planting himself in the aisle between the sheriff and the stage with his arms crossed and his face saying *you'll go through me*. Two other drivers drifted over without being asked. The family, choosing.

Ada stepped up onto the stage beside Corliss Havel.

"Miss Havel," she said, and her voice carried further than she expected, clean and steady in the microphone's reach. "Before you drink — could you ask the room to hold their glasses a moment? I have an entry to read."

A murmur went through the hall. Corliss's smile stayed exactly where it was, but Ada, who had spent ten years reading voices through static, heard what was underneath it: the fine high note of a wire beginning to sing.

"I'm sorry?" Corliss said pleasantly. "Who are you, dear?"

"My name is Ada Oyelaran. For ten years I've been the night dispatcher at Marrow Creek Taxi. Every fare this town has taken after dark is written in my ledgers. Forty-one volumes." She held the green book up. "I keep them because a record is a promise. What happened gets written down. Nobody gets erased."

"Is this appropriate?" Corliss asked the room, laughing lightly, inviting them into the joke. A few people laughed with her. Most didn't. They were looking at the ledger now, at the plain green cover, and something in the room had begun to tilt.

Brandt reached the edge of the stage. "That's enough. Miss Oyelaran, step down."

"No," said Nadia Voss, loudly, from the sound board. "Let her read. Or I start playing audio, and believe me, Sheriff, you'd rather hear her voice than his."

Brandt stopped. He looked at the sound board, at Nadia's steady hands on the cables, and Ada watched him do the arithmetic and come up short. He glanced toward the doors. The deputies looked at each other. Neither moved.

Ada opened the ledger to the tabbed page.

* * *

"Entry one," she said. "June fourteenth, twelve years ago. 2:14 a.m. Pickup, reservoir road, mile marker three. Passenger unnamed. Cash. Destination logged by the trainee dispatcher on duty: the Havel boathouse."

The words dropped into the hall like stones into still water. Somewhere a fork rang against porcelain and was stilled.

"The tape of that call exists," Ada continued. "It was supposed to be erased. The order came from Sheriff Brandt, who called it 'closing the wound.' The trainee who was made to erase it kept a copy instead, because he couldn't live with what he'd done. Teo Reyes is here tonight. He's twenty-six years old. He was nineteen when a grown man put that weight on him."

Teo's head came up. His face was gray. But he didn't deny it. He looked straight ahead, at Corliss, and he nodded once.

"Entry two." Ada turned the page. Her hands were steady now; the shaking had burned off somewhere between the motel room and the stage, leaving only the clean cold clarity of the record itself. "Same night, 3:02 a.m. Pickup, Havel boathouse road. One passenger. Paid cash. The passenger was soaked through. Driver noted it in the remarks column — 'passenger wet, declined blanket.' The driver was Renata Ferreira. She has driven this town for thirty-one years, and she has carried that remark in her chest for twelve of them, praying over it every single day, because she was told it was nothing. Because she was afraid. Because this town makes fear look like loyalty."

Renata stood up. Slowly, one hand on the table, rosary swinging free. She didn't speak. She just stood, facing the front of the room, and the sight of her upright undid something in the crowd — a woman everyone loved, standing witness.

"Entry three." Ada's voice didn't waver. "The distress call. 3:40 a.m., June fourteenth. A payphone at the reservoir boat launch. A young male voice, slurred but coherent, requesting pickup. Fare number 4471. Dispatched to unit six." She paused. "Unit six never arrived. Because at 3:38 a.m., a reroute slip was issued from this company's owner — a shell company called Bluewater Holdings, registered to Aldous Havel — ordering all units pulled from zone six. The slip was signed by Corliss Havel."

"Fare 4471 waited eleven minutes," Ada said. "Then the log goes quiet. Danny Voss drowned a quarter mile from a road where taxis ran all night. He called for one. We were ordered not to come."

The hall made a sound Ada had never heard a room make — not a gasp, not a cry, but a long collective exhale, three hundred people exhaling twelve years of held breath at once. Someone's glass hit the floor and broke and nobody bent to pick it up.

Corliss Havel had not moved. Her glass was still raised, arm perfectly level, smile still fixed — but the smile had gone strange, stretched over something else, and the pearls at her throat rose and fell too fast.

"You are ill, child," she said softly. "You've been taken advantage of by grief and gossip—"

"Entry four." Ada kept reading. "Beginning eight months after Danny's death, Bluewater Holdings disbursed monthly payments to an account controlled by Sheriff Dale Brandt. Fourteen years of service, the memo says. The payments continued until last month. Entry five: Aldous Havel demanded the case be closed in September of that year. He died of a heart attack eight months later, alone, at the boathouse. Entry six: Danny Voss's jacket was recovered last week from a skiff in the Havel boathouse, weighted with chain. I held it. It still has his name inked inside the collar."

She looked up from the page, and she looked directly at Corliss Havel, and she let the silence stand for one full breath.

"You quoted my letter to me in your kitchen," Ada said quietly. "'Filed where you'd file it.' You thought I wouldn't know my own system. But whoever wrote those letters knew it because they rode these routes for thirty years. And Corliss — the letters weren't a confession. They were a summons. You've been summoned."

Corliss set her glass down on the podium. The click of crystal on wood was very small and very loud.

* * *

What happened next, the town would retell for decades, each version growing more precise and less true. But Ada's account, later entered in her own hand, was this:

Corliss Havel did not scream. She removed her reading glasses, folded them, and placed them beside her untouched toast. Then she said, in a voice pitched only for the microphone and therefore for everyone: "Danny Voss was blackmailing my husband."

The hall went dead silent.

"Aldous was moving money through Bluewater for men in the county who needed it moved, and Danny found the ledger, and he wanted five thousand dollars and a car and a life, and Aldous paid him twice and then went to reason with him at the boathouse, and Danny grabbed him, and Aldous shoved him, and the boy hit his head on the dock cleat and went into the water and Aldous stood there and *did not go in after him.*" Her voice cracked precisely, cleanly, like ice under load. "My husband came home soaked to the waist and sat in his chair until morning, and then he went to Dale Brandt, and Dale solved it, the way Dale solves things. And then my husband's heart gave out eight months later because a secret that size will eat a man from the inside, and I have spent twelve years feeding mine to it instead."

She looked out at the room — at the faces she had patronized and protected and purchased for half a century.

"You all drank her wine," she said. "You all took her scholarships. Mercy holds a town together. I told you that tonight. I've been telling you that for fifty years, and you believed me, because believing me was cheaper than the alternative."

"Dale," she added, almost gently, "is standing by your door deciding whether to arrest me or run. Do hurry, Dale. One of those is still possible."

Brandt moved. Not toward Corliss — toward the side exit, hand going to his radio, and that was his mistake, because Ozzie had spent twelve years learning the geometry of rooms and he simply shifted his bulk two feet left and filled the doorway with himself. "Sit down, Dale," Ozzie rumbled. "You're off shift."

The deputies exchanged a look. Then, slowly, one of them — a young deputy named Parrish whose sister had been in Ada's graduating class — stepped forward and took the sheriff's elbow. "Sheriff. I think we should talk outside." It wasn't an arrest. It was worse than an arrest. It was an escort, in front of everyone, past three hundred people who did not look away.

And then Nadia Voss leaned into the sound board and flipped the switch.

Static bloomed through the hall speakers, huge and hissing. Then a young man's voice, alive, annoyed, drunk and breathing and *real*: *"Yeah — reservoir launch — I need a ride, man, I'm at the payphone, hurry up—"*

Three hundred people heard Danny Voss's voice for the first time in twelve years. Somewhere in the crowd a woman began to sob — his mother, Ada realized, his mother was here, the Vosses had come to be honored — and the sound of that crying under the boy's living voice broke the room open completely. Glasses went down untasted. People held each other. The wheat garlands swayed slightly in the draft from the opening doors as Brandt was led out into the June night.

The tape played eleven minutes. The reroute. The waiting. The last call cut short by the payphone's coin limit, Danny's voice saying *come on, come on* — and then the dial tone, enormous, filling the grange hall like a bell.

Ada stood on the stage with the open ledger in her hands and let it play. Beside her, Corliss Havel sat down slowly in a folding chair, folded her hands in her lap, and watched her town hear its own heartbeat exposed. She did not look away either. It was, Ada thought, the first honest thing the woman had done in years.

When the dial tone finally ended and Nadia killed the speakers, the silence was total. Then Danny's mother rose from her seat, crossed the floor on unsteady legs, climbed the two steps to the stage, and took the green ledger out of Ada's hands — not to take it, but to hold it with her, one mother's hands over the record-keeper's hands.

"Read the rest," Mrs. Voss said. "All of it. Every entry. We'll stay all night if we have to."

So Ada read. The payments, month by month. The reroute slip, line by line. Aldous's death, the boathouse, the jacket, the cipher spelled across ten years of mundane fares — initials and times and zone codes stitching a name into the ordinary fabric of a town's nights. She read until her voice went raw, and the town listened, and outside the windows the patrol cars sat empty while their officers stood in the parking lot listening through the doors, unwilling to miss any of it.

Near midnight, Deputy Parrish came back in alone, hat in hand, and spoke quietly to the front table.

"They've got Brandt at the county line," he said. "State troopers picked him up. And ma'am—" he hesitated, looking at Ada, and something in his young face had changed, some weight handed off, "—there's a message for you. From dispatch, of all places. County's asking whether Marrow Creek Taxi keeps records."

Ada closed the ledger gently, the way she closed it every dawn.

"We keep everything," she said.
