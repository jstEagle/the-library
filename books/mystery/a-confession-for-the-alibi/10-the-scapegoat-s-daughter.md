## 10. The Scapegoat's Daughter

The address Ilsa had given me was a third-floor walk-up above a nail salon on Corvus Street, in the part of town where the buildings leaned toward each other like old friends sharing a secret. I climbed the stairs slowly, not because they were steep—though they were—but because every landing gave me one more chance to turn around and go back to the shop, where the worst thing waiting for me was a mis-shelved copy of *The Moonstone*.

I didn't turn around. That was becoming a habit I didn't trust.

The door at the top was painted a stubborn, hopeful yellow. A wind chime made of mismatched spoons hung beside it, and before I could knock, a voice through the wood said, "It's open. But if you're from the insurance company, we already told them about the water heater."

"Not the insurance company," I said. "My name is Coral Amberdale."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight to it, that presses against the other side of a door like a palm laid flat. Then the lock turned, and the yellow door opened on a young woman with her father's jaw and her own fierce, unblinking eyes.

"You're the detective," she said. "The real one. The one who solved the Fenwick drowning."

"I read a lot," I said, which was true, and also the closest thing to modesty I owned.

"Nadia Quill," she said, though she didn't need to. I'd have known her anywhere. She had Danny Quill's face—the man who had confessed to murdering my partner and then died in his cell before he ever stood trial—and she wore it without apology, as if daring me to flinch.

I didn't flinch. But something behind my ribs did.

* * *

Her apartment was small and fiercely organized: textbooks stacked by subject, a corkboard dense with index cards connected by red string in a pattern that would have made Ilsa weep with professional envy. On the kitchen table, under a jar of pens, sat a folder thick enough to stop a bullet. She saw me looking at it.

"Twenty-two years," she said. "That's everything my family knows about the case. Everything I've collected since I was fourteen." She pulled out a chair for me with the brisk hospitality of someone raised by a grandmother. "Sit. Can I make you tea? It's the only thing I can offer besides grief, and most people find the tea more palatable."

"Tea would be fine."

She put the kettle on and talked while she worked, her back to me, which I suspected was deliberate. Easier to say hard things to a wall of cabinets than to a stranger's face.

"My dad confessed when I was six. I don't remember him confessing. I remember him being gone, and then everyone saying he did it, and then him dying, and then everyone saying at least it was over." She set out two mugs, chipped but clean. "At least it was over. That's what people said. Like it was weather. Like he was a storm that had passed."

"I'm sorry," I said, and meant it, and knew how thin it sounded.

"Do you know what a posthumous conviction is?" she asked, turning to face me at last. "Because I've become an expert. When someone dies before trial, the court can still enter a conviction on the record. Finalizes it. Closes the file forever. There's a hearing next month. After that, my father isn't a man who died accused. He's a murderer. Officially. In perpetuity. On paper that outlives all of us."

The kettle began its low climb toward a whistle. Nadia poured water over the leaves with a steady hand, and I noticed her hands were steady everywhere except her eyes, which had the wet brightness of someone who had cried herself dry years ago and now just leaked a little at the seams.

"Why me?" I asked. "Why call a retired bookseller?"

"Because you're not a bookseller." She set my mug down in front of me with a small, decisive click. "Everyone thinks you are. My mother thought so too, until the Fenwick thing. Until she started reading between the lines of the newspaper. She said—" Nadia's voice caught, and she smoothed it flat like a wrinkle in a bedsheet. "She died in March. Before she died, she made me promise to find you if anything ever moved on this case again. And then a woman turned up dead in the harbor wearing your clothes, and Detective Vann came asking questions at the courthouse where I work part-time, and I thought: *something moved.*"

So the threads were converging on her too. I drank my tea and let her talk.

* * *

"Danny Quill was not a brave man," she said. "I know that sounds terrible coming from his daughter. But it's important. He wasn't brave, and he wasn't clever, and he was afraid of exactly three things: my grandmother, deep water, and going to prison." She ticked them off on her fingers. "And then one day he confesses to a murder involving all three—killing a cop, hiding the body near the harbor, facing life inside—and he does it calmly, with a lawyer present, no negotiation, no plea deal, nothing. Does that sound like the same man to you?"

"No," I admitted. "It sounds like a man reading lines."

"That's what Mom always said. *He was reading lines.* She visited him eleven times before he died. Eleven times, and every time she asked him privately—just mother to father, wife to husband—*Danny, tell me the truth*, and every time he looked at her like a man watching a train go by that he wasn't allowed to board." Nadia sat down across from me. "The last visit, three days before he died, he held her hand and said, 'Tell Nadia the truth keeps. Like jam.' And then he laughed, which he never did in there, and two days later he was dead of 'natural causes' at forty-one years old, in a cell by himself, with a clean bill of health at his last physical."

"Natural causes," I repeated. The words tasted like harbor water.

"Heart failure," she said. "In a man who swam laps every morning until the day he was arrested. My father was afraid of deep water, Detective, but he loved pools. Swam a mile a day. His heart was the strongest part of him." She smiled, and it was the saddest smile I'd seen since Pru talked about her late husband. "They didn't even try very hard. That's what I've learned, going through all this. They didn't try, because nobody was watching. Nobody ever watches for the Quills of the world. We're the people things happen *to*."

I set down my mug. "Nadia. What do you want from me?"

"What I've wanted since I was fourteen." She stood, went to the corkboard, and unpinned a single card from the center of the web of string. She brought it back and laid it in front of me. On it, in careful teenage handwriting, older now but preserved like a relic, were four words:

**FIND THE TRUTH KEEPS.**

"I spent ten years thinking it was a code," she said. "A cipher. Dad loved puzzles. I tried acrostics, anagrams, book ciphers—you name it. Then last year I finally understood. It's not a code. It's a promise. He knew the truth was out there, and he knew it wouldn't rot. Jam keeps. Truth keeps. Somebody just has to come back for it someday and open the jar." Her chin lifted. "I think you're somebody, Mr. Amberdale. I think you've been opening jars your whole retirement, quietly, in the dark, and I think one of those jars has my father's name on it."

* * *

Here is the thing about promises: I had spent twenty-two years making none. Anonymity was my religion, and its first commandment was *promise nothing to anyone.* Promises are hooks. Promises are debts. Promises are the reason men like me get found.

I looked at Nadia Quill, who had her father's jaw and none of his fear, and I heard myself say:

"If there's a way to clear him, I'll find it. You have my word."

Her whole body changed—shoulders dropping half an inch, breath leaving her like air from a valve. Twenty-two years of holding it, and some stranger in a corduroy jacket had just told her she could put it down.

"You should know," she said, wiping her eyes briskly, angrily, "that everyone who's helped me ends up sorry. The paralegal who got me the old file transfer records? Reassigned to Records, which is the courthouse equivalent of Siberia. The journalist who wrote one paragraph questioning the confession? Lost his column. Even my mom—" she stopped. Started again. "People who touch this case get hurt, Mr. Amberdale. I need you to understand that I know that, and I'm asking anyway. That's how much it costs me to ask."

"I've noticed the pattern," I said grimly. "I'm currently its main character."

"Then why?"

Because a woman had died wearing my cardigan. Because a widow's house had burned the night after she gave me hope. Because somewhere in the dark, someone was tidying up loose ends, and I was tired of being a loose end that kept getting retied.

"Because I promised someone else once," I said, "and I broke it. I told my partner I'd be right behind him. I wasn't. This is the only kind of apology I know how to make."

Nadia studied me for a long moment, the way you study a chessboard when you suspect the game is older than it looks. Then she reached into the thick folder and slid a photocopied page across the table.

"One more thing. Since you're promising things." Her voice dropped. "This is from the courthouse intake log, the night my dad confessed. Look at the time stamp."

I looked. The confession was logged at 9:47 p.m. Below it, in the same clerk's hand, a routine entry: *Interviewing officer departed 9:15 p.m.*

An officer who left fifteen minutes before the interview began. A confession recorded by no one. A dead man's signature on a story he'd been reading off a page.

"The clerk who signed both entries," Nadia said quietly, "still works there. Same desk, twenty-two years. Never promoted, never transferred, never missed a day. Last spring she bought a house. Outright. Cash." She met my eyes. "Detective, clerks don't buy houses with cash. Something moved. And I think it's moving again, because yesterday someone called our office asking whether the posthumous hearing date could be *moved up.*"

The spoons chimed faintly at the door, though there was no wind.

"When?" I asked.

"They wouldn't tell me. But the request came from upstairs. From the Deputy Chief's office."

I sat very still, tea going cold in my hands, doing arithmetic I didn't like. A hearing accelerated. A witness list that would soon be permanent. A window closing over a truth that keeps—jam in a jar, and someone upstairs reaching for the lid.

"I need a copy of that log page," I said.

"It's already in your pocket," Nadia said, and for the first time she smiled a smile that wasn't sad at all. "Check your coat. I slipped it in while you were admiring my corkboard. Mom taught me: never hand a man evidence in front of a window."

I walked down the yellow-staircased dark with a photocopy against my chest like a second heartbeat, and by the time I reached Corvus Street the streetlights had come on, and parked across from the nail salon, engine running, was a gray sedan with a man inside it who was very carefully not looking at me—

and whose face, when the light swept past, I recognized from a photograph I'd seen only that afternoon, pinned to the center of a dead confessor's daughter's corkboard: the interviewing officer who had departed at 9:15 p.m., twenty-two years ago, and apparently never stopped watching.
