## 3. A Town Built on Toasts

Wren Marchetti had a rule about daylight: he didn't trust it.

Daylight was when Ashford Vale performed itself. At night the town told the truth in fragments—drunk drivers confessing to their steering wheels, teenagers reporting things they'd swear they never saw, the domestic disputes that dissolved by morning like breath off glass. The scanner gave him the real town, the one that lived under its own skin. Daylight gave him the costume.

But today he put on his coat and walked out into it anyway, because the coffin was empty and everyone else seemed determined not to notice.

* * *

Main Street had been dressed for the centennial like a body for a viewing. Bunting in the Hale colors—deep green and gold—hung from the iron brackets above the storefronts. A crew from the historical society was unrolling a banner across the face of the old opera house, now a pharmacy, now a vape shop, the letters stretching wide: **ASHFORD VALE: 100 YEARS OF BUILDING TOGETHER**. Someone on a ladder steadied the corner while someone below shouted directions that nobody followed.

Wren moved along the opposite side of the street with his collar up, hands in his pockets, doing what he did best: being furniture. He'd worked nights for eleven years precisely so he wouldn't have to learn how to do this—to be seen walking without a destination, to be looked at and returned to. On the night shift he was a voice, a frequency, a comfort. In daylight he was a tall man with tired eyes who people tried to place and failed.

"Morning, Wren." Denny Okafor, sweeping the steps of the hardware store, nodded at him like they were friends. They weren't friends. Denny called dispatch twice a year to report raccoons in his dumpster.

"Morning."

"Terrible business about old man Hale, huh?"

Wren stopped. "What business is that?"

Denny leaned on his broom, lowering his voice with the relish of a man who'd been waiting all morning for an audience. "The grave. You heard? They opened it up yesterday for the ceremony—moving him to the new crypt at the memorial garden, big deal, whole thing's going to be televised—and the box is empty." He said *empty* the way you'd say *free beer*. "Not disturbed. Not robbed. Just empty. Like he got up and walked out a hundred years ago."

"That's a strange thing," Wren said carefully.

"Vandalism, according to the mayor." Denny spat lightly off the step. "Somebody stole a hundred-year-old corpse. Who steals a corpse?"

A good question. Wren had spent the previous night asking himself a better one—not *who*, but *when*. Because he'd read the exhumation permit, or rather he'd listened to the police band while the work crew argued about it, and the paperwork said the crypt seal had been intact. Intact granite, intact lead lining, no tool marks. Whatever was missing from that coffin hadn't left through any door anyone had cut.

He kept that thought folded small in his chest, where he kept everything.

"Probably kids," Wren said. "Some kind of prank."

"Yeah." Denny resumed sweeping. "Kids."

Neither of them believed it. That was the first thing Wren noticed about the day, and it would turn out to be the most important: nobody in Ashford Vale believed the vandalism story, and everybody was going to repeat it anyway.

* * *

The mayor held his press conference on the courthouse steps at noon, and Wren stood at the back of the small crowd with a coffee he'd bought only to have something to hold.

Corliss Hale was a handsome man built from his grandfather's portraits—the same long jaw, the same silver-streaked hair worn slightly too long, the same way of standing as if the ground had been arranged for his convenience. He wore a dark overcoat despite the mild morning, grief's uniform, and he spoke into a thicket of microphones with the cadence of a man reading scripture he'd written himself.

"What was done to my grandfather's resting place is not a prank," Corliss said. "It is a violation—of a family, and of a town. One hundred years ago, Edmund Hale came to this valley with nothing but a vision and the willingness to work. He built the mill that fed our grandparents. He laid the streets we're standing on. He deserves better than this."

Wren sipped his coffee and watched the crowd nod.

"He deserves the truth," someone said—a woman's voice, thin, from near the front. A reporter, out-of-town plates on her rental car visible past the rope line. "Mayor Hale, has anyone considered that the coffin might have been empty before yesterday? That your grandfather might not be—"

The murmur that went through the crowd wasn't shock. Wren catalogued it professionally, the way he catalogued tones on the radio. It wasn't the murmur of people hearing something unthinkable. It was the murmur of people hearing something *unsayable*—a word everyone knew and no one used, suddenly spoken aloud in daylight.

"That's an absurd suggestion," Corliss said, and he smiled, and the smile didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "My grandfather is in that mausoleum. Or he was, until someone desecrated it. We will find who's responsible, and we will reinter him with full honors at Saturday's ceremony—as originally planned. Ashford Vale does not cancel its promises because of a coward with a shovel."

Applause. Real applause, warm and rhythmic, from people who had never seen Edmund Hale, whose grandparents had maybe seen him, whose entire relationship with the man consisted of his name on the water tower and his portrait in the bank lobby and the annual toast at the founding dinner. *To the Founder.* Glasses raised every December since before television. Wren had grown up hearing that toast through the walls of other people's houses.

The reporter tried again—"Has the remains been examined, can you confirm—" and then Vivian Ashe appeared at Corliss's elbow.

Wren had seen councilwoman Ashe perhaps a dozen times in his life, always at a distance, always in photographs, but she was unmistakable: a small, precise woman in her seventies with white hair set like architecture and eyes that performed warmth the way a stage light performs the sun. She touched Corliss's sleeve—a gesture of maternal support, perfectly calibrated—and said something too quiet for the microphones, and Corliss stepped back from them, and the conference was over.

The crowd dispersed the way crowds do, already converting the event into anecdote. Wren stayed, watching the Ashe woman guide the mayor inside like a pilot boat bringing in a freighter.

On every founding document in the historical society's window display behind him—every charter, every land deed, every yellowed photograph of men in sepia standing proudly before the mill—two names recurred like a signature written twice: **HALE** and **ASHE**.

* * *

He walked the rest of the afternoon because he couldn't make himself go home.

Home was the apartment above the dispatch office, which was the same building as the dispatch office, which meant home was the job, which meant home was the scanner, and the scanner was currently carrying nothing but the centennial preparations—road closures, parking assignments, a state trooper liaison confirming security for Saturday. He'd been listening to the town plan its party for three days. He needed to see it with his eyes instead.

And seeing it, he began to count.

That was the dispatcher's disease, if he was honest: the compulsion to map. Every conversation he overheard became coordinates. And the town, dressed in green and gold, was giving him more traffic than usual.

Outside the VFW hall, volunteers stacked folding chairs under a tarp, four hundred of them, counting the stacks. A woman with a clipboard told another woman with a clipboard that the Hale family had donated the hall's use "for the whole weekend, isn't that generous," and the second woman agreed that it was generous, and neither of them mentioned that the Hale family owned half the block the hall sat on.

At the florist, two men loaded buckets of white lilies into a van bound for the memorial garden. "For the crypt," one said. "Reburial flowers. Ashe wants them white, nothing colorful, she says color reads as celebration." Wren slowed. *Ashe wants.* Not the mayor. Not the family. Ashe.

In the diner window, a hand-lettered sign announced extended hours for centennial week and a special menu item: **THE FOUNDER'S PLATE — roast pork, apples, brown bread — $12.99, as served at the first founding dinner, 1925.** Inside, the lunch crowd talked over their Founder's Plates about the empty coffin in voices pitched exactly between horror and delight. Wren stood outside long enough to hear three separate theories. Grave robbers. Satanists. College kids. None of them suggested the obvious thing, the simple thing, the thing that had occurred to a single out-of-town reporter on the courthouse steps:

That there had never been anything in the box at all.

That was the shape of it, Wren thought, watching through the glass. Not denial. Something worse. A town performing grief for a man whose body none of them had ever seen, at a funeral none of them had ever attended, mourning a loss they had no evidence had ever occurred—and doing it with such practiced fluency that the performance had hardened into fact. You could raise a toast to a founder for a hundred years, Wren thought, until the toast mattered more than the man. Until the story was load-bearing. Until pulling one brick out—*the coffin is empty*—threatened the whole facade, and everyone instinctively, unanimously, reached to patch it with the nearest available lie.

*Kids with a shovel.*

Everyone repeating it. Everyone relieved to repeat it. And underneath the relief, if you listened the way Wren listened, something else: a flicker of fear, quickly covered, like a hand smoothing a tablecloth over a stain.

He thought of the call. Twenty-five years ago, logged at 2:47 a.m., a woman's whisper saying she saw what happened in the Hale house, and then the line going dead, and the tape he still had—God help him, the copy he'd made at nineteen years old and hidden in a shoebox because he'd been afraid, and had kept because he'd been afraid, fear being the only loyalty he'd ever managed to sustain.

She'd said she saw what happened. Everyone in this town had decided, twenty-five years ago, that she hadn't seen anything, because she was dead, because they said she was dead, because it was easier.

Wren stood on the sidewalk of his hometown in the honest afternoon light and felt the alienation arrive fully, like weather. These were his people. He knew their voices better than they knew each other's—he'd talked a plumber through CPR on his wife, talked a lost kid through a night in the woods, talked half the town through the worst hours of their lives, all without a face, all without thanks. And they would stand up on Saturday in their green and gold and raise their glasses to the Founder, and they would say the vandalism words, and they would mean none of it and suspect all of it, and the machine would run one more century.

Unless somebody pulled the brick.

* * *

It was nearly dusk when he passed the cemetery gates on his way back—the long way, he admitted, though he'd have denied routing himself past them. The iron arch read **ASHFORD VALE MEMORIAL**, and beyond it, on the hill, the Hale mausoleum stood against the reddening sky like a tooth.

There was fresh gravel on the drive. There was a work crew's abandoned barricade. And there, just pulling away from the gate in a dark sedan with municipal plates, was a car Wren recognized—not the driver, he couldn't see the driver—but the car itself, because he'd heard it dispatched twice this week. Councilwoman Ashe's office vehicle.

Visiting the grave, he supposed. Paying respects. Perfectly natural, with the ceremony coming.

Except the sedan didn't head toward the municipal lot downtown. It turned up Ridge Road, away from everything, and Wren—tired, cold, and done pretending he wasn't curious—did something he almost never did in daylight.

He keyed his radio.

"Any unit clear for a traffic check?" he said, casual as weather, to the evening shift drivers. "Ridge Road, heading north. Black sedan, municipal plates, last three digits seven-one-one. Probably nothing."

Static. Then Marisol's voice, dry as ever: "Seven-eleven's been up and down that road three times today, dispatch. My cousin lives up there. Says it's been circling since noon."

Circling. Since noon. Before the press conference even started.

"Copy," Wren said, and stood a moment longer in the blue dusk, looking up at the mausoleum on its hill, at the town below it stringing its lights for a hundredth birthday party, at the whole glittering arrangement of bunting and lies.

Tomorrow, he decided, he was going to pull the overnight cab logs. Every fare. Every address. Everybody who'd been near that cemetery after dark.

Because somebody in this town had been visiting an empty grave—and Wren Marchetti intended to know who.
