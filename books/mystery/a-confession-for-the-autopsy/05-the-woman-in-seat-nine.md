## 5. The Woman in Seat Nine

The fare ledgers lived in the basement, in the archive room nobody visited unless the state auditors came sniffing or a driver needed to prove he'd actually made the run he claimed. Wren went down at four in the afternoon, while the day dispatcher—Doreen, who smelled of menthol and crossword puzzles—was still on shift and would not ask why he wanted the old books. He carried a flashlight and a reason he hadn't yet admitted to himself: the name on the tape.

Lena Voss.

He'd slept three hours after his supervisor, Halvorsen, took the original recording, and he'd spent most of them staring at the ceiling, replaying that whisper in his head. *I saw what happened in the Hale house.* A woman's voice, young, frightened, careful. Twenty-five years old, the call. Logged at 11:47 p.m. on a night in October 1999, and then—nothing. No follow-up. No incident report. The tape had been pulled and boxed and forgotten, which in Ashford Vale was the local equivalent of a burial.

The Voss name had snagged in him the way a nail snags a sleeve. He knew it from somewhere. Not from the police chatter, not from the gossip that drifted through his headset like weather. From somewhere smaller. Closer.

He found the ledgers on the bottom shelf, bound in cracked maroon covers, one per year, going back to the company's founding. The cab company was older than the town's pretensions—founded by a man named Oskar Lindqvist, according to the flyleaf of the first book, which was a coincidence Wren noted without understanding yet. He pulled the 1999 volume and set it on the scarred table under the bare bulb, and he began to read.

Fare ledgers are the most honest documents a town produces. Nobody lies to a ledger. Names, addresses, times, destinations, the driver's initials. People forget what they told a priest but they never forget where they asked to be taken, and the ledger keeps it all in a hand that is either careful or careless but never strategic.

He found the Voss address first: 14 Corliss Lane, a house he knew only as a shape behind overgrown hedges on the poor side of the tracks. Then the entries. October 1999, a scatter of them, all the same pattern. Pickup at 14 Corliss Lane, destination the Hale estate on Whitmore Hill, driver initials R.K. Then, on the last entry of the month, in a hand that pressed harder than the others:

*P/U 14 Corliss Ln. — no answer at door. Neighbor states family gone. Destination: n/a.*

*Family gone.*

Wren sat with that for a long time. The bulb buzzed. Somewhere above him, a cab door slammed and an engine turned over.

He knew the official story the way everyone in Ashford Vale knew it, the way you know the shape of a scar without remembering the wound. Lena Voss, seventeen, daughter of a housekeeper employed at the Hale estate, had drowned in the Miller's Bend stretch of the river in the autumn of 1999. An accident. The town had held a small service—Wren had been eleven, and he remembered only that his mother had said *such a shame* in the tone she reserved for things she didn't want to look at directly. The body, he now understood, had been found downstream, two days later, identified by clothing. No family had pressed for anything more. The family, per the ledger, was already gone.

He turned pages backward, then forward, looking for the father. And there he found the second thing, the thing that made his hands go still on the paper.

Elias Voss. Housekeeper's husband, odd-jobs man at the Hale estate. Dozens of fares through the late nineties, all the same route: Whitmore Hill to town, town to Whitmore Hill. Then, in March 2000, a single entry, driver initials R.K., destination listed as *county hospital*, and after that, nothing. Elias Voss had died that spring—Wren remembered this too, vaguely, a man who fell off a roof, they'd said, a ladder, ice, an accident. The town had been full of accidents that year. The town was always full of accidents, if you never looked closely at the arithmetic of them.

Wren closed the ledger and stood in the dark with his heart going hard.

*Presumed dead.* That was the phrase the police scanner had used once, years ago, when a state cold-case unit had briefly flickered across Ashford Vale like weather from another climate. *The Voss girl, presumed deceased.* Presumed. Not confirmed. The body had been identified by clothing, by a town that wanted the story closed, by a county coroner who had since retired to Florida. Nobody had pressed. Nobody ever pressed, in Ashford Vale. Pressing was for people who could afford the answer.

He should have stopped there. He knew that even then. He had a tape in a supervisor's drawer and a name in a dead girl's file and a town upstairs polishing its centennial like a brass knob. The sensible thing—the invisible thing, the thing the Wren Marchetti of last week would have done—was to put the ledger back on the shelf and go upstairs and take calls and say nothing to anyone, ever.

But he pulled the 2000 volume instead. And the 2001. He told himself he was looking for the mother, for some thread of the family, and that was even true. What he was actually doing, though he wouldn't name it for another hour, was looking for a girl who might not be dead.

* * *

He found her the way you find anything in this town: by accident, in the small hours, when your defenses are down.

It was nearly two in the morning. The overnight fares were the usual ghosts—hospital runs, a drunk councilman's aide deposited at the wrong house on purpose, the diner waitress who always rode home with her shoes off. Wren had the 2003 ledger open on the dispatch desk, hidden inside a phone directory, tracing the family name that had appeared on the flyleaf of the first book. Lindqvist. The cab company's founder. He'd pulled the incorporation papers from the county file while Doreen was at lunch, and he'd read them twice, and he still didn't know what to do with the fact that the company Wren worked for had been founded by a man who shared a surname with the house at 14 Corliss Lane—and that Oskar Lindqvist's only child, according to the papers, was a daughter named Ada, born the same year as the company itself.

Ada Lindqvist. The name sat in the ledger of his memory like a fare waiting to be logged. Because Wren knew that name. Every dispatcher in a small town knows the regulars, and there was one regular who rode every Thursday, 9:40 p.m., from the medical building on Delancey to the rooming house on Fenwick Street, and paid cash, and sat in seat nine—the bench seat behind the driver, the one nobody chooses—and said, in a voice like paper settling, *thank you, that's all,* and was gone.

Ada Lindqvist. Age, if the incorporation papers were right, sixty-one. The woman in seat nine was hard to age—still, careful, dressed in colors that declined to be noticed—but she could be sixty-one. She could be fifty. She could be anything, in the dark of a cab, in a town that had decided not to look at her.

And Lena Voss would be forty-two now. If she was alive. If a drowned girl's clothing had been found in the river and a drowned girl had not.

Wren pulled the current address book—the company's own, the one with the regulars—and ran his finger down to the L's. *Lindqvist, A. — Fenwick St. rooming house, acct: cash. Note: prefers R.K.*

R.K. Ray Kowalczyk, the senior driver, twenty-six years with the company, the same initials that filled the 1999 ledger, the same initials on the last fare Elias Voss ever took. Wren looked at those two letters for a long time. It could mean nothing. Ray was the steadiest driver they had; half the town's old ladies asked for him by name. It could mean that Ray Kowalczyk was simply a man who'd been driving this town so long that his initials were stitched through its secrets like thread through cloth.

Or it could mean that the one driver trusted to carry a ghost every Thursday night was the same driver who'd carried her family before the river took them.

The scanner crackled. Unit twelve, out by the fairgrounds, a raccoon in a dumpster, nothing. Wren logged it without looking. His eyes stayed on the address book, on the small neat note in Doreen's handwriting from some year before his time: *prefers R.K.*

He thought about the whisper on the tape. *I saw what happened in the Hale house.* He thought about a girl of seventeen who had watched something in the town's most sacred house, and whose family had been erased inside a year—mother gone, father fallen from a roof, girl in the river—and who had then, impossibly, not died at all, but had instead folded herself into the one institution that heard everything and saw nothing. A cab company. The town's confessional on wheels. If you wanted to survive in Ashford Vale, where would you hide? Not in the shadows. The shadows were where they looked. You'd hide in the open, in the mundane, in the back seat of the one service every household in town used, including the powerful ones. You'd hide in plain sight, in seat nine, and you'd pay cash, and you'd say *thank you, that's all,* and for twenty years nobody would look at you twice, because looking is a habit of the powerful and invisibility is a discipline of the hunted.

Wren's mouth had gone dry. He got up, poured coffee he didn't drink, and came back and looked at the name again, as if it might rearrange itself into innocence.

It didn't. And it didn't rearrange into guilt, either—that was the strange part. He'd expected, if he found her, to feel the cold triumph of a detective. What he felt instead was something warmer and more frightening. He felt recognized. He sat in his booth with his headset on and his log open, and he thought: *I have been invisible in this town for eleven years, and I thought I was the only one who'd chosen it.*

* * *

Thursday came the way Thursdays come on the night shift—slowly, then all at once.

Wren had spent the intervening days doing nothing, officially. He'd logged the fares, taken the calls, endured Doreen's crossword commentary and Halvorsen's new obsession with centennial logistics—the company was contracted to run guests up to the Hale estate for the anniversary dinner, and Halvorsen wanted the routes laminated. Unofficially, Wren had done three things. He had checked the rooming house records at the Fenwick Street desk under the pretense of confirming a delivery address, and learned that Ada Lindqvist had rented her room for nineteen years, in cash, no references, and that the landlady described her as *the best tenant I ever had, quiet as furniture.* He had pulled the medical building's sign-in log from the recycling—Thursday evenings, Dr. Amara Osei, internal medicine, a doctor whose practice Wren knew from scanner chatter to be the one physician in the county who did not bill through the Ashford Vale family medical group, which was to say the one doctor not owned, however loosely, by the founding families. And he had said nothing to anyone, which was becoming the hardest work of his life.

Now it was 9:31 p.m., and Ray Kowalczyk's cab was finishing a fare across town, and Ada Lindqvist would be standing outside the medical building in nine minutes, the way she stood every Thursday, and Wren had a decision to make.

The dispatch board let him do it cleanly. He didn't have to falsify anything—yet. He just had to be the one who assigned the fare. Ray was still on his drop at the far end of Delancey; there was a closer unit, Unit Seven, four minutes out. Any dispatcher in the world would send Unit Seven. Wren sent Ray.

*Unit Four, you're up. Fare at the medical building, Delancey and Ninth. Regular.*

Ray's voice came back, easy, gravel-warm: *Copy. Tell her I'll be there before her coat's buttoned.*

Wren watched the fare go out on the board and felt the small, secret weight of what he'd done. It was nothing. It was everything. He had chosen, for no defensible operational reason, to keep one woman's ride in the hands of the one driver whose initials ran through her family's last days. He told himself it was surveillance. He told himself he wanted the pattern intact so he could keep watching, keep learning, keep the evidence clean. All of that was true. None of it was the whole truth, and he knew it, and the part that wasn't true had a shape he didn't want to look at yet—the shape of a woman in a dark cab saying *thank you, that's all,* in a voice like paper settling, and the ache he'd felt, hearing it, of one invisible person addressing another.

At 9:52, Ray's cab passed the dispatch office on its way to Fenwick Street, and Wren, on impulse he didn't examine, keyed the mic on the driver channel.

*Unit Four. How's your nine o'clock tonight?*

A pause, the sound of the road under the tires, then Ray, mildly curious: *Quiet as ever. You know her?*

*Regular. Just checking she's settled.*

*Settled like a hymn,* Ray said. *Nineteen years, same seat, same thank-you. You could set a church by her.* A beat, and then, in the tone of a man offering something he'd been holding a long time: *You know she asked for me, the first time? Twenty years back. Walked into the office and said, I'll ride with the man who drove the Vosses.*

The cab turned onto Fenwick and the signal softened, and Ray's last words came through half in static, but Wren heard them whole, and they went through him like cold water.

*She said, you drove my father home.*

The transmission ended. Wren sat in the booth with the scanner murmuring around him—unit twelve, unit seven, a fender-bender on the bridge, the town's small nightly litany—and he put both hands flat on the desk to stop them shaking, and he understood that he had just heard a dead woman's daughter announce herself, obliquely, to the one man in the cab company who might remember her father.

She hadn't hidden from Ray. She'd chosen him. Nineteen years of Thursdays, and every ride had been a message in a bottle: *I'm here. I survived. Do you remember?* And Ray Kowalczyk, steady, discreet Ray, had carried the message every week and never said a word—until tonight, until Wren's question opened the bottle.

Wren pulled up the next Thursday's schedule and did the thing he'd been circling for days. He blocked himself onto the dispatch rotation for Thursday nights. Indefinitely. Doreen would grumble; Halvorsen would be pleased—someone volunteering for the worst shift was a centennial miracle. Nobody would question it. Nobody ever questioned Wren Marchetti. That had been his armor for eleven years, and now, for the first time, it was becoming his instrument.

He told himself it was for the investigation. The witness on the tape, the empty coffin, the town's polished lie—all of it pointed at this woman, and he needed to be close to the pattern, needed to be the hand on the board when her fare came up, needed to see who else watched her, who else assigned drivers, who else knew.

He told himself all of that. And then, at 9:58, he pulled up her account one more time and looked at the photograph-less entry, the cash payments, the note in Doreen's hand—*prefers R.K.*—and he changed the note. He typed, in the dispatcher's flat institutional language that was itself a kind of code: *All Thursday fares: W. Marchetti to coordinate personally.*

It was a small thing. It was the least invisible thing he had done in eleven years.

Outside, the fog was coming up off the river, the same river where a girl had drowned without drowning, and the scanner murmured its nightly litany, and somewhere on Fenwick Street a woman was climbing the stairs of a rooming house to a room she'd rented for nineteen years with money earned invisibly, and Wren sat in his booth and felt the two halves of himself—duty and want, investigator and man—begin, quietly, dangerously, to pull apart.

He had one week until the next Thursday. And he had, he realized as he logged the last fare of the night, absolutely no idea what he was going to say to her when she got in the cab and found the dispatcher's voice coming out of the driver's seat.

The scanner crackled. Unit twelve, out by the cemetery again—*probably another raccoon, dispatch*—and Wren logged it, and did not believe it, and began, in the quiet of the booth, to plan a route he'd never driven: the one that led to seat nine.
