## 7. Vivian's Version

The summons came the way everything important in Ashford Vale came: through a channel too polite to refuse.

It was nine-forty on Tuesday morning, which meant I'd been asleep for forty minutes when my phone lit up on the nightstand. I stared at it the way you stare at a spider on the ceiling—calculating whether ignoring it would make things better or worse. The caller ID said ASHE FAMILY OFFICE, and beneath it, in smaller type, an address on Corliss Street that I had driven past a thousand times and never once looked at directly.

I answered because not answering was itself an answer.

"Mr. Marchetti." A woman's voice, young, rehearsed smooth. "Councilwoman Ashe would like to see you this afternoon. Four o'clock. She'll be at the family office until five."

"I'm on nights," I said. "I sleep during—"

"Four o'clock," she repeated, with the gentle finality of a train schedule. "She asked me to mention that she's read your file. The one from your hiring, back in '09. She keeps very complete files."

Then she was gone, and I lay there under the blackout curtain I'd bought secondhand from the funeral home, listening to the town wake up outside my window—the leaf blower at the Kowalczyks', the school bus wheezing on Birch, the church bell that rang eleven times no matter what hour it was because nobody had fixed it since the Bicentennial—and I understood that something had ended.

For thirty-one years I had been invisible. That was not an accident; it was a discipline. You learn it on the night shift, how to become furniture, how to be the voice in the headset that drivers forget is attached to a body, how to take a call from a frightened woman and log it as a prank and keep the tape anyway—not out of heroism, God knows, but out of some animal refusal to let a thing be erased entirely, even while you're too cowardly to do anything with it. Invisibility had kept me safe through every storm this town ever cooked up. And now Vivian Ashe had pulled my file from 2009, which meant she had done what I had spent two weeks doing: followed the thread from an empty coffin backward through time until it reached a dispatcher who once took a call he should have logged differently.

They knew about the tape. Or they knew enough.

* * *

I arrived at four o'clock exactly, because being early would look eager and being late would look afraid, and I wanted very badly to look like neither.

The Ashe Family Office occupied the ground floor of a brick building older than the county, wedged between a title company and a law firm that shared a parking lot with both. Inside, it smelled of lemon polish and old paper, the particular smell of money that has stopped trying to impress anyone. Portraits lined the hallway—Ashe men in daguerreotype sepia fading into oil paint fading into photographs, each generation standing slightly straighter than the last, as if the family had been slowly ironing itself flat over a century and a half. At the end of the line hung Edmund Hale himself, younger than any photo I'd seen in the paper, dark-haired, with the kind of mouth that looks like it's about to say something funny and then doesn't.

"Mr. Marchetti."

Vivian Ashe stood in a doorway at the end of the hall, and I will admit—I had expected armor. What I got instead was a woman of about sixty in a gray cardigan, reading glasses pushed up into silver hair, holding a mug of tea like somebody's grandmother. It was the most dangerous costume I have ever seen. Power in Ashford Vale didn't wear power; it wore comfort. It wore the confidence of someone who has never once needed to raise her voice because the room was built by her grandfather.

"Come in, sit down. You take coffee? Tea? There's a kettle."

"Coffee's fine."

She poured it herself from a carafe on the sideboard, handed me the cup, and settled into the chair across from mine—not behind the desk, I noticed. Across. We were going to have a conversation, the staging said. Two people. Equals.

"You've been with Vale Taxi sixteen years," she began. "Night dispatch, full-time since 2011. No disciplinary actions. No complaints from drivers—in fact, Marisol Vega says you're the only reason she stayed after her husband left. You covered double shifts during the flood year without filing for overtime." She sipped her tea. "You are, by every measure we have, the best dispatcher this town has ever employed. And yet you've never applied for a promotion. Not once. Sixteen years."

"The hours suit me."

"Do they?" Something moved behind her eyes—not menace, exactly. Curiosity, maybe, or its colder cousin, assessment. "Or does the dark suit you, Mr. Marchetti? There's a difference. Some people work nights because they like the quiet. Others because they'd rather not be seen. I've found the second kind are usually the more interesting employees."

I held the coffee cup steady. Somewhere in the building a clock ticked with the patience of inherited wealth.

"I'll come to the point," she said. "The centennial committee needs a communications director. Radio coordination, transport logistics, liaison with the state police for the ceremony security. It pays nearly double what you make now. It comes with benefits you currently don't have, a title you can put on a stone someday, and—" she smiled, small and warm, "—a seat at the dinner. Inside the ballroom, not out at the cabstand. After twenty-five years of listening to this town, Mr. Marchetti, I think it's time you heard it properly. From the table."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange for nothing." She set down her mug. "That's what an offer is. It only becomes something else if you make it so."

* * *

We sat with that for a moment. Outside the window, two workmen were unrolling bunting along the Corliss Street lampposts—green and gold, the Hale colors, going up over the whole town like ivy.

"Tell me what you know about Lena Voss," Vivian said.

The name landed in my chest like a dropped wrench. I kept my face still. Sixteen years of keeping my face still.

"Not much," I said. "Name comes up in old logs. Ran away, 1999. Presumed deceased."

"Mm." She turned her mug a quarter-turn on its saucer, precise as a dial. "Lena Voss was the daughter of Anton Voss, who worked for my father. She was a troubled girl. Unstable—that's not slander, Mr. Marchetti, that's diagnosis; there were hospitalizations, records sealed by a judge who owed my family nothing and still saw the truth of it. In the spring of 1999 she made a series of claims about the Hale household. Serious claims. And then she disappeared, and everyone who loved her agreed that the kindest interpretation was also the likeliest one." She looked at me over her glasses. "Grief makes people say terrible things. Sometimes it makes them say them to emergency dispatchers at three in the morning."

My blood went cold and stayed cold. *She knows the date. She knows the call.*

"That chapter closed twenty-five years ago," Vivian continued, voice softening into something almost kind. "It closed with signatures and settlements and a great deal of pain that decent people agreed not to reopen, because reopening it would hurt only the living. Edmund Hale built this town. Whatever else he was—and great men are rarely simple—he deserves his rest. The empty coffin is vandalism. Ugly, sacrilegious vandalism, probably by kids, and the state police agree with me. But it has stirred up old stories, and old stories attract people who mistake noise for knowledge." She paused. "Dispatchers, for instance. Dispatchers hear everything and understand almost none of it. A whisper on a bad line, a drunk, a kid playing with the phone—all of it sounds equally real through a headset. The dangerous ones are the dispatchers who decide their fragment is the whole story. They start digging. They start asking drivers questions. They start pulling tapes out of archives." Her eyes came up and held mine, mild as milk. "And then they become liabilities, Mr. Marchetti. To themselves first, and then to everyone around them. Liabilities get audited. Their employment histories get examined. Their judgment gets questioned in ways that follow them to whatever town they move to next, because Ashford Vale is small but its paperwork travels."

The clock ticked. The bunting men laughed at something outside.

"I'm offering you a way to be on the right side of this history," she said. "A good job, a real future, a place at the table when this town celebrates what it survived. All you have to do is what you've always done so beautifully: listen, coordinate, and let other people decide what the record says." She rose, smoothed her cardigan, and offered her hand. "Think about it tonight. Marisol can cover your shift. Give me your answer tomorrow, and whichever way you answer, Mr. Marchetti—this conversation stays between us. Discretion is the first qualification for the position."

Her hand was dry and cool and perfectly steady.

* * *

I walked out past the portraits. At the end of the hall, Edmund Hale's painted mouth still hovered on the edge of the joke it never told, and I stopped in front of him for a second, close enough to see the craquelure running through his forehead like a river delta, and I thought: *you're not in that coffin, and she knows you're not in that coffin, and she called the empty grave vandalism.*

Vandalism. As if the dead man had been stolen rather than having never been buried at all.

On the sidewalk, the green-and-gold bunting snapped in the wind. Down the block, a Vale Taxi cab idled at the light—unit twelve, Danny Okafor driving—and I heard him key his mic before I even felt my phone buzz against my hip.

"Dispatch, unit twelve. You seeing all this decoration? Whole town's dressing up for a corpse."

"Copy, twelve," I said, and my voice came out level, professional, the voice of a man who had just been offered everything and understood it was a price tag. "Whole town's dressing up for something."

I gave Ada Lindqvist's address to unit twelve without thinking—her weekly run to the pharmacy, same as every Tuesday—and then stood there on Corliss Street with my heart hammering, realizing what I'd just done. Realizing that Vivian Ashe hadn't threatened me, not once, not with a single word. She had promoted the threat. She had wrapped it in a salary and a chair at the table and the warm smell of lemon polish, and she had told me exactly what happens to people who dig, and then she had shaken my hand like a lady.

And I had gone home route by route in my head, all the way back to the office, and pulled tomorrow's schedule, and begun—quietly, in the system, where no audit would look for weeks—to change one passenger's pickup times.

Because if dispatchers who dig become liabilities, then I intended to find out what happens to the woman they're digging for.
