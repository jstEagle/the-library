## 10. Dead Man's Frequency

The basement of the Kavanagh Taxi & Dispatch building smelled like every basement in Ashford Vale: cold concrete, old paper, and the faint mineral tang of water that had been leaking somewhere for years and would keep leaking long after everyone currently alive was gone. Wren came down the stairs with a flashlight in one hand and Ada's brother's notebook in the other, and he stood at the bottom step for a full minute before moving, listening to the building breathe.

He'd worked here eleven years. He knew the upstairs—the dispatch console with its worn foam chair, the corkboard of driver photos going back three generations, the coffee maker that only worked if you kicked it. He had never had a reason to know the basement. The basement was where records went to be legally retained and practically forgotten. Boxes of fare ledgers from the eighties. A dead vending machine. And along the far wall, behind a hanging sheet of plastic that someone had stapled up sometime during the Clinton administration, the server rack.

It wasn't much of a rack. Two tower units, a shelf of external drives labeled in marker by a man named Dombrowski who had retired before Wren was hired, and a tangle of coaxial cable that ran up through the ceiling like the building's nervous system. Wren had heard Dombrowski mentioned exactly once, at a retirement party he hadn't attended: *the old-timer who set up our backup logging.* Backup logging. Every transmission on every band the company held—dispatch, driver-to-driver, and the emergency frequency the FCC had assigned them in 1974 and which nobody had ever used, because Ashford Vale had never once had an emergency that required its taxi company to coordinate a response.

The notebook lay open against his forearm. Tomas Lindqvist's handwriting was small and slanted, the letters of an engineer or a man who'd learned to write in cramped spaces. Wren had read it twice since Ada pressed it into his hands two nights ago, her kitchen light buzzing overhead, her hands wrapped around a mug she never drank from. Twenty-five years of observations. Dates, license plates, meeting times, names. And near the back, in an entry dated four years ago, a single line that had brought him down here:

*They think the old band is dead. Nothing on this frequency dies. It just waits.*

Wren clicked on the rack's power strip. One of the towers whirred to life with a sound like a tired animal; the other made a clicking noise and gave up. The monitor took thirty seconds to wake, then glowed a soft amber over a login prompt.

Dombrowski. Wren tried the name. Denied. Tried it capitalized. Denied. Then he flipped to the front of Tomas's notebook, to the first page, where the handwriting was younger and less careful, and found what he'd assumed was a serial number scrawled in the corner: *KV-7411-D.*

The door opened.

* * *

The archive room fire had been three days ago, and the official story—electrical fault, old wiring, no foul play suspected—had settled over the town like the fog off the reservoir. Wren had stood across the street at 2 a.m. watching the volunteers knock it down, feeling the heat on his face and understanding, with a clarity that still frightened him, that the fire was a message addressed personally to him. They didn't know he'd already moved everything. His paranoia had outpaced their arson by seventy-two hours, and now the tapes lived in a duffel bag under his bed like contraband, and he slept badly beside them, and he could not explain to anyone, least of all himself, why he'd done it.

But the fire had destroyed the paper logs. The physical call sheets, the signed maintenance records, the decades of handwritten dispatch entries that might have shown him how to navigate what he was looking at now. He was working blind, with only a dead man's notebook and a guess.

*KV-7411-D.* Accepted.

The interface that loaded looked like it had been designed by NASA and abandoned by God. Green text on black. A directory tree. Wren scrolled with the arrow keys, breath fogging in the cold air, reading band designations he half-remembered from dispatcher certification: CH-1 DISPATCH, CH-2 DRIVER, CH-3 MAINT, and then—

CH-4 EMERG — UNUSED — LOGGING ACTIVE SINCE 11/74

Logging active. Not unused. Unused by people. But something had been listening.

The directory under CH-4 was enormous. Thousands of files, named by date and time stamp, stretching back further than Wren's own life. He scrolled down through 2019, 2015, 2010, watching file sizes flicker past—most of them tiny, seconds long, static captures triggered by some threshold of signal strength. Ghost transmissions. Leakage from other bands. Or—

He stopped scrolling. Went instead to the search function, which mercifully accepted a date range, and typed in the dates that had organized his entire understanding of the last month: October 14–15, 1999.

Fourteen files. He selected them all and hit playback on the first.

Static poured out of the little desk speaker Dombrowski had wired to the rack, and beneath the static, faint as a voice carried across a lake at night, something else. Wren turned the volume until the speaker crackled in protest and leaned in close enough that his forehead nearly touched the plastic housing.

A man's voice, distorted, clipped by the compression of twenty-five years: *"—told you the river road, not the main—"*

Then nothing. File two: more static, longer, forty seconds of it, and then a woman laughing somewhere in the noise—or crying, it was impossible to say—and cutting off mid-breath.

File three was empty. File four was empty. Wren's hands had gone cold on the keyboard. He worked through them one by one, methodical, the way he did everything, letting the dead frequency talk to him in fragments. A car door. An engine idling too long. A snatch of radio playing "Total Eclipse of the Heart" from what must have been a cab's dash mic, because the song was layered under another voice saying *fare's waiting, sir,* and then the song again.

And then file nine. Timestamped 11:47 p.m., October 14, 1999.

It began with engine noise—the particular rattle of a Crown Victoria idling, a sound Wren knew in his bones, because he'd listened to it ten thousand nights through his headset. Then a door chime. Then voices, close to the microphone, so close they seemed to be standing beside him in the dark.

*"Mr. Hale, please. Get in the car. We'll drive you home ourselves, no charge, it's freezing."*

Edmund Hale's voice. Wren recognized it from the centennial documentaries, from the oral history recordings the historical society played at the school every year—a warm baritone, amused, slightly slurred. *"I'm walking, Danny. Best thing for a man after brandy is cold air. You go on."*

*"Sir, your daughter asked us specifically—"*

*"My daughter worries too much. Tell her I said so."*

A pause. The engine note changed—someone shifting into park, or out of it. And then a third voice, lower, rougher, a voice that made the hair on Wren's arms stand up not because he recognized it but because of what it said next, flatly, without heat:

*"Get in the car, Edmund."*

Not *sir*. Not *Mr. Hale*. Just the name, stripped bare, the way you address a man when the politeness is finished.

Hale laughed. It was a terrible thing to hear, that laugh, knowing what Wren knew, hearing it ring out confident and unafraid across a quarter century. *"Is this a robbery? Because I've got about forty dollars and a pocketful of antacids, and you're welcome to both."*

*"Danny,"* the low voice said, *"go check the gate."*

Footsteps receding. Gravel crunching. And then, in the sudden quiet, the sound Wren would carry for the rest of his life: the wet, heavy percussion of something striking flesh, once, twice, a third time, and Hale's laugh collapsing into a grunt and then into nothing at all, and the Crown Vic's engine still idling patiently through all of it like a metronome keeping time for a piece of music no one had meant to record.

Wren realized he was gripping the edge of the desk hard enough to hurt. He made himself let go. Made himself keep listening.

Gravel returning. The low voice breathing hard. Then Danny's voice, farther away, thin with panic: *"Oh my God. Oh my God, is he—is he—"*

*"Help me lift."*

*"We can't—we can't just—"*

*"His car's at the house. The river takes care of the rest. That's the whole point of the river, Danny. Now help me lift him, or you're an accessory either way, and one way has a future in it."*

Straining sounds. Fabric dragging over gravel. The engine revving. And then, as the voices moved away from the dash mic, fading toward the edge of audibility, one last exchange that Wren played four times in a row, headphones on now, eyes closed, because he needed to be certain:

*"What about the girl? The Voss kid saw the car leave the party."*

*"The Voss kid is fourteen and hysterical. Nobody believes hysterical girls. And by morning, nobody will need to."*

Silence. The file ended. The timestamp glowed green in the dark: duration, six minutes, twelve seconds.

Wren sat back in the desk chair—it groaned under him, the only living sound in the room—and stared at the ceiling pipes without seeing them.

Six minutes and twelve seconds. Captured by accident, by a parked cab whose dash mic had been keyed—maybe the driver had been transmitting, maybe the mic had stuck, maybe Tomas himself had left channel four open on purpose, because Tomas had been listening to this frequency for years, had known that nothing on it ever died. The town's own infrastructure. Its taxis, its radios, its humble municipal wiring—had been recording while the founding fathers committed murder on the river road, and the recording had sat in this basement through five mayoral administrations, two floods, and a centennial, waiting for someone to come downstairs.

Ada's father. Wren thought of the entry in the notebook, the one she'd touched with one finger when she showed him: *R. Voss refused. Said he wouldn't sign. Found at the quarry Tuesday.* The man who'd said no. And the fourteen-year-old girl who'd seen the car and been discredited before breakfast, who had spent her whole life since being nobody, being Ada, being invisible—which was, Wren understood now with a tenderness that felt like grief, the only safe thing a person could be in Ashford Vale.

He copied the file. Copied all fourteen. Copied the entire CH-4 directory onto two drives from the shelf, labeling them in marker the way Dombrowski would have: boring names, forgettable names. *FARE LEDGER BACKUP '99. MAINT RECORDS Q4.*

Then he played the murder file one more time—not for the violence, but for the last line. *By morning, nobody will need to believe her.*

Except somebody had believed her. Somebody had spent twenty-five years believing her, guarding her, keeping watch from inside a hollowed-out mausoleum with canned food and old newspapers, logging license plates in a notebook until the watching killed him. Tomas had heard this same recording—had to have, had found this rack years ago, had built his whole silent vigil around it—and he had died before he could use it.

Wren powered down the rack. In the sudden silence, the basement felt different—not abandoned anymore, but occupied. Haunted in the literal sense: full of voices that had never left.

He climbed the stairs with the drives in his jacket pockets, heavy as stones, and pushed through into the dispatch office—and stopped.

The night light was on. Someone was sitting in his chair at the console, silhouetted against the board's glow, and when the figure turned, the pale oval of a face resolved into features Wren knew from council meetings and ribbon cuttings and the society pages of the *Ashford Vale Courier*.

Councilwoman Vivian Ashe smiled at him, warm as a hostess, and gestured at the headset resting on the console.

"Late night, Mr. Marchetti?" she said. "I do hope you haven't been digging around in things that don't concern you."

Behind her, near the basement door, her aide stood with his hand resting lightly on the light switch—as if he'd been down there already. As if he'd been checking.

Wren kept his face the way he kept his voice on the radio: flat, professional, empty. But his heart was hammering against the drives in his pocket, and he understood, with perfect clarity, that the fire had only been the opening move.
