## 1. The Second Signature

The Ashcombe house smelled of cold lavender and older things beneath it — dust, paper, and the faint mineral sweetness of a house that had been shut up with its dead still inside it, in every sense. Elodie Villalobos stood in the study at nine in the morning, gloves on, coffee untouched, and let the room introduce itself before she touched anything.

Marguerite Ashcombe had died eleven days ago in the east bedroom, alone except for the caretaker who found her. The house sat above the harbor road like a held breath — granite shoulders, slate roof gone green at the seams, windows that had watched three generations of ships come home without ever once being opened to the sea air. Elodie had probated enough estates of this kind to know that houses like this one kept their own ledgers. Her job was only to read them.

The safe was behind the portrait of Marguerite's father, because it was always behind the portrait of the father. The combination came in a sealed envelope from the court, and the dial turned under her gloved fingers with the reluctance of an old man asked to repeat himself.

Inside: a deed box, a bundle of letters tied in ribbon gone brittle as onion skin, and two wills.

Two.

Elodie set them side by side on the green baize desk and did not touch either for a full minute. This was the discipline her first mentor had beaten into her: *the document is not the truth, Miss Villalobos; the document is only where the truth left its fingerprints.*

The first was notarized. Dated the fourteenth of March, fourteen months before death. Cream stock, embossed seal of Hollis & Prentice, the signature of a solicitor named Ambrose Prentice attesting in his careful clerk's hand. It divided the estate among named beneficiaries with the tidy arithmetic of a mind that liked columns: the bulk to a nephew, Gerald Ashcombe; a bequest to the church; a modest annuity to "my faithful servant Tomas Reyes, in consideration of long service." Everything accounted for. Everything *correct*. She read it twice and found nothing wrong with it, which was itself a kind of wrongness. Wills this clean usually had something buried under the floorboards.

The second will was handwritten.

It lay on top of the first, which meant whoever closed the box had wanted it found first. Four pages, blue-black ink, written in a hand that shook but did not falter — the script of someone old and ill who had made up her mind anyway. Dated the second of November, six weeks before death. No witnesses. No seal. Under statute it was worth barely more than the paper it burned into.

But the words. The words disinherited the nephew entirely — *I leave Gerald nothing, and I leave him my reasons, which are these* — and the reasons ran half a page, most of it struck through so violently the pen had torn the paper. What survived the strikethroughs was worse than what didn't: fragments about *the nursery*, about *what was taken from that room*, about *the winter of the fire* and a debt owed that could never be paid in money. The residue of the estate went instead to "the child of my heart, whose name I have kept as I kept everything else in this house — locked."

No name. A will that gave away everything to no one, witnessed by nobody, sealed in a safe with its own contradiction.

Elodie sat back. Outside the window, the harbor light blinked through thin fog, patient as a heartbeat.

She had opened four hundred estates. She knew the taxonomy of disputed wills the way a doctor knows rashes: the forgery, the coercion, the senile revision, the bitter scrawl written in a dying grudge. This was none of those, or all of them. Both documents were authentic-looking. Both were *authentic-feeling*, which was different and more dangerous. Somewhere between March and November, Marguerite Ashcombe had changed her mind about everything, and then died without telling the world — or telling it in a letter that hadn't surfaced yet.

"Two signatures," Elodie murmured to the empty room. "One hand."

She photographed everything, catalogued everything, resealed the box with her own numbered tag, and drove back down the coast road with the fog closing behind her like a door.

* * *

The confession arrived at seven-forty that evening.

Elodie lived above her office on Cutter Street, in rooms that smelled of old varnish and her own ambition, and she heard the mail slot clack while she was still at her desk with the case file open. She went down in her socks. The hallway was empty. The stairwell light buzzed. On the mat lay a single envelope, heavy cream stock, hand-addressed:

**Miss E. Villalobos, Esq.**
**By hand, and none other.**

The postmark was dated the day after Marguerite Ashcombe's funeral.

She stood there longer than she should have, letting the cold from the street seep under the door. Then she carried it upstairs, laid it on blotting paper, and opened it with a bone folder, photographing each stage — habits that until this morning had been professional ritual and now felt like superstition.

The letter inside was unsigned. Four pages, the same blue-black ink, the same shaking-but-resolute hand as the second will. She recognized it instantly, the way you recognize a voice through a wall.

*I write to you by name, Miss Villalobos, though we have never met, because I know you have taken the Ashcombe estate, and I know how you work. You opened the safe on Tuesday morning. You wore grey gloves. You read the second will twice and then you sat very still for one minute and nine seconds, looking out the window at the harbor light, and you said aloud, to no one: 'Two signatures. One hand.'*

The coffee cup stopped halfway to her mouth.

*I know your habits because I have needed to know them. You are careful, but care leaves patterns, and patterns can be loved. You take your coffee black and let it go cold. You photograph documents in stages, always four. You keep your case files in the bottom drawer, unlocked, because you believe locks invite curiosity. You are right about that, incidentally. It was not a lock that protected them. It was the fact that no one thought to look.*

Elodie set the cup down. Her hands were steady — she made them steady, deliberately, finger by finger — but something behind her sternum had begun to move like water finding a crack.

The letter continued, unhurried, almost tender.

*There were two wills because there were two truths, and Marguerite could not sign her name to both. The first will tells you what the family believes. The second tells you what the house knows. Neither will tell you what happened in the nursery in the winter of the fire, because that is not written anywhere yet. That is why I am writing to you.*

*A man sits in Cranmer State Penitentiary for a murder he did not commit. He has sat there for thirty-one years. Marguerite knew. Marguerite chose, near the end, to bury the truth rather than shame the living, and she asked me to help her bury it, and I did, God forgive me, I dug with my own hands. But she is dead now, and the truth has outlived its keeper, and it wants a lawyer. Not any lawyer. You.*

*I will send you the rest when I am certain of you. Be certain quickly. The others are watching you too, and they are not patient, and they do not love your patterns.*

There was no signature. No date beyond the postmark. The paper ended mid-thought, clean-edged, as if a second page had been removed — and Elodie turned the sheets over twice, held them to the lamp, saw nothing.

She sat for a long time after that. The radiator ticked. Down on Cutter Street a delivery truck groaned past, and somewhere a dog complained at the fog.

Here was the arithmetic that would not resolve: the letter described her day at the Ashcombe house in detail no surveillance could plausibly capture — the exact words she'd spoken alone in a locked study, the duration of a silence only she had measured. Which meant the writer had been in the room. Or had seen through eyes that were. Or —

Or she had told them herself, somehow, without knowing. Through her files. Through her unlocked drawer. Through some channel she hadn't yet imagined, feeding her just enough truth to keep her reading.

Professional confidence curdles slowly, then all at once, like milk in a warm room. Elodie had spent twenty years believing herself the reader of other people's secrets. Now, holding four pages of another person's handwriting that knew her better than her own diary did, she understood that she had been opened like a safe — gently, expertly, by someone who wanted her to find exactly this and nothing more.

She looked at the postmark again. The day after the funeral. Whoever wrote this had posted it before Elodie had even accepted the case.

Before she'd said yes, they had already known she would.

Elodie rose, locked the letter — and the photographs, and the memory of her own voice saying *two signatures, one hand* — into the drawer that was supposed to be unlocked, and stood in the dark of her office listening to the fog press against the windows.

Tomorrow she would interview the household. The bitter nephew. The evasive solicitor. And the faithful servant, Tomas Reyes, who had served long enough, and loved carefully enough, to learn anyone's patterns.

She already suspected which of them had learned hers.
