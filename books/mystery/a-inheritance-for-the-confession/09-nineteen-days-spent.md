## 9. Nineteen Days, Spent

Dale Corven came into the shop on a Thursday, which was fitting, because Thursdays were when I did the accounts, and Dale Corven had always understood that everything, in the end, was accounting.

He wore a raincoat that cost more than my monthly mortgage payment and carried an umbrella he didn't need, having apparently sprinted the twelve feet from his car to my door like a man crossing enemy lines. Which, in fairness, he was.

"Mrs. Villalobos." He said it warmly. He'd been saying it warmly for thirty years, back when I was just the sheriff's wife and he was just a councilman with ambitions. "Terrible about the fire."

"Terrible," I agreed. The back room still smelled of smoke. I'd stopped lighting candles in memoriam; the shop smelled of ash now whether I wanted it to or not.

"Heard you lost some stock." His eyes went past me, up the ladder, along the shelves where the rare editions lived — the ones that hadn't burned, because three days before the fire I'd moved them into a storage unit under Luz Reyes's maiden name, because grief teaches you things and suspicion teaches you better ones. "First editions are irreplaceable."

"Some things are," I said. "Some things aren't."

He smiled at that like a man who thought he'd caught a code and cracked it. Maybe he had. "Xanthe. May I call you Xanthe? We've known each other long enough." He didn't wait for permission. He never had. "I'm going to speak plainly, because I think we're both tired of weather talk."

"I do love weather talk," I said. "It's so honest. Nobody pretends the rain is anything but rain."

"The mortgage on this building." He set a folded paper on the counter between us, squared its corners with two fingers, precise as a man laying down a card he believed was an ace. "The bank holds it, but the bank answers to people who answer to me. I can make it disappear. Free and clear. This shop, yours outright, no payments, no worry, for the rest of your life."

I looked at the paper. I looked at him. Somewhere behind his eyes, a man was doing arithmetic, and I let him believe the sum was coming out in his favor.

"And in exchange?"

"You found something in those books. I don't know what. Frankly, I don't care what. Old ledgers, old debts, old mistakes — this town is built on all three. I want them gone. Burned, shredded, buried in your yard, I genuinely don't care. You hand them over, the mortgage dies, and we both get to grow old in a town that remembers us kindly."

*Us*, as if we were in the same story. As if we'd been reading the same book at all.

"You know," I said, "my husband used to say that about this town. *Remembered kindly.* He said it the way other men say *amen*."

Corven's smile thinned by a degree. "Gideon was a good man."

"Yes," I said. "That's rather the point of all this, isn't it."

I picked up the paper. I read it slowly, though I could have recited its terms from the shape of the silence around them. Then I folded it once and put it in my apron pocket, and I watched Dale Corven's shoulders come down half an inch, and I hated myself a little for how easy it was, and I did it anyway.

"I'll need a few days," I said. "To say goodbye to them."

"To books?" He almost laughed. "You've sold thousands."

"Not these." I met his eyes and gave him what he needed: a widow's weariness, a woman ready to be bought. "A few days, Dale. You've waited seven years. Wait four more."

He waited until he was at the door to deliver the line he'd clearly rehearsed in the car. "You were always the smart one in that marriage, Xanthe. Don't outsmart yourself now."

The bell above the door rang him out. I stood in the smell of smoke and old paper and took the folded paper from my pocket and set it under the cash register, next to the float, where a woman keeps the things she means to remember exactly why she refused.

Then I went upstairs, unlocked the flat-panel case Gideon had built me for our anniversary — *for your most dangerous inventory*, he'd joked, meaning the first editions, meaning, God help me, meaning the ledger all along — and began feeding pages into the scanner, one by one, while the rain came down honest as ever on the roof.

* * *

It took two days. Two hundred and eleven pages, front and back, scanned at six hundred dpi, saved to three drives and a cloud account whose password Luz chose and refused to tell me, which I understood was her way of saying she trusted me and also that she'd been betrayed by enough people keeping secrets alone.

Luz sat across from me at the kitchen table while the scanner hummed, collating copies in the order Gideon's marginalia had numbered them. She worked with a steadiness that frightened me a little. Seven years she'd carried Marisol's death like a stone in her shoe, walking on it daily, and now that the stone was finally being pried loose she handled it like something holy.

"My mother used to say the church would fix it," Luz said, not looking up. "She wrote letters. To the diocese, to the state attorney, to the governor. Every one came back with a sympathy card attached. You know what the governor's office sent? A fridge magnet. *Visit Beautiful Harrow Creek.*"

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be accurate." She tapped the page in front of her — column after column of dates and initials, the red-inked entries Gideon had decoded in his own hand, translating money into motive. "Accurate is: a boy drove a boat drunk because another man put the keys in his hand and paid for the gas, and then that man became mayor, and everyone who remembered got a mortgage forgiven or a job created or a quiet warning. Accurate is: my sister drowned in nine feet of water wearing no shoes, and the report says *accidental*, and the report was typed by a deputy who died last week of *natural causes* while a fire burned down a bookshop the same night."

"Emmett didn't push her," I said. "I believe that."

"I believe it too. That's the worst part." She finally looked up. Her eyes were dry and furious and clear. "He spent thirty years terrified of a man who never had to touch him. Fear is cheaper than violence. Corven always knew that."

The scanner finished the last page. I lifted it out warm from the machine — the final entry, the one from Gideon's Bible, the one with the name written plain. I had read it forty times. I read it again anyway, the way you touch a bruise.

*I followed them to the dock. I saw what Dale did. I wrote the report that buried it. Marisol Reyes deserved better than both of us.*

Both of us. My husband had signed himself into the sentence with the killer, and I had loved him for twenty-six years without knowing he was carrying that, and I would carry it now, publicly, because love that hides the truth isn't love, it's just a longer kind of lying.

"Nineteen days left when he made his offer," I said. "We've spent five."

"What happens on the last day?"

I told her.

Luz listened all the way through, and then she smiled, and it was Marisol's smile, I realized — I'd seen it in photographs all these years without knowing why it looked familiar — and she said, "My mother would have brought the casserole."

* * *

The statute expired on a Monday.

We started at dawn at the courthouse steps, because if justice wasn't going to live in the building, it could at least stand on its porch. Luz had printed two hundred copies at the library in Millbrook, where nobody knew her face and nobody asked questions, and we stacked them in two copy-paper boxes with the lids off, and I will admit that my hands shook setting them down. Not fear. Something adjacent. The tremor of a woman about to do permanently what cannot be apologized for.

By seven-fifteen the courthouse staff began arriving. A clerk named Dottie Pell, who had processed every document in that building since before Marisol died, took a copy, read the first page standing on the steps, and went very still. Then she walked inside, and ten minutes later the copier in the records office started running, and did not stop for an hour.

By eight, the diner. Rosie Tavares cleared a space on the counter next to the pie case and laid the pages out flat under the glass, all of them, so that every person who ordered eggs that morning read the ledger while they waited, and nobody complained, and old Walt Merrick, who'd fished the river for fifty years, stood up from his booth and said, loud enough for the whole room, "Nine feet. I always said nine feet of water doesn't take your shoes off."

By nine, the church. Father Anselm met us at the door, and I braced for the conversation about appropriate venues, but he only held out his hand for a copy and said, "The narthex table. Next to the bulletins." And then, quieter, looking at the final page, at Gideon's name in Gideon's handwriting: "He came to see me, you know. Three years ago. Sat in that last pew for an hour and left without speaking. I've wondered ever since what he couldn't say." He looked at me. "You said it for him."

"No," I said. "He said it. He just made me the messenger. Twenty-six years of marriage and his last letter made me the messenger."

"That's marriage," Father Anselm said, and handed the copies through the door.

By noon, the boxes were empty. By one, someone had photocopied the photocopier's work, and pages were appearing taped inside shop windows down Main Street, and a local kid — not a kid anymore, a man of thirty who'd been in Marisol's class — livestreamed himself reading the red entries aloud on the courthouse steps while a crowd grew around him the way crowds do when the truth arrives late and everyone wants to witness it personally.

No arrest was possible. That was the whole architecture of it — the statute had closed at midnight Sunday, and Dale Corven had spent seven years buying his way past the law, and he had succeeded, completely, legally, forever. What he had never purchased was the town. You can buy a sheriff's report and a fire marshal's silence and a deputy's fear. You cannot buy two hundred copies on a courthouse steps and the face of Dottie Pell reading them, or Rosie Tavares sliding a plate of eggs away from a man who says *maybe I'll eat at home today*, or the way a congregation goes silent when the truth sits on the narthex table between the bulletins and the collection plate.

At four o'clock, Corven's office announced he would address the community "regarding recent misinformation" at six. At four-fifteen, the announcement was quietly deleted. At five, his car was seen leaving the municipal lot with the trunk loaded, headed east on Route 9.

At five-thirty, Luz called me from the courthouse steps, where a crowd had not dispersed but thickened, and where someone — she wouldn't say who, and I suspected Dottie Pell — had produced a box of candles.

"It's not over," she said. "You know that."

"I know."

"A grand jury can still convene. New evidence. The ledger itself, the arson, Emmett's death. Statutes have exceptions, Xanthe. Murder has no clock."

"I know that too," I said, and looked out the shop window at Main Street, where the evening light was coming down gold and honest on a town holding pieces of paper, and where, at the far end, past the diner and the church, the lights of the mayor's office blazed on in an empty building, burning electricity for no one, like a man who has forgotten how darkness works.

"But tonight," Luz said, "they're reading."

Tonight they were reading. All down Harrow Creek, in kitchens and trucks and pews, they were reading my husband's confession in his own hand, learning what their beloved sheriff had done and what he had spent his last years trying to undo, and somewhere in that arithmetic — the same arithmetic Dale Corven had always trusted — a new sum was forming.

I turned the sign on the door to CLOSED, though there was nothing left to close against, and reached for the phone, because Dottie Pell had just texted me four words that changed the temperature of everything:

*Grand jury. Tomorrow. Testify?*
