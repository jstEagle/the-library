## 2. Ledger of Small Debts

The Marrow Bay Historical Society occupied what had once been the town's first bank, and it had never quite stopped being one. The façade still wore its granite dignity, the arched windows still held their iron grilles, and inside, where tellers' cages had stood, glass display cases now held cannery photographs and a ship's wheel salvaged from something called the *Pelican Rose*. The vault in the back—door open, hinges oiled, a hand-lettered sign reading ARCHIVES WELCOME YOU—had become a basement of shelves instead of safe-deposit boxes. Dominic Reyes liked that about it. Towns kept their money in vaults; they kept their secrets there too.

She arrived at nine sharp on Tuesday morning, recorder in her jacket pocket out of habit rather than intention, because Ida Brandt had told her on the phone that the society opened at ten but that she herself came early "to let the basement breathe." Dominic had been in enough small towns to know that phrase meant: *I want to look at you before you look at anything.*

Ida was waiting under the portico when she climbed the steps, a small woman in a cardigan the color of wet sand, silver hair pinned with the kind of efficiency that suggested she'd been doing it for sixty years and resented every minute spent on it. Her eyes were pale blue and very steady.

"You're the podcast woman." Not unkind. Just placed, like a specimen on a slide.

"Dominic Reyes."

"I know. You called twice." She unlocked the door. "Coffee's in the back if you drink it. The good cups are mine. The chipped ones are for visitors."

Dominic smiled and took the chipped cup anyway, and something in Ida's face flickered—approval, maybe, or amusement—as though Dominic had passed a test whose questions she hadn't known she was answering.

* * *

The basement smelled of paper and cold stone and, faintly, of salt—the way everything in Marrow Bay smelled of salt, as if the sea had gotten into the mortar decades ago and simply refused to leave. Fluorescent tubes hummed over rows of gray shelving. Boxes were labeled in faded typescript: CANNERY PAYROLL 1938–1954. SCHOOL DISTRICT MINUTES. LIGHTHOUSE COMMISSION CORRESPONDENCE. Dominic's fingers itched at that last one, but she made herself start where she'd said she'd start.

"Thorne materials are on the east wall," Ida said behind her. "Business papers, some personal correspondence, whatever the family didn't burn or take to Portland when the last of them left. It's not much. Elias Thorne was careful with his paper."

"Careful how?"

"The way men are careful who have something to be careful about." Ida set a pair of white cotton gloves on the table without ceremony. "Gloves. And don't use pens down here. Pencil only. The ink gets ideas."

Dominic pulled on the gloves and found the Thorne boxes. There were fewer than she'd hoped. A folder of letters of incorporation for Thorne & Partners, Shipping and Cannery, dated 1949. Photographs of three men standing on a dock with their arms folded in the universal posture of men who own things: Elias Thorne in the center, lean and dark-eyed, with the half-smile of someone who finds the joke before anyone tells it; flanked by two broader men with the same jawline and different eyes—one watching the camera, one watching Thorne. The Voss brothers. Henrik and Aldous, according to the pencil notation on the back. Dominic photographed both sides of the picture and filed the brothers away in her head under *later.*

Then, near the bottom of the second box, wrapped in acid-free tissue like something precious or something dangerous: a ledger.

It was a business ledger, cloth-bound, the spine cracked into a pale seam from years of opening. THORNE & PARTNERS was stamped on the cover in gold that had gone the color of old teeth. Dominic carried it to the reading table under the brightest light and opened it with the care Ida was pretending not to watch.

The early pages were exactly what a ledger should be—dry, orderly, almost soothing. Fuel oil. Deckhands' wages. Netting repairs. Ice, by the ton. Thorne's handwriting marched across the ruled lines in a tight, upright script, each figure double-checked with a small tick that looked like a bird's footprint. Here and there a second hand appeared—smaller, slanted, quicker—a corrections hand that initialed itself M.C. Marguerite Calloway, Dominic thought. The bookkeeper. She'd find her later; everyone in this story seemed to lead to someone else, which was either the nature of towns or the nature of guilt.

She worked through 1955. 1956. The entries stayed mundane, and that mundanity was its own kind of music—she could hear the whole harbor economy ticking in these pages, boats going out, fish coming in, money moving through Marrow Bay like blood through a body that didn't yet know it was sick.

1957 brought the first oddity. An entry for *Salvage survey — S/V Meridian — consult.* No amount attached. Just the tick, the bird's-foot checkmark, as if the transaction had been approved somewhere other than this book. Dominic wrote the word MERIDIAN in her notebook and underlined it twice. She'd never heard of a vessel by that name connected to Marrow Bay, and she'd read every scrap the internet had on this town, which wasn't much. A ship that existed in a ledger and nowhere else.

1958 was thicker. More entries in the same vein—*survey,* *adjustment,* *settlement, see file*—and no files anywhere in the boxes. And then, in November of 1958, an entry that stopped her breath for a moment just because of how ordinary it refused to be:

*Insurance proceeds received in full. Account closed.*

No vessel named. No policy number. Just that flat, satisfied sentence, followed by a distribution: three shares, initialled E.T., H.V., A.V. Three men splitting something large enough that the ledger's ruling lines couldn't quite hold the figure comfortably—it crowded its column the way big money always does.

And then 1959 began, and the ledger changed temperature. The entries grew sparser. Thorne's handwriting loosened, the ticks less frequent, as though the man himself were getting tired of checking his own work. In March, a single line: *H.V. — passage booked.* Henrik Voss, dead at sea that same spring, per the historical society's own display upstairs. Dominic sat back and looked at the ceiling pipes for a while.

* * *

It was mid-afternoon when she reached the end, and the end was wrong in a way she felt in her sternum before her brain caught up.

The final signatures of pages were simply gone. Not torn raggedly, not water-damaged, not eaten by mice—cut. Sliced close against the binding with a blade, so cleanly that at first glance the ledger just seemed to end, the way books do. But run a fingertip along the stubs—Dominic did, gloved and gentle—and you could feel them, maybe twenty pages' worth, each one severed at the root. Someone had wanted those pages gone forever and had gone about it with the patience of a surgeon.

Twenty pages. The last twenty pages of a shipping firm's ledger, covering—by her count backward—the tail end of 1961 and the beginning of 1962. The months before Elias Thorne walked out of his life and into the sea that never gave him back.

Dominic sat very still in the humming fluorescent quiet and listened to the building settle around her. This was the part of the work outsiders never understood, the part that never made it into the episodes: the long silences, the sitting-with, the way a fact had to be given room to become a certainty. Twenty pages cut out by someone with steady hands and a reason.

But whoever cut them had missed one thing. They always missed one thing. That was the whole secret of archives, in her experience: destruction was an act performed by people, and people got tired, or hurried, or careless at the margins.

She turned back through the ledger slowly, page by page, hunting the margins this time. And there—in the upper corner of a page dated June 1961, squeezed into the space beside an entry for engine parts, in that smaller, slanted hand, the bookkeeper's hand, M.C.—a note she'd already passed twice without seeing:

*R.C. — paid in full, per agreement. Do not enter again.*

Do not enter again. As in: stop writing this down. As in: this is the last time this appears in any book.

R.C. Two initials. Dominic stared at them until the fluorescent light buzzed louder than it had any right to. R.C. could be anyone. Half the surnames in a coastal town started with C—Christensen, Calloway, Cole, Crane. But the phrasing snagged her the way a nail snags a sweater. *Paid in full, per agreement.* That wasn't wages. Wages were entered weekly and entered again. That was a settlement. A buyout. Somebody had been paid once, completely, on condition of silence—or on condition of departure—and the bookkeeper had been instructed to make sure the payment lived nowhere else on paper.

Some debts, she thought, get paid in currencies that don't fit ledgers.

She photographed the margin four times, with and without flash, then copied the entry by hand in pencil because some things you want living in your own handwriting too.

* * *

Ida found her there at closing time, standing over the mutilated ledger with her gloves off and her notebook open, and Dominic watched the older woman's gaze go to the cut stubs of pages and stay there a moment too long. Not surprise. Recognition. The look of someone greeting an old acquaintance across a room.

"They told me the records were incomplete," Dominic said carefully. "Flood damage, I think someone said."

"Mm." Ida gathered a stray pencil from the table, aligned it parallel with another, a housekeeping gesture that bought her time. "There was flooding. '64. Took some of the school minutes and ruined Mrs. Petraglia's recipe collection, which she never forgave the river for." She looked up, and her pale eyes were mild and absolutely clear. "That wasn't flood damage, Ms. Reyes. Floods tear. That was cut. Whoever did it took their coat off first—I'd wager on it."

"You knew?"

"I knew the ledger had an ending that came too soon. I've known since I was nineteen years old and shelved it myself." Ida smoothed the tissue paper back over the cover with two fingers, tucking it in like a blanket. "I didn't know what was on the missing pages. Nobody living knows that, probably. But I'll tell you something my grandmother used to say, and she was born in this town and buried in it and never once lied to me, which in Marrow Bay is a rarer accomplishment than you'd think."

Dominic waited.

"She'd say: in this town we keep two sets of books. The ones with numbers"—Ida tapped the ledger's shrouded shape—"and the ones with favors. And the second set is the one that decides who eats." She said it without drama, the way you'd report the tide schedule. Then she straightened, and the warmth came back into her face like a lamp turned up, and she was again the pleasant archivist who kept good coffee and chipped visitor cups. "You'll want the lighthouse commission correspondence next, I expect. Everyone does. Box forty-one. It's on your left as you come down tomorrow."

"Tomorrow?"

"Well." Ida paused at the foot of the stairs, one hand on the rail, and for a heartbeat the lamplight caught something in her expression that wasn't quite a smile. "You didn't think I'd give you the whole basement in one day. Some debts, Ms. Reyes, are paid in other currencies. Patience is one of them. So is knowing which drawer not to open until you're asked."

She climbed the stairs, and Dominic stood alone in the humming dark among the gray boxes, listening to the door bolt home above her like a period at the end of a sentence.

On the walk back through the fog-thick streets, past shuttered canneries and porches where lamps glowed amber through the mist, Dominic turned the note over in her mind until the letters wore grooves. *R.C. Paid in full, per agreement.* Somewhere in this town, sixty years ago, someone had taken money to disappear from a book—and the bookkeeper who recorded it had signed her own initials to the act, which meant she'd known, which meant she'd been afraid or loyal or both.

Dominic had come to Marrow Bay to ask why a founder's coffin held nothing but stones. She was beginning to suspect the coffin was the least of what this town had buried.

Back at her rental cottage, she pinned the photograph of the margin note above her desk, and beneath it wrote a single line in her notebook, the working title of a season not yet announced:

*Marrow Bay keeps two sets of books. Find the second one.*

Outside, the fog pressed against the window like something listening. And in the morning, she decided, she would start with box forty-one—but tonight, before sleep, she allowed herself one search she'd been saving, typing the initials into the genealogy database along with the year 1961 and the words *Marrow Bay.*

One result. A marriage record, 1954. Marguerite Calloway, bookkeeper, to a man named Edmund Crane—who had, according to the same database, drowned off the jetty in 1971, the same year his wife's obituary said she had too.

Two drownings. One marriage. And a marginal note in a dead man's ledger that said somebody named R.C. had been paid to vanish.

Dominic turned off the lamp and lay in the dark listening to the sea work the rocks below the bluff, patient as a creditor.
