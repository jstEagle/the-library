## 3. Everyone Swears She Drowned

The Marrow Bay public library smelled of salt-damp paper and the particular melancholy of institutions that had outlived their purpose but not their funding. Dominic Reyes had spent enough mornings in libraries like it—coastal towns, mill towns, mining towns, all the places where the past sat heavier than the present—to know the smell by heart. It was the smell of a story nobody local wanted told, preserved anyway by accident or inertia.

She'd claimed the microfilm reader in the back corner, beneath a window fogged with condensation, and she'd been there since the library opened at nine. It was now nearly noon. The librarian, a young man with a beard he was clearly still negotiating with, had brought her coffee without being asked, which either meant small-town hospitality or that Ida Voss—no, Ida something else, the archivist from the historical society—had already made calls on her behalf. In Marrow Bay, Dominic was learning, everything traveled by telephone, and everything traveled slowly, and nothing traveled without commentary.

*The Marrow Bay Sentinel*, 1971. The reels were brittle, the images gray-edged and soft as old bruises. She worked backward from the date Ida had given her—the second week of October—and threaded the film through the reader with hands that had learned patience the hard way, across eleven seasons and forty-one cold cases, most of them solved not by brilliance but by the willingness to read things other people had stopped reading.

October 9th: harvest festival coverage, a church bake sale, an editorial about the new breakwater. October 11th: nothing. October 12th:

**FORMER THORNE FIRM BOOKKEEPER DROWNED OFF JETTY**

Dominic leaned closer, close enough that her breath fogged the reader's glass.

*Mrs. Marguerite Calloway, 44, longtime bookkeeper for the Thorne & Partners shipping concern, is presumed drowned following an apparent accident at the municipal jetty Tuesday evening. Mrs. Calloway, widow of the late Robert Calloway, was known to many residents through her decades of service to the firm founded by the late Elias Thorne. A memorial service will be held Saturday at St. Brendan's. The family requests privacy.*

Presumed drowned. Apparent accident. *Requests* privacy—not *the family requests*, but *the family*, singular, definite, as though everyone knew exactly which family was meant.

Dominic had read ten thousand obituaries. They were the most honest documents a town produced, because nobody thought to lie in them—or rather, nobody thought anyone would ever check. And this one was lying by omission, the way a locked door lies by being locked. No surviving relatives listed. No mention of where she'd lived. No detail about how she'd come to be on the jetty at all, on an October evening, alone, forty-four years old, bookkeeper for a firm whose founder had vanished into the sea nine years earlier.

Two drownings. One firm. Nine years apart.

"Coincidence," she said aloud, testing the word the way you'd test a floorboard you suspected of rot. It gave under her weight immediately.

* * *

The county records office was a twenty-minute drive up the coast road, through fog so thick the pines on either side appeared and dissolved like thoughts you couldn't hold onto. Dominic drove with the windows down despite the chill, letting the salt air clear the library's staleness from her head. She'd learned long ago that the drive between documents was where the shape of a story revealed itself—the way separate facts began, unbidden, to arrange themselves into a pattern, the way a tide chart and a ledger page and an obituary suddenly became three corners of the same triangle.

The county annex occupied a squat concrete building that looked like it had been designed by someone who hated both architecture and the coast. Inside, a woman named Dolores—her nameplate said DOLORES M., her manner said *state your business quickly*—sat behind reinforced glass.

"Coroner's files," Dominic said. "1971."

"Public record," Dolores said, in a tone suggesting that public record was a technicality she honored under protest. "Sign here. No photographs of the building. Files don't leave the room."

The reading room was a converted closet with a folding table and a fluorescent light that buzzed like something trapped. The file came up on a cart fifteen minutes later, a thin manila folder with a rubber band around it gone to powder. CORONER'S CASE FILE — 71-0447. CALLAWAY, MARGUERITE E.

Dominic put on gloves—Ida had pressed a pair on her at the historical society, along with a warning about the humidity—and opened it.

The first thing she noticed was what wasn't there.

A coroner's file on a drowning should contain certain things. A recovery report. A description of the body. Photographs, or at least a notation that photographs existed. An autopsy report, or a signed waiver if the family declined one. Identification of remains. A certificate of death signed by the coroner himself, with the cause of death stated in his own hand.

Case file 71-0447 contained a single-page incident report, typed on a manual typewriter with a failing *e*. It stated that Mrs. Marguerite Calloway was reported missing on the evening of October 10th, 1971, by persons unknown, that her shoes and handbag were recovered from the jetty, and that "search of waters conducted with negative result." Cause of death was listed as *presumed drowning*. The certificate of death attached to it bore a signature Dominic didn't recognize and a date two weeks after the disappearance—which was legal, bodies sometimes took time—but no body was ever noted as recovered. Not in this file. Not in any attachment. Not in the coroner's logbook entry for October 1971, which Dolores grudgingly fetched and which recorded, in the space reserved for remains disposition, a single word: *none*.

No body. No autopsy. No inquest. A death certificate issued anyway, on the strength of some shoes and a handbag left neatly on a jetty.

Dominic sat back in the plastic chair and let the fluorescent light buzz over her.

In eleven seasons of podcasting, she had developed a private taxonomy of deaths. There were deaths that happened, messy and sad and random. There were deaths that were covered up after the fact, clumsy affairs of panicked husbands and bribed deputies. And then there were deaths that were *manufactured*—not the killing but the dying itself, the paperwork of mortality assembled like a stage set, every document present and correct except the one thing that couldn't be faked: a body.

Marguerite Calloway had died the way Elias Thorne had died. Thoroughly, officially, and without a corpse.

"Find what you were looking for?" Dolores asked when Dominic returned the folder.

"I'm not sure yet," Dominic said honestly.

"Huh." Dolores studied her for a moment. "You're the podcaster. The one asking about Thorne."

"News travels fast here."

"It travels at exactly the speed people want it to." Dolores slid a form across the counter. "Log your checkout time."

Dominic signed. Then, because she had learned that the most valuable sentences in any town are spoken by people who have decided they've already said too much, she paused at the door.

"The deputy who handled the Calloway search," she said. "Is he still around?"

Dolores's face did something complicated—a flicker of warning, of memory, of the specific exhaustion of someone who has watched this town chew through its own history for decades. "Walt Merrick. Lives out past the cannery, blue house with the anchor fence. He doesn't like visitors."

"Nobody out here likes visitors."

"No," Dolores agreed. "But most of them have the decency to pretend. Walt never learned." She turned back to her screen, then added, without looking up: "He was young then. Nineteen seventy-one. Newest deputy on the force. Whatever he saw, it was the first thing he ever saw that mattered, and it taught him everything he knows about this town. Draw your own conclusions about whether he'll talk."

* * *

The blue house with the anchor fence sat at the end of a gravel lane below the cannery, its paint weathered to the color of a winter sky. The anchor fence itself was genuine wrought iron, ancient, each post crowned with a small anchor—somebody's life's work, somebody's obsession. A garden that had once been magnificent was now mostly rosemary gone woody and roses left to run wild.

Walt Merrick answered the door before she knocked, which meant he'd watched her come up the lane.

"I don't give interviews," he said. He was mid-seventies, built like the fence posts, with a face that had been handsome once and had decided, somewhere along the way, to stop pretending.

"This isn't an interview," Dominic said. "It's a conversation between two people who both know the Calloway file is wrong."

Something moved behind his eyes. Not surprise—she'd expected surprise and gotten instead a kind of grim confirmation, the look of a man who has waited fifty years for someone to say the obvious and resents them for taking so long.

"File's not wrong," he said. "File's *empty*. Different thing entirely." He looked past her at the fog rolling in off the water. "You want coffee?"

"I'd love coffee."

"You'll drink it on the porch, and you'll turn that recorder off, and whatever I say stays between us until I'm dead. Those are the terms."

"Those are terrible terms for a journalist."

"They're excellent terms for a witness." He held the door open. "Take 'em or drive back down the hill."

Dominic turned off her recorder—an actual sacrifice, she felt it physically, the little click of the power switch like a bone setting wrong—and followed him onto the porch, where two chairs faced the water and a third, newer chair sat slightly apart, facing the house, as though someone had once tried to join him and been positioned accordingly.

He poured coffee from a thermos, strong enough to stand a spoon in, and sat looking at the sea for a long moment before he spoke.

"Nineteen seventy-one, I'm twenty-three years old. Six weeks on the force. Dispatch calls it in—woman missing off the jetty, shoes found, bag found, no woman. So we search. Tide's going out, water's maybe four feet deep off the jetty rocks at low, six at high. Four feet, Miss Reyes. You could stand up in it if you were drunk enough, and half this town was drunk enough most nights. Nobody drowns in four feet of water off a jetty they've walked a thousand times. Not unless they went in wanting to, and even then—" He shook his head. "Even then, a body doesn't go anywhere. That cove holds what falls into it. Ask any fisherman. We dragged it three days. Found a bicycle, a washing machine, and a boot. No Calloway."

"What did the sheriff say?"

"Sheriff said write it up as drowning, presumed." Walt's voice had gone flat, the voice of a man reciting something memorized against his will. "I said, Sheriff, we got no body. He said, son, we got a death certificate coming regardless, so which story do you want to be attached to when it arrives? I was twenty-three. I had a wife pregnant with our first. I wrote it up as drowning, presumed." He drank his coffee. "Fifty-three years ago. Some nights I can still taste that pen."

"And the funeral?"

"Closed casket." He said it simply, without drama, and somehow that was worse. "St. Brendan's, Saturday morning, packed house. Half the town crying, the other half checking who else was crying. Closed casket, and everybody knew—everybody always knows—that there was nothing to put in it. And nobody said a word. Not one word, in fifty-three years." He set down his cup. "You know what a town does when it agrees together to believe a lie? It stops being able to tell the truth about anything smaller. That's what happened here. That's why your Thorne case went cold. It wasn't cold. It was *frozen*. Same freezer."

Dominic wrapped her hands around her cup. "Who called it in? You said persons unknown."

"Anonymous call, payphone outside the Legion hall. Voice we never matched to anybody." He almost smiled. "I checked that phone booth myself, first week on the job, thinking I'd find something. Nothing. But here's the thing I've thought about for fifty years: whoever called it in knew about the shoes before we did. Reported the shoes, the bag, the location—all of it, exact. Called it in before any civilian could've seen the jetty that time of night, in that fog. Whoever called it in was *there*."

"Or arranged for it to be there."

"Or that." Walt looked at her directly for the first time. "Marguerite Calloway kept the books for Thorne & Partners for nineteen years. You know what a bookkeeper sees, Miss Reyes? Everything. Every payment, every debt, every number that doesn't add up. And then Elias Thorne goes in the water in sixty-two, and she keeps the books alone for nine more years, and then one October evening she leaves her shoes on a jetty and becomes a ghost." He shook his head slowly. "I don't know if she's dead. I never believed she drowned. But I'll tell you what I do know, and you can take it or leave it: dead women don't need their deaths staged. Only living ones do."

The fog was thickening, swallowing the cannery, the lane, the world beyond the porch. Dominic felt the itch—that cold-case itch, the one that had driven her whole career—sharpening into something harder and more certain. Someone had erased Marguerite Calloway on purpose. Someone had built her death out of shoes and paperwork and a closed casket, and a town full of people had agreed to look at the stage set and call it a grave.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked. "Off the record, no recording—you get nothing out of this."

Walt Merrick was quiet for a while. Out past the jetty, barely visible, a buoy bell tolled somewhere in the murk.

"Because I'm seventy-six," he said finally, "and I wrote that report with a coward's hand, and I've got maybe five years left to unsay it. Because Dolores called me this morning and said a journalist was pulling the Calloway file, and I sat on this porch and realized I'd been waiting my whole retirement for exactly that phone call." He stood, joints cracking like the porch boards. "And because there's one more thing, and you didn't hear it from me. The night before the funeral—Friday night—my sheriff got a phone call, and after it he drove up the headland road. Came back near midnight. Never said where he'd been. But the next morning, the death certificate paperwork was already done. Already *signed*. Somebody up that headland had the documents ready before the body—if there ever was a body—was even declared lost."

He walked her to the gate, to the little anchors on their iron posts.

"One more thing," he said. "The headland house. The old Hollis place, up past the lighthouse trail. It's been in a trust since before my time, and the taxes get paid regular as sunrise, and in fifty-three years not one soul in this town will tell you who lives there. We all pretend the road ends at the trailhead." His grip on the gate was firm. "You seem like the kind of woman who doesn't pretend well. So I'll just say it plain: whatever you find up there, remember that the last person who asked questions about Marguerite Calloway was me, in 1971, and I was told to write *drowning, presumed* and go home to my wife."

He let her out. Dominic stood a moment on the gravel lane, fog pressing in from the water, the buoy bell tolling its slow count somewhere offshore.

Four feet of water. A closed casket. A certificate signed before the search ended. A road everyone pretended didn't exist.

She got in her car and sat with the engine off, watching the fog eat the headland, and thought: *the dead woman bought groceries somewhere.* Dead women didn't need prescriptions filled, or roofs repaired, or taxes paid on houses in trusts older than anyone's memory. Somewhere in this town's ledgers—its tax rolls, its delivery routes, its pharmacy receipts—Marguerite Calloway was still leaving a trail, because the living always do.

She started the engine and drove back toward town, toward Ida's basement and its locked drawers, already composing the question she would ask next:

*Who delivers to the Hollis house?*
