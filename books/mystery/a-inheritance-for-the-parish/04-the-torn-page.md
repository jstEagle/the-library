## 4. The Torn Page

Marguerite Sterling did not sleep so much as lie down inside his own memory and let it run.

The motel room in Bellhaven had the particular silence of river towns at night—a silence with a current under it, water moving somewhere below the level of hearing, carrying things along. He sat on the edge of the bed with the lamp on and his eyes closed, and he went back to the summer of 1979, to the basement of the Halloway & Pruitt claims office in Carrow, to the smell of cold concrete and toner and the specific dust that settled on forty-year-old paper.

He had been seventeen. He had been invisible, which was the whole point of him. Mr. Pruitt had said, *Boy, you'll do the estate files, the dead ones nobody wants, and you'll catalog them exactly, and you won't read them.* And Marguerite had done exactly that and had read every word, because he was seventeen and because his mind did not have a door it could close. Things went in and stayed in. Whole pages stayed in. He could still see the coffee ring on the third page of the Wren file—oval, brown at the rim, placed slightly left of center, as if set down by someone reading standing up, holding the cup in their right hand.

The file had been thin. Estate of Aldous Wren, deceased March 4, 1961. Death benefits paid out to the estate, then reassigned. Standard riders. A life insurance ledger referenced but not attached—*see annex*—and the annex had never come. And then the gap.

He saw the gap now the way other people saw a face.

Page forty-one. The numbering jumped from forty to forty-two, and the stub of the torn page was still there in the binding, ragged at the gutter, torn by hand and torn in haste—no, not haste. He corrected himself, lying in the dark. Torn *deliberately*. A hurried tear leaves a diagonal. This one was straight, a clean vertical pull, the work of someone who took a breath first and meant it. Someone who wanted the file to look whole at a glance and hollow at a read.

And he had read the page before it was torn. That was the thing he had understood only slowly, over years, the way you understand a photograph of yourself: he had catalogued the file on a Tuesday, and the file had been complete, and the torn page had been in his hands, under the lamp, in his memory forever. He had simply never known it mattered.

Now he lay back on the motel bedspread, laced his fingers behind his head, and opened the page.

---

The paper was onionskin, the typewriter a Royal with a slightly depressed *e*—he could see the letter sitting lower than its neighbors, a small valley in every line. The header read:

**BENEFICIARY RIDER — POLICY NO. 4471-C — WREN, ALDOUS**

*In consideration of premiums paid and continuing, the undersigned directs that upon disclosure of certain matters pertaining to the dissolution of the partnership of Wren & Bell, Riverworks concern, the sum of ten thousand dollars ($10,000) shall be paid to:*

And then the name, and Marguerite had to make himself see it whole, because his mind wanted to flinch the way a tongue finds a broken tooth.

*Miss Lavinia Bell, daughter of Cyrus Bell, of Bellhaven.*

*Payable upon disclosure.*

He sat up in the dark.

*Upon disclosure.* Not upon death. Not upon maturity of the policy. Not upon proof of loss. Upon *disclosure*—upon the telling of a thing. Ten thousand dollars in 1961 was a house, was a life, was a decade of a schoolteacher's wages, and it had been promised to the daughter of the dead partner on the single condition that somebody told the truth about something. The rider didn't say what. The rider didn't have to say what. The rider was a tripwire, a landmine buried in a filing cabinet: the moment anyone ever disclosed *the matters pertaining to the dissolution of the partnership*, the money would move, and the moving money would point like a compass needle at the truth.

Aldous Wren had built a dead man's switch out of insurance paper.

Marguerite got up and turned on the desk lamp and found the hotel notepad and wrote, because the discipline of writing helped him hold the frame steady, the way a camera operator breathes out before pressing the shutter. He wrote in his small, even hand:

*Page 41. Rider 4471-C. Beneficiary: Lavinia Bell. Condition: disclosure. Signed —* and here he stopped, because the signature line was the part he had to go back for.

He closed his eyes. Back to the basement. The lamp with the green shade. Mr. Pruitt's radio playing a Braves game too low to follow. The file in his hands, third of eleven that afternoon.

The signature was not Aldous Wren's. He was sure of that the way he was sure of the coffee ring. Wren's signature appeared twice earlier in the file—on the original policy application and on a change-of-address form from 1958—and it was a tall, impatient scrawl, the *A* like a leaning fence post. The signature on the rider was different: smaller, careful, the letters of an accountant or a clergyman, someone who wrote slowly on purpose.

*H. Pruitt, for the company.*

His own boss's name. His boss's father, rather—the elder Pruitt, dead now twenty years, who had witnessed the rider in March of 1961, eleven days after Aldous Wren's funeral, which meant the rider had been executed *after* Wren was in the ground. Which meant either the company had honored a document signed before death—

—or Aldous Wren had not been dead when it was signed.

Marguerite put the pen down. His hand was steady. His pulse was not.

*No,* he told himself. *Slow down. That's the vertigo talking.* There were a hundred explanations. A clerical date error. A backdated instrument. The kind of sloppiness that fills every archive in America. He was an insurance investigator; he had built a career on not leaping. He picked the pen back up and wrote the only thing he knew for certain:

*The page was torn out between Tuesday, June 12, 1979, and the file's next review. Someone did not want this rider found. Someone knew it existed.*

And then the second certainty, the one that sat in his stomach like a swallowed stone:

*Someone has been looking for it recently. Or someone has known about it all along, and has been waiting fifty years for the page to matter.*

---

Morning came gray and thin over the river, and Marguerite drove out to the county seat with the notepad in his jacket pocket and the memory in his head, which was the only copy of the rider left in the world, as far as he knew.

The Carrow County Records Annex was a converted feed store with a new handicap ramp bolted over the old loading dock, and it smelled the way all county archives smelled: dust, glue, and the faint vinegar of degrading microfilm. The clerk behind the desk was a young woman with reading glasses pushed up into her hair, and she had the wary friendliness of someone whose job was mostly telling people no.

"Marguerite Sterling," he said, and offered the card. "Independent investigations. I'm working on a probate matter touching an old Halloway & Pruitt file—estate of Aldous Wren, 1961. I'd like to see your copy."

"Copy of what, exactly?"

"The estate file. Counties used to take duplicate filings on anything touching a death benefit. It would be in probate cross-reference, probably under 61-something."

She looked it up with the unhurried competence of the recently trained. "Estate of Aldous Wren... here. One folder, forty-one items." She frowned at the screen. "Checked out."

Marguerite felt the morning tilt a degree. "Checked out," he repeated. "County archives don't circulate."

"They don't." She turned the monitor slightly toward him, an act of small rebellion he'd seen a hundred times in a hundred clerks. The entry was there, plain as a confession: **OUT — 03/14 — HOLLOW, E.** "That's... that's odd," she said. "That's a paper checkout. We went digital in 2019. Somebody entered this into the old system."

"Who is E. Hollow?"

She typed. Frowned again. "I can pull the card file." She went into the back room and was gone long enough for Marguerite to read the fire extinguisher's inspection tag—*last serviced 14 months ago*—and to notice, without deciding to notice, that the probate stacks were organized by decade, and that the 1960s bay was the third from the window, and that the window's latch was painted shut but the frame showed fresh scratches around it, as if someone had recently tried.

The clerk came back holding an index card as if it were a dead bird.

"E. Hollow," she said. "Elias Hollow. Died in—" she checked the card, though she clearly already knew— "died in 1988. He was a county surveyor. He's been dead thirty-seven years, and his checkout card says he took the Wren estate file out on March fourteenth. Three weeks ago."

The silence in the annex had a current under it too.

"Has the file ever been checked out before?" Marguerite asked. "In the whole history of the card?"

She flipped the card over. The back was blank. The front had one entry.

"That's the thing," she said quietly, the way people speak when a building they trust has creaked. "This card's never been touched. Nobody has ever wanted this file. And now a dead man wants it."

---

He walked the length of the annex's reading room without reading anything, which was its own kind of reading. The window with the scratched frame. The third bay. He stood at the 1960s stacks and ran his finger along the spines—*Ward, Weatherby, Whitcomb*—and felt the absence the way you feel a missing tooth with your tongue. The folder would have been here, between Weatherby and Whitcomb. He could see the gap in the dust pattern on the shelf edge, a clean stripe where something had sat for sixty years and then, three weeks ago, had not.

Three weeks ago. He did the arithmetic standing there, and the arithmetic was ugly. The exhumation order had been posted in early March—cemetery relocation, routine, the parish moving its dead up the hill away from the floodplain. The checkout was March fourteenth. The coffin had come up empty on the twenty-second.

Someone had read the county copy of the Wren file *before* the exhumation. Someone had known what was in it—known enough to want the last physical copy gone—*and then the coffin had come up empty anyway.*

Which meant the empty coffin was not a mystery that had happened to coincide with a stolen file. The empty coffin and the stolen file were the same event, viewed from two angles. Somebody had opened a grave and emptied a filing cabinet in the same month, and both acts pointed at the same buried thing: a page that said a dead founder had promised ten thousand dollars for the telling of a truth about 1961.

Marguerite went back to the desk. "The checkout card," he said. "May I photograph it? And the screen entry?"

The clerk hesitated, then slid both toward him. "Don't put my name in whatever this is," she said. "My supervisor signs off on the church historical society's requests. Half this town is on the Friends of the Parish board." She said it without bitterness, as a fact of weather. "Dead man's name or not, somebody's going to ask who pulled this card."

"I'll be discreet."

"They always say that." She almost smiled. "The last man who asked about the Wren estate was here in February. Nice older fellow. Said he was doing family history."

Marguerite went very still. "Name?"

"He didn't leave one. Paid the photocopy fee in cash. But he signed the visitor log, they all do." She turned the log around and ran a finger down February's dates and stopped. "There. February ninth."

The signature was careful, small, the letters of a man who wrote slowly on purpose. *H. Dane.* And beside it, in the purpose column, in the same careful hand: *Personal research.*

Sheriff Dane had been in the county annex five weeks before the exhumation, looking at the Wren estate file—or looking at the hole where it would soon be.

*Careful,* Marguerite told himself, and the word had Mr. Pruitt's voice on it, the old claims man's voice, *don't leap, boy, walk.* A sheriff doing personal research in his own county's records was not a crime. A sheriff whose town was about to dig up its founder might reasonably look at the founder's estate. Half the town had been looking. Effie Marsh had been looking; Effie Marsh had told him so, in her kitchen that smelled of cedar and old paper, with her oral history laid out like a quilt that didn't match the official pattern at any seam.

*And how is Effie,* he thought, *and who else has been to see her lately,* and the thought arrived with a coldness he didn't authorize, and he put it down on the notepad anyway, because the discipline was the discipline:

*People who have touched this file: me, 1979, complete. The torn-page hand, date unknown. H. Dane, Feb 9, photocopies. E. Hollow, dead 37 years, March 14, took the folder. The exhumation, March 22, ledger in the coffin.*

*Question: did Dane take the folder, and sign a dead man's name because his own would be remembered? Or did someone use Dane's research as a mask?*

*Question: who tears a page in 1961 and waits fifty years to steal the rest?*

*Answer forming: someone who thought the page was dead. Someone who only learned it wasn't.*

He thanked the clerk, left his card because leaving no card would have been louder than leaving one, and walked out into the gray morning. The river was up; he could smell it. On the courthouse lawn, workers were unloading a crate the size of a coffin, stenciled on its side in fresh black letters: **WREN MONUMENT — FOUNDER'S DAY UNVEILING.**

He stood there a moment longer than a man with no interest in local pageantry would stand.

Then he got in his car, and before he started it, he opened the notepad and wrote one more line, the line he had been circling since the annex, the line that turned the mystery over and showed its underside:

*The rider pays on disclosure. Which means every person who might disclose is now either a beneficiary or a threat. Find out which one Lavinia Bell's family is. Find out who else knows the page by heart.*

*And find Effie Marsh before someone else does.*
