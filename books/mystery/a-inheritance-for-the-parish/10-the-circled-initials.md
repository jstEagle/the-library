## 10. The Circled Initials

Marguerite Sterling had spent thirty years reading other people's paper, and he had learned that a list was never just a list. A grocery list told you about appetite. A creditor's list told you about desperation. And a list of initials written in the margin of a dead man's insurance file, in ink that had gone brown as old blood, told you about fear.

He sat in his rented room above the Bellhaven pharmacy with Case File 61-114 spread across the desk, though *spread* was generous. The file lived now only in his head; the physical pages were back in cold storage, and after what had happened to the county archive copy, he wasn't going to touch them again. Instead he worked from memory, which was the one archive nobody could break into—though someone, he suspected, was trying to find the key.

The marginalia sat in the lower corner of the final page, four sets of initials in Aldous Wren's cramped, slanted hand:

**A.W. — C.B. — V.A. — H.T.**

Two of them were accounted for by history. Aldous Wren himself. Cyrus Bell, the partner who "moved away" in 1961 and left no forwarding address because, Marguerite now believed, he had never moved anywhere at all.

That left two initials belonging to two families that had, in 1961, been the other pillars of Bellhaven's founding generation: the Ashcombes and the Thibodeauxes. Wren had written all four names together, and then he had done something deliberate. He had drawn a single circle around **V.A.**, pressing hard enough that the pen had nearly torn through the page.

Marguerite had stared at that circle for a long time the night before, coffee going cold beside him, asking himself the question an investigator always asks of a dead man's handwriting: *Is this an accusation, or is this a warning?*

There was only one way to find out. You followed the money. There was always money.

* * *

The Bellhaven county recorder's office occupied the ground floor of the old federal building, a granite pile from the WPA era with a brass plaque honoring civic improvement and a smell of toner and dust that Marguerite found more comforting than any church. The clerk on duty, a young man named Petey with a face like unbaked dough, recognized him from the exhumation crowd.

"You're the insurance man," Petey said. "The one Sheriff Dane told to—"

"To consult," Marguerite said pleasantly. "I'm consulting."

Petey looked at him the way small-town clerks look at outsiders who have already made enemies: with sympathy and a strong instinct for self-preservation. But he pulled the land books anyway, because Marguerite asked in the tone of a man who has never once been refused access to a public record and does not intend to start.

Marguerite did not need to search so much as confirm. His memory held the plat maps of Bellhaven from the estate files of 1974, when he'd catalogued a dozen deeds connected to the Wren holdings. He needed only the last eighteen months laid against what he already knew.

The pattern emerged inside an hour, and it was ugly.

The Thibodeaux family had owned two hundred acres of riverfront bottomland since before the war—the good soil, the kind that grew anything and forgave everything. In March of last year, Harlan Thibodeaux III had sold it to a holding company called Meridian River Holdings LLC for less than a third of assessed value. Marguerite ran the buyer's registered agent through the state database on his phone while Petey watched over his shoulder with frank curiosity. Meridian's agent was a law firm in the capital. The law firm's managing partner had clerked, forty years ago, for Judge Edmund Ashcombe.

"Fast settlement, too," Petey offered, warming despite himself. "Old Man Thibodeaux had been fighting the highway easement for a decade. Sued everybody. Then one day last spring he just—signed. Moved to his daughter's place in Tennessee. Didn't even have a yard sale."

"When exactly?"

"April." Petey frowned. "Right around when his son's boating accident was, I guess. Everybody said how lucky it was Harlan Junior didn't drown."

"Lucky," Marguerite repeated, and wrote nothing down, because he never wrote anything down.

The second family fell faster. The Delacroixes—no, not Delacroix; he corrected himself; the founding family matching the fourth pillar had been the Hollands, and the Holland name had died out into the Ashcombe line by marriage in the seventies, which meant the circle around V.A. might encompass more than one household. But the third set of initials, H.T., belonged beyond doubt to the Thibodeauxes, and the fourth name on Wren's list—

He stopped himself. Four initials. A.W., C.B., V.A., H.T. Three families plus the dead. And yet three of the four living lines had recently behaved like people paying off blackmail.

Because that was what it was. Marguerite had seen the shape of it often enough in fraud cases: not the crude shakedown but the slow tithe. Land sold cheap. Lawsuits settled fast. Families thinned by departure or misfortune until only the trunk remained. Someone had been collecting from the descendants of 1961 for years—quietly, patiently, the way interest compounds—and every payment was itself evidence, which meant every payer was also a hostage.

Three of the four had paid.

Which left Vivian Ashcombe.

* * *

He walked past the Ashcombe house first without stopping, the way you're taught to do—a surveyor's pass, eyes forward, taking the whole scene in one sweep rather than staring at any part of it.

The house sat on Calloway Hill above the town, white columns and green shutters, older than any of the brick storefronts below and better kept than all of them. Live oaks framed the drive. A gravel turnaround gleamed fresh-raked. On the front lawn, between the drive and the road, stood a modest granite marker—not a grave, Marguerite noted, but a memorial stone, the kind families erect when they want the appearance of remembrance without the inconvenience of proximity:

**EDMUND ASHCOMBE, 1921–1988. JUDGE. FOUNDER'S DESCENDANT. "THE LAW IS THE TOWN'S MEMORY."**

Marguerite stood on the public sidewalk for perhaps four seconds, long enough to register three things. First, a security camera under the eave of the carriage house, new, its housing still bright white. Second, a man pruning roses along the side fence who did not look up and did not need to. Third, the curtains in the upstairs parlor window, which shifted once, gently, as though a hand had released them.

By the time he reached the bottom of Calloway Hill, his phone buzzed.

The number was local. The voice, when he answered, was dry and precise and old, the voice of a woman who had spent eighty years making other people wait.

"Mr. Sterling. This is Vivian Ashcombe. You've been looking at my family's records at the courthouse today, and yesterday you were at the library asking Effie Marsh questions she had no business answering, and the day before that you stood in a cemetery watching us dig up a founder." A pause, unhurried. "You are a very busy man for someone on no business of your own."

"Mrs. Ashcombe—"

"I am inviting you to tea. Tomorrow, four o'clock. You will come alone, and you will not bring anything to record, because I will know, and it would be tedious for both of us." Another pause. "You may bring your memory, Mr. Sterling. I'm told it's considerable. Mine is too."

The line went dead before he could answer, which was, he understood, the point. She hadn't invited him. She had summoned him, the way you summon a witness—or a suspect—or prey.

He stood on the riverbank below the hill, watching brown water carry last night's rain toward the sea, and let himself feel the thing he had been refusing to feel since the coffin came up empty.

For six days he had believed himself the hunter. He had imagined himself moving unseen through Bellhaven's records while whoever emptied that grave scrambled in the dark behind him. It was a flattering picture, and flattering pictures are how investigators die.

The truth was simpler and colder. Effie Marsh had fallen from her ladder nine days after talking to him. The county archive copy had vanished under a dead man's name. Ruthie's grandmother's house had been searched. Every person who touched this story was being catalogued, contacted, or removed—and now he had received a phone call from the one woman whose family name was circled in a dead man's ink, a call demonstrating that she knew his movements day by day, hour by hour.

She wasn't reacting to his investigation.

She was mapping him.

And the invitation to tea was not an opening move. It was a checkmark—the moment she confirmed what he knew, measured how much, and decided whether he was a problem to be managed or a page to be torn out.

Marguerite turned back toward town. Behind him, on the hill, the parlor curtains hung still again, and the rose-pruner clipped steadily along the fence line, patient as a clock.

Four o'clock tomorrow. He would go, because not going would tell her more than going. He would drink her tea and admire her silver and answer her courtesy with courtesy, and somewhere between the first cup and the second, one of them would learn exactly how much the other knew.

He only hoped that when her hands shook—and they would shake, everyone's hands shook eventually—it would be over something he'd planned for.

* * *

That night he did not sleep so much as stand watch over his own mind, walking the file again in the dark the way a soldier walks a perimeter. Page fourteen, the rider. Page twenty-two, the beneficiary clause. The margin, the four initials, the circle pressed hard enough to scar the paper.

A.W. — C.B. — V.A. — H.T.

Wren had written them all together, which meant they had all been together—in a room, at a table, at some moment when the four of them became the kind of secret that requires a list. And then he had circled one.

Not the partner he'd lost. Not himself. Vivian Ashcombe—or her husband, the judge, or whichever A. stood behind that initial in 1961. Wren had spent fifty years building a machine out of ledger entries and buried bones, and at the center of it, the one name he'd bothered to mark, was the family that still owned the hill above the town.

At 3 a.m., Marguerite got up and wrote nothing down, and remembered everything, and understood that tomorrow he would be drinking tea with either the spider or the fly—and that from where he sat, at this particular moment, he honestly could not tell which chair he was sitting in.
