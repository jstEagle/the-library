## 15. Who Read It Last

The kitchen of Ruthie's grandmother's house still smelled of the break-in—dust knocked loose from places dust had lived for decades, and beneath it the faint sweetness of old paper disturbed. They had swept up the glass from the back door two days ago, but Marguerite could still see, in his mind's eye, the exact scatter of it: a fan of shards reaching four feet from the doorframe, consistent with a shoulder-driven entry, not a pry bar. Whoever had come through hadn't been hurried. Hadn't been afraid. Had simply arrived, done what they came for, and left the house looking like a storm had walked through it.

Now the same kitchen table held the only tools Marguerite trusted anymore: a legal pad, two pencils sharpened to needles, and a pot of coffee Ruthie had made strong enough to strip varnish.

"Start at the beginning," Ruthie said. She'd pulled her hair back with a rubber band, and she sat the way her grandmother must have sat—spine straight, hands flat on the table, a woman preparing to hear something she'd already half-paid for. "The file. When did it come into existence?"

Marguerite closed his eyes. He didn't need to; the file lived behind his eyelids the way a photograph lives in a drawer, whole and permanent. But closing his eyes helped him speak in the file's own order instead of the order his fear wanted.

"Case File 61-114. Opened September 4th, 1961, following the drowning death of Cyrus Bell at the Bellhaven millrace. Closed October 19th, 1961, ruled accidental. The estate file was consolidated into the archive in 1963, when probate on the Wren holdings finished. I catalogued it in the summer of 1978. I was sixteen."

"And who touched it between 1963 and 1978?"

"That's what we're building." He wrote *1963 — consolidated, probate clerk* at the top of the pad. "The county archive was a two-person operation until 1974. One senior clerk, one assistant. Before that, one clerk and whatever boy they hired for the summer."

"Name the clerk."

"Edmund Hale." Marguerite wrote it. The pencil moved steadily. His hand always moved steadily; that was the curse of him. "Hale ran the archive from 1949 until his death in 1976. Heart attack, at his desk, they say. He trained every assistant who came through for twenty-five years."

"Was he careful?"

"Hale invented careful." Marguerite allowed himself a thin smile. "He was the one who taught me that a file isn't paper, it's a chain of custody. Every checkout signed. Every return verified. He kept a ledger of the ledger. If a moth landed on a document, he'd have written down the date."

"Then he'd have recorded every person who read 61-114."

"He'd have recorded every *authorized* person." Marguerite tapped the pencil against the pad, once, twice. "That's the distinction that matters. The checkout ledger tells us who came through the front door. It doesn't tell us who Hale let in through the back."

Ruthie frowned. "You think he let someone in the back?"

"I think somebody read that file recently. Recently enough to quote it to me in the dark—page numbers, clause numbers, the marginalia. That's not memory from 1961. That's familiarity. That's a person who has sat with those pages the way I sat with them." He drew a line down the center of the pad. "So we build both columns. The front door and the back door."

* * *

They worked through the morning, and the house worked with them. It was a strange thing, Marguerite thought, how a ransacked room could become an office. The overturned drawers of the sideboard had been righted but never quite re-sorted, so that the house itself seemed to lean in and listen. Ruthie's grandmother looked down from a sepia photograph above the stove—a narrow-faced woman with Cyrus Bell's jaw and an expression that suggested she had waited her whole life for someone to finally say a true thing in her kitchen.

The front-door column filled first, and it filled fast.

"1963 to 1971: no checkouts," Marguerite read from memory. "The file sat. 1972, one checkout—county surveyor, road easement dispute on the Wren parcel. He'd have pulled the deed annexes, not the case file itself. I remember his signature; he signed with a fountain pen, blue ink, and the nib caught on the page and left a scratch through the *y* in his name. Hale noted the scratch in the margin. Of course he did."

Ruthie almost smiled at that. Almost.

"1974 through 1978: nothing. 1979 to 1984, I'm the assistant—I know every checkout because I logged half of them myself. Nothing on 61-114. Then 1985, one checkout, county attorney, boundary dispute again. 1986 through 1999: nothing. 2000: nothing. 2001—"

He stopped.

Ruthie looked up. "2001 what?"

"Nothing," Marguerite said slowly. "That's what's wrong. The ledger shows nothing from 2001 to the present. But the county archive copy—the duplicate that's supposed to live in the courthouse vault—was checked out under the name of a man who died in 1998. I told you that. I found it three weeks ago."

"Which means the courthouse copy wasn't taken through the courthouse's front door either." Ruthie was leaning forward now. "Two archives. Two locked rooms. And the same file missing from both."

"Not missing from mine." Marguerite's voice went quiet. "The original is still in cold storage at my old employer's. I pulled it. I memorized it. It's the only complete copy in existence, and it exists now in exactly two places: a climate-controlled vault, and my head."

"Then whoever read it recently read the original."

"Or read it *before* it was the original. Before it mattered." Marguerite turned the pad around so she could see the columns. "Look at the shape of it. Forty years of nothing. The file was dead paper—nobody cared, because nobody knew what was missing. Then the exhumation is scheduled. The parish announces the cemetery relocation. And within weeks, the courthouse copy vanishes, Effie Marsh falls off a ladder, your grandmother's house gets torn apart, and a man in the dark recites clause numbers at me like a litany."

"Something woke it up," Ruthie said.

"The exhumation woke it up. Or the person who arranged the exhumation did." He let that sit between them. "Which brings us to the back door."

* * *

The back door was Edmund Hale, and Edmund Hale was dead, and that should have ended the question. It didn't.

"Hale had keys to everything," Marguerite said. "Cold storage, the courthouse annex, the record vaults. He was the archive for twenty-seven years. If anyone could open a file without a checkout signature, it was him."

"But he died in 1976."

"His access didn't die with him. That's the thing about trust—it outlives the person you gave it to." Marguerite wrote Hale's name at the top of the second column. "Now. Who had Hale's trust? Who could stand beside him in the vault, watch him open a file, and be forgotten the way furniture is forgotten?"

Ruthie's voice came out careful. "You said he trained every assistant."

"I did."

"How many?"

"Four that I know of. Two left for the state archives in the seventies—one died in 1991, one moved to Ohio and is, as far as I can confirm, still alive and still in Ohio. The third was a woman who lasted eight months and went into real estate. And the fourth—" Marguerite set the pencil down. He found he didn't want to hold it anymore. "The fourth was a boy Hale took on in 1968. Seventeen years old. Hale liked him, by all accounts. Mentored him the way Hale mentored everyone who was worth the trouble. The boy stayed on part-time through the academy—"

He heard Ruthie's breath change.

"—through the police academy. He'd come to the archive on his days off, help Hale rebind ledgers, learn the filing system. Hale called him, in his own handwriting in the staff log, *my protégé.*" Marguerite turned the pad fully toward her. At the bottom of the second column, in his needle-point hand, sat a name.

*Raymond Dane.*

The kitchen went very still. Outside, a truck ground past on the gravel, and neither of them turned to look.

"No," Ruthie said. But it wasn't denial, not exactly. It was the sound of a person testing a floorboard before putting weight on it. "No. The sheriff? He's been sheriff since—"

"Since 1989. Eighteen years. Before that, deputy. Before that, academy, 1971 to 1973, with weekend hours at the county archive under Edmund Hale." Marguerite recited it flatly, because the flatness was the only honest way to say it. "Ruthie, I've spent a week telling myself Dane is covering for someone. Corrupt, or scared, or paid. Every explanation except the one that fits."

"And the one that fits?"

"That he's not covering up the crime at all." Marguerite looked at the name on the pad. "That he's finishing it."

* * *

Ruthie stood and walked to the window, and Marguerite let her. He understood the need to put distance between yourself and a sentence. He'd been doing it for days.

"Say it plainly," she said, her back to him. "Say what you actually think happened."

"All right." He turned the pencil over in his fingers. "Edmund Hale dies in 1976. His protégé is twenty years old, already wearing a badge, already deep in a town where the founding families are generous to promising young men—I know they were generous to Dane, Vivian Ashcombe all but told me so at tea. Somewhere in those years, before or after the academy, Dane read Case File 61-114. Maybe Hale showed it to him as a lesson. Maybe Dane found it on his own. Either way, he read what Aldous Wren wrote in the margins—the initials, the circled name—and he understood what it was worth, and to whom."

"To the circle."

"To the circle. And here's the part that turns my stomach." Marguerite stood and joined her at the window. The yard was bright and ordinary; the river glinted through the trees. "A file like that is only dangerous if it surfaces. Dane could have destroyed it a hundred times. He had access for thirty-five years. But he didn't destroy it, and neither did anyone else—until now, and now they're not destroying it, they're *collecting* it. The courthouse copy. Effie Marsh's memory. Your grandmother's letter. Me." He paused. "You don't hunt down every copy of a document unless you intend to control it. And you don't control a confession unless you intend to keep it buried *and* keep the option of using it."

"Using it how?"

"Insurance, Ruthie. Leverage. That file is the founding families' death warrant, and someone has been holding it over them for decades. Why do you think three of the four families sold land cheap and settled lawsuits fast? Why do you think Vivian Ashcombe's hands shake when 1961 comes up? They've been paying. Quietly, for years, to a man with a badge who can make any investigation in this parish die of natural causes."

Ruthie turned to face him. Her eyes were wet, but her voice was level—her grandmother's jaw, her grandmother's spine. "Effie Marsh. Was that him?"

Marguerite thought of the ladder. The lock, new and unbroken, that should have been worn and loose. He thought of Sheriff Dane standing in the library parking lot, telling him the case was closed with heat that didn't fit a careless death.

"I don't know," he said. "That's the worst of it. I can't prove Dane pushed her, and I can't prove he didn't. But I know this: when I gave him a fabricated detail about the exhumation, he repeated it back wrong and warned me off like a man defending his own house. And when the man in the archive quoted the file to me, he quoted it like scripture. Page, clause, marginalia. There are two people alive who know that file that well. One of them is standing in this kitchen."

"And the other one is the law."

"The other one is the law." Marguerite gathered the pad from the table and looked at the two columns, the two names, the whole terrible arithmetic of it. "Edmund Hale, who is thirty years in his grave and can't defend himself. And Raymond Dane, who has spent his entire career being exactly where the law needed him to be, every single time the law needed to look the other way."

"Then we go to the state police. The attorney general. Someone above him."

"With what? A timeline built from a dead clerk's ledger and my memory?" He shook his head. "They'd laugh us out of the building, and Dane would know within the hour, and then Effie Marsh wouldn't be the last accident. We need what's in your grandmother's letter, Ruthie. We need the confession authenticated—Wren's hand, Wren's words. And we need it before the Founder's Day unveiling, because I am increasingly certain that ceremony isn't a celebration. It's a deadline."

Ruthie went to the sideboard. From the back of the middle drawer, behind a tangle of string and rubber bands, she lifted a sealed envelope—yellowed, soft at the corners, addressed in a hand Marguerite recognized from the file's marginalia because he had studied that hand until it lived in him like a second heartbeat.

*For Ruthie, when she is ready. — A.W.*

His breath stopped. "That's Wren's writing."

"I know." Ruthie held the envelope without opening it, her thumb resting on the seal the way you'd rest a hand on a sleeping animal. "I've had it for six years. I never opened it, because I thought it was just an old woman's letter. Now you're telling me the man who wrote it may have faked his own death and spent fifty years building a case against the people who killed my great-grandfather." Her voice didn't shake. "You're telling me my family has been holding the murder weapon this whole time."

"I'm telling you it might be."

Ruthie looked at the envelope a long moment. Then, instead of opening it, she set it down on the table between them—deliberately, like a card played face-down—and covered it with her palm.

"Not yet," she said. "First you tell me one more thing, Marguerite. If Dane read that file as a boy, if he's been the circle's collector all these years—then the night someone broke into this house, they were looking for this letter. They tore this place apart for it." Her eyes came up to his. "So how did they know I had it? Only three people alive knew my grandmother kept a sealed letter from Aldous Wren. Me, you—and whoever read the file closely enough to know a letter existed at all."

The clock in the hall ticked into the silence. Marguerite looked at the envelope, at the name on the legal pad, at the window where the river glittered, indifferent, on its way through Bellhaven.

"Founder's Day is Saturday," he said. "We have three days to find out whether the sheriff is the man we're running from—or the only man standing between us and him."
