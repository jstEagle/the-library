## 1. Two Envelopes, One Morning

The till told the same story it had been telling for three years, and Ada Nystrom was tired of hearing it.

She counted the morning's takings twice, as if arithmetic might soften under repetition. Forty-one pounds sixty. Two paperbacks, one hardback somebody's aunt had ordered in March and never collected, and a birthday card purchased by a man who clearly grabbed the nearest one with a hedgehog on it. The hedgehog card had saved the day. Ada made a small note of that — *hedgehogs sell* — and wrote it on the back of a receipt in her precise, slanted hand, the same hand that had once logged seventeen hours of interviews without a wobble.

Outside, Harrowgate High Street went about its business with the unhurried air of a town that had peaked sometime around the construction of the library. Rain tapped the shop window in a rhythm too irregular to be soothing. The gutter above the door dripped onto the welcome mat, which now read WELCOME only if you were generous about the E.

Five years since she'd closed the file for good. Five years since Daniel. Five years of alphabetizing other people's mysteries instead of solving her own.

"Morning, Ada!" called Mrs. Prentice from the doorway, shaking out an umbrella with the violence of a woman who held weather personally responsible. "Any new thrillers in?"

"Three," said Ada. "All featuring detectives who are retired but keep getting pulled back in."

Mrs. Prentice snorted. "Unrealistic. Once you're retired, you're retired." She bought the hedgehog-adjacent thriller anyway, because it was set in Cornwall and Cornwall sold, and left with the satisfied air of a woman who had done her civic duty.

Ada watched her go and thought: *that's exactly what I used to think.*

The bell over the door gave its tired jangle at half past ten, and with it came the post — one rubber-banded bundle dropped through the slot by the new postman, who was still learning which businesses wanted their mail behind the counter. Ada untied the band with her thumb. Bills. A publisher's catalogue. A flyer for a septic-tank service that had somehow concluded she was in the market.

And two envelopes that did not belong.

The first was cheap, the kind sold in packs of twenty at the newsagent's, addressed to *The Bookshop, High Street* in block capitals pressed so hard the pen had nearly gone through. No stamp — hand-delivered, then. She turned it over. Nothing. She weighed it in her palm the way she'd once weighed everything: evidence first, curiosity second.

Inside, a single sheet, torn from a lined pad.

*I killed Elias Vane. I pushed him into the water at Calder Reservoir on the night of the 14th of August, 1994. Everyone said it was an accident. It was not. I have carried this thirty years and I am tired. I am not asking forgiveness. I am telling someone before I go.*

No signature. The handwriting was shaky but deliberate, the letters of an old person who had learned penmanship when penmanship mattered.

Ada stood very still in the middle of her own shop, between the B's and the C's, and felt something turn over inside her chest like a key in a lock nobody had oiled in years.

Elias Vane. Drowned in the drought summer. She'd been a young detective then, newly transferred, handed the file because the older hands were busy with a burglary spree in Millbrook. She'd read the statements, noted the missing shoes — *who swims in his boots?* — and accepted the coroner's verdict because three witnesses swore Elias Vane was alive and well at midnight, drinking at the Boar's Head, hours after he must already have been dead. Three witnesses. Case closed. She'd even written the closing summary herself, neat as a hymn.

She had not thought about Elias Vane in years. That was not entirely true. She had thought about him every time the reservoir came up in conversation, every time a drought made the water drop and show the pale ribs of the old boathouse, every time she shelved a true-crime title and her hand hesitated a half-second longer than it needed to. But she had trained herself not to *think* about him, and she had been very good at her training.

Now someone had broken thirty years of silence with a ballpoint pen and a sheet of lined paper.

She reached for the second envelope almost without deciding to. This one was different — heavier stock, cream-colored, addressed simply to *A. Nystrom*. Her name. Not the shop. Her. The handwriting was elegant, old-fashioned, confident. No stamp either.

Inside, a card. On it, in the same fine hand, five words:

*The rest is where it was left.*

Ada read it four times. It meant nothing. It meant everything. It sat in her palm like a stone pulled warm from a riverbed — smooth, purposeless, impossible to put down.

*Where what was left? Where was anything left?*

She looked at the confession again. Then at the card. They didn't match — different paper, different hand, different temper. One was a wound; the other was an invitation. And both had arrived on the same grey Tuesday, in the same bundle, addressed to a failing bookshop run by a woman who hadn't opened a case file since the doctor told her to find a quieter life and she'd obeyed, because obeying was easier than grieving.

Her instincts stirred against her will, the way an old dog's ears lift at a sound it hasn't heard in a decade. She felt the familiar click of pieces wanting to fit. *Who drowns in his boots. Who confesses unsigned. Who writes to me by name.*

"Ada?" Theo from next door — no, not that Theo, the other one, the boy who delivered her coffee — poked his head in, saw her face, and wisely withdrew.

She locked the shop at noon, which she never did, and took the letters into the back room where the kettle lived and where, beneath a false bottom in the filing cabinet she'd bought secondhand and never questioned until she needed somewhere no one would look, her old notebooks slept. She didn't open the drawer. Not yet. She just stood there with her hand flat on the cold metal, the way you'd rest your hand on a sleeping animal you weren't sure you wanted to wake.

Daniel would have told her to leave it. Or he'd have told her to dig — Daniel had always contained multitudes, most of them contradictory. She missed him in the ordinary way now, the way you miss a coat: reaching for a sleeve that isn't there.

But this wasn't grief stirring. This was the other thing. The thing she'd been careful to starve.

Forty-one pounds sixty. A hedgehog card. A town that had peaked with its library. And two envelopes sitting on her counter like the opening move of a game she'd sworn off.

Ada made tea she forgot to drink, and while the rain kept its uneven time against the glass, she found herself doing the thing she'd promised herself she never would.

She started a list.

*1. Who delivered the letters?*
*2. Why a bookshop?*
*3. Why me?*

And underneath, in smaller writing, the question she'd been circling since the first sentence:

*4. Who else knows Elias Vane didn't drown by accident — and what have they been protecting for thirty years?*

The kettle clicked off unheard. Outside, the gutter dripped. And somewhere across town, in a house she hadn't entered since the funeral, the answer to question two was waiting — though she wouldn't understand that until much later, when the cost of knowing had already begun to mount.

Ada Nystrom picked up her pen, crossed out nothing, and underlined everything.

* * *

That night she dreamed of water going down, and down, and showing things at the bottom that everyone had agreed to call rocks.
