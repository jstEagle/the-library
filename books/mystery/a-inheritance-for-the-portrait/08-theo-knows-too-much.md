## 8. Theo Knows Too Much

Theo Aldous had three rules for working at Vane House, and he'd written them on the inside of his toolbox lid in his neatest handwriting, which was not very neat at all.

One: arrive before the family's people did, so nobody watched you let yourself in.
Two: fix what's broken, mention nothing that isn't.
Three: never, ever say the name *Vane* out loud in front of anyone whose last name wasn't Vane.

He'd added rule three himself, two years ago, after his uncle Gerald — who had held the caretaker's position before him, and his grandfather before that — took him aside on his first day and said, "You'll hear things in these walls, boy. Old things. Your job is to be a wall yourself." Then he'd handed Theo a ring of keys heavier than a newborn and driven off to his retirement bungalow in Marlow Bend, where, as far as Theo knew, he had not once looked back.

So when Ada Nystrom came up the gravel drive on a gray Thursday morning with her coat buttoned wrong — one button off, so the whole coat hung crooked, like a picture frame someone had nudged — Theo felt all three rules sit up inside him like dogs hearing a car in the lane.

"Morning," she said. "I'm sorry to bother you. I was in the neighborhood."

Nobody was ever in the neighborhood of Vane House. It sat at the end of a mile of private road above Calder Reservoir, surrounded by pines that had been old when the reservoir drowned the valley under them. You went there on purpose or you didn't go at all.

"You're the bookshop lady," Theo said, because it seemed safer than saying anything else.

"I'm the bookshop lady." She smiled. It was a good smile, warm around the edges, but Theo noticed — the way you notice things when you've grown up learning to notice them without appearing to — that her eyes were doing inventory. The roofline. The shutters. The windows of the long gallery where the portrait hung. "Theo, is it? Gerald's grandson?"

"That's me." He set down the gutter scoop he'd been carrying, mostly so his hands would have something honest to do. "We're closed to visitors till spring. Family's orders."

"I'm not a visitor," Ada said. "I'm a customer. Sort of. I'd like to buy your time for an hour, if it's for sale."

Theo looked past her shoulder at the drive, empty except for a jay scolding something invisible. Rule two said: mention nothing that isn't broken. But there was a fourth rule he'd never written down, one he kept somewhere behind his sternum, and it said: *you are twenty-four years old and you have been afraid of this house your whole life and you are tired.*

"How much?" he said.

* * *

They ended up in the kitchen, because kitchens were neutral territory. The great rooms of Vane House belonged to dead people; the kitchen still smelled faintly of the coffee Theo made every morning and drank alone at the scrubbed pine table while the house ticked and settled around him like an old man rearranging himself in a chair.

Ada declined coffee, then accepted it when he'd already poured it, which told him she was being polite about wanting it.

"Gerald trained you?" she asked.

"He trained my dad, and my dad left, so it skipped a generation. Like a curse." He meant it as a joke. It didn't quite land as one. "He used to say the house talks if you listen long enough. I thought that was caretaker nonsense until my first winter. Pipes do sound like whispering. I slept with the radio on for a month."

"And did it tell you anything? The house?"

Theo turned his mug a quarter-turn on the table. Here it was, then. The fork in the road. Down one path: the wall. Down the other: whatever this woman was buying with whatever she'd offered, which had turned out to be forty dollars and a first-edition Ruth Rendell from the shop's back room, pressed into his hands before he could refuse.

"There was an appraiser," he said. "Maybe six years ago now. The family brought him in — insurance thing, after the storm damaged part of the roof and they had to claim on the collection. Water damage in the gallery. Most of the paintings were fine but they wanted everything valued anyway, top to bottom."

Ada had produced a small notebook. She didn't open it. She just held it, the way some people hold a cigarette they're trying not to smoke.

"His name was Fennimore. Mr. Fennimore. Little guy, glasses like bottle bottoms, talked to paintings more than to people. Honest — I liked him. He'd stand in front of a canvas and mutter at it, 'Come on, then, what are you hiding,' like it owed him money." Theo almost smiled at the memory. It was one of the few memories of Vane House that didn't have frost on it. "He was here nine days. On day ten he was gone. Didn't finish the job. Just packed his van and left, and when Mrs. Aldous's office called his firm, they said he'd taken early retirement. Moved to his sister's in Scotland. Nobody here ever mentioned him again."

"What happened on day nine?"

Theo took a breath. It shook slightly, which annoyed him. Courage, he'd learned, wasn't the absence of shaking; it was doing the thing while your hands betrayed you, and hoping nobody filmed it.

"The portrait," he said. "The big one. Elias Vane, over the gallery fireplace. Marguerite painted it — that's the famous part, the widow painting her drowned husband, whole town wept, et cetera. It's the crown jewel of the collection. Fennimore spent two full days on it. Longer than anything else. And on the last evening he asked me to help him take it down off the wall, and I helped him, and he examined the back of it under a raking light for — God, it must have been an hour. He barely looked at the front again after that."

"What did he find?"

"He didn't say. That's the thing. He just went very quiet, in a way that was louder than talking. Then he asked me — and I remember this exactly, because it was such a strange question — he asked me if the frame had ever been off the canvas. Reframed, reglazed, anything. And I said I didn't know, and he said, 'Find out. Quietly.' So I did what I always do. I went to the records room and I read."

Ada leaned forward. Outside, the jay had given up its argument and the silence of the house pressed in, thick as the water at the bottom of the reservoir.

"The reframing receipt is in the estate files," Theo said. "Nineteen sixty-one. Seven years after Elias drowned. The work order says the original frame was water-damaged — which is strange, because the portrait hung in the dryest room in the driest house in the county — and a new frame was fitted by a framer in Ashford. And here's the part I can't stop thinking about." He looked up at her. "The invoice line item. It doesn't say *reframe*. It says *reframe and reline*. Relining means putting new canvas backing onto the old canvas. It means somebody opened the sandwich. Canvas, lining, stretcher bars, backing board — the whole layered thing comes apart, and goes back together, and anything could go in between, and nobody looking at the front would ever know."

Ada's notebook was open now. She wrote one word and underlined it twice.

"Fennimore saw the seam," she said quietly.

"Fennimore saw the seam." Theo nodded. "And two days later he was gone. And I've done the arithmetic on that about four hundred times, Ms. Nystrom, and every time it comes out the same. Men don't retire early to Scotland because a painting has a seam. Men leave town overnight because somebody with money explained their options to them."

* * *

She asked to see the photograph, and Theo's stomach dropped, because he hadn't mentioned a photograph yet, and her asking for it meant she'd already known there would be one, which meant she knew more than he did, which was a feeling he hated and craved in equal measure.

"It's not much," he said, going to the dresser drawer where he kept the things that didn't belong anywhere else. "Fennimore took pictures of everything. Insurance documentation. But this one he printed extra copies of, and on the last morning he gave me one. He said—" Theo stopped. His throat had gone tight.

"He said what?"

"He said, 'Keep this where nobody tall looks for it. One day someone short will ask.'" Theo laughed, a small cracked sound. "I thought he was being funny. He wasn't a funny man. I think he was scared and it came out sideways."

He laid the photograph on the pine table between them, face up, and Ada went still in a way Theo recognized from his grandfather's stories — the stillness of people who had once hunted other people for a living, the stillness of a heron over water.

It was a photograph of the back of the portrait. To anyone else it would have been nothing: brown board, wooden stretcher bars crossing in a grid, brass plates, dust. But Theo had spent six years looking at it, and he walked her through it the way Gerald had taught him to walk visitors through the grounds — slowly, pointing.

"Here. Along the lower third. See how the light catches differently?" There was a line running nearly the full width of the backing board, straight as a ruled pencil mark, and along it, if you knew to look, the faint shadow of a join. "Backing boards come in one piece, normally. This one's been opened and closed. Sealed again — wax or shellac over the seam, aged to match. Somebody wanted it invisible. And somebody else found it anyway."

Ada touched the edge of the photograph, not the image itself, careful as touching a sleeping animal. "Sixty-one years," she said. "It's been in there since 1961. Or something was put in in 1961. Or—" She stopped herself, and Theo watched her decide not to say the rest aloud, and respected her for it.

"Or it was put in earlier and discovered in 1961," Theo finished for her, because he'd had six years of nights to think it. "And instead of taking it out, whoever opened it... sealed it back up. Kept it. Why would you keep a secret you found in a dead man's portrait?"

"Because of what it's worth," Ada said. "Not money. Leverage. Or guilt. Or both." She looked up at him, and her eyes were kind and terrible at once. "Theo. Does the family know about the photograph?"

"No." The answer came fast and hard, faster than his fear could edit it. "Gerald didn't know. My dad didn't know. Nobody knows. It's been in a sock drawer through two moves and my grandmother's funeral." He heard his own voice climb and made himself stop, breathe. "That's the thing, Ms. Nystrom. That's why I'm telling you this, when every rule I've got says don't. Because I've been keeping this house's secrets since I was twelve years old, and I never minded, because they were just — atmosphere. Ghosts. The kind of secrets that make a place interesting. But this one has a seam in it. This one was *made*. Somebody built it, and somebody hid it, and somebody chased off the one man who found it, and those are all things people *do*, and I keep thinking—" His voice did the climbing thing again and he let it. "I keep thinking about Hollis Brandt."

Ada's pen stopped moving.

"You know him," Theo said. "Everyone knows him. He was the night watchman at the reservoir boathouse, back in the nineties. My granddad played cards with him every Thursday for thirty years. And three weeks ago Hollis started coming by the house, walking up the drive unannounced, which Hollis never did, standing in the courtyard looking up at the gallery window like he was waiting for the painting to wave at him. Last time, he asked me — he'd had a couple, his hands were shaking — he asked me if the portrait was still hanging. *Still hanging,* like it might not be. Like he needed to check on it." Theo swallowed. "And then two weeks ago he stopped coming by, and yesterday I heard my aunt say he'd had a fall on his stairs, poor old soul, and everyone said *how sad* and moved on, and I stood there in the kitchen holding a watering can and I thought: *that's the second man.*"

"The second man," Ada repeated.

"Fennimore left. Hollis fell. Both of them after the portrait." Theo spread his hands. "I'm not smart like you, Ms. Nystrom. I fix gutters. But I can count to two."

For a long moment Ada said nothing. The house ticked around them. Somewhere upstairs a door eased shut on its own weight, the way doors did in old houses, and Theo watched Ada track the sound without turning her head, and understood that she was not entirely comfortable in this kitchen either, and drew an unreasonable amount of courage from the company.

Then she tore the page from her notebook — carefully, along the perforation, precise even in haste — folded it once, and slid it across the table to him.

"If anyone asks what we talked about today," she said, "you showed me the leak in the gallery ceiling and quoted me an outrageous price to fix it, and I said I'd think about it. That's all. Nothing else happened."

Theo unfolded the page. It was a single line, in her small square handwriting:

*If I don't come back, give this to the police. Not the local police. County.*

He looked at it for a long time. Then he folded it back along her crease, went to the dresser, and put it in the sock drawer, behind the photograph, where nobody tall would look for it.

"They'll know I talked to you," he said. "Mrs. Aldous knows everything that happens on this road. There's a man who fishes below the weir and reports license plates, I'm fairly sure. I've never proven it, but I'm fairly sure."

"Probably," Ada agreed, standing, buttoning her crooked coat properly this time, one button at a time, until it hung true. "But here's the difference between us and them, Theo. They've spent thirty years making sure nothing changes. We only need one thing to change." She paused at the kitchen door, and the gray light from the hall caught her face, and for a moment she looked less like a bookshop lady and more like what Gerald had always claimed the walls whispered about: the kind of person who arrived in towns like this one, and after whom towns were never quite the same. "One seam," she said. "That's all it takes. Everything hidden comes apart along its seam."

After she'd gone — gravel crunching, engine fading, the jay resuming its grievances — Theo stood in the kitchen a while, listening to the house tick.

Then he climbed the back stairs to the long gallery, quiet as a wall, and stood in front of the portrait of Elias Vane, drowned man, painted by his widow, hanging over the cold fireplace in its 1961 frame.

"Come on, then," Theo said softly, to the canvas, to the seam, to the sixty-one years of patience behind it. "What are you hiding?"

The painting said nothing. Paintings were good at that. But for the first time in his life, Theo Aldous looked at it and felt sorry for it — hung there facing the room, keeping its secret face-out, while everything that mattered waited behind its back.

Down the drive, far off, a car door slammed. Not Ada's — hers had gone minutes ago. Theo stepped to the window and watched a dark sedan idle at the gate, too far to read the plate, close enough to mean something.

Rule two, he thought. Fix what's broken, mention nothing that isn't.

He picked up his gutter scoop and went back to work, whistling, badly, the way innocent men whistle.
