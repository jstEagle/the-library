## 13. Vivienne's Version

The invitation arrived on cream cardstock, hand-lettered, smelling faintly of lilies and money.

*Vivienne Aldous requests the pleasure of Ada Nystrom's company for tea, Thursday at four, Aldous House.*

Ada read it twice, standing behind the register with the shop's takings still spread out in front of her — forty-one dollars and sixty cents, one of them a Canadian quarter. She had been counting the same forty-one dollars for ten minutes because her mind kept sliding sideways to Hollis Brandt's funeral, which she had attended two days earlier, standing at the back of a chapel that smelled of lilies too, watching a doctor with soft eyes and an Aldous Foundation lapel pin explain to the mourners how a careful old man had simply missed his footing on his own stairs.

Lilies again. The town only owned so many flowers.

She turned the card over. On the back, in the same elegant hand: *I've taken the liberty of asking Theo to mind your shop. He seemed agreeable.*

Which meant Vivienne Aldous knew about Theo. Knew he worked for her, knew his hours, knew enough to arrange her afternoon like a dinner reservation. Ada set the card down on the counter, next to the Canadian quarter, and felt the particular coldness that used to visit her back when she carried a badge — the coldness of realizing you'd been watched longer than you'd been watching.

She went anyway. Of course she went. You don't decline an invitation from the spider just because you suspect the web.

* * *

Aldous House sat on the highest ground in Calder Falls, a gray stone pile built by men who'd wanted the whole valley visible from their breakfast table. It had the kind of gardens that made you understand why people once accepted feudalism — hedges clipped into obedience, roses trained along stone walls, everything green and gold and arranged.

Vivienne Aldous was waiting on the terrace, small and silver-haired in a lavender suit, pouring from a porcelain pot as though she'd been born holding it. Which, Ada reflected, she more or less had.

"Detective Nystrom." Vivienne smiled. "Or may I say Ada? We're neither of us young enough for formality to be worth the effort."

"Ada is fine."

"And you must call me Vivienne. Sit, sit. Earl Grey, or something stronger? I have a Darjeeling the bishop swears by, though I've never understood what bishops know about tea."

"Earl Grey is fine."

"Of course it is. It's everyone's fine." Vivienne handed her a cup with hands that were steady and smooth and seventy-some years old, and Ada thought: *these are the hands that signed whatever was signed.* Then she thought: *careful.* Thirty years of marriage to Daniel had taught her that the most dangerous assumptions were the satisfying ones.

They sat beneath a trellis heavy with white roses. Below them, the town lay spread out like a model railway — the mill lands, the reservoir glinting through the trees, Main Street with its awnings, and somewhere in it, small as a grain of rice, her bookshop.

"You'll forgive the theatrics of the invitation," Vivienne said. "But I find that if I ask people to meet me here, they arrive already half-convinced I'm harmless. Old ladies and teacups. It saves so much time."

"And are you harmless?"

"I'm seventy-eight, my hips are worse than the church roof, and I haven't lifted anything heavier than a trowel since the Reagan administration." She stirred her tea without looking at it. "Harmless is the wrong question, dear. The right question is whether I'm useful. To you, I mean. And I think I might be."

Ada sipped her tea. It was excellent, which annoyed her.

"Hollis Brandt died last week," she said.

"Yes. A tragedy. He was our night watchman for years — reliable man, quiet. The whole family sent flowers." Vivienne's face did something complicated and brief, like weather crossing water, and then settled back into pleasantness. "You attended the funeral. I saw you there. Back row, black coat, taking notes with your eyes. You never stopped being a detective, did you? They tell me it's like being an alcoholic. You're only ever between cases."

"What do you want, Mrs.— Vivienne?"

"What I want," Vivienne said, setting down her cup with a small click, "is to tell you a story. Because I'm told you're a woman who deals in stories now, and I believe this one has been told to you badly. By silence, mostly. Silence tells stories badly; it leaves all the casting to the listener's imagination. So let me cast it properly for you."

She folded her hands in her lap, and for a moment — just a moment — the pleasantness dropped away entirely, and Ada saw the woman underneath: sharp, patient, and old in a way that had nothing to do with years.

"In 1994," Vivienne began, "my father was seventy-one years old and had spent his life building the one thing this town ran on. Not the mill itself — Elias Vane owned the mill — but the land under it, the water rights, the leases, the rail spur. Half of what made the mill work belonged to us. My father leased it to Elias's father, and then to Elias, on terms that were generous to the point of charity, because that's what the Aldouses did. We held this town together the way a spine holds up a body. Unseen, unthanked, and occasionally resented."

"Elias wanted out," Ada said.

"Elias wanted *out*," Vivienne agreed, with something that was almost admiration. "That drought summer, he'd found a buyer — a development group from the city, offering him four times what the mill was worth, cash. But there was a complication. The sale included the water rights, and the water rights included our leases, and the buyer's lawyers wanted the leases terminated. Clean title. Which meant my father would lose the income that paid for the hospital wing and the library roof and half the scholarships this town hands out every June. Elias knew that. He negotiated anyway. He was going to sell the ground out from under us and leave town rich, and there wasn't a thing in any contract we could do about it."

"So half the town wanted him gone."

"Oh, more than half." Vivienne smiled thinly. "You were living here in '94, Ada. Do you remember that summer? The reservoir down to mud and bones, the mill running half-shifts, men lining up at the food bank in their own work boots? Elias Vane sat on his porch with a gin and tonic and told the Rotary Club that market forces were nobody's fault. When he drowned, do you know how many people came to me privately, over the following months, to say some version of *well, he had it coming*? Eleven. I counted. Eleven respectable citizens, including two deacons." She refilled Ada's cup without asking. "I'm not telling you this to excuse anything. I'm telling you so you understand the soil the story grew in. A town can love a man and still be relieved when he dies. Both things fit in the same heart. Usually at the same time."

"And Marguerite?"

Something moved behind Vivienne's eyes — a door closing on a lit room.

"Marguerite," she said carefully, "was a painter who married above her weight and knew it. She was beautiful and strange and she loved Elias in the way that storms love coastlines. Afterward — after the drowning — she was the one who kept the town's conscience. She funded the scholarship in his name, sat on every board, gave to every cause. Penance, people called it. I called it something else, but I never said what, because the dead deserve better than the living's arithmetic." She paused. "You've seen the portrait."

It wasn't quite a question. Ada kept her face still. "Everyone's seen the portrait."

"The famous portrait. Marguerite's masterpiece. It hangs at Vane House where it's hung for thirty years, and every art student and appraiser in three counties has stood in front of it and pronounced it a minor miracle of grief." Vivienne looked out over her roses. "My son Edmund had it insured, cleaned, reframed twice. It's the closest thing this family has to a relic." Her voice cooled by a degree. "So when I hear rumors — rumors, Ada, merely rumors — that someone has been asking questions about its provenance, about whether it's been opened, you understand how a mother's attention sharpens."

The teacup was very light in Ada's hand. She set it down before it could tremble.

"I don't know what you mean by *opened*."

"No," Vivienne said pleasantly. "I imagine you don't."

* * *

The second pot of tea arrived, and with it a plate of shortbread cut into the shape of the Aldous crest, which struck Ada as either charming or fascistic and possibly both.

"Let me tell you the rest of the story," Vivienne said, "the part the silence left out. Your husband."

The name landed in Ada's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She waited for the splash.

"Daniel Nystrom was the best police officer this town ever had," Vivienne said. "I mean that sincerely. In thirty years I watched him handle every hard thing Calder Falls produced — the drownings, the fires, the year the Kessler boy disappeared — and he handled them straight. Except one. One time, one summer, one case, he bent. And I will tell you why, because I think you deserve to hear it from someone who isn't guessing." She leaned forward slightly. "He bent for love. Not for money — well. Money changed hands, I won't insult you by pretending otherwise, and my father's money bought the alibi that closed the case. But Daniel didn't sign that statement for cash. He signed it because the person who needed protecting was someone he loved. Someone whose sister—" She stopped herself, delicately, like a woman declining the last biscuit. "Well. You were married to him. You know what he was capable of, for the people he loved. Don't you."

Ada looked at her hands. They were steady. She was almost proud of them.

"You're telling me," she said slowly, "that you know who killed Elias Vane."

"I'm telling you that in 1994, a frightened young woman stood at the edge of a thing she couldn't take back, and a kind young policeman decided the world would be better off calling it an accident, and my father decided to pay for the kindness because it happened to serve us all. Three decisions. None of them made by monsters. That's the version I know, Ada, and I've had thirty years to check it against every other version, and it's the one that holds." Vivienne sat back. "Now. Here is my offer, and please hear all of it before you decide I'm threatening you, because I am not threatening you. I am *warning* you, which is different, though I grant the difference is subtle."

"I'm listening."

"There is a version of the next six months where you finish your inventory, you sell your paperbacks, you close the shop on Sundays like you've always wanted to, and Calder Falls keeps its one decent secret, and everyone who's still alive gets to die in peace — Hollis excepted, poor man, and I grieve him genuinely. And there is another version." Vivienne's voice stayed gentle, which somehow made it worse. "In the other version, you keep pulling threads. And threads, in this town, run through living fabric. Through the bank that holds your mortgage. Through the landlord who owns your building — did you know the Aldous Trust owns your building? Charming little lease, month to month, very flexible. Through the health inspector, the fire marshal, the zoning board. I don't have to lift a finger, dear. I only have to stop holding fingers back. Towns like ours don't need villains. They need permission, and everyone knows where the permission lives."

The roses nodded in the breeze. Somewhere below, the reservoir glittered, innocent as glass.

"You'd bury the bookshop first," Ada said.

"I'd bury nothing," Vivienne said. "Gravity does the burying. I'd simply stop standing in front of it." She picked up her cup again, and the pleasantness returned like a lamp being relit. "But here's the thing I actually wanted to say, the reason I asked you here at all, and you may believe this or not as suits you." She hesitated — the first true hesitation of the afternoon. "Daniel deserved better than what he got. Whatever else he was in '94, in the end he was trying to fix it. I know that better than anyone, because I'm the one who noticed him trying. And the people who noticed him trying are the reason he drove off that road in the rain. So when I warn you away from this, Ada, understand: I am not protecting myself. I stopped being afraid for myself around the second Nixon administration. I am trying to keep the last honest thing Daniel Nystrom did from getting anyone else killed. Including you. Especially you."

For a long moment, neither woman spoke. A bee investigated the shortbread and departed, disappointed.

"Why tell me any of this?" Ada asked finally. "If you wanted me stopped, a warning wrapped in threats would do it. Why give me the truth about Daniel? Why confirm the alibi was bought?"

Vivienne studied her for a moment with an expression that was, unexpectedly, almost tender.

"Because you were going to find it anyway," she said. "I've read your file, dear — the real one, not the newspaper one. You don't stop. You never stopped. So I'd rather you heard it from me, sitting down, with good tea, than from some archive box at two in the morning with your heart already broken." She rose, smoothing her lavender skirt, and the audience was clearly over. "One more thing, and then Theo can walk you back to your shop before he reorganizes your mystery section by height, which I understand he's been longing to do."

At the terrace door, she paused.

"When you do open that painting — and you will, I've known you for five days and I already know you will — remember what I told you about the soil. About eleven respectable citizens and two deacons. Whatever you find inside that frame, it wasn't hidden there to protect the guilty." Her voice dropped, and for one unguarded instant, Vivienne Aldous looked every one of her seventy-eight years, and tired clear through. "It was hidden there by a woman who wanted the truth to outlive everyone strong enough to suppress it. Marguerite was many things, but she was not on my father's side. Think about who she trusted with the hiding. Think about who had access to that frame, all these years, and never once opened it — until someone finally did."

She smiled then, bright as the roses.

"Drive safely, Ada. The roads past the reservoir are treacherous when it rains. Everyone says so."

* * *

Theo was waiting by the car, hands in his pockets, radiating the specific guilt of a young man who has accepted money to betray his employer and spent the afternoon failing to enjoy it.

"She offered you a job?" Ada asked.

"She offered me a *scholarship*," Theo said. "And then asked what you'd talked about, and I said tea and roses, and she looked at me like she was doing long division." He opened the car door for her, then hesitated. "Ms. Nystrom — can I say something weird?"

"You've earned it."

"That photo I showed you. The backing board, the seam. I've been thinking about it all week, and I checked the insurance records online — the ones that aren't locked, anyway. The portrait was reframed in 1996. Two years after Elias died. And the invoice for the reframing wasn't paid by the estate." He swallowed. "It was paid by a private account. And the signature on the authorization is Daniel Nystrom."

Ada stood very still in the golden evening light, the smell of lilies still in her hair, Vivienne's warning and Vivienne's gift tangled together in her chest like thread.

"Get me the appraisal file," she said. "Tomorrow night. Bring gloves."

Behind them, high on its hill, Aldous House watched the valley with all its windows.
