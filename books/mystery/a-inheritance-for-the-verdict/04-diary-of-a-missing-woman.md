## 4. Diary of a Missing Woman

Lucía Vega opened the door before Hazel finished knocking.

She'd been watching through the peephole—Hazel could tell by the way the chain rattled a half-second too late, by the way her eyes went first to Hazel's hands, then to the street behind them, then back again. A woman who'd spent three weeks learning that strangers at the door were either useless or dangerous.

"You're not police," she said.

"No."

"The last person who said that was a reporter. She wanted a photo of Marisol for the front page. Like she was missing jewelry or a cat." Lucía stepped back anyway, holding the door wide. "You're the one who called back. Nobody calls back."

Hazel stepped into the apartment on Delancey Street and understood immediately why Marisol had lived here and nowhere else. The place was small but ferociously alive—plants on every sill, papercut banners from some family celebration still taped above the kitchen doorway, a wall of framed photographs climbing the hallway like ivy. In three of them, Hazel recognized the woman from the news footage: dark hair pinned up, a stenographer's posture even in candids, spine straight as if she were always mid-transcription of something important.

"Tea?" Lucía asked, already moving toward the kettle, because grief in this house apparently ran on hospitality.

"Sure."

The kettle filled the silence while Hazel catalogued the room out of habit. No mail piled up—someone was collecting it. Dishes done. A man's jacket on the coat hook that didn't match anything else in the apartment; a brother-in-law's, maybe, or a friend taking shifts. The Vega family had organized itself around an absence the way water organizes around a stone.

Lucía came back with two mugs and sat across from Hazel at a table set for four, though only two places had been used recently. She was younger than Marisol by what looked like five years, with the same dark eyes and none of the same patience. Her hands wrapped the mug like it might bolt.

"Three weeks," she said. "Twenty-two days. I've counted. The police say she ran. They keep using that word—*ran*. Like my sister woke up one morning and decided to abandon her testimony, her job, her *me*, and catch a bus to somewhere with better weather." Her voice cracked on the last word and she pressed her lips together until it passed. "Marisol doesn't run. Marisol finishes things. She finished law school applications she never sent because I needed help with rent. She finishes other people's sentences. She would have finished that trial if they let her."

"What makes you think they didn't let her?"

Lucía looked up sharply. "That's why you're here."

"I called about a cab," Hazel said. "A pickup on Cortland Avenue, eleven-forty p.m., that your sister booked and never took. You reported it to the taxi company because you wanted a record. Paper trails. That's a stenographer's family habit, isn't it? Everything documented."

"Everything," Lucía whispered. "It's how we were raised. Our father crossed the border twice. He used to say the only thing that can't be taken from you is what's written down somewhere safe." She laughed, wet and bitter. "Someone took my sister anyway."

"Tell me about the last week. Before she disappeared."

For a moment Hazel thought Lucía wouldn't answer. Then she set down the mug and pulled her sleeves over her hands—the gesture of someone bracing for cold—and started talking, and once she started she couldn't seem to stop.

"She was tired. Not working-tired. *Watching*-tired. She kept checking the locks twice, three times. She started parking around the corner from our building so nobody would learn her car. She told me it was nothing, stress from the trial, long hours transcribing. But Marisol is—" Lucía corrected herself with visible pain. "*Was*. Is careful. Careful people don't invent new parking habits overnight."

"Did she say anything specific? Anyone bothering her?"

"There was a man." Lucía's voice dropped. "At the courthouse. She mentioned him exactly once, about ten days before—she called me from work, which she never did, and she was whispering, and she said, 'If anything happens, don't let them make me into a crazy person.' Just that. I asked what she meant and she said she had to go, there was someone at her door." Lucía's jaw tightened. "I should have driven down there. I should have—"

"You'd have been standing next to her when whatever happened, happened," Hazel said quietly. "That's not a rescue plan. That's a second victim."

Lucía stared. "You talk like a cop."

"I talk like somebody who's read a lot of case files." It wasn't entirely a lie. Hazel had read enough official files to know their shape, their omissions, their comfortable conclusions. "Tell me about the whispering call. 'Don't let them make me into a crazy person.' Did she ever explain it? Even partially?"

"She said—" Lucía closed her eyes, retrieving it. "She said, 'They'll say I imagined it. They always do. But I have proof.'" Her eyes opened. "I thought she meant notes. Work notes. Transcripts."

"Did the police ask about any of this?"

"They asked if she'd been depressed. If she'd broken up with anyone. If we'd fought." Lucía's laugh was razor-thin. "As if the interesting question about a missing woman is whether she was sad enough to deserve it."

Hazel felt the old familiar heat rise under the sternum—that anger that had no address, no badge number to file against. They kept their voice level. "You called me here for more than tea, Ms. Vega."

"Lucía." She reached beneath her chair, into the canvas tote hanging off its arm, and brought out a book.

Hazel knew what it was before seeing the cover. Some part of the brain that had spent years reading patterns recognized the object's weight in a person's hands—the way you hold something you've been carrying around the house, moving from drawer to nightstand to purse, unable to read it and unable to put it down.

A journal. Navy cloth cover, unlined pages, the spine cracked white from use.

"This was in her nightstand," Lucía said. "Under a false bottom in the drawer. Marisol built that false bottom herself, when we were teenagers, to hide letters from a boy I liked. She remembered how to do it. She built one in her own bedroom." She pushed the book across the table. "The police have her laptop. They have her phone records. They have nothing from this, because I didn't tell them it exists."

"Why not?"

"Because everything in it sounds paranoid, and paranoid women get filed under *ran away* faster than scared ones." Lucía's chin lifted, defiant and terrified at once. "But you found a cab record in three days that the police ignored for three weeks. So. Read it. And then tell me if I'm crazy too."

* *

Hazel read it at the kitchen table while Lucía washed dishes that were already clean, because grief needs somewhere to put its hands.

The early entries were ordinary in the way that made the later ones unbearable. Marisol Vega wrote the way she presumably transcribed: fast, precise, allergic to ornament. Grocery lists adjacent to observations about jurors. Jokes about the defense attorney's cologne ("applied like he's basting himself"). Complaints about her chair in Courtroom 4B, which squeaked on every Wren ruling, which meant she'd learned to time her keystrokes to the squeak so no one would hear her typing during the dramatic pauses.

Judge Aldous Wren appeared in the entries the way weather appears in a farmer's almanac—noted, tracked, respected with a thin edge of wariness.

*Wren gave the jury a history lecture today. Twenty minutes on his father the senator, the family legacy of public service. Half the gallery was moved. I was typing the whole time so I noticed what everyone missed—he does it whenever Halloran's name comes up. Changes the subject to his own bloodline. A man who keeps redirecting questions has usually already answered one.*

Hazel turned pages. The entries compressed as the trial advanced, the handwriting tightening, the margins filling with small annotations in a shorthand Hazel didn't recognize—legal citation marks, maybe, or something private.

Then, eleven days before the disappearance, the tone shifted.

*I'm being watched. I know how that sounds. Write it down anyway, Marisol, because writing things down is the only power they haven't figured out how to take.*

*There's a man who writes the record. That's not poetry—I mean it literally. Everything I type becomes official when he touches it. My words are true until they pass through his hands, and then they're whatever the record says they are. Last month I caught a discrepancy. One word. A juror said "arranged," the transcript says "discussed." One word, and suddenly a bribe is a conversation. I checked the audio backup. The audio matches ME. The paper matches HIM.*

*You can't report that. Who do you report it to? The court employs him. The court certifies him. The court IS him, at some level. I tried to imagine explaining it to a detective and heard myself saying "the transcript changed a word" and heard exactly how it would sound.*

*So I'm making my own record. Duplicate pages. Hidden where he'll never look, because he thinks like a clerk and clerks believe in filing systems. I am not a filing system. I am the woman who writes the record now.*

*If anything happens to me, find the duplicates. Don't trust anyone whose job depends on the official version being true.*

Hazel's hands had gone still on the page. Outside, a bus sighed past, ordinary as breathing. Inside, the kitchen seemed to have gotten quieter, the refrigerator hum dropping away, the whole world narrowing to navy cloth and cramped ink.

*The man who writes the record.* Not a suspect's name. A function. A role in the machinery. Someone whose entire authority derived from controlling what the official version said—and who had discovered, apparently, that he could edit reality one word at a time.

And Marisol had seen it coming. Had written it down. Had built a false bottom in her own nightstand and hidden her own testimony inside it, the way her father hid documents crossing borders, because some lessons are genetic.

"Hazel."

Lucía stood at the counter, dish towel wrung between her hands. "Your face. What is it? What did you find?"

Hazel hesitated. There was a decision point here, one of those forks that didn't look like much and changed everything. Hazel could hand the diary back, offer condolences, walk out, and return to the console and the small solvable mysteries where the worst thing waiting at the end was a sad answer. That was the sensible route. That was the route that kept Hazel invisible, uninvolved, safe.

But the entry was still open on the table. *One word, and suddenly a bribe is a conversation.* Somewhere in a courthouse annex, duplicate pages existed that could undo a corruption verdict, and the woman who'd hidden them had been erased three weeks before she could testify about them. And the police were calling it *ran away*, and a family was setting four plates at a table for two.

"Keep reading," Hazel said. "All of it. There may be things she wrote that only matter later—names, times, habits. I need to take photos of every page."

"You can borrow it." Lucía slid back into her chair. "On one condition."

"Name it."

"When you figure out who did this—you tell me first. Before the newspapers. Before the police press conference. My sister gets to be a person before she gets to be a headline." Her voice didn't waver. "She hated headlines. She said headlines are transcripts written by people who weren't in the room."

Hazel extended a hand. Lucía shook it, grip fierce.

"One more question," Hazel said, standing. "The man at her office door, the day of the whispering call. Did she ever describe him? Height, clothes, anything?"

"No. She hung up fast." Lucía walked Hazel to the door, past the wall of photographs. At the last frame she stopped—a picture of the two sisters at some beach, Marisol mid-laugh, sunburned, human. "You think whoever it was came back. For her."

"I think," Hazel said carefully, "your sister was smarter than everyone involved, including whoever took her. She left a trail. We just have to be willing to follow it somewhere uncomfortable."

"Where?"

Hazel looked at the diary in their hands. "Wherever the record leads."

* *

Back at the apartment, Hazel photographed all ninety-three pages against a sheet of white foam board, timestamping each shot, then locked the originals in the fire box under the bed. Procedure mattered. If Marisol had taught anything from beyond her own disappearance, it was that the physical original was the only version of the truth that couldn't be edited retroactively.

Then Hazel sat down with the images and read the final week again, slowly, cross-referencing dates against the trial calendar pulled from the public docket.

The pattern emerged like a photograph developing.

Every entry in the last ten days mentioned the annex. Not by name—by habit. *Stayed late in the annex again. The annex smells like toner and old carpet. He was in the annex when I left; I heard his machine running.* Whatever happened in Marisol's final days was happening in that building, in the rooms where transcripts were produced, certified, archived—where the official version of reality was manufactured and stored.

And the final entry, dated the night before the cab she never took:

*He knows I know. Today he stood outside Courtroom 4B for six minutes watching me pack up. He smiled. Clerks shouldn't smile like that—it's the smile of a man doing arithmetic. I counted the minutes because counting is what I do, and when I got home I wrote this instead of calling anyone, because who would I even call?*

*The man who writes the record can erase me with a keystroke. But he can't erase this book. He doesn't know it exists. Nobody knows it exists.*

*Nobody knows my name either. That used to feel like safety.*

Hazel set the tablet down.

The apartment was silent except for the radiator ticking through its nightly argument with the cold. On the desk, the scanner murmured its low traffic—units checking in, a fender-bender on the bridge, the city metabolizing its small disasters. Hazel's whole world, usually. Tonight it sounded very far away.

*Nobody knows it exists.* Except, evidently, someone did. Because Marisol Vega had vanished three weeks ago, and the diary had stayed hidden in a false-bottomed drawer ever since—which meant the killer had never found it, had never known to look.

Which raised a question Hazel didn't want to ask and asked anyway, aloud, to the empty room:

"If he never found the diary... what was he so afraid she'd written down?"

Unless the fear hadn't been about the diary at all. Unless there was another record—one the killer *did* know about, one worth erasing a witness to protect. Duplicate transcript pages, hidden in the annex, in a place "he'll never look because he thinks like a clerk."

Hazel pulled up the courthouse floor plans on the second monitor and began, methodically, to study the annex.

It was nearly four a.m. when the mistake happened—the kind of mistake Hazel would replay a hundred times in the coming weeks. Reading the diary images in order, top to bottom, one last sweep for anything missed. Page eighty-seven. Page eighty-eight. Page eighty-nine—

Hazel stopped.

The entry was dated March 14th.

Marisol Vega disappeared on March 11th.

Hazel read it twice, three times, the words refusing to rearrange themselves into sense. The handwriting was hers. The voice was hers. The date was three days after the last confirmed sighting, three days after the cab that never arrived, three days after the search began.

And the entry began:

*Kavanagh is asking questions now. H.K. The dispatcher. Interesting.*
