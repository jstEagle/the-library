## 14. Dispatch as Confessional

The archive room at Citywide Taxi smelled like burnt coffee and old carpet, and Hazel loved it the way other people loved churches.

It was 2:40 in the morning, and the night dispatcher on duty—Dev, a twenty-two-year-old with earbuds permanently lodged in one ear—had waved Hazel through without a second look. *Going over old logs for a thing,* Hazel had said, and Dev had said *cool* and gone back to his phone. That was the beauty of being nobody. People didn't just let you into rooms; they forgot you were in them.

Hazel set up at the terminal in the corner, the one with the dead monitor arm and the chair that leaned left, and pulled up the call archive.

Every cab company kept recordings. It was insurance—against disputes, against lawsuits, against the kind of passenger who claimed they'd never said the address out loud. Citywide's retention policy was eighteen months, which meant Marisol Vega's last three weeks of calls were still sitting in cold storage, timestamped and indexed, waiting for someone to ask the right question.

Nobody had asked. The police had subpoenaed the logs around her disappearance, Hazel knew that from Imani's careful, off-the-record briefing—the driver who'd been assigned the pickup had been interviewed, cleared, forgotten. But the police had asked about the *pickup*. They'd wanted to know who called, where she was going, whether the driver saw anything.

They hadn't asked about the *pattern*. Police thought in incidents. Hazel thought in rhythms. Three weeks of nights behind a console taught you that people were loops before they were events. They went to the same diner on Thursdays. They called from the same payphone when their wife took the car. They lied in the same cadence every time.

If Marisol Vega had been afraid of someone—if she'd been meeting someone, or avoiding someone, or circling something—that fear would be in the record. Not in what she said. In how often she moved.

Hazel typed her name into the search field: VEGA, MARISOL. Date range: ninety days back from the disappearance.

Forty-one calls.

That was already wrong. A court stenographer with a fixed income and a courthouse-adjacent apartment did not take forty-one cab rides in three months. Hazel pulled up the list and started reading it like a pulse.

Most of them were what you'd expect. Home to the courthouse annex, mornings. Courthouse to home, evenings. A grocery run. Two trips to St. Anthony's hospital—Hazel made a note; Lucía hadn't mentioned anyone sick, but sisters kept things from each other, and so did diaries.

Then there was the cluster.

Fourteen calls in the final six weeks, all between nine p.m. and midnight, all pickups from the same block on Calloway Street—a strip of shuttered print shops and a notary office six blocks from the annex, nowhere near anything Marisol needed after dark. And all fourteen calls had one more thing in common.

Hazel leaned closer to the screen.

All fourteen had been *cancelled or reassigned*. The original cab dispatched, then the call flagged—customer cancelled, or no-show declared, or the run transferred to another vehicle mid-route. Fourteen times. Nobody cancelled fourteen times unless they were waiting for something, or watching for something, or—

Or unless someone else was controlling the record.

Hazel sat back. The chair creaked its leftward creak. The building hummed its building hum.

*The man who writes the record.* Marisol's own words, in her own hand, in an entry dated four days before she vanished. Hazel had read it so many times the ink felt like it was inside their skull now. She'd been afraid of someone who controlled documents—who could make things true by writing them down, or make them never have happened by deleting them.

And here was a woman whose transportation history looked like it had been edited.

Hazel pulled the audio files. The system played them through the headset, one by one, and Hazel listened the way they listened to everything—with half their brain on the words and the other half on everything underneath the words.

Call one, six weeks before the disappearance. Marisol's voice was crisp, professional, the voice of a woman who transcribed speech for a living and therefore spoke like punctuation mattered. "Calloway and Ninth, please. The notary on the corner." A pause. "Actually—no. Cancel that." Click.

Call two, five days later. Same block. This time she gave the address, waited through the dispatch confirmation, then, quietly, almost to herself: "Never mind. Sorry." Click.

Call seven. She got as far as confirming the fare estimate before hanging up.

Call eleven was different. Her voice was lower, faster. "I need a car to the annex. Not the front entrance—the loading dock on Delancey. Can the driver wait? Ten minutes. I'll pay for the wait." A beat. "No—forget the wait. Forget it. Is there a way to book without it going in the log?"

The dispatcher on that recording—Hazel recognized the voice, a day-shift guy named Tompkins who'd retired the previous spring—had laughed gently. "Everything goes in the log, ma'am. That's kind of the point."

A silence on the line, long enough that Tompkins asked if she was still there.

"Yeah," Marisol had said. "Yeah. Everything goes in the log."

She'd hung up. The call lasted forty seconds. Hazel played it three times.

There it was. Not paranoia, not drama—recognition. A stenographer spent her whole life watching words become records, watching records become power. She knew exactly what a log was. She knew that every ride she took to Calloway Street was a sentence in a file somewhere, and she knew who could read files.

And she'd gone anyway. Fourteen times. Whatever was on Calloway Street—or whoever—was worth the risk of being written down.

Hazel checked the last call. Nine days before the disappearance. Pickup confirmed, cab assigned—unit 214, driver R. Osei—and then, four minutes into the run, the transfer flag: *reassigned to unit 309, driver T. Brandt.* No reason logged. Hazel cross-checked unit 214's shift sheet. Osei had been mid-shift, nowhere near end of route. Transfers happened—breakdowns, emergencies—but they got reasons. This one had nothing. Just a clean little deletion, the digital equivalent of a page torn out.

Someone had reached into the dispatch system and moved Marisol Vega's last documented ride from one car to another.

Hazel's hands were very still on the keyboard.

Unit 309. Driver T. Brandt. Hazel pulled the personnel file—drivers' info was in the same database, because dispatchers needed to know who they were sending into the dark. Theodore Brandt, fifty-eight, nineteen years with the company, no flags, no complaints, a man so unremarkable he was nearly invisible. Night shift, mostly. The airport runs nobody wanted.

He was still driving. He was, in fact, on the road right now.

Hazel looked at the clock. 3:15. Brandt's GPS ping showed him idling at the airport staging lot, between fares, for the next hour at least. Every driver took their break somewhere, and airport drivers took theirs at the staging lot, in their cars, listening to sports radio and eating things wrapped in foil.

Hazel stood up, grabbed their jacket, and told Dev they were taking a smoke break even though Hazel didn't smoke. Dev grunted. Perfect.

* * *

The airport staging lot at four in the morning was a grid of tired sedans under sodium lights, steam rising off hoods, the terminal glowing in the distance like a cruise ship that had run aground. Hazel walked the rows until they found 309—a beige hybrid with a cracked taillight and an air freshener shaped like a pine tree that had given up years ago.

Brandt was in the driver's seat, door open, feet on the pavement, a thermos balanced on the roof of the car. He watched Hazel approach with the flat, patient wariness of a man who'd spent two decades being approached by strangers at four a.m.

"Ride?" he said.

"Information, actually." Hazel held up their phone, screen showing the Citywide dispatch app, the little gold badge that meant *staff*. Half true. Close enough. "I'm going through some old logs. You had a fare nine months ago—March fourteenth. Pickup on Calloway Street, transferred to you from unit 214."

Brandt's face did something small and complicated. He picked up the thermos, drank, bought himself three seconds. "Nine months is a long time."

"It is," Hazel agreed. "But you're the kind of driver who remembers. Nineteen years. The new guys forget a fare before they've dropped them off. You remember the ones that were strange."

"I remember strange," Brandt conceded. He studied Hazel—not the badge, Hazel noticed, but Hazel. Hands, eyes, the way they stood slightly angled, weight on the balls of their feet. "You're not management."

"I'm the person asking."

Another drink of coffee. Then he shrugged, and something in him settled, like a man deciding the stranger was at least interesting. "Calloway Street. Yeah. Weird one. Dispatch sends me to take over a run—no explanation, which happens, fine. I get there, and there's this woman standing under the awning of the closed notary, and she's—" He searched for it. "Braced. Like somebody bracing for a wave. She gets in, gives me the annex address, and she spends the whole ride checking the mirrors. Not nervous like scared-of-me. Nervous like scared-of-the-road-behind-us."

"What did you see behind you?"

"That's the thing." Brandt set the thermos down and turned fully toward Hazel, and now he was remembering in earnest, the memory coming up whole, the way old drivers' memories did—vivid, specific, slightly ceremonial. "Two cars back, there's a black SUV. Government plates—I notice plates, it's a habit, everybody's plates are a habit after nineteen years. It follows us three blocks. Then at Delancey it peels off, but slow, like it wanted us to see it leave."

"Government plates. Which agency?"

"That's the part I told the detective and the detective wrote down and nothing ever came of it." Brandt's mouth twisted. "It wasn't a plate number I knew. But the plate *frame* said something. Little gold letters. I only caught it under a streetlight, but I catch things under streetlights, it's my whole job." He tapped the roof of his car. "*Judicial Security Division.*"

Hazel kept their face still while everything underneath it rearranged itself. "Courthouse security detail."

"Courthouse security, state marshal service, judge's protection units—they all use variants. But here's what stuck with me." Brandt lowered his voice, though the lot was empty except for sleeping cars and one raccoon conducting business near a dumpster. "When she got out at the annex loading dock, there was already a man waiting. Suit, earpiece, the whole uniform. And he didn't come to meet her—he came to meet *me*. Leaned down to my window while she walked inside, real friendly, and he says, 'Sir, that fare's already been taken care of. Company knows. You can head out.'"

"He paid you?"

"Nope. That's what got me. 'Taken care of'—like it was handled upstream. Like the paperwork was already done before I got there." Brandt shook his head slowly. "And sure enough, next week my log shows that run as completed-and-cleared, no fare recorded, no note. I mentioned it to my supervisor and he looked at me like I'd asked him to audit the moon. Nineteen years, I learned one thing: when the paperwork fixes itself, somebody important broke it."

Hazel breathed out slowly. "The man at the dock. Could you describe him?"

"Tall-ish, gray at the temples, built like a retired linebacker. Earpiece. And—" Brandt snapped his fingers. "Lapel pin. Little gold scales of justice. The fancy kind, not the cheap union ones. Guys wearing those pins are always *with* somebody. They're never the somebody."

"A protection officer. For a judge."

"For *a* judge, sure." Brandt gave Hazel a long look. "You know how many judges work out of that annex?"

"I'm finding out."

"You know which one had a trial running in March? Big one? All over the news?" Brandt didn't wait. "I drive night shift. I hear the radio. Halloran. Everybody in this city knows that name right now. And everybody in this city knows whose courtroom that trial lived in." He picked up his thermos again, and his voice dropped to almost nothing, the voice men use when they say the unsayable out loud for the first time. "Wren. Judge Aldous Wren. The man they're gonna put on a stamp someday, if you believe the papers."

The sodium lights buzzed. Somewhere across the lot, a plane lifted off, a low grinding roar that swallowed everything for ten seconds.

When it faded, Hazel said, "Why tell me this? You wouldn't even tell the detective."

"The detective had a badge and a boss and a reason to forget." Brandt's eyes were steady and sad. "You've got a phone and no reason to lie to me. Besides—" he nodded at the dispatch app still glowing on Hazel's screen, "—you found me in the logs nine months later. At four in the morning. That means you care more than the law does. I've seen what happens to women who get into cars near courthouses and don't come back. Somebody ought to care more than the law does."

He screwed the cap onto his thermos, closing the conversation like a ledger.

"One more thing," Hazel said. "The woman. Marisol Vega. Did she say anything? In the car?"

Brandt was quiet for a moment. "One thing. Right before she got out. She looked at the annex doors and she said—" he closed his eyes, retrieving it exactly, a stenographer's precision arriving secondhand, "—she said, '*Whatever happens, the transcript has to survive me.*'" He opened his eyes. "I figured it was work talk. Court stuff. Now I figure maybe it wasn't."

* * *

Hazel walked back across the lot with the sky still black and the word *Wren* sitting in their chest like a stone dropped down a well, falling and falling, not yet hitting bottom.

It fit. God help them, it fit. The presiding judge of the biggest corruption trial in a generation—a man whose entire public story was integrity, whose legacy was being written in real time by every newspaper in the state—would have a security detail. Would have access to transcripts, to logs, to the machinery of records. Would have a reason, if the verdict was threatened, to reach downstream into systems like a taxi company's dispatch archive and move a woman's last ride from one column to another.

But a suspicion wasn't a case. *Wren* was a name attached to a plate frame and a lapel pin, and Brandt hadn't seen the man himself, only the weather around him. Hazel needed the shape of the man—his schedule, his habits, his vanity, the architecture of his self-regard. Because everyone who erased people eventually believed their own press, and belief left footprints.

Back in the archive room, Dev hadn't moved. Hazel sat down at the leaning chair, opened a fresh document, and typed a single line at the top:

**ALDOUS WREN — presiding judge, Halloran trial.**

Then, underneath, where the cursor blinked like a small patient heartbeat:

**What does a good man do when the record turns against him?**

Hazel started typing, and outside, over the airport, the first gray light of a morning Wren didn't yet know was coming began to bleed up over the city.
