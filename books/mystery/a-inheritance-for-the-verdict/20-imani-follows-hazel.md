## 20. Imani Follows Hazel

Imani Okafor had been a cop long enough to know the difference between a hunch and an obsession. A hunch was a whisper. An obsession was a drumbeat, steady and stupid, that you followed anyway because the alternative was admitting you'd lost control of your own case.

The Vega file was her case. Had been for eleven months, gathering dust in a drawer until Hazel Kavanagh started poking at it like a stick in a hornet's nest.

She'd told herself she was watching Kavanagh to protect the investigation. That was the official version, the one she'd give her lieutenant if he ever asked—which he wouldn't, because he'd made it clear the Vega case was closed and anyone who reopened it was looking for trouble that didn't have his name attached.

But tonight, sitting in her personal car two blocks from the courthouse annex with the engine off and the heater ticking, Imani admitted the truth: she wasn't protecting the case. She was following a person she couldn't figure out, and she needed to know which side of the line they were standing on.

Kavanagh's cab had turned onto Calloway Street at 11:47 p.m. Imani knew because she'd been parked outside the dispatch office since eleven, running on cold coffee and the kind of stubbornness that gets cops demoted or decorated, depending on how the story ends.

The annex was a squat limestone building behind the main courthouse, mostly empty after hours except for a security desk and whatever ghosts lived in its records room. Nobody went there at midnight unless they were looking for something they weren't supposed to find.

* * *

The problem with Hazel Kavanagh, Imani thought, was that they were either the best witness she'd never officially met or the most careful suspect she'd ever chased. There was no middle ground. Every conversation left her holding facts she couldn't source—taxi logs, work schedules, details about Marisol Vega's final days that hadn't come from any police channel Imani knew about.

And then there was the diary.

Kavanagh had shown her photocopies three nights ago in a diner booth that smelled like burnt coffee and old grease. Entries dated after Marisol Vega disappeared. Entries describing a dispatcher by initials only—H.K.—who was asking questions in all the wrong places.

Imani had read those pages twice and felt the same thing both times: cold spreading up from her stomach like water finding its level. Not because of what the entries said about Kavanagh. Because of what they said about whoever was writing them. That was a mind that didn't just want a victim dead. It wanted a witness to the killing—a witness it could play with, string along, teach a lesson to.

Her lieutenant's voice came back to her: *You're chasing ghosts, Okafor. The Vega case is a missing persons file with no body and no leads. Let it go.*

But ghosts didn't write diary entries. Ghosts didn't know dispatcher initials. Somebody very alive and very connected was keeping score, and every instinct Imani had—the ones that had survived fifteen years of working cases the department preferred forgotten—told her the scorekeeper wasn't done.

So here she was. Off duty. Unmarked car. Watching a building where nobody had business being, waiting for a person who might be a victim-in-waiting or a perpetrator-in-progress to do something that would finally tell her which.

11:52 p.m. The annex door opened.

Not the front entrance—the service door on the north side, the one delivery drivers used. A figure slipped through, moving low and quick, and even at this distance Imani recognized the deliberate economy of the motion. Kavanagh.

She gave them ninety seconds, then got out of the car.

* * *

The service door had been jimmied—not broken, persuaded. Imani noted the scratch marks around the lock plate with professional disapproval. Kavanagh was careful but not careful enough, or maybe didn't care anymore. Both possibilities worried her.

Inside, the hallway smelled like floor wax and paper dust. Emergency lighting painted everything the color of weak tea. She could hear something faint from deeper in the building: a drawer opening. Paper shifting. The particular rhythm of someone searching with purpose rather than panic.

Imani had her weapon on her hip and her badge in her pocket, and she stood in that hallway for a long moment weighing them against each other. Procedure said announce yourself, detain, question, report. Procedure said Kavanagh was a civilian breaking into a government facility at midnight, and whatever they found—or planted—would be evidence tainted beyond use.

Procedure also said that if she called this in, the Vega case would vanish into the same drawer it had been rotting in for a year, and whatever Kavanagh knew would die in an interview room while lawyers negotiated access.

She moved toward the sound instead. Quietly. Which was its own confession, and she knew it.

The records room was at the end of the hall, door half open, light spilling out in a thin blade. Imani flattened herself against the wall beside it, hand resting on her holster without drawing, and looked inside.

Kavanagh stood at a filing cabinet with their back to the door, wearing dark clothes and thin gloves, holding a folder under one arm and photographing pages with a phone camera. The shutter click was soft but distinct in the silence. Click. Page turn. Click.

"Kavanagh." Her voice came out level, which surprised her. "Put the folder down."

They didn't jump. That was the first thing she noticed. They went still—completely still, the way people do when they've been expecting this moment—and then they set the folder down on the cabinet with exaggerated care before turning around slowly, hands visible, expression unreadable.

"Detective Okafor." No surprise in the voice either. "You followed me."

"You broke into a courthouse annex."

"I did." They tilted their head slightly. "Are you arresting me?"

It was a genuine question. That was the thing about Kavanagh that kept unbalancing her—they treated consequences like weather, something to be assessed rather than feared. Imani had arrested people who cried, people who ran, people who talked until she wished she'd never Mirandized them. She'd never arrested someone who looked at her handcuffs like a math problem.

"That depends," she said. "On what's in the folder."

"Transcript pages. From the Halloran trial. Pages that don't exist in the official record." Kavanagh's eyes held hers, steady and dark in the emergency lighting. "Marisol Vega hid duplicates. She knew somebody upstairs was editing the record, so she made copies and stashed them here, in the one place nobody would look because nobody looks at the annex after hours. Except whoever killed her is still editing, Detective. Still cleaning up. And if we don't get these pages somewhere safe, they'll disappear too."

"We."

Something flickered across Kavanagh's face—not quite a smile, more like acknowledgment of a point scored. "I'm optimistic."

Imani looked at the folder. At the gloves. At the phone with its incriminating photographs. Everything about this scene was a prosecutor's nightmare and a defense attorney's dream, and if she touched any of it as a cop, she'd be burying the truth under procedure forever.

If she walked away, she'd be burying her career instead.

"Give me the folder," she said.

Kavanagh didn't move. "And then?"

"And then I go home, and tomorrow morning I request the Halloran transcript through proper channels, and if the official version doesn't match what's in that folder, I have grounds to reopen the Vega case as a homicide." She paused. "That's the clean way. It's slower. But it holds up."

"It's slower," Kavanagh agreed. "Slower means Pruitt has time to shred. Slower means Wren's clerk makes a phone call and the official record develops a gap exactly where these pages should be. You've seen how fast things move when they want them to move, Detective. Marisol Vega moved fast, and she's gone."

The name landed between them like a dropped glass. Imani heard herself say, "You think Judge Wren—"

"I think the man who writes the record decided what the record should say." Kavanagh picked the folder back up, slowly, giving her every chance to stop them. "I think Marisol figured it out, and I think she died for it, and I think the person who killed her has been writing me letters in her voice because he thinks I'm the only loose end that matters." Their voice stayed flat, but Imani caught the tremor underneath, the hairline fracture in all that control. "He's right. But not for the reason he thinks."

The hallway hummed with the building's nighttime silence. Somewhere above them, a clock marked the hour with a single dull chime.

Imani thought about her lieutenant. About the drawer. About fifteen years of doing it the clean way and watching the clean way lose. She thought about the diary entries, and the initials H.K., and the fact that whoever wrote them had chosen Kavanagh specifically—which meant Kavanagh was in danger whether Imani sanctioned this or not, and a person in danger deserved a cop, even a dirty-handed one.

"Here's what happens now," she said. "You give me copies. Originals stay with me—I log them into evidence through a friend at the state attorney's office, not ours. If my lieutenant finds out, I fall alone. And Kavanagh—" she stepped closer, close enough to see the exhaustion under their eyes, the fear they were managing like a budget, "—if I find out you manufactured any of this, if those pages are forged, there won't be a partnership to betray. Understood?"

For the first time since she'd known them, Hazel Kavanagh looked young. Just for a second—the mask slipping enough to show the person underneath, tired and scared and carrying something heavy up a hill alone for far too long.

"Understood," they said. And then, quieter: "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet." She held out her hand for the folder. "This isn't trust. This is a bet."

"What are the odds?"

Imani took the folder. It was lighter than it should have been, considering what it weighed. "Ask me after the gala."

Kavanagh's head came up sharply. "How did you—"

"The anniversary dinner? Catering staff list went out two weeks ago. Your name isn't on it, but 'H. Kavanagh, temp agency' is." She allowed herself the ghost of a smile. "You're getting sloppy."

For a moment Kavanagh just stared at her, and then something loosened in their shoulders—not defeat, Imani realized, but relief. The specific relief of a person who has been carrying a secret so long that having it seen feels like setting down luggage.

"There's something else," Kavanagh said. "A new entry appeared under my door yesterday. Entry nine. It mentions the dinner. It says—" they stopped, jaw tightening, "—it promises a toast. With my name in it. Out loud, in front of everyone."

Imani felt the cold again, that water-level cold rising through her chest. "He's going to name you at the gala."

"He's going to end me at the gala. One way or another." Kavanagh met her eyes. "Which means I'll be there. And which means, Detective, that in forty-eight hours, you're either going to arrest me or stand next to me. I need to know which before I walk through that door."

The emergency lights buzzed. Down the hall, the building settled around them like an old man shifting in sleep.

Imani looked down at the folder in her hands—stolen pages, an unsanctioned alliance, a career balanced on a knife's edge—and made the leap.

"Stand next to you," she said. "But we do it my way. We plan it like a sting, not a stunt. And Kavanagh?" She was already moving toward the service door, already past the point of no return and strangely calm about it. "Next time you break into a building, lose your tail properly. Because next time, the tail might not be me."

Outside, the night air hit cold and clean. Kavanagh unlocked their cab and paused before getting in, keys in hand.

"Why?" they asked. Simple. Direct. The question underneath every other question. "Why cross the line for me?"

Imani thought about Marisol Vega, who had trusted the system and been erased by it. She thought about the drawer, and the lieutenant, and every clean procedure that had ever protected the guilty.

"Because somebody erased her," she said. "And I'm tired of helping."

She watched the cab pull away into the dark, taillights shrinking down Calloway Street, and stood there a moment longer than she needed to—long enough to understand that whatever happened at the anniversary dinner, there was no version of her life afterward that looked like the life before it.

Then she got in her car, tucked the folder under the seat, and drove home the long way, checking her mirrors, planning a sting, and trying not to hear the drumbeat getting louder.
