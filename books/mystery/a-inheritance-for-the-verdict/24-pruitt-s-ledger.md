## 24. Pruitt's Ledger

The financial crimes unit kept its records in a basement that smelled like wet carpet and dead ambition, and Imani Okafor had spent enough of her career down there to know the smell meant nobody up top wanted what was in these boxes to be found.

She'd signed out the Halloran trial exhibits under a routine audit pretext—*case review, pre-anniversary procedural sweep*—which was the kind of phrase that made clerks nod and stop asking questions. The audit was real enough. She just hadn't mentioned whose case she was auditing, or why, or that the reason was a night dispatcher with no badge, no warrant, and a talent for making Imani feel like the amateur.

Two days until the gala. Forty-eight hours, Hazel had said, like they were reading a countdown off some internal clock the rest of the world couldn't see.

Imani pulled the box marked *HALLORAN — FINANCIAL EXHIBITS — SEALED PER COURT ORDER* and set it on the scarred steel table under the flickering fluorescent tube, and she told herself the flutter in her chest was caffeine.

It wasn't. It was the feeling she got right before a case broke open—the same feeling she'd had eleven years ago as a rookie, standing outside an apartment door in the Fenwick projects, knowing before anyone said a word that the woman inside was dead. Her body knew things before her mind agreed to know them. Right now her body was saying: *you're about to find something someone killed to keep buried.*

She cut the seal with her pocketknife and started pulling folders.

* * *

Dale Pruitt's name appeared in the Halloran file exactly four times, and none of those appearances were interesting. Witness logistics. Exhibit transport signatures. A scheduling conflict memo from 2019 when he'd double-booked a courtroom. He was furniture. He was the man who refilled the water pitchers while powerful people decided other people's lives, and the prosecution team hadn't given him a second thought, which was precisely why he'd been perfect.

But Hazel hadn't been looking at the trial. Hazel had been looking at the money—and the money, Imani was learning, always lived somewhere the trial never went.

Halloran's bribes had moved through a shell company called Meridian Court Services LLC. That much was public record; it had come out at trial, one of the few threads the defense hadn't managed to snip. Meridian billed the city for "transcription support," "records management," "courthouse facilities consulting"—a fog of invoices so boring that three separate auditors had waved them through. Behind the fog, Halloran's fixers paid judges' debts, bought silence, funded vacations that never appeared on any disclosure form.

Meridian's registered agent was a lawyer who'd since been disbarred. Its listed address was a mail drop. On paper, the company had dissolved eighteen months ago, after Halloran went away.

On paper.

Imani spread the old Meridian invoices across the table and photographed each one, then opened her laptop and pulled the subpoena returns she'd quietly requested six weeks ago—back when this was still about finding a missing stenographer, back when she still believed the Vega case might have a clean answer.

Pruitt's bank records, obtained under the pretense of a routine background check for a court security review. She'd felt dirty requesting them. She felt dirtier now, looking at them.

And there it was.

Every month, on the ninth, for fourteen consecutive months—starting four months *before* Marisol Vega disappeared—a payment to Dale Pruitt from an entity called Cortland Administrative Group. Amount: $2,400. Memo line: *consulting.*

Cortland Administrative Group. Imani stared at the name. She didn't recognize it, but her gut was already moving, dragging her attention across the table to the Meridian invoices, to the disbarred lawyer's filing history she'd printed on a hunch.

Cortland Administrative Group had been registered by the same filing service Meridian used. Same registered agent of record—an elderly paralegal in Yonkers who almost certainly had no idea his name was on anything. Same incorporation date pattern: born quietly, fed quietly, ready to dissolve the moment anyone looked.

Different name. Same skeleton.

"Come on," she whispered to the basement, to the boxes, to the dead ambition in the carpet. "Show me the rest."

* * *

The rest took four hours.

She built it the way she'd learned to build money cases back in her narco days: not by finding the smoking gun but by laying bricks until the wall was undeniable. Payment nine of every month, $2,400, Cortland to Pruitt. Then she cross-referenced the dates against everything else she had, and the pattern stopped being a pattern and became a story.

Payment one: two months after Judge Aldous Wren was assigned to the Halloran retrial docket.

Payment four: the week Marisol Vega first transcribed closed-session testimony—the sessions Wren had ordered sealed, the ones whose contents existed only in the official transcript and in whatever notes a careful stenographer might keep.

Payment seven: three weeks before Marisol disappeared.

Payments eight through fourteen: after. Steady as a heartbeat. A dead woman's murder, invoiced monthly.

Imani sat back and looked at the wall of photographs she'd pinned to the corkboard the unit kept down there, red string optional because she refused to be a cliché even in her own head. Fourteen payments. One shell company wearing another shell company's bones. And at the center of it, a court clerk who emptied wastebaskets and scheduled courtrooms and had access to every transcript that passed through the Halloran courthouse—including, presumably, the sealed sessions, including the original transcripts, including anything a missing witness might have duplicated and hidden.

Her phone buzzed. Hazel.

**HAZEL:** any movement?

She typed, deleted, typed again. There was a rule she'd broken once in her career—told a source more than the source needed—and the fallout had taken two years to clean up. She wasn't breaking it again. But Hazel wasn't a source, exactly. Hazel was something the rulebook didn't have a column for.

**IMANI:** cortland administrative group. ring a bell?

Three dots appeared instantly. Hazel Kavanagh answered messages the way they did everything else: fast, precise, slightly unnerving.

**HAZEL:** shell co? meridian's ghost?

**IMANI:** how did you know that.

**HAZEL:** i didn't. guessed. you just confirmed it. where are the payments going

Imani hesitated. Then, because the alternative was carrying this alone:

**IMANI:** pruitt. 2400/mo. 14 months. starts before marisol vanished.

The typing indicator ran for a long time. Longer than Hazel ever let it run.

**HAZEL:** that's not a retainer. retainers front-load. that's salary. he's on payroll.

**IMANI:** whose payroll?

**HAZEL:** whose payroll does it have to be, detective. wren assigned him to halloran logistics personally. lucía told me marisol said the clerk "answered to someone above the chief judge." we thought it was paranoia. it was org chart.

Imani put the phone face-down on the table. She pressed both palms flat against the cold steel and breathed, the way her therapist had taught her, four counts in, six counts out, except her therapist had never imagined this particular scenario so the technique mostly just gave her something to do with her lungs while her brain screamed.

Because here was the thing she hadn't told Hazel, the thing sitting under the vindication like a stone under snow:

If the payments started before Marisol disappeared—if Pruitt was on Wren's payroll months before the stenographer vanished—then Marisol's disappearance wasn't a reaction. It was a plan. Scheduled. Budgeted. Line-itemed. Someone had looked at a woman who transcribed words for a living and calculated, in advance, what it would cost to make her stop.

And if they'd budgeted for her, they'd budgeted for contingencies. For investigators. For nosy dispatchers who pulled taxi logs. For detectives who signed boxes out of basements.

For her.

* * *

She should have stopped there. She knew that later, in the way you know all the exits only after the fire. She should have photographed the ledger, logged the evidence, walked it up to her lieutenant, and let the machine grind forward at the machine's pace.

Instead she pulled the last folder. The one she'd saved for the end, the way you save the worst thing, because some part of you already knows what it says.

Wren's disclosure forms. Filed annually, as required, by every judge in the state. Income: salary. Investments: municipal bonds, a lake house held in his wife's maiden name, a modest portfolio managed by a firm called Ashford Private Wealth.

Ashford Private Wealth.

Imani's pen stopped moving.

She flipped back through her photographs of the Meridian invoices. Third invoice from the top, dated the year before the trial: *professional services rendered to Ashford Private Wealth, records archiving.* Meridian had done work for Wren's own investment manager. The shells weren't parallel companies. They were a family. Cousins. All of them descended from the same parent structure that the disbarred lawyer had built, and all of them orbiting, eventually, around the same sun.

Aldous Wren hadn't been bribed by Halloran. That was the elegant horror of it, the thing that would make prosecutors weep and defense attorneys salivate. Wren had never taken a dollar. Wren had built the plumbing. Halloran's money flowed through pipes Wren owned, and Wren's cut came back disguised as fees, as consulting, as archival services—as *maintenance.* As the cost of keeping the record clean.

The man who writes the record. Marisol's diary had said it, and Imani had read the phrase as poetry, as a victim's frightened metaphor.

It was a job description.

* * *

The basement lights were on a motion sensor, and they clicked off twice while she worked, plunging her into blackness until she waved an arm and the fluorescents stuttered back. Each time, the dark lasted three seconds, and each time, in those three seconds, she was absolutely certain someone was standing behind her.

Nobody was. Both times.

That was the fear now—not the sharp fear of a drawn gun but the slow civic dread of understanding how deep the rot went. Because if Wren owned the plumbing, Wren owned the courtroom where any case against him would be heard. Wren's colleagues would recuse, sure, and the case would bounce to federal court, and meanwhile Wren's lawyers would move to suppress, and the suppression hearing would land in front of some jurist who owed a favor to somebody in the same ecosystem, and—

And Pruitt was still employed. Still badging into the annex every morning. Still emptying wastebaskets, still scheduling courtrooms, still holding a master key to every room where every document in this case lived.

Including, she realized with a cold drop in her stomach, the evidence room logbooks. Including the chain-of-custody records for the Halloran exhibits. Including, potentially, the box she was sitting next to right now.

She looked at the folder in her hands. *HALLORAN — FINANCIAL EXHIBITS — SEALED PER COURT ORDER.* If Pruitt—or whoever Pruitt answered to—wanted to know who had pulled this box, the checkout log would tell them. Her name. Her badge number. Today's date.

Imani Okafor, Detective, Second Grade. Now a line item in somebody's contingency budget.

She could de-register the box, blur herself back into the system, become the invisible thing Hazel had taught her to value without ever meaning to. Or she could leave her name on the log, loud and legible, and dare them to come for her in daylight.

Her hand hovered over the checkout sheet.

Then she thought about Marisol Vega, who had seen it coming and testified anyway—had planned to testify anyway, Lucía said, had ironed her good blazer the night before she vanished. Who had used her position inside the machine to make copies, hide pages, build her own little insurance policy in a courthouse crawlspace. A stenographer with no badge and no gun, who understood exactly what it would cost, and did it anyway.

Imani signed the sheet. Printed clearly. Full name.

Let them read it.

* * *

She surfaced at 9 p.m., into rain and sodium light, and stood on the steps of the municipal building letting the water hit her face because the alternative was screaming. Her phone buzzed. Hazel again.

**HAZEL:** status. i've mapped wren's gala security. two exits unmonitored. need your answer tonight: are we doing this or am i doing this alone

Imani watched the rain crawl down the screen. Somewhere behind her, forty stories up, the machinery of justice hummed along on rails laid by a man who believed he was its finest product. In two nights, that man would stand at a podium and raise a glass to justice served, and the whole city would applaud, and the applause would be the sound of the record being written—one more clean page in Aldous Wren's legacy.

Unless someone corrected it.

She thought about her lieutenant's warning. *Drop the Vega thing, Okafor. You want a career or a crusade?* She thought about the wrongly arrested cab driver sitting in county lockup right now because the frame needed a body. She thought about Hazel, who had spent years building a life with no fingerprints on it, and who was about to walk into a room full of wolves wearing a server's jacket and a dead woman's transcript over their heart.

She typed with wet thumbs.

**IMANI:** we're doing this together. one condition.

**HAZEL:** name it

**IMANI:** when it goes wrong—and it will—you don't disappear. you stay on the channel. you let me hear your voice. whatever happens, hazel, you don't go back to being nobody. not tomorrow. not ever.

The reply took a long time. When it came, it was four words, and Imani read them three times in the rain, and something in her chest that had been clenched since the day Lucía Vega first called the precinct finally loosened half a turn.

**HAZEL:** deal. channel three. always.

Imani pocketed the phone and headed for her car—and stopped.

Taped under her windshield wiper, soaked but legible, was a single sheet of paper. Court stationery. No signature.

One line, typed in a font she recognized from a thousand filed documents, from a thousand transcripts written in a dead woman's voice:

*Detective—nice work in the basement. Entry ten is about you. Ask your new friend what happened to the last cop who read the ledger.*

The rain fell. The traffic hissed past. And Imani Okafor stood very still on the courthouse steps, holding the killer's handwriting in her shaking hands, understanding at last that the countdown hadn't started two days ago.

It had started the moment she signed her name.
