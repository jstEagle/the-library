## 29. The Toast

The Grand Meridian's ballroom smelled like money trying very hard to smell like history—beeswax and lilies and the faint chemical sweetness of fresh carpet laid over marble that had hosted a century of verdicts, bribes, and both at once.

Hazel moved through it with a tray of champagne they were not technically authorized to carry.

The jacket fit better than expected. Imani had pulled it from an evidence locker—seized from a catering company that had gone under two years ago, its logo still crisp on the breast pocket: *Meridian Events*. Nobody questioned a server. That was the whole architecture of invisibility, Hazel had learned it young: people didn't see uniforms, they saw through them. A woman in black slacks carrying a tray was furniture with legs. Furniture got everywhere.

Including here. Including forty feet from Judge Aldous Wren.

Hazel had spent forty-eight hours learning him the way you learn a frequency—by listening until the pattern stops being noise. Wren arrived at 7:12, eleven minutes late, because punctuality was for clerks. He wore midnight blue instead of black, because black was for funerals and this was his resurrection. He worked the room in slow counterclockwise arcs, always moving toward the light, always ending conversations himself, never letting anyone walk away first. He touched shoulders. He remembered names—not because he cared about people but because names were leverage, and Aldous Wren had never met a piece of information he didn't file for later use.

He was, Hazel thought, exactly what Marisol must have seen from her stenographer's chair every day for months: a man who believed the record existed to serve him, rather than the other way around.

*You're going to hear your own words tonight, Your Honor.* The thought sat in Hazel's chest like a swallowed coal. *Not the ones you said. The ones she wrote down.*

"Champagne?"

A guest—a woman in silver, diamond drops swinging—plucked two flutes without looking up from her conversation about school zoning. Hazel nodded as if acknowledged, drifted on. The tray grew lighter. The room grew warmer. Somewhere near the string quartet, a cellist sawed through something baroque and mournful, and Hazel thought: *she would have hated this music. She'd have wanted something with drums.*

They knew that about Marisol Vega now. Chapter thirty-six of a diary investigators had finally recovered—the real one, the true final entries, hopeful and funny and full of plans. *Testify anyway. Wear the red blazer. Buy Lucía dinner after, somewhere with cloth napkins.* Hazel had read those pages four times. They carried them now not in paper but in the body, the way dispatchers carry voices: permanently, in the ear.

The red blazer detail was what did it, every time. Not fear. Not dread. A shopping plan.

Hazel set the emptying tray on a passing station and checked the time. 8:41. Nineteen minutes.

* * *

The display binder sat on a raised table near the head of the room, inside a glass case that was less security than theater—Wren wanted the trial's artifacts seen, wanted them lit like relics. Inside: the gavel from the Halloran verdict. A framed copy of the front page. And the binder itself, open to a laminated page of official transcript, *State v. Halloran*, Day Forty-One, the day the jury foreman read the word *guilty* six times.

The case was locked. Of course it was locked. But Hazel had watched the docent—a hired event staffer, bored, checking his phone—open it twice already tonight, once to straighten the frame, once because a councilman's wife asked to photograph the gavel and he'd obliged with the weary gallantry of a man being paid eighteen dollars an hour to guard objects worth more than his life.

People don't guard things. They guard their idea of how much things matter.

Hazel had spent two nights with the duplicate transcript pages recovered from the annex—Marisol's own hand, her own shorthand expanded in her own marginal notes—and then another night with Imani building the forgery. Not a forgery of content. A forgery of *error*. That had been Imani's insight, delivered flatly over cold takeout at 2 a.m.: "Pruitt can't leave a wrong record alone. Twenty-six years as clerk of court. He corrected my reports so many times I started writing them wrong on purpose just to watch him twitch." She'd almost smiled. "We don't give him evidence. We give him a typo."

So the doctored page looked authentic because it was built from authentic pages—but it contained three deliberate errors. A misquoted line of testimony. A date off by one day. And a transcription notation in the corner, a stenographer's mark, rendered slightly wrong—the way someone would render it if they'd never sat behind a steno machine, if they'd only ever *read* transcripts, if they'd only ever *corrected* them.

Bait, dressed as a record.

And when Dale Pruitt couldn't stop himself from fixing it—when he came to the display case, or sent someone, or stood up mid-toast because the error was physically painful—Imani would be listening. They all would. Because the second half of the trap wasn't the binder at all.

It was Channel Three.

* * *

8:52. Hazel collected a fresh tray from the kitchen corridor, mostly as camouflage, and used the trip to run the checklist one more time, the way they ran pre-shift checks at dispatch, hands knowing the sequence without consulting the mind.

Earpiece: in, live, mic hot but muted. Imani's channel check had come through twenty minutes ago—two clicks, meaning *in position*, meaning she was somewhere by the coat check in a dress Hazel had never seen her wear, looking like exactly what she was, which was the only honest person in the building. Two marked units staged on the cross street. State investigators' liaison holding a warrant that wouldn't be signed until there was cause, because warrants needed cause and cause needed Pruitt to do what Pruitt could not help doing.

Backup recorder in the left jacket pocket, running since 7:00, battery good.

The duplicate transcript: folded once, in the inner pocket over Hazel's heart, where it had ridden all evening like a second heartbeat.

And the last item on the checklist, the one that wasn't equipment: *Are you sure?*

Hazel had asked it every night for a week. In the shower. On the platform waiting for the train. Once, absurdly, mid-call, while talking a lost tourist through finding the aquarium. *Are you sure? Because once you put that page in that case, in that room, in front of those people, you are not invisible anymore. You are a name attached to an act. Names get found. Found people get controlled.*

That was the old belief. The load-bearing wall of the whole hidden life: *to be known is to be held.*

But Marisol had known she might die for speaking, and she'd planned a dinner with cloth napkins anyway. And Hazel had spent three weeks being hunted by a man who'd turned their own invisibility into a weapon—who had written fiction in a dead woman's voice and mailed it to the one address in the city no one should have been able to find. Hiding hadn't protected them. Hiding had just made them easier to write about.

*Fine,* Hazel told the old belief, the way you tell a caller who won't hang up. *Then we go loud.*

* * *

At 9:04, a chime rang through the ballroom—actual chimes, staffed by an actual man with a mallet, because wealth loved ceremony even in its doorbells—and the crowd began folding itself toward the head table.

Hazel drifted with the current, tray abandoned, and took up position along the east wall beside a pillar hung with ivy that was real enough to be watered weekly. From there: full view of the dais, clear angle on the display case twelve feet to the right, sightline to the coat check where a woman in green stood pretending to admire the quartet.

Wren rose.

He didn't rush it. He let the applause happen, let it crest and fall and rise again, a conductor managing a room he'd conducted for thirty years. When he finally lifted his hands, the silence came obediently.

"A year ago tonight," Wren said, and his voice was made for ballrooms—warm oak, unhurried, "this city handed down a verdict that some said couldn't be reached. They said the machine was too big. They said the rot went too deep. They said a jury would never find Victor Halloran guilty, because men like Victor Halloran are never guilty—they're merely *accused*, briefly, before the system remembers whose interests it serves."

Polite laughter. Hazel watched the faces turn up toward him like plants toward sun.

"But this city proved them wrong," Wren continued. "Not because of any one man. Not because of the judge who presided—" a modest gesture at himself, executed with the precision of long practice, "—but because of the *record*. Because somewhere in this building, every day, ordinary people wrote down what actually happened. Testimony. Evidence. Truth, transcribed." He smiled, and it reached his eyes, which was the most frightening thing about him. "Justice is not a feeling, ladies and gentlemen. Justice is a *document*. And documents," he said, lifting his glass, "do not lie."

*No,* Hazel thought, moving. *Documents don't lie. People lie about documents. People erase witnesses and forge diaries and bury transcripts in annex basements, and then they stand in ballrooms and toast the paper.*

"To justice served," Wren said. "To the record. And to everyone in this room who keeps it."

Three hundred glasses rose. Three hundred voices murmured the toast back. The quartet swelled.

And Hazel Kavanagh crossed twelve feet of marble floor in a borrowed server's jacket, past the docent who was watching the toast like everyone else, past the councilman's wife still holding her photographed gavel, and opened the unlocked latch on the side of the display case—the one the docent never fully closed, because who steals justice at its own party—and slid the doctored transcript page into the binder, beneath the laminated verdict, angled so that the flawed stenographer's mark faced outward like a wound.

It took four seconds. It took a year.

Hazel closed the latch, stepped back into the ivy's shadow, and pressed the transmit key on the earpiece. One click. *Done.*

Across the room, Imani Okafor did not look at the pillar. She lifted her glass of untouched wine in a small private salute to no one, and drank.

* * *

The toast ended. The band resumed. The room exhaled into dessert and deal-making, and for perhaps ninety seconds nothing happened at all, and Hazel understood that this was the true point of no return—not the moment the page went into the case, but this one: the standing-still, the waiting, the moment when retreat was still technically possible and already meaningless.

Because the page was in. Which meant that within the hour, someone loyal to Wren would review the display materials for tomorrow's press tour. Within the hour, Dale Pruitt—or whoever Pruitt trusted—would see a transcript that was *wrong*. Wrong testimony. Wrong date. Wrong mark. And a man who had spent twenty-six years as the keeper of the record, who had killed to keep it clean, who had forged a dead woman's diary entry by entry because he couldn't bear an untidy narrative—

—that man would have to fix it.

"You're smiling," said a voice at Hazel's elbow, and Hazel's whole body went to ice before the voice resolved: Imani, materialized out of the crowd with the ease of a woman who'd done undercover work before the academy. She was looking straight ahead, at the dais, speaking barely above breath. "Don't. You look like a server who poisoned something."

"I feel like one."

"Channel's hot. Liaison's in the lobby. Pruitt's at table six, third from the end, blue tie." A pause. "He hasn't touched his dessert. He's been watching the display case since the toast ended."

Hazel followed the glance. Table six. Dale Pruitt sat with his hands folded around a coffee cup he wasn't drinking, a gray, tidy, forgettable man in a room designed to make everyone else memorable—and his eyes, when they flicked up, went not to Wren, not to the crowd, but to the glass case, with the fixed, hungry attention of a man reading a document across a room.

"He sees it," Hazel breathed.

"Maybe."

"No. He *sees* it." Hazel's mouth had gone dry and their pulse was doing something arrhythmic and ancient. "Imani. He's already reading it. He knows the testimony line is wrong. He's—" Hazel stopped. Watched. Pruitt set down the cup. Straightened his napkin. Began, with terrible patience, to fold it into eighths.

"He's deciding whether he can wait," Imani said quietly. "Whether fixing it can wait until the room empties. Whether anyone will notice a clerk touching a binder."

"And if he decides it can't wait?"

Imani finally turned her head, just enough. Her eyes in the chandelier light were steady and dark and absolutely certain. "Then he walks to that case in front of three hundred witnesses, corrects the record with his own hands, and hangs himself with a laminated page." She set down her wine. "Which is why you're going to pick up that tray, walk to the far side of the room, and be holding something innocent when it happens. Both of us. We're furniture. Furniture doesn't smile."

Hazel picked up the tray.

And then—before either of them could move—Dale Pruitt unfolded his napkin, laid it beside his untouched plate, stood, buttoned his jacket, and began to walk toward the display case with the unhurried, righteous stride of a man going to correct an error.

The band played on. Wren laughed at something a senator said. Three hundred people toasted each other.

And Hazel Kavanagh, heart slamming against the folded transcript's ghost in their jacket pocket, keyed the mute off their microphone, felt the little red light of transmission bloom warm against their jaw, and whispered into the open channel, to every scanner and squad car and state investigator listening in the dark:

"Channel Three, this is Kavanagh. He's moving. Recording live."

Pruitt reached the case. His hand settled on the latch.

And he glanced—just once, quick as a card sharp—at the pillar where Hazel had been standing, and found it empty, and frowned, because somewhere in that tidy gray mind a loose end had just refused to stay tied.
