## 38. Shift Change

The board still smelled like dry-erase marker and old coffee, the way it had for six years.

Hazel stood in front of it at 11:40 p.m., forty minutes before shift start, and looked at what remained of their life's quiet work: eleven open cases, each one a name in black ink and a date in red, each one a person somebody had stopped being able to find. The fluorescent light above the board buzzed at the frequency Hazel had long ago stopped hearing—until tonight, when they heard everything.

They'd given notice two weeks ago. Two weeks' notice felt right for a job like this. Not dramatic, not abrupt. Just enough time to hand off the headset to someone who wouldn't know what it had carried.

"You're early." Devraj came through the break room door with his jacket half on, keys jingling. He was the day-shift lead now, promoted last spring, and he still walked into every room like he was apologizing for occupying space in it. "You didn't have to come in tonight. I told you we'd cover."

"I know." Hazel picked up the eraser. "I wanted to do this part myself."

Devraj watched them wipe the first name off the board—Tomas Reyes, missing nine days, found in Reno working under his cousin's name because he'd owed money to people who didn't accept explanations. Closed three weeks ago. The ink smeared gray and came away clean.

"You're really doing it," Devraj said. "The casework thing. With the detective."

"With Detective Okafor as legal liaison, yeah." Hazel erased another name. "It's not a big thing. A phone line. A filing system. A promise that someone will actually call back."

"That's a big thing." Devraj leaned against the console bank, arms crossed. "That's the thing nobody does. You know how many calls we take a night that go nowhere? Not because we don't care. Because there's nowhere for them to *go*."

Hazel did know. That was the whole architecture of their old life: they had built themselves into the gap between the call and the nowhere, an anonymous voice in the static that could move without leaving a shadow. It had worked. For years it had worked beautifully.

And then Marisol Vega's diary had started writing back.

* * *

The first hours of the shift passed the way the quiet hours always did—wrong numbers, a driver with a flat on Delmore, a bachelorette party that needed a van split into two sedans. Hazel handled all of it with hands that knew the console the way a pianist knows a keyboard, without looking, without thinking. Channel one, channel two, channel three. The open line they'd used to catch Dale Pruitt confessing murder in front of the entire city's legal establishment still had a strip of tape over its label where someone had written, in Imani's blocky handwriting, *THE CHANNEL*, and nobody had peeled it off since.

At 1:15 a.m., the phone rang on Line Four.

Hazel's hand paused above it. Line Four. Of course it was Line Four. The universe had a sense of humor and no taste.

"Citywide Dispatch, this is Kavanagh."

"Hi—hi, sorry, is this—is this the number from the news?" A young voice, male, cracked with exhaustion and something sharper underneath. "The article said you used to work here, but—I called the other number and it just rings—"

"The new line doesn't open until Monday," Hazel said gently. "But I'm here now. Talk to me."

A breath on the other end, ragged, held too long. "My brother. Mateo. He's nineteen. He didn't come home Tuesday and my mom keeps saying he's fine, he's staying with friends, but his friends aren't saying that, and the police took a report but the officer kept checking his phone while I was talking to him, and—" The voice caught. "You're the one who found all those people, right? The ones in the article?"

"Some of them," Hazel said. "Tell me about Mateo. Start with Tuesday morning. Start wherever it's easiest."

And so, on their second-to-last night at Citywide Taxi Dispatch, Hazel Kavanagh sat in the same chair they'd sat in for six years and took the first case of the rest of their life, scribbling on the back of a delivery slip because the new case files hadn't been printed yet. Mateo Ruiz. Last seen near the Eastside transit center. A jacket left behind that he never went anywhere without. A mother who couldn't say the word *missing* out loud.

"I'm going to write all of this down," Hazel told him when he ran out of breath. "And on Monday, when the line opens, you're going to be my first official caller. But I'm starting tonight, because you called tonight, and that matters. Okay?"

"Okay," he whispered. "Thank you. Thank you."

Hazel hung up and sat for a moment in the hum of the console, the delivery slip trembling very slightly in their hand. Then they smoothed it flat, folded it once, and put it in their breast pocket—the pocket that had held a murdered woman's duplicate transcript into a room full of champagne and lies—and got back to work.

* * *

At 3 a.m., Devraj brought two coffees to the console and set one down beside Hazel without asking.

"So the article," he said. He'd clearly been waiting all night to bring it up, maybe longer. "The one in the *Ledger*. They called you 'the dispatcher who heard everything.' That's—honestly, that's a great line. Did you write it?"

"A reporter wrote it. I just failed to correct her."

"Six years," Devraj said, shaking his head. "Six years you sat in that chair, and I thought you were just—good at your job. Really good. Weirdly good. Like the time you told Marcus to check on the driver who hadn't radioed in, and the guy was having a stroke behind the wheel of cab 214. Or when you flagged that fare pattern on the west side and it turned out to be the guy doing the carjackings." He laughed softly. "We used to joke you were psychic. Ray called you the Oracle."

"Ray called me a lot of things."

"Yeah, well." Devraj turned his cup in his hands. "He also cried at your going-away thing, so."

"There was a going-away thing?"

"There was cake. There was a card. It's in the break room. You're legally obligated to look at it before you leave." He hesitated, then: "Can I ask you something? And you can tell me it's none of my business, which it is."

"Ask."

"Why the hiding? All those years. The article talked around it—said you 'preferred to stay out of records,' whatever that means—but everybody here has been losing their minds trying to figure it out. Were you in witness protection? Running from somebody? Are you secretly rich?" He grinned. "Please say secretly rich."

Hazel looked at the board. At the eight names still standing in black ink, waiting for the eraser.

"For a long time I believed that if nobody knew who I was, nobody could use me," they said. "Being known felt like handing someone the manual. Here's how to find Hazel. Here's what Hazel cares about. Here's the lever." They took a sip of coffee. "And then a woman disappeared, and she'd written about me in her diary before I even knew her name, and I understood that hiding wasn't protecting anyone. It was just a smaller cage with better lighting."

Devraj was quiet for a moment. "That's the saddest thing anyone's ever said to me at 3 a.m., and I work nights."

Hazel laughed—a real one, surprising them both.

"But you're out of the cage now," he said.

"I'm out of the cage." Hazel set the cup down. "Turns out the door was unlocked the whole time. I just liked the cage."

"No offense," Devraj said, "but that's the most dispatcher thing I've ever heard."

* * *

At 4:50, ten minutes before the board-closing ritual Hazel had invented and no one else had ever been allowed to perform, the radio crackled.

"Dispatch, cab 309."

"Go ahead, 309." Hazel knew the voice—Yolanda, fourteen years driving, saw everything, reported half of it.

"You got a minute? Weird one. Picked up a fare outside St. Anthony's, young guy, crying his eyes out. Took him to the bus station, and halfway there he asks me—if I've ever heard of that dispatcher lady. From the news. Says his brother called her last night and she actually listened, and now he's got a reason to stay in town instead of running." Yolanda paused. "Anyway. Thought somebody should know it landed. Over."

Hazel pressed the transmit key and found, briefly, nothing to say.

"Copy, 309," they managed. "Thanks for the ride report. Drive safe."

They sat back. The console hummed. Somewhere across the city, a nineteen-year-old was buying a bus ticket he wasn't going to use, and a mother was about to get her son back, and none of it would ever appear in any article or any file, because most of what mattered happened exactly like this—invisible, unrecorded, real.

Hazel had spent six years believing invisibility was their superpower. Standing in the yellow light of the dispatch bay, they finally understood it had only ever been the training. The point was never to be unseen. The point was to see.

* * *

At 5 a.m., Hazel stood at the board with the eraser in hand and closed the cases.

Not solved—all of them, honestly, could not be solved; some names on that board belonged to people who were gone in ways that couldn't be fixed by anyone, and Hazel had made peace with the difference between closing a case and closing a wound. But each name got its ritual. Each name got spoken aloud, once, in the empty dispatch bay, the way they'd done it alone for six years: *Tomas Reyes, found. Denise Okafor-Hale, no relation to the detective, found safe. Peter Lindqvist, remains identified, family notified.* Down the board, name by name, until only one entry remained.

Marisol Vega. Missing March 14th. Found—or rather, found *for*, which was its own category Hazel had added in blue ink the night after the gala, after the handcuffs, after Wren was led past the cameras with his face doing arithmetic instead of grief.

Hazel erased her name last. The ink came away in a single clean stroke, and beneath it the board was blank for the first time in six years, white and empty and waiting.

"There," Hazel said quietly, to the board, to the room, to the woman whose diary had dragged them into the light. "That's all of them."

Then they signed off. Officially, procedurally, on the log: *Kavanagh, H. Shift complete. No incidents outstanding.* Their signature looked different than it used to—steadier, somehow, though it was the same hand.

In the break room, the card was waiting, three feet of poster board covered in signatures and inside jokes and one drawing of a headset with angel wings. Ray had written *THE ORACLE RETIRES* in letters visible from orbit. Someone had taped a twenty-dollar bill inside with a note: *For the phone line. Don't argue.*

Hazel argued silently, lost, and put the twenty in their pocket next to Mateo Ruiz's delivery slip.

Devraj walked them to the door. Outside, dawn was doing its slow gray work on the skyline, and a taxi idled at the curb—Yolanda's cab, 309, engine running.

"Heard somebody might need a ride," she said through the window.

"Where am I going?" Hazel asked. "I don't start the new office till Monday."

"Then let's drive around," Yolanda said. "I know this city pretty well. Somebody once told me every street in it has a story nobody's listening for yet."

Hazel looked back once at the dark windows of the dispatch bay—at the blank board, the humming consoles, the chair by Line Four where an invisible person had spent six years learning how to hear. Then they opened the cab door and got in, and the city unspooled ahead of them, wide awake, full of missing people and the ones who loved them, and for the first time in their life, Hazel Kavanagh was not afraid to be seen looking for either.

The sign on the new office door was already ordered. It read:

**KAVANAGH & OKAFOR — MISSING PERSONS CASEWORK**
*Someone will call you back.*
