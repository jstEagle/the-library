## 1. The Ledger of Not-Yet

The rain had been falling on St. Aldhelm's for three days without pause, and I had begun to suspect it was less weather than atmosphere — the sky itself settling in over the cathedral like a mourner who had nowhere better to be. I mention this not because it matters to what follows, but because it was raining when Aldous Fenwick came through my door, and rain is the only honest witness I had that afternoon. Everything else that entered my archive that day was lying.

I should introduce myself properly, since introductions are a courtesy I extend so rarely that I've grown rusty at them. My name is Vesper Underhill, and I am the archivist of St. Aldhelm's Cathedral, which is a way of saying that I live with the dead. Not unpleasantly — the dead are excellent company. They ask nothing, they interrupt nothing, they never arrive at closing time wanting something fetched from a box that has been mislabeled since the reign of a king whose name I can never remember correctly. For thirty-one years I have catalogued their leases, their wills, their grievances, their tithes of wax and wool. I know the smell of every century this building has stood through. The fourteenth smells of iron and vellum. The seventeenth smells of candle smoke and stubbornness. The nineteenth smells of dust and denial.

The living, by contrast, are unreliable narrators.

So when the door at the foot of the archive stairs opened at twenty minutes past four — an hour when no living person has business with me — I confess I felt something adjacent to pleasure. A visitor. An interruption. A crack in the long grey afternoon.

"Mr. Underhill?" The voice came down the stairwell before the man did, pitched carefully, as though he'd rehearsed it in a doorway somewhere. "Mr. Vesper Underhill?"

"The same," I called back, not rising. There is a certain power in not rising. It is one of the few powers available to archivists, and I hoard it shamelessly.

He descended badly. That was my first impression — a man descending badly, one hand on the rail, shoulders drawn up around his ears as though the low ceiling might take offense at him personally. He was perhaps fifty-five, thin in the way of men who have recently stopped eating properly rather than men who never started, dressed in a good coat that had been expensive some years ago and had since learned to look anxious. His eyes did the thing I have only seen in photographs of trapped animals: they moved everywhere at once and settled nowhere.

"You're the cathedral archivist," he said. "The records man."

"I am the records man," I agreed. "Among other things. I'm also the man who locks the doors at five, so if you've come about a burial plot or a disputed pew, you've chosen your hour poorly."

"It's not a plot." He sat down across from me without being asked, which told me something; then he perched on the very edge of the chair as though it might be revoked, which told me more. "It's — I need you to prove something hasn't happened yet."

I set down my pen. In thirty-one years, I have heard requests of every conceivable shape. Prove my grandfather wasn't a bigamist. Prove the chapter owes us the east field. Prove my wife's family were gentry, or failing that, make it look as though they were. But *prove something hasn't happened yet* was a new entry in the catalogue.

"That," I said, "is either very easy or very difficult, depending entirely on the thing."

"A murder." He said it flatly, the way a man says a word he has been saying to himself for weeks until it has worn smooth and lost its edges. "You're to prove a murder hasn't happened yet."

"Well," I said. "That side of it at least should present no difficulty. Unless you've come to tell me otherwise."

I meant it as a joke. It was a poor joke, and I knew it was a poor joke even as it left my mouth, but loneliness does that to a man — it makes him reach for humor the way a starving man reaches for bread, without much care whether the loaf is stale. And Fenwick did not laugh. Fenwick went white to the lips and gripped the arms of his chair, and I understood, with the particular clarity that comes to those of us who spend our lives reading dead men's handwriting, that I had just made a joke about a murder to a man who believed — genuinely, completely, with his whole frightened body — that he was going to be murdered.

"I see," I said, more quietly. "Perhaps you'd better begin at the beginning."

"There isn't a beginning," he said. "That's the whole point. That's why I came to you and not to — " He stopped himself before *the police*, but I heard it anyway. "There's a record. In your crypt ledger. The sealed ledger, the old one, the one nobody's allowed to touch. It has an entry in it. Dated the fourteenth of November." He swallowed. "Three weeks from now. It gives a name. Mine."

The rain filled the silence for both of us. Somewhere above us, in the nave, a verger's footsteps crossed the stone, unhurried, indifferent, a living man walking over the heads of ten thousand dead ones.

"The crypt ledger," I said slowly. I know every volume in my keeping the way a father knows his children, including the difficult ones, and the crypt ledger is the most difficult of all. It is a great bound register, calf over oak, kept from 1741 to 1847, recording interments in the crypt below the nave — the plague years, the drowned, the grandees the chapter considered worth the best real estate in the building. It has been sealed since 1847, when the crypt itself was closed to burials by order of Bishop Thorne. The key lives with the chapter. The ledger lives in the strong room behind my office, under lock, under seal, under a hundred and seventy-eight years of institutional *don't*.

"No one has opened that ledger," I said, "in my lifetime."

"Someone wrote in it," said Aldous Fenwick.

Now, I want to be precise here, because precision is the only religion I have ever practiced. What he was claiming was impossible in a way that went past mere improbability into territory I would normally classify as madness. A sealed ledger cannot acquire new entries. Ink does not travel backward through time. Vellum does not grow younger. If a man told me the moon had landed in the close and asked me to verify it, I would have shown him the door with more patience than I showed Fenwick what followed.

And yet.

And yet there was something in the way he said it. Not the certainty of a madman — I have met the mad, in this archive, bent over genealogies, finding their own names in centuries where they could not possibly have been. Madmen are certain. Fenwick was not certain. Fenwick was *terrified*, and terror, in my experience, is a far more reliable witness than certainty. Certainty performs. Terror simply sits there, dripping.

"Show me," I said.

* * *

The strong room is behind my office, behind a door most people assume is a cupboard, because I encourage them to assume that. I let Fenwick wait outside it while I unshelved the crypt ledger myself — partly out of protocol, and partly because I wanted to watch his face when he saw it. He looked at the great black-bound volume the way a man looks at a dog that has bitten him once already.

"Where did you hear of this entry?" I asked. "Who told you?"

"No one told me." His hands were working at each other in his lap. "I saw it."

"Saw it where? The ledger is sealed. The crypt is sealed. The key—"

"I know where the key is," he said, too quickly, and then, catching himself: "I mean — I know it's the chapter's. I haven't — Mr. Underhill, please. Just open it. Look at the page. Tell me I'm mad. I'll pay you to tell me I'm mad. That's all I'm asking. Tell me I'm a lunatic and I'll go away and bother you no further."

There is a version of me — the sensible version, the version my colleagues in the chapter would endorse — that would have done exactly that. Opened the ledger, run a finger down the index, found nothing, pronounced him mad, collected nothing, and returned to my cataloguing like a man returning to a warm bath. It would have taken four minutes.

But I will tell you a secret about the sensible version of me: he has been alone in this building for thirty-one years, and he is tired, and when a frightened stranger walks down his stairs asking for the impossible, some small starved thing in him sits up and pays attention. Curiosity, they call it, when they're being kind. I have never been sure it isn't just loneliness wearing a better coat.

I broke the seal.

I want to be clear that breaking that seal was a serious matter — a matter of chapter discipline, potentially of employment. The wax crumbled along its old score line with a sound like a small dry cough, and neither of us moved for a moment, as though we'd woken something.

Then I turned to the pages near the end, to November, and found the year — the current year, our year, written in a hand I did not recognize, in ink that was brown-black and iron-galled and looked as though it had been drying for two hundred years.

*14th November. In the crypt below the third pillar of the north aisle, the body of Aldous Fenwick, gentleman, slain by the knife. Entered by order.*

Below it, the registrar's formula for interment, complete, formal, finished. As though it had been waiting for him since before he was born.

I read it twice. Three times. I am not a man who frightens easily — the dead have cured me of that — but I felt the chill move up my arms nonetheless, the specific chill of the archivist who has just encountered a document that should not exist. Every instinct I possess, every year of training, every grain of professional knowledge said *forgery*. And every one of those instincts also said: *but it doesn't look like one.*

"How," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved, "did you know this was here?"

Fenwick was staring at the page. At his own name, in antique ink, in a book older than his grandfather. When he spoke, it was barely above the rain.

"Because I've seen this book before," he said. "When I was a boy. My father brought me. He made me put my hand on the cover and he said, *remember this book, Aldous. One day it will matter.*" He looked up at me, and his eyes were wet and furious and afraid. "I thought he meant the family name was in it. I thought he was being sentimental. Then three weeks ago I find a copy of this page slipped under my hotel room door, and now I can't sleep, and I can't eat, and every bell that rings in that tower I count, because I keep thinking — what if it's a knell? What if it's mine?"

I closed the ledger gently, the way one closes a door on a sickroom.

"Mr. Fenwick," I said, "what I am about to say may disappoint you professionally, since you came here hoping to be called a lunatic. But I have handled documents for thirty-one years, and I am going to examine that entry with everything I know, and if it is a forgery, I will find the forgery. And if it is not a forgery—" I paused, because the alternative was absurd, and yet I heard myself say it anyway, "—then someone has committed a crime against time itself, and I should very much like to meet them."

He laughed then — a short, cracked sound, half relief and half despair — and fumbled inside his coat for an envelope. He pushed it across the desk toward me without opening it first, which told me he had counted it, and recounted it, and did not trust himself to look at it again.

"That's triple your going rate," he said. "I asked. Don't ask how. Triple, in advance, and the same again when you prove that page is a lie. Because it *has* to be a lie, Mr. Underhill. Doesn't it? It has to be."

I looked at the envelope. I looked at the man. Outside, the rain went on falling on St. Aldhelm's, patient as ever, indifferent as ever, and the five o'clock bell began to toll above us, and I watched Fenwick flinch at every stroke, counting, counting, counting.

"Yes," I said. "It has to be."

I took the envelope. I told myself afterward, many times, that I took it for the money, and that is true in the way that all self-deceptions are true — technically, conveniently, and beside the point. The truth is that no one had needed me for anything in a very long time, and a man can live among the dead for thirty-one years before he notices that what he actually misses is the living.

"Come back Thursday," I said. "Bring me everything your father ever told you about this book. Everything. Even the things that seem like nonsense."

"Especially those," said Aldous Fenwick, rising, and for the first time since he'd come down my stairs, he almost smiled. "My father's nonsense was the only inheritance he ever left me."

He climbed back up into the rain, badly, one hand on the rail. I stood a while at the foot of the stairs listening to the door close, and then I went back into the strong room and unlocked the cabinet where I keep the things I do not show visitors, and took out my magnifier, my reagent kit, and a bottle of the good whisky I save for discoveries.

One of these, I suspected, I was going to need very soon.

What I did not suspect — what no amount of thirty-one years among the dead could have taught me — was that before I reached the bottom of that bottle, I would find a second entry in that ledger, dated one day after Fenwick's murder.

With my own initials in it.
