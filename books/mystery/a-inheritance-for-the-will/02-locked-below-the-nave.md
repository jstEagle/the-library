## 2. Locked Below the Nave

There is a particular hour in an archive when the building forgets it belongs to the living. For St. Aldhelm's, that hour arrives around four in the afternoon in November, when the light through the clerestory goes the colour of weak tea and the radiators tick like something counting down. I have spent thirty-one years in that hour. Most men my age count their friends; I count folios. It has been, on the whole, a fair trade — the dead ask so little of you, and they never arrive at closing time wanting to talk about themselves.

Fenwick had arrived at closing time wanting to talk about himself, and he had left behind three hundred pounds and a problem that would not sit still.

I locked the outer door out of habit, then unlocked it again out of superstition. If a man was going to be murdered in three weeks' time, I reasoned, it seemed unkind to seal him out of the only building that knew about it.

The crypt ledger lived in the iron cabinet at the back of the archive, the one with two locks — mine and the chapter's — because the chapter had never entirely trusted its archivists, and its archivists had long since returned the compliment. The ledger itself was a monster: calf-bound, brass-cornered, heavier than a sleeping cat and considerably less affectionate. *Registrum Cryptae*, 1741–1847. Every soul interred beneath the nave for a hundred and six years, entered by hand by a succession of vergers whose handwriting deteriorated in exact proportion to their job security.

I carried it to the reading table under the good lamp, put on my white cotton gloves — not because the vellum needed protecting from me, but because I needed reminding that this was work and not haunting — and opened it to the last quarter of 1847.

The entry was where he said it would be.

*November the fourteenth, in the year of our Lord 1847.* A Tuesday. I checked; I always check. *Aldous Fenwick, gentleman, of this city, slain within the precincts of this cathedral by persons unknown. Interred in the lower vault, the service read by none, the stone left blank.*

I sat with that for a while. The radiator ticked. Somewhere above me a verger was dragging chairs across the nave floor with the deliberate violence of a man who hates his employment, and I confess I envied him. His mysteries ended at five o'clock.

Here is what any sensible person does upon finding a murder recorded in a ledger a hundred and seventy-eight years before the murder: they conclude forgery, and they go home. That is what I told Fenwick I would do, and I meant it, in the way one means things at half past four with money in one's pocket.

But I am not sensible. I am an archivist. Sensibility is the first thing the profession removes, along with any capacity to leave a document alone.

So I did what I do. I examined the entry.

***

The ink first. Ink is honest in a way people are not. Iron gall ink — the standard for the period — is made from oak galls and ferrous sulphate, and once it dries into paper or vellum it begins a chemical conversation with its host that cannot be faked quickly and cannot be undone at all. After a century and three-quarters, genuine iron gall darkens, browns at the edges of each stroke, and in places eats clean through the writing surface where the acid has done its patient work. Forgers know this. Good forgers simulate it with tea, with dilute acids, with careful burnishing. Bad forgers simply write in brown ink and hope.

Under the loupe, the Fenwick entry showed everything I expected and nothing I wanted. The browning was real browning — not applied to the surface but rising up *through* the strokes, which is the tell that separates chemistry from cosmetics. There were two microscopic perforations where the gall had eaten through, exactly where the pen had paused longest, on the downstroke of the *F* and the crossbar of the *t* in "gentleman." Acid follows dwell time. No forger alive thinks to vary his pressure so precisely, because no forger alive is thinking about where a tired verger's hand hesitated in 1847.

And the hand itself. The entry was written in the style of Josiah Crake, verger of St. Aldhelm's from 1831 to 1849, whose script I could draw from memory — a cramped, downward-slanting secretary hand with a peculiar hooked ascender on the letter *d*, as though every *d* were trying to escape the page backwards. I have catalogued eleven hundred entries in Crake's hand. This was Crake's hand. Not an imitation of it. The rhythm was right, the ink pooling at the joins of letters where a right-handed writer lifts and re-lands, the small hesitation tremor that appears in all of Crake's later work and worsens after the death of his wife in 1845.

If it was forged, it was forged by someone who had studied Crake more devotedly than I had, which I found professionally insulting, and who had access to period materials of a quality that does not exist on the open market, which I found worse than insulting. It found it frightening. Because there is a third possibility, and I had begun, against every instinct of my training, to consider it.

The vellum.

Vellum can be dated. Not precisely — but a skin carries its history the way a face does. This leaf had been ruled with the same dry-point lines as the rest of the quire, pricked in the same margins, and — this was the detail that stopped me — it bore a palimpsest ghost. Hold vellum to raking light and older writing sometimes rises faintly through, scraped away but never gone. Under the lamp, angled just so, I could see it: a previous entry, effaced, in a different hand, dated by its few surviving words to somewhere in the 1790s. Someone had erased a burial record from this leaf and written Fenwick's murder over the grave of it.

That was wrong in a way I couldn't yet name. Ledgers are not palimpsests. Vellum was expensive, yes, and a careless verger might scrape a leaf rather than waste it — but not in a *chapter* ledger, not in the one book the bishop's visitors were entitled to inspect. Unless the erasure happened after the inspections stopped. Unless it happened recently, by someone using old materials on a leaf that had been sitting blank for a century and a half, waiting.

Which raised the question of how a blank leaf had survived at all. Quires were made to be filled. I turned back through the ledger, folio by folio, checking the collation — the sequence of leaves, the way you'd check the teeth of a comb. Every gathering was complete except the last. One leaf, between the final burial entry of October 1847 and the colophon where Crake closed the book, had been left empty. Left empty, and then — sometime, somehow — filled.

I wrote the date of my examination in my own notebook, because archivists are vain creatures and we like to imagine our successors reading us. Then I sat back and looked at the whole impossible thing laid out under the lamp: authentic ink, authentic hand, authentic vellum, authentic erasure, recording a death that had not happened, of a man who had shaken my hand that afternoon with fingers cold as the ledger's brass corners.

There are two explanations. Either Aldous Fenwick had found the finest forger in England and paid him in museum-grade antiques to perpetrate a joke costing three hundred pounds —

— or the entry was exactly what it appeared to be, and the world was less orderly than I had spent my life assuming.

I did not believe the second explanation. I want that on the record. I am a man of documents, and documents obey rules. But belief, I have learned, is not required. Only attention.

***

The crypt itself was the other half of the problem, and the half I liked least, because it involved leaving my chair.

St. Aldhelm's crypt lies beneath the eastern end of the nave, reached by a spiral stair behind the choir vestry that I have descended perhaps nine times in thirty-one years, and six of those were to retrieve a dropped torch. It is not a romantic place. It is cold, low-ceilinged, and smells of stone and standing time. The plague dead are supposed to be down there — the parish dead of the 1660s, bricked into niches when the cathedral ran out of consecrated ground — though in truth I have always suspected the niches hold more rubble than bones, rubble being cheaper than exhumation and considerably less controversial.

Since 1847, the crypt door has been locked. Not merely closed — *locked*, and the lock changed twice, most recently in 1953, and the key held solely by the chapter, meaning in practice by whichever canon currently draws the short straw of custody. The official reason is structural: the vault roof is unsound, inspection denied, public excluded. The unofficial reason, if you pressed anyone, was a shrug. Cathedrals are institutions, and institutions do not explain locked doors; they simply maintain them, generation after generation, until the reason rots away and only the lock remains, polished by use.

Polished by use. That phrase had come to me on the stair and it would not leave.

I stood before the crypt door at ten past five, having signed myself into the restricted area with the mild fiction of a "condition survey." The door is oak, double-leaved, iron-strapped, and the lock is a heavy lever affair set in a brass escutcheon. Brass, in an unused doorway, dulls. It tarnishes green-black and grows a fine bloom of oxide in the grain. The escutcheon on the crypt door gleamed. Not cleaned-gleamed — *handled*-gleamed, the warm wear of fingers across the keyhole plate, the way a church door handle goes bright where a thousand hands push it.

Someone used that lock. Recently. Regularly.

I crouched and looked closer, feeling like a fool and then, rapidly, not feeling like a fool at all. In the dust of the threshold — dust that lay thick everywhere else, on the sill, on the iron strap hinges, in the corners — there was a swept channel. Narrow. The width of a door swinging inward. Someone swept the threshold, or wiped it, or the traffic itself kept a path clear. And beside the hinge-side jamb, half in shadow, a scuff mark in the stone: the repeated drag of something heavy, moved in and out, many times, always at the same angle.

Bodies go down into crypts once. Whatever came in and out of this room often enough to polish its lock and sweep its threshold was not a body.

I stood up too fast and my knee cracked in protest, and in the silence that followed I heard, very clearly, the sound of the cathedral settling above me — that groan and tick of old stone and older timber that I have heard ten thousand times and never once listened to. It sounded, just then, like footsteps overhead. It was not footsteps. I am nearly certain it was not footsteps.

Here is the arithmetic that followed me back up the stair, and I set it down now exactly as it set itself down in me then:

An entry in a sealed ledger, in a perfect antique hand, recording a murder three weeks hence. A crypt sealed since 1847 whose lock shines with use. A blank leaf that waited a century and a half to be filled. And a client who flinched — I remembered it now, the way he flinched when the hour bell rang, ducking his head as though the sound were aimed at him — a client who had walked into my archive knowing precisely which book to fear.

Fenwick had not asked me to prove a murder hadn't happened. He had asked me to prove it hadn't happened *yet*. I had taken that for the grammar of a frightened man. It might be the grammar of a man who had read the entry himself, recently, somewhere he should never have been able to read it.

Which meant the question was no longer *is the entry false?*

The question was *who has been inside my ledger?* — and behind that, quieter and colder, the question underneath all questions in this building: *what else has been rewritten while we weren't looking?*

I climbed back into the archive as the light failed completely. The ledger still lay open on the table, patient as a trap. I looked at the entry a long moment — *slain within the precincts of this cathedral* — and then, because I am who I am, I did the only thing that has ever steadied me in thirty-one years among the dead.

I began to read the marginalia.

It was Cobb who interrupted me, an hour later, tapping at the archive door with his torch already lit, though the corridor beyond was bright enough. The old sexton stood in the doorway with his cap in his hands and a look on his face I had never seen on him before — and Cobb's face has a limited repertoire, mostly weather and complaint.

"You've had the ledger out," he said. Not a question.

"I've had the ledger out," I agreed.

He looked past me at the open book, at the lamp, at the entry, and something crossed his face that in a younger man I would have called fear. In Cobb it looked more like recognition.

"Put it back," he said quietly. "And don't go asking after the crypt door, Mr. Underhill. Some doors are locked for a reason."

"Whose reason?"

He settled his cap on his head, and did not answer, and left me alone with the dead, the lamp, and the growing conviction that the only thing more unsettling than a murder recorded before it happens

is a sexton who already knows about it.
