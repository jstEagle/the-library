## 3. The Client Who Waits

I have never trusted a man who keeps his office underground. I want to say that plainly, at the outset, because everything else I did that afternoon contradicts it, and a man ought to be allowed one honest statement before his contradictions begin.

The archivist's name was Underhill — Vesper Underhill, which is the sort of name a novelist would invent and then be accused of trying too hard. He had received me in the archive beneath St. Aldhelm's Cathedral, among shelves of ledgers so old their spines had gone the colour of tea left standing, and he had listened to my impossible request with the expression of a man deciding whether to be flattered or insulted. Then he had said yes. Triple fee, paid in advance, and he hadn't even blinked at the sum, which told me either that he was honest or that he was expensive, and in my experience the two categories overlap more often than anyone admits.

So there we were: two men and several tons of other people's deaths.

"You'll want to know why," I said.

Underhill folded his hands on the desk between us. They were long hands, ink-stained at the cuticles, the hands of someone who has spent a lifetime handling things that crumble. "Only if you'd like to tell me."

"Most men would insist."

"Most men," he said, "think the records are furniture. You think they're testimony. That makes you interesting, Mr. Fenwick. Interesting men can tell me as much or as little as they like."

I liked him immediately, which frightened me, because liking people is how they get at you.

* * *

Let me explain what kind of man I am, since you'll be spending this chapter in my company and deserve fair warning.

I am sixty-one years old. In thirty-nine days I will be sixty-two, and on that birthday — the twenty-second of November, mark it however you like — a trust matures. My family's trust. The Fenwick Trust, established 1847 by my great-great-grandfather Josiah, a wool merchant with a genius for money and, if the family portraits are anything to go by, a face like a closed door. On my sixty-second birthday, per the terms, the principal passes wholly to me. Not to the cathedral chapter that has administered it for a hundred and seventy-odd years. To me. All of it.

You will say: congratulations. You will say: what a pleasant problem to have. And then you will hear the rest, which is that in the past four months I have been followed twice, my solicitor has died suddenly of a heart attack at fifty-four, and someone has been asking questions about me in the town where I was born — questions about whether I have any living kin, any heirs, anyone whose interest in my continued existence might outweigh theirs in its conclusion.

I am not a paranoid man. I was an accountant for forty years. Paranoia is bad arithmetic; you check the figures twice before you panic. I checked them three times.

"I don't trust lawyers," I told Underhill. "My solicitor is dead, and the replacement came recommended by the chapter itself, which is rather like having your auditor nominated by the man being audited. I don't trust the police, because the police need evidence, and evidence is precisely what gets made to disappear by people with keys. I don't trust doctors, banks, or my own cousin Gerald, who has looked at me lately the way a dog looks at a sausage on a high shelf." I paused. "What I trust is paper. Paper doesn't lie. Paper only waits."

"And paper," Underhill said quietly, "is what brought you to me."

"Paper is what brought me to you." I reached into my coat — slowly; I've learned that sudden movements make everyone nervous, myself included — and produced a photograph. I had taken it in the cathedral library two weeks earlier, when they let me consult the burial registers for my family's plot. A page from the crypt ledger. Sealed ledger, sealed crypt, kept under lock since 1847, accessible only to the chapter. And yet I had seen it, briefly, through a door left carelessly ajar while a verger fetched something, and what I saw on the open page had rearranged my understanding of my remaining days.

Underhill took the photograph and went very still.

He was still for perhaps ten seconds. For an archivist, I would learn, that is a very long time — the equivalent of an ordinary man dropping his coffee.

"Where did you get this?"

"A door that shouldn't have been open, was."

"This entry is dated the ninth of December." His voice had changed. The dry amusement was gone out of it, and underneath was something harder and much less comfortable. "That's three weeks away. This entry describes a death that hasn't happened."

"Now you understand my request."

"Mr. Fenwick." He set the photograph down with exaggerated care, the way one sets down something that might be armed. "Either this is a forgery of extraordinary quality, inserted into a ledger that has been locked for a century and three-quarters — or it is not a forgery, in which case I have no vocabulary for what it is. Do you appreciate that neither possibility is comforting?"

"I appreciate," I said, "that I have thirty-nine days to find out which."

* * *

We talked for two hours. I gave him more than I meant to. There is something about a man surrounded by the dead that loosens the tongue; the dead are such patient listeners that the living feel obliged to fill the silence on their behalf.

I told him about the trust. About the terms — Josiah's quaint, controlling little conditions, the annual payments administered by the chapter, the principal maturing to the eldest legitimate line upon the holder's sixty-second year. I told him about my wife, Eleanor, dead eleven years now, and about — and here I stumbled, because I had not said this aloud in a decade —

"About?" Underhill prompted. Not unkindly. Archivists, I suspect, are trained in prompting. Their whole profession is getting reluctant documents to speak.

"My daughter," I said.

The word sat between us like a coin dropped on stone.

"Maren," I said. "She'd be twenty-six now. She left after her mother's funeral. Or — no. That's the tidy version. The truth is I sent her away, effectively, by being the sort of man who cannot say the word *stay*. She wrote to me for two years. I answered maybe four times. Then she stopped writing, and I told myself that proved she was fine, because children who are drowning write, don't they? Don't they?" I heard my own voice climb and brought it down. "There was a falling-out over the estate. Over Eleanor's things. I was — precise. I am a precise man, Mr. Underhill. Precision is a virtue in accounts and a vice everywhere else."

Underhill said nothing. He had a way of saying nothing that felt like being read.

"The point," I said, "is that Maren is legally — faded. Erased isn't the word; nobody erased her. She just stopped appearing. No marriage record, no property, nothing filed anywhere under our name. And the trust—" I hesitated, because the next part sounded mad even inside my own head. "The trust's terms turn on the register. On who is *remembered*, legally speaking. Josiah was obsessed with lineage. If the direct line fails or is unrecorded, the principal reverts to the chapter's perpetual care. That's the phrase. *Perpetual care.* It sounds like gravestones, doesn't it? It means the cathedral keeps the money."

"And your birthday ends the reversion window."

"It ends everything. After the twenty-second of November, the money is mine outright, and no amount of paperwork can call it back. Which means that between now and then, I am worth considerably more dead than alive — but only if I die *unrecorded*. Without issue. Without a documented heir." I laughed, and it came out wrong, thin as old vellum. "Do you see why I came to an archivist? Everyone else investigates the man. You could investigate the *record*. Find out who's been touching it. Because somebody has been touching it, Underhill. Somebody opened a sealed ledger and wrote my death in it three weeks before the fact, and I want to know if that's a threat, a trap, or a rehearsal."

* * *

It was somewhere in the middle of all this — I was explaining about Gerald, about the second time I was followed, outside the bank in the rain — that the bells began.

St. Aldhelm's rings the quarter-hour. I knew this. Any visitor knows this. But when the first stroke came down through the vaulted ceiling above us, my whole body betrayed me. I flinched. Shoulders up, breath caught, the full coward's catalogue — and worse, I couldn't stop it happening again at the second stroke, or the third. Eight strokes for the hour. I counted every one of them with my nerves instead of my ears.

Underhill noticed. Of course he noticed. I had the impression the man noticed the grain of paper and the direction of dust motes; a grown man flinching at bells would have been legible to him from across a county.

He didn't comment. He simply waited until the last echo died, and then he said, mildly, "The bells were recast in 1861. Before that, the tenor cracked for nearly a decade. The chapter couldn't afford to replace it, so the quarter-hours rang flat. The sexton's diary complains about it constantly." A pause. "People forget the bells used to be the clock, the newspaper, and the fire alarm all at once. They've watched everything, these bells. Weddings, funerals, riots, one regicide, two fires, and — according to Cobb, our sexton — at least one midnight opening of the crypt that nobody will discuss."

"Why tell me that?"

"Because you flinched," he said, "and I wanted you to know I'd seen it, and that it doesn't change my answer. Fear isn't evidence against a client. Half the testimony in these archives was given by terrified people, and it's the terrified ones who tend to be telling the truth." He turned the photograph toward himself again, studying the impossible entry. "The composed ones are usually lying."

I found, to my considerable embarrassment, that my eyes had gone hot. I am sixty-one years old and I have not cried since Eleanor's funeral, and I did not cry then either, but it was a near thing, and over a sentence about bells.

"When did you last sleep properly?" Underhill asked.

"Define properly."

"That's answer enough." He rose, went to a shelf, and came back with a tin that had once held tobacco and now held biscuits of alarming age. He offered it. I took one, mostly to give my hands employment. "Here's what happens next. I examine the ledger. Properly — materials, hand, binding, everything. If that entry is a forgery, I'll find the seam. If it isn't a forgery—" he paused, biscuit halfway to nowhere, "—then the seam is elsewhere, and we look for whoever sewed it. Either way, Mr. Fenwick, you do not come back here. Not for three weeks. If someone is watching you, this building is the worst place you could stand still."

"They'd never act in a cathedral."

"No," he agreed pleasantly. "They'd act in a hotel corridor, or a car park, or a quiet lane. Cathedrals are where institutions keep their sins, not where they commit them. Go home. Lock your doors. Leave a forwarding address with no one, and contact me only by letter, posted from varying districts, because I assume you're capable of that level of theatre."

"I'm capable of considerably worse."

"I'm beginning to hope so." He walked me to the stair that led up out of the archive, and at the foot of it he stopped. "One question. The daughter. Maren. If she appeared tomorrow — if she were entered back into the record, legally remembered — what would happen to the trust?"

The question caught me off guard. I stood there with my hand on the cold iron rail and thought about a girl of nineteen standing in a train station with one suitcase, not looking back, because looking back was the one thing I had never taught her not to do.

"She'd be my heir," I said. "She'd be — everything, I suppose. Everything I arranged to lose by being proud."

Underhill nodded slowly, and there was something in his face I couldn't read — some private arithmetic running behind his eyes.

"Then," he said, "someone may already be asking the same question about her. Sleep well, Mr. Fenwick. And mind the bells. They remember more than we do."

I climbed the stair into the grey afternoon light of the nave, and behind me, below the floor, among the shelves, a man who lived with the dead took up my photograph of an unwritten murder and began, I would later learn, to hunt.

As for me — I stepped out past the lychgate, checked the street twice, and flinched, one last time, at the quarter-hour.
