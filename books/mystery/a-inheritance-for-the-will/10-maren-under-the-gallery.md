## 10. Maren Under the Gallery

There is a particular kind of invisibility that comes with wearing a volunteer's badge and carrying a feather duster. I have tested it extensively. I can dust the same brass lectern for forty minutes while two canons discuss parish finances three feet away, and neither will remember my face afterward. I once sneezed during a funeral and was blamed on a pigeon.

My name is Maren Fenwick, though no document in this cathedral — or in any registry, parish roll, school ledger, or probate file in the county — will admit to it. I have spent six years and most of a modest inheritance from my grandmother (the only relative who ever wrote my name down anywhere) proving that I exist. It is slow work. Erasure, it turns out, is very thorough when done by people with time, money, and a vested interest in your nonexistence.

I was eight when my mother died. I remember the funeral mostly through the smell of lilies and the sound of my father's shoes walking away across gravel — not away from the grave, exactly, but away from me, which amounted to the same thing. After that there were aunts, then foster placements, then a series of forms that asked, again and again, *next of kin*, and a series of answers that came back, again and again, as though I had been typed onto the wrong form entirely. *No record of a Maren Fenwick.* As if I were a misprint. As if someone at the printer's had caught me in time.

People assume erasure is dramatic. It isn't. It's clerical. It happens one omitted line at a time, in rooms you're not invited into, by hands that never tremble.

So I came to St. Aldhelm's. Not because I expected to find anything — I had learned not to expect things — but because if my name had been removed from somewhere, then somewhere was where the removal had happened, and removals leave marks. A page torn out leaves a stub. A line struck through leaves ink ghosts. Even a lie, written often enough, wears a groove into the record that an honest eye can feel with a fingertip.

I volunteered. They accepted me within the week, because cathedrals are always short of hands and never short of things to dust, and because I was, in the words of the verger, "a quiet girl who doesn't ask questions."

He meant it as praise. I took it as a job description.

* * *

The archive lives beneath the north transept gallery, behind a door that smells of cold stone and old glue. Vesper Underhill keeps it like a dragon keeps gold — except that dragons hoard out of greed, and Vesper hoards out of something closer to grief. I watched him for months before he ever spoke to me directly. He has a way of moving among the shelves as though apologizing to them. He touches the spines of ledgers the way other men touch relics. Once I saw him stand for a full five minutes before a burial register from 1811, not reading it, just looking, the way you'd look at a photograph of someone you'd loved and lost.

I understood him immediately. That was the problem. Understanding people is dangerous when you're trying to remain furniture.

"You've alphabetized the pamphlet case," he said to me one Tuesday, appearing at my elbow without sound. "The pamphlets were arranged by accession date."

"I know," I said. "It was bothering me."

Something happened at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile; more like a smile being considered by a committee. "It bothered me too," he said. "For eleven years. I didn't dare touch it."

"Why not?"

"Because Mr. Prowse arranged it that way in 1963, and Mr. Prowse is dead, and there's a certain loyalty owed to the dead." He looked at the case a moment longer. "Though perhaps," he added, almost to himself, "loyalty to the dead shouldn't extend to their filing systems."

That was the whole conversation. It lasted ninety seconds and changed everything, because it was the first time in six years that anyone in that building had treated my opinion about records as though it might be worth having.

After that, he started leaving me small tasks. Indexing. Collating. Checking a list of burials against a list of grave markers. Nothing that touched the crypt materials — those he kept locked in a cabinet whose key lived on his person at all times, and I mean *at all times*; I once saw him reach for it in his sleep. But enough to keep me under the gallery, near the records, near the place where names live or die.

And enough, eventually, to put his notes within my reach.

* * *

I want to be clear that I did not set out to spy on him. Spying implies intent, and what I had was more like gravity. His desk sits under the gallery overhang where the light is bad and the drafts collect, and he works in a hand that slants leftward and presses hard, and when he goes up to fetch a volume or answer the telephone, his current page stays open to the room like an unattended window.

On the fourth of March, the window was open.

He'd been working on something he hadn't told me about — I knew that much from the way he'd begun locking even his scratch paper in the drawer, and from the shadows under his eyes, which had deepened from *archivist* to *insomniac*. The page on his desk was covered in his slanting hand: dates, cross-references, a transcription of something in period script. And a name.

**Fenwick, Aldous.**

The world did not stop. I want to report that it stopped, that the dust hung motionless in the light, that some organ in my chest performed a dramatic solo. What actually happened was smaller and worse: nothing stopped. The radiator ticked. Somewhere above us the tenor bell practiced its quarter-chimes. And I stood holding a duster over a shelf of Victorian diocesan reports while my own surname sat on a dead man's desk in another man's handwriting, and I read.

*Aldous Fenwick. Client. Claims entry exists in crypt ledger dated [three weeks hence] recording his murder. Materials authenticated. See Harrow correspondence.*

Client. Murder. Three weeks hence.

I read it twice, three times, committing it to memory the way you commit anything when you cannot take it with you — the way, as a child, I had memorized the one photograph of my mother that survived, because photographs, like daughters, could be taken away.

Aldous Fenwick was alive. Aldous Fenwick had hired the archivist. Aldous Fenwick believed someone intended to kill him, and had gone to the one man in this city who dealt in proof.

And the crypt ledger — the sealed ledger, the one no one had opened since 1847, the one the chapter guarded like a reliquary — contained a record of a murder that had not yet happened.

Here is what six years of archival self-education gives you: not comfort. Pattern recognition. I had read enough forged charters and doctored rolls to know that documents do not lie casually. If that entry existed, someone had made it exist, recently, deliberately, with access to antique materials and antique skills. Someone inside. And they had chosen Aldous Fenwick as its victim-in-advance, which meant Fenwick mattered to them — mattered enough to kill on paper first, the way surveyors stake out ground before breaking earth.

Why would anyone bother to pre-record the murder of a man nobody had heard of?

Unless he wasn't nobody. Unless the name Fenwick meant something to the people who kept the records — the same people who had once, twenty-six years ago, made a small girl vanish from every register in the county.

I put the duster down. I picked it up again. I dusted the diocesan reports until my arm ached, because my hands needed a task that wasn't shaking.

* * *

That night I went back to my flat and pulled out the box.

Everyone who is investigating their own disappearance should keep a box. Mine is a biscuit tin, which I acknowledge is absurd, but absurdity is a kind of armor. Inside: my mother's death certificate, the last document bearing my name in connection with hers. My grandmother's letters. A copy of the trust instrument my grandmother's solicitor had shown me once, briefly, before advising me — kindly, gently, in the tone one uses with the mad — that I should let it go.

I had not let it go. I am constitutionally incapable of letting things go. It is why I am good with records and bad at parties.

The trust. The Fenwick Family Trust, established 1847 — the same year, I now remembered with a cold sliding feeling, that the crypt had been sealed. I had noticed the coincidence years ago and filed it under *coincidence*, because the alternative was unbearable. But coincidence is just a pattern you haven't earned yet.

Established 1847. Sealed 1847. And now a future-dated murder entry in the crypt ledger, naming a Fenwick, surfacing precisely when — I did the arithmetic I had done a hundred times, and did it again anyway — precisely when the trust's principal was due to mature and pass to whoever the instruments recognized as heir.

The instruments recognized no one. The instruments had been amended, restated, and quietly re-amended across four generations, each version thinner on Fenwicks and heavier on trustees, until the family it was built for had been edited out the way I had been edited out — one omitted line at a time, in rooms we weren't invited into.

They had practiced on me. Or rather, they had practiced on my mother, and I was what the practice left behind.

I sat on the floor of my flat with the biscuit tin open and the radiator ticking, and I thought about Vesper Underhill's slanting handwriting, and the phrase *see Harrow correspondence*, and Canon Precentor Harrow's voice, which I knew well from volunteering at services — a beautiful voice, mellow and reasonable, the voice of a man who has never once raised it because he has never once needed to. A voice that said *the dead rest in peace* every Sunday to a congregation that believed him.

I thought: he knows I'm here. Not me specifically. But a Fenwick-shaped absence, somewhere in his paperwork, and a living Fenwick asking questions, and an archivist underneath his cathedral pulling threads. Three weeks. Whatever was staked out was going to be broken ground within three weeks, and the surveyors' marks were already in the ledger.

Which meant I had a choice, and the choice was this: stay furniture, or become evidence.

* * *

The trouble with becoming evidence is that evidence gets handled roughly. I had watched what happened to people who asked questions in that building. Not Vesper — not yet — but others. A researcher from the university, two years ago, who had wanted to see the crypt registers for a thesis on Victorian burial practice. Granted access for a fortnight, then denied it, then granted it again with a chapter steward sitting beside him taking notes, until he gave up and wrote his thesis on something else. A parish secretary who mentioned, at tea, that she thought the memorial plaques in the south aisle didn't match the burial rolls. She resigned within the month. Nobody asked her to. She just said the atmosphere had changed, and atmospheres in that building change very efficiently.

So I did what I had trained myself to do for six years. I waited. I watched. I became more furniture than ever — so punctual, so unremarkable, so relentlessly useful that within a week Vesper had given me a key to the pamphlet room and stopped noticing when I arrived or left.

And I began, carefully, in the evenings, using the microfilm reader at the public library where no one knew my face, to build the thing I had been circling for six years but had never had a reason to finish: a complete reconstruction of the Fenwick Trust's documentary history. Every amendment. Every trustee appointment. Every signature.

Because here is what I knew that Vesper didn't, and what Fenwick — wherever he was hiding, flinching at bells — apparently didn't either: the murder entry wasn't the beginning of the forgery. It was the latest one. The oldest forgeries in that ledger weren't in the ledger at all. They were in the trust deeds, and they were a hundred and seventy-eight years old, and they had been renewed, lovingly, by every generation of chapter leadership since, the way you renew a lease.

Someone had finally gotten sloppy. Or someone had finally gotten scared. Either way, they had reached for the tool their predecessors had always used — the record itself — and used it carelessly, dating a murder that hadn't happened, and thereby handing the whole rotten edifice a loose thread.

You do not pre-write a murder unless you intend the record to be believed afterward. And a record is only believed if the archivist who authenticates it is credible, or discredited, or dead.

I thought about Vesper's shadows under his eyes, and the phrase *see Harrow correspondence*, and I felt something I hadn't felt in six years, something I distrusted instantly and completely:

Hope. For other people, admittedly, which is the safest kind. But hope behaves like water; it finds the cracks in you whether you invite it or not.

* * *

On the eleventh of March, Vesper asked me to stay late.

"Miss—" he began, and stopped, and I realized with a small internal jolt that in eighteen months he had never once used my name. I had checked the volunteer roster once, early on, out of habit: I was listed as *M. Corbett*, the surname of my last foster placement, the one identity the state had grudgingly issued me. Corbett. A borrowed coat I'd worn so long I'd stopped noticing the fit.

"Maren," I supplied. "Maren Corbett."

"Maren." He tried it out the way he'd try an unfamiliar cataloguing system. "I need a second pair of eyes on some marginalia. Burial entries, 1790s forward. There's a pattern in the annotations and I can't tell whether I'm seeing cipher or wishful thinking."

Cipher. My heart performed a small, undignified maneuver. "Burial entries," I repeated, keeping my voice at furniture pitch.

"The dead keep better books than the living," he said, and there was something underneath the wryness, some tiredness with a blade in it. "Or someone has been keeping books *on* the dead. Come and look."

He spread the pages under the lamp — photostats, careful ones, of burial registers spanning two centuries — and showed me the marginalia: little flourishes, dates rendered oddly, numbers tucked into descenders and ascenders like birds roosting in the letterforms. And as he talked me through it — keyed to dates of death, he thought, a running total, payments maybe — I nodded and asked intelligent questions and gave him nothing of myself.

But all the while, underneath the scholarly excitement, a colder current ran: *he's close. He's decoding the money. And the money leads to the trust, and the trust leads to 1847, and 1847 leads to the crypt, and the crypt leads to whatever they've decided to do to Aldous Fenwick in three weeks — and possibly, after that, to the quiet man with the tired eyes who is showing me his life's work under a lamp.*

Because I had read enough of his notes by now, in glimpses, in open windows, to know how these things go. The outsider who pulls the thread always ends up in the ledger himself. It was practically a rule of the collection.

"Your hand is steady," Vesper said suddenly, watching me copy a sequence of figures. "Most volunteers press too hard or drift. You write like you've done this before."

"I read a lot," I said, which was true, and insufficient, and he seemed to accept it the way he accepted most things — filing it, I suspected, in whatever vast interior catalogue he maintained, in a drawer marked *later*.

Later. Everything in my life had been later. Later I would tell him. Later, when I knew whom to trust. Later, when telling wouldn't make me a liability he'd be wise to turn away — because that was the real fear, the one under all the others. Not that he'd betray me. That he'd protect me. That he'd look at the daughter of Aldous Fenwick, the erased girl, the loose end, and decide the kindest thing was to send her far from the archive before the three weeks ran out.

And the terrible, humiliating truth — the one I dusted shelves rather than examine — was that I didn't want to be sent away. Six years I had worked in that building, invisible, patient, telling myself I wanted only the truth. But the truth was that I wanted the lamplight too. I wanted the drawer marked *later* to have my name in it. I wanted, with an ache that embarrassed me, to belong to the one place that had helped erase me — to fix it from inside, to be entered in its books, in a good hand, in full ink.

Under the gallery, among the shelved dead and the murmuring living, I copied Vesper Underhill's cipher figures into my notebook in my steadiest hand, and I did not tell him who I was.

Three weeks, his notes said. Two weeks and five days, now.

Somewhere above us, the quarter-chimes rang, and I counted them all the way to twelve, and made myself a promise in the margin of my own life, in pencil, where promises can still be revised: *before they write his ending, I will show them mine was never erased — only waiting to be read.*
