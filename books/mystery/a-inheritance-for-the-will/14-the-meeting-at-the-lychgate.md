## 14. The Meeting at the Lychgate

The note came to my hotel on Wednesday, folded inside a copy of the *Diocesan Gazette* that I had never subscribed to and would not have read if I had. Someone at the front desk said a boy had left it. Someone always says a boy left it; boys are the ghosts of every conspiracy, doing the errands no adult will admit to.

The note itself was brief, and written in a hand so careful it might have been printed: *St. Aldhelm's lychgate, Friday, half past eleven at night. Come alone. Come with nothing in your hands. I can show you who is writing the ledger.*

I sat with it for the better part of an hour, turning it over as though the paper itself might confess its origins. It was good paper — cathedral stock, I'd have sworn to it, the same heavy cream vellum the chapter used for its correspondence. That should have comforted me. Instead it did the opposite. Whoever wrote it had access to the chapter's stationery cupboard, which meant whoever wrote it was either of the chapter or robbing it, and I could not decide which possibility frightened me more.

I thought about taking it to Vesper Underhill. I want that recorded honestly, because in the days that followed I would tell myself I never trusted him at all, and that would be a lie. I did trust him — cautiously, grudgingly, the way one trusts a surgeon whose fees are ruinous but whose hands are steady. He was the only man in this city who seemed to care whether the records told the truth, and I had watched him worry at that impossible ledger entry like a dog worrying a bone it cannot crack but will not surrender.

But there were reasons I didn't go to him. The first was that he had looked at me, more than once, with an expression I couldn't parse — a kind of scholarly pity, as though I were a document he'd already catalogued and filed under *doomed*. The second was that someone had searched my room while I was at dinner, and had taken nothing but left everything slightly wrong: the Bible shelved a quarter-inch proud of its neighbours, the wardrobe door hanging by a breath instead of closed. A professional's search. And Vesper was the only person alive who knew both what I carried and where I slept.

The third reason was simpler and worse. The note promised proof. Proof is the only currency I have ever valued, and I am weak before it the way other men are weak before drink.

So I went.

* * *

The lychgate of St. Aldhelm's stands at the head of the old burial path, a roofed timber porch over the gate itself, all black oak and lichen-eaten slate, older than most of the graves it guards. By day it is picturesque. By night, with the cathedral rising behind it like a ship of black stone becalmed, it is something else entirely — a threshold, the place where the living are formally handed over to the dead. I have since learned that corpses were once rested beneath its roof while the priest went ahead to consecrate the ground. They wait for you there. That is the whole architecture of the thing. Waiting.

I arrived early, because I am a coward, and cowards arrive early enough to run. The streets around the close were empty in that particular way city streets are empty at midnight — not deserted but *held*, as though the buildings were inhaling. A thin rain had begun, the kind that doesn't fall so much as hang in the air and let you walk through it. My breath made small ghosts. Somewhere across the close, a window burned amber in the precentor's lodging, then went dark, as if someone had been watching and decided I'd seen enough.

I stood under the lychgate roof out of the wet and took inventory of myself like a man checking his pockets before a customs inspection. Fifty-eight years old. No weapon — I had never held one and wouldn't have known which end argued. In my breast pocket, the copied page of cipher I had stolen from Vesper's desk, which I now suspected was the true reason for all of this. If someone wanted that page badly enough to stage a murder in a two-hundred-year-old ledger, then the page was worth more than my life, and everyone involved understood that except, until recently, me.

Half past eleven came. The cathedral clock spoke once for each half-hour, and the sound rolled over the close like a stone dropped down a well. I flinched. I always flinch. Bells have been the punctuation of my life's worst moments — my wife's funeral, the reading of the will that cut Maren out with four lines of legal prose, the day the solicitors explained the trust's terms to me in voices like apologising machines. When the last stroke faded, a figure detached itself from the shadow of the buttress nearest the north porch and came toward me at an unhurried walk.

He was muffled to the eyes in a scarf, hat brim low, gloved — dressed, in short, exactly as an informant in a penny dreadful dresses, which ought to have warned me. Nobody genuinely afraid dresses like fear. Fear wears ordinary clothes and hopes not to be noticed. This man wanted to be noticed; he wanted me to remember a costume rather than a face.

"You came alone," he said. His voice was pitched low and even, unremarkable, the voice of a thousand men. "Good."

"Who are you?" I asked. "And how do I know you're not the one writing the ledger?"

A sound that might have been laughter, muffled by wool. "You don't. That's rather the point of meeting in a churchyard, Mr. Fenwick. Dead men keep few secrets, and the dead here are very well organised." He stopped just beyond arm's reach, and I noted distantly that he had positioned himself between me and the open street. "You want to know who has been altering the crypt register. I want to know what the archivist gave you. Show me the page."

"The note said come with nothing in your hands."

"The note said come with nothing *visible*. You've been carrying it against your heart like a love letter. Did you think a man who can forge ink onto 1840s vellum couldn't follow a nervous guest through his own hotel lobby?"

My hand went to my breast pocket without my permission. I cursed it silently. "Then you know what I carry. So take it and leave me be."

"Oh, I intend to take it." The evenness of his voice did not change at all, and that was the moment the cold walked up my spine and settled between my shoulders. "The manner is negotiable. You can give it to me, walk back to your hotel, and spend the rest of your life wondering. Or we can do this the difficult way, and you'll spend considerably less of it."

I did what any sensible man does when the trap closes: I talked. "Underhill decoded half of it already. Killing me for the page gets you nothing — he'll reconstruct it from memory. He's the best archivist in this diocese. Possibly the country."

"I know," said the figure, and there was something almost tender in it, which was somehow worse than menace. "That's why he's next on the list after you. But you knew that too, didn't you? You've seen the second entry." A pause, delicately cruel. "Initials V.U. He showed you. Of course he showed you — a man like that can't resist showing his work. Which means, Mr. Fenwick, that the archivist knows exactly when you're scheduled to die, has known for weeks, and has told you anyway while continuing to poke at the very documents that will convict him. Ask yourself why a murderer would tolerate a detective."

"I don't believe—"

"He keeps you close because you're the loose end. He forged the entry himself, Fenwick. Who else has the keys to the archive? Who else reads dead hands for a living? Who else could make antique ink sing on antique vellum?" The voice softened further, became confiding, almost kind. "You hired a man who lives among the dead because you thought the dead were neutral. But he *is* the dead, and they vote with him."

I would like to say I saw through it. I would like to report that some iron core of judgement held firm while the lie wrapped itself around me. What actually happened was smaller and sadder: I thought of Vesper's pitying looks, of his office ransacked by persons unknown yet somehow untouched in its most valuable drawer, of the way he had accepted triple payment without blinking, and the story assembled itself in my frightened mind with the terrible efficiency of a legal argument. Every fact fit. That is the horror of a good lie — it doesn't contradict the evidence; it organises it.

"Give me the page," the figure said again, and stepped closer, and I saw the lamplight from the far gate catch something in his right hand that was not a document.

I ran.

* * *

I am fifty-eight and I had not run since the Armistice celebrations, and my body informed me of this immediately and at length. I went not toward the street — he stood between me and the street — but along the burial path into the older part of the churchyard, where the graves crowd together and lean like tired congregations, where the ground itself is uneven and hostile to pursuit. Behind me his footsteps came quick and light, unhurried still, the footsteps of a man who knows the geography better than his prey.

A hand caught my coat collar. I twisted, fell against a chest tomb whose occupant, according to the worn inscription under my palm, had died in 1802 and presumably bore me no ill will, and felt a line of fire open across my upper arm as the blade — I understood it was a blade now, intimately, educationally — dragged through wool and skin. I heard myself make a noise I had never made before, high and undignified, and I struck out blindly with my elbow and connected with something soft — a face, a scarf, a grunt.

He staggered. I scrambled up and ran again, blind now, rain in my eyes, blood sliding warm and ridiculous down inside my sleeve. I crashed through a yew branch that whipped me across the cheek, vaulted — the word is generous; I fell over — a low wall into the lane beyond, and ran with no plan except *away*, which is the oldest plan and the worst.

I ran perhaps three streets before my legs staged their mutiny. I stopped in the doorway of a shuttered chandlery, gasping, clutching my arm, listening to a close that had gone utterly silent behind me. No pursuit. There didn't need to be. He had what he wanted — or believed he did, for in my fall against the tomb I had felt the copied page slip from my breast pocket, and I had made the split-second calculation of a desperate man and let it go. Let him have it. Let him read it and believe Vesper's decoding was all there was.

Except that wasn't why I let it go, and I have never been able to lie to myself successfully for longer than a minute. I let it go because I needed both hands to save my life, and because some animal arithmetic decided the page was worth less than the blood leaving me.

I bound the arm with my scarf, badly, in the chandlery doorway, and the rain kept falling through the gaslight, and I began — as the shock receded and the colder machinery of the mind resumed — to think.

Whoever he was, he had known things only three people could know: that I carried the page, that Vesper had shown me the second entry, that the archive held the key to everything. One of those three people had set me in that churchyard like a baited trap. And the voice — God help me, the voice had been *familiar*, smoothed and lowered though it was. Not Vesper's. Vesper's voice has a dryness to it, a paper-and-dust timbre I would know anywhere. This was another voice, a softer one, a voice used to being believed. A voice from the chapter, perhaps. Or a voice trained beside it.

But familiarity is not exoneration. Because here is the arithmetic that finally broke me, standing bleeding in a doorway while the cathedral loomed black against the clouds: Vesper had proposed this meeting's *possibility* days ago — "if anyone contacts you, tell me" — and I had agreed, and I had told him nothing, and yet the trap had been laid with knowledge only my side of that bargain contained. Unless he had lied about wanting to be told. Unless the whole arrangement — the hire, the hunt, the gentle unfolding of secrets — was itself the long con, designed to flush me into a churchyard at midnight and put a knife in me on schedule, so that the ledger's prophecy would come true and its author would walk away clean, triple fee refunded to an estate with no heir to contest it.

No heir. Except the one written out. Except—

I stopped that thought where it stood. I was not ready for it. Some part of me understood, even then, that if I followed that thread — *who benefits, who was erased, who has been searching* — I would have to look at things about myself that the knife had merely introduced.

What I felt instead, cleanly and completely, was betrayal. It is a remarkable substance, betrayal; it fills every cavity, sets broken reasoning like cement. Vesper Underhill had been the one man I had chosen to trust, and every road now led back to his desk, his keys, his patient scholar's hands. He had found me wounded and alone in the world and offered to help, and I had paid him triple, and the payment had purchased my ambush to the inch.

I straightened up. The rain thinned. Across the rooftops, the cathedral clock began to strike the three-quarters, twelve soft strokes counting down the hour I was meant to die in, and I flinched at every one of them, and then, deliberately, painfully, I stopped flinching and listened to the bells all the way through.

When they finished, I turned my collar up and started walking — not to my hotel, which was surely watched, but toward the police station on Cordwainer Street, where a bleeding man with a coherent story and a famous name can accomplish in an hour what three weeks of archives could not.

Let them seal the archive. Let them lock the crypt. Let the archivist sit among his precious dead and learn what it costs to deal with the living.

By morning, Vesper Underhill would be answering questions he had no answers for. And I would make certain of it.
