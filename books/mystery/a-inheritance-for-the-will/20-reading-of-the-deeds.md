## 20. Reading of the Deeds

The solicitors arrived at half past nine on a Tuesday, which felt right. Nothing about my father had ever happened at a dramatic hour. His betrayals, his disappearances, his one great act of cowardice — all of it conducted in office hours, with tea.

I watched them from the gallery above the chapter house, where Vesper had stationed me with instructions to say nothing and remember everything. *You're an archivist now,* he'd said. *Archivists witness.* He said it as though it were a small thing, a technicality of the trade. He had no idea what witnessing cost me, or how long I'd practiced it in secret — years of standing in this cathedral's shadows, filing hymnals and dusting brass, watching men who didn't know my name decide things about people who did not exist. People like me.

The chapter house smelled of beeswax and old argument. Canon Harrow's chair stood empty — police tape has a way of emptying chairs — and his absence changed the room's gravity. Men who had spent decades deferring to him sat slightly straighter, like furniture finally allowed to admit it was uncomfortable.

Mr. Quill, the senior solicitor, unrolled the founding deeds across the table with the tenderness of a man handling a sleeping animal. They had been read aloud once already, that night in the crypt, by torchlight and shouting — but torchlight is not law. Law required daylight, and witnesses, and the slow terrible dignity of procedure.

"By virtue of the instruments before us," Quill intoned, "the Fenwick Trust of 1847 is restored to its original terms and beneficiaries."

Original terms. I mouthed the words silently. Original beneficiaries. Somewhere under those phrases was a girl of nine whose mother had died and whose father had signed her out of existence with a solicitor's pen and a cheque. The trust had hinged on who was *legally remembered* — that was the phrase Vesper used, tapping the vellum, his eyes bright with the particular joy of a man who has caught history in a lie. And for thirty-one years, the answer had been: no one. A beneficiary erased is a beneficiary dissolved, and a dissolved trust flows to its custodians. The cathedral chapter had been drinking from my inheritance for three decades, one discreet sip at a time, and calling it stewardship.

I thought I would feel triumphant. I had imagined this moment so many times, in so many versions — always ending with me saying something devastating, something my mother would have been proud of.

What I actually felt was tired, and cold in my hands, and aware that Aldous Fenwick was sitting four seats away from me, looking smaller than any man I'd ever seen.

* * *

They made him speak. Of course they did; the restoration required the trustee's formal assent, and the trustee was my father, restored to the role by the very documents that had convicted his partners in theft. The deeds were clear: the trust belonged to the Fenwick bloodline. The chapter had merely been its thief. Harrow and Pell had stolen from a family that included, technically, the man who had helped them do it — because thirty-one years ago, a grieving, greedy, frightened Aldous Fenwick had agreed to let his daughter vanish from the record in exchange for a clean inheritance and no inconvenient child.

He stood. He had shaved, I noticed. Someone had told him this was an occasion.

"The trust," he began, and stopped, and started again. "The trust returns to its lawful purpose. I—" His eyes came up, found mine in the gallery, and I watched him lose his prepared remarks entirely. "I have a statement for the record."

Quill nodded. Fenwick took out a folded paper, looked at it, and put it away.

"No statement," he said. "Just — let it be entered that I assent. Fully. Without reservation." A pause. "And that I withdraw the complaint against Mr. Underhill. All of it. It was made under—" he glanced at the police liaison at the end of the table, "—under circumstances I regret."

Under circumstances where two men with a knife and a lawyer's smile had explained to him exactly how useful a live accuser could be. I understood it. That was the worst part. I had spent years understanding my father, building the whole architecture of his excuses myself, because someone had to live in that house of reasoning and it might as well be the daughter he'd erased. He had been afraid. Fear makes men sign things. My mother's death certificate had taught me that much.

Understanding is not forgiveness. I want that entered into the record too.

* * *

Afterward, we went down to the archive. Not the chapter house, not the solicitors' chambers — the archive, because Vesper said records belong where records live, and because I think he wanted, for once, to do a thing in the right room.

The afternoon light came through the high slit windows in dusty bars. Vesper had cleared a space on his worktable, laid out a fresh register — the new ledger, the one begun after everything, its first pages still smelling of sizing and intent — and beside it, the old burial registers, the cipher, the confession of the dying verger, all of it evidence now rather than secret, catalogued and boxed and labeled in his small precise hand.

"You understand what I'm about to do," he said. It wasn't quite a question. He was nervous. I had never seen Vesper Underhill nervous over paper, and seeing it now did something to my chest I wasn't ready to name.

"Tell me anyway."

"In thirty-one years of keeping this archive, I have entered ten thousand names." He uncapped the pen. "Every one of them dead. That's the job — you receive them, you fix them in place, you file them where they can't hurt anyone or be hurt. It's a comfortable profession for a man who finds the living difficult." He looked at me over the register. "I have never once added a name. Only received them."

"And mine?"

"Yours," he said, "I'm adding. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole point of the trade, though it took a murdered trust and a forged ledger to teach me." He smoothed the page. "A record isn't a cage, Maren. It's a door. Most people only ever get carried through it one way. You get to walk through it yourself."

He wrote. I watched his hand move — that careful, unhurried script, each letter placed like a stone in a wall meant to last centuries.

*Maren Elisabeth Fenwick. Daughter of Aldous James Fenwick and Celia Anne Fenwick. Born 14 March, St. Aldhelm's parish. Restored to the record this day by testimony, deed, and the truth, which keeps poor hours but arrives.*

I laughed at the last line, unexpectedly, wetly. "You can't write that in a legal register."

"It's my register," Vesper said. "I've decided the archive is permitted opinions now. One per generation, sparingly used." He blew gently on the ink. "There. Thirty-one years late by the clock. Exactly on time by every measure that matters."

My name. In ink. In a book that would outlive everyone in the room. I had spent nine years as a volunteer in this cathedral, dusting the memorials of strangers, and not one stone, not one page, not one soul had known I belonged here. Now the building itself, through its most stubborn servant, had said otherwise.

I didn't cry properly until later. At the table, I only put my hand flat on the page, next to the wet ink, the way you'd touch a wound to confirm it's real.

* * *

Fenwick came at five, when the light was going amber and Vesper had tactfully withdrawn to the far stacks with the pretense of reshelving — a man who knew exactly how loud his absence could be.

My father stood in the doorway of the archive like a man at a graveside who isn't sure he's welcome. He had aged a decade in three weeks. The sharpness I remembered from childhood — the charm deployed like currency — was gone, spent, and what remained was just a tired man in a good coat.

"Maren."

"That's my name," I said. "It's in the book now. You're welcome to look."

He flinched. Good, some petty thing in me said. Then, immediately, tiredly: oh, stop. This is the trouble with inheritance — you get the debts along with the deeds.

"I won't ask you to forgive me." He said it fast, like ripping off plaster. "I've rehearsed asking, about forty times, and every version sounded like I was asking you to make it easier for me. So I won't. I'll just say what's true." He swallowed. "Your mother died in March. The solicitor came in April. He said the trust would fracture if there was a minor heir, said the chapter would take everything, said the cleanest path was—" he gestured vaguely, a man indicating an erasure, "—and I let him draw the line through you because I was drowning and he offered me air. That's the whole story. No one held a knife to my throat. I signed because signing was easier than fighting."

"I know," I said. "I reconstructed it years ago. I've read the correspondence, Father. I know the whole story better than you remember living it."

That landed. I saw it land.

"Of course you do," he said quietly. "You're her daughter. And apparently you're Underhill's apprentice now, God help the archive." He almost smiled. It didn't survive. "I'm not going to contest anything. The trust is yours — yours first, mine second, if I'm in it at all. I've instructed the solicitors. And I'm moving to Lyme. There's a house. I'll leave an address with—" he looked around the archive, helpless, "—with whoever keeps such things."

Vesper, from the stacks, without turning: "I keep such things."

"Then leave it with him," my father said, and then, to me, standing there with his hat in his hands like a penitent in a painting: "I don't expect an answer. I only wanted to ask in person, once, like a man instead of a signature. Will you ever—" He couldn't finish it. *Will you ever forgive me* died somewhere behind his teeth, and maybe that was right. Some questions should die there.

I looked at him for a long time. I looked at the hands that had signed me out of the world, holding that ridiculous hat. I looked at the door behind him, open, the way doors should be.

"I neither grant it nor refuse it," I said finally. "Not today. Maybe the record will sort it out — it seems to be the family trade now. Ask me again in a year. Leave the address."

He nodded, and I saw that this — the not-refusal, the suspended sentence of it — was more mercy than he'd expected and less than he'd hoped, and that both of those things were exactly true, and that imperfect was the only temperature this reconciliation would ever hold. He put out his hand. After a moment, I shook it. His grip was careful, like he was afraid I'd break, or he would.

"Goodbye, Maren."

"Goodbye, Aldous."

Using his Christian name was a small cruelty and a small freedom, and I took both.

* * *

He left. The cathedral bells rang six, and for the first time in my life I heard them without flinching — they weren't counting down anything anymore, just telling the hour to anyone who cared.

Vesper emerged from the stacks carrying two cups of tea and the expression of a man who has been listening at a distance precisely calibrated to respect grief while missing nothing.

"Well?" he said.

"He gets an address in the file. Cross-referenced. Annual review pending."

"A fair ruling." He handed me tea and nodded at the new ledger, lying open on the table, my name drying in it. "You know the strangest thing? Ten thousand entries, and that one line took more out of me than all of them together. Adding is harder than receiving. Receiving, you're just the riverbank. Adding—" he shook his head, "—adding means you decided someone belongs."

"Do I?"

He looked at me over his cup, and behind the wry archivist's mask there was something older and kinder, the thing he kept buried under cataloguing systems and dry remarks, the thing that had taken in a stranger's impossible request three weeks ago because loneliness recognizes loneliness.

"The record says so," he said. "And I wrote the record. I stand by my sources."

I turned back to the ledger — and stopped.

Because there, on the following page, in Vesper's hand but not Vesper's usual ink, was a heading I hadn't seen him write. Tomorrow's date. And beneath it, nothing. A blank entry, waiting.

"What's this?" I asked.

Vesper set down his cup. "Ah," he said. "That. I suppose you were going to find it eventually. We need to talk about what comes next — because according to the solicitors' final inventory, there's one document from the crypt still unaccounted for. And Cobb swears it wasn't there when he gave us the key."
