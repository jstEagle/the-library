## 8. The False Detail Bites

The letter came on a Thursday, which felt deliberate. Everything about the confessor felt deliberate now—the cream paper, the fountain-pen ink gone brown at the edges of each stroke, the way the envelope bore no postmark, only his name in that careful, schoolroom copperplate. It had been left. Not posted. *Left*, which meant someone had stood on the pavement outside Kavanagh's Books and slid it through the slot while Xanthe slept three metres away above the shop.

He found it at six-forty in the morning, face-down on the doormat beside a pizza menu and a notice from the council about recycling. He knew what it was before he touched it. His hands knew. They had developed a tremor over the past week that he chose to interpret as too much coffee.

Priya wasn't due until ten. The shop was dark and smelled of dust and old glue, and Xanthe sat down behind the counter with the letter unopened in front of him for a full four minutes before he broke the seal.

*Mr. Kavanagh.*

*I have been thinking about what you will be thinking about, and I want to help. You are a careful man. I have read enough of you to know that. So let me be careful too.*

*You asked yourself whether the man who killed your mother wore gloves. He did not. This is one of the things the file gets wrong, or rather, one of the things the file was made to get wrong. There were no gloves because there was no need for them. He was never going to be suspected. That is what protection looks like—not the absence of evidence, but the certainty that no one will ever look for it.*

*You will also remember the coat. In your statement you said it was long and dark. You were nine years old and it was raining and you were hiding behind the dinghy rack, so forgive me when I say that your memory has been edited by thirty years of telling yourself stories. The coat was not long. It was a short waxed jacket, green, the kind every man in Saltmere owned, which was precisely the point. Your mother's killer dressed like everyone else because everyone else would swear they saw nothing unusual.*

*One more thing, since you are being brave enough to look: the tide. The file says she went into the water at high tide. She did not. She died two hours before it, on the ebb, which is why the boat moved and why the rope did what it did. Check the harbourmaster's log from the eleventh of October 1994 if you doubt me. But you won't find it. That page is gone too.*

*Yours in truth,
A friend to the case*

Xanthe read it twice. Then a third time, standing very still in the middle of his own shop with rain starting against the window like something trying to get in.

The coat. He had planted the coat.

Three weeks ago—no, twelve days—he had written to the confessor himself, the first and only time he had broken his own rule about answering. A single paragraph, typed, posted from a town forty miles inland where nobody knew him. He had described the night as he remembered it, faithfully in almost every particular, except one. He had said the man wore a long dark coat, because the man in his memory had worn no coat at all, or none he could see, and he wanted to know what the writer would do with an error.

Now the writer had corrected him. Gently. Almost kindly. *Your memory has been edited by thirty years of telling yourself stories.* And offered in its place a detail so specific, so confident—a short green waxed jacket—that it could only come from one of two places: the truth, or the file.

And there was the trap inside the gift, the thing that made Xanthe sit down slowly on the stool behind the till and put both hands flat on the counter to stop them shaking.

Because he had checked. After the first letters, after the missing pages, after the realisation that someone had been inside the archive recently, he had done the one thing a careful man does: he had gone back to the county records office under a false name and requested the 1994 file again, and this time he had photographed every page they still held, including the pages describing the scene. The file said nothing about a coat. Nothing about gloves either. The scene description ran to four paragraphs and mentioned the rope, the boat, the water in her lungs, and stopped. Whoever wrote the scene report had been either incompetent or obedient, and Xanthe now suspected both.

So where had the green waxed jacket come from?

Either the confessor had been there—and if he had been there, why correct a detail instead of confirming it?—or the confessor had access to something beyond the official file. A private copy. A second record. The kind of thing a policeman might keep.

Xanthe got up, made tea he didn't drink, and opened the notebook he had started keeping in the safe beneath the till, the one Priya didn't know about. He wrote:

*1. Confessor knows the file better than the file knows itself.*
*2. Confessor corrects errors — but only errors I introduce. Never volunteers anything checkable.*
*3. Except the tide. The tide is checkable. Or was.*
*4. Therefore: the corrections are not for my benefit. They are for theirs. They are demonstrating ownership.*

He stared at point four for a long time. Ownership. That was the word that had been nagging at him since Saltmere, since Edith Vane's careful non-answers, since the whole village smiled at him with its mouth while its eyes stayed shut. Someone didn't just know what happened to Maren Kavanagh. Someone *owned* what happened to her. Owned the narrative of it, the file of it, the silence around it, the way you might own a house you never lived in but kept locked and heated and insured against prying eyes.

And now someone was walking him through it, room by room, correcting his furniture.

* * *

Priya arrived at ten and knew immediately, the way she always knew. She didn't ask. She made herself a coffee, opened the till, counted the float twice even though nothing had sold overnight, and then said, without looking up: "Another one?"

"Another one."

"Bad one?"

"Different one." He weighed how much to give her. He had been rationing her for weeks—fragments, mostly, the shape of the case without its teeth—but the arithmetic had changed. If the confessor was watching the shop, and the letter on the mat said he was, then Priya was already inside the game whether he liked it or not. "They're correcting me," he said. "I fed them a lie and they've handed it back polished."

Priya set down the coffee. "That's good, isn't it? That's what you wanted."

"It's what I wanted if all I wanted was to know they're reading what I send. But think about what the correction actually says." He tapped the letter. "A short green waxed jacket. Not 'a jacket.' Not 'a coat, maybe.' A specific garment, described with the confidence of someone who's seen it. And here's the problem, Priya: that detail isn't in the file. I've been through every surviving page twice. So either they were standing in the rain in October 1994, or they're working from a document that isn't the official file."

"A second file."

"The kind a senior investigating officer keeps in a locked drawer at home. The kind that exists precisely because the official version was wrong."

Priya was quiet for a moment. Then: "Who ran the case? In ninety-four."

"Superintendent Gerald Voss." The name came out of Xanthe with surprising ease, as if it had been waiting thirty years for the question. "Retired the year after. Moved away. Died—" he paused, dredging it up, "—died in 2019, I think. There was an obituary. Full honours, forty years' service, pillar of something or other."

"And who inherits a dead policeman's locked drawers?"

Xanthe looked at her. Priya looked back, level, unblinking, the way she did when she'd followed a thought further than she meant to say out loud.

"Family," he said.

"So we're looking for Voss's family. Someone with the house, the papers, the access." She frowned. "But wait. You said the archive was accessed recently. Physically. Missing pages. Would a person with their own private copy bother breaking into the county records office?"

"To correct it," Xanthe said, and heard the answer land in his own chest like a dropped coin. "To make sure the two versions matched. Or to remove things from the official record that contradicted the private one. Think about it—if you're curating a story, you don't just guard your manuscript. You edit the library."

The bell over the door jangled and a customer came in wanting a book on coastal birds, and Xanthe sold it to him with the part of his brain that had learned, long ago, to keep working while the rest of him was elsewhere. When the door shut he turned the CLOSED sign anyway, though it was barely eleven, and spread the notebook open between them on the counter.

"Here's what we can actually hold onto," he said. "The confessor has archive access—recent, physical, or through someone who has it. The confessor has or had access to Voss's private papers, or knows who does. The confessor writes like an educated man in his sixties. Copperplate hand, formal diction, phrases like 'yours in truth,' which no one under fifty produces unironically. And the confessor is local, or was. The letters arrive by hand. The knowledge of the boathouse, the tides, the harbourmaster's log—that's Saltmere knowledge."

"So: a man, sixties, Saltmere, connected to Voss or the archive." Priya chewed the end of her pen. "That's still a village worth of people."

"Then let's shrink it." He pulled the photograph he'd taken of the surviving witness index toward him. "Every statement in the original investigation was logged by the same officer—DS Raymond Ellery, Voss's number two. Every page that's missing from the file is a page Ellery logged. My statement. The harbourmaster's log. The pathologist's addendum. All of it passed through one pair of hands before it reached the file, and all of it vanished from those same hands' section."

"You think Ellery took them?"

"I think Ellery either took them or watched whoever did. And here's the part I keep circling." Xanthe turned the photograph flat and laid his finger on a line near the bottom. "Raymond Ellery retired to Saltmere. Still lives there, according to the electoral roll I pulled last week. Corner house on Fennel Street. And he would be—" he did the arithmetic, "—seventy-one."

Priya exhaled slowly. "Fits everything."

"Fits everything," Xanthe agreed. "Which is exactly what worries me. Because it fits too neatly, and I've spent thirty years learning that the detail that fits perfectly is usually the one somebody placed there for me to find." He closed the notebook. "Voss is dead. Ellery is visible. And the confessor keeps saying *he*—did you notice? Every letter, the killer is *he*. The man. Him. Our writer performs neutrality, but the pronouns lean."

"You think the writer is protecting a woman?" Priya asked. "Or is a woman?"

"I think," Xanthe said, "that I've been reading these letters as a confession when they're actually a curriculum. Someone is teaching me the case. Lesson by lesson. Correcting my homework. And you don't build a curriculum for a stranger—you build it for someone you've chosen. Which means the question isn't only *who killed my mother.*"

He looked at the letter lying on the counter, at the brown ink drying into permanence, at the words *yours in truth* signed by a hand that had held his childhood in its fist for weeks without him knowing.

"The question is why they chose me. And what happens when I graduate."

Outside, the rain thickened. Somewhere down the street a postman's bicycle rattled past, and Xanthe caught himself watching the slot in the door the way sailors watch the horizon—for weather, for sails, for whatever comes next.

Priya locked up the till and said quietly, "There's one more thing. While you were in Saltmere, I did what you asked. Looked into the sealed records." She hesitated, and he saw something cross her face that he had never seen there before—fear, or its close cousin. "Xanthe, your juvenile witness statement wasn't sealed alone. There were two names on the order. Yours was one."

She slid a photocopied fragment across the counter, redaction bar heavy and black across the second line.

"The other name's redacted. But the order was signed the same day as yours, by the same superintendent." She met his eyes. "Gerald Voss. You weren't the only child who saw something that night."

Xanthe stared at the black bar and felt the whole case tilt beneath him, the curriculum revealing its next chapter, the ghost at the other end of the letters suddenly much closer than any dead man could be.

Somewhere out there was a second witness. And somewhere out there, someone had decided Xanthe Kavanagh was ready to meet them.
