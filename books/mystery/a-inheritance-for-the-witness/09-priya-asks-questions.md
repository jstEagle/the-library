## 9. Priya Asks Questions

Priya had worked for Xanthe Kavanagh for four years, and in that time she had learned exactly three things about his past: he was from somewhere coastal, he didn't celebrate his birthday, and he never, ever talked about his mother.

Everything else she knew, she'd assembled the way you assemble a picture of someone from the objects they leave behind. The way he alphabetized crime fiction but shelved true crime face-out, like he was keeping an eye on it. The way he flinched—actually flinched, a small involuntary twitch of the shoulders—when the post came through the letterbox on certain mornings. The way he drank his tea when it had gone cold without noticing, staring at nothing, and then surfaced and said something sharp and funny as if the last ten minutes hadn't happened.

She'd never asked. That was the deal, unspoken, the way most real deals are. He paid her fairly, trusted her with the till and the rare cabinet and, once, with a first edition worth more than her car. In exchange she didn't ask why a man who read detective novels the way other people read scripture kept a shop that was quietly dying and seemed to prefer it that way.

But the letters had changed something.

She'd seen them arrive—two envelopes, same morning, both heavy paper, neither with a return address. She'd watched him carry them into the back room and not come out for forty minutes. When he finally emerged, he looked like a man who had opened a door in his own house and found a wall where a room used to be.

Since then he'd been requesting things. Police files. Archive records. He'd made calls in the low, careful voice he used for negotiating with book scouts, the one that meant he didn't want to be overheard. And twice this week he'd stood at the window watching the street with an expression she'd only ever seen on customers who came in looking for books about grief.

So on Thursday, while Xanthe was at the county records office in Ashcombe, Priya locked the till, turned the sign to CLOSED FOR LUNCH, and did something she'd been circling for days.

She asked questions.

* * *

She started where everyone starts, which is to say nowhere: the internet. Xanthe Kavanagh, it turned out, barely existed. No social media. No wedding announcements. A single listing in a trade directory for independent booksellers, and a review of the shop from 2019 in some local blog that described him as "reserved to the point of rudeness" and gave it three stars anyway because the stock was good.

No birth record she could find without paying, and she wasn't going to pay—not yet. But there were other ways. Her cousin Dev worked for a firm that did background checks for employers, and Dev owed her a favor from a wedding where she'd talked him out of proposing to a woman who, it later emerged, already had two husbands. She called him from the back step, cupping her hand around the phone against the drizzle.

"Priya Sharma," Dev said. "This is either a favor or a warning."

"A name check. Nothing weird. Just want to know if there's anything public."

"There's always something public. Spell it."

She spelled it. She heard keys. Then she heard the keys stop.

"Huh," said Dev.

"'Huh' what?"

"You said nothing weird."

"I said I wanted to know if there's anything public."

"Yeah, well." Papers rustled. "Xanthe Kavanagh, born March 1985, Saltmere-on-Sea. Mother Maren Kavanagh, deceased 1994. Father—" another pause, longer, "—father unrecorded. There's a court order here, Priya. Sealed juvenile record, 1994. Witness protection of identity, family court, county jurisdiction. That's not normal. Kids end up in sealed records for all kinds of reasons, but a witness seal means somebody testified."

The rain ticked on the step. Somewhere down the alley a gull screamed, absurdly, miles from any sea.

"He was nine," Priya said.

"How do you know that?"

"Maths, Dev. Do your job."

"I'm doing my job. My job says there's a nine-year-old who witnessed something bad enough that a judge sealed his statement, and thirty years later he's running a bookshop and flinching at the post." Dev's voice had gone careful, the way people's voices go when they're deciding whether to say the next thing. "There's one more line. It's redacted. Not blacked out—redacted properly, court-level, which costs money and intent. But the format's standard, so I can tell you the shape of it. It reads: witness statements taken from subject and—" he paused, "—and one other minor, name withheld by order."

Priya closed her eyes.

"One other minor," she repeated.

"That's what it says. Same incident. Two child witnesses. Only one got a bookshop and a life. The other one got a black bar." Keys again, faster now. "I can't get past the seal, Priya. Nobody gets past a seal like that without a court order or a very good lawyer, and honestly, even then—"

"Thanks, Dev."

"Priya." The word landed heavy. "What is he mixed up in?"

She looked through the back window into the shop, at the crime shelves, at the desk where Xanthe sat every morning arranging the day like a man defusing something. Four years of not asking. Four years of the deal.

"I don't know yet," she said. "That's the problem."

* * *

She told herself she'd stop there. She was good at telling herself things; she'd told herself she'd quit smoking twice and meant it both times.

But the shape of it wouldn't leave her alone. Two children. One grew up to be a man who bricked over his past so thoroughly that even his assistant of four years couldn't find a brick loose. Where was the other one? What happened to a nine-year-old who saw whatever Xanthe saw, and didn't have—what? Luck? A grandmother somewhere inland? Whatever it was that had caught Xanthe when he fell?

On Friday, Xanthe went up to Saltmere-on-Sea. She knew because he'd left a note on the desk in his slanted, pressurized handwriting: *Back late. Mind the Tillamook collection, buyer coming at two.* No explanation, no destination. But she'd answered the phone Wednesday when he'd called a trainline, and she'd heard him say Saltmere the way you say a word you've been avoiding saying aloud.

So while he was walking back into whatever village had made him, Priya walked into the county library and asked for the local newspaper archive.

The librarian found her a microfilm reader older than both of them. *The Coastal Herald*, 1994. Priya didn't know what month; she started in January and scrolled, and the year rolled past her in headlines about fishing quotas and a fete and a planning dispute about the seafront, all of it sunny and small, until October.

October 1994, the front page carried a photograph of a house she didn't recognize—a big grey pile above a curve of coastline—and a headline about a woman found dead below the cliffs near the old boathouse. Maren Kavanagh, 34. The article called it a tragic accident. Falling tide, wet rocks, a woman known to walk the cliff path in the evenings.

Tragic accident. Except that a judge doesn't seal a nine-year-old's testimony over a tragic accident. Judges seal testimony over things people don't want said.

She scrolled forward. Two weeks later, a follow-up piece, shorter, buried on page seven: inquest returns verdict of accidental death. A quote from the investigating officer—Detective Superintendent Crane, no, wait, she scrolled back—the surname was different. Superintendent Aldous Vane? No. She wrote it down. *Supt. R. Calloway*? No—

She stopped. She made herself go slow. The name under the quote was **Superintendent Harrow**? No. She pressed her palm flat on the reader and breathed, because her hands weren't steady, and the film kept sliding.

The name was **Superintendent Aldous Crane**.

She wrote it on the back of a receipt. Then, because the machine was warm and the archive was open and stopping felt worse than continuing, she kept scrolling. November. December. Nothing. The story died in October, wrapped up neat as a parcel: accident, inquest, verdict, silence. A whole year of village news before and after, and the death of Maren Kavanagh occupied eleven column inches total.

Eleven inches for a woman whose son flinched at the post.

* * *

Monday morning, Xanthe came in smelling faintly of salt and train, carrying a canvas bag of photocopies and a look she recognized now: the hunter's look, lit from behind. He'd found something in Saltmere. He was moving. And Priya stood behind the till with Dev's information and a newspaper clipping folded in her apron pocket and said nothing, because the deal was still the deal, wasn't it, except that the deal had been made with a man who apparently had a sealed record and a dead mother and a second child who'd vanished into a black bar, and none of that had been part of the price.

He sorted his photocopies at the desk. She made tea. She set his mug down and watched him not drink it, watched it go cold, watched him stare at the middle distance where, presumably, a boathouse or a cliff or a grey house was playing on a loop.

"Xanthe."

"Mm."

"What happened to you in 1994?"

The question landed in the shop like a dropped glass. He didn't startle—that was the strange thing. He went very still instead, the way water goes still right before it freezes, and when he turned to look at her his face had done something complicated: surprise rearranging itself into calculation rearranging itself into something almost like relief, and then all of it shuttered away behind the polite blankness she knew better than his smile.

"Why do you ask?"

"Because you've been requesting police files. Because you flinch at the post. Because there's a sealed juvenile record with your name on it and a second name beside yours that somebody paid real money to erase." She heard her own voice rise and made herself lower it. "Because I've spent four years not asking, and I'm asking now."

For a long moment he said nothing. Rain started against the window, soft, indifferent. Then he picked up the cold tea and drank it, all of it, which he never did, and set the mug down with a small precise click.

"The second name," he said quietly. "You're sure it was redacted. Not absent. Redacted."

"Court-level. Deliberate. Someone decided that kid stays hidden."

Something moved behind his eyes—she'd have sworn later it was fear, but aimed outward, not in. At whoever held the black bar. Or for them.

"Tomas," he said, half to himself. "Tomas Reyes." And then, catching her expression: "There was a boy. We were on the beach that night, both of us. We saw—" His jaw worked. "We told different stories. His was smarter than mine. Mine got me a sealed file and a bus ticket to my aunt's. His must have gotten him something else."

"What?"

"I don't know. I was nine. I never saw him again." He looked down at the photocopies, and his thumb moved across the top sheet absently, the way you touch a scar. "Priya. If anyone asks you about me—if anyone comes into this shop asking questions about me—you tell me the same day. Whoever they are, however friendly. Especially if they're friendly."

"Why?"

"Because the person who wrote those letters knows the file better than the police did." He met her eyes, and for the first time since she'd known him, he let her see all the way in, just for a second: a frightened man standing very straight inside a burning building, pointing calmly at the exits. "And now they might know about you."

He turned back to his papers. Conversation over, deal restored, or something like it. Priya went back to the till and stood there with her heart going too fast, doing sums she didn't want to do: two children, one black bar, a superintendent named Crane signing off on an accident, and her boss walking toward all of it with his coat on.

That night she stayed late after he left. She took out the clipping, smoothed it flat on the counter, and beneath the photograph of the grey house she wrote the only question that mattered, the one she intended to answer before anyone else did:

*Where is Tomas Reyes now?*

Then she opened the laptop, and began.
