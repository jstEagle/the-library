## 1. The Weight of Empty Paper

The rain had been coming down since Tuesday, and by Friday it had found its way through the roof of Sterling's Books in three places — one above the poetry section, which Gideon considered a fair comment on the genre; one above the register, which she did not; and one directly over the armchair by the window where she sat most mornings pretending to read inventory lists.

She had the bucket positioned under the third leak with the practiced indifference of long marriage. It caught the water in a slow, metronomic *plink*, and she had stopped hearing it weeks ago, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat or the traffic on a street you've lived on too long.

The shop smelled of wet cardboard and old glue. Forty years of paper breathing in a coastal town will do that — the damp gets into everything eventually, into the spines, into the floorboards, into the woman behind the counter if she stays long enough. Gideon was fifty-eight and had stayed long enough to feel the sea in her knees when the barometer dropped, which it had been doing all week like a man slowly sitting down.

She was doing sums she didn't want to do. That was the morning's real occupation, though the ledger open before her said *October Inventory* at the top in her own square handwriting. The numbers underneath told a story she already knew the ending to: accounts payable in red, a heating bill she'd been meaning to argue about since September, and a wholesale order from the distributor that she'd have to cancel or defer or somehow make vanish, the way small debts do when you look at them sideways and refuse to blink first.

*Six months,* she thought. *Maybe eight if the tourist season is kind.* The tourist season had not been kind in four years. The highway bypass had seen to that, rerouting the summer people forty minutes east of Harbor's Rest, so that now they blew through on their way to somewhere with a marina and a wine bar and never learned that this town had once mattered — that a Voss had built half its waterfront, that the cannery had employed three hundred souls, that there had been a murder worth talking about even if nobody talked about it anymore.

Gideon closed the ledger. There was no point in reading the same bad news twice before nine in the morning.

She poured herself coffee from the pot behind the counter — the good machine had been sold in March, along with the display case and her last illusions about expanding into the back room — and took up her post in the armchair with a paperback she'd read twice already, because a shopkeeper who looks busy discourages browsers, and browsers were all she had left.

The bell over the door rang at 9:14.

She knew before she looked up that it wasn't a customer. Customers came in out of the rain with that particular hunched apology, shaking off water, murmuring something about the weather as if the weather were a shared acquaintance they'd both been avoiding. This visitor came in the way official business comes in anywhere: too fast, too dry under an umbrella held by someone else, carrying a manila envelope the way a priest carries the collection plate.

"Ms. Sterling? Gideon Sterling?" The young man was maybe thirty, in a county windbreaker with a sheriff's office patch, and he had the nervous energy of someone delivering news he'd rehearsed on the drive over.

"That depends on who's asking."

He fumbled a badge from his jacket. "Deputy Hale. Marcus Hale — people call me Deputy Hale, it's less confusing." He said it automatically, the way a man says a thing he's said ten thousand times, and then seemed to remember himself and straightened. "I've got papers for you. Court service." He held out the envelope. "You're requested to appear. As a witness."

Gideon set down her paperback. She took the envelope without hurry, because hurry was a tell, and twenty-six years of detective work didn't wash out of a person just because she'd spent the last six selling used novels to retirees. The envelope was heavy for a summons. Official weight. She turned it over. The return address was the county courthouse, and beneath it, in the blocky type of legal boilerplate: *In re: Exhumation Order 74-CV-0113, Voss, Eleanor M.*

The name went through her like cold water through a crack in a hull.

She'd known, of course. Everyone in Harbor's Rest had known for a month — the cemetery relocation had been front-page news in the *Gazette* for three weeks running, the state finally forcing through the bluff stabilization project that meant moving the old hillside graves before winter storms took them into the Pacific whether anyone consented or not. Eleanor Voss's plot sat right at the eroding edge, the headstone leaning at an angle that had grown more pronounced every year, as if the founder herself were trying to get a better view of the water.

But knowing a thing was coming and having it arrive in a manila envelope were different animals entirely.

"This is a witness summons," Hale said, filling the silence because silences made him itch — she could see that already, see the whole shape of him, young and earnest and slightly terrified of her, which amused her in a tired way. "For the exhumation. Wednesday morning, seven o'clock, Resthaven Cemetery. You're named specifically. Judge Wexler signed it himself."

"Why me?"

Hale shifted his weight. "It says — well, it lists your credentials. Former lead investigator, Major Crimes, State Bureau. Case 1974-11 is referenced. The Voss drowning." He swallowed. "It says you're required to witness the proceedings given your, uh, historical involvement."

*Historical involvement.* Twenty-six years ago she'd been thirty-two years old and certain of everything, standing on those same cliffs in a rain not unlike this one, looking down at black water and telling herself the case was closed because the paperwork said it was closed. A woman had gone into the sea. A body had gone into a coffin. A town had mourned its founder and moved on, and Gideon Sterling had moved on too, eventually, after the promotion she didn't take and the transfer she did and the early retirement nobody at the Bureau quite understood.

"I'm retired," she said.

"Yes, ma'am. The summons doesn't care." He almost smiled, then thought better of it. "Judge Wexler said — I'm supposed to tell you this directly — he said, 'Tell her the ground doesn't keep secrets forever, and neither should she.'"

Gideon looked at him for a long moment. Old Wexler. He'd been a junior prosecutor in '74, wet behind the ears, and he'd signed off on her closing report without asking the questions she'd sometimes wondered, in the small hours, whether she'd wanted him to ask.

"Tell the judge," she said, "that I'll be there."

Hale nodded, relieved, and let himself out into the rain. The bell rang behind him, a bright little sound, absurd against what sat now in Gideon's hands.

She didn't open the envelope right away. She sat in the leaking armchair and listened to the bucket fill, *plink, plink, plink*, and felt something move in her chest that she hadn't felt in years — not excitement exactly, nothing so clean as that. More like an old injury announcing a change in the weather. The ache of a thing that had healed crooked.

*Eleanor Voss.*

She'd been dead longer than Gideon had known her alive, which was to say not at all. Gideon had come to the case after, when the drowning was already a closed file and a headstone, when the town had already built its tidy story: the widow-founder, frail after her husband Marcus's long illness, walking the cliff path alone on the fourth of October, 1974, slipping on wet grass, taken by the sea. A tragedy. An accident. A town's grief, folded and put away like winter clothes.

And a case file, 1974-11, that Gideon had reviewed in her second year with the Bureau and found — what? Nothing. She'd found nothing. She remembered the nothing clearly: the witness statements that matched each other a little too neatly, the medical examiner's report that was thorough in all the wrong places and silent in one place she couldn't name even to herself, the general texture of a story that had been ironed flat. But she'd been young, and the file was old, and the sheriff's department wanted no trouble with the Voss estate, which employed half the town, and so she'd written *no evidence of foul play* in her own square handwriting and filed the whole thing away in a drawer in Sacramento and, eventually, in the deeper drawer where she kept the parts of herself she'd decided to stop being.

Now the ground was giving her back.

* * *

She opened the envelope at noon, after two customers — Mrs. Aldana buying a large-print western she'd buy again next month, and a teenager who sheltered from the rain for forty minutes and bought nothing — and read the summons twice through.

The language was standard. The attachments were not.

There was a copy of the exhumation petition, filed by the Voss Estate Trust through its administrator. There was a surveyor's report on the bluff erosion, with photographs: the cemetery terrace crumbling like stale bread, headstones sliding by inches per year toward the drop. And there was a single sheet, separate from the rest, that stopped her breath for a moment.

An inventory of the grave's expected contents, prepared by the court. *One (1) burial casket, hardwood, interred October 12, 1974. One (1) set of remains, identified as Eleanor Marie Voss, deceased age 61. Associated burial clothing and personal effects.*

Expected contents. As if the court already suspected the ground might disagree.

Gideon read the phrase three times. Then she got up, crossed to the history section — local interest, bottom shelf, mostly unsold — and pulled down the town history she'd read a dozen times, *Harbor's Rest: A Century on the Water*, with its photograph of Eleanor Voss on the frontispiece. The founder stood on the steps of the cannery in the picture, small and upright in a dark coat, her face sharp and unsmiling, eyes fixed on some point beyond the camera. A woman who looked like she was waiting for something. Or watching for it.

Fifty-one years in the ground. Or fifty-one years wherever she was.

Gideon put the book down. Her hands weren't quite steady, and she noted that with clinical displeasure, the way she'd once noted a suspect's trembling cigarette hand. Six years behind a cash register, and still the old machinery turned over when you gave the crank a pull. Still the pulse picked up. Still the mind began, unbidden, laying out the board: who benefits, who grieves, who signs the paperwork, who holds the keys.

The Voss estate. She knew the name attached to it without looking — everyone did. Crane. Aldous Crane, the family lawyer, silver-haired and immaculate, who'd handled the trust since before Miriam was born, probably, since before—

Miriam.

The name arrived uninvited, the way it always did, riding in on the coattails of the Voss name like weather behind weather. Thirty years since Gideon had seen her. Thirty years since a kitchen in Sacramento and a suitcase by the door and words said in the particular cold voice people use when they are protecting themselves from the thing they most want. *You'll always choose the job. I can't love a woman who's married to a case file.* And Gideon, too proud to say *then come with me anyway*, too proud to say anything but *fine*.

Miriam Voss. Granddaughter of the woman in the coffin. Or the woman who should be in the coffin.

Gideon stood at the window and watched the rain work on the street. The gutters ran brown and fast. Down at the end of the block, the harbor was invisible behind the gray curtain, but she could hear the buoy bell, and beyond it the sea, patient as bookkeeping, keeping its accounts.

*The ground doesn't keep secrets forever, and neither should she.*

"All right, Wexler," she said aloud, to the empty shop, to the rain, to the lean ghost of the woman she'd been at thirty-two. "Wednesday."

That night she climbed the stairs to the apartment above the shop and took down the box from the top of the closet — the box she'd carried through three moves and never opened, the one labeled in fading marker *PERSONAL — DO NOT SHIP*. Inside, under a layer of newspaper from 1998, was her old notebook from the Voss file. Her own hand, younger and harder, recording interviews and timelines and one marginal note, circled twice, that she had no memory of writing:

*ME report — why no stomach contents?*

She sat on the edge of the bed with the notebook open on her knee while the rain kept on, and somewhere below her the bucket overflowed onto the floorboards of the empty shop, and Gideon Sterling did not hear it, because she was already halfway back to 1974, and the water was rising everywhere at once.
