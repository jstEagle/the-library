## 14. The Inheritance Clause

The reading of the will was held in the front parlor of the Voss house, because Aldous Crane insisted that Eleanor Voss's final wishes be honored on ground she had owned, and Miriam understood now — standing at the window with her arms crossed against the cold that the old house kept like a grudge — that every detail of this afternoon had been arranged with the same care a man might use to arrange flowers on a coffin.

There were eleven chairs set out. Eleven. As if Crane had counted the living heirs himself and found them wanting.

Miriam counted them anyway. Her cousin Daniel from Portland, who had flown in twice already for the exhumation and would fly out again tomorrow, his grief worn like a rented coat. The Halloway sisters, granddaughters of Eleanor's late brother, both of whom had spent their childhoods being told the Voss name was a burden they should carry quietly. Old Mrs. Pettibone, who was not an heir at all but came to everything in this town that involved death, taking notes in a small leather book. Deputy Hale stood by the door in his uniform, uncomfortable, his eyes finding Gideon Sterling's across the room more than once — Gideon, who sat slightly apart from everyone in a chair near the cold fireplace, her hands folded, her face doing the thing it had always done: nothing at all.

And Crane himself, at the head of the room, behind Eleanor's writing desk, which he had no right to sit behind and had sat behind for fifty years, apparently, without anyone thinking to object.

Miriam had not wanted to come back to this house. She had grown up two thousand miles away from it, raised on postcards and her mother's careful silences, and when she finally saw it as an adult she had found it exactly what she'd feared — a monument built to a woman nobody remembered honestly. But the exhumation had changed something. An empty coffin does that. It reaches backward through time and unseats every certainty you were ever handed, and Miriam had spent three weeks now sleeping badly in a guest room upstairs, listening to the sea work at the cliffs below, feeling like a guest in her own inheritance.

Gideon had told her, two nights ago, over coffee neither of them drank: *Watch Crane during the reading. Not when he reads. When he waits.*

So Miriam watched.

* * *

Crane rose at four o'clock precisely, and the room settled around him the way water settles around a stone. He was seventy-eight years old and moved like a man of sixty, silver-haired, immaculate, his voice trained by half a century of funerals and closings into something warm and graveled and utterly trustworthy. He had read wills in this parlor for three generations of Voisses. He had, Miriam realized with a chill that had nothing to do with the house, probably read Marcus Voss's will too. And whatever had been done to Marcus in 1991.

"Thank you all for coming," Crane said. "Eleanor Voss was the founder of this town, and its faithful servant, and I had the honor of serving her family for—" he paused, and the pause was perfect, calibrated, a craftsman's pause— "most of my life."

*Not most,* Miriam thought. *All. You were twenty-eight in 1974. Your father before you.* She kept her face still. She was learning that from Gideon too.

"The document I hold is the last valid testament of Eleanor Voss, executed March 3rd, 1971, and never amended." He held it up briefly, the cream paper, the ribbon, the wax seal stamped with the Voss anchor. "I will read it in full, as the law requires, and then we will discuss disposition."

He read. The opening formalities. The revocation of prior wills. The specific bequests — modest ones, a ring to a niece, books to the historical society, an annuity to a housekeeper long dead whose children would receive it now, and Miriam watched those small kindnesses land in the room like stones dropped in still water, ripples of surprise and softening. This was Crane's genius, she thought. He let Eleanor be generous first. He let the room love her before he did what he'd come to do.

Then he turned the page, and his voice changed. Not much. A half-tone lower. Graver. The voice of a man about to deliver difficult news he wished he didn't have to deliver.

"I come now to Article Nine," he said. "I ask your particular attention."

The room leaned in. Even Mrs. Pettibone's pen stopped.

"'Article Nine,'" Crane read. "'Knowing that death comes to us all, but that the manner of a death may be mistaken, misrecorded, or maliciously reported, I direct the following. If my death shall be proven — by competent evidence, in a court of law — to have occurred other than as officially recorded, then all provisions herein are void. In such case, the entirety of my estate, real and personal, including the house, the lands, the endowments, and all accounts held in the name of Voss or any alias thereof, shall pass in full to the Voss Continuance Trust, established under this article, of which my attorney shall be sole trustee, with discretion absolute and accountable to no one.'"

He lowered the paper.

Silence. The particular silence of eleven people each doing arithmetic at once.

Daniel spoke first, because Daniel always spoke first. "That can't be enforceable. That's — Crane, that's absurd. A condition like that, voiding the whole will if—"

"It is unusual," Crane agreed gently. "It was also entirely legal in 1971, and it has been upheld since. Mrs. Voss was a precise woman. She distrusted imprecision in all things, including her own death." He smiled faintly, a smile meant to be shared, and most of the room shared it. "She said to me once — I remember it exactly — 'Aldous, everyone lies about how people die. I intend to make lying expensive.'"

A ripple of laughter, nervous and quickly smothered. Miriam did not laugh. She was watching Gideon, and Gideon was watching Crane, and neither of them was smiling, and between them passed some current that Miriam could feel on her own skin like weather coming.

"And the trust," Hale said from the door. His voice was careful. "Who's the trustee?"

"I am," Crane said simply. "As I have been since its establishment. It has never been activated. With luck, it never would have been." He folded his glasses. "Of course, recent events have made the question... less academic."

"What recent events?" asked Ruth Halloway, the younger sister, sharp-voiced.

"The exhumation," Crane said, spreading his hands, sorrowful, reasonable. "An empty coffin. A ledger where a body should be. If it is proven that Eleanor Voss did not die in October of 1974 as recorded — drowned off her own cliffs, God rest her — then Article Nine activates, and the estate passes wholly to the trust. To my administration." He looked around the room with genuine-seeming regret. "You would all receive, I'm afraid, nothing. The trust's purpose, as written, is preservation of the estate entire. Not distribution."

"That's theft," Daniel said, standing now. "That's — she wrote that fifty years ago, she couldn't have known—"

"She knew exactly," Crane said, and there was something in his voice for just a moment, something dry and admiring, that made the hair rise on Miriam's arms. "She knew precisely what she was doing. That was the point of her."

* * *

Afterward the room broke apart like ice going out. Daniel on the phone to a lawyer in Portland, loudly. The Halloway sisters in the corner, whispering, frightened, greedy. Mrs. Pettibone asking Hale questions he couldn't answer. And Crane accepting condolences and complaints with the same mild competence, moving among them, untouchable, until he reached Miriam.

"Miss Voss." He inclined his head. "You've been quiet."

"I don't know enough to be loud," Miriam said.

"No one ever does, at these things. That's rather the point of lawyers." He studied her a moment with pale, patient eyes. "You'll want counsel of your own, of course. The trust will be challenged, certainly. I welcome the challenge. Challenges clarify." He paused. "Your grandmother was a remarkable woman. I hope, whatever comes of all this, that you come to feel you knew her."

"I'm starting to," Miriam said. "Though maybe not the way you mean."

Something flickered across his face — so fast she almost missed it, a shutter drawn and released — and then he was all courtesy again, bowing slightly, moving on to shake Daniel's hand and promise him copies of everything, everything, of course.

When the last car had gone down the drive, the parlor was left to Miriam and Gideon and the smell of cold ash and old roses. Gideon hadn't moved from her chair by the fireplace. She was looking at the writing desk where Crane had sat.

"He counted the chairs," Miriam said. "Eleven chairs. He knew exactly who'd come."

"He's known the shape of this room for fifty years." Gideon's voice was low, roughened. "Miriam. Walk it back with me. Slowly."

Miriam sat on the arm of the opposite chair, close enough that their knees nearly touched, and did not pretend she wasn't glad of that.

"The will was executed in 1971," Gideon said. "Three years before Eleanor disappeared. Which means Article Nine wasn't a reaction to anything. It was preparation."

"Eleanor planned to vanish."

"Eleanor planned to vanish, and she built a tripwire into her own death. If anyone ever proved the drowning was false, the estate wouldn't scatter to heirs who might talk, sell, dig. It would consolidate. Into one trust. Administered by—" Gideon stopped.

"By Crane," Miriam finished. "Her attorney. Who helped her disappear." She felt it arriving, the thing she'd been circling for days, cold and enormous. "But Gideon. The exhumation. Nobody chose to exhume her. The cemetery relocation — that was the county, the highway survey, it was bureaucratic, it was in the paper for months—"

"Was it?" Gideon turned her head. "Or did someone make sure it was? Someone who knew Article Nine existed, knew it had slept for fifty years, and needed it woken?" She stood, wincing slightly at her hip, and went to the window, looking down at the gray water beyond the cliff grass. "Think about what an empty coffin actually does, Miriam. Legally. Emotionally. It proves nothing by itself. A body can be lost, stolen, misplaced by a lazy undertaker in 1974. But it starts a clock. It forces the question into court. And the only way Article Nine gets triggered — the only way Crane gets the whole estate, free and clear, accountable to no one — is if her death is proven *other than as recorded*. Proven. In court. By competent evidence."

"So he needs the truth to come out," Miriam whispered. "He needs Eleanor's secret exposed. After keeping it fifty years."

"After keeping it fifty years." Gideon's reflection in the dark glass was grim. "Because something changed. Because he ran out of time, or money, or patience. Because the embezzlement — and it is embezzlement, I'd stake the shop on it — finally caught up with the estate's income. Or because Eleanor died natural, recently, somewhere far away, under another name, and he learned of it and knew the clock had started whether he liked it or not." She turned from the window. "Either way, the empty coffin wasn't a wound the past opened. It was a door he opened on purpose. He walked the county into exhuming her. He sat in the front row at the cemetery looking appropriately horrified. And today he read us a will designed in 1971 to hand him everything, and we all sat in his chairs and listened."

Miriam's hands had gone cold. "The ledger. The one in the coffin. Why leave it there? Why give us evidence?"

"Because evidence is what makes it *proven*," Gideon said. "He doesn't fear the truth, Miriam. He needs it. What he fears is the wrong truth — the truth with his name attached. The ledger in that coffin was bait, same as the exhumation. Everything we've found, we've found because he let us. Or thinks he let us." She met Miriam's eyes. "Which means everything we do next has to look like we're following his path. And be doing something else."

Outside, the tide was turning; they could hear it change below the cliffs, a long sighing shift in the sound of the water, the sea beginning to give back what it had borrowed. Gideon glanced toward the window, toward the sound, and Miriam saw the calculation move behind her eyes like a lamp behind a curtain.

"The low tide Thursday," Gideon said quietly. "The caves. If there's anything down there that names him — anything at all — it has to be in our hands before his petition to activate the trust reaches a judge. He filed yesterday. I checked. We have maybe six days."

"Six days," Miriam said. "And he's watching you."

"He's always watched me." Gideon almost smiled, and it was the old smile, the dangerous one, the one Miriam had fallen for thirty years ago across a different room in this same house. "Good. Let him watch. Let him see exactly what I want him to see."

Down the hall, in the study that had been locked for fifty years, a phone rang once, twice, and stopped — and Miriam, who had not yet given anyone the new number, went very still.
