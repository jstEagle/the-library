## 9. Three Weeks Earlier

The journal came out of the satchel the way everything about my sister did: sideways, without permission, and impossible to put down.

I had told myself I would catalog it. That was the word I used—*catalog*—because it sounded like work, and work was the shape I gave to things I couldn't bear to feel. Photograph each page, log each item, maintain chain of custody. I am very good at procedure. Procedure is what you build when you don't trust yourself with anything looser.

I photographed four pages before I stopped photographing and started reading.

It was nearly midnight. The morgue's back office smells of formalin and old carpet, and the fluorescent hum is pitched at exactly the frequency that makes silence feel deliberate. I sat down with the journal on my knees like it was a hymnal, and I read my sister's handwriting for three hours.

She wrote in pen. Blue ink, small and slanted, the letters leaning right as if they were always about to go somewhere. She crossed out almost nothing. Whatever she felt, she committed to it on the first pass—a discipline I have never once managed in thirty years of talking myself out of sentences.

The first entry was dated twenty-six days ago. The day after she arrived in Hollow Creek.

---

*I found the town on a map before I found it in the world,* she wrote. *That sounds more mystical than it is. I'd been searching county records for two years and Hollow Creek kept appearing like a bad penny—births, deeds, a marriage license with Edith Sterling's name on it. I bought a bus ticket because the alternative was buying another year of not knowing.*

*The bus let me off at the gas station at 4:40 in the afternoon. The attendant asked if I was passing through and I said yes, and then I walked up Main Street and saw a woman locking up a funeral home, and I stood in the doorway of the hardware store like an idiot until she got in her car.*

*It was like looking into one of those trick mirrors at a carnival, except the trick is that both reflections are real.*

*I know her name now. Emrys. Who names a child Emrys? Our mother, apparently. She named me Liora, which means light, which is the kind of thing a woman tells herself when she's giving one daughter away. I looked it up when I was nine and hated her for a week.*

*I didn't approach. I want that written down somewhere so future me can't pretend otherwise. I stood there and she drove past me close enough that I could have touched the car, and I said nothing, and she has no idea she has a sister, and I made sure of it by being a coward for one more day.*

---

I set the journal down.

This is the part where I'm supposed to say something composed. Something clinical. *The subject's account corroborates the timeline established by—* No. Let me be honest, since honesty is apparently the theme of this chapter of my life, whether I consented to it or not.

My sister watched me lock up my funeral home and I never knew. There was a woman on Main Street with my face, standing in the hardware store doorway, and I drove past her, and whatever I was thinking about—it was probably inventory, or the broken latch on the cooler, or nothing—I drove past my own reflection and went home.

Twenty-six days. She was in this town for twenty-six days and I never saw her once. Or worse—I *did* see her. Some part of me registered the familiarity and filed it under *one of those faces*, the way you do, the way we all do, and moved on. I keep replaying every stranger from the last month looking for her and finding only blanks.

I picked the journal back up. What else was I going to do—sleep?

---

*Day three. I've rented a room above the diner from a woman named Dot who asks no questions, which either means she's discreet or she's seen everything and questions stopped working. The room smells like cinnamon and radiator dust. It's perfect.*

*I followed her today. God, that sounds deranged. I followed my sister home from work and then stood across the street from her house for forty minutes like a ghost auditioning for its own haunting.*

*Her house is small. White siding, blue door—the blue is chipped at the bottom corner, someone kicked it or a dog scratched it and she never fixed it, which tells me she notices damage and doesn't prioritize it. There's a light over the porch that comes on at dusk automatically, and one window upstairs that stays lit until 1 or 2 a.m. She works late. Or she can't sleep. Both, probably. We have the same face; why wouldn't we have the same insomnia?*

*I practiced what I'd say. Hi, I'm Liora, I'm your twin sister, Mom gave me away when we were born, sorry to ambush you outside your own house. Every version ends with her looking at me the way people look at missionaries and lottery scammers. So I keep practicing and keep not doing it.*

*Tomorrow. Everyone always says tomorrow. Tomorrow is the most dishonest word in the language and I use it constantly.*

---

There it was. The window. My window—the one above the study, the one I leave lit because a dark house feels like an admission. She had watched that light burn and counted the hours of it, and three weeks later she climbed through it, and I had spent the intervening days resenting her for the intrusion.

You can't resent the dead. I know that. It's the first rule of grief and I break it hourly. But sitting there at midnight with her handwriting under my thumb, I resented her anyway—for watching me without my knowledge, for knowing the hour my light went out, for having data on me I'd never consented to give. And underneath the resentment, shamelessly, unbearably: I understood it completely. If our positions were reversed—if I had learned at thirty that there was a person walking around with half my history and all of my face—I would have done exactly what she did. I would have stood across the street. I would have rehearsed. I would have chosen tomorrow, nightly, forever.

We were the same coward in different clothes. One of us just ran out of tomorrows first.

---

*Day five. I went to the cemetery.*

*I know, I know. But hear me out: if you want to understand a town, read its stones. And if you want to understand a family, find their plot and count the gaps.*

*Sterlings are everywhere out there. Rows of them. Edith Sterling died eight years ago—our mother, though writing that word still feels like borrowing someone else's coat—and there's space beside her. Fresh-turned earth gets filled in eventually but the plot itself has room. Someone bought it decades ago and left it waiting.*

*I stood next to our mother's grave and introduced myself. Out loud. An old man walking his dog gave me a wide berth, which, fair.*

*I told her I wasn't angry anymore. Which was a lie, but a polite one, the kind you bring to a funeral. I told her I'd found Emrys. I said, "She looks like you." I don't actually remember what our mother looks like beyond photographs, so what I meant was: she looks like me, and I look like you, and isn't that a strange inheritance—to be handed a face by a woman who handed off one of its owners.*

*Then I cried like an idiot in a cemetery, which is at least the traditional venue for it.*

---

I had to stop again here. Not because of the crying—God knows I've done my share of professional crying, the controlled kind, in supply closets, between cases. It was the plot. The space beside our mother.

I bought that plot. Eight years ago, the morning after the funeral, I went to the cemetery board and purchased the adjoining ground, and when the clerk asked why, I said, "For family," and he looked at me with pity because everyone in Hollow Creek knew I had none. I told myself it was sentiment. Practical sentiment. Somewhere to be buried someday, near the only person who'd ever—

And my sister had stood on that same grass, looking at that same gap, and drawn the obvious conclusion: *someone left this space open.* Two women who had never met, standing in the same spot years apart, believing the empty ground was theirs alone. Or hers. Or ours. The plot was the only conversation we ever had, and neither of us knew the other was speaking.

I poured coffee I didn't drink and kept reading.

---

*Day eight. I've learned her schedule, which I recognize is stalker behavior, and I've decided to forgive myself for it on the grounds that stalkers want something FROM people and I only want something FOR her. Namely: the truth. She deserves to know what happened in 1994. She's a coroner, for God's sake—she deals in truth professionally. Surely she of all people will know what to do with it.*

*Except.*

*Except I've started pulling the older threads and they're not pretty. The land records around here are a spiderweb, and every strand runs through Crane. Aldous Crane owns half this valley and the other half owes him money. And guess whose signature shows up on the deed transfers from '94? Ours. Mother's. Four parcels, signed the same week I was born, to a woman flat on her back in a hospital with a fever of 103.*

*I'm not saying I understand it yet. I'm saying the timing is obscene.*

*Also: someone noticed me asking questions at the county office today. A lawyer type, silver hair, too-friendly smile, introduced himself as Renner. He remembered my name after hearing it once. He said, "Researching family history? Hollow Creek's full of it." Full of it. I laughed. He didn't.*

---

Renner. Of course Renner. He was already circling her before I knew she existed. I read that paragraph four times, hunting for what Liora had missed, some telltale detail that would have warned her—but there was nothing to miss. That was the whole horror of Silas Renner: he arrived looking like helpfulness. He probably offered her assistance. He probably offered her *coffee*.

She'd met him on day eight. She died on day twenty-six. Eighteen days in which she kept digging anyway, because she was going to bring me the truth whether it killed her or—well.

---

*Day eleven. Tonight I almost knocked.*

*She came out onto the porch around ten with a mug of something and just stood there looking at the dark, and I was across the street in the shadow of the Hendersons' hedge like a burglar with a conscience, and I got as far as the curb. One foot in the road. I swear to God, one foot.*

*And then a car slowed down—not stopping, just slowing, the way cars do when a street has a stranger standing in it—and I stepped back and the moment closed like water over a stone.*

*Here's what I couldn't get past, standing at that curb: what do I do to her life by existing? She built something. You can see it from the sidewalk—she built a whole quiet careful life with routines and a porch and a mug at ten o'clock, and it's the life I would have built if anyone had let me. Do I get to walk up and hand her a bomb labeled SISTER?*

*Mom traded me away to save her. Fine. But nobody asked ME. Nobody checked whether I wanted to be the price. And now I'm supposed to be the one who decides whether the bill comes due?*

*Tomorrow. Definitely tomorrow.*

---

*Definitely tomorrow.* I read those two words and had to press the heel of my hand against my eyes for a while. The office clock said 2:40. Somewhere in the building a pipe ticked as it cooled, patient as a pulse.

She had been at the curb. One foot in the road. Twenty feet of asphalt between us, and a car slowed, and that was that—that was the entire tragedy of my family, compressed into a single failed crossing. If that car had been five seconds later. If she'd taken the second step. If I had come out onto the porch ninety seconds earlier and seen a woman with my face standing in the Hendersons' hedge shadow and called out—what? *Can I help you?* And she'd have turned around, and I'd have been looking at myself, and everything after that would have been different. Everything.

Instead: water over a stone. And eighteen days later, Miller's Pond.

---

*Day fourteen. I found the hospital records. Or the shape of them, anyway—a delivery nurse named Ruth Ibbetson is still alive and living out past the quarry, and she remembers. Oh, she remembers. She talked for two hours. Two infants, born nineteen minutes apart, one healthy transfer arranged overnight, a doctor paid to falsify the birth certificate. She held my hand while she told me, like I was the one delivering.*

*"Your mama cried the whole time," she said. "But she signed."*

*Signed. Everyone in this story signs something. Mother signed the deed. Mother signed the certificate. Mother signed me over to a couple in Cedar Falls who wanted a baby and got a girl who grew up asking why.*

*I have copies of everything now. Deeds, certificates, Ruth's statement. It's enough. It's finally enough, and I don't know what to do with enough.*

*So: tomorrow. I'm going to knock on her door tomorrow. I've decided. I even bought a card, which is ridiculous, what do you write in a card for this—"Thinking of you, also I'm your twin"? I'll figure it out. I'll stand on the porch and I'll say it plain: my name is Liora, I'm your sister, I've been across the street for two weeks, please don't call the police.*

*Wish me luck, whoever reads this. Probably nobody reads this. Probably it's just me and the radiator dust.*

---

Probably nobody reads this.

I closed the journal and held it against my sternum like you'd hold a compress against a wound, and I sat in the fluorescent hum and let myself do the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the dental records came back.

Day fourteen, she resolved to knock. Day fifteen, sixteen, seventeen—no entry about knocking. The entries thinned toward the end, shorter, more careful, the handwriting tightening like a fist. On day nineteen she wrote a single line: *Met R. again. He's offering to make it easy. Easy is never easy.* On day twenty-two: *He wants to buy the truth. I told him I'd think about it. I need to think about what it costs HER if I sell.* And the last entry, day twenty-five, undated in any formal way, just a fragment at the bottom of a page:

*If anything happens to me, it was him. Tell her I was coming up the walk.*

Tell her I was coming up the walk.

I set the journal down with hands that were steadier than they had any right to be, and I reached for my coat, because it was 3 a.m. and the cemetery was a mile away and there was a question I suddenly needed answered with my own eyes instead of hers: what does the ground beside our mother look like when you're the one standing on it, reading a stone, keeping a promise made to a woman who never got to keep hers?

I was halfway to the door when I remembered the last line of the satchel inventory. The cashier's check stub. Made out to *cash*, dated day twenty-three, for an amount with a lot of zeroes in it.

Liora had written *he wants to buy the truth*—and two days later, someone had cut her a check.

She never cashed it. I was almost certain she never cashed it. Which meant she'd refused the money, refused to sell, gone up that walk anyway—or meant to.

Which meant Silas Renner hadn't killed a blackmailer.

He'd killed a woman who wouldn't take the bribe.

I turned off the office light behind me, out of habit, out of thirty years of habit—and then, for the first time in my careful invisible life, I turned it back on, and left it burning, and went out into the dark to see what my sister had left me.
