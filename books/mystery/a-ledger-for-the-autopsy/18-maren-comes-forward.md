## 18. Maren Comes Forward

I had the suitcase on the bed by seven in the morning, which for a woman who hasn't packed for anything but funerals since 2011 is roughly the equivalent of a teenager getting a tattoo: impulsive, permanent-looking, and guaranteed to be regretted.

The suitcase was brown vinyl with a broken zipper and a smell like the inside of a piano. It had belonged to my husband, Walt, God rest him, who used it exactly twice before deciding that travel was a scam invented by airlines and motels to separate honest people from their money. I inherited it along with his opinions, which is how most marriages end—one party dying and the other inheriting everything they were wrong about.

I was packing because somebody had cut the brake line on Emrys Sterling's car.

I know that's not how a rational person responds to news of that kind. A rational person thinks, *How terrible, I hope she's all right,* and then makes a casserole. But I am not a rational person. I am a retired schoolteacher with thirty-four years of fourth graders behind me and one very large secret sitting on my chest like a cat that has decided this lap is its territory forever, and when I read about those brakes in the *Hollow Creek Courier*—page three, below an ad for gutter cleaning—my hands started shaking so badly I spilled coffee on the obituaries, which felt rude.

Because here is the thing nobody says out loud in this town: we all knew something was wrong with 1994. Not the details. The details were Aldous Crane's business, and Aldous Crane's business was the weather in Hollow Creek—you didn't question it, you just dressed for it. But you could feel it, the way you can feel a storm coming in your hip. Edith Sterling went into the hospital pregnant as a June field and came out with one baby instead of two, and nobody asked where the other one went, because asking Aldous Crane questions was how you ended up renting instead of owning, or teaching fourth grade in a town forty miles east, or not existing at all.

And now the other one had come back. And now she was dead in Miller's Pond. And now someone was cutting brake lines, which meant the weather was turning again, and I had spent thirty years telling myself that silence was survival when what I actually meant was that silence was easier than courage and I could afford easy.

So: the suitcase.

I got three cardigans in—the gray one, the green one, the one with the moth hole shaped like Portugal—before my hands stopped working entirely. I sat down on the edge of the bed next to Walt's stupid suitcase and I thought about Maren Voss, age twenty-six, standing at her kitchen window on a night in October thirty years ago, watching fog come off the pond road like the whole world being erased and redrawn by a careless hand.

I saw a truck that night. County plates. And I told the police about the fog, because the fog was safe, and the fog was true, and truth comes in grades the way eggs do, and I have been living on the cheapest grade ever since.

* * *

Here is what I remember about 1994, and I want it noted that I remember everything, that remembering is the one punishment I never managed to escape:

Edith Sterling came to see me on a Tuesday. She was twenty-three, maybe twenty-four, thin as a hymnal, with that pale hair that made her look lit from inside even when she was crying, which she often was by then. Her husband had died in the spring—the mill accident, the one the company settled quietly—and she had twin girls coming in November and medical bills arriving like tide.

She didn't ask me for money. She wasn't the type. What she asked me was whether Aldous Crane was a good man, and I said yes, because I was twenty-six and stupid and he'd donated the gymnasium floor to the school, and I have thought about that lie every day of my life since, the way you think about a stone in your shoe that you've worn so long it's grown into the bone.

She went to see him that Thursday. I know because she told me afterward—or rather, she came to my door at eleven at night, gray-faced, holding a piece of paper, and she sat in my kitchen and drank tea she never touched and told me what he'd offered.

He'd pay for everything. The doctors, the delivery, the bills already stacked on her table. He'd set her up somewhere dry and warm with the surviving child. All he wanted in return was one of the babies—"the spare," was the phrase Silas Renner used later, though Edith said Aldous himself called it "the arrangement"—and her signature on some documents, and her silence, permanent, notarized, absolute.

"And if I say no?" she asked me, in my kitchen, in my house, as if I were the town instead of just another woman in it.

What could I tell her? That Aldous Crane owned the hospital board, the bank, the sheriff's uncle, and the only employer within commuting distance? That saying no meant both girls growing up in poverty while he watched from his hill? That the documents weren't requests?

I told her to pray on it. Pray on it. As if God had ever once involved Himself in the real estate transactions of Hollow Creek.

She signed. I know she signed because I drove her to the county office myself—I've never told anyone that part, not even under anesthesia—and I stood in the parking lot while she went in, and when she came out she looked like a woman walking away from her own funeral, and she handed me the carbon copy of the deed transfer because she couldn't bear to keep it and couldn't bear to throw it away, and I kept it, in a tin box behind Walt's war medals, for thirty years, until last week, when I burned it.

But I'm getting ahead of myself.

The night Liora arrived in town—three weeks ago now, though it feels like the fog from that road has followed me indoors ever since—I was up late. Old women don't sleep; we just take shifts at the window. I heard a vehicle slow down on the pond road around midnight, and I saw the truck, county plates, idling there in the dark with its lights off, and then a figure walking, and then nothing, because the fog did what fog does.

I told Deputy Hale about the fog. Just the fog. Because the last time I'd seen a truck with county plates on that road at night was 1994, and the man driving it was Silas Renner, and Silas Renner had smiled at me through the windshield, and I had understood, in the wordless way women of this town understand things, that I had seen nothing at all.

Thirty years apart, same silence. You'd think I'd have gotten better at it. Instead I'd gotten better at pretending it was a virtue.

* * *

The morning after I read about the brakes, I put on my good coat, the wool one with the brass buttons, the one I wear to funerals and school board meetings, which in Hollow Creek are frequently the same event. I took the suitcase off the bed and put it back in the closet, next to Walt's medals and the empty space where the tin box used to be, and I stood there a moment feeling like a coward in both directions—too scared to run, too scared to stay, which is the exact geometry of a life wasted.

Then I made tea I also didn't drink, and I walked downtown.

Emrys Sterling works out of the old municipal building, in the office that used to be the records room, which I find either very funny or very sad depending on the hour. There's a bell over the door that doesn't ring so much as clear its throat. She was at her desk when I came in, and I want to describe her properly, because everyone in this town has been describing her wrong since she arrived—*the coroner, the recluse, the strange woman who talks to graves.* What I saw was a young woman with her mother's pale hair pulled back too tightly, sitting very straight, with the particular stillness of someone who has learned that moving first gets you noticed.

"Maren Voss," I said. "Retired. Fourth grade. You won't remember me—we've never met."

"I know who you are." Her voice was quiet but it didn't waver. "You taught Ruth Ibbetson's grandson. You spoke at Walt Voss's service. You're the one who reported fog on the pond road the night my sister died."

Not *the victim*. My sister. She'd already claimed her, then, in whatever private ledger she kept. I hadn't known that yet—I wouldn't learn the dental records had come back until later that afternoon—but she said it like a fact already filed.

"May I sit down?"

"Please."

I sat. The chair was hard. Everything in that office was hard except the woman in it, and even she was hard the way ice is hard, which is to say under pressure.

"I'm going to tell you something," I said, "and I need you to let me finish, because if I stop I won't start again. I've been practicing in the mirror since six o'clock and the mirror keeps interrupting me."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment that I was a person, which is more courtesy than I deserved.

"In 1994," I said, "your mother came to me and asked if Aldous Crane was a good man. I told her yes. She was carrying twins, Mrs.—Emrys. Two little girls. And Aldous Crane bought one of them."

The clock on her wall ticked. It was the loudest thing I'd ever heard.

"He paid the medical bills. He paid for the apartment in Cedar Falls. And in exchange she signed papers—deed transfers, mostly land along the mill race that the Crane family had been trying to consolidate for a decade—and gave up one daughter, and swore silence, and the doctor falsified the birth records, and the town closed around it like water. Like your pond." My voice cracked on *pond*, which embarrassed me, so I pressed on faster. "I drove her to sign. I held the carbon copy of the deed for thirty years. I burned it last week because I was frightened, and I would like the record to show that burning it made me feel worse, not better, because apparently I am capable of learning absolutely nothing."

Emrys had not moved. I don't think she breathed. Then, very carefully, like handling evidence: "The signatures. On the deeds. Are they in the county archive?"

"They are. Book ninety-one, pages forty through fifty-six, give or take. I memorized the book number because I memorized everything, it's what guilt does, it turns you into a filing cabinet." I leaned forward. "Edith's signature on those deeds doesn't match her signature anywhere else. I've seen her sign a hundred things—petitions, sympathy cards, the PTA ledger. She wrote a round, careful hand. Those signatures are jagged. Someone practiced them. Someone practiced them *well*, but not well enough, and I always assumed that was deliberate on somebody's part—a lawyer leaving himself deniability—but maybe I'm giving too much credit."

"Silas Renner handled the paperwork," she said. Not a question.

"You know."

"I know the shape of it. I don't have the middle." For the first time she looked tired—not weak, tired, the way a load-bearing wall looks tired. "My mother left a letter. Half a letter. She never finished it. I found the beginning and I've spent weeks imagining the rest, and every version I imagine is kinder to her than the facts probably are."

"The facts aren't kind," I said. "But they're not cruel either, if that helps. She was twenty-three years old, her husband was dead, and a rich man offered her a choice between losing one child and losing two. That's not a bargain. That's a mugging with paperwork." I heard my own voice go hard, thirty years of fourth-grade-parent-conference steel. "Aldous Crane didn't buy a baby, Emrys. He bought leverage. A healthy infant girl was the receipt. Every year Edith stayed quiet, the receipt appreciated. And when your sister came back here three weeks ago asking questions, somebody decided the receipt had finally expired."

The clock ticked. Somewhere outside, a dog was barking at the mail carrier, who has worked this route for nine years and remains, in the dog's considered opinion, an existential threat.

"Why now?" Emrys asked. "Why come to me today? You've had thirty years."

It was a fair question and I flinched from it honestly.

"Because of the brakes," I said. "Because whoever killed Liora isn't finished, and I recognized the method. In 1994, after the signing, a man named Dale Pruitt—he worked the mill, he'd been drinking loudly at the Rusty Anchor about what Edith Sterling was hiding—went off the bridge on Route 9. Single car. No witnesses. Ruled accidental." I watched her face do something complicated and controlled. "Dale Pruitt drowned in four feet of water, Emrys. I've lived beside Miller's Pond my whole life. Four feet of water doesn't drown a grown man unless somebody holds him there or he's already asleep. And everybody who mattered knew it, and everybody who mattered said nothing, and I have been saying nothing so long it's grown into my bones like that stone in the shoe."

I took a breath. This was the hard part, harder than the deeds, harder than the mugging-with-paperwork.

"And because I saw the truck," I said. "Both times. 1994 and three weeks ago. County plates, same model, same dent in the rear fender—I'd know it anywhere, I've dreamed about that dent. And I told your deputy about fog. Twice, if you count the intervening decades as one continuous report of fog. And when I read about your brakes, I understood that the fog was going to close over you next, and that I would be complicit in a third drowning, and that I am seventy-one years old and I do not want to die having spent my retirement as furniture in a crime."

Emrys Sterling looked at me for a long moment. Then she opened a drawer, took out a legal pad, uncapped a pen, and said, in the flattest, gentlest voice I have ever heard from another human being:

"Start with the night in October. Everything you saw. Don't organize it—just say it. We'll organize it together after."

And I did. I talked for two hours. I told her about the dent in the fender, about Dale Pruitt, about the tin box and the burned letter and the shape of Edith's handwriting, about the gymnasium floor and the hospital board and the way Aldous Crane used to attend the spring recital and clap exactly twice for each child, evenly, like a metronome, like a man auditing joy. She wrote it all down without once looking at me the way people usually look at confessors—with pity, or relief that the sin isn't theirs. She looked at me the way I imagine she looks at the dead: with complete attention and no judgment, which is either the coldest kindness in the world or the warmest, and I've decided it's the second.

Near the end, my voice gave out entirely, and she poured me water from a pitcher on the filing cabinet, and I drank it, and it was the best water I've ever tasted, which tells you something about what confession does to the mouth.

When I finished, she sat back and was quiet for a moment, and then she said, "Mrs. Voss. Maren. If this goes where I think it goes, you'll be a witness. Publicly. Under oath. There will be no fog left to hide in—for either of us."

"I know," I said. "That's why I brought the good coat."

She almost smiled. Almost. It was like watching frost form.

"One more thing," I said, gathering my purse, my cane, my remaining dignity. "The deed transfers. When you pull book ninety-one—and you will, tonight, I can see it in your face—check the dates against the hospital records. Check when the second birth certificate was filed, and who countersigned it. Because there's a name on those documents that isn't Aldous Crane's, and it isn't mine, and it isn't Edith's, and I have spent thirty years trying to make it mean less than it does."

"What name?"

"Renner," I said. "Silas Renner. He didn't just handle the paperwork, Emrys. He drafted it. He witnessed it. And he was the one driving the truck."

I got up to leave, and at the door I turned around, because there was one more thing, the smallest thing, the thing that had actually gotten me out of bed past the suitcase.

"Your mother," I said, "came back to my kitchen the night before she left town for good. After everything was signed. She stood where you're standing and she said, 'Tell whichever one survives that I loved them both.' And I said I would. And then I didn't. Because telling felt like confessing, and I wasn't ready to be guilty, only to be quiet."

Emrys stood very still behind her desk, pen capped, pad full, her mother's face looking back at me across thirty years of damage.

"So consider yourself told," I said. "Thirty years late. Both of you."

The bell over the door cleared its throat as I left, and behind me I heard her pick up the phone, and I heard her say, in that flat, gentle, terrible voice: "Deputy Hale. I need you to pull a deed book from the county archive. Tonight."

I walked home in the good coat, lighter by one secret and heavier by one witness, and I did not burn anything on the way.
