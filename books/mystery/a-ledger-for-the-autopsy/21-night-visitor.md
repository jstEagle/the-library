## 21. Night Visitor

There are three kinds of nights in Hollow Creek, and I've catalogued them the way other men catalogue fishing lures or grudges. There's the quiet kind, which is most of them, where the loudest thing in town is the ice machine at the Gas-N-Go deciding whether to live or die. There's the drunk kind, which is quieter than you'd think, because our drunks are sentimental and mostly cry at their own kitchen tables. And then there's the kind I got on a Tuesday in October, which doesn't have a name yet, but if you pressed me I'd say it was the kind where you learn something about yourself that you'd have been happier not knowing.

It started with paperwork, which is how all my sins start.

I want to be clear about that up front, because later—when Emrys Sterling found out what I'd done, or rather what I'd failed to do—I needed the whole ugly chain of causation laid out like evidence on a steel table. Paperwork led me to the file cabinet. The file cabinet led me to 1994. And 1994 led me to standing alone in the coroner's office at eleven-forty at night like an idiot with a flashlight and a guilty conscience, about to get introduced to the floor.

But let's do this in order. Coroners love order. It's contagious.

* * *

The discrepancy had been sitting in my inbox for two weeks, wearing a Post-it note in my own handwriting: *ASK E. ABOUT CLOTHING.* I'm not a lazy man. I want that on the record too. But asking Emrys Sterling about anything required a certain approach, the way you'd approach a deer that hadn't decided yet whether you were a threat or just weather. She answered questions the way a well answers buckets—eventually, and only as much as you lowered down.

The question I couldn't lower anymore was this: the Jane Doe pulled from Miller's Pond had been wearing clothes. Not generic clothes. Specific clothes. Gray cardigan, leather boots broken in along the left instep, a wool scarf with a moth hole near the fringe. I knew this because I'd stood in the morgue while Emrys photographed every item before bagging it, and I'd watched her hands go very still over the cardigan, and I'd told myself it was professional detachment, because telling myself anything else would have meant having a conversation I wasn't ready for.

Then the dental records came back and the Jane Doe became Liora Sterling, born the same day as our coroner, same mother, thirty years apart from each other by exactly zero minutes—and Emrys signed nothing for a night and a day, and when she finally did sign, her signature looked like it had been written by someone else using her hand as a glove.

And here's the thing that kept me up: there was no report on the clothing. No entry connecting the victim's effects to any residence, any owner, any theft. In a county where a stolen bicycle gets three paragraphs, a dead woman dressed out of somebody's locked closet got zero. Either Emrys had missed it, which I didn't believe, or she'd buried it, which I did.

So I started pulling old files. Quietly. After hours. The way you check whether your spouse is lying to you—not because you want to find something, but because not looking is its own kind of lie.

That's how I found the 1994 drowning.

Miller's Pond again. A man named Thomas Vane, forty-one, ruled accidental, signed off by a coroner named Pruitt who retired to Florida the following spring and died there fat and happy, according to the obituary I looked up at two in the morning like a man scratching a mosquito bite. The file was thin. Thinner than thin. Photographs missing from their sleeves—the sleeves were there, acid-free, archival, empty. A witness statement from one Maren Voss, retired schoolteacher, describing fog. Just fog. Three pages of fog, which even for Maren—who could describe fog the way other people describe grandchildren—struck me as a lot of fog.

And a transfer of land, referenced in a margin note in Pruitt's handwriting, that I couldn't find anywhere in the county records. As though someone had reached into the file and lifted it out with tweezers.

I sat with that file open on my desk for a long time, listening to the radiator tick, and I thought about Emrys's face over the cardigan, and I thought about a woman drowned in the same pond twenty-eight years apart, and I thought: *these two things are the same thing.*

Which is when I heard the sound from across the hall.

* * *

Here's what you need to know about the municipal building after ten p.m.: it sounds like a ship. Pipes knock, the boiler groans through its dreams, and the old floors talk to each other in a language of settling wood. I know every voice that building has. So when I heard the coroner's office door open—slow, controlled, the hinge eased rather than swung—I didn't think *raccoon*. Raccoons don't ease doors. Raccoons are anarchists; they throw themselves at things.

I didn't think *Emrys*, either. She kept her own hours, strange ones, but she never came in without her headlights sweeping the lot first, a habit I'd clocked months ago and filed under *coroners are weird* instead of under *coroners are afraid of something*, which in retrospect was the correct drawer.

So I turned off my desk lamp, took the 1994 file with me because I am apparently incapable of leaving evidence lying around even when sneaking, and crossed the hall in my sock feet like a man in a bad play.

The office door stood open six inches. Inside, a flashlight moved in small, methodical arcs—not searching, *inventorying*. That distinction matters. A searcher looks for one thing. An inventoryist looks at everything, because they don't know yet what will matter later. I've inventoryisted myself, plenty of times, and I recognized the rhythm the way you recognize a walk through a wall.

I should have called dispatch. I want that noted too. I had a radio on my belt and a phone in my pocket and a perfectly good doorframe to stand behind, and instead I pushed the door open and said, "Office is closed," in my best daytime voice, because apparently my survival instinct had taken early retirement to Florida with Coroner Pruitt.

The flashlight swung toward me and then away, deliberately, and a shape crossed between me and the window—big, unhurried, moving the way large men move when they've done this before—and then the world went white at the edges and sideways at the middle.

I want to describe getting hit from behind in a way that does it justice, but the honest description is: there was a sound like a car door closing, and then there was carpet, and the carpet was very interested in my face. Somewhere above me, drawers opened and closed. Paper rustled. A zipper, long and patient. At some point I thought, with tremendous clarity, *he's taking the files, he's taking all of them,* and I tried to say so, and what came out was a sound like a bathtub draining.

Then footsteps. Unhurried. The door eased shut the way it had eased open. A vehicle started in the lot—diesel, heavy, no hurry—and faded toward the pond road, because everything in this town eventually fades toward the pond road.

I lay on that carpet for—I don't know. Four minutes. Ten. Long enough to conduct a full inventory of my own shortcomings, which took considerably less time than you'd think, because I keep the list current.

Then I got up, checked that my teeth were all still employed, and looked at the filing cabinet.

The bottom drawer hung open. The 1994 folder was gone from my desk where I'd left it—no, wait. Let me be accurate, since accuracy is the only friend I've got some nights. I'd carried the file across the hall. It wasn't in my hand when I woke up on the carpet. My hands had been empty. Which meant somewhere between the doorway and the floor, I'd dropped it, and whoever inventoried this room had found it, recognized it, and taken it.

Every other folder was there. Every report, every ruling, every dusty piece of Hollow Creek's official memory—untouched. He'd taken exactly one file. Mine. The one I'd carried into the room like a man bringing his own ransom.

I stood there holding my jaw and understanding, with the special clarity available only to the recently concussed, that I had just become a witness to a crime I couldn't report without confessing three smaller crimes of my own: snooping after hours, withholding suspicions from my superior officer—which was Emrys, God help us both—and getting knocked flat by an intruder I never saw, which would make me either a laughingstock or a suspect, depending on the mood of the county board.

So I did what men in this town have always done with inconvenient knowledge.

I put it in my pocket and said nothing.

* * *

The next morning I told Emrys there'd been a break-in attempt at the Gas-N-Go, which was even true—somebody had jimmied the ice machine bay, probably kids—and watched her nod and go back to her reports, and felt the lie sit in my stomach like a swallowed coin.

She looked tired. She'd been looking tired for weeks, but that morning there was something new in it, a flinching quality, like a woman walking past windows she didn't trust. Later I'd understand she'd already gotten her first anonymous note by then, the politely worded kind, the kind that reads like a lawyer wrote it after a bath. We were both being threatened, it turns out, in our different dialects—hers in prose, mine in blunt force trauma—and neither of us said a word, two deputies of the same secret sheriffing separate counties.

I worked the day. I took a statement about a mailbox. I ate lunch at the diner and listened to Dot Kessler explain her nephew's custody situation in the tone other women reserve for opera. And all day, under everything, ran the same cold current: *he came for the file. He knew the file existed. He knew where it was, tonight, now.*

Which meant he was watching the building. Which meant he might be watching me.

I went home. I slept four hours in the chair with a bag of frozen peas against my skull, because I wasn't going to any doctor who might ask how, and I woke at dawn with the peas thawed and my conscience unchanged, and I made coffee, and I sat at my kitchen table with a legal pad, and I did the thing I should have done in the first place.

I started my own investigation. Off the books. Out from under the roof that had already been searched once.

Here's what I had, and it wasn't much, but I wrote it all down anyway, because a list is a weapon you can hold:

One. Two drownings, Miller's Pond, 1994 and now. Same pond, twenty-eight years apart, which is not a coincidence, it's a habit.

Two. A witness statement consisting entirely of fog, from a woman who I happened to know had lived on the pond road her whole life and missed nothing, ever, including the year I grew sideburns, which she mentioned to me weekly for a decade.

Three. A land transfer referenced in a dead coroner's margin and missing from county records, connected to a family whose name is on half the buildings in town, including, I noted with interest, the building where I'd recently been introduced to the carpet.

Four. A dead woman wearing a living woman's clothes, and a coroner who photographed those clothes like a woman photographing her own reflection before it could be stolen.

Five. A diesel truck, heavy, unhurried, gone toward the pond road at approximately 11:52 p.m., driven by a man whose flashlight discipline suggested either law enforcement or law enforcement's lawyer.

I looked at that last item for a long time. Because here's the thing about Silas Renner—I'd known him since I was a kid, since he'd handled my grandmother's estate with such gentle competence that she'd left him five hundred dollars in her will out of pure gratitude. Renner was unfailingly courteous. Renner remembered birthdays. Renner had once helped me jump my truck outside the courthouse and refused gas money with a wave, saying, "Consider it a retainer, Deputy," and we'd both laughed, and I'd thought about that laugh maybe four thousand times since Tuesday night.

Big man. Diesel truck. Moves like he's done this before. Knows where files sleep.

I didn't write his name on the pad. Writing it felt like signing something. But I underlined item five twice, which in my private system means the same thing.

* * *

For three days I worked parallel to Emrys, close enough to feel her heat, far enough to keep my secret. It was the loneliest work I've ever done. Every morning I'd see her cross the parking lot with her thermos and her terrible posture, and every morning I'd think, *tell her, you coward, tell her someone was in her office, tell her someone is hunting the same trail you're hunting and is ahead of you,* and every morning the coin in my stomach would turn over and I'd swallow it again.

Because here's what I couldn't get past: if I told her, I'd have to tell her why I was there at midnight. I'd have to admit I'd gone digging into her dead sister's case behind her back, suspicious of her, of *her*, of the one person in this town more alone than I am. And worse—much worse—I'd have to admit that my midnight snooping was what drew the intruder. That the file was safe until I picked it up. That the 1994 folder might have slept another twenty-eight years in that cabinet if I hadn't carried it across the hall like a torch through dry grass.

He came for the file. But I rang the dinner bell.

On the fourth day, Emrys ordered the pond dragged.

I was there when the divers went in, standing at the edge of Miller's Pond in the gray light, watching the water give up its pockets. And when they brought up the satchel—Liora's satchel, swollen with adoption papers and photographs—and Emrys knelt on the bank spreading those photographs out with wet hands, and I saw what was in them, I forgot every resolution I owned.

Aldous Crane. At the boathouse. Recently. Meeting someone whose face was angled away, big-shouldered, unhurried, familiar in the way a shape is familiar before you name it.

Emrys looked up at me across the photographs, and her face did something complicated—grief and fury and a question—and I realized she was about to ask me something, and I realized I was about to either lie to her again or not, and the coin turned over one more time.

"Deputy Hale," she said, "when you pulled the old files—"

And I swear to God, before I could decide which man I was going to be, she stopped herself. Shook her head. Gathered the photographs like cards.

"Forget it," she said. "I'll do this part myself."

She walked back toward her truck, and I stood at the edge of the pond watching her go, holding my secret in one pocket and my guilt in the other, both of them heavier than the file had ever been.

That night I went home and uncapped a pen and wrote, at the top of a fresh page, in letters I finally let myself make real:

*SILAS RENNER.*

Then, underneath it, because I am a deputy sheriff of Hollow Creek and honesty has to start somewhere, even if it starts small and late and alone:

*Tell her tomorrow. Tell her all of it. Tomorrow.*

Tomorrow, I told myself, is a promise I intend to keep.

Tomorrow, I said, the way men in this town have been saying it for thirty years.
