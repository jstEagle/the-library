## 35. What the Dead Are Owed

The form was a single page, and it had taken thirty years and one murder to fill out.

I sat in my office at nine in the morning with the amended death certificate in front of me, the county's cheap ballpoint in my hand, and I will confess that for a long moment I did nothing at all except look at the line marked *Cause of Death*. The original said *Asphyxia due to drowning — pending investigation*, which is the bureaucratic equivalent of shrugging. Below it, in the space reserved for *Manner*, someone had penciled *accidental* in a hand that wasn't mine, because I had refused to pencil it myself, and the board had grown tired of waiting.

I erased the pencil mark with an eraser I keep for exactly this purpose—the correction of other people's conclusions—and wrote, in careful block letters that would survive any photocopy: **HOMICIDE**.

Then, on the line for decedent's name, where *Jane Doe* had lived for five weeks like a squatter, I wrote:

**LIORA MARGARET STERLING.**

My pen hovered over the date of birth. It was the same as mine. It would always be the same as mine. I wrote it anyway, and the two dates sat side by side on the paper like strangers who'd just discovered they were wearing the same coat.

There is a version of me—I've met her; she lives in the part of the night between three and four—that wanted to make a ceremony of it. To light a candle, say something formal to the empty office, give the moment its due weight. But the truth is that paperwork is not a ceremony. Paperwork is a shovel. You don't consecrate a grave with it; you dig, and later, if you're lucky, someone plants something on top.

I signed. I stamped. I filed the original with the county clerk by hand, because I no longer trusted anything in this town to travel through the mail or the network without being read by eyes I hadn't authorized. The clerk, a young woman named Dotty who had worked there eleven years and seen everything twice, looked at the amendment and then at me.

"Sterling," she said. "Same as you."

"Same as me."

"Twins?"

"Twins."

She nodded slowly, the way people do when a town rearranges itself around a new fact and they can feel the furniture moving. "The Crane trial's got everyone talking about it," she said. "Half of them knew your mother. The other half are pretending they did."

"That sounds about right."

"You want a copy certified?"

"I want six."

She raised her eyebrows but didn't ask. That's one of the reasons I liked Dotty. In Hollow Creek, curiosity is currency, and she had apparently decided years ago to live within her means.

* * *

The funeral home was mine, technically—or rather, it had been my mother's, and then mine by inheritance, which is how most things in Hollow Creek change hands: not through purchase but through attrition. Edith Sterling had run it alone for twenty-two years after arriving here with one infant and no history, and she had run it well, in the way of people who have something to prove to a town that never asked.

I went down into the preparation room that afternoon, not because Liora needed anything—she had been released to my custody weeks ago, resting in the cooler with the patience of the dead, who are the only ones in this county who never file complaints—but because I needed to stand in a room where my hands knew what to do while my head did something harder.

Grief, I have learned, is a lot like rigor mortis. It sets in on a schedule whether you invite it or not, it passes through stages you cannot rush, and if you try to force the body past it, something tears.

For five weeks I had kept Liora on ice the way I kept everything: sealed, labeled, deferred. There had been too much to do. Renner to catch, ledgers to find, state troopers to brief, a trap to bait at Miller's Pond with my own self as the worm. Grief had waited its turn in the anteroom of my attention like a well-mannered mourner, and now the arrest was made, the ledger was in evidence, Silas Renner was in a cell in the county seat writing polite letters to his lawyer, and the anteroom door had blown open.

I uncovered her face and looked at my own face looking back at me.

People assume that would be the worst of it—seeing yourself dead on a table. It wasn't. What was worst was that she wasn't me. Thirty years of different weather had fallen on us. Her hair was cut shorter, her hands were softer—she'd been a bookkeeper in Spokane, the journal said, twelve years at a firm that folded—and there was a small scar above her left eyebrow from a childhood I had not been present for. A bike accident, probably. A fall off something. Some foster parent or other bandaging a stranger's child and never knowing they were holding half of a pair.

"Hello," I said to her, because the room was private and the dead don't repeat gossip. "Sorry it took so long. I'm slow with correspondence. Ask anyone."

Her face said nothing, graciously.

I washed her myself. I want to be clear about why: not sentiment, though there was that, but jurisdiction. No one else was going to touch my sister. Not an assistant I didn't have, not a contract mortician from Carver County, not anyone who might handle her with the brisk impersonality the job requires and deserves in every other case. This once, professionalism could stand aside at the door and let the family in. I was the family. Both halves of it, as it turned out.

I dressed her in clothes I bought new, because everything she'd arrived in was evidence and would remain evidence until the trial concluded sometime next spring. A plain blue dress. The gray cardigan I did not return to her—it was hers originally, stolen from my closet, but wearing it felt like a costume of me, and she had spent her whole life being mistaken for things she wasn't. She got her own color.

When I finished, I stood back and found that my eyes were wet, which annoyed me on principle. I am a coroner. I have a professional relationship with tears and we keep our interactions strictly business. But grief doesn't check credentials at the door either.

"There," I said. "That's better. You look like somebody's sister."

* * *

The plot beside my mother was empty, and had been empty for eleven years, and I had told myself for eleven years that it was empty because Edith Sterling had purchased a double plot for practical reasons—funeral directors think in doubles, the way bakers think in dozens.

Standing at the graveside that evening with a surveyor's stake in my hand, I understood that she had bought it for the same reason she'd kept the unfinished letter in her desk drawer for thirty years: hope and guilt, sharing a duplex.

I drove the stake myself. The ground was soft from October rain, and the mallet rang against the wood in the flat evening air, and behind me a car slowed on the cemetery road, hesitated, and moved on. Word travels fast in Hollow Creek. By morning everyone would know the coroner was burying her twin beside their mother, and by noon they'd have opinions about it, and by supper those opinions would be served at every table in town alongside the meatloaf.

Let them eat. I was done starving quietly.

Maren Voss found me there just before dark. She came up the hill with the careful steps of a woman in her seventies who has recently decided that fear is a worse companion than gravity, carrying a paper bag.

"I brought sandwiches," she said. "You haven't eaten today. Don't argue; I taught fourth grade for thirty-one years, I can hear an empty stomach lying from forty feet."

"Maren—"

"Egg salad. Sit down."

I sat down on my mother's headstone, which she would have objected to and which is therefore exactly why I chose it, and Maren settled onto the neighboring slab of old Aldous Crane's father—a man whose crimes, per the ledger, included two drownings in 1961 that the pond had obligingly forgotten—and handed me a sandwich wrapped in wax paper.

We ate in silence for a while. Crows conducted their evening business in the pines.

"The service is Saturday," I said finally. "Ten o'clock. Pastor Lindqvist agreed to do it, though he's never met her. I gave him the journal to read. He cried at the part where she writes about watching my house." I paused. "Everyone cries at that part. I'm the only one who hasn't yet, and I'm starting to suspect I'm saving it up."

"Saving up is a family trait, maybe," Maren said gently.

"Maybe."

"I'm speaking at the service. If that's all right. I talked to Pastor Lindqvist." She smoothed the wax paper flat on her knee, over and over, a small machine of nerves. "Thirty years I carried what I saw in 1994. Thirty years, Emrys. And then five more weeks carrying what I saw on the pond road. I'm done being a vault. Vaults are for banks and cemeteries, and I'm neither."

"You could be sued," I said. "Renner's lawyer will be fishing for inconsistencies. Anything you say publicly—"

"Will match what I tell the state police, because I'll be telling the truth, and the truth doesn't have inconsistencies. That's its whole charm." She looked at me with the level gaze of a woman who had spent decades watching children lie about homework and had developed an instrument for detecting falsehood calibrated to surgical tolerances. "Your mother begged Aldous Crane in my classroom, after hours, thinking I'd gone home. I heard every word through the supply closet door. I was twenty-six years old and scared of a man with land and lawyers, and I stayed in that closet until they left, and then I went home and kissed my husband and put it away like Christmas decorations. Every year it got easier to leave in the box. Until a woman with your face floated up out of Miller's Pond and the box wouldn't close anymore."

I didn't say anything. There was nothing to say that wouldn't have been either absolution or judgment, and she deserved neither from me. Absolution wasn't mine to give—Liora's, maybe, posthumously, and I suspected Liora, who had written in her journal about wanting to see me *once, just once, alive and breathing*, would have been the more generous twin. Judgment was the court's.

So instead I said: "Saturday, then. Ten o'clock. Wear something warm; the wind comes off the ridge."

Maren nodded and gathered her bag, and then stopped, halfway standing, and looked at the stake I'd driven beside my mother's stone.

"She'd have wanted that," Maren said. "Edith. Whatever else she did—whatever she traded—she used to come up here on Sundays and pull weeds off both plots. I saw her once, from the road. Thought she was tending a husband. Never occurred to me she was keeping a place ready for a daughter she'd given away."

"It occurs to me now," I said.

"Yes," said Maren. "I expect it does."

* * *

That night I went home—to my house, the quiet one with the locked closet and the window Liora had studied for days before climbing through it—and I did a thing I had been avoiding since the arrest.

I opened her journal to the last written page.

I had read it before, of course, in the course of the investigation, catalogued and photographed and entered into evidence. But reading a document as an investigator and reading it as a sister are two different procedures, performed on the same tissue, yielding entirely different findings.

The last entry was dated the day before she died. It was short. Investigators' hearts sink at short final entries; sisters understand them completely.

*Saw her at the cemetery today. She talks to the stones. She knows all their names—I watched her lips move, going down the rows like a teacher taking attendance. I used to think I wanted to knock on her door and say* I'm your sister. *Now I think what I really want is to be one of her stones. To have someone walk down the rows saying my name because I matter enough to be counted.*

*I took the cardigan from her line. I know that's wrong. I wanted to know what it felt like to be warm the way she's warm.*

*Tomorrow I meet the lawyer. He says Mr. Crane wants to make things right. I don't believe him. But I believe the check is real, and I believe if I take it, I can stop being a ghost.*

*I don't want to stop being a ghost anymore. I want to be counted.*

I closed the journal and sat in my kitchen with the lights on—all of them; old habits of vigilance die slower than old habits of silence—and I let the thing happen that I had been postponing for six weeks.

I cried.

Not elegantly. Not the single sanctioned tear of the dignified professional. I cried the way you cry when a wall you built at nineteen comes down all at once instead of brick by brick, which is to say messily, loudly, alone, with a glass of water going warm on the counter because you forgot you poured it. I cried for the bike-accident scar I'd never gotten to ask about. For the twelve years of ledgers in Spokane, columns of numbers balanced by a woman who was herself an unbalanced column. For a mother who had chosen between her children with a gun to her head made of medical bills and a patriarch's mercy, and who had then spent thirty years pulling weeds off an empty grave because the grave was the only apology she was permitted to make.

And underneath all of it, the thing I'd least wanted to look at: I cried because some small, shameful part of me had resented her. Had flinched at the theft of the cardigan, the invasion of the closet, the mirror walking around town with someone else's gait. Had thought, in the ugly cellar of the heart, *why couldn't she have stayed a stranger?*

She had stayed a stranger her whole life. That was the entire tragedy. She had knocked on no doors, made no claims, demanded nothing but a look—and even that she took from across the street, uninvited, apologetic, a trespasser in her own reflection.

When it was over—I won't say finished; grief doesn't finish, it just changes shifts—I washed my face, and I took down the photograph I'd printed from her effects, the only one she'd owned of herself, a DMV-quality thing against a blue screen, and I hung it on the kitchen wall beside my mother's letter, which I had framed, because a confession that names the price of a town deserves a frame even if forgiveness remains under negotiation.

Two faces. One of them mine, mostly. One of them my mother's, in handwriting that pressed hard enough to emboss the page.

"Counted," I said to the kitchen. "Both of you. Starting Saturday."

* * *

Friday I spent at the county courthouse with Hale, who arrived at my office at eight sharp looking like a man who had slept in his truck and was not sorry about it.

"State's attorney wants to prep you for trial," he said. "Renner's pleading not guilty, obviously. His lawyer's already filed motions to suppress the ledger—chain of custody, the receiving vault search, all of it."

"The receiving vault search was witnessed by two troopers and documented in triplicate."

"Sure. And their motion is garbage. But garbage takes months to haul, and meanwhile they'll drag your name through everything. Your mother, the forged deeds, the fact that you signed an accidental ruling under pressure before flipping it." He turned his coffee cup a quarter-turn on my desk, a habit of his I'd started finding less annoying than reassuring. "They'll say you amended the ruling out of grief. That you're not a credible coroner, you're a grieving twin with a badge."

"They'd be half right," I said. "I am a grieving twin. The badge, however, was doing its job the whole time. Grief doesn't invalidate toxicology. Sedatives in the blood don't care how I feel about them."

Hale almost smiled. "You want me in the courtroom?"

"I want you doing what you've been doing. Pulling threads. Renner didn't work alone for thirty years—not the way he worked. Somebody processed those payments. Somebody at the bank looked the other way. Somebody at the county recorded those deeds without blinking." I tapped the amended certificate copy on my desk. "One man goes to trial. A system stays open for business. I'd like to know which it is."

"You always think in systems."

"I think in bodies. Systems are just bodies arranged badly."

He left, and I sat for a while with the certificate, and then I did the last piece of the day's work, the piece that mattered more than all the motions and filings and pretrial maneuvering combined.

I opened the big register—the old leather-bound one my mother had kept, the one where every burial in Hollow Creek for forty years was recorded in her upright, unforgiving hand—and I turned to the current page, and I wrote:

*Sterling, Liora Margaret. Born [date], Spokane, Washington. Died [date], Hollow Creek. Daughter of Edith Sterling. Twin sister of Emrys Sterling. Interred beside her mother.*

Under *Cause of Death*, in the register where my mother had always written the clinical truth and nothing else, I wrote *Homicide*, and beneath it, because the register was mine now and the rules were mine to extend: *Named at last.*

Then I blotted the ink, closed the book, and sat in the lamplight of the office where my mother had sat for twenty-two years, listening to the building settle around me the way old buildings do—like large animals getting comfortable.

Outside, far down the valley, the lights of the Crane estate burned in their usual constellation. Aldous Crane was in hospice now, the nurses coming and going in shifts, the empire he'd built on drowned men and traded infants entering its own final accounting. He had told me, in that room that smelled of lavender and decay, that my mother understood some children were the price of a town. On Saturday, that town was going to gather in my cemetery and watch me refuse the exchange rate.

I checked the locks. I left the lights on.

And in the morning, when the phone rang at seven-fifteen—an unfamiliar number, the area code local, the voice asking for *the coroner* in a tone I had learned, over these weeks, to distrust—I answered on the second ring, awake, named, and counting.

"This is Emrys Sterling," I said. "Who's calling?"
