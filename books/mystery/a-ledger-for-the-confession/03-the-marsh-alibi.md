## 3. The Marsh Alibi

The county records office smelled of toner and old paper, and Bram Blackwood had spent enough of her life in rooms like it to know which smell lied. Toner lied constantly. Paper, if you were patient, told the truth.

She spread the 1995 file across a scarred oak table and let Thomas Voss assemble himself from fragments. A fishing license, renewed every spring for eleven years and then never again. A marriage certificate — Voss, Thomas Alan, to Marsh, Delia Anne, 1981, dissolved 1993. A tax record that ended mid-year, the way tax records do when the man paying them stops needing to pay them. And finally, the death certificate itself, signed by her predecessor, Coroner Aldous Prewitt: *accidental drowning, Blackwater Bend, October 14, 1995. Blood alcohol 0.19.*

Bram read the blood alcohol number twice. It wasn't impossible. Men drank on the riverbank in Hollow Creek the way they breathed — without thinking about it, without stopping. But she'd pulled three bodies out of water in her career, and every one of them had taught her the same lesson: rivers keep what they're given, and they don't give receipts.

*Accidental.* The word sat on the page with the confidence of something nobody had ever bothered to question.

She turned to the witness statements. There were four. Three of them were from men whose names she knew the way she knew the cemetery — Hollis Crane, Ambrose Tate, Silas Wren. Founding families. The kind of names that got carved into the dedication plaque at the town hall and the lintels of buildings that predated the county itself. Each statement was a paragraph long. Each said, in slightly different words, that they had been together at the Grange Hall that night, playing cards, and had seen nothing, heard nothing, and could offer nothing.

The fourth statement stopped her hand flat against the page.

*Elias Marsh, son of deceased. States he was at the Voss property assisting his father with winterizing the boat shed until approximately 9 p.m., at which time he departed for his mother's residence in Cedar Falls. Returned the following morning to find his father's truck still at the bend. First to report the body.*

No alibi after nine o'clock. No corroboration at all — except that the three founding families, in their identical little paragraphs, had each added a single sentence vouching for him. *Young Marsh is known to us. He would have no cause. He was a boy who kept to himself.*

Three men who saw nothing had seen fit to swear to the character of a nineteen-year-old boy none of them had any reason to know.

Bram sat back in the creaking chair and let the arithmetic of it settle. In thirty years of coroner's work — ten of them hers, twenty of them Prewitt's — she had learned that an alibi was rarely a wall. It was usually a door, and the interesting question was never whether it locked, but who held the key.

Someone had handed Elias Marsh an alibi he hadn't earned, wrapped it in the names of the most untouchable men in the county, and filed it away like a deed.

And now a letter sat in her desk drawer at home, postmarked the day after Ada Voss's funeral, daring her to open that door before the law closed it forever.

She photocopied everything, paid the clerk in exact change, and asked, as casually as she could manage, "Whatever happened to the Marsh family? Delia?"

The clerk, a soft-bodied man named Ferro who had worked the counter since before Bram arrived in Hollow Creek, didn't look up from his screen. "Moved away after. The boy stayed on a while, then went too. Came back maybe eight years ago." He paused, and something flickered across his face — the particular hesitation of a small-town man deciding how much a stranger should be told. "Keeps to himself. Lives up past the old orchard road. Does carpentry, boat repair. Folks say he's odd."

"Folks say that about anyone who keeps to themselves," Bram said.

Ferro almost smiled. "Then this county's full of odd folks."

* * *

The orchard road climbed out of town through a corridor of bare maples, their branches interlacing overhead like ribs. Bram drove with the window cracked despite the cold, because the letter was in her coat pocket and she wanted to feel its weight against her ribs while she did this. She had read it so many times the creases were going soft.

*I drowned him the way the river drowns — by standing there. Ask your questions before the clock runs out, Coroner. Thirty years is nearly up. — A debtor.*

Ask your questions. Fine. She was asking.

The house appeared where the orchard thinned: a low cedar-shake place with a boat shed behind it, large enough for two hulls, one of them up on blocks with its belly opened to the sky. Woodsmoke stood straight up from the chimney, unhurried. A man was splitting wood in the side yard, and Bram watched him for a moment from the car before she got out, because watching people before they knew they were watched was the only honest data collection left in the world.

He was perhaps fifty, built like the work he did — not gym-built but *used*, forearms corded, shoulders carrying the permanent set of a man who hauled hulls alone. Dark hair going gray at the temples without apology. He swung the maul with an economy that made the wood give up without drama, split halves flying, and Bram thought, absurdly, that he moved the way she imagined Thomas Voss must have moved, because sons absorb their fathers' bodies even when they hate them.

She got out. The gravel announced her, because gravel always does, and he turned with the maul still in his hands.

"Mr. Marsh? Elias Marsh?"

His eyes did the thing she'd learned to expect from people in Hollow Creek when they saw her face — a half-second of cataloguing, *not from here*, followed by the polite blankness the town wore like a uniform. But then it shifted. Something sharper came into his gaze, and it took her a moment to identify it, because she almost never saw it directed at her.

Recognition. Not of her name. Of her *function*.

"You're the coroner," he said. His voice was low, roughened at the edges, the voice of a man who talked mostly to wood and water. "Blackwood."

"You know me?"

"I know of you." He set the maul against the chopping block, deliberately, the way a man puts down a weapon to show he isn't reaching for one. Or to show he could pick it back up. "You signed my aunt's death certificate last week. Ada Voss."

"I did."

"Heart failure," he said. It wasn't a question, but it wasn't agreement either. It was a door held open an inch, waiting to see what she'd push through it.

"That's what the evidence supported," Bram said carefully.

Something moved behind his eyes — disappointment or relief, she couldn't tell which, and the not-knowing snagged in her chest like a fishhook. Most people, shown her credentials, performed relief. This man looked at her the way she looked at drowning victims' lungs. Like he was checking her for water.

"What can I do for the county?" he asked.

"I'm reviewing some older files. Routine." The lie tasted like copper; she was bad at lying and always had been, which was why she'd built a life around documents instead of conversation. "Thomas Voss. Your father. I understand you found him."

The change in him was immediate and total, the way weather changes on open water. The roughness in his face didn't harden — it *emptied*. Thirty years fell off him and then thirty more settled on, all in the space of a breath.

"Routine," he repeated. "Thirty years later."

"The statute of limitations on—"

"On what?" He came down the slope toward her, gravel crunching, and up close he was taller than he'd seemed, and warmer, heat coming off him from the work, woodsmoke and sweat and cedar. She held her ground and hated how aware she was of holding it. "There's no crime in my file, Ms. Blackwood. My father drowned. Drunk, alone, at a bend he fished every week of his life. That's not a mystery. That's a Tuesday."

"Your file," she said. "Not the county's."

He stopped three feet from her. Close enough that she could see the scar through his left eyebrow, old and white, and the exhaustion underneath the anger, worn smooth like river stone from decades of handling.

"Why are you here?" he asked quietly. "Really."

And Bram, who had spent ten years being invisible in this town, who had perfected the art of asking questions without seeming to ask them, heard herself say the true thing instead.

"Because someone sent me a letter confessing to your father's death. Postmarked Hollow Creek. The day after we buried Ada Voss."

The silence that followed had texture. The wind moved through the bare orchard, and somewhere down the hill a crow said one flat syllable and thought better of it.

Elias Marsh didn't look surprised. That was the first thing she understood, and it landed in her stomach like a dropped stone. He looked like a man hearing a sentence he'd been braced for since 1995.

"Describe the handwriting," he said.

"Slanted right. Heavy pressure. The letters are careful — someone who learned penmanship properly and never lost it. Old-fashioned loops on the capitals." She stopped. "Why?"

"Because my grandmother taught school for forty years," he said, "and she taught everyone in this valley to write exactly like that." He turned away from her, looked at the boat shed, at the river invisible beyond it, and when he spoke again his voice had gone somewhere she couldn't follow. "Ada wrote to me once a year. Every Christmas. Same hand. Last one came two weeks before she died. I haven't opened it yet. It's sitting on my kitchen table right now."

Bram felt the case tilt beneath her feet, the whole geography of it shifting, the dead woman stepping out of her grave and into the room between them.

"Mr. Marsh," she said, "I need to see that letter."

He looked back at her, and for one unguarded second the suspicion dropped and she saw what was under it — grief, yes, but also something that recognized her, coroner to coroner, keeper of other people's worst days. Something that made her pulse behave badly and her professional distance feel like a coat that no longer fit.

"You'll tell me everything you find," he said. "Not the county. Me. First."

"That's not how—"

"It's exactly how it's going to be, or that letter stays sealed." He held out his hand, not for a shake — palm up, open, an offer or a dare. "You want the truth, Ms. Blackwood? Come get it. But you don't get to carry it alone. Nobody carries this alone. That's how it stayed buried thirty years."

Bram looked at his open hand and understood she was standing on a threshold with two doors in it — duty on one side, and something with no name yet on the other — and that whichever one she walked through, she would not come back the same.

She reached into her coat pocket instead and drew out the confession letter, the paper gone soft at the folds.

"Then start by reading this," she said. "I think your aunt wrote it."

Elias took it, and as his eyes moved down the page, all the color left his face like water finding a crack — and he said, in a voice she would remember for the rest of her life:

"This isn't a confession. It's a list. And your name is already on it."
