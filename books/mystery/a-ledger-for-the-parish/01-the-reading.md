## 1. The Reading

The rain found Marrow Bay before I did, and by the time my bus groaned down the last hill into town, it had already made the place its own. Water sheeted off the rooftops of the harbor houses, pooled in the cobbles of Front Street, and turned the bay itself into something gray and breathing, like an animal asleep under a wet blanket. I stepped off with one leather case, one umbrella I would lose within the week, and the particular professional mood I carry into every new engagement: pleasant, watchful, and entirely without opinions.

I am a lawyer by trade, though "estate lawyer" is what people put on the sign, and what they mean is: a man who reads dead people's paper. Forty years in the trade teaches you that the dead are more honest than the living, mostly because they've stopped having anything to protect. Their documents say what they say. It's the living who lie around them.

The client who retained me had done so by letter, unsigned, on paper that smelled faintly of camphor. The letter was brief to the point of rudeness:

*Mr. Underhill — You will come to Marrow Bay and read the last will and testament of Aldous Vane aloud at the parish hall on the fourteenth. Your fee is enclosed. Do not inquire further.*

The fee was enclosed. That was the part that kept me on the bus for six hours through weather that would have turned back a man with better sense. It came in a canvas bank bag, old-style notes — crisp, unworn, denominations from a currency series retired decades ago. Not counterfeit; I checked, because checking is half my profession and the other half is listening. Genuine notes, withdrawn from circulation years before I finished law school, in condition suggesting they had been stored rather than spent. Somebody had been sitting on this money since roughly the era of the will itself.

That should have been my first unease. Instead it was my first interest, which in my line amounts to the same thing wearing a nicer coat.

* * *

Marrow Bay has perhaps four hundred souls when the fishing is good and fewer when it isn't. The parish hall sits at the head of Front Street, a stone building with a slate roof and the posture of a church that decided against God and went into local government instead. Inside, it smells of wood polish, damp wool, and the particular staleness of rooms where important things are discussed slowly.

They had set out folding chairs. Perhaps sixty were occupied, which for Marrow Bay constituted a civic event on the order of a royal visit. The front row held the parish council — five people arranged with the unconscious hierarchy of long service, the chairman in the center like a keystone. A woman beside him wore a chain of office that looked older than the building. Behind them, the townspeople sat in their Sunday clothes, hands folded, faces arranged into expressions of solemn anticipation.

And underneath all of it — I noticed this within thirty seconds, because noticing is what I'm paid for — there was something else. Not grief. Not curiosity, exactly. Something closer to *readiness*. The look of an audience that has rehearsed its reactions.

"Mr. Underhill." The chairman rose to meet me, extending a hand dry as August grass. He was seventy if he was a day, silver-haired, with the ruddy, wind-cured complexion of a man who has spent his life telling the sea what he thinks of it. "Edmund Tarrow. Chairman of the council. We're honored. Aldous Vane founded this parish — built the harbor wall, endowed the church, laid out half the streets you see. Whatever that document says, we hope you'll read it with the respect his memory deserves."

"I always read documents with respect," I said. "It's the only way to hear what they're actually saying."

Something flickered across his face — not quite displeasure, but the neighborhood of it. "Of course."

I asked the standard questions, because standard questions are how you find out what isn't standard. Who engaged me? Tarrow didn't know. Where had the will been held? With a solicitor's office in the county seat, closed now forty years, its files transferred to — he gestured vaguely eastward — somewhere. Had anyone read it before today? A pause, a fraction too long. "No," he said. "No one has."

I have been lied to by better liars than Edmund Tarrow, but rarely by ones who seemed so uneasy about doing it.

* * *

The document itself was waiting in a steel deed box on the table before me, flanked by two candles nobody had lit, which told me somebody on the council had wanted ceremony and somebody else had vetoed it. The box was unlocked. I put on my reading glasses, broke the seal — wax, stamped with a V monogram, brittle with age — and lifted out a single sheet folded in thirds, plus a shorter enclosure.

The paper was good stock, heavy, cream-colored, the kind that costs money and announces it. The handwriting was not. It was a secretary's hand, copperplate-precise, each letter standing at attention:

*This is the last will and testament of Aldous Vane of Marrow Bay, being of sound mind and body, which latter fact may be verified by any who doubt it.*

I paused there. So did the room — I could feel sixty people leaning forward in unison, a soft rustle like wind through wheat.

*I leave everything I am and everything I hold — the house on Harbor Hill, the accounts, the papers, the parcel of tideland below the bluff, and such other property as may be discovered to be mine — to M. Hale, whom I trust above all persons living or dead, and to whom I owe more than this page can carry.*

*Signed this day, the ninth of November, 1984.*

I read it twice. Then I read the date again, because dates are the load-bearing walls of my profession, and this one had just dropped a beam on my foot.

Aldous Vane had died — according to every record, every gravestone, every anniversary notice the parish had printed for four decades — on the sixth of November, 1984. His funeral had been held on the eighth. Half this room had stood in the rain watching the coffin go down; I'd seen the photographs in the parish archive on the ferry over, black-and-white, umbrellas, bowed heads.

This will was dated the ninth. Three days after they buried him.

And it named as sole beneficiary a person identified only as *M. Hale*, whom I did not know, and about whom the room, judging by the silence, knew nothing either.

"Mr. Underhill." Tarrow's voice had gone carefully flat, the flatness of a man holding a door shut against weather. "Read the date again."

"I've read it three times," I said. "It says the ninth of November."

"That's impossible. Mr. Vane was buried on the eighth."

"Then either the date is wrong," I said, "or the burial was."

You could have heard the rain on the roof after that. Sixty people, and not one of them breathed loud enough to matter. And here is the thing I want to set down honestly, because it matters later: nobody in that room looked surprised. Shocked, yes — the way a congregation is shocked when the sermon goes somewhere unexpected. But there was no scrambling, no puzzled murmuring, none of the small honest chaos of genuine surprise. They sat very still, all together, like people waiting to see whether a floor would hold.

Except one. An elderly woman in the third row, small and straight-backed, with white hair pinned severely and hands knotted in her lap. She wasn't looking at me. She was looking at the far wall, at nothing, and her face had gone the color of the paper in my hand. Of everyone in the parish hall, she was the only one who looked like a woman watching a coffin open.

I noted her. I note everyone; it's cheaper than insurance.

"The signature?" asked the woman with the chain of office. Her name, I'd learn, was Councilor Pryce, and she had the eyes of someone who balanced books for a living and resented the entries that wouldn't balance.

"Witnessed and attested," I said. "Two signatures. Names I don't recognize — E. Crane and F. Dill. Signatures witnessed before a notary whose commission I can verify." I turned the page over. "The hand is not Aldous Vane's, I'd wager. It's a secretary's hand. But the signature at the bottom—" I held it up to the light, more for theater than necessity. "That's his. Or it's the finest forgery I've ever seen, and I've seen some very fine ones."

"And the enclosure?" Tarrow said.

The second sheet was smaller, folded once. I opened it. Four lines, same precise hand:

*M. Hale will know what to do. No one else need do anything at all. Let the town keep its founder. He served it well enough while he was here, and better, perhaps, afterward. — A.V.*

I read it aloud. When I looked up, the stillness in the room had changed quality — from held breath to something harder, something with edges.

"This is a forgery," said a man near the back, standing, voice cracking on the word. "Vane was a good man. He built this town. He wouldn't leave it all to some — some *Hale*."

"Who is M. Hale?" someone else demanded, and then several voices at once, and Tarrow's gavel — of course there was a gavel — came down twice without producing order.

I stood at the table with a dead man's will in my hands and let the noise wash over me, and I did what I always do in chaos, which is nothing. Watching. Listening. The chairman was shouting for quiet and looking, not at the crowd, but at the old woman in the third row. Just once. A glance lasting less than a second, quick as a card sharp's.

She didn't look back. But her hands stopped moving.

* * *

Afterward, in the vestry-like anteroom where the council retreated to argue in low voices, I dried my glasses on my handkerchief and took stock.

Here is what I knew, laid out in the ledger of my mind the way I lay out everything, debits on the left:

One. A client unknown had paid me a fortune in obsolete banknotes to read a will dated after its testator's funeral. Two. The will was authentic in execution if impossible in chronology. Three. The beneficiary, M. Hale, was a name that produced in sixty townspeople the specific silence of people who have agreed in advance not to know something. Four. The chairman and the old woman in the third row shared a secret the way two people share an umbrella — close, and careful, and both pretending to look elsewhere. Five. Nobody in that room had asked the question a stranger would ask first, namely: *if Vane died on the sixth, who wrote a will in his name on the ninth?*

They hadn't asked it because asking it out loud would make it real. I've seen parishes do this before, in smaller ways — a drowned fisherman written up as lost to weather when everyone knew he'd been drinking, a bankruptcy described as retirement, a marriage described as happy. Towns keep ledgers too. Sometimes the entries are wrong, and sometimes — this is the part that interests me — sometimes everybody knows they're wrong, and the wrongness becomes the truest thing about the place.

My fee had been paid in full, in advance, with instructions not to inquire further. A sensible man would read the will, file his certificate, collect his train home, and let Marrow Bay keep whatever story it was keeping.

But the fee bothered me. Old notes, mint-condition, decades out of circulation. Money that had been *waiting*. Somebody had prepared for this reading a very long time ago — had possibly prepared for it since 1984. And whoever they were, they had chosen me, a stranger with no history in this parish, no loyalties, no one to disappoint. They had hired an invisible man to do a visible thing.

I never take a case I can't close. That's the whole of my reputation, and I guard it the way other men guard their health. Which meant that before I slept that night, in the rented room above the chandlery with the rain working at the window like a creditor, I had already decided — quietly, without ceremony, the way most of my decisions arrive — that I would find out who M. Hale was.

Outside, the tide was coming in. I could hear it even through the rain, that long patient dragging sound, like the sea taking inventory.

Somewhere in Marrow Bay there was a grave that might be empty, a name nobody would say, and a dead man who had signed his own will three days after his funeral. Professional curiosity, I told myself. Nothing more.

I have been lying to myself professionally for forty years. I'm very good at it. But even I didn't quite believe it that night, listening to the water, and thinking about a woman in a third row whose hands had gone still when the chairman glanced at her — still, the way hands go still when they've been caught holding something.

The rain kept on. In the morning, I would start with the parish records.

In the morning, I would start pulling threads.
