## 2. A Town That Remembers Wrongly

The rain had moved on by morning, leaving Marrow Bay rinsed and gleaming, the way a liar looks after a good night's sleep. I walked from the Saltbox Inn to the parish offices with my coat still damp at the shoulders and my briefcase holding the only honest thing in town, as far as I could tell: a will dated three days after its subject's burial.

Parish Hall sat on a rise above the harbor, a squat granite building that looked like it had been designed to withstand argument. Inside smelled of floor wax and old paper and, faintly, of coffee that had been made with more ceremony than skill.

They were waiting for me. All five of them, arranged behind a long table like a panel of judges who'd rehearsed their expressions in a mirror. At the center sat Council Chairman Harold Brine — I want to say the name suited him, all brine and no broth — a broad man with a fisherman's forearms and a mayor's handshake he hadn't offered yet. To his left, a thin woman with reading glasses on a chain, introduced as Mrs. Pettibone, who kept the parish records the way some people keep grudges: carefully, and against future need. The other three were younger, mostly silent, mostly nodding.

"Mr. Underhill." Brine stood to shake my hand. His grip was calibrated to remind me of something. "We appreciate you coming all this way. Though I'll be frank — we don't know who's paying your fee, and frankly we don't much care for the arrangement."

"Anonymous clients are legal," I said. "Common, even. People are shy about lawyers the way they're shy about doctors."

"And this document you're carrying." He didn't sit so much as lower himself, slowly, giving the room time to settle around his weight. "You've read it."

"I have."

"And?"

"And it's my job to determine whether it's genuine before anyone acts on it. Which is why I'm here. I'd like to ask each of you a few questions about Aldous Vane."

Mrs. Pettibone adjusted her glasses. "What would you like to know? We have extensive records. The Vane Room at the library. The historical society has published two monographs."

*Extensive records.* Two monographs. In forty years, a town this size had built an entire archive around one man, the way a pearl builds around an irritant.

I opened my notebook. "Tell me about the funeral."

Brine answered first, which told me something. "October 1984. Largest gathering in the parish's history. Half the county came. He was our founder — bought this land when it was nothing but gull rocks and salt marsh, dredged the harbor channel himself, practically with his own hands—"

"With contractors," said the youngest councilman, a soft-faced man named Dill, who immediately looked as though he'd confessed to heresy.

"With *oversight*," Brine corrected, without heat, the way a man corrects a child for the hundredth time. "Aldous Vane was not a man who delegated what mattered."

"What did he die of?"

A pause. Small, but I've learned to hear pauses the way sailors hear weather. Mrs. Pettibone filled it. "His heart. He was sixty-one. There'd been warnings — he'd slowed down that summer. The doctor came out to the house."

"Which house?"

"The Vane house, on the headland. It burned in '89, sadly. Nothing left of it."

Nothing left of it. Of course. I wrote that down anyway. "And the doctor's name?"

Another pause, longer this time. Brine's jaw worked. "Dr. Ellery. Dead now, I'm afraid. Twenty years gone."

Everyone in this story was dead or burned or buried. It was becoming a pattern, and patterns are what I'm paid for. "Who prepared the body?"

"The sexton did most of it," said Dill. "Old Tom Crane. And Edith Crane kept house for Mr. Vane — she saw to the linings and such. But the coffin was closed at the house, per the family's wishes. Closed viewing. Very private man."

"Closed casket," I repeated. "Was there an open viewing beforehand? A chance for the town to—"

"No." Brine again, flat and final. "He wouldn't have wanted it. He hated fuss."

"He founded a town," I said mildly, "and hated fuss."

"He hated *fuss*," Brine said, with the patience of a man explaining tide tables to a tourist. "Not commemoration. There's a difference. Look him up, Mr. Underhill. Everything about the man is documented. Birth in Bristol, 1923. Came here in 1951 with capital and a plan. Married Eleanor Ashcombe in '58. Built the cannery, the church roof, the seawall. Died October 1984. Buried in the churchyard under a stone he chose himself while he was living. Every fact accounted for."

Every fact accounted for. It was the phrase itself that bothered me, the way it came out smooth and oiled, like a passage memorized for recitation. I've sat across from enough witnesses to know the difference between memory and curriculum. Memory stumbles. It digresses, contradicts itself, remembers the wrong detail with perfect clarity — the smell of the lilies, the cousin who drank too much. Curriculum recites.

These five were reciting.

"Mrs. Pettibone," I said. "You keep the death register. May I see the entry?"

She produced it almost before I finished asking — a ledger already open on her lap, marked with a ribbon. That, too, was a small tell. You don't happen to have a forty-year-old register open to the relevant page unless you expected the question.

There it was. *Aldous James Vane, died 14 October 1984, cause: cardiac failure. Certified: Dr. R. Ellery.* The ink was brown with age, the hand confident.

"Certified by the doctor," I said. "But who registered it? Who brought the certificate in?"

Pettibone's finger hovered over the line. "It would have been... the family. Or the secretary. Miriam Hale handled Mr. Vane's correspondence."

"And where is Miss Hale now?"

"She left," said Brine. "After the funeral. Grief, presumably. She'd served him eleven years. Went to stay with relatives somewhere inland. Never came back."

"Did anyone write to her? Visit?"

"Why would we?" Brine spread his hands. "She was staff, Mr. Underhill. Good staff, by all accounts, but staff."

Eleven years running a founder's affairs, and the town she'd served never once checked on her. Either they were cold, or they were careful. With this crowd, I was betting on careful.

"One more thing about the register." I tapped the page. "The burial date."

"17 October," said Pettibone.

"And this will I'm holding is dated the 20th. Three days after he went into the ground." I let that sit. "Signed, witnessed, and — according to my preliminary examination — in a hand consistent with Mr. Vane's own."

The room did something interesting then. Nobody spoke, but everybody moved — Pettibone closed the ledger, Dill looked at his shoes, the two silent councilmen exchanged a glance so quick it might have been a muscle spasm. Only Brine stayed perfectly still, and that was its own kind of answer.

"You're suggesting," Brine said carefully, "that a dead man signed a document."

"I'm suggesting nothing yet. I'm noting a discrepancy that requires explanation. Either the date is wrong, the signature isn't his, or—" I shrugged. "Or something else entirely."

"It's a forgery." Pettibone said it like a verdict. "Some crank's idea of a joke. Or someone trying to cloud the title to the tideland parcel. We've had our share of speculators sniffing round that land since the highway went through."

"That may well be," I said. "I'll need samples of his handwriting. Letters, ledgers, anything original."

"The Vane Room," she said. "By appointment."

"I'll make one."

"There's another matter," Brine said, leaning forward, and now I got the handshake voice, the one meant for closing deals. "Whatever your client is paying you, I'd ask you to consider whose interests you serve by pursuing this. This parish has spent forty years honoring its founder. There's a statue on the green. Children are taught his name before they can write their own. If some paper turns up claiming he left everything to his secretary — well. You can imagine what that does to a town like ours. What it does to *property values*, if we're being blunt."

"I'm always blunt about fees," I said, "and never about conclusions. I follow documents where they lead."

"Documents lie," said Brine.

"People lie," I said. "Documents just sit there. It's why I prefer them."

He didn't laugh. None of them did. I gathered my notebook, thanked them for their time, and felt their eyes on my back all the way to the door — five pairs, uniform in weight, like the eyes of people watching a boat they hoped would sink far from shore.

* * *

The church of St. Brendan-the-Navigator stood at the foot of the hill, grey stone going darker where the damp climbed. Its churchyard ran down toward the water in terraces, and there, in the best position — facing the harbor he'd dredged, naturally — stood the Vane monument. Granite, taller than me, carved with a schooner and a wreath and ALDOUS JAMES VANE, FOUNDER, 1923–1984. Fresh flowers at the base. Someone tended that grave. Forty years on, someone still tended it.

I was reading the inscription a second time when a voice behind me said, "Mind the third step. It's loose."

An old man was coming up the path with a wheelbarrow full of sand and a spade handle worn to the color of honey. Sexton's clothes, sexton's hands. He set the barrow down and looked at the grave with me, companionably, the way men look at weather.

"You're the lawyer," he said. Not a question. News travels fast in towns where news is the main industry.

"Ines Underhill."

"Tom Crane. Sexton." He wiped a hand on his trousers and offered it. His grip was honest and dry. "You'll be wanting to know about the grave."

I blinked. "Should I?"

"Everybody does, eventually." He nodded at the stone. "Forty years I've kept this yard. Mowed round that stone four thousand times, near enough. And in forty years, that ground has never been opened. Not once. Not for a double burial, not for repair, not for anything. Most founders' plots get opened eventually — wives go in beside husbands, that sort of thing. Mrs. Vane's buried up in Ashcombe, with her people, so there was never cause. But even so." He picked up his spade, considered it. "Ground settles different over a coffin. Any sexton knows it. That plot's settled like there's something down there. Or like there isn't, depending how you read it."

"How do you read it, Mr. Crane?"

He looked at me for a long moment. Old eyes, tide-colored, giving away nothing and everything at once.

"I read it the way I read the water," he said. "Something's off the mark, and I don't cast for it till I know what. You'll pardon an old man's caution, but there's folk in this town who'd rather that ground stayed shut. And there's folk — fewer — who pray every night it stays shut, for reasons that'd break your heart."

"Which are you?"

He smiled then, briefly, and it was a sad piece of work. "I'm the man who dug the hole in 1984. Six feet, straight down, clean sides. I lowered that coffin myself, with Edith's boy helping. It weighed what a coffin with a man in it ought to weigh." He turned back to the wheelbarrow. "Course, I wasn't the one who carried it out of the house. That was done upstairs, private, before ever it came to me. So I can only testify to what went into the ground."

"Which was?"

"A box heavy as sin," said Tom Crane, "and quiet as a stone." He lifted the barrow handles. "You want my advice, lawyer — and nobody ever does — talk to my sister-in-law Edith before you talk to anybody else. She kept his house. She knows where everything in this parish is buried, including the things that aren't under ground at all."

He trundled off toward the tool shed, leaving me alone with the monument and the fresh flowers and the settled earth.

I stood there a while, doing sums. A will dated after a burial. A register kept ready like scripture. A doctor dead, a house burned, a secretary vanished, a casket closed, a grave unopened in forty years. Five council members reciting identical facts with identical pauses.

Somewhere in this town was a story everyone had agreed to tell, polished smooth as beach glass from forty years of handling. And somewhere beneath it — maybe six feet beneath it, maybe not — was the story that actually happened.

I flipped my notebook to a fresh page and wrote one word at the top: *Hale.*

Then, underneath, because Tom Crane had earned it: *Edith Crane. Ask her first.*

The tide was coming in below the churchyard wall, patient as a creditor. I had the feeling it knew something I didn't.
