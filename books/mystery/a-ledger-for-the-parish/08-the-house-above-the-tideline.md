## 8. The House Above the Tideline

Edith Crane's key was brass, old, and warm from sitting taped beneath a hymnal for what I estimated to be thirty years. Hymnals, apparently, are excellent hiding places. Nobody under eighty has ever opened one voluntarily, and nobody over eighty wants to admit they know where the tape is.

The house sat where the map said it would: above the tideline, at the ragged northern edge of the disputed parcel, where the bluff grass gave way to gorse and the gorse gave way to a view that could make a grown man forget his billing rate. The Hale house. In Marrow Bay it was known, when it was known at all, as "the old place up past the point," which is the highest honor a town pays a house it has agreed to forget. Windows boarded. Roofline honest. A chimney still standing, which surprised me—chimneys are the first to go, in my experience, the way secrets are the first to go in small towns, except when the small town has decided, collectively, to keep one.

I checked the road twice before I walked up. Old habit. Invisibility is a discipline, like flossing.

The key turned on the second try, after I'd made peace with the possibility of the first try being the only try. The door opened inward with a sound like a held breath released, and the smell came out to meet me: salt, cedar, dust, and beneath all of it something domestic and stubborn—soap, maybe, or the ghost of soap, forty years of mornings that had happened in this room while the town down the hill mourned a man who was, at that very moment, probably arguing about whether to buy the good coffee or the coffee that was merely adequate.

I stood in the doorway a long moment. I want to be precise about what I felt, because precision is my trade and because what I felt was not what I'd expected. I expected triumph. The ledger-brain that runs most of my waking hours had already posted the entry: *evidence located, case advancing, fee justified.* Instead what I felt was something closer to the embarrassment of walking into a stranger's kitchen and finding your own mother's curtains at the window.

"Mr. Vane," I said to the empty room, because I am a professional and professionals announce themselves. "I've come about the estate."

The empty room did not object. It was, I would learn over the next several hours, the most articulate empty room I have ever stood in.

* * *

The front room first. Boarded windows meant I worked by flashlight and by the gray light leaking through the gaps in the planking, which gave everything a drowned, underwater quality, as though the house had been submerged all these years and I was diving it. A sofa, slipcovered. A bookshelf—Dickens, Trollope, a surprising amount of poetry, the spines cracked in a way that suggested reading rather than decoration. A card table with two chairs, and on the card table a cribbage board with a game frozen mid-run, pegs at ninety-one and eighty-four.

Ninety-one and eighty-four. I stood there longer than a grown man should stand over a cribbage board. Somebody had been winning. Somebody had been losing. Somebody had said something cutting and funny about it, and somebody else had laughed, and then they had both gotten up to make tea, and the game had never been finished, because one day there was no one left to finish it.

You will forgive me. I am an estate lawyer. I spend my life among the possessions of the dead, and I have a theory—untested, unpublishable—that a house tells you the shape of the life lived in it the way a shell tells you the shape of the animal. This shell had been occupied by two animals, and they had been, by every measure I knew how to read, content.

The kitchen confirmed it. The kitchen is where a house confesses.

Two toothbrushes in a cup on the sill above the sink. I want that sentence to land properly, so I'll say it again: two toothbrushes. One green, one white, both fossilized into bristle-splayed relics, standing in a ceramic cup like a married couple at a party where they only know each other. Toothbrushes are not evidence. Toothbrushes are not probative of anything a court would care about. But I stood there holding that cup and I felt the whole architecture of the fraud—no, not the fraud, I must stop calling it that; the *arrangement*—become human in my hands. Aldous Vane, founder of Marrow Bay, dead forty years and buried in river stones, had stood at this sink every morning of his secret life and brushed his teeth next to Miriam Hale, and one of them had bought the green one and one of them had bought the white one, and neither of them had ever once, in forty years, been able to tell a single soul about it.

I set the cup down very carefully. Evidence handling. Also, I found, my hands were not entirely steady, and I preferred to attribute that to the climb.

* * *

The ledgers were in the pantry, which was the first genuinely strange thing, because the pantry was organized. Not tidied—*organized*, with the particular logic of long habitation: the ledgers shelved by decade, the decades by year, forty-one of them, cloth-bound account books of the sort that went out of fashion around the time Marrow Bay buried its founder. I took down the earliest. 1984.

Two hands.

I know handwriting the way a priest knows Latin. It is my profession's oldest tool and its most honest. The first hand was small, upright, economical—a bookkeeper's hand, a hand that had been trained to make every figure legible to an auditor who might arrive angry. *Flour, 2 lb. Tea. Mending thread. Lamp oil.* The second hand was looser, slanted, given to flourishes on the descenders and the occasional marginal note in a different ink, as though the writer kept losing the pen and refusing to be defeated by this.

The first hand was Miriam Hale's. I had her signature from the will, and the will was the one document in this affair nobody had questioned, because it was flawless.

The second hand I knew from somewhere else, and it took me the length of one ledger—1987, a good year for mackerel, a bad year for the roof—to place it. Then I took out my copy of the founding documents of the parish of Marrow Bay, the land grants, the subscription lists, the beautiful copperplate signatures of the men who had built a town out of mud and ambition. And there it was. *Aldous Vane,* signed in 1951, the same loose slant, the same flourishes, the same stubborn marginalia.

The founder of Marrow Bay had spent forty years keeping the grocery accounts of a house that did not exist, in a hand that half the town could have identified from a single letter, twenty minutes' walk from his own gravestone.

I sat down on the pantry floor. I am not a man who sits on floors. But the alternative was to keep standing while my understanding of the world reorganized itself, and I have found that reorganization goes better seated.

Here is what the ledgers told me, entry by entry, year by year. They told me that in the winter of 1984–85, the first winter, they had bought an enormous quantity of lamp oil and very little else, as though the two of them had been afraid to leave the house, as though the house were a lifeboat and the shore were patrolled. They told me that by 1989 the entries had relaxed—eggs from the Hutchins place, mending for the Crane farm, a piano tuner, *piano tuner!*—as though a town that believed itself childless of its founder had quietly adopted him without knowing it, the way a village adopts a harmless madman, except that he wasn't mad, he was only dead. They told me about a dog, 1993 to 2001, entries for "dog meal" that grew and then shrank and then stopped, and a single line in Miriam's hand in the spring of 2001 that was not a grocery item at all: *Bury him under the rowan. He was a good boy.*

They told me, in short, everything a ledger can tell, which is everything except the one thing I needed, which is whether anyone had ever been happy here. And then, in the front room, in the drawer of the card table, under the unfinished cribbage game, I found the photograph, and the ledgers were answered.

* * *

It was a snapshot, the kind a Kodak makes and a drugstore develops and nobody thinks twice about, unless the two people in it have been legally dead or legally single for seven years apiece. The porch. I recognized the porch from the outside of the house, the west-facing one, the one that caught the evening light. Two people in it. A woman in a cardigan the color of the sea on an indecisive day, caught mid-laugh with her head thrown back, one hand raised as if to fend off whatever the man beside her had just said. And the man—gray now, heavier than the photographs in the parish hall, bald in the way that dignity permits—was laughing too, leaning toward her, caught in the act of saying the thing that had made her throw her head back.

On the back, in that loose, slanted, flourished hand: *A. & M., August 1991. Miriam says I look ridiculous. Miriam is right.*

I have read wills that made me weep—not many, but some—and I have read confessions that made me need a drink, and I have read love letters that belonged in a fire. But I had never, until that gray afternoon in a sealed house above the tideline, held a piece of paper that made me feel like a trespasser and a witness at the same time, which is, I suppose, what an estate lawyer is, if you strip away the fees.

August 1991. I did the arithmetic the way I do everything, compulsively and without being asked. Seven years after the funeral. The town had held its memorial services. The statue—there is a statue, I had seen it, bronze and solemn and wrong about everything—had already been bolted to its plinth. Children had been taught his name in the school he had endowed. And the man himself had been on this porch, laughing at his own joke, being told he looked ridiculous by a woman who was right, and the whole town had been twenty minutes' walk away, downhill, mourning him.

Twenty feet from the town's memory. That is what I kept coming back to. Not twenty miles. Not hidden in some far country under a changed name, which would have been sensible, which is what I would have advised, which is what any competent adviser would have said: *go far, go anonymous, go where nobody knows your signature.* Instead they had come home. Or rather—they had never left. They had moved up the coast to a house everyone knew belonged to the secretary who'd resigned, and they had stayed, and they had bought their groceries from the town that wept for him, and the town had sold them eggs and lamp oil and never once looked twice, because grief, I learned that afternoon, is the best disguise ever devised. Nobody looks at a dead man's face. They've already made up their minds about it.

* * *

I worked until the light through the boarded windows went from gray to bronze, and then I did something I almost never do, which is to stop working while there was still work. I went out onto the west porch, because it seemed owed, and I sat in one of the two chairs—there were two chairs, of course there were two chairs—and I watched the water do what water does, which is everything, patiently.

From the porch you could see the parish. The church spire. The roofs of the shops. The plinth, if you knew where to look, with its bronze man standing in a pose suggesting he was about to found something. And you could see, I realized, the graveyard—just, at the tree line, the pale scatter of stones. He could have sat here, in this chair, on any evening of his last forty years, and looked at his own grave the way a man looks at an old coat he's outgrown.

What does a man think about, I wondered, when he watches the town bury him? Does he feel freed? Guilty? Does he miss them—the councilmen, the shopkeepers, the children in his school—or does he miss only the version of himself they were mourning, the founder, the pillar, the name on the bronze? I have sat with enough dying clients to know that the question men ask at the end is never *did I succeed* but *was I known*—and Aldous Vane had solved that problem with a scissors and a paste pot, cutting himself out of the town's ledger and pasting himself into a smaller one, a grocery ledger, a cribbage ledger, a ledger with two entries in every line.

And Miriam. I kept circling back to Miriam, because the will had been hers to execute and the risk had been hers to carry and the house had been, by every sign, as much hers as his. She had typed his letters for twenty years before the burial. She had known his hand well enough to forge a death and his heart well enough to share one. What had he offered her, that she traded a whole town's daylight for a porch and a card table? And what had she seen in him, that she thought it was a fair trade?

The photograph said she thought it was a very fair trade. The photograph said she'd have done it twice.

I put the photograph back in the drawer, exactly as found—evidence discipline, but also something else, a sense that some things in a house should stay where the house put them—and I made my notes, and I photographed the ledgers and the toothbrushes and the cribbage board with the pegs at ninety-one and eighty-four, and I locked the door behind me with Edith Crane's warm brass key, and I stood a moment on the path looking back at the house that had been full of life for forty years while the town below it performed a funeral.

That was when I saw the fresh footprints.

Not mine. Mine were the only prints on that path when I'd come up—I'd checked, old habit, invisibility is a discipline—and the ground had been damp from the morning fog, holding prints like wet clay holds a seal. Someone had come up the path after me and stood at the window. The north window, the one with the gap in the boarding. There were two heel-marks pressed deep in the soft ground, the stance of a person who had stood there a long while, looking in. And then the prints turned, unhurried, and went down the path toward the tideline, and I followed them as far as the bluff's edge, where the grass gave out and the shingle began, and there they stopped.

Not faded. Stopped. As if the person who made them had walked down to the water and simply ceased to be—a thing people do not do, except in Marrow Bay, where it is apparently a local custom.

I stood at the bluff's edge with the wind coming off the water and did the arithmetic again, the other kind, the kind I like less. Someone knew I had been in the house. Someone had come to look at what I'd found. And the tide was coming in over the place where the footprints ended, smoothing the shingle, erasing the question, the way this coast has been erasing questions for forty years—and I had the sudden, cold, professional certainty that whatever came next would not wait politely for the probate calendar.
