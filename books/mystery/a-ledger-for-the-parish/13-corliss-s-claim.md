## 13. Corliss's Claim

The summons came by hand, which in Marrow Bay meant someone had walked it across town rather than trust the post, and that told me everything before I opened it. People who want to talk to you through lawyers send letters. People who want to watch your face while they ruin you send a boy with an envelope and instructions to wait for a reply.

The envelope was heavy cream stock, unmarked, and inside was a single line in a confident, expensive hand: *Mr. Underhill — The Parish Council chamber, Thursday, ten o'clock. Mr. Vance requests your attendance. He believes you will want to hear what he has found.*

*Found.* Not *learned*. Found, the way you find a thing that has been hidden from you, the way a dog finds what it isn't supposed to dig up.

I sat with the card for a long while at my table above the harbormaster's office, watching rain come off the bay in gray sheets, and I did something I rarely do: I rehearsed. I laid out the board in my head — Corliss Vance, the council, Edith Crane, Ruth Hale and her letters, the strongbox still sitting in its pit of storm-turned earth under tarpaulin and constable's tape — and I tried to see where Vance would strike first.

I couldn't see it. That should have worried me more than it did.

* * *

The council chamber had not changed since my first week in town, except that now it smelled of wet wool and nerves instead of just dust and varnish. Every seat was taken. Word travels fast in a parish the size of a hymnal, and word had it that Corliss Vance had called this session himself, had paid the filing fee out of his own pocket, had arrived on the morning ferry with two leather cases and a lawyer whose shoes cost more than the council's annual budget.

Vance sat at the head of the crescent of tables like a man who had been practicing the posture all his life. His lawyer — a narrow, silvered man named Pell whom I recognized from a land-use arbitration in Portland years ago — sat slightly behind him, arranging papers with the tenderness of a nurse preparing instruments.

Ines Underhill took a chair at the edge, where the invisible men sit. Or so I intended. But when I came in, Vance looked directly at me, and smiled, and nodded me toward the front row as if I were family.

"Mr. Underhill," he said, loud enough for the room. "Good. I'd hate for you to miss this."

Councilor Pruett, chairing because the actual chairman had developed a sudden and convenient laryngitis, banged a gavel that had never once in its life commanded silence. "This session is convened at the request of Mr. Corliss Vance regarding the tideland parcel. Mr. Vance, you have the floor."

"Thank you." Vance stood. He wore a charcoal suit and the expression of a man about to do everyone a favor they won't thank him for. "I'll be brief, because the facts are brief."

He opened the first leather case and drew out a sheaf of pages in a plastic sleeve, and my stomach dropped before I saw a single word, because I knew those pages. I knew the weight of them, the cheap wartime paper, the slanted hand that leaned right like a man walking into wind.

"Some of you know," Vance said, "that an old document was discovered recently on property I have a lawful claim to. A document written by Aldous Vane. In his own hand. After his death."

The room made a sound I've only heard twice in my career — once at a will reading, once at a funeral where the widow laughed. A sound like a whole town inhaling through its teeth.

"I have here," Vance continued, laying the pages flat, turning each one toward the council like a dealer showing cards, "a confession. Dated 1984. Signed Aldous R. Vane. In it, the founder of this parish admits that his burial was staged. That his coffin was filled with stones. That he defrauded his creditors, his insurers, and every one of you — your parents, your grandparents — and lived out his life in hiding not four hundred yards from the statue you pay to polish."

He let that sit. He was good at letting things sit; you could tell he'd learned it somewhere expensive.

"Now. I'm not a vindictive man." He said this the way other men say *trust me*. "What happened forty years ago is beyond the law's reach, mostly. Statutes have run. The dead can't be prosecuted. But the *estate* can be unwound. Every transfer made on the strength of a fraudulent death certificate — and there were transfers, ladies and gentlemen, considerable ones — is voidable. And this parcel—" he touched the map of the tideline, the one with the Hale house sitting on it like a period at the end of a sentence, "—reverts. To the rightful claimant. Which is me."

Pruett's voice came out thin. "You're saying you'll expose all of it unless—"

"I'm saying nothing of the kind." Vance spread his hands, wounded. "I'm saying the truth is going to come out regardless. It's already out. What remains negotiable is only the *manner* of it. If the parish concedes the parcel quietly, I'll have no reason to pursue the estate question. The founder's memory stays intact. Everyone keeps their statue." He looked around the room, gathering eyes the way a man gathers firewood. "Or we can do this publicly. Depositions. Exhumation records. Forty years of parish finances dragged into court, and every one of you answering questions about what you knew and when. I don't want that. I suspect none of you want that."

Nobody spoke. In the third row, Edith Crane sat with her hands folded in her lap like a woman at a graveside, and I watched her knuckles go white, and I understood that she had known this moment was coming since the night the footprints led down to the tide and stopped.

Pell slid a copy of the confession across to the council. Pruett wouldn't touch it. Councilor Marsh picked it up with two fingers, read the first page, and set it down as if it were hot.

It was then that I noticed the thing I should have noticed at the door.

The confession on the table was not the confession I had read aloud alone in the locked bedroom of the Hale house. I had read it twice — once silently, once aloud, because some documents deserve the dignity of a voice. I knew its condition: foxed at the edges, water-stained along the bottom margin from decades in a desk drawer beside a window that leaked. The pages Vance was displaying were clean. Too clean. Someone had flattened them, maybe even washed or bleached them, but the stain ghost was still there if you knew to look — a faint tide line across the lower third, exactly where mine had been.

Same document. Different custodian. Which meant whoever searched the house hadn't photographed it and returned it. They'd kept the original and given Vance the original, and that told me the theft wasn't opportunistic. It was commissioned.

And that raised the question I'd been circling for three days without letting myself land on it: why would Corliss Vance, developer, outsider, spend money and risk felony burglary for a forty-year-old piece of paper?

Land was the obvious answer, and land was true as far as it went. But men like Vance don't commission burglaries over four hundred yards of tidal marsh. Men like Vance buy marsh. Something else was paying for this.

* * *

The session collapsed into procedural noise — motions to recess, objections, Pruett calling for counsel, the council's own attorney arriving breathless with his tie askew — and in the churn of it, Vance came down the aisle toward me.

"You're quiet, Mr. Underhill," he said. "That's unlike your reputation."

"You know my reputation?"

"I made it my business to." He glanced at the emptying rows, then back at me, and for a moment the performance dropped and something rawer looked out — something young, almost, though he was past sixty. "My grandmother used to talk about you. Eleanor Vane. She said if you ever came to Marrow Bay, the game was already lost."

The floor did not move. I am a professional; the floor never moves. But somewhere behind my sternum, a ledger I'd been keeping without knowing it suddenly refused to balance.

"Eleanor Vane died in 2011," I said carefully. "Her estate passed to—"

"To her daughter, Catherine. My mother. Who died owing more than the inheritance covered, thanks to a husband with a boat and a gambling habit you could see from orbit." Vance's voice stayed level, but the levelness had gone thin as ice. "So no. I got nothing. Nothing from her, nothing from him, nothing from the great sainted founder whose portrait hangs in the church while the man himself was living rent-free above the tideline writing grocery lists with his mistress."

"Mistress" landed hard in the near-empty chamber. I let it.

"The real will," I said. "The second document. You've read it. You know he left the parcel to Miriam Hale, and Miriam left it to her line. You know your claim was void before you ever filed it."

"Oh, I know it." He smiled again, and this time the smile had teeth in it and grief behind it, and the grief was worse. "I've known since I was twelve years old and asked my grandmother why she never talked about Grandfather Aldous, and she looked at me like I'd struck her. Twelve years old, Mr. Underhill, and I already knew there was a hole in our family shaped like a man. She sent money to this parish every year until she died. Every single year. Do you know what she called it?" His jaw worked. "She called it *keeping the light on*. I thought she meant the church. I was thirty-five before I understood she meant the house on the bluff. She was paying for his quiet. While my mother sold her jewelry to make mortgage payments."

There it was. The motive, snapped into focus at last, and it was not greed, or not only greed. It was the oldest grievance there is: *inheritance denied.* Not the land — the love. A boy who grew up in a house with a hole in it, watching hush money flow seaward to keep a dead man comfortable, and who had decided, somewhere in the dark arithmetic of childhood, that if the family fortune would never flow to him, he would burn the channel it flowed through.

"You could have come to me," I said. "When you learned what I was here for. There are versions of this that end differently."

"There are versions where I get a check and a handshake and everyone agrees to keep lying." He gathered his cases. "I'm done being lied to on behalf of a dead man. You of all people should understand, Underhill. You read the confession. You read what he wrote about Eleanor." He paused at the door, and the last thing he said was quieter, almost private, and it was the most dangerous thing he'd said all morning:

"She forgave him. Read it again. She *forgave* him. And nobody ever asked what that forgiveness cost the rest of us."

Then he was gone, and Pell's paper rustled after him like a wake.

* * *

I sat in the emptying chamber for a long time after, long enough that the caretaker began stacking chairs around me with pointed courtesy. On the table lay the council's copy of the confession, abandoned now that its threat had been delivered, and I did the one thing I could still legally do: I read it again.

Eleanor appeared on the fourth page. *Eleanor knows,* Vane had written, in that right-leaning hand. *I told her myself, the night before. She could have stopped me and didn't. Whatever that makes her, she chose it, and I will spend what's left of my life trying to be worth the choice.*

Worth the choice. Forty years of grocery ledgers and two toothbrushes and a photograph on a porch, and underneath it all a debt he was paying to a wife he'd left behind — and a grandson who had grown up counting the payments and finding himself owed.

I thought of Ruth Hale's letters in their oilcloth bundle. I thought of the strongbox under the tarpaulin, and the letter inside dated 2003, and the deed it supposedly contained. I thought of Edith Crane's folded hands.

And then I thought of the one thing Vance had said that didn't fit, the splinter in the whole elegant machine of his claim. He'd said the theft was *found* — his word, on the card. But the footprints had gone down to the tideline and stopped, and no boat had been reported, and the tide that week had been running wrong for a landing.

Which meant either Corliss Vance was lying to me about how he got the confession.

Or someone else had gotten there first, and handed it to him, and I very much wanted to know what else they'd taken from that house — and whether the strongbox on the bluff was still where the storm had left it.

I was reaching for my coat when the caretaker cleared his throat.

"Begging pardon, Mr. Underhill. Constable's been by twice looking for you." He lowered his voice, pleased to be the bearer of it. "Seems there's been trouble up at the Hale place. Tarpaulin's torn. Pit's open."

"And?"

"And," he said, savoring it, "the box is gone."
