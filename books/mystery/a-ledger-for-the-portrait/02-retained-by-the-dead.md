## 2. Retained by the Dead

The law office of Corinne Ashe occupied the second floor of the old Merchants & River building, above a pharmacy that still mixed its own cough syrup and smelled like it. Hazel climbed the stairs at nine sharp on Monday morning, past a wall of framed photographs documenting Marrow's Bend's century of self-regard: flood years, parade years, the year they paved Main Street. In every third frame, the same man appeared — tall, silver-templed, standing with one hand resting on whatever was nearest, as if the town itself were furniture he owned.

Ashford Bell. Dead thirty years and still holding court.

Corinne Ashe met Hazel at the top of the stairs with a handshake that was dry, brief, and calibrated to communicate exactly how much time she intended to spend on pleasantries: none.

"Ms. Delacroix. Thank you for coming." She was perhaps fifty-five, gray at the temples in a way that looked maintained rather than earned, wearing a suit the color of river stones. Her office behind her was neat to the point of aggression — files squared, pens parallel, not a single paper out of alignment except one folder lying deliberately apart from the others on the blotter, face down, like something that had been turned over so it wouldn't have to be looked at.

"Ms. Ashe."

"Call me Corinne." She closed the door. "I'll be direct, because I suspect you prefer it. The estate would like to retain you. Quietly."

"Quietly," Hazel repeated. They took the chair across from her desk without being invited, because waiting to be invited gave people time to change their minds.

"The land dispute you witnessed on Saturday has consequences neither party anticipated. If Bell's grave is empty, then questions arise that go well beyond a property line along the Bellwater slough. Questions about the estate's probate. About the trust instruments. About—" she paused, choosing the word with tweezers, "—historical documentation."

"You mean the death certificate."

"I mean everything downstream of it." Corinne sat, folded her hands. "There is also the matter of the federal fraud case against Bell — the Marrow Bend Development Corporation matter, 1994. It was dismissed when he died. It is being reopened. A judge in Little Rock signed the order Friday. You may have seen it in the *Gazette*."

Hazel had seen it. Had read it twice at the diner counter while their eggs went cold, the typeface photographing itself into memory: *IN RE: ESTATE OF ASHFORD BELL; UNITED STATES v. BELL (DECEASED), motion to vacate dismissal granted.* Somewhere in a federal building, prosecutors who had spent three decades assuming their quarry was dead were now being told their quarry had never been buried at all.

"So the estate wants to get ahead of it," Hazel said.

"The estate wants to know the truth before someone else tells it to us badly." Corinne's voice didn't rise, but something under it did. "Whatever happened in 1994, Ms. Delacroix, I represent heirs who were children then. They deserve to learn what their grandfather did or didn't do from an investigator, not from a subpoena or a headline. And if there is an innocent explanation—" her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile, "—I'd very much like to hear it, because I've been trying to construct one since Saturday morning and I can't."

"And if there isn't?"

"Then we'll need to know that too. Before the United States Attorney does."

Hazel studied her. Photographic memory was mostly a curse dressed as a party trick, but it was useful for this: reading a face the way one reads a document, noting what was printed and what had been redacted. Corinne Ashe's face said *I already believe the coffin was empty on purpose.* Her hands, folded neatly on the blotter beside the face-down folder, said *and I am afraid of what my own clients did about it.*

"What's the scope?" Hazel asked.

"Find out why the coffin came up light. Discreetly. No press, no sheriff's deputies kicking in doors, no interviews anyone will remember having. Report to me and no one else. Six weeks, maybe less, before the federal order forces discovery." She slid the face-down folder across the desk and turned it over. "Retainer agreement. My cell number is on the back of my card. I don't sleep much; use it accordingly."

Hazel read the agreement — all four pages, in the time it took most people to read the first line, which Corinne either knew or would soon learn — and signed.

"One condition," Hazel said, capping the pen. "I don't work blind. If your clients know something — anything — and it comes out later that they withheld it from me while I was standing in front of a moving car, I walk, and I leave a very detailed file with the U.S. Attorney on my way out the door."

For the first time, something moved behind Corinne Ashe's eyes that might have been respect, or relief, or both wearing the same coat.

"Agreed," she said. "God help us, agreed."

* * *

The estate's records lived in the archive room off the county courthouse, a windowless chamber that smelled of dust and cold iron shelving, and Hazel spent the afternoon there alone with the dead man's paperwork.

The 1994 death certificate first. Hazel laid it flat under the lamp and let the memory do what it always did — take the whole page at once, every field, every stroke, filed away permanently whether Hazel wanted it or not.

*ASHFORD J. BELL. Date of death: October 14, 1994. Cause: myocardial infarction.* Signed by Dr. Edwin Pruett — father, Hazel noted, of the current mayor. *Burial permit issued October 16. Interment: Bell family plot, Riverside Cemetery, October 17.*

Neat. Complete. Ordinary. That was the problem. Hazel had handled enough fraudulent documents to know that forgery rarely failed on the forged thing itself; it failed on the seams around it. And these seams were seamless. A heart attack, certified within hours, buried within days, witnessed by half the town. No hospital transfer record. No autopsy — not unusual for a man of sixty-one with a documented cardiac history, but convenient. The attending physician just happened to be the father of the man who would spend the next thirty years running the town Bell built.

Hazel photographed nothing. Didn't need to. The page was in there now, permanent as a scar.

Next, the funeral photographs. The estate had retained a set from Crane & Sons Funeral Home — Silas Crane had been the undertaker even then, young then, though Hazel supposed everyone in this story was young once, which was somehow worse. There were eleven photographs, professionally shot, the kind small towns bought to remember the day properly.

Hazel spread them across the table and stood over them the way other people knelt in prayer.

The first: the open coffin, pre-closing. Ashford Bell in repose, waxen and dignified in a charcoal suit, hands folded over a hymnal. Even dead, he managed to look proprietary. Hazel noted the details automatically — the slight asymmetry of the tie knot, the powder too heavy at the hairline, the way the left hand lay a quarter-inch higher than the right. Embalmers' work. Or something else's work. Hazel made a mental note to ask, someday, delicately, how well Silas Crane had actually known the man he'd prepared.

The second through sixth: the procession down Main Street, the storefronts draped, the schoolchildren lined up with paper flowers. The seventh: the graveside, wide angle, perhaps two hundred mourners in dark wool under a sky the color of wet slate.

And it was the seventh photograph where it happened.

Hazel had been looking at funeral photos for two hours by then, cataloguing faces out of habit — the mayor's father looking appropriately stricken, Odette Marsh in black lace at the front row, a young Emmett Marsh, maybe twenty-two, hollow-eyed and gripping his mother's arm. All of it filing itself away, grain by grain, into the archive behind Hazel's eyes.

Then, near the back edge of the crowd, half-cut by the frame, a boy.

Fifteen, maybe sixteen. Too-thin jacket over a collared shirt that had been ironed by someone who cared. Dark hair falling into his face because he was looking down, not at the coffin but at his own shoes, shoulders drawn in like he was trying to take up less space in a town that had never quite made room for him.

Hazel knew those shoulders. Had watched them hunch like that across a dinner table for six years, in a house with a broken screen door and a foster mother who meant well and a boy named Theo who had promised —

The recognition arrived not as a thought but as a physical event, a cold drop straight through the sternum, and for a moment the archive room was gone and Hazel was eleven again, sitting on the porch steps of the Delacroix house, watching a caseworker's sedan pull away with Theo in the back seat, his palm pressed flat against the rear window until the car turned the corner and the glass showed nothing but street.

*He got adopted,* the caseworker had said. *A good family, honey. The Marshes. It's a blessing.*

A blessing. Upriver, forty miles, to a town called Marrow's Bend.

Hazel stood very still over the photograph, breathing, letting the professional machinery reassert itself over the eleven-year-old still living somewhere behind the ribs. *Focus. Catalogue. Later you can feel things.*

Theo had been here. At the founder's funeral, three months after his adoption, standing at the edge of the crowd like a guest at someone else's wedding. Which made sense — the Marshes would have brought him, the whole town turned out, it would have been stranger if he hadn't come.

Except.

Except that Theo had never once mentioned it. Never mentioned Marrow's Bend at all, in any of the letters that thinned and stopped over the years, in the one phone call five years ago that ended badly, in the silence that followed. Hazel had assumed distance. Now Hazel wondered what else the distance had been covering.

Hazel looked at the boy's bowed head a long moment, memorizing the exact grain of the photograph, the position of every figure relative to every other, locking the whole composition into permanent storage. Then set it aside, face down, the way Corinne had kept her folder.

* * *

The portrait hung in the town hall rotunda, and Hazel went to see it at dusk, when the building emptied and the janitorial staff left the great doors unlocked for the evening cleaning crew.

It was famous the way small-town things are famous — reproduced on the chamber-of-commerce brochure, on the sesquicentennial plate, in the lobby of the Bellwater Inn. *Ashford Bell, Founder.* Twelve feet of oil paint dominating the rotunda's north wall: Bell standing at a railing overlooking a painted river, one hand resting on a rolled survey map, the other extended toward the horizon as if beckoning the future forward. Behind his shoulder, ghosted in sepia glaze, the skyline of Marrow's Bend itself — courthouse spire, mill, bridge — rendered smaller than life, the town literally painted beneath his gaze.

Hazel had seen reproductions all week. The original was different. The original had weight.

They stood before it for a long time, letting the eye travel the canvas the way it traveled documents — systematically, greedily, noting everything.

The signature in the lower right corner: *R. Calloway, 1993.* Painted the year before the death. Fine. A living man commissions his own monument; founders always do.

But Hazel's eye snagged where trained eyes snag — on the varnish. The surface gloss was uneven. Across the upper third of the canvas, over the sky and the ghosted skyline, there was a faint tonal shift, a rectangle of slightly warmer amber, visible only when the dying daylight hit at an angle. Not damage. Not restoration. A seam.

Someone had worked on this painting after it was finished. Substantially. The kind of overpainting that happens when a canvas needs to say something new.

Hazel stepped closer, close enough that the guard rail's shadow fell across the frame, and looked at the sky above Bell's head. Under the warm varnish, faint as a watermark, there was texture that didn't belong — a suggestion of different brushwork beneath, something structural, something that had been covered rather than cleaned.

Painted over. The thought arrived quietly and refused to leave. Someone had taken a finished portrait of Ashford Bell and painted on top of it — and not recently. The overpainting was aged, settled, decades deep. Whatever the canvas originally showed, it had been changed within a few years of its completion, changed carefully, expensively, by someone with access and motive.

Hazel pulled out a phone and photographed the seam from four angles, knowing the camera wouldn't capture what the eye could. Some evidence only existed in one place, and that place was currently walking out of the rotunda wearing Hazel's coat.

In the painting, Bell's painted eyes looked out over the painted town, serene and certain, presiding. Thirty years dead and still the largest thing in the room.

"You're new," said a voice.

An old man with a mop bucket had appeared at the rotunda's edge — the evening custodian, seventy if a day, cardigan under his coveralls. He nodded at the portrait with the easy contempt of a man who had dusted it for decades.

"First time seeing the original," Hazel said.

"Hm." He wrung his mop. "Folks come stand there sometimes. Stare at him like he's going to say something." He squinted at Hazel. "You're the one that was out at the cemetery Saturday. When they dug him up."

Word traveled fast in towns like this. Faster than cars. Possibly faster than light.

"That's me."

The old man considered Hazel for a moment, then lowered his voice as though the portrait might hear. "My daddy helped dig that grave in '94. Second shovel on the left." He nodded toward the painting. "Said it was the easiest dig of his life. Ground already turned soft." He picked up his bucket. "October ground don't come soft, miss. Not around here. Not ever."

He shuffled off toward the east wing, wheels squeaking, leaving Hazel alone beneath twelve feet of painted certainty.

Soft ground. An easy grave. As if someone had known, well in advance, exactly where the coffin would need to go — and how little it would need to hold.

Hazel stood a moment longer, the custodian's words settling into place beside the death certificate, the funeral photographs, the overpainted sky. Four facts now, each innocent alone, each beginning to lean against the others.

Then Hazel looked one last time at the edge of the seventh photograph — not the painting, the photograph, which lived now in perfect resolution behind Hazel's eyes — at the thin-shouldered boy staring at his shoes at the back of the crowd.

Tomorrow, Hazel decided, they would pay a visit to the town archivist.

Tomorrow, Hazel would knock on Theo's door.
