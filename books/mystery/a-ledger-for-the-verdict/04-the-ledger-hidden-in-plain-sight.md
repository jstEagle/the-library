## 4. The Ledger Hidden in Plain Sight

The rain came at dusk the way it always came in November — sideways, patient, as if it had all the time in the world to wear the town down to nothing. Edmund Blackwood watched it from behind the counter of Thornbury's Books, a mug of tea gone cold at his elbow, and did not watch it at all, because his eyes were on the diary.

Marion Verdict's diary. Open to the entry he had read eleven times now.

*The accounts don't reconcile. They never reconcile. I keep thinking if I go through them one more time, slowly, the way Papa taught me, the numbers will confess. Numbers always confess. You just have to ask them in their own language.*

He read the phrase again. *In their own language.*

He had spent thirty years asking numbers to confess. Fraud cases, mostly, in the years before the homicides found him — the tedious middle years of his career that nobody remembered because nobody writes novels about a man who reconciles ledgers. But he remembered. Numbers had a language, and Marion Verdict had spoken it fluently, and somewhere in that language was the reason she had stopped writing in her own hand.

He turned back through the diary, hunting the references he'd underlined in pencil — lightly, so the pencil could be erased, so the book could be returned to its shelf as if untouched. He had learned long ago that the best way to work a case was to leave no evidence you'd worked it.

*Tuesday. The accounts again. He moves it through the trust the way water moves through limestone — you never see the river, only the caves it leaves behind.*

*Friday. I've started keeping my own book. If they want a ledger, I'll give them one they can't burn.*

There it was. *My own book.*

Marion Verdict had kept a ledger of her own — a record of what she'd found in the trust's accounts. And that ledger was not in the missing-persons file. Reyes had shown him everything the police held, and there was no ledger, no notebook, no loose arithmetic in Marion's handwriting beyond the diary itself.

So either the ledger was destroyed.

Or it was hidden.

Edmund set down the diary and looked out at his shop — really looked at it, for the first time in months, the way you look at a room when you begin to suspect the room is looking back.

* * *

Thornbury's Books had been dying slowly for eleven years, and Edmund had let it, because a dying shop attracted no attention. The window display hadn't changed since the Queen's jubilee. The carpet was the colour of weak tea. The heating worked on alternate Tuesdays, or so he told customers, and the customers, who were few, laughed, and bought nothing, and left him to his shelves.

But the shelves — the shelves were the one part of the shop he had never allowed to decay.

Three months ago, a solicitor's clerk had arrived with a van and a donation: the estate library of a deceased collector, some four hundred volumes, accepted by Edmund without ceremony because accepting things without ceremony was what kept him invisible. He had catalogued them in his usual way — accession numbers in pencil inside the front cover, a card in the wooden catalogue drawer behind the counter, and the books shelved where they fit, which is to say nowhere in particular, in the order that a man with a decade of habit and no assistant achieves when he works alone at night.

He had not thought about that order since.

He thought about it now.

He fetched the accession book — a plain ledger, ironically, in which he recorded every donation — and turned to the entry from three months ago. *Estate of H. Calloway, per Mr. T. Finch, solicitor's clerk. Approx. 400 vols., mixed subjects. No valuation required.*

No valuation required. He had priced them himself, quickly, in pencil on the flyleaves, using his old system — a system so old it predated barcodes, a private shorthand he'd used since his first secondhand shop in another life: the price encoded the shelf, and the shelf encoded the date of accession, and the date of accession keyed to the accession number. A book marked *3/50* was three pounds fifty, shelved on bay 3, position 50. To anyone else, pencil marks. To Edmund, a complete map of the collection.

Marion had written: *If they want a ledger, I'll give them one they can't burn.*

And someone — someone — had delivered four hundred books to a failing shop run by a man nobody remembered, and those books had been shelved by a man who couldn't help encoding everything he touched.

Edmund's hands were not steady as he pulled the first volume. He noticed that and made himself notice that he noticed it, because noticing your own fear is the first discipline of the work. *Principles of Trust Administration*, 1968. Price in pencil: *7/12*. Bay seven, position twelve.

He went to bay seven. Counted to position twelve. The book that lived there — the book that had lived there since the night he shelved the Calloway donation — was *A Treatise on the Law of Trustees*, 1911.

Not the same book.

He stood very still in the aisle, rain drumming the dark window, and felt the old sensation come over him, the one he had no right to feel anymore, the one he had sworn off like drink: the hunter's joy. That cold bright lift behind the sternum when the world stops being furniture and becomes a puzzle with a killer at the centre of it.

Someone had rearranged his shelves. Or — he checked the accession book again, cross-checked the cards in the catalogue drawer — no. The cards matched the accession numbers. The accession numbers matched the books. But the prices — the prices on the flyleaves, in his own hand, or in a hand so like his own that—

He took the *Treatise* to the lamp at the counter and opened the flyleaf. The pencil mark read *7/12* in a hand that was almost his. The loop of the seven was wrong. He never looped his sevens. He had not looped a seven since 1987, when a forger he'd helped convict had looped his, and Edmund had gone home and trained the habit out of himself in an evening, writing sevens until midnight, a man at a kitchen table practising his own signature like a schoolboy.

Someone knew his pricing system. Someone knew his hand well enough to imitate it. Someone had written prices into his books that pointed not to shelves but to *something else*.

He sat down. He made tea he didn't drink. He began, methodically, the way Marion's Papa had apparently taught her, to go through the collection one book at a time.

* * *

It took him four days.

He worked at night, after the shop was dark, with the good lamp and a legal pad, and by the second night he had the shape of it. Forty-one of the four hundred books carried flyleaf prices in the false hand — the looped seven, a slightly heavy downstroke on the one. Forty-one numbers. And when he wrote them out in sequence, in accession order, they stopped being prices.

*7/12. 3/50. 12/04. 9/31...*

He had spent the first day assuming shelf positions, and the shelf positions meant nothing — bay seven position twelve held a book on alpine flora, bay three position fifty held a novel about a clergyman. But on the second night, unable to sleep, he had got up and written the numbers as dates, because dates were how ledgers thought, and the first number — 7/12 — was the twelfth of July, and the twelfth of July was the date, he remembered now from Reyes's file, of a transfer from the Verdict Family Trust to something called the Ashcombe Maintenance Company. A company that, per the file's single thin page on it, existed to maintain the Verdict estate grounds.

Except Edmund had walked past the Verdict estate twice in the past week, on the pretext of walking, and its grounds were maintained the way his carpet was maintained — which is to say, by neglect. The Ashcombe Maintenance Company was a cave the river had left behind. Marion's limestone. Money went in; nothing came out but silence.

He worked the numbers against the dates. Forty-one entries. Forty-one movements of money through the trust, spread across six years — small enough to pass audit, regular as a heartbeat, each one dressed as legitimate maintenance, insurance, consultancy. And against each date, an amount: the price, read not as pounds but as thousands. *7/12* — twelfth of July — *seven thousand*. *12/04* — fourth of December — *twelve thousand*. Six years of quiet siphoning, two hundred and sixty-odd thousand pounds in total, drained from a family trust into companies that did not maintain anything except the comfort of whoever signed the cheques.

Marion had found it. Marion had recorded it. And Marion — or someone who wanted him to find what Marion had found — had encoded her record into the flyleaves of a dead collector's library and shipped it to the one man in the county who read pencil marks the way priests read Latin.

He sat back on the fourth night, the legal pad filled with columns, and allowed himself thirty seconds of the joy. It was real joy, he'd forgotten how it felt — the whole world clicking into arithmetic, the sense that chaos was a costume and underneath it everything was ordered and therefore *catchable*. Numbers always confess. You just have to ask them in their own language. Marion had known. Marion had spoken to him across eleven months and a probable grave, and he had understood her, and for thirty seconds Edmund Blackwood — no, not that name, the other name, the buried one, the man who had once been *good* at this — for thirty seconds that man was alive at his counter under a dead man's lamp.

Then the dread arrived, because it always arrives on schedule, and it arrived with a question he should have asked on the first night and had been avoiding like a man avoiding a diagnosis.

*Who donated the books?*

He had the accession book open in front of him. *Estate of H. Calloway, per Mr. T. Finch, solicitor's clerk.*

He looked up H. Calloway in the next hour's research — the electoral roll, the probate notices in the county paper he still took out of habit, the parish records he could access without leaving a digital footprint because he had never, in eleven years, allowed the shop so much as a website. There was no H. Calloway. There had never been an H. Calloway. The probate notices listed no such estate. The solicitor's firm named on the delivery note — a firm he knew, a small firm in the market town — had no record of the donation when he telephoned the next morning and asked, in the mild baffled voice of a shopkeeper doing his accounts. The girl on the switchboard was helpful and knew nothing. Mr. Finch, she said, had left the firm in the spring. No forwarding address.

A dead man's library, delivered by a vanished clerk, from a firm that never handled it.

The collection had not been donated to Thornbury's Books. It had been donated to *him* — to whatever remained, under the faded signage and the cold tea, of the detective who had once been famous for seeing what was written in the margins. Someone had studied him. Someone had known his pricing cipher, his shelving habits, his accession rituals, his hunger. Someone had built a message that only he could read and then walked it through his door in a cardboard box, and he had shelved it, obediently, in his own hand, like a man filing his own indictment.

Which meant two things, and he wrote them on the legal pad in capital letters because capital letters made them harder to disbelieve:

*ONE: MARION TRUSTED ME. OR SOMEONE WANTED ME TO THINK SHE DID.*

*TWO: WHOEVER SENT THIS KNOWS EXACTLY WHO I AM.*

He sat with that second sentence for a long time. The rain had stopped. The shop was silent in the way only an empty shop at three in the morning is silent, a held-breath silence, and Edmund Blackwood, who had spent eleven years being no one, understood that his invisibility had not been pierced — it had been *used*. Someone had needed a man who noticed things, and had known precisely where to find one, and had known that he would rather die than report the finding to the police, because reporting meant names, and names meant the past, and the past was the one case Edmund Blackwood had never closed.

The diary lay on the counter beside the legal pad. He opened it to the last entry — the one dated four months after she vanished — and read it again with new eyes, because now he knew the diary was not only a record. It was a letter. It was addressed to whoever was patient enough to find it, and it had been found by exactly that person, and either Marion Verdict was alive and had engineered all of this from hiding—

—or she was dead, and her killer had kept writing in her hand, and had then, for reasons Edmund could not yet see, mailed her accusations to the one man who might act on them.

He looked at the columns of figures. Two hundred and sixty thousand pounds, moved through a trust, signed by someone with authority. *The one who signs*, Marion had written. He had a list of movements, dates, amounts, destination companies. He did not yet have a name.

But he had something else, something the numbers had confessed in passing, something he'd noted and not yet understood: the forty-first entry, the most recent, dated eleven days before Marion's disappearance, was different from the others. The amount was wrong. Not seven thousand, not twelve — the flyleaf price read *40/55*. Forty thousand, fifty-five hundred. Or — and the thought arrived with a chill that had nothing to do with the unheated shop — or it was not an amount at all.

Forty. Fifty-five.

He turned to the diary and counted the pages. Page forty began a run of entries he had read a dozen times without weighting them — a fortnight in Marion's life, domestic and dull, visits to an aunt, a purchase of boots, nothing but a fortnight of an ordinary woman being ordinary. Pages forty through fifty-five.

The last encoded entry in the ledger did not record a payment.

It recorded a *location*.

Edmund closed the diary, and stood in the dark of his shop, and understood that he had just been given instructions by a woman who might be a ghost — or by the man who had made her one.
