## 6. An Invitation to Dinner

The envelope came on a Tuesday, which was the day the shop received its least traffic and therefore the day Edmund did his real work—reconciling the till, updating the ledger of stock that had become something far more dangerous than an inventory, and reading Marion Verdict's diary again from the beginning with the patience of a man learning a language.

He heard the post before he saw it. The letterbox at the front of Blackwood's Books had a particular rattle, a dry wooden cough he'd known for eleven years, and most mornings it produced nothing but menus for restaurants that had closed and charity appeals addressed to the shop's previous owner, a Mr. Fenwick who had died in 2014 and whose name still received more mail than Edmund's own. That was by design. Edmund had built his life on other people's names arriving in his place.

But this envelope did not rattle. It slid through with the heavy, deliberate weight of good cardstock, and it landed face-up on the mat beneath the window, and even from behind the counter Edmund could see the engraving—the raised ink catching the grey morning light like frost on glass.

He did not move toward it at once. That was the first discipline they had taught him, back when there had been people to teach him anything: never approach a thing that frightens you at the speed it expects. He finished pencilling a price into the margin of a water-damaged copy of *The Moonstone*, set down the pencil, wiped his hands on his apron out of habit rather than need, and crossed the shop as though the envelope were a book he'd been meaning to shelve.

It was cream-coloured, heavy as a paving stone in miniature. The edges were bevelled. The return address was embossed rather than printed: *Verdict House, Ashcombe Vale.* And in the centre, in copperplate so fine it might have been cut by a jeweller:

*Mr. E. Harrow.*

Edmund stood very still.

Not Blackwood. Not the name on the lease, the name on the tax filings, the name painted in modest gold leaf above the door. *Harrow.* A name he had not written, spoken, or answered to in eleven years. A name he had buried with the same ceremony one buries a body—carefully, privately, and with every intention of never digging it up.

He looked through the window at the street. The baker's opposite was steaming its windows; a woman pushed a pram past the estate agent's; two boys were kicking a flattened football against the church wall. Ordinary Tuesday. No one watching that he could see, which meant either no one was or whoever was had learned from him.

He carried the envelope to the back room, laid it on the table beside the diary, and put on the cotton gloves he used for handling first editions. Paranoia, perhaps. But paranoia was only foresight wearing a coat, and he had learned to dress for all weathers.

Inside the envelope: a single card, engraved both sides. On the front, the invitation itself.

*Mr. and Mrs. Alistair Verdict request the pleasure of the company of*

*Mr. Edmund Harrow*

*at the Annual Anniversary Dinner of the Verdict Family*

*to be held at Verdict House, Ashcombe Vale*

*on Saturday the fourteenth, at seven o'clock.*

*R.S.V.P.*

On the reverse, in the same flawless hand—not typed, not printed, but *written*, a pen pressed into soft paper, someone sitting at a desk taking their time—a single line:

*You have her diary. We have your name. Let us discuss what each is worth over dinner.*

Edmund read it three times. Then he took off his gloves, folded them neatly, sat down on the old chair by the boiler, and discovered that his hands were shaking.

Not from fear of exposure—or not only that. He had rehearsed this moment, in the way a man rehearses his own funeral without admitting it. In the dark years after the trial, after the woman's face had begun appearing in the corner of rooms, he had imagined a hundred versions of the knock on the door, the letter, the voice saying *we know who you are.* He had imagined journalists. He had imagined police. He had imagined, once, in a nightmare that kept its grip on him for a week, the woman herself.

He had never imagined an engraved invitation.

That was what undid him. The courtesy of it. Whoever had sent this hadn't wanted to threaten him—they had wanted to *invite* him, to fold him into the machinery of the Verdict family the way one folds a guest into a seating plan. As though his exposure were not a weapon but a formality. As though Edmund Harrow had always been expected.

He got up, filled the kettle, and did not drink the tea. He let it go cold while he worked.

* * *

By noon he had turned the shop inside out, and by that he meant he had examined it the way he would examine a crime scene if it belonged to someone else.

The question was simple: how had they found him? He had been careful. He had been *very* careful. He had changed counties twice before settling here, taken no references that could be traced, paid cash for the lease through a solicitor in Leeds who asked no questions because Edmund paid him not to. The name Blackwood had come from a gravestone—he had walked a cemetery for an afternoon until he found a stone bearing a name common enough to hide in and rare enough to remember. For eleven years the system had held.

But systems hold against accident, not against intent. And someone had intended.

He went through the possibilities the way he once went through suspect lists, aloud, to the empty back room, because speaking made the logic honest.

One: the estate donation. The collection had arrived eight weeks ago, forty-one cartons from a house clearance in Ashcombe Vale, donated anonymously through a firm of solicitors. He had catalogued it gladly—it was a beautiful collection, too beautiful for a random clearance—and somewhere in those cartons, tucked into a volume of Dickens, had been Marion Verdict's diary. If the donation was bait, then whoever packed it had known exactly where it would land. Which meant they had known about the shop. Which meant they had known about *him*, before the diary ever arrived.

Two: Reyes. He dismissed it almost immediately and then, being honest, un-dismissed it. Imogen was loyal, but she was also thorough, and thoroughness was a kind of loyalty to something larger than any one man. Had she mentioned him to anyone? Had the file he'd read on Monday left fingerprints of its own? He thought of the thinness of that missing-persons file, its curated neatness, and felt the unease in it deepen. Someone had tidied that file. Tidy people noticed untidy men.

Three: Marion herself. This was the possibility he kept returning to, the one with teeth. Her diary spoke of a bookshop owner she trusted but never named. Trusted. What if trust, for Marion Verdict, had been reconnaissance? What if she had found him not despite his invisibility but *through* it—because a man hiding from his own name was exactly the man who could help a woman hide from hers? She had staged her disappearance; the ledger said so. Perhaps she had needed a template. Perhaps she had needed a confessor. Either way, she had known who he was, and whatever she knew could be taken from her.

And if it had been taken from her—if someone had found Marion after she vanished for the second time, the real time, the time the diary entries stopped mid-sentence in a hand that trailed off the page—then her knowledge had died with her, unless she had left it somewhere. Unless she had left it *here*, in the ledger woven through his shelves, waiting for a patient reader.

In which case the invitation was not a threat aimed at him.

It was a threat aimed at whatever she had hidden.

* * *

He spent the afternoon with the diary open to the last intact entry, reading it not as evidence now but as correspondence—as though the girl were writing to him across the gap of months, and he owed her the courtesy of listening properly.

*"A. smiled at me across the table tonight and I understood, finally, that the smile is the mechanism. Everything else—the charm, the generosity, the way he remembers the names of the staff—is housing for the smile. Grandmother signs what he puts in front of her. Pell drafts what he tells Pell to draft. I am the only one who reads the accounts line by line, and he knows it, and tonight the smile told me he has decided what to do about it."*

And below that, the entry Edmund had read a dozen times and would read a hundred more:

*"If anything happens to me, the answer is in the shop. He'll never look there. Nobody looks at books anymore—that's why I chose him. That's why he'll understand."*

*Nobody looks at books anymore.* He had taken it as a joke, the first time, a wry aside from a woman who loved reading. Now it sounded like instructions. Like a will.

The shop closed at six. At ten past six, with the door locked and the sign turned, Edmund did something he had not done since the donation arrived: he took the diary, the decoded pages of the ledger, and the invitation, and he photographed everything—every page, every number, every engraved flourish—with the cheap digital camera he kept in the till drawer for insurance purposes. Then he copied the files to a memory card, sealed the card inside a padded envelope, wrote no address on it, and hid it inside the hollowed spine of a damaged atlas on the top shelf of the geography section, where it would sit unnoticed until the end of time or until he retrieved it, whichever came first.

It was not much of a dead-drop. It was, however, a beginning. If he was going to walk into Verdict House under his own buried name, he was going to do it the way he had done everything else for a decade: leaving nothing essential where it could be taken.

He was locking the till when he saw the second envelope.

It had not been there at closing. He was certain of it—certain the way he was certain of footprints, of disturbed dust, of a book shelved half an inch out of true. The shop had been empty. The door had been locked. And yet there, on the counter beside the register, lay a plain white envelope with his assumed name on it, *Mr. Blackwood*, in ordinary ballpoint, and no stamp, because it had not come through any letterbox.

His heart performed a slow, heavy percussion against his ribs. He did not touch it. He circled the counter instead, checked the back door—locked, bolted, chain on—checked the cellar hatch, checked the window latches, and returned to find the envelope exactly where it had been, patient as a spider.

Inside was a single sheet of paper, handwritten, the same hand as the card's reverse. The same pen. The same unhurried pressure.

*We know you've started decoding it. We know what you were before you became a bookseller—we've known for some time, and we've said nothing, because silence is a currency and we prefer to spend it well.*

*Come to dinner. Come as Harrow or come as Blackwood; the family will call you whatever you wish. But come. Because the person who sent you that diary wanted you at this table, and it would be a poor tribute to her memory to disappoint both of us.*

*A.V.*

Edmund read it twice. Then he laughed—one short, involuntary bark of sound that startled him in the empty shop, because he could not have said whether it was fear or something adjacent to admiration.

*A.V.* Alistair Verdict. Or Annabel, the matriarch. The initials were a game, a small cruelty of ambiguity, and the message itself was a masterpiece of its kind: it confirmed surveillance without revealing its method, offered menace wrapped in flattery, and—this was the part that chilled him most thoroughly—it claimed Marion had *wanted* him at the table.

Had she? He searched the diary in his memory for any mention of a dinner, an anniversary, a table. There: an early entry, weeks before her disappearance. *"Grandmother insists everyone attend the anniversary dinner this year, even estranged cousins and new wives. She says the table is the family's conscience—everyone must see everyone, once a year, and account for themselves. I used to think it sentimental. Now I think it's an audit. One night a year, the whole estate in one room, and everyone performing innocence."*

An audit. One night a year when the whole estate gathered and every lie had to sit upright in its chair.

Marion had vanished eleven days before the last anniversary dinner. She had not merely fled—she had *missed the audit*. And now, a year later, the family was inviting the one outsider who might stand in her place at that table, the one man who could read the accounts line by line, and calling it hospitality.

They were not inviting him despite what he knew.

They were inviting him *because* of it. Because a thing known only in private can be denied forever, but a thing spoken at the anniversary table becomes part of the record—and whoever controlled the table controlled what entered the record. They meant to give him a seat and watch what he did with it. Or they meant to make certain that whatever happened to him at Verdict House happened in front of witnesses primed to call it an accident, a heart attack, a sad misadventure involving a man with a history of depression and a name nobody could quite verify.

Or—third possibility, the one that sat coldest in his chest—they were telling the truth. Marion had wanted him there. The diary had been delivered to him deliberately, the ledger left decodable, the whole apparatus of clues assembled by a woman planning her own disappearance and needing, afterward, one honest pair of eyes at the family's annual reckoning. She had chosen him because he was invisible, and invisible men see everything, and she had never imagined she wouldn't be alive to meet him at the door.

Either way, the arithmetic was the same. His decade of silence had ended the moment that cream envelope slid through the letterbox. The only choice remaining was whether he arrived at that table as a corpse-in-waiting or as an auditor.

Edmund took a clean sheet of paper from the drawer—the good stock, the heavy laid paper he reserved for special orders—and uncapped his fountain pen. His hand, he noticed, had stopped shaking.

*Mr. Alistair Verdict,* he wrote, in a hand he had not used in eleven years, the confident slanted script of Detective Inspector Edmund Harrow, *accepts with pleasure.*

He signed it *E. Harrow*, blotted it, and slid it into an envelope.

Then he sat for a long time in the lamplight, looking at the shelves that held a dead woman's cipher, and began, methodically, to plan what he would wear to his own unburial.
