## 4. Inspector Halloway Suspects

The dead man's shirt was the first thing that bothered Inspector Ambrose Halloway, and it was not because of the drowning.

Drownings bothered him on principle—St. Aldhelm's had a millrace, a river, a canal, and three centuries of practice at making accidents look like accidents—but this one bothered him in the specific, itch-under-the-collar way that meant his instincts were working overtime without being paid for it. He stood at the edge of the millrace at six in the morning, rain needling the collar of his coat, and watched the mortuary attendants work the body out of the reeds with the tenderness of men who had long ago stopped expecting gratitude from corpses.

"Time of death?" he asked.

"Twelve to eighteen hours," said Dr. Ferro, crouched at the waterline, her voice flattened by the wind. "Waterlogged. No signs of restraint. No bruising consistent with a struggle."

"No signs of anything," Halloway said.

Ferro looked up at him. She was a small woman with the patience of someone who performed autopsies for a living and therefore did not scare easily. "You want me to find something, Inspector?"

"I want you to tell me why a man drowns in four feet of moving water when there's a footpath along the entire length of the race and the nearest pub doesn't close until eleven." He nodded at the body. "And I want to know about the clothes."

The clothes were good. That was what bothered him. Good clothes, well kept—a charcoal overcoat, wool trousers, leather shoes worn at the outer heel by a man who walked a great deal but walked carefully. And beneath the coat, a white cotton shirt, laundered soft with age, with a small embroidered monogram at the cuff: **M.R.**

"Could be secondhand," said Constable Pell, who had the optimism of youth and would lose it within the decade if Halloway had anything to do with his training. "Charity shop. People buy monogrammed things all the time."

"People buy monogrammed *cufflinks*," Halloway said. "Nobody buys a stranger's shirts. They fit wrong. This fits right." He crouched, careful of the mud, and looked at the collar size, the sleeve length, the way the shoulders sat even on a body slack with water. "This shirt belonged to whoever wore it. Or someone built exactly like them."

The dead man's face, once they'd turned it toward the light, told them nothing. Sixtyish, maybe younger worn old, no wallet, no keys, no phone. The pockets held a bus token two years expired and a scrap of paper gone to pulp. A man with no name, drowned in a borrowed coat, wearing another man's initials at his wrist.

Halloway had been a policeman for twenty-six years, and in that time he had learned that the city of St. Aldhelm's committed its sins in one of two registers: loud or silent. The loud ones came with sirens and witnesses and grief-stricken families on the evening news. The silent ones came dressed as accidents, and they were the ones that kept him up at night, because the silent ones were committed by people who understood the machinery of the city—who knew which coroner signed off quickly and which constable couldn't be bothered to walk the towpath twice.

He stood, knees cracking, and looked down the grey length of the millrace toward the cathedral spire rising over the rooftops like a needle through cloth.

"Run the prints," he said. "And get me everything on the clothes. Manufacturer, batch, the lot. If that shirt was bought, someone sold it. If it wasn't bought—" He stopped himself. "Just run it."

* * *

The missing-persons report crossed his desk at four that afternoon, and Halloway read it three times before he let himself feel what he was feeling.

It had been filed fourteen months ago, by a woman named Edith Marlowe, reporting the disappearance of her lodger, one *Merritt Rowan*. The report was thin—the kind of thing a busy desk sergeant processed in ten minutes between tea runs. Merritt Rowan, male, sixty-one, last seen leaving his lodgings on Chandler's Lane. No next of kin. No employment history. The photograph attached was grainy and slightly out of focus, the kind of photo taken by someone who didn't want to take it.

The desk sergeant who'd filed it had noted, almost as an afterthought: *Reporter declined to provide own contact address; gave cathedral office as callback.*

Cathedral office.

Halloway pulled the file on the drowned man and laid the photographs side by side. The build matched. The height matched. The age was close enough, given what water does to a face. And when the lab report came back at seven—prints run through the national database, coming back to nothing, which itself was a kind of answer—he sat back in his chair in the empty incident room and allowed himself to say it aloud, just once, to the radiators:

"Merritt Rowan."

*M.R.* Monogrammed shirt. A false name filed under a false name, reported missing by someone who hadn't wanted to leave an address, and the only callback number in the system rang through to St. Aldhelm's Cathedral.

There were perhaps forty people who worked in or around the cathedral close. Most of them were vergers, cleaners, volunteers, the organist, the dean. But Halloway had spent fifteen months of his career, years ago, circling around one particular person in that building, and he had never managed to convince himself he'd been wrong to do it.

Mara Ravensworth. Archivist.

He remembered the case the way you remember a stone in your shoe—not painfully, exactly, but constantly. Three deaths in five years, each one ruled accidental, each one quietly reopened by nobody official at all. The bell tower fall. The almshouse fire. The communion wine. In each instance, evidence had surfaced after the fact—photocopied pages left anonymously on desks, a ledger entry highlighted, a witness statement mailed to the station in an envelope with no return address. The evidence had been impeccable. It had been *too* impeccable. It had been assembled with an archivist's hands: catalogued, cross-referenced, dated, sourced. Whoever sent those packets hadn't stumbled onto the truth. They had curated it.

And every trail, however carefully scrubbed, passed within a hundred yards of the archive.

Halloway had brought it to his superintendent, once, and been given the look that senior officers reserve for inspectors who suggest investigating the cathedral. *The Ashcombe family endowed that archive, Ambrose. The Ashcombe family endows half this city. Find something real or find something else to do.* So he had found something else to do, and he had told himself the story all reasonable men tell themselves—that coincidence is a load-bearing wall in any large city—and he had mostly believed it.

Mostly.

Now a man was dead in the millrace wearing a shirt with the archivist's initials, reported missing under a name that existed nowhere except in a filing cabinet in the cathedral office, and Halloway sat very still in the blue hour of the incident room and felt the old stone shift back into his shoe.

Because here was the thing. Here was the thing that made him turn off his desk lamp and sit in the dark like a man at a séance: the missing-persons report was *wrong*, and it was wrong in a way that was almost elegant.

Nobody files a missing-persons report for a lodger and gives the cathedral as a callback unless they want the report found. Nobody invents a name whose initials match a monogrammed shirt unless they want the connection made—or unless they are so practiced at constructing identities that the seam showed anyway. Either Mara Ravensworth had invented a fictitious lodger to explain away clothing that might surface somewhere it shouldn't—

Or someone had stolen Ravensworth's clothes, put them on a corpse, and then used Ravensworth's own tradecraft to salt the record. To make it look like the archivist had tried to hide the connection and failed.

Halloway pressed his thumb and forefinger against the bridge of his nose. Two possibilities, and both of them meant the same thing underneath: somebody in this city was playing games with identity, with records, with the quiet machinery of paperwork that decides what is true. And whether the player was the archivist or someone hunting the archivist, the game board was the same one Halloway had been staring at for fifteen years.

He got up, put his coat on, and drove to Chandler's Lane.

* * *

Edith Marlowe turned out to be real, which surprised him pleasantly. She was eighty-one, sharp as a hatpin, and she answered the door in a cardigan the color of moss and looked him over with the frank appraisal of a woman who had outlived everyone entitled to lie to her.

"You'll be the police," she said. "About Mr. Rowan."

"You remember him."

"I remember everything; it's my curse." She led him into a front room crowded with porcelain dogs and offered tea, which he accepted because refusing took longer. "Quiet gentleman. Kept to himself. Paid cash, always, three months ahead. Read a great deal. Helped me with my late husband's papers—there were boxes and boxes, and Mr. Rowan sorted them like he was born to it." She poured with a steady hand. "Then one morning he was gone, and Miss—" she paused, "—the young person who arranged the lodging came round looking terribly worried, and we filed the report together."

"The young person," Halloway said.

"The archivist. From the cathedral. Lovely creature. Quiet as dust." Mrs. Marlowe smiled into her tea. "You're wondering if I ever saw the two of them in the same room."

The inspector set his cup down with exaggerated care. "Is that a question you anticipated?"

"It's a question everyone asks eventually, dear, when a person keeps to themselves as much as that one did. No. I never saw them together. I saw the archivist maybe six times in two years, always about the lodger, always worried, always helpful." She looked at him over the rim of her cup. "And now your man's turned up dead in the millrace wearing the archivist's initials, and you've come round here with your hat in your hands hoping an old woman will tell you the archivist invented him."

Halloway said nothing, which was answer enough.

"He didn't invent him," Mrs. Marlowe said. "I fed the man supper for two years. He had a scar on his left hand from a burn, old, and a way of humming hymns under his breath when he read. He was real. Whether he was *Merritt Rowan*—" she shrugged, porcelain dogs watching from every shelf, "—well. Names are coats, Inspector. You of all people should know that. You wear yours like it fits, but I'd wager it pinches."

He left her house at nine with the rain come back and his mind running the corridors it always ran. A real lodger, or a real enough one. An archivist who had placed him, vouched for him, reported him missing, and then—what? Lost track of him? Known exactly where he was? Known, perhaps, that a dead man in a millrace was going to need a paper trail waiting for him like a bed turned down for the night?

Or none of it. Or the archivist was being framed by someone who had studied the Ravensworth method the way a counterfeiter studies engraving.

Halloway sat in his car outside Chandler's Lane and did the thing he had learned to do instead of deciding: he made a list.

One. The drowned man's prints came back clean. A man with no history is either a ghost or a construction, and constructions have builders.

Two. The clothes were genuine, well-worn, correctly fitted. Not purchased for a corpse. Taken from a living wardrobe—which meant someone had access to the archivist's home, or the archivist had provided them.

Three. The missing-persons report was filed fourteen months ago. Whatever happened at the millrace, it had been in preparation for more than a year. Patient work. Archival work.

Four. Fifteen years ago, give or take, someone had dismantled three of the Ashcombe family's tidy little accidents using nothing but parish records and patience, and the only suspect he'd ever had was currently cataloguing donations in the crypt-adjacent silence of St. Aldhelm's, invisible as furniture, harmless as dust.

Unless they weren't harmless. Unless they never had been.

He started the engine. The wipers thumped once, twice, clearing a view of wet brick and dark windows, and Halloway thought about the superintendent's voice—*find something real*—and about how the most real thing in this city had always been paper. Paper decided who owned what, who owed whom, who was alive, who was dead, who had testified and been buried for it. Paper had convicted and acquitted more souls than any judge. And somewhere in the cathedral archive, in the ledger room where the light never quite reached, a person who understood that better than anyone alive was shelving documents tonight, unaware—or perfectly aware—that the law had finally come back around.

He wouldn't accuse. Accusation required proof, and proof required time, and besides—if he was wrong, he'd hand the Ashcombes' lawyers a gift-wrapped grievance, and if he was right, he'd spook the one person in St. Aldhelm's who might actually lead him to the truth. No. He would watch. He would pull the threads gently: the shirt manufacturer, the millrace footpath cameras, the coroner's docket for the morning, the list of everyone who'd accessed the missing-persons file since it was created.

That last one he requested before he went home, sitting in the car with his laptop balanced on his knees, and the result came back while the rain ticked on the roof.

Fourteen accesses in fourteen months. Desk sergeants, routine audits, a records clerk. Nothing unusual—except the date of the most recent access, logged eight days ago, from a terminal in the central records office, under credentials belonging to a detective constable who had transferred to Bristol two years ago and whose account had never been deactivated.

Someone inside the police system had opened Merritt Rowan's file a week before a man wearing Rowan's shirt floated into the millrace.

Halloway closed the laptop slowly. Outside, far off across the wet roofs, the cathedral spire caught the sodium glow of the streetlamps and held it, thin and pale, like a bone standing upright in the dark.

"Well," he said to the empty car, in the tone of a man acknowledging an opponent across a chessboard. "Let's see who's been reading my mail."

He drove home the long way, past the cathedral close, and noted—with the part of his mind that never stopped taking minutes—that the archive window on the lower floor was lit. Just one lamp. Just one figure, bent over a desk, patient as sediment.

He didn't stop. He didn't slow. But in the morning, when the duty roster came out, Inspector Ambrose Halloway assigned himself to the millrace drowning personally, and he began, quietly and without telling a soul, to keep a file of his own—one page to start, one line written in his tight, unhurried hand:

*The archivist knows more than the dead do. Find out which of them is lying.*
