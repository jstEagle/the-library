## 9. The Choice of the Keeper

The crypt did not echo. That was the first thing I understood about it, standing there with my phone light shaking against the stone: sound went into this room and did not come back. Three hundred years of listening had taught the walls to keep things.

Edmund Vasse sat on a plain oak chair at the center of the floor, the way a man sits by a fire he has owned longer than the fire has owned him. He was not a corpse and he was not a statue. He was worse than both: he was *well*. Pink-cheeked, clear-eyed, dressed in a coat of good black wool that had been mended and mended until the mending was the coat. His hands lay open on his knees like a man who had long ago stopped needing to hold anything.

"You screamed so beautifully, little one," he said, and his voice was warm as a hearth, and I hated that it was warm, hated that some animal part of me wanted to lean toward it the way I had once, at six years old, in this exact room.

"I'm recording," I said. It was the only weapon I had brought, and we both knew it was a pebble.

"Of course you are. You've been recording since you got off the train. It's what they make you for." He said *they* the way other men said *weather*. "Sit. Your legs will go. Mine went in 1741 and they've never entirely come back."

I did not sit. I stood at the foot of the steps with the ledger under my arm like a breastplate and asked the question I had carried down eleven stone steps and across three hundred years of small-town silence.

"What are you?"

He smiled. It was a kind smile. That was the horror of it — it was genuinely, exhaustingly kind, the smile of a man who had had three centuries to perfect his patience.

"I'm the founder," he said. "That's not a title, Beatrix. It's a function. A town is a body, and a body needs a heart, and a heart needs blood. Every generation, the witness feeds the ledger, and the ledger feeds me, and I hold the town together the way a keel holds a boat — invisibly, underwater, where no one thanks you." He spread his hands. "Alderfen has never had a famine. Never had a fire that spread. Never lost a war. Do you know why?"

"Because someone drowned in 1897."

"Cordelia didn't drown." He said it gently, correcting a child's arithmetic. "Cordelia *transcribed*. She was my first witness — the first who understood what she was carrying. The drowning was the story we agreed on, because a town can live with a tragedy far more easily than it can live with a truth. Tragedies close. Truths stay open forever. You of all people should understand that. You've made a career of refusing to let things close."

The phone light trembled. I steadied it against my sternum.

"Maren Quill is dying," I said. "She passed me the ledger on her deathbed. Chapter and verse. If every witness dies within a year of handing it on—"

"Then Maren is already past the border, and she knew it when she called you. She didn't give you the book to save herself. She gave it to you because the book *must* be given, and she wanted it given to someone who would ask questions." His eyes were old in a way that had nothing to do with years — old the way a well is old. "She liked your podcast. She told me. She said you had the two qualities the work requires."

"Which are?"

"Stubbornness," said Edmund Vasse, founder of Alderfen, "and mercy. In that order."

* * *

He laid it out the way a solicitor lays out a will — plainly, without flourish, because the horror was in the plainness.

Option one. I took the ledger. I took the chair. I took the function. The town stayed whole; the ritual continued; some child, thirty years from now, would be brought down these steps and would scream, and I would sit in this chair and call it beautiful, and mean it, because the meaning was part of the maintenance. I would not die. I would not age. I would watch Halcyon grow old, watch the town grow old, watch everything I loved become the thing I tended.

Option two. I burned the ledger. The inheritance unraveled. The keel came out of the water. Edmund dissolved — he said *dissolved*, and he said it the way you'd say *retired* — and Alderfen would be a town like any other town, with fires that spread and wars that took its sons and a slow ordinary decay that no ritual could hold off.

"Or," I said, "option three. I walk out of here, I publish everything, and the town decides for itself."

He looked at me for a long moment with something that was almost fondness.

"That's not an option, little one. That's just option two with extra steps. A town that knows what it's built on can't keep building on it. The knowledge *is* the burning."

"And the livestream I started twenty minutes ago?"

"Is buffering," he said, not unkindly. "The crypt has never been friendly to signals. The town made sure of that. You'll find the upload fails, and you'll find that no one is surprised, and you'll find that the failure teaches you something the ledger cannot." He rose from the chair — slowly, joints that had been mending since the reign of Anne — and held out his hand. "Choose. That's all a keeper does, in the end. Everything else is commentary."

I did not take his hand.

I took out the ledger instead, and opened it to the last written page, and read aloud the newest entry — my own name, in ink two decades old, in a hand I almost recognized as mine. And beneath it, in fresher ink, a line I had not seen in Maren's kitchen:

*The witness chooses. The keeper is chosen. Be both, or be neither.*

"What does that mean?" I asked.

"It means," said a voice from the top of the steps, "that I wrote it, and I've been waiting thirty years for someone to read it out loud."

* * *

Halcyon came down the stairs the way she did everything — like a woman arriving slightly late to a party she had organized. She was not dressed for the anniversary dinner anymore. The pearls were gone. She looked, for the first time in my life, like my aunt rather than the town's.

She held a tin of paraffin and a box of matches, and Edmund watched them the way a drowning man does not watch a rope.

"You," I said.

"Me." She descended the last steps. "You want the confession now or after? I'd rather do it now, while I can still pretend I'm the villain. It's easier than being the coward."

"Aunt Halcyon—"

"You were six." Her voice did not waver, and I understood suddenly that it had taken her thirty years to make it not waver. "Maren brought you down — she was keeper's hand then, before her hands failed — and the ritual requires a witness to be *marked*, not just named. Marked means brought here. Marked means he looks at you, and the ledger takes the ink, and the ink takes the memory, because a witness who remembers can't live in the town. A witness who remembers either runs or goes mad, and we'd had enough of both." She looked at Edmund. "But you came back wrong anyway. You came back screaming at night. Asking who was under the town hall. Drawing the crypt on the backs of envelopes. So I did what the ritual didn't do. I took the rest. I took all of it. I sat at your bedside with a cup of tea and the family's oldest trick, and I took my niece's childhood from her to spare her this exact staircase."

"This exact staircase," I repeated. "This exact choice."

"You were the only person I've ever loved more than the town." She said it flatly, a fact laid on the table like a deed. "And I still almost didn't do it. I want you to carry that. Whatever you choose down here, carry that: I hesitated. That's the one sin this family never commits, and I committed it, and it made me love you more, and it made me weak, and tonight I'm handing you the matches because I am done being the generation that kept things."

Edmund had not moved. But something in the crypt had changed — a pressure, a listening, the walls leaning in.

"Halcyon," he said, "you know what burning costs."

"I know what *keeping* costs," she said. "I've been paying it for sixty years. It's the only bill I've ever read to the end."

I looked at the ledger in my hands. My name in old ink. My name in new ink. The line between them: *Be both, or be neither.*

And I understood, finally, what Maren had written — not a riddle but an instruction. The witness chooses. The keeper is chosen. Every keeper before me had been *chosen* — by the town, by the family, by the function — and had called it duty because calling it duty was easier than calling it surrender. But I had been a witness my whole life. Witnessing was the one inheritance they couldn't erase, because they'd built it back into me every time they took something away.

I could be both. Or I could be neither. But there was a third thing the ledger didn't have a word for, and I was holding it in my hand: the one who chooses *out loud*.

"Edmund," I said, "when I burn this — how long does the town have?"

He considered. Honest to the last, the way only the very old and the very guilty are honest.

"Generations," he said. "Not forever. Nothing is forever. That was always the lie we told."

"Good," I said. "Then they get the truth and the time. That's more than you gave anyone."

I struck the match.

Halcyon did not flinch. Edmund closed his eyes — not in fear, I realized, but in *relief*, three hundred years of relief arriving all at once, the way a held breath finally leaves the body — and the flame went to the corner of the first page, where Cordelia's handwriting began, *In the year of our Lord 1697, I set down what I witnessed—*

The fire took it fast, faster than paper should burn, as if the ledger had been waiting to be asked.

And then the crypt did something it had never done in three hundred years.

It echoed.

My own breath came back to me off the walls, and under it, faint as a radio between stations, a sound I had heard once before, at six years old, and had spent my whole life trying to transcribe: a scream, going up the stairs, going out into the town, going — for the first time in three centuries — all the way out.

The last of the ledger crumbled to ash that was not grey but gold, the color of the moss that had lined an empty coffin, and Edmund Vasse, founder of Alderfen, smiled at me with genuine, exhausting kindness, and said:

"Thank you, little one. Now run. The town is going to want to know what it heard — and the first person at the top of those stairs is going to be holding a new book."

I took the stairs two at a time.

At the top, through the crypt door, I could already hear it: the anniversary dinner, a thousand voices going quiet all at once, and beneath the silence, the sound of a page turning.
