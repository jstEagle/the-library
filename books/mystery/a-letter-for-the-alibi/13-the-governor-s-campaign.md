## 13. The Governor's Campaign

The banners went up on a Tuesday, which felt like an insult to Tuesdays everywhere.

Isandro noticed them first from the window of Rosa's diner, where she'd taken to eating breakfast with the regularity of a woman conducting surveillance disguised as enjoying huevos rancheros. Blue and gold, snapping in the salt wind along the harbor road: **FERRER FOR A FUTURE**. Beneath the name, in smaller letters, the slogan that made her jaw tighten every time she read it: *Punta Clara deserves a governor who never forgets his own.*

"Never forgets his own," Isandro murmured into her coffee. "Unless they're legally dead and inconveniently alive."

Rosa set down the coffee pot with the particular care of a woman who had opinions and was choosing, for once, not to voice them. She failed.

"You're going, aren't you," she said.

"I'm considering it."

"You're going." Rosa wiped her hands on her apron and sat down across from Isandro uninvited, which was itself a small revolution—two weeks ago she'd have stood at a respectful distance, wringing that apron. "Aurelio Ferrer launches his campaign on the pier Saturday. Whole town will be there. Free empanadas. Camille says the man hasn't eaten one in his life, but he'll hold one up for the cameras like it's a newborn."

"That's politics."

"That's *theater*," Rosa said, "and you're going to sit in the front row of it wearing that face you make when you've already won an argument nobody else knows is happening."

Isandro looked down at her face. It gave away nothing, as far as she could tell. It had given away nothing through three depositions, two mediations, and one memorable arbitration where opposing counsel had actually asked if she was a hologram.

"I don't make a face," she said.

"Mija." Rosa patted her hand once, briskly, the way you'd pat a dog that didn't know it was wet. "You make the face."

* * *

Saturday arrived wrapped in fog that burned off by ten, leaving behind a sky so clean and blue it looked laundered for the occasion. The pier had been transformed. A stage. A sound system. Bunting in Ferrer blue. Folding chairs arranged in neat rows facing the water, as though the sea itself were expected to applaud.

Isandro arrived early—not out of eagerness, but because arriving early meant she could watch people arrive, and watching people arrive was half of any investigation. She took a seat at the end of the third row, close enough to see everything, far enough back to be furniture.

The town turned out. Of course it did. Punta Clara was a place where memory ran long and grudges ran longer, but it was also a place where free food was free food, and where a governor—even a *maybe*-governor—might mean dredged harbors, repaired seawalls, jobs for kids who'd otherwise leave. Isandro watched fishermen in their good jackets, shopkeepers with their children, old men who'd known Aurelio Ferrer when he was just a rich boy with a fast boat. They came the way people come to a funeral: to witness, not necessarily to mourn.

Teo was there, standing near the harbor office with his arms crossed, radiating the enthusiasm of a man attending his own audit. He caught Isandro's eye and gave her a nod so slight it might have been a muscle spasm. *I'm here. I hate it. We're agreed.*

Camille did not come. "I have patients," she'd said, which everyone understood to mean *I would rather perform my own appendectomy.*

Lucía came because refusing wasn't allowed for fifteen-year-olds, and because, as she'd whispered to Isandro outside the diner that morning, "Marisol said once that you should always watch what powerful people do with crowds. Crowds are how they check their locks." The girl had gone pale after saying it, as if the words had escaped without permission. Then she'd added, quickly: "Don't tell anyone I talk like that. People already think I'm weird."

"You're not weird," Isandro had said. "You're well-trained."

Lucía had looked at her then with an expression Isandro couldn't parse—something between gratitude and grief—and slipped off toward the pier before Isandro could ask what she meant.

* * *

Aurelio Ferrer took the stage at noon exactly, because powerful men are always punctual; it's one of the ways they demonstrate the universe cooperates with them.

He was smaller than Isandro expected. Photographs gave him height he didn't possess, and a jaw he hadn't earned. In person he was compact, silver-haired, with the tanned, well-rested look of a man whose problems were handled by other people. His suit probably cost more than Teo's boat. His smile was calibrated to the millimeter.

But he was good. Isandro had to give him that. He was very, very good.

He talked about the harbor first—the silted channel, the boats that couldn't launch at low tide, the young people leaving for the city because Punta Clara had nothing to offer them but scenery. He talked about his grandfather who'd built the cannery, his father who'd kept it alive through two recessions, himself who'd dragged the family business into the modern age. He talked about hard work and harder weather and the kind of people who stayed when staying cost something.

And then, near the end, his voice changed. Softened. Dropped into the register men use when they want you to lean in.

"Some of you knew Marisol Vega," he said.

The crowd went still in that particular way crowds go still—not silent, but *held*, like a breath.

"She was going to marry my son. She was going to be part of this family, part of this town's future. And seven years ago, the sea took her." He let the pause stretch. Somewhere in the audience, a woman began quietly crying, and Isandro thought: *that grief is real, whoever it belongs to.* "I think about her every day. I think about what this coast takes from us, and what we owe each other in return. When I'm your governor, I will never forget that debt. I will never forget *her*."

Applause. Warm, genuine, complicated applause. Isandro found herself clapping too before she caught her own hands and stopped them, furious at the reflex.

Because she'd read the diary. She knew what the sea had *actually* taken—or rather, what it had been falsely credited with taking. And she had just watched a man stand over the grave of a woman he'd helped hunt, and invoke her name like a blessing, and receive love for it.

It was, she thought, the most obscene thing she'd ever witnessed professionally. And she had once watched a man contest a will on the grounds that his dead mother has appeared to him in a dream and revised it.

* * *

The speech ended. The crowd surged forward to shake hands, eat empanadas, be seen. Isandro stayed in her chair a moment longer, watching Aurelio Ferrer work the line—every handshake firm, every child touched on the head, every elderly woman addressed by name. He was a machine built entirely of charm, and the machine was running smoothly.

Which was why she noticed the moment it stuttered.

A man detached from the cluster of security at the stage's edge and began moving through the crowd—not toward Ferrer, but scanning faces. Methodically. Row by row. He was big, gray at the temples, with the flat, patient look of a man who had done security work long enough to stop performing it. He wore an earpiece and a jacket just slightly too heavy for the weather.

Isandro knew him.

She knew him the way you know a face you've studied in a photograph you took yourself—the boathouse, three weeks ago, the door jimmied and the diary drawer disturbed, and later, reviewing her own photos of the marina's security footage, a figure crossing the frame at 2:14 a.m. with a flashlight cupped against spillage. She'd zoomed in until the pixels betrayed her. She'd memorized what the pixels left her.

Gray temples. Heavy jacket. A way of walking like the ground owed him money.

She had perhaps four seconds to decide what to do, and she spent all four of them doing nothing, because running was confirmation and hiding was worse. Instead she picked up the campaign pamphlet someone had left on the empty chair beside her and began reading it with the absorbed interest of a swing voter.

His shadow fell across her.

"Ma'am."

She looked up. Up close he was even more familiar—the same heavy jaw, the same flat eyes—but now she could add details the footage hadn't given her: a scar through one eyebrow, a wedding ring worn smooth, and a laminated credential on his belt that read **D. OKONKWO — SECURITY DIRECTOR, FERRER CAMPAIGN**.

"Enjoying the event?" he asked. His voice was pleasant. Everything about him was pleasant, the way a riptide is pleasant right up until it isn't.

"Very informative," Isandro said. "I liked the part about the harbor channel. My firm has clients with moorings here."

"Your firm?"

"Sandoval & Associates. Estate law." She smiled the smile she used with hostile trustees. "I'm in town administering an estate. You may have heard of it. Vega."

Something moved behind his eyes—not surprise, exactly. More like a card being marked. "Small town," he said. "Lots of estates."

"Lots of drowned women, too, apparently." She held his gaze. "That was a joke, Mr. Okonkwo. Lawyers make them when we're nervous."

"Are you nervous?"

"Never. It's bad for billing."

He almost smiled. Almost. Then he glanced past her shoulder—at the crowd, at the exits, at some mental map only he could see—and said, lightly, "You should introduce yourself to Mr. Ferrer. He loves meeting new residents."

"I've met the family," Isandro said. "Emilio and I are old acquaintances now."

This time the flicker was unmistakable. Okonkwo filed it away somewhere behind those flat eyes, and Isandro understood, with cold clarity, that she had just told the enemy's intelligence chief something valuable for free—and that the alternative, letting him think she was a stranger, had died the moment he recognized her from the boathouse anyway.

"Old acquaintances," Okonkwo repeated. "Well. Enjoy the empanadas, Ms. Sandoval."

He walked away, unhurried, and Isandro watched him go and did the arithmetic she hated most: *he knows I know, and now I know he knows I know.* The boathouse search hadn't been random vandalism. It had been reconnaissance, and she'd been photographed doing her own reconnaissance of the reconnaissance, and somewhere in a campaign office there was probably a folder with her name on it getting thicker.

* * *

She found Lucía afterward, sitting on the breakwater with her knees pulled up, watching the stage crew strike the bunting.

"He saw me," Isandro said, sitting down. The concrete was cold through her coat. "The security chief. He searched the boathouse—I'm sure of it—and today he confirmed who I am."

Lucía didn't answer right away. Below them, the tide worried at the rocks with its endless patience.

"Did you see his face during the speech?" Lucía said finally. "Okonkwo's. Everyone watched Ferrer. I watched Okonkwo."

"And?"

"During the Marisol part." Lucía's voice dropped. "When Ferrer said the sea took her. Okonkwo looked at the water. Just for a second. Like he was checking whether it was going to say anything." She hugged her knees tighter. "People don't look at the ocean like that unless the ocean knows something about them."

Isandro stared at her. Fifteen years old, and reading a security chief's micro-expressions like a probate filing. *Well-trained,* she'd said that morning. Trained by whom? By a woman who'd spent seven years teaching a child to watch crowds and check locks.

"Lucía," Isandro said carefully, "when Marisol tutored you—what did she teach you, exactly? Besides ciphers."

The girl was quiet for a long moment. Gulls argued over something in the shallows. When Lucía spoke, her voice had gone careful and flat, a child's imitation of an adult's composure, and it was worse than tears.

"She taught me that the truth is like a lifeboat," she said. "You only get in it when the ship is actually sinking. Not before. Because if you climb in too early—" she shrugged, elaborately casual, "—you drown anyway. Just colder."

Isandro felt something twist under her ribs. She thought of the sealed letter waiting in Lucía's keeping, *for when the lawyer asks,* and wondered how much longer the ship could stay above water—and how much colder the water was getting.

Down on the pier, the last of the blue-and-gold bunting came down. Workers folded it into boxes, efficient and anonymous, as though the whole bright morning had never happened.

Her phone buzzed. Unknown number. A single text:

**Nice speech, wasn't it? — T**

She looked up. Across the lot, Teo leaned against the harbor office door, phone in hand, watching her with an expression that was either a warning or an invitation.

Probably both.
