## 19. Dead Man's Testimony

The hospital in Punta Clara smelled of antiseptic and salt, as if the sea itself had been asked to disinfect the place and had only half obliged. Isandro signed the visitor log at six in the morning, her handwriting steadier than she felt. The night nurse looked at the name twice.

"You're the third person asking after Mr. Ferrer," the nurse said. "The other two didn't sign."

"Then I'm the first one doing it correctly," Isandro said. "Which room?"

"Fourteen. And before you go up—" the nurse lowered her voice, glancing down the corridor where a man in a gray jacket sat too still on a plastic chair, "—he's been there since four. Says he's family."

Isandro followed the nurse's glance. Gray Jacket was perhaps thirty, built like a man who lifted weights for a living rather than pleasure, and he was watching the elevator bank with the patience of someone paid by the hour. He did not look at her. That was the tell. Everyone in a hospital corridor looked at everyone else; it was the one place strangers made eye contact out of shared misery. A man who refused to look was working.

"Family," Isandro repeated pleasantly. "How nice. Mr. Ferrer has so much of it."

She took the stairs.

* * *

Emilio Ferrer looked like a man who had been unfolded and refitted by someone in a hurry. His left arm was in a cast to the shoulder, his face carried the purple-yellow map of the airbag's affection, and an IV line ran into the back of his right hand. But his eyes found her at the door and something in them—relief, or its close cousin—was unmistakable.

"The lawyer who never loses," he rasped. "They told me you'd come. I told them you'd come at an hour that would annoy the nursing staff."

"They were right on both counts." She pulled the chair to the side of the bed, positioning herself so she could see the door in the window's reflection. Old habit. New necessity. "You went off the coast road."

"I went off the coast road." He said it flatly, testing the sentence for lies and finding none he could sell. "My brakes. Or my judgment. At that speed it's hard to say which failed first."

"Your father's security chief followed me back from the mainland yesterday. Same car model as the one Teo saw parked near the boathouse. Same driver, if the build is anything to go by."

Emilio closed his eyes. For a moment Isandro thought he'd drifted off, but when he spoke again his voice had dropped into something rawer. "I called him from the road. After we talked. I told him I was going to sign whatever you put in front of me, and that he should tell my father the way sons disappoint." A breath that might have been a laugh, if laughter could bruise. "I think he decided to disappoint me first."

"Are you saying your own security chief ran you off the road?"

"I'm saying I'm not going to swear to things I can't prove. You taught me that much in one afternoon." He turned his head toward her, wincing. "But I'll swear to what I know. All of it. Get your recorder."

She set her phone on the blanket between them, pressed record, and recited the date, time, location, and names for the file. Then: "State your full name for the record."

"Emilio Aurelio Ferrer Vega." He said his mother's surname deliberately, letting it sit in the air like a flag planted. "And I'm going to start seven years ago, because if I start last week none of this will make sense."

What followed took forty minutes and cost him visibly, each sentence extracted like a splinter. He told the phone about the night Marisol came to him, pale, holding printouts of her own surveillance photographs—herself at the market, herself at the chapel, herself through her own bedroom window—and how she'd said *your father watches me the way other men watch weather*, and how Emilio had laughed, because laughing was easier than believing. How she'd been gone within the month. How the drowning had been announced before the search was even properly organized, and how he'd stood at the funeral listening to his father eulogize a girl Aurelio had never once invited to dinner, and understood, all at once and forever, what kind of house he'd been raised in.

"And then?" Isandro prompted, when he paused too long.

"Then nothing. For years. I drank, I worked, I did what Ferrers do." His good hand flexed against the sheet. "Three years ago I found the second file. My father kept investigators on retainer—Vega surveillance, ongoing. Ongoing, Isandro. Seven years after we buried an empty box. I confronted him. He told me I was grieving a fantasy and that the family's future required certainty." His jaw worked. "I copied everything. Photographs, payment records, the lot. I've had them in a safe deposit box under my mother's name ever since, waiting for courage I apparently needed a broken collarbone to find."

"Where's the box?"

"First National, mainland branch. Key's taped inside the base of the lamp in my study—the ugly lamp, the one my father hates. He'll have torn the house apart by now looking for exactly that, which is why I'm telling you instead of writing it down." He managed something like his old charm, frayed at every edge. "See? I'm learning."

"Last question for this session." She leaned forward. "The night Marisol escaped the first time. Someone inside the Ferrer house arranged the boat. Teo says initials: A.V."

Something crossed Emilio's face—not surprise. Confirmation arriving late. "Ana Vega was in and out of our house constantly those last months. My father liked having her nearby. He said she was *cooperative*." He spat the word like a seed. "I don't know what she did. But I know my father trusted her, and my father trusts no one he hasn't bought."

Isandro stopped the recording, saved two copies—one to the cloud, one to a drive she'd sewn into her coat lining three days ago, a paranoia she now considered fully vindicated—and looked at the man in the bed.

"You understand what happens when this goes public."

"My father ends. Possibly I end with him." He smiled, and it was terrible and true. "Worth it. Tell Marisol—" He stopped. Started again. "Tell her I'm sorry I laughed."

* * *

He'd barely finished signing the certification page—his signature a wounded scrawl, witnessed by the night nurse, who read every word before countersigning with the thoroughness of a woman who had clearly once been lied to by a doctor—when Isandro's phone buzzed.

Lucía. Which was wrong, because Lucía did not call. Lucía texted, in lowercase, with the economy of a teenager conserving emotional bandwidth.

She answered on the second ring. "Lucía?"

"He's here." The girl's voice was a whisper stretched thin. "The gray-jacket man. He's sitting outside the diner and Rosa told me to call you and then she went out to talk to him and she told me to lock the office door and not to open it for anyone but her voice."

"Stay on the line. Don't hang up no matter what you hear."

Through the phone, muffled, Isandro heard the diner bell—Rosa's bell, the one she claimed had hung there since before either of them was born—and then Rosa's voice, pitched bright and carrying, performing calm for an audience of one frightened girl. Words surfaced in fragments. *...not welcome... ...my diner, my rules... ...you want coffee, order coffee...*

Then a second voice, low and official-sounding, and the word *detained* arrived with terrible clarity, followed by *assisting with an investigation*, followed by the scrape of a chair and the bell again, and then footsteps running, and a small fist hammering on wood, and Rosa's voice through the office door, cracking now: *"Mija, open, it's me—"*

Isandro was already moving, phone pressed to her ear, taking the hospital stairs three at a time while Lucía fumbled the lock. When the connection reassembled, the girl was crying without sound, the way children cry when they've learned crying costs extra.

"She made me hide the diary pages first," Lucía whispered. "Before she went out. She said, *if they take me, the paper goes to the lawyer.* She said you'd know what to do." A wet, furious breath. "I don't know what to do. I'm sixteen. I'm supposed to be worried about my chemistry final."

"Listen to me." Isandro pushed through the exit into the gray morning, hailing a cab with the arm she usually reserved for objections. "You are going to be fine, and here is why: you are the least important person in this whole affair, which makes you the most protected. Nobody spends their dangerous men on a sixteen-year-old. They spend them on diners and diaries and lawyers." She heard the girl hiccup something that was almost a laugh. "There. See? Already better material than your chemistry final. Now: Camille's house. Go now, walk fast, don't run—running attracts eyes. Call me from there. And Lucía?"

"Yeah?"

"You did exactly right. Both times. Write that down somewhere you'll find it when you're thirty."

She hung up and gave the cabbie Camille's street, then dialed Teo. It rang out. She dialed Camille. Voicemail. By the fourth unanswered call, the pattern had assembled itself with the cold elegance of a well-drafted contract: they weren't arresting witnesses. They were collecting them. Detaining the loose threads before the hearing could pull.

Her phone buzzed. An email, from the county probate clerk—a name she knew, a woman who had owed her a favor since Isandro had untangled her mother's estate pro bono years ago. The subject line contained no words at all, just a forwarded notice, and Isandro read it standing on the curb while the cab idled and the fog came in off the water like something being poured.

*Notice of Emergency Petition: In re Estate of M. Vega (deceased). Petitioner: Ferrer Family Trust. Grounds: fraudulent instrument, improper execution, contested capacity. Hearing scheduled: Thursday, nine a.m., Department Three.*

Today was Tuesday.

Forty-eight hours. And buried in the petition's boilerplate, drafted by lawyers who billed more per hour than Rosa grossed in a month, was the motion Isandro had been waiting for and dreading in equal measure: *petitioner further moves to exclude from any consideration the document known as the Vega diary, as the product of an unauthenticated source, obtained improperly, and bearing no evidentiary weight.*

They weren't just coming for the estate. They were coming for the diary itself—to have it ruled inadmissible, sealed, vanished into a file room the way Marisol had vanished into the sea. If they won that motion, everything Isandro held became paper. Everything the circle had suffered became anecdote. Everything Emilio had bled onto a certification page this morning became a dead man's testimony in the worst sense: sworn, recorded, and legally irrelevant.

The cab driver cleared his throat. "Lady, you getting in or conducting business from my bumper?"

"Both," Isandro said, and got in.

* * *

Camille's house was a narrow blue cottage with a garden gone gloriously feral, and by noon it had become the circle's bunker. Teo arrived first, sheepish and windblown—he'd been out on the water, radio off, because *the fish don't ask questions and neither do I*. Then Lucía, red-eyed, clutching the diary pages wrapped in a dish towel like contraband pastry. Then, to Isandro's genuine surprise, the priest from the will's witness list, Father Benítez, who let himself in with the ease of a man visiting family and announced, "Rosa called me from the county building. One call, thirty seconds. She said, *Benítez, they've finally given me something worth praying about.*"

"Is she charged?" Isandro asked.

"'Obstruction.' 'Harboring.' Vague as fog. No arraignment until Friday." The old priest settled into Camille's kitchen chair as though it were a pew. "Which means, if my Latin still serves, they intend to keep her uncomfortable through Thursday."

"It means they intend to trade her," Isandro said quietly. "Her release for the diary. Her freedom for the circle's silence. That's the offer that'll be on the table by tomorrow, dressed up as prosecutorial discretion."

Silence around the kitchen table. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Lucía stared at the dish towel in her lap.

"Then we don't trade," Teo said at last. It cost him; Isandro watched it cost him. This was the man who had rowed Marisol into the dark seven years ago, whose loyalty ran deeper than his fear and always had. "We don't give them one page."

"We don't give them one page," Isandro agreed, "because we're not going to need to. Listen carefully, all of you, because I'm only going to explain the plan once and then I'm going to lie to several important people about it, which is a thing lawyers are ethically permitted to do only on Thursdays."

Despite everything, Lucía snorted.

"Thursday morning, Department Three, I stand up and argue the will. Not the diary—the *will*. The diary is my ammunition, not my argument. The argument is arithmetic: a validly executed, validly notarized will dated three years after a declared death is either fraud or proof of life, and the Ferrer Trust cannot survive the second possibility, which is why they're trying to make the world stop at the first one." She looked around the table. "For that argument to land, I need three things. One: the notary. Two: Emilio's statement, already secured and duplicated beyond anyone's reach. Three—"

"Marisol," Camille said softly.

"Marisol," Isandro confirmed. "Or proof of her, recent enough to bleed. Because a judge can dismiss a lawyer, dismiss a diary, dismiss a widower with a grudge. What a judge cannot easily dismiss is a living woman standing in his courtroom asking the state to correct its own records." She paused. "I know what I'm asking. I know what invisibility has cost her to build and what it will cost her to burn. So I'm not asking the circle to deliver her. I'm asking the circle to make sure she knows the door is open, and that on Thursday at nine a.m., she will not walk into that room alone."

Father Benítez laced his fingers. "And the gray-jacket men? The ones collecting us?"

"By Thursday they'll be busy." Isandro allowed herself the smallest smile, the kind she usually spent only on opposing counsel who had just realized, mid-cross-examination, that they'd walked into her trap. "Because tonight, a certain false diary location becomes active, and certain very patient men get to spend forty-eight hours watching an empty fishing shed in San Andrés while their principal's empire files past them in a courtroom."

Teo grinned like a boy. "The shed with the leaky roof?"

"The shed with the leaky roof."

"That shed's been empty since the Carter administration."

"Then it should feel right at home with the evidence inside it."

The laughter that went around the table was thin and startled, the laughter of people remembering they owned the equipment. Even Lucía smiled, and then, abruptly, didn't, and slid the dish towel across the table to Isandro.

Inside, the diary pages. And tucked among them, in Marisol's slanted hand, a note Isandro hadn't seen before—addressed not to the lawyer who never loses, but simply:

*To whoever is holding these: thank you for keeping them safe. And Isandro—if it's you, and I suspect it is—check the margin of the entry dated the eleventh of March. I left you a message the day Ana's letters stopped coming. I think you'll understand why I wrote it in cipher. Some truths are only safe in code.*

Outside, the fog thickened over Punta Clara, and somewhere across town Rosa Reyes sat in a county holding room rehearsing her indignation, and somewhere on the coast road a gray-jacketed man reported to a candidate for governor, and somewhere in the dark of the water a second boat had once slipped free of the harbor carrying a woman who refused, with magnificent stubbornness, to be dead.

Isandro smoothed the pages flat and reached for Lucía's cipher key.

Forty-eight hours.
