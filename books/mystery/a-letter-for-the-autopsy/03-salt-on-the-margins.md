## 3. Salt on the Margins

The house above the tideline had opinions about winter.

Juniper Voss heard them every morning — a groan in the joists, a rattle in the storm windows, the chimney exhaling cold like an old man clearing his throat. She answered all of it aloud. "Yes, I know. Yes, I'm getting to it." Talking to houses was considered eccentric in Grayhaven, but then again, so was staying in one for thirty years after everyone else your age had left, and Juniper had long since stopped keeping score.

She was sixty-one now, and she still made coffee in Elin's old percolator, the dented aluminum one that boiled everything down to something resembling tar. It had been Elin's coffee. Drinking it felt less like grief and more like company.

Her mornings had a shape: percolator, woodstove, the slow inspection of the house's complaints, and then — since October — the letters.

They came on Tuesdays. Always Tuesdays, always the morning ferry post, always unsigned. The first one had been so ordinary she'd nearly thrown it out: *I am sorry for your loss. I know it was a long time ago, but loss doesn't keep a calendar.* The handwriting was small and precise, slanting slightly leftward, as if the writer were leaning away from their own words.

She'd kept it. Then came another, and another, each one gentle, each one strange. *You must have been very young to carry something so heavy. Seventeen is too young to be the last one who remembers someone whole.*

Juniper stood at the kitchen counter now, the newest letter open in her hands, flour on her knuckles from the bread she'd abandoned mid-knead.

*Dear Miss Voss,* this one began. *I hope you don't mind me writing again. You never answer, which I understand. Some doors only open from the inside. But I wanted to tell you that I've been reading about your sister. Not the newspaper version — the real one. The one who used to sing to the gulls off the north jetty because she said they were bad critics but loyal ones.*

Juniper sat down slowly at the kitchen table.

Elin *had* sung to the gulls. Off the north jetty, specifically, because the south jetty gulls were, in her sister's firm opinion, "philistines." Juniper had never told anyone that. Not the police, not the neighbors, not the priest who'd spent three patient years trying to get her to talk about anything at all. It wasn't in any article. It wasn't in any file.

It was in *her*.

She read the letter twice more, looking for the trick in it. There didn't seem to be one. That was the unsettling part — these letters weren't cruel, weren't prying. They were kind in a way that felt like being seen through a window you hadn't known was there.

*I won't ask you to write back,* the letter finished. *But if you ever want to say something out loud that you've only ever said to the house, I'm a good listener. Even at a distance. Especially at a distance.*

Juniper looked up at the ceiling, where the water stain above the stairwell had spread into a shape like a hand. "Did you send these?" she asked it. The house groaned, noncommittal.

That night, for the first time in thirty years of Tuesdays, she took out paper and a pen.

She didn't mail anything. She just wrote. *You're right that seventeen is too young. You're right that I was the last one who remembers her whole. Here is what I remember that nobody asked me:* And then the pen kept going, past midnight, past the point where the words stopped being careful.

In the morning she put the pages in a drawer instead of an envelope. But they existed now. That was different.

* * *

Astrid Hartwell drove toward the coast with the Voss file riding shotgun, belted into the passenger seat like a nervous relative.

It was a four-hour drive she'd made once before, thirty years ago, in a department car that smelled of wet wool and other people's cigarettes. This time it was her own ancient Volvo, which smelled of old paper and the ghost of Earl Grey, and which made a sound on hillsides like a man being asked to reconsider his choices.

"Nobody's rushing you," she told it.

Grayhaven announced itself the way dying towns do — a sagging welcome sign, a shuttered cannery, then suddenly the sea, enormous and indifferent, laid out below the road like a table cleared for a meal no one was coming to. Astrid pulled over at the overlook and looked down at the tideline. Somewhere down there, in the gray-green churn, was the place where a nineteen-year-old girl had come ashore in 1987, and the place where, according to a letter written by a dead woman, Astrid herself had gotten everything wrong.

"You flatterer," she murmured, and drove on.

The original coroner was named Dr. Ambrose Pell. Astrid found him in a care home two streets back from the water, in a room with a view of a parking lot, which struck her as the sea's idea of a practical joke. He was smaller than she remembered — everyone was — folded into a wheelchair by the window, his hands arranged on a blanket like two sleeping birds.

"Astrid Hartwell," he said when she introduced herself. His voice was a dry whisper, but his eyes did a slow, satisfied blink. "I wondered how long it would take you."

"How long it would take me to what?"

"To come back." He smiled with perhaps six teeth. "Sit down, girl. My legs are decorative and my time is rented, but my memory is the last thing working, and I intend to use it."

She sat. He studied her for a moment the way he'd once studied slides.

"You got my letter," he said.

Astrid went very still. "Your letter?"

"The one about the case. About the body." His whisper gained a thread of impatience. "Don't do that thing where you go polite and blank. I was reading faces before you were born."

"I haven't received any letter from you, Dr. Pell."

He frowned. The frown cost him effort; she could see it in his neck. "I wrote it weeks ago. Told them to post it. Said it was urgent." A pause, and then something flickered across his face — doubt arriving late to its own party. "Did I? Or did I only mean to?"

"Dr. Pell—"

"Moved," he said, cutting through her gently, the way tide cuts through sand. "The body was moved. After death. Before it came ashore." He raised one bird-hand and tapped his own sternum. "I wrote it in my report and they made me soften it. 'Consistent with' — bah. Consistent with nothing. Lividity doesn't lie, Hartwell. It pools where gravity puts it, and gravity put it wrong. She didn't drown where they said she drowned. She drowned somewhere else, and somebody carried her."

"Carried her where?"

"To the tideline. To be found." His eyes fixed on hers, bright and terrible. "Or to be found by *you*, specifically. Everyone knew you'd take the grid search. Everyone knew you were thorough. Thorough people find things. Maybe somebody wanted a thing found."

Astrid's mouth had gone dry. "Who would want that?"

Pell leaned back. The effort of the conversation was settling over him like snowfall; she could see him dimming, politely, by degrees.

"That's the question I've been dying — literally, girl, *dying* — to ask somebody for thirty years." He closed his eyes. "Come back Thursday. I have good Thursdays. On Thursdays I can finish sentences."

She stood to go, and his eyes opened one more time.

"Hartwell. The letter I sent. If it comes — and it will or it won't, my follow-through is weather these days — check the postmark." A faint, wicked smile. "I like to think I'm still punctual."

* * *

The Grayhaven public library occupied a converted chandlery that smelled of salt and glue, and its librarian was a round, cheerful woman named Dot who greeted Astrid like a returning relative and produced the 1987 newspapers without being asked twice.

"Voss," Dot said, thumping the bound volume onto a table. "Whole town still talks about it, when it talks at all. Mostly it doesn't. We're a town that prefers its tragedies pre-salted." She lowered her voice conspiratorially. "You'll want the microfiche too. Half the good details never made print. And you'll want to talk to the Kettlewells — they ran the ferry post back then. Postmistresses see everything. Well. *Saw*. Marge Kettlewell's in the ground now, but her daughter Ruthie runs the counter same as ever, and Ruthie remembers *everything*, mostly things people wish she wouldn't."

"Ferry post," Astrid repeated. Something tickling at the edge of her attention. "Mail comes in on the ferry?"

"Comes and goes. Once a day, weather permitting. Why?"

"No particular reason." But there was a reason, and it was sitting in her bag: a confession letter postmarked the day after Elin Voss's funeral, thirty years after Elin Voss's funeral. If the postmark was genuine — and she was increasingly afraid it was — then someone had either held a letter for three decades or posted one impossibly. And if the postmark was forged, then whoever forged it knew enough about Grayhaven's postal rhythms to make it look right.

Dot lingered, clearly hoping for gossip, and Astrid bought a bookmark shaped like a herring to reward her, and then sat alone with the bound newspapers while rain began ticking against the old chandlery windows.

The articles were exactly as she remembered them: tragic accident, storm-swollen seas, beloved local girl, services held at St. Brendan's. Photographs of Elin Voss smiling in a way that looked, to Astrid's older eyes, less like happiness than like performance. And in one article, a photograph of the mourners outside the church — black coats, bowed heads, umbrellas.

She found the sister immediately. Juniper Voss, seventeen, half a step behind the rest of the family, her face doing something complicated that the camera had caught and the caption ignored. Astrid stared at that face for a long time. Grief, yes. But underneath the grief, something else. Something that looked, in grainy 1987 newsprint, almost like *attention*. Like a girl watching the funeral to see whether anyone else noticed what she noticed.

Astrid turned the page. And stopped.

There, in the corner of a follow-up piece about the coroner's findings, was a small item she had no memory of reading, though she must have, thirty years ago: a brief note thanking the community for its cards and letters of condolence, sent to the Voss family *care of the ferry post office*.

Care of the ferry post office. Where Ruthie Kettlewell remembered everything.

And where, Astrid thought, her pulse picking up a quiet, cold rhythm, someone had recently been sending letters. Gentle ones. Weekly ones. Letters to a grieving sister from an anonymous friend who knew things only Elin — or Elin's last living witness — could have known.

Two people, being written to by the same invisible hand. Herself, with corrections and challenges. And Juniper Voss, with kindness.

Astrid packed up the herring bookmark, thanked Dot, and walked out into the rain toward the ferry post office, telling herself she was simply following procedure.

She was lying, and she knew it. She was walking toward the one person in Grayhaven who might understand what it felt like to receive a letter from the dead — and she wanted, badly, to watch her face when they talked about it.

Ruthie Kettlewell was already watching hers.
