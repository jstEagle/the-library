## 8. Rising Water

The envelope was waiting on the mat when Astrid came down on Thursday morning, and she knew before she touched it. She knew the way you know a kettle is about to sing — some small change in the pressure of the world.

She had planted the seed nine days ago. A false detail, buried in a public records request filed with the county archive: a note that the original 1987 search grid had been redrawn at 6:40 p.m. on the night of Elin Voss's drowning, per "Detective Hartwell's field log, entry eleven." There was no such entry. There was no such time. Astrid had invented it out of whole cloth, stitched it into an official-looking request, and mailed it to an office where exactly four people would see it. Then she had gone home and done what detectives do best, which is wait badly — pacing her flat above the bookshop, making tea she forgot to drink, telling herself that no one would take the bait because no one was watching.

The envelope in her hand said otherwise. Same heavy cream paper. Same careful hand. The postmark read GRAY HARROW, which meant it had been posted from the coastal town itself, not from wherever the writer lived — or the writer *was* Gray Harrow now, or had never left, or wanted her to think so. Every possibility forked like lightning.

She sat down on the bottom stair to open it, because her knees had opinions about standing.

*Dear Detective Hartwell,*

*I've been thinking about your field log. Entry eleven. The grid correction at 6:40 p.m. — such a precise thing to remember after all these years. You must have been very sure of yourself that evening. Or very frightened.*

*You're right about the tide, by the way. It always comes back for what it's owed.*

*Come to the water. Juniper will be there when it turns.*

Astrid read it three times. On the third pass, her hands were steady enough to notice what mattered: *entry eleven.* Verbatim. Not paraphrased, not guessed — quoted back with the exact false timestamp she'd seeded into a records request that existed in exactly one filing cabinet in the county annex.

So. The confessor had access to the county archive, or to someone inside it. Four people had handled the request: the clerk who logged it, the archivist who pulled the box, the intern who photocopied it, and the records supervisor who signed off. Astrid had already run quiet checks on all four. Three of them had no conceivable connection to 1987, to Elin Voss, or to Astrid herself. The fourth — the records supervisor, a soft-spoken man named Alden Pike — had retired two years ago and moved, according to his file, to a cottage outside Gray Harrow.

Her stomach did something complicated. Because that wasn't the impossible part. The impossible part was the last line.

*Juniper will be there when it turns.*

The letters had never named Juniper before. They had circled her — the condolences, the gentle nudges toward the shoreline, the darker turn in recent weeks — but they had never once put her name in ink addressed to Astrid. Naming her now meant one of two things: the writer was finished being subtle, or the writer wanted Astrid at that beach at that hour, and wanted her to bring whatever she knew.

Both, probably. People who played games this long always wanted both.

Astrid went upstairs, took down the Voss file — her private copy, the one that breathed — and laid everything on the kitchen table like a surgeon laying out instruments. The annotated pages in fresh ink. The photograph of her own bookshop taken from across the street. The three previous letters. And now this one, the fourth, the one that broke the pattern.

She made herself a pot of coffee she actually drank, and started building the cage around the answer.

Who could quote her unpublished notebook? Only someone who'd been in her flat, decades ago, when the notebook still lived in her desk drawer — or someone who'd been given it, or someone who'd *been there* when she wrote it. She turned that thought over slowly, the way you turn a stone to see what's underneath. In 1987 she'd spent eleven days in Gray Harrow working the drowning. She'd kept the notebook in her room at the Anchor Inn. Who had access to her room? The landlady. The maid. Herself. And —

No. She set the thought down carefully, like something that might go off.

Who could correct her case file with perfect accuracy? Someone who knew the investigation from the inside. Not a cop — she'd checked every living member of the old force; two were dead, one was senile, the rest scattered and clean. But there was another kind of person who lives inside a case. The family. The family knows everything: what the police asked, what they missed, what was said at the kitchen table afterward when the detectives thought the house was asleep.

And the Voss house sat above the tideline. The Voss house had a view of the water. The Voss house had held, for thirty years, one grieving sister who received gentle anonymous letters of condolence — letters that had recently begun urging her toward the shore.

Astrid stood up so fast her chair went over backward.

"Oh," she said, to the empty kitchen. "Oh, you clever, cruel—"

Because it fit. It fit horribly. Whoever wrote the letters had access to the county archive through Alden Pike's old position or his old friendships. Whoever wrote the letters had access to Astrid's notebook from 1987 — and Astrid suddenly remembered, with the cold clarity of a woman falling through ice, that she had interviewed Juniper Voss twice in that inn, and once, memorably, in her own room, because the girl had come to deliver a statement and it had been raining and Astrid had let her in out of decency. The notebook had been on the desk. Open. She remembered thinking, even then, *I should put that away,* and not doing it, because the girl was seventeen and wet and heartbroken and harmless.

Harmless. Thirty years of detective work, undone by the word *harmless.*

But no — the plan said the letters to Juniper were part of it too, and if Juniper was writing them to herself, then who was Alden Pike? Astrid pressed her palms against the table and forced herself to be a detective instead of a horror story. The trap had sprung; the false detail had surfaced verbatim. That narrowed the field to people with access to both the file and her life. If Juniper had written the letters herself, she'd have needed access to the archive — possible, if Pike was an old family friend, if grief made strange alliances. Or if Pike was simply the unwitting conduit, the man who processed a routine request without knowing he was feeding a ghost.

Either way, the geography didn't lie. The letter pointed to the tideline below the Voss house. The spring tide was due tonight. The storm coming off the North Sea had been forecast for two days, and the local radio had that particular cheerfulness stations reserve for weather that might kill you.

Astrid packed in ten minutes: the file, the letters, a torch, her phone, and — after a moment's consideration — the flask of whisky she kept for funerals and emergencies, having decided this qualified as both. She left a note pinned to the bookshop door: *CLOSED FOR WEATHER. BACK TOMORROW, PROBABLY.* It felt important, suddenly, that someone should know where she'd gone, even a stranger reading a sign in the rain.

Then she drove into the storm.

* * *

The road to Gray Harrow was ninety minutes of white-knuckle theater. Rain came sideways off the fields in sheets, wipers losing their argument with it, lorries throwing up walls of spray. Astrid drove the way she did everything these days — competently, stubbornly, and with her knees complaining — and narrated the case aloud to keep herself awake and honest.

"Say it's her," she told the windshield. "Say Juniper wrote every word. The condolences to herself, the confession to me. Why?"

To force the truth out. That was the theory she'd been circling for a week, the one she didn't yet believe because believing it required a kind of heartbreak she wasn't ready for. A girl who'd lied at seventeen — whose lie had closed the case too neatly — spending thirty years marinating in it until she split herself in two: the penitent and the priest. Writing letters as a stranger so that someone, anyone, would finally drag the truth into daylight. Staging a funeral. Luring the town, luring the detective, luring herself to the water.

It was either the saddest thing Astrid had ever heard or the most dangerous, and the older she got the more she understood those were usually the same thing.

"And if it's not her," she said, taking the coast road turnoff at forty miles an hour, "then someone is grooming that woman toward a shoreline tonight, and I'm the only one who knows."

The radio said the tide would turn at 9:47 p.m. The letter said *when it turns.* The Voss house sat above the shingle bank where Elin had been found, and between the house and the water lay half a mile of flat sand that the spring tide would swallow whole by ten o'clock. Anyone standing at the tideline at high water would be standing in the sea.

Astrid's phone buzzed at the top of the hill. She pulled over — barely, half onto the verge — and looked at the screen. Unknown number. A text, short as a knife:

*You're late, Detective. She's already walking down.*

Astrid stared at it. Then at the clock: 8:52. Then at the sea beyond the dunes, invisible in the dark but audible now, a vast animal breathing under the rain.

Every clue had converged on one address and one hour, and the hour was now, and the address was downhill, and whoever had sent that text was watching the road — watching *her* arrive — which meant the game wasn't between Juniper and the water anymore. It was between Astrid and whoever wanted them both there.

She drove down.

* * *

The Voss house appeared out of the rain like a shipwreck that had decided to keep living — dark windows, one light burning low upstairs, gulls screaming around the chimney the way gulls do when the weather turns biblical. Astrid parked behind the dune fence, killed the lights, and got out into a wind that tried immediately to take her umbrella, her dignity, and possibly her life.

The path to the tideline was a groove of worn grass between the dunes. At its head, a single set of footprints led down, filling with rain. Small boots. Recent. Walking unhurried, the stride of someone who had made this walk a thousand times and expected to make it a thousand more.

Astrid followed.

The beach opened up gray and enormous, the tide already chewing at the shingle bank, foam running up the sand in long grasping fingers. And there, fifty yards ahead, at the exact line where wet met dry, stood a figure in a long coat, hood down, hair plastered flat, facing the water with the stillness of someone listening to it.

Juniper Voss. Sixty-ish now, but built the same as the girl in the 1987 photographs — narrow shoulders, chin up, defying the weather on principle.

"Miss Voss!" Astrid shouted into the wind, and started forward.

Juniper didn't turn. But she raised one hand — not waving, *stopping* — and called back over her shoulder, calm as Sunday:

"You shouldn't shout, Detective. He doesn't like it."

Astrid stopped dead, rain streaming down her face.

"*He?*"

"He's been writing to me for months." Juniper still hadn't turned. The tide surged up around her ankles and she didn't move. "He knew things about Elin. Things nobody knew. I thought — I thought God had finally noticed us." A small, terrible laugh. "And then tonight he asked me to wear Elin's coat to meet him. And I'm wearing it. So whichever one of us he wanted—" she lifted her arms slightly, presenting herself to the storm, to the sea, to the dark shape Astrid now saw standing far down the beach near the old groynes, motionless, holding something long and thin—

"—he's got."

The tide turned at 9:47. It was 9:31.
