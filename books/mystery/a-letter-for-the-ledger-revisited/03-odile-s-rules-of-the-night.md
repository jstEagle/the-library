## 3. Odile's Rules of the Night

The rules of the night shift were not written down anywhere. That was the first thing Odile Voss had taught him, back when he was a green kid with a headset too big for his skull and a tendency to panic whenever two drivers called in at once. *Nothing important is ever written down,* she'd said, tapping her temple with one lacquered fingernail. *Paper gets subpoenaed. Memory just gets tired.*

Isandro had been at Halcyon Cabs for six years now, and his memory was very good indeed.

He thought about that — about memory, and what it was for — as he sat in the break room at 4:40 in the morning with Vera Ostrander's diary closed on his knee and the vending machine humming its one long note across from him. The diary had ridden home in his jacket pocket like a stone. He'd told himself he would hand it in. Of course he would hand it in. A missing woman's property turns up in a company vehicle; you log it, you bag it, you call the number the police left on a flyer that was still taped to the break room wall, sun-bleached now, Vera's smile gone the color of weak tea.

He had not called anyone. He had read forty pages instead, and then he had read them again, because somewhere around the entry dated eleven days after her disappearance — an entry describing, in flat and patient detail, a man reading this diary in a break room with a humming vending machine — his hands had stopped being entirely steady.

*He reads slowly,* the entry said. *Like someone who learned to read late, or who never trusts the words to stay put. Good. Careful readers find things.*

The vending machine hummed. Isandro Kavanagh, careful reader, sat very still and felt the floor tilt a degree or two beneath everything he understood about the world.

At 5:15, the night shift began its slow exhale. The last of the airport runs cleared, the radio traffic thinned to the occasional yawned check-in, and the first gray light started working at the edges of the lot windows like a burglar too polite to break anything. And at 5:20 exactly — because it was always 5:20 exactly — Odile Voss came down from the dispatch floor to see how the night had gone.

She came down the stairs the way she did everything: without hurry, without apology, in low heels that clicked a rhythm Isandro could have set a metronome by. She was sixty-one years old and built like a lighthouse — broad, immovable, lit from within by a pair of eyes that missed nothing and forgave less. Her hair was iron-gray and cut short, her cardigan was the color of oatmeal, and she wore a wristwatch so old it had to be wound, which she did every morning at 5:22 while surveying the break room like a captain taking ownership of a newly captured ship.

"Kavanagh." She wound the watch. "You look like a man who lost an argument with his own bed."

"I've been up since three."

"I know. Cab 44 came back empty." She said it lightly, the way she said most things, but Isandro had learned over six years that Odile's lightness was load-bearing. It held things up. You just couldn't always see what. "That's twice this year. Once in March — driver walked off after his divorce, remember, poor bastard left the car at a Denny's — and once tonight."

"The door was unlocked," Isandro said. "Keys in the ignition. No sign of trouble."

"Mm." She poured herself coffee from the communal pot without asking whose it was, because all coffee in the building was conceptually hers. "And yet here you are at five-fifteen, pale as the milk carton girl, when your shift ended at four."

Isandro weighed, in the space of half a second, roughly nine different answers.

Odile watched him weigh them. That was the thing about her — she let you have the whole performance, start to finish, and then she told you which parts she'd believed.

"You found something in that cab," she said.

"A diary," Isandro said. Because lying outright to Odile Voss was like lying to a polygraph that also raised you. "Under the seat. Wedged down where the seat meets the frame — you'd only find it if you were cleaning, or if you dropped something and went fishing."

"And?"

"And it belongs to Vera Ostrander."

For the first time in six years, Isandro saw Odile Voss set down a coffee cup without finishing it. She didn't spill it, didn't fumble it. She simply placed it on the counter with a care that was somehow louder than a crash, and then she looked out the window at the lot, at cab 44 sitting there under the sodium lights with its empty driver's seat, and she was quiet for a count of five.

"Show me," she said.

* * *

Here is what Isandro could not explain, later, even to himself: he showed her the outside of the diary and nothing more. He handed it over — cover, spine, the embossed initials V.O. in gold that had mostly rubbed away — and he watched her turn it in her hands, and he heard himself say, "It's blank," and he watched her hear him say it.

Blank. Forty pages of dated entries in a dead woman's handwriting, including entries written after she died, including an entry about him, and he stood there in the gray morning light and said *it's blank* the way a man says *nice weather.*

Odile opened the cover. Turned a page. Turned another. Her face did absolutely nothing, which from Odile was itself a kind of weather report. Then she closed it and held it out to him.

"Well," she said. "Either it's blank, or you're protecting something. Or someone." She smiled — the small, dry, real smile she saved for drivers who passed their probation, the one that had gotten him through his first winter. "I'll tell you what I told you when you were twenty-three and crying in the stairwell because you sent a car to the wrong hospital. The truth is always easier to carry than the story you build on top of it. Heavier, sometimes. But easier."

"Yes, ma'am," Isandro said, which was what everyone said to Odile, and which meant, variously: yes, no, maybe, I love you, and leave me alone.

She studied him a moment longer. Then, briskly, as if closing a ledger: "Police already have a file on the Ostrander thing. Detective named Ruiz — decent, overworked, allergic to paperwork. I'll call him at a civilized hour and tell him a diary turned up. Unwritten in. Company property recovered, routine." She picked up her coffee again, finished it in three efficient swallows. "You'll bring the book to me before end of shift tomorrow. Whatever state it's in."

"Whatever state it's in," Isandro repeated, and hated how much it sounded like a confession.

"That's the spirit." At the doorway she paused, one hand on the frame, and glanced back at him with something almost gentle in the lighthouse eyes. "Vera Ostrander used to sit right there, you know. Third stool. Did her crossword during slow hours and complained the coffee was criminal. She wasn't wrong." A beat. "People leave holes, Kavanagh. Best thing you can do is not fall into them."

Then she went upstairs, heels clicking her exact rhythm, and Isandro sat down on the third stool because apparently he wanted to make everything worse.

* * *

The day crew arrived at six in their usual cloud of complaints and cologne, and Isandro drifted through the handoff in a fog. Marcus took the board. Priya argued with the fuel invoice. Somebody's radio crackled a joke about the mayor. The ordinary machinery of Halcyon Cabs ground forward, indifferent, and Isandro understood for the first time how strange it was that it should be so indifferent — that a woman had vanished out of this exact building six weeks ago and the machinery hadn't so much as changed its oil.

He went home. He slept four hours, badly, in a bedroom where the diary sat on the nightstand like a guest who wouldn't take a hint. He dreamed of dispatch boards stretching to the horizon, every fare a little light, and one light blinking in a pattern he almost recognized.

When he woke at four in the afternoon, he made eggs he didn't eat and tried to think like a dispatcher instead of a suspect.

Because that was the other thing the diary entry had done to him. *He reads slowly.* Someone knew how he read. Someone knew about the vending machine. Either Vera Ostrander was alive and had been watching him in a break room she hadn't entered in six weeks — or the diary was bait, laid carefully, aimed precisely, by someone who knew the building and knew him.

And if it was bait, then the question stopped being *what happened to Vera?* and became *who wants Isandro Kavanagh to go looking?*

He got dressed. He drove back to the lot early, because there was nowhere else his feet wanted to go, and because — though he'd have denied it under oath — he wanted to be where Odile was. Six years of nights will do that to a man. When the world goes sideways, you want the person who taught you which way the floor tilts.

* * *

The evening shift was quiet, and Odile ran it like she ran everything: with a pencil, a board, and a memory that shamed the computer. Isandro watched her work from the second chair, the way he had a thousand times, and tried to see her fresh — tried to see her the way a stranger would, or a detective.

Odile Voss had run the night desk at Halcyon for nineteen years. She'd trained four generations of dispatchers, including two who'd gone on to bigger companies and one who'd gone on to city council. She knew every driver's birthday, every driver's ex, every driver's tell. She kept a tin of butterscotch candies in her desk for drivers coming off bad calls, and she kept a second, unlabeled list in the same drawer that nobody had ever seen the inside of. She had buried a husband, raised a nephew, and once talked a gunman out of cab 17 using nothing but the fare meter and a tone of voice. Ambrose Halcyon himself — founder, legend, the man whose face was on the lobby wall shaking hands with two mayors — called her *the real boss* every time he came through, and everyone laughed, and everyone knew it wasn't entirely a joke.

She was, in short, the best person Isandro knew. Which was why, at 9:40 that night, when she appeared at his elbow and said, "Walk with me, Kavanagh," his stomach performed a small, professional drop.

They walked the length of the dispatch floor, past the board and the bank of radios, past the framed photo of the 1998 company picnic where everyone was squinting and Ambrose was laughing at whatever Ambrose was always laughing at.

"Tell me about the cab," Odile said. "Not the diary. The cab. Walk me through it like a fare."

Isandro did. The return at 3:04, logged automatic off the transponder. Door unlocked, keys in. Fuel at three-quarters. No damage. Diary under the passenger-side rear seat, wedged deep.

"Who found it?"

"I did. Cleaning rotation."

"Mm. And the last fare on 44's log that night?"

Isandro pulled it up without thinking — that was the point of Odile's drills, six years of *run the record, Kavanagh, don't remember it, run it* — and frowned. "Last paid fare drops at Delmore and Ninth, 1:38 a.m. After that, nothing until the auto-return ping at 3:04. An hour and a half of unlogged driving."

"An hour and a half," Odile repeated softly. "In a city where a cab can cross town in twenty minutes." She stopped walking. They were standing by the window that overlooked the garage, and below them cab 44 sat in its bay, washed and vacuumed and innocent. "You know what I did when you told me about the diary yesterday morning? Before I came downstairs?"

"No."

"I checked the garage camera." She said it mildly, watching the cars below. "Storage retention's ninety days. The bay camera caught 44 coming in at 3:06. Driver's side. Empty. Nobody got out, nobody got in. Car rolled to a stop against the tire stop and just... sat there, like it was waiting for someone to notice."

Isandro's mouth had gone dry. "You didn't mention the camera yesterday."

"You didn't mention the diary was blank when it clearly isn't." She turned and looked at him then, fully, and there was no anger in it — that was the terrible part. There was patience. There was something that might, in a kinder light, have been fear. "We're going to do each other the courtesy of pretending we're both bad liars, Kavanagh, instead of pretending we're good ones. Now. I'm going to ask you three questions, and you're going to answer them honestly, and then we're going to get back to work and neither of us will speak of this again until we have to."

"Okay," Isandro said.

"Did you read the whole diary?"

"...Most of it."

"Does it say where Vera is?"

"It doesn't say where she is. It says—" He stopped. The break room entry sat behind his teeth like a coin he shouldn't swallow. *He reads slowly.* If he gave Odile that entry, he gave her himself. "It says things it shouldn't be able to say."

Odile nodded slowly, as if this confirmed some private arithmetic. "Third question. Has anyone else asked you about the cab? Anyone at all — cops, managers, drivers, anybody?"

"No. Should they?"

"No," she said, too quickly, and then, smoothing it: "No. Not yet. Ruiz will come around eventually; he's thorough in the way of men with too many files. Until then, this stays between us. The diary comes to me tomorrow, end of shift, and I decide what the police see. Agreed?"

Every instinct Isandro owned — the ones Odile herself had installed, the *run the record* instincts, the *nothing important is written down* instincts — rang like a struck bell. Handing the diary to Odile meant handing it into the building. Into the machine that had swallowed Vera Ostrander whole and kept running.

But refusing Odile meant telling her he didn't trust her, and he wasn't ready to live in a world where that sentence was even grammatical.

"Agreed," he said.

"Good boy." She squeezed his shoulder once — her hand heavy and warm through his shirt, the same grip that had steadied him through his first death call, his first winter, his worst mistake — and walked back toward the board, heels keeping their time.

Isandro stayed at the window. Below, cab 44 gleamed under the lights. Behind him, the radio murmured its nightly litany of addresses, most of them real. And it occurred to him, standing there in the warmth of his mentor's trust like a man warming himself at a fire he suddenly couldn't account for, that Odile had answered his question — *should they ask?* — one beat too fast.

And that she had known, before he'd said a word about cameras, exactly which questions to ask.

* * *

He didn't sleep when he got home. He sat at his kitchen table with the diary open to the final entry — the one dated three days from now, the one that read, in Vera's patient hand:

*The dinner will be on the anniversary. They always need an anniversary. He'll give a speech, and everyone will cry, and the books will balance beautifully. Come to the dinner, careful reader. Bring the numbers. I'll bring myself.*

Isandro read it four times. Then he went to the corkboard above his desk, where the company memo fluttered under its pushpin — *HALCYON CABS ANNUAL SERVICE AWARDS & REMEMBRANCE DINNER, honoring the memory of Vera Ostrander, Friday next, Grand Meridian Ballroom, black tie optional, founder Ambrose Halcyon presiding* — and he looked at the date for a long, cold moment.

Three days from now. The date the diary had named weeks ago, in ink, in a dead woman's hand.

Someone had planned this dinner the way Odile planned a shift: nothing important written down, everything accounted for.

Isandro reached for the phone, to call the police, to call Nell Ostrander, to call anyone — and stopped, because the first name his thumb knew by heart was the extension upstairs.

That, more than anything, frightened him.
