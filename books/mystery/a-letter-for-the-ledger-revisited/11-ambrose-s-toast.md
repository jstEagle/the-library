## 11. Ambrose's Toast

The press event was held in the lobby of the Halcyon building, which had been transformed overnight from a place where tired people argued about fares into a place where well-dressed people stood around holding glasses of orange juice and pretending it was champagne.

Isandro knew this because he'd been asked to attend. Not invited — asked. There was a difference, and Odile had made the difference clear at shift change that morning.

"Ambrose wants dispatch represented," she said, pinning her hair up with the two silver clips she always used, quick and precise, like she was closing a circuit. "You're the face of the night shift these days. Clean record, good posture. Try not to slouch."

"I don't slouch."

"You slouch like a man carrying invisible luggage." She looked him over, adjusted nothing, and nodded once, which was Odile's version of a hug. "Ten o'clock. Wear the good jacket."

So here he was, ten-oh-four, wearing the good jacket, standing near a potted fern that was almost certainly plastic, watching a crew of television people angle their cameras toward a podium draped in blue bunting. Someone had hung a photograph of Vera Ostrander behind the podium — enlarged, softly lit, taken from her company ID badge and blown up so large that the pixelation gave her face a strange, watercolor quality. Vera as rendered by a machine that only half remembered her.

That bothered Isandro more than he could say. He'd known Vera for eleven years of radio static and clipped addresses and the occasional 3 a.m. joke about the coffee. The woman in the photograph was a stranger wearing Vera's expression, the way a cab with the right medallion number can still be entirely the wrong car.

"You're Isandro, yes?"

He turned and found a young woman with a lanyard and a clipboard and the particular brightness of someone whose job today depended on everything going smoothly. "Kavanagh. Dispatch."

"Perfect. Mr. Halcyon may want to say hello to staff afterward. If he approaches you, just — be natural."

"Natural," Isandro said. "I'll give it my best shot."

She smiled the way people smile when they haven't listened, and moved on to brief the fern.

* * *

Ambrose Halcyon entered the room at 10:15, which meant he'd planned to enter at 10:15, because men like Ambrose didn't arrive; they were timed, like trains, like tides.

Isandro had seen him maybe a dozen times over the years — holiday parties, the fiftieth-anniversary gala, once in an elevator where Ambrose had held the door and said, "Night shift keeps this city breathing, son," and Isandro had floated for three days on it. That was the thing about Ambrose that no one ever mentioned out loud: being noticed by him felt like being awarded something. Even now, knowing what Isandro suspected — what the diary all but screamed — some old, loyal animal in his chest lifted its head when the man walked in.

Ambrose was seventy-one and carried it like a tailored coat. Silver hair swept back, reading glasses pushed up on his forehead though everyone knew he could see fine, hands clasped in front of him in the attitude of a man perpetually about to thank you. He stopped twice on the way to the podium to touch shoulders, to ask after wives and hip surgeries, to remember — or appear to remember — the name of a mechanic's daughter who'd just started nursing school. Each small transaction took four seconds and left the other person glowing.

*He knows everyone,* Isandro thought. *And everyone feels known.* And underneath the thought, colder: *A man can't skim millions from a city for fifteen years if the city doesn't love him. Love is the lock. He never had to pick it.*

The crowd hushed. Cameras found their marks. A woman from the *Ledger-Dispatch* uncapped her pen with the reverence of a squire drawing a sword.

"Thank you all for coming," Ambrose began, and his voice did the thing it always did — dropped half a register, went rough at the edges, the voice of a man holding grief politely at arm's length so it wouldn't spill on anyone. "I had hoped this day would never need marking. A year ago tomorrow, we lost Vera Ostrander. And I have spent much of this year learning that 'lost' is a word that does too much work. Because we haven't lost her. We've lost her presence. Her laugh in the break room. Her insistence that the fourth-floor vending machine was stealing from us — which, I am told, it was." 

A ripple of laughter, wet-eyed and grateful. Isandro felt his own mouth twitch before he caught it. *That's how he does it,* he thought, watching the crowd lean in. *He gives them one small true thing so they'll swallow the large false ones whole.*

"Vera came to me twenty-two years ago with a degree in accounting and a correction to our books," Ambrose continued. "Our first conversation lasted forty minutes and consisted mostly of her explaining, kindly, everything I was doing wrong." More laughter. "I hired her on the spot. Not because I enjoyed being corrected — though I've learned to — but because Halcyon Cabs has only ever been as honest as its ledgers. Vera was the most honest ledger we ever had."

Isandro's stomach turned slowly over, like a cab taking a corner too fast.

Because he'd read the diary. He'd read it four times now, cover to cover, and he knew that Vera had come to Ambrose with corrections exactly as described — and that somewhere along the way, the corrections had stopped being welcome. The diary's middle entries traced it in her dry, numbered hand: *Q3 discrepancy, flagged. Told to let it go. Flagged again. Meeting scheduled with A.H., then cancelled by A.H. Then another. Then the entry where her handwriting changed, got smaller, pressed harder into the page.*

*He's telling the true story backwards,* Isandro realized. Every fact in the speech was accurate. The sequence was the lie. It was like hearing a dispatcher read back a call log with the timestamps shuffled — every word real, the night itself erased.

"And so," Ambrose said, and here his voice gathered itself, climbed, became the voice from the anniversary galas and the chamber-of-commerce luncheons, the voice that had named a bridge after his late wife, "it is my honor — no. It is my obligation. To announce the Vera Ostrander Scholarship for Young Women in Accounting and Public Finance. Full tuition. Two recipients per year, in perpetuity. Because the best way to honor a woman who kept our books clean is to train the next generation to keep them cleaner."

Applause broke like weather. Somewhere a camera flash popped. The clipboard woman dabbed her eyes. Beside Isandro, a driver from the day shift — Marek, big hands, gentle heart — whispered, "Christ, that's beautiful," and wiped his nose with the back of his wrist.

And Isandro stood there in his good jacket feeling like the only sober man at a wedding where everyone else was crying happy tears for a couple he knew was already lying to each other.

*Seductive,* he thought. That was the word. Watching Ambrose was seductive even now, even armed with suspicion, because the charisma wasn't decoration — it was structural. It was load-bearing. Fifteen years of phantom fares and shell companies hadn't survived on cleverness alone. They'd survived on this: two hundred people in a lobby wanting so badly for the story to be true that they furnished it themselves.

It was sickening the way rich food is sickening — you only notice after you've swallowed.

* * *

The speech ended. The scholarship check — comically oversized, Vera's name in calligraphy — was presented to a beaming dean from the community college. Ambrose worked the room afterward, exactly as the clipboard woman had promised, moving through the crowd like current through wire.

Isandro stationed himself near the coffee urn with the strategy of a man avoiding a spotlight, which is, of course, the surest way to stand in one.

"Dispatch," said a voice at his elbow, warm as a heated car seat. Ambrose Halcyon, hand extended, reading glasses now down on his nose, looking at Isandro with an expression of frank, grandfatherly appraisal. "Isandro Kavanagh. Night dispatcher. Odile tells me you're the best she's trained in twenty years."

"She's generous," Isandro said, shaking the hand. Firm grip. Slightly cool palm. *Or is that my imagination? Everything is my imagination lately.*

"Not generous. Precise. Odile Voss has never inflated a number in her life." Ambrose smiled, and for one vertiginous second Isandro wondered — wildly, absurdly — whether the man knew. Whether this was the spider asking the fly how the web felt from the inside. Then the moment passed, because Ambrose had already turned slightly, angling them both toward the enlarged photograph, and his voice softened into something genuinely affecting. "Terrible business. Terrible. You knew her, I understand. Night shift."

"Eleven years," Isandro said. "Mostly through a radio."

"That's the thing about this company," Ambrose said. "Half our friendships are conducted through static. But you hear a person, truly hear them, when there's nothing to look at." He tapped the frame lightly with one knuckle. "I keep thinking about her last fare. Unlogged. She signed out a car that Friday and never brought it back. The police think — well. The river, probably. Drowned women and rivers, this city's oldest bad habit." He sighed, and the sigh was perfect, worn smooth as a river stone. "I'd give a great deal to know what happened to her. A great deal."

*I bet you would,* Isandro thought, and kept his face doing the honest, grieving-employee thing it was supposed to do, and marveled at himself — at how easily the performance came, at how much of his life had apparently been rehearsal for lying to a beloved founder in a lobby full of orange juice.

"Her sister's here somewhere," Ambrose added, scanning the room. "Nell. Estranged, sadly — families are complicated, aren't they? I hope she'll feel welcome at the dinner tomorrow. Grief should have witnesses." He patted Isandro's shoulder, twice, the exact cadence of a man closing a transaction. "Keep the night running, Kavanagh. This city sleeps easier because you don't."

And then he was gone, absorbed into a cluster of reporters, leaving Isandro holding a paper cup of terrible coffee and a fresh, cold certainty:

*Nell is here. Nell, who got one postcard with a medallion number. Nell, who is currently standing in a room with the man her sister ran from.*

He found her ten minutes later, at the edge of the crowd, in a gray cardigan, holding a program she hadn't opened. She looked like Vera around the jaw. When she saw Isandro, her chin lifted — recognition without warmth, the look of a woman deciding whether you were weather or threat.

"He asked about you," Isandro murmured, sidling close enough to speak under the ambient chatter. "Wanted to make sure you felt welcome tomorrow."

"Did he." Nell's voice was flat as a fare receipt. "Vera used to say he remembered everyone's name because names are cheaper than raises." She folded the program in half, then quarters, until it was a small hard square in her fist. "One year ago tonight, Isandro. Whatever she left you to find — whatever those numbers mean — tomorrow there will be two hundred cameras in that banquet hall and a man on a stage with a lifetime of practice."

"I know."

"Do you?" She turned and looked at him fully, and it was Vera's look, the unlogged-fare look, the one that saw past the uniform to the arithmetic underneath. "Because he just told this entire room a true story in the wrong order, and they applauded. Tomorrow he'll do it bigger. If we're going to beat him, we'd better stop reading his speeches and start reading his books."

Isandro went still.

His books. His records. Years of them, floor to ceiling in the archive room, every fare Halcyon had ever logged — and somewhere in all those columns of ordinary numbers, rounded and routine and trusted by every auditor in the city, Vera had hidden the truth in plain sight.

He was still staring at nothing when Nell touched his sleeve.

"You've thought of something," she said.

"Yeah," Isandro said slowly, the pattern assembling itself behind his eyes like headlights resolving out of fog. "I think I finally know why she wrote the diary in numbers."
