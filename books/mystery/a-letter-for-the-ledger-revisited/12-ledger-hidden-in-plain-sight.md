## 12. Ledger Hidden in Plain Sight

The break room at Halcyon Cabs had a coffee machine that predated the invention of regret. It gurgled and shuddered through its cycle like an old man climbing stairs, and Isandro Kavanagh had long ago made peace with the fact that whatever it produced would taste like hot cardboard with ambitions. He drank it anyway. Night shift ran on worse.

He sat at the corner table with two things spread out in front of him: Vera Ostrander's diary, open to its final pages, and a printout of fare records he'd pulled from the archive room under the pretense of "auditing last quarter's zone reports." Nobody audits zone reports at 2:40 in the morning. Nobody had questioned it either, which was either a mercy or a message.

For three nights now he'd been staring at the diary's numbers like a man trying to read his own reflection in a fogged window. They were arranged in columns — dates on the left, then strings of digits that looked almost like medallion numbers but weren't. Medallions were four digits. These ran six, seven, sometimes eight. He'd tried phone numbers. Coordinates. License plates. He'd even tried, at one low point around four a.m. on Tuesday, treating them as lottery picks, on the theory that if Vera had left him nothing else she might at least have left him a joke.

Then, somewhere between his second cup of cardboard and his third, the pattern walked up and introduced itself.

It was the rounding that did it.

Fare records are honest in the way only machines are honest. A trip from the airport to the Marlowe Hotel costs $34.60, and the record says $34.60, down to the dime, forever. Dispatch logs are messier — human-entered, abbreviated, full of shorthand — but the billing system doesn't lie. Money goes where money goes.

Isandro pulled up the billing archive for March of last year and started checking trips against their dispatch entries. Trip 4471: billed $41.00, metered $37.20. Trip 4480: billed $58.00, metered $52.90. Trip 4490: billed $29.00, metered $26.35.

Every tenth trip.

He sat back so fast his chair squeaked against the linoleum, loud as a gunshot in the empty office. Down the hall, the night printer chattered on obliviously.

He counted again. Trips ending in zero — every one of them, going back months — billed to a rounded-up figure, the difference skimmed off the top. A dollar here, five dollars there. Nothing anyone would notice on a single receipt. A passenger paying cash wouldn't blink at being handed a total rounded to the nearest dollar; a corporate account wouldn't audit a forty-cent variance. But multiply forty cents by every tenth fare in a fleet of three hundred cabs running twenty-four hours a day, seven days a week, for years —

Isandro did the math on the back of a shift sheet, his handwriting getting uglier as the numbers got bigger. Then he checked the payee field on the inflated invoices. Most went to ordinary vendors: fuel companies, the garage, the insurance broker. But a handful — always the same handful, always attached to those tenth-trip invoices — went to three names he'd never heard a driver mention. Meridian Fleet Services. Corvid Logistics. The Blue Door Group.

He looked them up. Meridian Fleet Services had a registered address that turned out to be a mail drop in a strip mall. Corvid Logistics had a website consisting of a logo and a phone number that rang to a voicemail with no name on it. The Blue Door Group didn't appear to exist at all outside of Halcyon's own accounting system.

Three shell companies, drinking forty cents at a time from every tenth fare in the city.

And there, in the diary, in Vera's small precise hand, the numbers he'd been unable to crack for three days suddenly resolved into plain English. The six-digit strings weren't codes at all. They were invoice numbers. She'd been listing them. The dates beside them matched the tenth-trip skims exactly. She hadn't hidden the ledger in the diary.

She'd hidden the diary *in* the ledger. Every number she wrote was a thread, and every thread led back into Halcyon's own fare records, where the whole crime sat in plain sight, dressed up as routine, filed and stamped and archived by people who had no idea they were keeping books for a thief.

*The whole city's routine was the crime scene.* That was how Nell would put it later, when he called her, and Isandro would think she'd found the exact right words. But sitting alone in the break room at three in the morning, all he could feel was the vertigo of it — the sense that the ground floor of his ordinary life had been hollowed out underneath him without anyone disturbing the carpet.

* * *

He called Nell at nine, which for her was early and for him was late, and heard her set down something heavy — a mug, maybe, or a grudge — before picking up.

"Tell me you have good news," she said. "I've been rationing hope like wartime sugar."

"I have arithmetic," Isandro said. "It's better than it sounds."

He talked her through it while he paced the length of his apartment, past the window where the morning light was doing its weak gray best. Every tenth trip. Rounded up. Three shells. Years of records. "It's not one big theft," he said. "It's ten thousand small ones. That's why nobody caught it. You can't miss what never arrives."

"And Vera found it because—"

"Because she was an accountant who got bored." He could hear the smile in it now, looking back. "She told her sister she took the job for the quiet. I think the quiet is exactly what she couldn't stand. So she started reconciling. Just to have something to do. And the numbers wouldn't reconcile."

Nell was quiet for a moment. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — less wry, more careful, the way people get when they're standing near something deep. "My sister used to alphabetize the spice rack when she was stressed. Once she reorganized my entire kitchen by frequency of use. I thought it was a quirk." A breath. "It was a weapon. She just hadn't aimed it yet."

"There's more," Isandro said. "The diary's final entry has a date in it. The anniversary dinner date. And there's a line I keep rereading." He'd copied it out that morning onto the back of a parking stub, though he no longer needed to; he knew it the way you know your own address. "'They'll want everyone watching the cake. Watch the books instead.'"

"She knew about the dinner?" Nell said. "Before any of this? Before she—" She stopped herself. Before she disappeared. Before she died, was what the world believed, and what some part of Isandro still flinched away from saying aloud, because saying it felt like agreeing to it.

"The dinner was scheduled before she vanished," Isandro said slowly, working it through as he spoke. "Ambrose announced it weeks ahead. It was in the company newsletter. If she was already pulling threads by then..." He trailed off, staring at the parking stub. "She picked the date. Or she saw it coming and decided to leave her last instruction on top of it. Either way, she wanted whoever read this to be looking at the fare records on the night everyone else is looking at Ambrose Halcyon raising a glass."

"Then we look at the fare records," Nell said simply.

"It's not that simple." He lowered himself onto the arm of the couch. "There are years of these files. Physical archives in the basement, plus the digital system, and the digital system has access logs. If I pull too much too fast, someone sees me pulling it. And we both know someone's watching." He thought of Odile's questions about the returned cab — casual, warm, delivered over her shoulder while she updated the board. He thought of how quickly word traveled in that building. How little stayed private.

"Then don't pull," Nell said. "Photograph. You're a dispatcher, aren't you? Isn't your whole job noticing things quietly?"

Isandro laughed despite himself. "You make it sound noble. Mostly it's listening to drunk people argue about routes."

"Same skill set," Nell said. "Trust me. My sister married a dispatcher once. Worst eight months of her life, but he could find anything."

* * *

The next three nights, Isandro became the most diligent employee Halcyon Cabs had ever employed, and it was the easiest acting job of his life, because the diligence was real. He audited zone reports. He reconciled fuel purchases. He flagged duplicate invoices with helpful sticky notes. He was, genuinely, good at it — Odile had taught him that a dispatcher's first virtue was patience with paperwork, that the radio got the glory but the ledger kept the lights on.

*The ledger keeps the lights on.* He wondered, filing that phrase away, whether she'd meant it as literally as he now suspected.

Each night he carried a stack of archive folders up to the break room under cover of legitimate work, and each night he photographed pages with his phone while the coffee machine performed its funeral rites. Tenth-trip invoices. Vendor payment runs. The monthly reconciliation summaries that showed, in tidy columns, the tiny variances nobody had ever questioned, accumulating month over month like silt. He built a timeline on paper towels because he'd run out of legal pads, and by Thursday the break room table looked like the wall of a conspiracy theorist, if conspiracy theorists worked in ballpoint and grease pencil and kept their handwriting neat.

The numbers told a story with a clear voice. The skimming started small — eleven years ago, barely a trickle, a few hundred dollars a month routed to Meridian Fleet Services. Then it grew. Corvid Logistics appeared eight years back. The Blue Door Group, five. The amounts scaled with the fleet itself: as Halcyon added cars, added zones, won the municipal contract, the tenth-trip tax grew with it. Whoever was running the scheme hadn't just been stealing. They'd been *scaling*. Treating embezzlement like a startup.

And threaded through it all, matching entry for entry, were Vera's numbers. She'd reconstructed the whole architecture from the outside — or rather, from the inside, quietly, one reconciliation at a time, until the day she'd understood what she was holding and understood, too, what happens to accountants who hold things.

The last page of her list stopped mid-column. The final invoice number was dated nine days after her disappearance.

Isandro stared at that for a long time. Nine days after the world decided she was dead, someone — Vera, in her own hand, or someone forging it well enough to fool him — had recorded another skim. Which meant the scheme hadn't paused for her death. Which meant either she'd written it before vanishing and postdated it, or she really had been alive, watching, counting, keeping her own books right up to the edge of whatever came next.

He was still staring when Odile came in for the start of her shift.

* * *

She arrived the way she always did, ten minutes early, coat over one arm, reading glasses pushed up into her gray hair like a headband. She took one look at the break room table — the folders, the paper-towel timeline, the cold cups — and raised an eyebrow that had ended careers.

"Zone reports," Isandro said, before she could ask.

"That's a lot of zone report."

"Quarter-end." He shuffled a folder on top of the timeline with a motion he hoped looked casual and probably looked like a man hiding a body. "You always say the ledger keeps the lights on."

Something crossed her face — quick, gone before he could name it. Not suspicion. Something older and wearier than that. "I do say that," she said. She poured herself a cup of the cardboard coffee, drank it black without flinching, which Isandro had always considered her most superhuman quality. "Find anything interesting?"

"Just the usual mess. Duplicate vendor entries. Sloppy coding." He kept his eyes on the folder. "I'll clean it up."

"Mm." She stood there a moment longer than the conversation required, and Isandro felt the silence the way you feel a cab idling behind you — engine running, driver watching, destination unknown. Then she tapped the table twice with one finger, a habit she had, a little metronome of approval or warning, impossible to tell which, and said, "Don't stay too late. The dead don't file anything worth staying late for."

She left. Isandro exhaled for what felt like the first time since she'd walked in.

Then he replayed the sentence. *The dead don't file anything worth staying late for.* Ordinary Odile gallows humor, the kind she traded every shift. Except — he pulled the diary toward him, hands not entirely steady — except that the last thing Vera had filed, the last invoice number in her column, was dated after she died. After, according to everyone, there was nothing left of her to file.

Odile knew the archives. Odile knew the schedule of every employee in the building. Odile had asked about the returned cab with the gentle persistence of a woman checking a lock.

Or Odile had said nothing at all, and grief and paranoia were simply doing what they always did to Isandro's three-a.m. brain, which was turn every kindness into a cipher.

He looked down at the paper-towel timeline, at the neat columns of stolen money marching across eleven years, and understood suddenly, completely, why Vera had chosen this method. Why she'd hidden her evidence inside the most boring documents in the building. Because the crime wore routine as camouflage, and the only way to see it was to become boring yourself, patient, meticulous, invisible — to sit with the numbers night after night until they confessed.

The dinner was in five days. The diary said: *watch the books.*

He photographed the final page of the timeline, folded it into his shirt pocket, and began, carefully, putting everything back the way he'd found it — every folder, every page, every decimal point of other people's honest-looking lies.

Down the hall, the printer chattered on, faithfully recording the night's fares. Every tenth one a little richer than it should have been. The city riding around on top of a theft so old and so polite it had become furniture. Isandro filed the last folder, turned off the break room light, and headed for the boards.

His radio crackled: a pickup on Delmore Avenue, party of one, no rush. An ordinary fare on an ordinary street. He dispatched it in a steady voice, and only after the driver acknowledged did he let himself think the thought that had been waiting patiently all night for him to sit down:

Five days. And somewhere in this building, someone already knew he'd been reading.
