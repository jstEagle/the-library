## 16. Counting Down the Glasses

The library on Delmore Street opened at nine and closed at six, which made it the only honest institution in Isandro's life that kept daylight hours. He'd chosen it for that reason. Nobody from Halcyon had a reason to be in a public library at ten on a Tuesday morning, and if they did, they'd have to explain themselves, and people who were doing what he suspected them of doing hated explaining themselves.

He sat at a carrel near the microfilm reader — an actual machine, older than he was, with a crank and a green glass screen and a hand-lettered sign that said PLEASE BE PATIENT WITH HER, SHE'S DOING HER BEST. Isandro liked her already. She was the only thing in this whole affair doing her best without an agenda.

On the desk beside him: a manila folder, unremarkable, containing forty-one pages of decoded ledger. Six years of Halcyon fare records, every tenth trip rounded up by amounts that looked like rounding errors to anyone who wasn't looking, skimmed into three shell companies whose names he'd traced through the diary's cipher to a post office box two blocks from Ambrose Halcyon's country club. Two point one million dollars, give or take, laundered out of the pockets of every customer who'd ever tipped a Halcyon driver and thought nothing of it.

Vera Ostrander had been looking. That was the whole tragedy in a sentence. She'd been looking when nobody else was, and it had cost her her name, her face, her life as she knew it. The least Isandro could do was make sure her work survived even if he didn't.

He fed the first page into the reader's copy slot and cranked.

* * *

It took four hours. The machine photographed each page onto a strip of film no wider than his thumb, and by noon Isandro had three complete copies — because Vera's diary said *three*, in its careful coded way, and he'd learned not to argue with the diary. One went into a padded envelope addressed to Nell Ostrander, to be mailed from a mailbox on the far side of the river, nowhere near his route home. One went into a bank safe-deposit box he'd opened under his mother's maiden name, which had felt like a betrayal of his mother until he remembered she'd have loved every second of it. *A spy box,* she would have said, delighted. *My son the spy.*

The third copy he kept on his person, in a film canister taped inside the lining of his dispatch jacket, right where Odile had once taught him to keep emergency cab fare back when he was nineteen and broke and prone to ending his shifts stranded across town.

*Always have an exit,* she'd told him then, tapping the seam of his jacket pocket. *Money for the door, and know where the doors are.*

He wondered, cranking the last page through, whether she'd meant it as a lesson or a confession.

* * *

The banquet hall was the Meridian Room at the Hotel Corvair, because Ambrose Halcyon had held his wedding reception there in 1971 and apparently considered the building part of the family. Isandro walked through it Wednesday afternoon wearing his good coat and his dispatcher's face — polite, attentive, slightly bored — on the pretext of confirming the company's table arrangements for Friday night.

The truth was he was counting doors.

There were four. The main entrance off the lobby, wide enough for a crowd but slow in an emergency, all that brass and carpet. A service corridor behind the head table that led to the kitchens, currently blocked by a rolling cart of folded chairs. A fire exit near the bandstand with an alarm bar that, he tested with his palm while pretending to admire the chandeliers, was disconnected — the little chain had been cut and tucked away neat as you please. And a freight elevator at the back, behind a curtain, that went down to the parking garage.

Four doors, and only one of them worked without ceremony.

He stood by the disconnected fire exit and let himself feel the shape of it. Somebody had prepared this room. Somebody had decided, in advance, how a crowd would flow and where a man might go if Friday night went wrong. Or — and here was the thought he kept trying not to hold up to the light — somebody had simply done their job, and hotels cut fire-alarm chains all the time, and he was a night-shift dispatcher reading conspiracy into maintenance schedules because paranoia was cheaper than sleep.

"Sir? Can I help you?"

A banquet captain, silver-haired, professionally kind. Isandro smiled his dispatcher smile. "Just checking sight lines for the toast. Mr. Halcyon likes to be seen from every angle."

"He does," the captain said, with the weary fondness of a man who'd poured for Ambrose Halcyon for thirty years. "Best tipper in the city, worst seat-chooser. We put him wherever he wants anyway."

Isandro laughed at the right volume and moved on, and all the while the film canister pressed against his ribs like a small cold heart.

* * *

He mapped the exits twice more from memory that evening, drawing them in the back of a fare log he'd marked DISCARD — 2019, because nobody audits garbage. Main entrance: ninety seconds to clear a crowd, longer if anyone panicked. Kitchen corridor: passable if the chair cart moved. Freight elevator: forty seconds down, direct to garage, and — this was the detail that kept him staring at the page — the garage connected to the alley where Halcyon cabs idled between fares.

Ambrose wouldn't run. Ambrose didn't need to run; Ambrose had spent fifty years building a city that would sooner apologize to him than arrest him. But if Friday went wrong, if Vera stood up or the ledger surfaced or the toast curdled in his mouth, the freight elevator was right there behind its curtain, and the garage was full of cars with keys in them, and the alley was full of cabs that could be anywhere in ten minutes.

Isandro knew, because he was the voice that sent them anywhere.

That was the thought that finally made him sit down on his own couch and put his head in his hands. His ordinary skill — the thing he was best at in the world, the thing Odile had polished in him like a stone — was moving vehicles invisibly through a sleeping city. If it came to a chase, he wasn't sure whether he'd be the hunter or the getaway map.

* * *

Thursday morning, one day out, he came into the dispatch office early to do it one more time — the copying, the checking, the quiet arithmetic of who knew what. The night shift was winding down. Two drivers drank coffee at the counter with the hollow courtesy of men who'd been awake too long. The board glowed amber. Somewhere a radio murmured fares in the low language of the trade: *unit twelve, clear; unit seven, en route.*

Odile was at her desk.

She was always at her desk, that was the joke of the place — *ask Odile, she lives here* — but today the joke landed differently. She had her reading glasses on, the little gold ones, and she was going over shift sheets with a red pen, making her small precise marks in the margins. Shorthand. Her shorthand. The same tight numerals that ran down the diary's coded pages like a private alphabet.

She looked up before he'd fully cleared the door. She always did that too.

"You're in early," she said.

"Couldn't sleep."

"Mm." She took off the glasses and folded them with a click. "You've been sleeping badly for two weeks. I can hear it in your call-outs. You're slower on the board. You second-guess assignments." She said it without accusation, the way she'd say *your tire's low.* "Whatever it is, Isandro — finish it or drop it. Either way, stop carrying it around the floor. It leaks."

"It leaks," he repeated.

"Everything leaks. That's the first thing I ever taught you." She picked the pen back up. "Go on. You've got a big weekend."

He crossed to the break room, got coffee he didn't want, and came back past her desk on the pretense of initialing the handover sheet. And there — he couldn't help himself, he was built to notice things, it was the whole disease of him — he saw, half-tucked under the red pen, a sheet of her shorthand with a date at the top.

Thursday's date. And beside it, in her tight numerals, a figure he recognized.

It was the number of pages in his folder. Forty-one. A number that existed nowhere in any official record, because he'd never written it down anywhere she could see, because he'd counted those pages alone in a locked room with the door chained from the inside of his own skull.

Except he hadn't, apparently. Except somewhere along the line the count had traveled from his hands to hers, and now it sat on her desk in red ink, waiting to be carried wherever she carried things.

Odile felt him looking — she always did — and turned the sheet over with two fingers, unhurried, and smiled at him the old warm smile, the one that had gotten a nineteen-year-old kid through his first winter on nights.

"Sign there," she said, tapping the handover line. "And Isandro. Whatever you're planning for Friday—" the pen paused, just barely, above the paper— "make sure it's worth it."

He signed. His hand was steady. He'd never been prouder or more frightened of anything than of how steady his hand was.

Then he walked out into the gray morning light, past the row of cabs gleaming in the lot like patient animals, and understood, with the clean terrible clarity of a pattern finally completing itself, that she had watched him count. That maybe she'd been watching since the beginning. That the question was no longer whether Odile Voss knew what he was doing.

The question was why she'd let him live long enough to do it.

* * *

He spent Thursday afternoon doing the last of it, methodically, in the order Vera's diary prescribed. The envelope to Nell went into a mailbox by the bridge, mailed at 4:07 p.m., timed so it would land Saturday — after everything, whatever everything turned out to be. The deposit box got its copy. The jacket lining got its canister, retaped, double-checked.

And then, because there was nothing left to prepare and he couldn't stand the stillness, he drove out to the Hotel Corvair one final time and stood across the street in the dark, looking up at the lit windows of the Meridian Room, where caterers were already moving like ghosts behind the glass.

Two days. One working fire exit. One freight elevator to a garage full of idling cabs. One mentor who knew his numbers and smiled while she wrote them down.

His phone buzzed. A text from a number with no name attached — Nell, using the burner they'd agreed on. Four words:

*She says it's time.*

Isandro stared at the message for a long moment, breath fogging in the cold, the hotel glowing warmly above him like a stage set waiting for its cast.

Friday, then. Everything on Friday.

He turned up his collar and went to find out whether he'd spend it as a witness or a body.
