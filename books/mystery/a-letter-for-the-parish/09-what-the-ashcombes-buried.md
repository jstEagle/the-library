## 9. What the Ashcombes Buried

There is a particular hour in an archive when the dust stops being an irritant and becomes a companion. It settles on your sleeves, your cuffs, the rim of your teacup, and after a while you stop brushing it away, because brushing it away would mean admitting you intend to leave, and by that hour you never do. I had reached that hour. The cathedral clock above me said half past nine in the evening, which the archive did not believe, because the archive kept its own time — the time of paper, which moves slower and lies less.

Mara had gone home at seven, muttering something about journalists needing sleep and archivists needing exorcists. I had let her go without argument, which she would later describe as suspicious in itself. In truth I wanted to be alone with what I was beginning to suspect, the way you want to be alone with a diagnosis before you tell your family.

I had the Ashcombe file open in front of me, or rather the absence of an Ashcombe file, which is a different and more eloquent object.

Let me explain how an archive lies. It does not lie by forging. Forgery is vulgar and leaves fingerprints, and St. Aldhelm's has never been vulgar in two hundred years of accumulating paper. An archive lies by *arrangement*. It lies by what it places next to what, by the gap it leaves in a shelf, by the way a folder is slightly too clean, too square, as though it had been made recently to stand in for something that was fed to a stove. I had spent thirty years learning to read the silences between documents the way a physician reads the skin. And the Ashcombe shelf was silent in a way that had begun to sound, to my ear, less like discretion and more like construction.

I had come looking for the estate papers of Edmund Ashcombe, the wool merchant whose fortune had, through a series of marriages and prudent deaths, pooled eventually in the hands of the present Eleanor. Every parish archive keeps estate papers; it is the one thing the wealthy have always paid the church to remember. Edmund Ashcombe's papers were there — his will, his probate inventory, the valuation of his warehouses on the quay — all of it in order, all of it catalogued in a neat Victorian hand.

What was not there was any evidence that the woman who inherited from him had ever existed.

* * *

Her name, in the probate record, was Eleanor Ashcombe. Not the present Eleanor — her great-great-grandmother, the matriarch from whom every subsequent Eleanor had taken the name, the way a family might pass down a candlestick or a curse. The will of 1819 left everything to "my beloved niece, Eleanor Ashcombe." A niece. Simple. Families are full of nieces; nieces are the furniture of inheritance law.

Except that Edmund Ashcombe, according to every record I could find, had no siblings.

I want to be careful here, because this is the sort of discovery that feels enormous at midnight and smaller at breakfast, and I have been wrong at midnight before. So I did what I always do when the archive offers me something too neat: I went looking for the correction. I pulled the baptismal registers for the years 1780 through 1800, the span in which a niece of Edmund's would have been born. I pulled the marriage registers. I pulled the burial registers, because the dead are more honest than the living — they have nothing left to protect. I pulled the parish's own copies of the census abstracts, kept by a vicar in the 1850s with the tidy obsessiveness of a man who feared God primarily in the form of disorder.

No Eleanor Ashcombe born in this parish in that generation. No Eleanor Ashcombe married in it. No Eleanor Ashcombe buried in it — not until the second Eleanor, in 1861, the one whose grave slab I had walked over a thousand times without a thought, in the north transept, worn smooth by feet.

The first Eleanor had appeared in the records only once: at the moment of inheritance. She was born, so far as St. Aldhelm's was concerned, at the reading of a will.

* * *

I made tea I did not drink and sat with it going cold, which is my version of prayer.

There are two explanations for a person who exists only in a will. The first is dull: clerical error, a name mangled across registers, a woman from a distant parish whose records sit in some other archive, some other county, some other silence. I have chased that kind of ghost before and it has usually turned out to be exactly that — a ghost of bad handwriting.

The second explanation is that she was never anywhere to find, because she was never anywhere at all. That she was a document before she was a person. That someone had needed an heir the way a forger needs a signature, and had manufactured one — a niece with no siblings to contradict her, no childhood to be remembered, no grave to be checked against. An heir made entirely of ink.

And if you are going to manufacture a person out of ink, I thought, looking at the ledger on the table beside me, you would want to be very good with ink.

The burial ledger sat there with its cipher of death-dates, the code Mara and I had only begun to unpick, the code that had already given us a name that belonged to no corpse. I had been assuming the cipher was a message *hidden in* the records. It occurred to me, somewhere around the second cold cup of tea, that I might have the direction of the trick reversed. Perhaps the records were the message. Perhaps the cipher was not hidden in the parish's memory — perhaps the parish's memory had been *built* around the cipher, the way a wall is built around a pipe.

I went back to Edmund Ashcombe's will and read it the way you read a hostage letter, looking for what was forced.

"My beloved niece, Eleanor Ashcombe, daughter of my late sister Margaret."

There it was. The will named a sister. Margaret Ashcombe. A sister the baptismal registers had no record of either — which was consistent, at least; a forger who invents a niece should invent a mother for her, and this one had been thorough. Margaret, dead before the will was written, conveniently unable to appear and claim her daughter. A family tree with two branches and no roots.

But here is what thoroughness cannot do: it cannot make other people remember. Documents can be forged. Memory has to be managed. And a niece does not inherit four warehouses, a house in Bath, and the largest private fortune on this stretch of the coast without *somebody* in the parish saying, at the funeral or the reading or the christening of her children, *I don't recall her*.

So I went looking for what the parish remembered instead. And the parish, bless its gossipy heart, had remembered everything — just not in the places a forger would have thought to clean.

* * *

The vestry minute books are my favourite dishonest documents. They record the official life of the parish — repairs, appointments, disputes over pew rentals — and because they were written by committees, they record it with the accidental honesty of people who were not trying to tell a story. In the minute book for 1818, sandwiched between a dispute over the organist's salary and a bill for slates, is this:

*"Mr. Tredwell raised the matter of the young woman lodging at the Ashcombe house on the Close, none knowing her name or kin, and whether the parish ought make enquiry. Agreed the Vicar should call."*

The Vicar's report, the following month: *"Called at Ashcombe house. Received civilly. Matter resolved."*

Matter resolved. Four words doing the work of a shovel.

I sat back. The young woman lodging at the Ashcombe house, none knowing her name or kin. A stranger in the merchant's home, a year before the will that named her his beloved niece. Either Edmund Ashcombe had taken in a niece so obscure that his own parish had never heard of her — or he had taken in a young woman whose name the parish would shortly be instructed to forget.

And then there was Tredwell himself. I pulled the burial register for the 1820s, because I have learned — three times now, three accidents, three files — to ask what happened to the people who ask questions. Mr. Tredwell, churchwarden, the man who raised the matter of the nameless young woman, died in March 1821. The register records the cause in the vicar's hand: *fever*.

Fever. The great all-purpose word of the nineteenth century, the word that swallowed everything from typhus to a push on a dark stair. I am not going to tell you I know what happened to Tredwell. I am going to tell you that his is the first entry in the burial ledger whose date, when I checked it against the liturgical calendar, resolved cleanly into a letter — and that the letter is the fourth letter of the name we have been decoding. The cipher was keeping score from the very beginning. The cipher was born with its first victim.

* * *

Because that was the thought that arrived then, finally, in full, the way the truth does when you have been circling it all night: the cipher was not old. I had been treating the ledger as a two-hundred-year-old puzzle, a relic, something buried by history and stumbled upon by us. But a cipher requires a designer, and a designer requires a *motive*, and the motive was sitting in front of me in the vestry minutes and the too-clean shelf and the niece who was born at the reading of a will.

Edmund Ashcombe died in 1819. The records say his heart gave out. But his business partner — I had the partnership deeds in front of me now, pulled an hour earlier on a hunch — was a man named Josiah Crane, and the partnership deeds contain a clause I have read four times to be sure of it. Upon the death of either partner, the survivor holds the whole in trust until the deceased's lawful heir comes forward to claim it. *Lawful heir.* If no lawful heir presented, the trust dissolved, and the survivor inherited outright.

Josiah Crane. I pulled the donor panels list, the one Mara had photocopied, the roll of parish benefactors carved into the glass of the south aisle. Crane's name is there. Crane paid for the rebuilding of the crypt stair. Crane endowed the vicar's stipend for a generation. Crane's money is in the stones of this building, and a man does not buy that much of a church with money he expected to keep only briefly.

Unless he knew the heir would never come forward. Unless the heir was a fiction he had helped to write — a niece with no kin, lodged in the dead man's house, coached and kept, her inheritance quietly managed on her behalf by trustees who answered to him. And unless he had a way of making sure that anyone who asked the wrong question — a churchwarden named Tredwell, say — met with a fever.

But a scheme like that has a flaw, and the flaw is time. Josiah Crane would die. His son would die. The secret would pass to people who had not chosen it and might not keep it. So Crane did what careful criminals have always done with the things they cannot afford to forget: he wrote them down. He built a record of every death the secret required, every fever, every drowning, every fall on the crypt stair — and he built it where no one would ever think to read it, in a ledger of burials, in dates, in the one language the parish already spoke fluently and never thought to question.

He didn't hide the crime in the ledger. He built the ledger *out of* the crime.

And the cipher — the letter-code, the name that belongs to no corpse — that was his insurance. A key, held by the family that tended it, so that the terms of the arrangement could be checked, maintained, *executed*, generation after generation, by people who inherited the duty along with the money.

I looked at my notes, at the little chain I had drawn: Crane, the trust, the fabricated niece, Tredwell's fever, the ledger's first letter. And beneath it, the present tense of it all, which was the part that made my hands unsteady: Eleanor Ashcombe, the living one, the woman who insisted she felt perfectly safe, sat at the end of that chain. Her fortune, her name, her family — all of it flowing from a murdered man and a woman who never existed. She was the heir of a fiction. And somewhere in this cathedral, someone was still tending the fiction's terms.

* * *

It was nearly midnight when I heard the footsteps on the archive stair, and I will confess that every theory I had built that evening rearranged itself around fear for a moment — because the last man who went to the police drowned, and the man before that had a fever, and I was sitting alone in the one room where all of it was written down.

But it was only Mara, in her coat, still holding her keys, looking at the state of the table — the registers fanned out, the partnership deeds, the minute book open to the resolved matter — with the expression of a woman who has come back for something she forgot and found the whole house on fire.

"You didn't go home," she said.

"I did. I came back." I took off my glasses, which is as close as I come to surrender. "Mara. The first Eleanor Ashcombe never existed. She was made up — made up to hold a fortune that belonged to a murdered man, and the man who murdered him built the cipher, and the cipher has been maintained ever since, and—"

"I know," Mara said.

I stopped. "You know."

She came down the last steps and put a folded piece of paper on the table in front of me, smoothing it flat with both hands, the way she does when she wants me to understand that whatever comes next is not a theory. "Eleanor called me an hour ago. She wouldn't talk to you. She said — and I'm quoting — *tell the archivist he's reading the wrong ledger*."

She let that sit for exactly as long as it needed to.

"There's a second one," Mara said. "And she's seen it. And she says the entries haven't stopped."
