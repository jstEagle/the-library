## 16. The Real Client

The solicitor's firm had been dead for forty years, which made it the most reliable correspondent either of us had.

I'd spent three days with the payment trail, and I'll admit what Silas would never say aloud: I'd been hoping it would lead nowhere. Not out of laziness — though there was a version of me, circa two years ago, who would have filed this whole affair under *ecclesiastical weirdness, unresolved* and gone back to writing obituaries for people still alive enough to complain. No, I hoped it would dead-end because a dead-end meant the money had come from somewhere ordinary. A trust fund. A crank with a fortune and a grudge. Anyone but the answer that had been sitting in my notebook for a week, underlined twice, refusing to be crossed out.

Here's what I had. The five thousand pounds had arrived in a plain envelope — no return address, no note beyond the typed request — but the *funds* had come by transfer, and transfers leave footprints. The account was held at a bank in Wells under the name of Prynne & Hallowes, Solicitors. Prynne & Hallowes had dissolved in 1984. Their client accounts should have been wound up, distributed, forgotten. Instead, one account remained open, active, and — as of six weeks ago — five thousand pounds lighter.

An account that outlived its own firm. Somebody had kept it alive the way you keep a grave tidy: not because anyone visits, but because letting it fall into ruin would be an admission.

I took the train to Wells on a Tuesday, because I'd learned by now that Silas's method — sit in the archive until the truth gets bored of hiding — only works when the truth is already in your building. Mine required a railcard and a lie about being a probate researcher. The lie was even mostly true. Probate was what this whole case was, when you scraped off the incense and the Latin.

* * *

The bank branch smelled of carpet and apology. The manager, a woman named Draycott with the patient expression of someone who'd spent thirty years explaining overdrafts, pulled the file for me after I showed her a letter I'd drafted on St. Aldhelm's letterhead — Silas's letterhead, technically, though he'd have had a small seizure if he knew. *Acting on behalf of interested parties in the Ashcombe estate matter.* Vague enough to be true. Specific enough to open doors.

"Prynne & Hallowes," she said, tapping the file. "We've flagged this account for review three times. Each time, instructions come through to leave it open. Standing instructions." She frowned at the screen. "The instructions renew themselves, if that makes sense. Every ten years, a letter arrives. Same hand. Same phrasing. *Maintain the account until the Ashcombe matter is concluded.*"

"When was the last letter?"

"2014. We're due another this year, as a matter of fact." She said it the way you'd mention a boiler inspection.

"Who signs it?"

She turned the screen toward me. A scan of a letter, typed — the same typewriter face, I'd have bet my last freelance cheque, as the note that had started all this — and signed at the bottom in a hand that was old-fashioned, upright, and entirely unfamiliar to me.

But the address in the letterhead was not.

Ashcombe House, Wells.

I sat down in the chair across from Draycott's desk because my legs had made the decision without consulting me.

"Is there a name attached to the house?" I asked, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved.

"The account's named signatories are historical — the partners, both deceased. But the *instructions* come from the family." She scrolled. "Eleanor Ashcombe. There's a note here from the 2014 review — a call was made to confirm. The lady of the house confirmed in person, the note says. She came into the branch."

Eleanor Ashcombe had walked into a bank in Wells ten years ago and ordered that a dead firm's account stay alive. And six weeks ago, that account had paid Silas Nystrom five thousand pounds to prove that Eleanor Ashcombe had not been murdered — *yet.*

I thanked Draycott, who asked if there was anything else, and I said no, and then I sat in my car in the drizzle for twenty minutes doing the arithmetic I'd been avoiding.

* * *

Let me walk you through the shape of it, because the shape is the whole horror.

Eleanor had told us — briskly, over tea she didn't drink, in a sitting room arranged like a museum of her own composure — that she'd never hired anyone. That she felt perfectly safe. That the letters she'd been receiving, the ones predicting deaths in her grandmother's name, were unpleasant but surely some hoax. We'd believed her, or I had, because Silas believed her, and Silas's instincts were the closest thing to scripture I'd encountered since I stopped going.

But the ledger named her. The letters named her. Three men had died pruning the parish's memory around her inheritance. And the money that set us on the trail had come from her own house.

She hadn't hired anyone — that part was true. She'd hired *Silas*, anonymously, because she knew that if she asked him to her face, he'd ask questions she couldn't answer without confessing everything: that she'd known for months. That she'd decoded enough of the letters to understand what they were. That she had sat in her beautiful house with her beautiful will unamended and watched the date approach like weather.

She hadn't trusted herself to be believed. That was the part that got under my skin and stayed there. Not that she'd lied — lying is a tool, and I'd used worse ones as a journalist. It was that she'd correctly judged that the truth, from her mouth, would have sounded like madness or guilt or both. *I am going to be murdered on a date written in a two-hundred-year-old cipher, and the letters are signed with my dead grandmother's name.* Say that to a detective inspector and watch him reach for the form marked *sectioning*. Say it to Silas and watch him go very still and very polite, which is worse.

So she'd bought the one man in England who might believe her, and she'd bought him invisibly, and she'd let us find her instead of finding us, so that when he came asking, it would look like his own idea.

It was elegant. It was desperate. I couldn't decide which disgusted me more, and the honest answer was: neither. What disgusted me was that I understood it completely. I'd spent fifteen years in newsrooms watching people not be believed — women, mostly, with stories that didn't scan — and I'd watched what the not-believing did to them. Eleanor Ashcombe had simply decided, in advance, to skip that part. She'd pre-empted our disbelief by making it structurally impossible to trace her until we'd already done the believing for ourselves.

The betrayal was that she'd used us. The pity was that she'd had no other move. Both things were true, and I was going to have to carry both of them into that sitting room, and I already knew which one would show on my face.

* * *

I called Silas from the car park. He picked up on the second ring, which meant he'd been sitting with the phone in his hand, which meant he'd been worrying, which for Silas was practically a press release.

"Tell me," he said. No hello. There never was.

"The money's hers. The account's been maintained under standing instructions from Ashcombe House for decades — the same typewriter as the original note. She confirmed the account in person in 2014. She's known."

Silence on the line. I could hear the cathedral in the background of his silence — that faint acoustic hush of stone, the way his rooms absorbed sound. When he spoke, his voice had the flatness he used when he was most disturbed. "She sat across from us, Mara. She told us she'd never hired anyone."

"She told us the truth the way a cipher tells the truth. Every word accurate, the meaning buried." I turned the key and let the engine idle, because the rain had gotten into my coat and I wanted the heat. "She watched us decode her own death sentence and served us tea."

"Why?" It wasn't rhetorical. Silas doesn't do rhetorical questions; he files them as genuine requests for information. "If she wanted protection, she had money, she had solicitors, she had—"

"She had the drowned man's ghost, same as we do. She went at this the only way she thought wouldn't get anyone killed. She bought an archivist instead of a police report." I pulled out of the car park. "And she was right about the police, Silas. We've both been dancing around it for a week. Everyone who's gone to the authorities on this is dead. She did the maths before we did."

"That's not maths, that's surrender with better branding."

"Maybe. Or maybe it's what you do when you're a woman with a story that doesn't scan and you've watched what happens to the ones who insist." I heard him breathing. "There's something else. The signature on the instruction letters — the bank's scans go back to 1974. Same hand for fifty years. Silas, she'd have been a child in 1974."

"So someone else wrote them."

"Someone in that house, for fifty years, keeping a dead firm's account alive like a votive lamp. Her grandmother's name on the death letters, her family's solicitors on the money, her name in the ledger. Whatever the Ashcombes built, she was born inside the machine of it. She may not even know which parts of her life were ever real."

That was the thought I'd been circling all the way home, the one that kept converting my anger into something sadder and less useful. We'd found, in the parish records, that the original Eleanor Ashcombe never existed — a fabricated heir, an identity built on a murder, a fortune laundered through two centuries of respectability. And the woman now bearing the name had inherited not just the money but the *method*: the secrecy, the indirection, the conviction that truth was a thing you engineered rather than spoke. You couldn't spend your whole life inside a forgery and come out with clean hands. You came out with hands that knew how to forge.

"Come back," Silas said. "We'll go to her together. Tonight."

"Together," I repeated, and heard what he meant: *so she can't manage us separately, and so you're not alone when it turns out we still like her.* Silas communicates mostly in subtext; it's the archivist in him. Everything important is in the margins.

* * *

Ashcombe House at dusk was worse than Ashcombe House by day. By day it was merely grand — Georgian bones, tired velvet, the smell of beeswax and cold fireplaces. At dusk the windows went gold from the inside and it looked like what it was: a box built to keep things in, occupied by a woman keeping things in herself.

She answered the door herself. No staff — she'd let them go weeks ago, she said later, because she couldn't bear witnesses. She looked smaller than she had at our first meeting. The briskness was still there, but I could now see it was load-bearing, and that the structure behind it had been sagging for months.

"You've found out," she said, before either of us spoke. Not a question. She stood aside to let us in. "The account. I wondered how long the bank would hold. Mrs. Draycott always was thorough." There was something almost fond in it, which somehow made it worse.

"You told us you'd never hired anyone," Silas said. He didn't do anger well — it came out as precision, each word placed like a specimen pin.

"I told you I'd never hired *you*." She led us into the sitting room, to the same chairs, and didn't offer tea this time. That absence told me more than the tea ever had. "I hired a dead firm that answers to a dead name, and I paid for a question instead of a person. If you want to call that hiring, I'll accept the charge. But I never lied to your face, Mr. Nystrom. I simply declined to be in the room."

"The letters," I said. "The ones in your grandmother's name. How long?"

She looked at me then, and the briskness finally cracked, and what was underneath wasn't fear exactly. It was exhaustion — the specific, bone-deep fatigue of someone who has been rehearsing a catastrophe alone for a very long time.

"The first was in March. Eleven months ago." She folded her hands. "It named a man I'd never heard of — a researcher, it turned out, someone poking at the family papers. It gave a date. He drowned on the date, in the way it described. I told myself it was coincidence. Then the second letter came, and I did the arithmetic you're all so fond of, and I understood that whatever was writing to me had been writing for a long time, and that the ledger — I found the ledger, Mr. Nystrom, don't look so surprised, my grandmother showed it to me when I was nineteen and told me it was a family prayer book — the ledger had a date in it, and the date was mine."

"Why not go to the police?" I asked, though I knew. I wanted to hear her say it.

"Because I read the newspapers too. Because the last man who tried is at the bottom of the Avon. Because I am a wealthy widow with a family history of fraud and a headstone date in a cipher, and I know precisely how that story ends when a woman tells it." Her voice didn't rise. That was the terrible thing — it never rose. "I couldn't be believed. I've known that my whole life, in one way or another. So I bought the only man in England whose entire reputation is believing what the records say over what people wish they said. And I made sure he'd never know it was me, because if he knew, he'd look at me the way you're looking at me now, Miss Voss, and I couldn't have that. Not yet. I needed him to believe the *case* first. The case would carry me."

Silence. The fire ticked in the grate. Somewhere upstairs a clock counted off a minute that none of us needed counted.

"You let us think we'd found you," Silas said at last.

"I let you *find* me. There's a difference, and it's the difference between why I'm still alive and why the researcher isn't." She leaned forward. "You want an apology from me, and I'll give it — I'm sorry. Truly. You've been lied to, followed, and one of you had her flat turned over, and that's on the same account that paid you, and I would take it back if I could. But I will not apologise for the method. The method is the only reason either of you is sitting in that chair instead of in the ledger."

And there it was. The betrayal and the pity, sitting side by side like strangers on a train, and I couldn't evict either of them. She'd used us, coldly, completely — and she'd used us because every straight road was mined, and she'd watched the last traveller on the straight road come home as water.

"Eleven days," Silas said quietly. "The ledger gives you eleven days. That was three days ago."

"Eight," she said. "I count them the way you'd count anything you'd spent your life being promised."

Silas looked at her for a long moment, and I watched him arrive at the same place I had, by his own road. Then he said the thing I'd been afraid he'd say and relieved he'd say in equal measure.

"Then we have eight days, and we can't use any of them on the police. Which means we do this the parish way." He paused, and his voice dropped into the register he saved for the archive, the one that meant a decision had already been made and filed. "Which means, Mrs. Ashcombe, you're going to have to tell me everything your grandmother showed you when you were nineteen. Every page. Every name."

Eleanor nodded — and then stopped, her eyes going past us to the window, to the lane, where a car's headlights swept the glass and kept moving, slow, unhurried, the way you drive when you want to be seen.

"That's the third time this week," she said.
