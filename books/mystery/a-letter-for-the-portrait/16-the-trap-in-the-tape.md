## 16. The Trap in the Tape

The rain came in off the river around nine that night, soft and steady, the kind of rain that made Halcyon Bend sound like it was breathing. Coral stood at the dispatch window with the phone pressed to her ear and listened to Marisol's voice come through the static like something carried a long way over water.

"Say it again," Coral said.

"I said the letter's gone." Marisol's voice was low, careful, the dispatcher's register she used when a line might not be private. "I put it back in Eunice Ashcombe's mailbox myself, an hour ago. Wearing gloves. Looking like a woman delivering church flyers. Nobody saw me. I checked twice."

"And?"

"And nothing yet. Lights on in the front parlor. A car in the drive I don't recognize — dark sedan, county plates. Coral, are you sure about this? Because once she opens that envelope, there's no taking it back."

Coral watched the rain crawl down the glass. On the desk beside her lay the twin of the confession letter — the second copy, the one that had been folded into the lining of Amara Voss's coat for twenty years, waiting in the dark the way certain truths wait, patient as sediment. They had photocopied it at the library on Elm Street, Teddy feeding quarters into the machine with hands that shook until Coral covered them with her own. Then Marisol had aged the copy with tea and a hair dryer and a patience that frightened Coral a little, until the paper looked like it had spent two decades learning how to be old.

They had mailed the original's twin back to the house where it had been written. A message in a bottle, thrown upstream.

"She'll open it," Coral said. "People always open their own handwriting."

* * *

Teddy Okafor arrived at ten with a canvas bag that clinked. He was seventy-three years old and moved like a man carrying furniture he no longer trusted, but when he unrolled the bag on Coral's kitchen table, his hands went steady and sure, muscle memory older than the tremor.

"You're sure you want to do it this way," he said. It wasn't quite a question.

"The anniversary dinner is in four days," Coral said. "Eunice will burn whatever she can't explain by then. We've seen her do it — she burned Amara's second desk down to the hinges rather than let us read what was in it. If we wait for the dinner, we walk in with nothing but our word against hers, and her word built this town." She laid the micro-cassette recorder on the table between them, small as a cigarette pack, its casing scuffed silver. "But if she opens that letter and thinks someone out there has the other half of the truth — she'll move tonight. People who've kept a secret for twenty years don't hesitate when it knocks. They answer the door with their hands up or their fists closed. Either way, they answer."

Teddy turned the recorder over in his fingers. "Voice-activated. Six hours on a fresh cassette. I used these before they went digital, back when warrants were things you actually got." He almost smiled. It didn't reach his eyes. "You know what this is, legally speaking? This is a felony in forty-nine states."

"Fifty," said Dev from the doorway.

He'd come in without knocking, wet to the shoulders, smelling of river rain and gasoline. He dropped a plastic bag on the table — inside it, a second recorder, cheaper, newer, still in its blister pack. "Fifty states and the District of Columbia. I looked it up on the drive over. I also bought this, because if we're doing something stupid, we're doing it redundantly." He peeled the plastic off with his teeth. "One for the mailbox post. One under the porch rail. Cross-coverage. If she comes out the front, we get her coming and going."

Coral looked at him. Three weeks ago he had shown up at Amara's funeral in a borrowed suit, a stranger wearing the judge's jaw and none of her warmth, and Coral had disliked him on principle — the estranged grandson, the one who hadn't called, hadn't visited, hadn't sat with Amara through the last gray winter while Coral had. Now he was standing in her kitchen at ten at night, soaking wet, committing crimes for a dead woman he'd barely known, and Coral found she couldn't locate the line where her dislike had ended. Somewhere between the torn page and the boathouse, apparently, it had quietly drowned.

"You changed your mind," she said.

"I changed my mind about standing outside it." He fed a cassette into the new recorder with fingers too big for the mechanism. "My grandmother wrote that letter. Or signed it. Or buried it — we still don't know which, and that's the part that's eating me. But whoever she was protecting, she did it for this family. For the Ashcombes. Which means it's mine to dig up same as anyone's." He snapped the battery door shut. "Besides. You were going to do it without me. Marisol told me. She tells me everything now; I think she's decided I'm a rescue project."

"Marisol has excellent judgment," Coral said.

* * *

They drove out to Ashcombe Hill in two cars, headlights off for the last quarter mile, moving through the rain on memory and moonless instinct. The estate rose out of the dark the way it always had, Coral thought — not built so much as installed, white columns and black shutters, a house that had never once needed to raise its voice. Every light in town bent toward it eventually. Her mother's had. Amara's had. Even the river, she sometimes thought, bent toward it, carrying everything down past the boathouse and the dock and the party lights, out toward the sea, where things could finally stop being Halcyon Bend's problem.

Teddy took the mailbox at the road. He worked fast, zip-tying the small recorder inside the post's hollow core, angled toward the drive, then palmed the tape and handed it to Coral without looking at her. Dev crouched under the porch rail of the side entrance, the service door the household staff had used since before the war, and wedged the second recorder into the rot-soft gap behind the lattice. His breath came out white in the cold.

"How long do we give it?" he whispered.

"As long as it takes." Coral pressed herself into the shadow of the boxwoods, rain ticking against the leaves above her like fingertips testing a window. Through the parlor windows, thirty yards off, she could see the shapes of movement — a woman crossing the room, the dark sedan's driver standing by the fire holding a drink he wasn't drinking. Eunice Ashcombe was eighty-one years old and moved through her own house like weather, slow and total, changing everything she touched.

Then the porch light came on.

It happened all at once, the way the important things always did — no warning, no drumroll. The side door opened and Eunice stepped out onto the stone stoop in a cardigan the color of oyster shells, barefoot on the wet stone, an envelope held in both hands. Even at thirty yards, even in the dark, Coral knew what it was. She had known the shape of that envelope since the day it arrived at her own door, postmarked the day after a funeral.

Eunice stood very still. Rain ran off the edge of the roof and struck the stones beside her bare feet. She didn't seem to feel it. She was reading the return address — there wasn't one — and turning the envelope over, and over again, and Coral realized with a lurch that the old woman was looking for a postmark, for a date, for some small printed mercy that would tell her the letter was old, that it had been sitting somewhere forgotten, that the past had not just grown a hand and knocked.

There was no postmark. Marisol had steamed it off and resealed it clean.

Eunice went back inside. The door closed. The porch light died.

For a long moment nobody breathed. Then Dev exhaled against the lattice, and the sound of it was the loneliest thing Coral had heard in weeks.

"She knows," he whispered. "She knows it's a fake. There's no postmark, Coral, we gave her a letter with no history—"

"No," Coral said slowly. "She knows it's real. That's worse."

* * *

They waited two hours in the rain before anything else happened, and Coral spent them cataloguing the sounds of the house the way she catalogued sounds for a living — the furnace cycling, a clock striking eleven in a room she couldn't see, the sedan's engine turning over at midnight and grinding away down the drive with its lights doused until the gate. At 12:40, the side door opened again.

Not Eunice. A man. Tall, narrow, moving with the particular care of someone who has practiced being quiet in this house his whole life. He came down the steps, crossed to the lattice, and knelt.

Dev's hand closed on Coral's wrist hard enough to leave marks.

The man reached into the gap behind the lattice. His fingers found the recorder. And then — this was the part Coral would replay for the rest of her life, frame by frame, the way she replayed every lie she'd ever caught — he didn't pull it out. He adjusted it. Turned it a few degrees, tilted the microphone toward the house, checked the little red eye of the record light, and settled it back into the gap exactly where Dev had left it.

Then he stood, wiped his hands on his trousers, and said, clearly, to the darkness, to the boxwoods, to the three people hiding in the wet garden of his childhood:

"It's on. Has been since you planted it. Come inside before you catch your deaths — Mrs. Ashcombe makes a mean cocoa, and she's expecting you."

* * *

They sat in the Ashcombes' kitchen because there was nowhere else to sit, and because refusing felt more dangerous than accepting. The room was warm and yellow-lit and wrong in every particular — copper pots hanging from hooks, a calendar from the feed store, a dish towel embroidered with someone's initials. A kitchen built by hands that had never cooked in it, Coral thought. A stage set of a kitchen.

The man was Julian Ashcombe. Forty-six, maybe, with the family's long face and a voice like a radio announcer who'd learned to modulate himself down to a murmur. He set three mugs on the counter and poured cocoa from an actual saucepan, which somehow made it worse.

"You bugged my aunt's house," Dev said.

"You bugged my aunt's house first." Julian slid a mug toward each of them. "I just improved your microphone placement. The lattice acts as a resonator if you angle it wrong — you'd have gotten mostly wind. Twenty years of police procedurals and nobody teaches the acoustics anymore." He leaned against the counter and looked at Coral directly, and his eyes were pale and level and entirely without fear. "Coral Ishikawa. The dispatcher. My aunt gets the clipping service; I get the police scanner app. You have quite the reputation for ears."

"What do you want?" Coral asked.

"That's my line, honestly." He wrapped his hands around his own mug. "Someone mails my aunt a twenty-year-old letter — a copy, and a good one, though the ink bleed pattern gives it away if you know what the original looks like — and within six hours three strangers are in the garden planting wire. So let's skip to the part where we're honest with each other, because I'm going to be honest first, and it's going to cost you."

He drank. Set the mug down. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped into something quieter and much older.

"My aunt didn't write that letter. She received it. Twenty years ago, the week after your mother drowned. And she didn't bury it alone — she buried it with Judge Voss, who was the only person in this county with the authority to seal a coroner's file and the only person in this county brave enough to try. Whatever you think you've found in Amara's desks, whatever the torn page says, whatever story you've built about complicity—" he spread his hands, apologetic, terrible, "—you've got it backwards. Amara Voss wasn't protecting us. She was holding us hostage. That letter was her insurance policy. And the reason my aunt has been afraid of the mail for twenty years is that the letter promised to surface if anything ever happened to the person who wrote it."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere upstairs, a floorboard settled.

"Who wrote it?" Coral asked. Her voice came out flat and strange, a dispatcher's voice, all signal and no feeling.

Julian Ashcombe smiled, and it was the saddest expression she had ever seen on a powerful man's face.

"That," he said, "is the question my family has paid everything to keep unanswered. And here's why you should care: the letter names a fourth death. Not your mother's. One that hasn't happened yet." He glanced at the window, at the dark, at the river beyond it running its long black errand. "The deadline is the anniversary dinner. Four days. So drink your cocoa, Miss Ishikawa. And then I'll play you the tape my aunt has kept locked in her safe since 2004 — the one recording she's never let anyone hear."

He reached into his cardigan pocket and set a micro-cassette on the counter between them, small as a cigarette pack, its label handwritten in a slanted, elegant script Coral recognized from the margins of a hundred case files.

Amara Voss's handwriting. And beneath it, five words:

*For the day I'm wrong.*
