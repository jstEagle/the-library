## 19. The Anniversary Dinner

The Ashcombe house had been built to be looked at, and for fifty years Halcyon Bend had obliged.

Coral saw it now from the circular drive, lit from below like something on display — the white columns washed in amber floodlight, the windows glowing honey-gold, the flagstone path scattered with rose petals that would be raked up by hired hands before dawn as if the evening had never happened. A string quartet played somewhere inside, Vivaldi thinned by distance and glass. Valets in gray vests took keys from arriving cars with gloved hands, and every one of those hands trembled slightly, Coral noticed, because everyone in this town knew what an Ashcombe invitation meant and what it cost to decline one.

She had dressed the part. Marisol had insisted, holding up the dark green dress in the thrift shop off Route 9 like a woman presenting evidence. *You can't walk into that house looking like grief,* she'd said. *You have to look like you belong there. Like you could be one of them.* So Coral wore the green dress and her mother's small pearl earrings, and she had pinned inside her clutch — flat against the satin lining where no pat-down would think to search — a slim digital recorder, fully charged, its red light disabled by Teddy's careful thumb two nights ago in the dispatch office while Marisol stood watch at the door.

*It only counts if they say it out loud,* Teddy had told her. *Courts don't take ghosts as witnesses, Coral. You know that better than anyone.*

*Then I'll make them speak,* she had said, and none of them had laughed, because they all understood she meant it the way other people meant vows.

Dev drove. He had borrowed his grandfather's old Buick — Eunice Ashcombe's brother-in-law's car, actually, a tangle of Halcyon Bend kinship Coral still couldn't fully map — because arriving in anything newer would raise questions, and arriving in anything older would raise worse ones. Dev sat behind the wheel in a charcoal suit that fit him like an apology, jaw tight, eyes fixed on the house he had grown up in and fled and now returned to on purpose.

"You don't have to come in," Coral said.

"Yes, I do." He pulled the parking brake. "If I don't, they'll smell it on you. They can tell, Coral. My family can always tell when someone's carrying something." He turned to look at her, and in the dashboard glow his face was younger and older than his years at once. "You're carrying my grandmother's whole life in that bag. Let me carry some of it."

* * *

The foyer smelled of lilies and old money — that particular scent of beeswax polish layered over decades, of cut flowers replaced before they could fade, of air that had never once been allowed to go stale. A woman Coral didn't recognize took her wrap. A man with a silver tray offered champagne. The quartet had moved into something slower, strings sighing through the open doors of a ballroom where maybe eighty guests drifted in clusters, jeweled and tanned and laughing at volumes calibrated not to disturb.

Coral worked the room the way she worked a frequency: scanning, sorting, listening beneath the words.

She heard the mayor's wife lie about how much she'd loved the tribute video. She heard a banker from two towns over lie to himself about why he'd really driven ninety minutes to be here. She heard Preston Ashcombe III — sixty-eight years old, silver-haired, straight-backed, the patriarch himself — tell a state senator that the river town had been good to his family, and hear, underneath the warm baritone, the faint static of a man who had rehearsed the sentence so many times it had worn smooth as river stone.

And she heard Eunice.

Eunice Ashcombe stood near the fireplace in deep blue silk, holding a glass of wine she wasn't drinking, and when Coral's gaze found her across the room, the old woman's spine went rigid in a way no one else could have read but which shouted to Coral like a struck bell. Eunice had known she would come. Had perhaps prayed she wouldn't. Their eyes held for three full seconds — long enough for Coral to see the fear there, yes, but also something stranger, something that looked almost like relief, like a drowning woman watching a rope come across the water.

Then Eunice turned away and said something bright to her neighbor, and the moment closed like water over a stone.

"Coral Ishikawa." Preston Ashcombe materialized at her elbow with the unhurried certainty of a man who had never once entered a room unannounced. His handshake was dry and precise. Up close, his face was a map of handsome erosion — the kind of face that photographed well and trusted itself completely. "The dispatcher. Amara spoke of you. She said you had the best ears in three counties." He smiled. "I imagine that's useful work."

"It is," Coral said. "People tell you things on the phone they'd never say to your face. Fear sounds different than people think it does. Most folks can't tell the difference between fear and guilt." She let that sit. "Can you?"

Something flickered behind his eyes — quick, professional, gone. "Fifty years of dinner parties," he said pleasantly, "and I've learned they sound exactly alike." He raised his glass an inch toward her, a toast or a warning, and drifted back into the current of his guests.

Dev appeared at her shoulder, breath shallow. "He knows."

"He suspects. There's a difference." Coral lifted her own untouched champagne. "Suspects are manageable. We came here to make sure he stops managing."

* * *

Dinner was served at seven-thirty: chilled cucumber soup, then duck, then something with figs, each course announced by a woman with a clipboard and a voice like a museum docent. Coral was seated at the far end of the long table between a retired dentist and a woman who ran the garden society, both of whom talked at her without requiring answers, which suited her fine. She listened instead to the head of the table, where Preston held court with anecdotes polished by fifty years of retelling — the founding of the mill, the flood of '81, the year the Ashcombes gave the town its library and its name on the cornerstone.

"And of course," Preston said, reaching the anecdote Coral had been waiting twenty years and three weeks to hear, "there was the summer everything almost fell apart. Nineteen—" he paused, savoring, "—well. Some stories keep." Laughter around the table, obedient and warm. "To keeping," he said, raising his glass, "and to fifty years of this house standing."

Glasses rose. Coral did not lift hers. At the table's midpoint, Eunice's hand shook so badly the wine trembled against the crystal, and only Coral saw it, because only Coral was listening with her whole body, the way she listened to a night shift, the way she had listened to Amara Voss's last phone call and heard the lie folded inside it like a letter inside an envelope.

After dinner, the guests spilled onto the terrace for coffee and brandy and the fireworks the town had promised at nine. The river showed itself beyond the lawn, black and patient, carrying the reflected lights of the house downstream toward the bend that gave the town its name.

Coral set down her cup and walked, unhurried, to where Eunice stood alone at the balustrade.

* * *

"They'll start the fireworks soon," Eunice said, without turning. Her voice was steady. Her knuckles, wrapped around the balustrade, were not. "Preston insists on them every year. Half the town watches from their yards. It's the one night we pretend we belong to them."

"You do belong to them," Coral said. "That's the problem."

Eunice turned then. Up close, in the spill of light from the ballroom doors, she looked less like a matriarch and more like what she was: a woman in her late seventies who had been holding a door shut for two decades with her whole body. "Amara sent you," she said. Not a question.

"Amara's dead."

"I know what she is." Eunice's eyes were wet and furious. "I've known since the funeral. Since the letter came to this house too — did you think you were the only one? Postmarked the day after we buried her. No signature. Just four lines." Her voice dropped to a whisper that Coral leaned into, ears straining past the string quartet, past the laughter, past the first whistling rise of a firework being loaded somewhere down the lawn. "*Ask her what she carried out of the water.* That's what it said. Ask her. As if I haven't been asking myself every night for twenty years."

"Then answer it," Coral said quietly. "Not for me. For the record." She touched her clutch, once, lightly — a gesture Eunice's eyes tracked and understood. "There's a recorder in my bag, Mrs. Ashcombe. Teddy Okafor taught me how to use it. Whatever you say next is going to exist outside this terrace, outside this family, outside the fifty years of stone your brother-in-law built around this town. You can walk inside right now and spend the rest of your life pretending. Or you can say it out loud, once, and be free of the weight of it."

"You don't understand what you're asking."

"I understand exactly what I'm asking." Coral stepped closer. The river breathed below them, dark and endless. "My mother drowned in that river when I was eleven years old. The police called it an accident in nine days. The coroner signed it in twelve. And Judge Amara Voss — the woman who taught me how to listen, who told me the truth was the only thing worth serving — sealed the file with her own gavel and never said one word to me for twenty years until she was dying, and even then she only said *look at the boathouse*, and then she died before she could explain what she was sorry for." Coral's voice stayed level; she had practiced that levelness on a hundred midnight frequencies. "So yes, Mrs. Ashcombe. I know precisely what I'm asking. I'm asking you to choose tonight whether you die as a witness or as a wall."

The first firework went up — a gold chrysanthemum cracking open over the river, light washing the terrace white, and in that flash of brightness Eunice Ashcombe's face was stripped of fifty years of composure, and what was left was just an old woman, terrified, and tired, and finally, finally ready.

"She wasn't alone that night," Eunice whispered.

The second firework bloomed red. Coral did not move. Did not breathe.

"Meredith," Eunice said — and the name came out of her like a tooth coming free, bloody and relieving all at once. "Your mother. Meredith. She came to the boathouse that night to meet someone. She'd found something — papers, I think, I never saw them, I swear I never saw them — something about the mill, about the money Preston's father moved through it in the seventies, money that should have gone to the families whose land flooded when they raised the dam. She was going to take it to the county attorney on Monday." Eunice's hand found Coral's wrist, gripping hard, an anchor or a plea. "But it wasn't Preston she met. It was his father. And there was an argument, and there was the water, and Coral — Coral, I heard it. I was sixteen feet away in the boathouse and I heard her go in and I heard her call out twice and I did not move, because my husband's uncle put his hand on my arm and said, *Eunice. Think of the family.*"

Above them the sky detonated in silver. Below them the river kept its black counsel. And Coral Ishikawa, who had spent her whole life hearing the truth under the lies, stood perfectly still and let the confession pour into the recorder hidden against the satin lining of her bag, word by word, like water filling a vessel —

— and that was when she saw, over Eunice's shoulder, at the edge of the terrace where the light from the ballroom fell short, a small figure in a party dress standing motionless in the shadows.

A child. Eight, maybe nine. Dark hair, wide eyes, a paper plate with an uneaten slice of cake forgotten in her hands.

Preston Ashcombe's youngest granddaughter — and she had heard every word.
