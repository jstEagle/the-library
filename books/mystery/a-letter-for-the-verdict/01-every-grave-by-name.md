## 1. Every Grave by Name

The dead of Harrow's Bend arrived on Tuesdays.

Dominic Okonkwo had never arranged it that way — the county simply seemed to prefer it, the way some people preferred to do their crying in the car before coming inside — but after six years as coroner they had come to trust the rhythm of it. Tuesday meant the transport van backing up the gravel apron at seven a.m., meant the gurney wheels squeaking on the ramp, meant a day spent in the company of someone who could no longer disappoint them.

This Tuesday it was Walter Pruitt, seventy-four, found in his recliner by a daughter who called from the porch because she couldn't make herself walk back into the living room. Dominic understood that. There were rooms you couldn't re-enter once you'd seen what they held.

"Walter Pruitt," they said to the body, unlatching the bag zipper with the two-handed care they gave everything. "Tuesday, the ninth of March. You're going to tell me how you went, and I'm going to write it down, and that will be the whole truth of it. Best deal in town."

Walter said nothing, which was one of Dominic's favorite things about him.

The autopsy suite smelled of formalin and lemon disinfectant and, underneath, the cold mineral smell of the refrigeration units — a smell Dominic had stopped noticing years ago the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat. Morning light came through the high frosted windows in gray rectangles and lay across the steel table like clean linen. Dominic worked to the radio, low, a classical station out of Ashfield that faded in and out depending on the weather. Today it held, mostly. Beethoven, then static, then Beethoven again, as if the composer were being transmitted from somewhere with a bad connection to the living.

They weighed Walter's heart and found it heavy in the way of hearts that had worked against the current for decades. They found the stents, two of them, silvered with age. They found the pulmonary edema that explained the wet sound his daughter must have heard, or imagined she heard, in the last minutes. Cause of death: acute cardiac failure, secondary to ischemic heart disease. Manner: natural. Dominic wrote it out in their small, exact hand, the one that had survived medical school and two residencies and the particular loneliness of being the only person in a county who touched the dead for a living.

*Walter Pruitt,* they wrote in the ledger. *Age 74. Retired millwright. He kept his tools clean.*

That last part wasn't required. Dominic put it in anyway, every time, a line of biography under the clinical record, because a file that said only *cardiac failure* was a file about a body, and Dominic had never once examined a body. They examined people. The people were just finished talking, that was all.

The mill had closed when Dominic was nine. Everyone in Harrow's Bend measured time that way — before the mill, after the mill — the way coastal towns measured hurricanes. After the mill, the young people left in a slow hemorrhage, the storefronts on Delancey Street went dark one awning at a time, and the town settled into its long negotiation with what it used to be. The cannery held on another decade, then the docks thinned to a handful of boats, then those too. What remained was the courthouse, the county offices, the church with its leaning steeple, and the founding families — the Cranes, the Calloways, the Harrow descendants themselves — whose names were on the library cornerstone and the bank charter and, if you knew where to look, on a great many other things.

Dominic's parents had come later, drawn by cheap housing and a hospital job for their father that evaporated within a year of their arrival. They'd stayed anyway. People stayed in Harrow's Bend the way water stayed in a low place. Adaeze had lasted until she was twenty-three. Then she'd gone — first to Ashfield, then to the coast, then to places she described in phone calls that arrived at holidays like weather reports from another planet — and Dominic had stayed, and the distance between them had grown from miles into something with no unit of measurement at all.

They thought of Adaeze sometimes while they worked. Not with bitterness, exactly. With the particular ache of a person who has one key to a house and knows the other key is in a drawer somewhere in a city they've never seen.

* * *

By noon Walter Pruitt was finished, sewn and cleaned and wrapped in dignity, and Dominic ate lunch at the steel counter standing up, the way they always did, a sandwich and coffee from the machine that tasted like the machine. The phone rang at 12:40. Sheriff Ilsa Brandt.

"Got a call I need to run out on," Brandt said, no greeting, which was normal. "Fella out past the quarry road, heart attack most likely, but he's got a head wound and I need you to look at him before I can call it."

"Send me the location."

"Already did." A pause. Brandt's pauses were a language of their own. "You eating lunch standing up again?"

"You can't see me."

"I can hear you chewing. Nobody who's happy chews standing up, Dominic."

"I'm perfectly content."

"Sure you are." And she hung up, because Brandt ended every conversation the way a door closes in winter — fast, to keep the heat in.

Dominic drove out the quarry road with the sandwich half-finished on the passenger seat and the radio off, because the quarry road deserved silence. The quarry had been dead longer than the mill — flooded, fenced, posted with signs that had rusted into suggestions — but it still drew the town's misadventures the way a cold hearth draws ghosts. Kids swam in it in summer against every warning. In the eleven months Dominic had held this office, the quarry had given up one stolen motorcycle, two abandoned dogs, and a great deal of beer cans, and nothing else.

The dead man was where Brandt said, at the pull-off by the fence, laid on the gravel with his jacket folded under his head by someone who'd gotten there first — a fisherman, it turned out, who'd found him and done what he could, which was nothing, which was what most people in Harrow's Bend could do for each other and did.

Dominic knelt, gloved, and read him.

Sixties. Head wound consistent with a fall against the fence post, abrasion pattern matching the rusted angle iron. No defensive injuries. The skin of his fingertips told the story of a man who'd worked with his hands and washed them carelessly, a mechanic's hands or a dockhand's. Lividity was settled and consistent with the position. No petechiae. Dominic spent forty minutes on the gravel in the wind, and when they stood up their knee cracked like a branch and the fisherman, a kid really, asked if they were okay.

"Fine," Dominic said. "He had a bad heart and a bad afternoon. The two found each other."

The kid nodded like that made sense, and maybe it did.

On the drive back, passing the water tower with HARROW'S BEND painted on it in letters the color of old teeth, Dominic did what they always did at the quarry turnoff: glanced at the water. Just glanced. A habit with no origin they could name, the way you check a lock you know is locked.

The quarry water lay flat and green and kept everything it was given.

* * *

The afternoon was paperwork, which Dominic did not mind. Paperwork was where the dead got their due. They updated the ledger, filed the Pruitt report, and re-shelved the archive cart that the county clerk's office kept promising to come collect and never did. The archive room was Dominic's favorite room in the building — a converted records closet, windowless, temperature-controlled, smelling of paper and dust and time. Row after row of gray boxes. Every death in Harrow's Bend for a hundred years, filed by name.

Dominic had a practice, on slow afternoons, of reading the old files. Not the recent ones — those were work. The old ones, the ones from before their time, were something else. History, but the kind with weight. They pulled a box at random sometimes, just to feel the town's whole mortality pass under their fingers. 1961. A drowning at the quarry, ruled accidental, a boy of nineteen. 1974, a fire on Delancey Street, three names. 1988, a mill accident the inquest had closed in a single afternoon, which even at a glance read wrong to Dominic's trained eye, though it was nobody's business now and everyone involved was dead or gone.

Every grave by name. That was the promise Dominic had made to themselves at twenty-six, standing in the anatomy lab at Ashfield while the other students complained about the smell. *Somebody has to know them. Somebody has to say the name.* It was not a noble feeling, mostly. It was more like a compulsion, the way some people straightened crooked pictures. But it was the truest thing about them, truer than any of the things people said about them — *the Okonkwo kid, the coroner, keeps to themselves* — and it was the thing that made the days bearable.

Because here was the arithmetic Dominic had never said out loud: the dead were easy. The dead wanted nothing. The dead did not ask how you were doing in a tone that meant they wanted you to ask back. The dead lay still and told the truth eventually, if you were patient, if you were careful, if you gave them the hours they deserved. The living lied, and left, and phoned at Christmas, and the living were Adaeze, who hadn't come home in four years, and the living were the town, which looked through you at whatever the founding families were doing behind the glass.

Dominic locked the archive room at 4:30, as always, and stood a moment in the dark with their hand on the door, listening to the building settle. Somewhere down the hall a phone rang and rang and gave up. The dead in their boxes did not ring. The dead waited.

* * *

The house was quiet when they got home, the way it was always quiet — a small rental on Corliss Street with a porch that listed slightly to the left and a kitchen where Dominic ate dinner standing up too, a fact they were aware said something about them and declined to investigate. They fed the cat they didn't have, a joke with no audience, and put on water for tea, and checked the mail.

Three items. A utility bill. A flyer for the spring estate sales — the paper had a map, and someone had circled the Voss house on Delancey Street in pen, though Dominic didn't know who and didn't think about it. And a postcard from Adaeze, four words on the back of a picture of a lighthouse:

*Thinking of you. Stay.*

No date. No return address. Adaeze's postcards always said *stay*, and Dominic had never decided whether it was a blessing or an instruction, whether she meant *stay who you are* or *stay there, it's safer* or simply *stay, so that when I come back something is still standing.*

They put the postcard on the refrigerator under a magnet shaped like a peach, next to the other four, a little archive of their own. Then they sat down at the kitchen table — the one concession, sitting, at the end of the day, when the standing was over — and opened the county file they'd brought home, because even Dominic's evenings had paperwork in them, and the paperwork was company.

Outside, the wind came down off the ridge and moved through the bare maples, and the streetlight at the corner flickered on the way it did, twice, three times, before committing. Somewhere a dog was barking at nothing. The town went about its evening of not being watched.

Dominic worked until ten. Then they washed the cup, checked the locks they knew were locked, and went to bed in the quiet house on Corliss Street, in the quiet town, in the long after-the-mill, where every grave had a name and every name had a file, and nothing, as far as anyone knew, had ever been filed wrong.

The estate sales started Saturday.

Dominic would be there.
