## 3. The Client Who Wasn't There

The envelope sat on my desk for two days before I opened it again.

Not because I'd lost interest. Because I'd learned the hard way that some things need to sit in the dark a while before you look at them straight. My father used to say that about fish. He meant it about people too, though he never said so out loud — Marcus Pemberton was a man who kept his metaphors gutted and iced like everything else in his life.

Two thousand four hundred dollars in cash, counted twice by me and once more by Ruthie, who ran the numbers through her fingers like rosary beads and pronounced them clean. "No powder, no dye," she'd said. "Whoever this is didn't rob an ATM. This came from a drawer."

A drawer. That word had been rattling around in my skull ever since.

So on Tuesday night, with the dispatch board quiet and Deacon doing homework at the corner table under the pretense of not listening to everything I said into my headset, I opened the envelope again and this time I read it like a dispatcher reads a call log — not for what's said, but for what the saying costs.

*To V. Pemberton. Retainer for surveillance services, night of the Harrow anniversary dinner, October 14th. Objective: establish that no incident occurs. Payment triple rate. No further contact required unless an incident occurs.*

No name. No phone number. No return address. Just a post office box on Delancey Street and, beneath the typed text, a letterhead that had been printed onto the paper itself — not letterhead stationery, but a facsimile, the way a forger reproduces a signature. Someone had wanted the paper to *look* official without being traceable to any actual office.

Except they'd made one mistake. Everyone makes one mistake. It's the whole reason I still have a job.

The letterhead read: **Whitcombe & Associates, Attorneys at Law, Suite 400, 11 Candlemas Row.**

I knew the building. Everybody in this parish knew the buildings on Candlemas Row — old money row, four stories of brownstone offices where the estate lawyers and the insurance men had kept their brass plaques polished since before the war. But I didn't know the firm. And in ten years of dispatching cabs past every door in this city, I'd have remembered a law office at number eleven, because number eleven was the one with the broken stoop light that Okonkwo complained about every winter for a decade.

I pulled up the dispatch archive on the terminal Ruthie had talked Bellwether into buying back when terminals were new, and I searched eleven Candlemas Row across all years on file.

The last pickup logged from that address was March 9th, fourteen years ago. A fare to the county courthouse. Passenger name field: blank. Driver: T. Okonkwo.

After that, nothing. Not because nobody needed a cab there. Because there was nobody there to need one.

* * *

Deacon looked up from his homework when I started pulling phone books out of the cabinet under the radio desk. We keep every phone book the parish has produced since 1998, because Ruthie doesn't throw anything away and because half our drivers can't be trusted to remember an address without looking at it twice.

"Are we doing a project?" he asked, in the tone of a boy who has learned that my projects are never the kind you get credit for.

"Research."

"Is research code for something illegal?"

"Research is code for something that isn't illegal yet." I fanned the books across the desk. "Hand me the one from fifteen years back."

He did, and together we went through them the way archaeologists go through strata. Whitcombe & Associates appeared in the 2008 book at 11 Candlemas Row, Suite 400. Appeared again in 2009. In the 2010 edition, the listing was gone, and in its place, in the fine print of discontinued numbers: *W. Whitcombe, dec.*

Dec. Deceased.

"Okay," Deacon said slowly, reading over my shoulder the way he does, close enough that I could smell the cheap shampoo the shelter hands out. "So your client hired you using the letterhead of a lawyer who's been dead for — how long?"

"That's the question, isn't it." I tapped the page. "Dead men don't retain taxi services."

"Maybe somebody kept his stationery."

"Somebody always keeps the stationery. That's not the interesting part." I closed the book. "The interesting part is that whoever printed this letterhead wanted it to look real enough to fool a casual glance but fake enough to leave no thread to pull. Except they pulled it from the wrong source. They copied it off something. And things get copied wrong."

Deacon grinned. He's got a face built for grinning, all sharp angles and mischief, and it's a good thing, because the rest of his life hasn't given him much else to work with. "You want to go look at a dead man's office."

"I want to drive past a dead man's office," I corrected. "At a legal and reasonable speed. With all appropriate documentation."

"You don't have documentation."

"I have a taxi."

* * *

Candlemas Row at midnight is a street of dark windows and one working streetlamp, and even that one flickers like it's trying to send a message in code. Number eleven stands between a shuttered florist and a title company that only opens during bank hours, which in this parish means never. The stoop light Okonkwo used to complain about is still broken. Some things outlast the people who noticed them.

Suite 400 would be the top floor. I stood on the sidewalk with my collar up and looked at the dark windows above the brass plate — **WHITCOMBE & ASSOCIATES**, the letters gone green with tarnish, the ampersand nearly eaten away — and I felt that feeling I get sometimes behind the headset, the one where the city goes quiet in a way that isn't peace. The way a room goes quiet when someone's stopped breathing in it.

There was dust on the brass plate. Thick, even, undisturbed dust, except for one patch at the lower right corner, wiped clean in the shape of a fingertip. Recent. Somebody had touched that plate within the last few days. Within the last week, anyway — long enough for a faint haze to start settling back over the shine.

I didn't touch it. I stood there with my hands in my pockets and let the cold come up through my shoes, and I thought about prepaid fares and dead lawyers and a woman named Eleanor Harrow whose death had been so neatly closed that the parish stopped saying her name, and I thought about my father in Cell Block D, ten years into a silence he wouldn't break even for me.

Then I walked back to the cab, and I drove home at a legal and reasonable speed, and I did not sleep.

* * *

Thursday I called the county records office and asked about probate filings for a W. Whitcombe. The clerk — a woman named Doreen who has been putting me on hold since the Clinton administration — came back after twenty minutes with the kind of voice people use when they've found something slightly stranger than expected.

"Wilbur J. Whitcombe, died October 2nd, ten years ago. Heart failure. Estate went through probate fast — small estate, no heirs listed. Everything liquidated by the executor."

"When exactly did the estate close?"

Pause. Paper shuffling. "March of the following year. Why do you ask?"

"No reason. Who was the executor?"

Another pause, longer. When Doreen's pauses get long, it means she's deciding whether telling me will get her in trouble. Doreen has never once decided against telling me. She likes me. She thinks I'm doing genealogy.

"The executor of record was Edmund Harrow," she said. "Of Harrow House. You know, the —"

"I know the family," I said, and thanked her, and hung up, and sat very still in the dispatch chair while the board blinked its little green lights at me and Deacon pretended to read a comic book and the whole parish went on making its small nighttime noises outside the window.

Edmund Harrow. The patriarch. The grieving father-in-law who had identified the body, retained the prosecution's star expert, and sat in the front row of the courtroom every day of my father's trial with his grief folded neatly in his lap like a handkerchief. The man whose testimony about Eleanor's habits, her fears, her enemies, had stitched the case shut so tight that the jury took four hours.

And now his estate — or someone using his dead lawyer's ghost — was paying me triple rate to prove that nothing would happen at his anniversary dinner.

You see the shape of it. I saw the shape of it, sitting there in the green glow of the board. But seeing the shape of a thing and understanding it are different animals, and I hadn't met the second animal yet.

* * *

Friday night I did the thing I should have done first, because I'm a dispatcher before I'm anything else, and dispatchers trust paper trails over hunches. I pulled the original booking record for the prepaid fare.

It had come in by phone, nine days ago, 11:47 p.m. — late, but not unusual; the strange jobs always come in late, when the caller thinks the night shift won't ask questions. Caller ID blocked. Call duration: six minutes, twelve seconds. Six minutes is a long time for a booking. Most bookings take ninety seconds. Which means whoever called spent five minutes talking around the transaction, and I had been on that call, and I had not been recording it, because we don't record calls, we just answer them, and I had done what I always do with strange callers — listened politely, quoted the rate, taken the cash drop instructions, and hung up thinking *odd* instead of thinking *evidence*.

But the switchboard logs time-stamp everything, and cross-referenced against the tower records Ruthie keeps for billing disputes, I could reconstruct the routing. The call had originated from a landline. Not a cell. A hardwired landline, in an era when hardly anyone under seventy still has one.

Landlines register to addresses.

I called Doreen back. Genealogy, I said. Family property records. She sighed the sigh of a woman who knows genealogy when she hears it, and gave me what I needed: the landline exchange serving the north parish, and the subscriber blocks within it.

Eleven Candlemas Row had its own line once — Whitcombe's line, disconnected at death. But the block assignment showed something better. The exchange also served Harrow House.

Harrow House had three registered lines. The main house line. A service line for the kitchen wing. And a third line, listed to *E. Harrow, private study*, active continuously for thirty-one years.

The booking call had routed through the north parish exchange at 11:47 p.m. on a Tuesday. I couldn't prove which of the three lines it came from — the carrier's detail records were beyond even Doreen — but I could prove the neighborhood, the exchange, the hour. And I could prove something else, something that turned the unease in my chest into a cold, precise, humming obsession:

The night of the booking call, Harrow House had held a dinner party. Twelve guests, catered, logged in the society column of the parish paper. The article said the family had gathered, as they did every year, to plan the upcoming tenth-anniversary commemoration of Eleanor's death.

Every year, the same week, the same ritual. And this year — the milestone year, the decade mark — someone in that house had picked up a private phone at quarter to midnight and hired a stranger to watch the house and prove that nothing happened.

Prove that nothing happens. As if the happening were a scheduled event. As if the murder were on the calendar, printed in ink, waiting for its date the way the dinner was.

Nobody hires a watcher to prevent a crime that isn't coming. And nobody — *nobody* — phrases it that way unless they already know exactly what the crime looks like, because they've seen it before.

Ten years ago. Same house. Same date.

* * *

I didn't tell anyone, at first. That's the part I'm not proud of. The found family — Ruthie with her casseroles and her ledgers, Okonkwo with his forty years of night routes mapped into his knuckles, Deacon asleep on the break room couch with his homework unfinished — they were right there, and I said nothing, because the shape of what I was seeing had my father standing in the middle of it, and love makes you secretive before it makes you brave.

But Saturday night, Okonkwo came in early for his shift, which he never does, and put a cup of coffee on my desk without being asked, which he also never does, and stood there looking at the phone books still stacked open on the corner of the desk, at the Candlemas Row pages, at the circled listing for a dead man's office.

"You been to Eleven Candlemas," he said. Not a question. Forty years of driving this parish at night gives a man a nose for where other people have been driving.

"How'd you know?"

"Dust on your coat sleeve. Brick dust, the yellow kind, from that row's mortar. Only place in the parish with mortar that color." He lowered himself into the chair across from me, slow, the way big careful men sit down when they've decided the conversation matters. "Vesper. What's the job?"

And because it was Okonkwo, and because the coffee was hot and the board was quiet and I was tired of carrying the shape of it alone, I told him. The envelope. The letterhead. The dead lawyer. The executor. The landline exchange. The anniversary dinner, and the phrasing — *prove nothing happens* — repeated aloud in my own voice for the first time, hearing how it sounded, how it sounded like a man quoting himself.

Okonkwo listened the way he drives: steady, both hands, eyes moving. When I finished, he was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, "Ten years ago, the night Mrs. Harrow died, there was a Bellwether cab at the Harrow gate."

The board blinked. Somewhere across town a fare waited and didn't know yet that the world had just tilted.

"What?"

"There was a cab at the gate." His voice stayed level, but his hands, folded on his knees, had gone white at the knuckles. "I know because—" He stopped. Started again. "Because I need to show you something in the archive, and I need you to understand that I should have shown you ten years ago, and I didn't, and there's a reason, and the reason is going to make you hate me."

"Okonkwo—"

"It's in the tape library," he said. "The old dispatch tapes. The ones the company was supposed to destroy after the trial."

He stood up. Behind him, Deacon had come awake on the break room couch, silent, watching us through the doorway with the particular stillness of a kid who has learned that the important conversations happen when adults think you're asleep.

Okonkwo held out his hand. "Come on then," he said. "Before I lose the nerve I've been rationing for a decade."
