## 7. Deacon Sees Something

The Harrow estate smelled like money trying to remember it was old. Deacon had been delivering there for three weeks now — flowers on Tuesdays, dry cleaning on Thursdays, and tonight, a Friday, a garment box from Marchetti's that weighed almost nothing and cost more than everything he owned, which wasn't much, but he'd stopped keeping score.

He pedaled his bike up the long gravel drive at 11:40 p.m., because the kitchen door was the only one that stayed open this late, and Mrs. Okafor — no relation to Okonkwo, though Deacon had checked twice, hoping for some cosmic joke — the cook liked her deliveries before midnight or not at all. The service entrance sat in the shadow of the east wing, all ivy and dead moths in the light fixture, and Deacon coasted to a stop with the practiced silence of a boy who'd learned that being heard was the same as being caught.

"Marchetti's," he called softly through the screen.

No answer.

He knocked again, gentler than the first time, which felt backwards but was correct. Inside, the house did that thing big houses do when they're empty — it breathed. Radiators ticking. A clock somewhere counting out seconds nobody needed counted. Deacon shifted the garment box under his arm and told himself the cook was just in the pantry, that the whole staff hadn't gone to bed, that this was normal, that nothing about this house was ever going to be normal and he should stop pretending otherwise.

Because here was the thing Deacon hadn't told Vesper yet, the thing sitting in his chest like a swallowed stone: three nights ago, he'd seen something. And every night since, he'd come back anyway, telling himself it was about the delivery money — forty bucks a run, cash, no questions — when really it was about wanting to see it again, or wanting to be sure he'd seen it wrong.

The screen door opened. Not the cook.

A woman in a gray uniform, maybe fifty, hair pinned so tight it looked structural. She looked at him the way people look at weather they've already decided to ignore.

"You're late."

"It's eleven-forty-two."

"She said midnight." The woman held out her hand for the box, then didn't take it. "Wait here."

She shut the door. Deacon stood on the mat with the moths and waited, and while he waited, he did what he always did: he mapped the exits. Kitchen door behind him. Service drive curving off toward the gatehouse, past the hedge. Windows along the ground floor, two of them dark, one lit gold with what looked like a study lamp. He'd learned mapping from Vesper, who said you could tell everything about a house by where its doors were, and everything about a person by which door they used.

Vesper would hate that he was here. That was the whole problem. Vesper, who'd taken him in off the bench outside the Greyhound station two winters ago, who'd taught him the dispatch board and the difference between listening and eavesdropping, who'd said *you don't go into houses, Deke, you wait for houses to come out* — Vesper would look at him with that flat dispatcher stare and say, *why are you delivering anything to the Harrows,* and Deacon would have to either lie or say the true thing, which was worse: *because I saw him.*

*Him.* Edmund Harrow. The patriarch. The man whose dementia was famous in the parish the way weather is famous — everyone discussed it, nobody questioned it. Ten years ago his wife had died in this house and a cab driver named Pemberton had gone to prison for it, and now Vesper was pulling threads on the whole tapestry, and Deacon had gone looking for loose ones himself, because that's what family does. Found family. Chosen family. Family that doesn't get a vote when you do something stupid.

The door opened again. The woman took the box without thanks and pressed an envelope into his hand instead of change. Thick envelope. Deacon thumbed it — twenties, more than forty — and the woman watched him count with something like contempt.

"Mr. Harrow likes punctuality rewarded," she said. "And discretion."

"I'm very discreet."

"Mm." Her eyes went over his shoulder, down the dark drive, checking for something. "You'll find the household restless this week. Anniversary preparations. If you hear anything — voices, movement — you were never here. Understood?"

"Sure," Deacon said, and meant: *I understand exactly as much as I want to.*

She closed the door. He pocketed the envelope, swung onto his bike, and got four pedal strokes toward the gate before the lit window stopped him.

It was the study. He'd noticed it on previous runs — second floor, corner room, the one window in the whole east wing that burned late. Tonight the shade was up. Tonight someone stood in it.

Edmund Harrow was supposed to be asleep by nine. Everyone said so. The parish said it with a kind of tender pity — *poor man, sundowning, doesn't know his own son half the time* — and the anniversary coverage in the *Gazette* had said it too, a whole paragraph about how the family cared for him through his decline, how the dinner would be kept small for his sake. Deacon had read it twice at the diner, chewing the free bread, filing it away next to everything else he was collecting for Vesper without quite admitting he was collecting it.

The man in the window was not declining. The man in the window moved like a metronome. He crossed the room, precise, unhurried, and came back carrying a stack of papers, and Deacon — who knew better, who had promised himself after the last time that he'd just ride home, just ride home, Deke — set his bike against the hedge and stepped into the flower bed below the study window, into the black gap between the wall and the rhododendrons, and looked up.

There was a shredder in the room. Deacon could hear it from the ground — a thin mechanical growl, pausing, resuming, patient as digestion. Edmund fed it page by page. He held each sheet up to the lamp first, reading it, and once he laughed. Deacon heard the laugh come through the glass, low and unbothered, the laugh of a man alone in his own house doing whatever he liked, and the sound went down Deacon's spine like cold water finding a crack.

Then Edmund turned toward the window, and Deacon dropped flat into the flower bed, dirt in his mouth, heart slamming, and lay there among the dead marigolds listening to his own blood.

When he risked the look again, the old man was still at the window — not looking out, though. Looking at something in his hand. A photograph, maybe; small, pale rectangle. He studied it for a long moment, and then he did something strange even for a strange night: he raised it slightly, the way you raise a glass, a small salute to nobody, and put it in his breast pocket. Then he pulled the shade.

Deacon lay in the flower bed another full minute. The shredder growled on inside, muffled now, eating its way through ten years of paper.

* * *

He didn't go straight home. He rode the long way, down River Road with the water black and flat on his left, because riding fast made the fear burn off and thinking required speed. The envelope crinkled in his jacket. Sixty dollars, maybe eighty. Payment for discretion from a household that shredded its own history at midnight.

Here was what he'd seen three nights ago, the thing he'd been circling back to ever since:

He'd delivered late then too — a pharmacy run, insulin, supposedly for the patriarch — and the nurse had let him leave it at the door. On his way out, cutting across the back terrace because it was faster, he'd passed the sunroom, and through the glass he'd seen Edmund Harrow standing at a writing desk, upright, clear-eyed, signing his name to a document with the steady hand of a bank president. And beside him, holding the inkwell steady, had been the gray-uniformed woman, and she'd been smiling at him the way employees don't smile at employers they pity. It had lasted four seconds. Deacon had filed it under *weird* and ridden home.

But weird compounds. Four seconds of lucidity plus a midnight shredder plus a salute to a photograph plus eighty dollars for a teenager's silence — that wasn't weird anymore. That was architecture.

By the time he reached the bridge he'd built the whole argument against himself and lost it. Tell Vesper. Don't tell Vesper. Tell Vesper. The case for telling: Vesper was already deep in this, chasing a client who paid triple rate to prove a murder wouldn't happen, chasing a ghost lawyer, chasing a decade-old verdict that had eaten their father alive. Anything Deacon found was a plank Vesper might need. The case against telling was shorter and sharper: the last person who talked about the Harrows ended up in Cell Block D, and the current list of people asking questions included a teenage delivery boy whose name appeared on no payroll, whose address was technically a cot behind Bellwether Cabs, and whose entire legal existence fit in a backpack. If Edmund Harrow was faking dementia to run his family like a puppet show, then the puppeteer also owned the scissors. Deacon had seen what happened to loose threads. They got shredded, page by page, into a machine that growled.

But Vesper. God. Vesper, who left the porch light on for him even though he came home at 2 a.m., who'd written DEACON'S KEY on masking tape above the hook by the door like it was official, like it was permanent. Vesper walking into the anniversary dinner blind because Deacon wanted to stay safe — that image was worse than any gray sedan. You didn't protect family by letting them walk into a house where a man saluted photographs and ate paper.

He locked his bike behind Bellwether and let himself in through the garage. The dispatch office glowed its familiar green — the board lights, the radio's standby hum, the smell of burnt coffee that never fully left the walls. Vesper's headset hung on its peg. Vesper themselves was out on a surveillance run, tailing the estate's comings and goings per the anonymous client's instructions, and wouldn't be back till one or two.

Which gave Deacon ninety minutes to decide.

He sat at the spare desk — his desk, unofficially, the one with the crossword half-finished and a photo of a dog he'd never owned — and pulled the ledger pad toward him, and started writing it down the way Vesper had taught him: facts first, feelings later, and never mix them in the same paragraph.

*Fact:* Edmund Harrow signed documents lucidly, Thursday, approx. 10:15 p.m.
*Fact:* Same subject operated paper shredder, Friday, approx. 11:50 p.m., duration unknown, volume significant.
*Fact:* Staff instructed delivery personnel to claim non-presence. Payment exceeds agreed rate. Phrase used: "discretion."
*Fact:* Subject saluted a photograph before closing blinds.

He stared at the fourth line. Saluted. Was that even a fact? It was a gesture. It meant something — men didn't toast photographs they didn't love or fear — but meaning wasn't fact, and Vesper would draw a hard line right there, would tap the pad and say, *which part did you see, Deke, and which part did you decide?*

Fine. Then write the decision separately. Decision: the old man knows exactly what year it is. Decision: the dementia is a costume, and costumes are worn for audiences, which means somewhere there's an audience being managed, which means the anniversary dinner isn't a celebration, it's a performance with a scheduled finale. Decision: whatever gets shredded between now and Saturday is evidence, and evidence destroyed on schedule means somebody has a countdown clock, and everybody in this story keeps talking about clocks — the client's prepaid fare, the tenth anniversary, the dinner reservation, the final declaration Edmund had reportedly announced to his heirs.

Deacon put down the pen. His hands weren't shaking, which surprised him. Maybe fear ran out of fuel if you fed it facts.

The radio crackled — a fare call, routine, some businessman at the airport — and Deacon automatically reached for the log, because that was his job on nights like this, the small honest work of a small honest life he'd been handed by accident. He logged the call. He dispatched Okonkwo, who answered with his usual grave warmth, "Copy, little dispatcher," and the phrase settled something in Deacon's chest. Okonkwo drove nights like a penance; Ruthie fed everyone like an apology; Vesper listened to the whole city like a debt being repaid. Nobody in this family was clean of the Harrow case. It had gotten into all of them ten years ago, before Deacon even arrived, like smoke into curtains.

So it wasn't really a choice, was it. It had never been a choice. You didn't hold out on family. You just picked the moment, and the words, and braced for what the knowing would cost them — because that was the real fear, the one under the fear: not that Edmund Harrow would come for Deacon, but that the truth would come for Vesper, and Deacon would be the one who carried it through the door.

Headlights swept the garage. Engine tick, key jingle, the side door — Vesper, home early, coat smelling of river fog and gas-station coffee.

"You're up," Vesper said, unsurprised. Vesper was never surprised by Deacon's hours anymore, only by his silences.

"Yeah. Delivery run." Deacon slid the ledger pad casually off the desk, under a magazine. Too casual. He saw Vesper register it — the small recalibration behind the eyes, the dispatcher noting an anomaly on the board.

"Harrows?" Vesper asked lightly. Too lightly.

"Just flowers."

The lie sat between them, ugly and obvious, and Deacon hated it, and Vesper let it stand, which was somehow worse — Vesper turning to pour coffee, giving him the courtesy of a bad lie, and Deacon understanding suddenly that Vesper already knew he was lying, had probably known for days, and was waiting, the way good dispatchers wait, for the driver to call in.

"Deke." Vesper didn't turn around. "Whatever you're carrying — you know I can hear it, right? Through the radio, through the walls, through everything. It's the one thing I'm actually good at."

Deacon looked at the magazine covering the pad. Looked at Vesper's back, narrow and stubborn under the fluorescent green. Outside, far off toward the river, a car passed on the wet road, slow, and neither of them moved until its sound faded.

"Vesper," Deacon said, and his voice came out steadier than he felt, "how sure are you that Edmund Harrow doesn't know what's going on?"

Vesper set down the coffee pot very carefully, the way you set down something that might go off.

"Why?"

"Because I think he's the one running the clock." Deacon pulled the ledger pad back out and laid it on the desk, open, facts facing up. "And I think I've seen him winding it."
