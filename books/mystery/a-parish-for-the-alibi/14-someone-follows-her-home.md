## 14. Someone Follows Her Home

The first sign was the smell of someone else's coffee in my kitchen.

I stood in the doorway of my own flat at eleven at night, keys still in my hand, and breathed in the ghost of it — cheap instant, brewed within the last few hours, the bitterness still hanging in the air like a guest who'd left without saying goodbye. I don't drink instant. I keep a French press and I'm precious about it. The mug in the drying rack wasn't mine either; it said WORLD'S OKAYEST FISHERMAN, and I have never owned anything that cheerful.

Most people would have called the police. Most people have lives that can survive being explained to police.

I set my keys down on the hall table, quietly, the way you do when you're trying not to wake something, and I walked through my flat the way I'd taught myself to walk through other people's houses: heel to toe, eyes moving, hands in pockets so I wouldn't touch anything.

They had been thorough. That was the first thing I felt, before fear — a kind of professional respect. My files were all where I'd left them, which is how I knew they'd been gone through. Nobody who ransacks a place puts the folders back with the tabs aligned. Nobody but someone who wants you to wonder whether they were ever there at all. The Voss file sat square on my desk, corners flush with the desk's edge, exactly as I'd left it — except I hadn't left it that way. I'd left it fanned open to the ledger photographs, because I'd been mid-thought when I went out that morning, and mid-thought people leave messes.

The laptop had been moved too. Half a millimetre off its alignment with the desk edge — I measure things, it's an occupational deformity — and when I opened it, the lid hinge caught for half a second longer than it should. They'd photographed the screen. Probably photographed everything. Somewhere out there was a folder full of images of my life laid flat and catalogued: three solved cases, the Voss ledger, Devi's production schedule, my grocery list, the photograph of my mother I keep in a drawer.

And then I went into the bedroom, and stopped being able to be professional about any of it.

On my pillow, squared to the headboard with the same terrible neatness, lay a single page of ledger paper. Old stock, foxed at the edges, ruled columns gone amber with age. The ink was fresh.

I didn't touch it for a long time. I stood in my bedroom doorway with the hallway light behind me throwing my shadow across the bed, and I understood that this was not a threat. A threat says *stop*. This was a calling card. This said *we know where you sleep, and we wanted you to see the work up close*.

The entry was Aldous Voss. Of course it was. The final line, dated eleven days out from the night I'd cracked the cipher — nine days out now. But beneath the date, in that same fresh hand, someone had added a new notation in the margin, small and precise:

*The archivist keeps her own accounts.*

My name. Not my name — my function. Edmund Zhou, who solves deaths nobody asked her to solve, reduced to a line item. I sat down on the edge of my bed because my legs decided the matter without consulting me, and I looked at that page for what felt like an hour, learning it the way I'd learned the others. The pressure of the pen. The slight forward slant. The way the crossbar of the *t* hooked left, like a hand reaching back for something.

Ruth Calloway's hand. Or a very good imitation of it. Or Ruth Calloway's hand guided by someone who knew exactly what she was doing and wanted me to think so.

That was the thing about the page on my pillow. It wasn't just a message. It was a misdirection wrapped in a message wrapped in a threat, and I couldn't unwrap it alone.

I called Marlowe at midnight. He answered on the second ring, voice wide awake, and I hated him a little for that — hated that some part of me had known he'd be awake, that some part of me had dialled his number before Devi's, before anyone's.

"They were in my flat," I said. No hello. We were past hellos.

Silence, and then the sound of him sitting up. "Are you hurt?"

"They made coffee." I heard myself say it and heard how it sounded — absurd, domestic, obscene. "They used my kitchen. They put everything back. They left a page of the ledger on my pillow."

"Edmund." My name in his mouth did something it had no business doing at midnight with my home broken open. "I'm coming over."

"That's not—"

"I'm coming over." And the line went dead, and I sat in my violated kitchen holding a phone that had gone warm in my hand, and I realised I hadn't argued very hard.

* * *

He arrived in nineteen minutes. I know because I watched the clock, because timing things is how I keep from feeling them. He came up the stairs quietly — he'd learned my building's staircase on his third visit, the one that creaked, the one to skip — and when he saw my face he didn't ask permission for anything. He just crossed the room and pulled me in, and I let him, and for maybe thirty seconds I was a woman being held in her own kitchen instead of an investigator assessing a crime scene.

Then I stepped back, because the page was still on my pillow and the coffee smell was still in the air and I don't get to fall apart until the case is closed. That's the deal I made with myself years ago, after the case I don't talk about, after an innocent man's life came apart because I got the story wrong before I got it right. You don't get to be fragile while someone else's fate is in your notes.

"Show me," he said.

I showed him. He read the marginal note twice, and I watched his face go through something complicated — fear, yes, but underneath it a flash of something that looked almost like recognition, like the handwriting itself was familiar in a way he couldn't name or didn't want to.

"You've seen this hand before," I said.

"No." Too fast. Then, honestly: "Maybe. The letters. The ones coaching me — I told you, they came typed, but there was one, early on, handwritten. A note tucked into the envelope. I thought it was nothing. I threw it away." He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "God. I threw away evidence because I was scared and stupid and I wanted the whole thing to stop."

"What did it say?"

"'Stay quiet. The account settles itself.'" He lowered his hands. "Same phrasing. Same slant. I remember the slant because it leaned forward, like it was hurrying somewhere."

Like a hand reaching back for something. I kept that to myself.

"Here's what I can't get past," I said, pacing now, because sitting still had become impossible. "They searched my flat and they photographed my files, and then they *left the files*. If the goal was to shut me down, they'd have taken the ledger photos, wiped the laptop, made my work disappear. Instead they gave me a souvenir. That's theatre. Someone wants me frightened, and someone wants me *looking* — and I can't tell yet if those are the same someone."

"And if they are?"

"Then I'm being managed, Marlowe. Like you were. Like Aldous might be. Someone out there is running this whole parish like a parish — shepherding everyone toward the same appointed hour." I stopped at the window. The street below was empty in the sodium light, but empty streets are never empty; they're just streets where you can't see. "Which means every move I make from tonight may be a move someone has already scheduled."

"Then don't make moves alone." He said it simply, no drama in it, and that was what undid me. "I'm staying. Don't argue. I'll take the sofa, I'll take the floor, I'll take the bloody hallway, but you're not sleeping in a flat that smells of strangers' coffee."

"Marlowe—"

"You've spent weeks inside my family's house counting its dead," he said. "Let me spend one night guarding yours."

* * *

He took the sofa. Of course he took the sofa; he was raised by Aldous Voss and Mrs. Pell between them, and whatever else that upbringing broke in him, it installed manners like load-bearing walls. I gave him a blanket and a pillow and stood in my bedroom doorway watching him arrange himself with the careful cheerfulness of a man who knows he's imposing and hopes charm will cover it.

"The sofa's short," I said.

"I'm flexible."

"You're six foot two."

"I'm spiritually compact." He looked at me across the darkened room, and the joke drained out of his face, and what was left was rawer and younger. "Edmund. I need to say something and then I'll shut up and let you sleep."

"Don't say it."

"You don't even know what—"

"If you say it tonight, in this flat, with that page on my pillow, then it becomes part of the case. It becomes something that happened under threat, and I will never be sure — neither of us will ever be sure — whether it was real or whether it was just adrenaline looking for a shape." I heard my own voice shake and despised it. "I've built everything on knowing the difference between evidence and noise. You're not allowed to be noise."

He was quiet for a long moment. The radiator ticked. Somewhere below, a car passed, and we both tracked it — I saw his head turn toward the window, saw mine do the same, and something almost like laughter moved through the fear.

"That," he said finally, "is the most romantic thing anyone has ever said to me."

"I'm serious."

"I know you're serious. That's why it's romantic." He settled back against the sofa arm. "For the record, then, since we're cataloguing evidence: I am staying because I'm terrified for you. Also because I'm terrified of you, a little, in a way I enjoy. Also because when you look at me across a table and dismantle my alibi, it's the most seen I've ever felt in my life. All of that is real and none of it is adrenaline, and I will say it again later, under better lighting, whenever you give the word."

I closed the bedroom door. I lay down carefully on my side of the bed, avoiding the pillow with the page on it, and stared at the ceiling, and listened to the sounds of a man making himself small on my sofa because he'd decided my safety mattered more than his comfort.

Duty and desire, braided. I'd spent my whole career keeping those threads separate — the rule about never meeting clients existed precisely so that the work stayed clean, so that I could follow evidence wherever it pointed without my heart filing objections. And now the evidence pointed at the man sleeping twenty feet away, and my heart had already signed the retainer.

Because here is what I hadn't told him, what I couldn't tell him yet: the debts were real. The inheritance was real. The timeline benefited him and no one else, and every rational instrument I owned said *Marlowe Voss*, and the only thing arguing otherwise was the sound of his breathing through a thin door and the fact that he'd thrown away a threatening letter rather than use it — because using it would have meant admitting the whole machinery was real.

Innocent people destroy evidence out of panic. So do guilty ones. That's the problem. That's always the problem.

I slept maybe two hours. At some point I woke to a sound and found him standing in my bedroom doorway, silhouetted against the window, checking on me — not coming in, just confirming I was breathing. He thought I was asleep. I let him think it. He stood there long enough to be certain, then withdrew, and I lay in the dark understanding that I was in more danger from that doorway than from the page on my pillow.

* * *

Morning arrived grey and thin. I made proper coffee — the French press, defiantly — and found him already awake, hair wrecked, wearing yesterday's shirt, studying the ledger page where it lay sealed now in a plastic sleeve on my kitchen table.

"I bagged it at dawn," I said. "Gloves. Before anything could degrade it."

"See, this is why you're the professional." He turned it toward the light. "Nine days."

"Eight now. It's past midnight somewhere in the arithmetic." I sat down across from him. "Here's where we are. Someone searched my flat, which means someone knows my address, which means someone has access to information I've never given to anyone except—" I stopped.

"Except your producer," Marlowe said quietly.

"Devi wouldn't."

"I know. But write down who knew, anyway. That's your method, isn't it? Write down who knew." He slid the sleeve back across the table to me. "There's something else. Last night, when I checked on you — I heard your phone buzz on the nightstand. Twice. Then once more, a minute later. I assumed it was Devi."

It hadn't been Devi. Devi had texted once, at 6:14 that morning, asking if I was alive, which was her standard greeting. I picked up the phone now and opened the messages, and there they were, timestamped while I slept: three texts from a number with no name attached.

The first: *You counted the portraits. Well done.*

The second: *Mrs. Pell means well. She always has. That's the tragedy of her.*

The third, sent last, sent while Marlowe stood in my doorway believing me asleep:

*Ask him what he buried in the boathouse.*

I looked up. Marlowe was watching my face, and whatever he saw there made him go still.

"Edmund," he said. "What is it?"

Outside, far down the street, a car sat with its engine running, patient as weather. And in my hand, the phone glowed with a question I suddenly dreaded answering — because the boathouse was where we'd met, where he'd hired me, where he'd been charming and evasive and terrified, and I had never once asked myself the simplest question of all:

What was Marlowe Voss doing there, that day, before he ever found me?

"Tell me about the boathouse," I said.
