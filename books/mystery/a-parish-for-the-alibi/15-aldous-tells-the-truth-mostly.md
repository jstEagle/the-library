## 15. Aldous Tells the Truth, Mostly

Aldous Voss received me in the morning room because the doctor said he shouldn't climb to the library anymore. The morning room was a compromise: tall windows facing the yew walk, a fire lit against a grey October that hadn't yet decided to rain, and Aldous himself propped in a wingback like a man who had arranged his own display and found it satisfactory.

He'd sent for me. That was the first strange thing. I had toured his house under a pretext weeks ago; I had interviewed his nurse under the fiction of an article on private geriatric care; I had never once been invited. Now there was a note in his own hand — spidery but decisive, the handwriting of a man who still signed things that mattered — asking Miss Zhou to call at eleven, alone.

Alone. No Ruth hovering with her gentle questions. No Mrs. Pell materializing in doorways with her warnings about accounts. Just the two of us and a fire that crackled like it had opinions.

"You look disappointed," Aldous said when Mrs. Pell had shut the door behind me. "I imagine you expected to have to break in."

"I considered it," I said.

"I know you did. Marlowe told me." He smiled at whatever he saw cross my face. "Oh, don't glare at the boy. He tells me everything. It's one of his few virtues. Loyalty without judgment — rare in this family. We're all judgment here. Judgment is our chief crop."

I took the chair opposite him. Between us sat a tea service no one poured from and a chessboard set up mid-game, though I doubted either of us would touch it. It was furniture for a conversation, not a game — something to make the room feel inhabited by more than two people and a secret.

"You know why I'm here," I said.

"Of course I do. You think I'm going to be murdered." He said it the way other men said *I'm having fish for dinner*. "You've thought so since the boathouse. You've been creeping around my house and my parish and my nephew's finances for a month, and you've been very good about it, actually — better than the last investigator we hired, who got as far as the gatehouse before Mrs. Pell turned him around. But yes. Murder. Eleven days, isn't it? Or fewer now. I lose track. Days compress at my age. Like fruit."

My chest went tight. "How do you know about the dates?"

"Because I've read the ledger, Miss Zhou. All of it. I've read it every year of my life." He leaned forward, and the firelight moved across a face that was a ruin and a fortress at once — the handsome bones of the portraits downstairs, collapsed into something older and harder. "You found St. Anselm's tithing records. Very clever. My grandfather's cleverness, mostly. Do you know what a tithe actually was, before it became a line in an accounting book? It was a tenth of everything you had, rendered to God, in acknowledgment that none of it was really yours. Property. Children. Life itself. This family has always understood that. We just kept better books than God."

"The ledger maps inheritances," I said. "Deaths against estates. And someone is still writing in it."

"Yes," he said. "I am."

The fire popped. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard settled, the house shifting its weight like a sleeping animal.

"You," I repeated.

"Every entry since 1994. My father's hand before that, and his mother's before his, back to ink that's flaking off vellum." He said it flatly, without pride, which somehow made it worse. "You seem surprised. Did you expect me to be the innocent in my own story? A wealthy old man, lucid, dying on schedule, surrounded by heirs with motives — surely he must be the victim. Victims are always the ones who least suspect. Isn't that your doctrine? I listen to your podcast, by the way. Devi's editing is excellent. You have a gift for making dread feel like arithmetic."

"You listen to—"

"Every episode. Twice. Once for the facts and once for how you arrange them." He picked up the teapot, considered it, and put it down again without pouring. "That's why I sent for you. Not the facts. The arrangement."

"Aldous." I made myself use his name, because the moment required it. "Someone has scheduled your death. There is a codicil — a second codicil — signed but undated, and whoever controls the date controls everything you own. Your nurse signs nursing charts in the same hand that's been updating the ledger. And you're telling me you're the author of the instrument being used to kill you."

"I'm telling you I'm the author of the instrument, full stop." His eyes were pale blue and absolutely steady. "What's being done with it is another matter. One thing at a time. That's how accounts work."

* * *

He told it in order, because he was a bookkeeper to the bone, and the story came out the way entries come out: dated, itemized, unembellished.

Nineteen sixty-eight. A girl born in Harwich to a woman named Enid Calloway, a seamstress Aldous had loved badly and briefly and, he admitted, cowardly. He was twenty-six and already half-married to the estate — to the expectations, the portraits, the parish pew with the family name brassed into it. When Enid wrote to tell him, he did what Voss men did with liabilities. He settled them. Money, monthly, through a solicitor, for eighteen years. Then the letters stopped coming, and he told himself that was resolution.

"It wasn't resolution," he said. "It was default. There's a difference, and any decent auditor would have caught it. I caught it myself forty years too late, which is the family specialty."

Two years ago, Enid Calloway died. And among her effects, passed along by a cousin with a conscience, was a shoebox: every letter Aldous had ever sent, every bank draft stub, and a photograph of a girl growing into a woman across twenty years of school portraits — a face that was, unmistakably, a younger version of the face now sitting across the fire from me, if you subtracted sixty years and added a woman's softness.

"My daughter," Aldous said. "Written out of every will ever drawn for this family. Never named in a document. Never brought inside these walls except once, when she was four, and Mrs. Pell's predecessor was told she was the gardener's niece." He looked at his hands. "She was in this room. She sat where you're sitting."

The vertigo started then, quietly, the way a floor starts to tilt before you consciously notice it. Because I knew the surname. Of course I knew the surname. Calloway. Ruth Calloway, who answered every question with a question, who never left the room, whose nursing charts matched the fresh ink in a centuries-old book of deaths.

But I didn't say it. Something in Aldous's voice — the careful, itemized flatness of it — told me he already knew what I knew, and that the shape of this conversation depended on neither of us saying it yet.

"So you wrote the codicil," I said instead.

"Two years ago. Restoring her inheritance. Her full share — not charity, not a settlement. Her share, as if she'd never been erased. Signed it in front of my solicitor, sealed it, and then—" he spread his hands, an old man's shrug that contained an empire of evasion, "—didn't date it. Didn't lodge it. Kept it in the safe with the rest of the family's unfinished business."

"Why not date it? Why not execute it? One signature, one filing, and she's provided for. No ledger, no schedule, no—" I stopped myself from saying *no murder*, because he was already shaking his head.

"Because a codicil can be contested. Because Marlowe would contest it, and his creditors would contest it, and the family would spend ten years and half the estate proving what should simply be true." He said it reasonably, and that was the horror of it — it *was* reasonable. Every step of this monstrous design was reasonable in isolation. "And because—" and here his voice changed, dropped into something lower, almost shy, "—because I wanted to see what they would do. What this family does, when the account comes due. Whether any of them deserved the name."

"That's not justice," I said. "That's a test. You've turned your own death into an examination."

"Yes," Aldous said, pleasantly. "Exactly that."

* * *

I stood up. I didn't decide to stand; my body did it, some animal part of me refusing to sit comfortably across from what he was describing. The fire had burned down to a red argument of coals, and the light through the windows had gone the color of pewter, and somewhere in the walls the house ticked and settled, counting.

"Do you understand what you've built?" I asked. "You wrote a document that says *my daughter inherits everything*. Undated. And then you let a ledger circulate in this house — a ledger this family treats as scripture — with your death entered in it, dated, in fresh ink. You didn't create a test. You created a permission slip. You handed anyone with grief or grievance or greed a map and a deadline and called it an examination. If Ruth Calloway is your daughter—"

"Don't," Aldous said. Not sharply. Wearily. "Don't do my thinking for me, Miss Zhou. It's insulting to both of us."

"Then tell me I'm wrong. Tell me you didn't learn her name, hire her, install her at your bedside, and then wait for the date you wrote in your own book of deaths. Tell me you haven't spent two years arranging yourself like an exhibit."

He was quiet for a long time. The coals shifted. Down the corridor, faintly, I heard Mrs. Pell's footsteps pass and fade — the housekeeper keeping her rounds, keeping her accounts.

"She applied for the position," he said finally. "Did you know that? Two years ago, after her mother died. Applied by letter, qualified on paper, interviewed by agency. I recognized her before she finished the second sentence. She has Enid's way of pausing before she answers — as if checking the sum twice." His mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly grief. "I could have turned her away. I could have hired the other candidate, dated the codicil, posted it, written to her, explained myself like a civilized man. Instead I hired her, and I signed nothing, and I waited. Yes. All right. I'll give you that. I waited."

"For what?"

"To see if she'd kill me." He said it with terrible simplicity. "If she'd take the inheritance the way I took her childhood — quietly, through paperwork, without ever looking at what I'd done. If she was mine. Blood will tell, my grandmother used to say. I wanted to know what it told."

"And if she doesn't?" I asked. "If she's not a murderer? What happens in eleven days when you're alive and the ledger is wrong?"

"Then the ledger gets corrected," Aldous said, "and she gets everything anyway, because I'll date the codicil, and this family will have produced exactly one honest person in six generations, and she won't even carry the name." He looked at me then, fully, and for the first time the old man's armor slipped and I saw underneath it something that frightened me far more than cunning did: relief. "Either way, Miss Zhou, the account settles. That's all I've ever wanted. To see it settled fairly, once, before I'm filed away with the rest of them."

* * *

I walked to the window because I couldn't look at him anymore. The yew walk lay dark and wet under a sky the color of slate, and beyond it the parish tower of St. Anselm's rose over the trees, holding its centuries of tithes, its careful columns, its names paired with sums. Six generations of a family rendering unto God a tenth of everything, and keeping the other nine-tenths in a book.

"You could die," I said to the glass. "Do you understand that? This isn't a parlor game with a guaranteed outcome. Someone waxed the stairwell. Someone loosened a banister bracket. Someone has been coaching Marlowe with letters, testing him, using him. Whatever you think you're examining, there are variables you didn't enter into the book. Real ones. People who aren't characters in your penance."

"I'm eighty-one," Aldous said behind me. "I have a heart that keeps its own irregular ledger. I could die on Thursday and embarrass everyone." A pause. "But you're right. That's why I sent for you."

I turned. He had lifted the chessboard's lid and taken something from beneath it — a folded page, old paper gone soft at the creases. He held it out.

"What's this?"

"The letter she wrote to apply for the position. The original. You'll want it for your comparison — you've been comparing hands, I assume, you're thorough. Mine, hers, the ledger." His eyes glinted. "And because I want one honest witness in this house, Miss Zhou. Not a detective. A witness. Someone who'll write down what actually happened here, someday, when the account closes — however it closes. Even if the entry reads: *the old man got what he asked for.*"

I took the letter. The paper trembled slightly in my hand, and I hated that he could see it.

"You're courting death," I said. "You know that. You've dressed it up as justice and arithmetic, but you're an old man inviting his own murder and calling it an audit. That's not settling an account. That's suicide with extra steps, and I won't help you launder it."

For a moment — just a moment — Aldous Voss looked genuinely happy. It was the worst thing I'd seen in this whole case, worse than the waxed stairs, worse than the ledger's patient ink. Because it confirmed what I'd been refusing to know since I walked in: the victim wanted the verdict.

"There she is," he said softly. "There's the voice from the podcast. Good. Hold on to that. You'll need it in the next nine days." He settled back into the wingback, closed his eyes as if the interview had cost him a week's strength, and said, almost idly:

"One more thing, since you're my witness. Ask Ruth about the night her mother died. Ask her what she was doing on the fourth of March, two years ago — and ask her why the agency file lists her previous employment ending in February." His eyes stayed closed. "I've wondered about that gap for two years. I decided it wasn't my business. I find, lately, that I'd like it to be someone's."

* * *

Mrs. Pell was waiting in the corridor with my coat, and her face, when she helped me into it, was the face of a woman who had heard everything through oak doors for forty years and would repeat none of it until it profited someone.

"He told you," she said. Not a question.

"He told me enough."

"Mm." She smoothed my collar with two brisk tugs, an oddly maternal gesture from a woman who had only ever warned me. Then, low, without meeting my eyes: "Whatever he's told you, miss, remember he's had sixty years of practice arranging the truth to suit the entry. He told you he learned of the daughter two years ago." Her hands stilled. "Ask him sometime why Enid Calloway's letters stopped in 1986. Ask him who answered the last one."

She stepped back, composed again, the servant's mask restored.

"The fourth of March," I said slowly. "Two years ago. You know the date."

"Everyone in this house knows the date," Mrs. Pell said. "It's the day Miss Ruth arrived." And she opened the front door onto the cold, and the rain finally began.
