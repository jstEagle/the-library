## 3. Detective Okafor

The file on Paloma Kavanagh was thicker than most murder suspects', and Adaeze Okafor hadn't written a word of it.

She'd read it twice — once on the drive over, once in the car park with the engine off and the rain ticking on the roof. Not a suspect file, technically. A folder. A manila folder with a coffee ring on the cover that she suspected had been there since before she transferred to the district, containing three incident reports, two inquest summaries, and a page of handwritten notes from a DI who had retired to Spain and could no longer be asked embarrassing questions.

Three deaths. All ruled accidental. All closed.

All three had been reopened, informally and unofficially, because of things Paloma Kavanagh noticed.

The first was a fall from the bell tower scaffolding, seven years ago. Ruled misadventure. Kavanagh had sent the coroner a letter — a *letter*, typed, footnoted — pointing out that the deceased's harness had been cut with a blade drawn left-to-right, and that the man had been right-handed, and that a right-handed man cutting his own harness in panic would have drawn it the other way. The letter was filed. The verdict stood. But the constable who'd attended the scene told anyone who'd listen, for years afterward, that Kavanagh had been right.

The second was a drowning in the canal, four years ago. Ruled accidental. Kavanagh had noticed that the deceased's watch had stopped at a time inconsistent with the tide tables, and had said so, in writing, to a police force that had not asked them to. The verdict stood.

The third was an elderly verger's fall down the vestry steps. Kavanagh had asked, in a letter the coroner described in his response as "not unhelpful," why a man with a documented fear of heights had been cleaning the high windows at all, when the rota said the glazier was scheduled to do it.

The verdict stood. Three for three. Kavanagh was nothing if not consistent: right, ignored, and still showing up to the cathedral every morning to dust the ledgers.

Okafor had transferred in eighteen months ago and been warned about them in her first week — not by name, at first. "The archivist," her sergeant had said, in the tone reserved for eccentrics and liabilities. "Solves things. Writes letters. Best avoided, honestly, they make paperwork."

She had avoided them. She had filed that away in the part of her brain reserved for people who were more interesting than they were convenient. And now a drowned man was in the morgue wearing the archivist's coat, and the folder was on her desk, and she had to decide what Paloma Kavanagh was: a killer with a strange hobby, or a person the institutions kept failing in the same way Okafor herself had been failed, once, by a different institution, in a different country, in a case she did not let herself think about before ten a.m.

She locked the car and went in through the rain.

* * *

The interview room at Millbrook Row was the small one, the one with the radiator that clanked and the window painted over with frosted film so the suspect couldn't see the car park. Okafor had asked for it deliberately. Small rooms told her things. Big rooms let people perform.

Paloma Kavanagh sat with their hands folded on the table, and the first thing Okafor noticed — the thing she would remember later, when everything else about the case had blurred — was that they had dressed *carefully*. Not well. Carefully. A plain grey jumper, sleeves pushed to the forearm, nothing that could be read as wealth or carelessness. Clothes chosen by someone who understood that every detail of them would now be read as evidence.

They'd already given a formal statement. Voluntarily, the night of the body, before anyone had asked. That was in the file too: Kavanagh had walked into the station at eleven p.m., wet to the knees, and asked to make a statement before they were even officially a person of interest. Most people ran. This one had come *in*.

"Ms. Kavanagh." Okafor sat, put the recorder on the table between them, and did not turn it on. Not yet. "Or is it Mx.? I don't want to get it wrong."

"Either. Paloma is fine." The voice was quiet and even. Tired, but not the tiredness of guilt. Okafor had sat across from enough guilty people to know the specific quality of their exhaustion — the performance of it. This was different. This was the tiredness of someone who hadn't slept because they'd been up all night doing what Okafor did, except alone, without a warrant, without a team, and without anyone to tell them to go home.

"Paloma, then." She laid the folder on the table but kept her hand on it. "I'm going to be honest with you, because I think you'll check everything I say anyway, and I'd rather you checked it against the truth."

Something flickered at the corner of their mouth. Not a smile. Recognition, maybe.

"The coat is yours. The scarf is yours. We've confirmed that with the cathedral staff. The victim — Tomas Reyes, forty-six, of no fixed address at the time of death, previously of this parish — went into the millrace wearing them. Your flat shows no signs of forced entry. You have no alibi for the window in question, because you live alone and were, by your own account, reading."

"Reading *The Golden Bough*," Kavanagh said. "Page 412. You're welcome to check whether the bookmark moved."

"I might." Okafor let that sit. "Here's my problem. The evidence, as it stands, points at you. Not overwhelmingly — I've convicted on worse, and I've watched better cases fall apart — but enough that if I do nothing, the CPS will eventually do something, and then you'll be a defendant instead of a witness, and everything you know about this parish will be locked behind a solicitor's advice to say nothing."

"And you'd rather I keep talking."

"I'd rather you keep *finding*," Okafor said. "Because here's what I actually think, and you can decide how much it's worth. I think a man drowned in your clothes because someone wanted us looking at you. I think whoever did it knew you, knew your flat, knew your habits well enough to take a coat and a scarf without breaking in. And I think that person is not you, because —" she tapped the folder — "you don't kill people. You *notice* them. Three times now. And you've never once been wrong."

The silence that followed had a texture. Okafor had learned to listen to silences the way other detectives listened to answers.

"You've read the letters," Kavanagh said at last. "To the coroner."

"I've read everything."

"Then you know the force's institutional opinion of me."

"I know the institutional opinion of anyone who tells the institution it's wrong." Okafor leaned back. The radiator clanked, twice, like something clearing its throat. "I grew up trusting institutions. Church, school, police — the whole liturgy. It took me a long time to learn that institutions don't fail by accident. They fail because someone inside them decides that the institution matters more than the truth. And when that happens, the person who notices becomes the problem. You've been the problem in this parish for seven years, Paloma. I'd like to make you something else."

"Which is?"

"A consultant. Unofficial. Unpaid. Unacknowledged, if it goes wrong." She said it plainly, because she'd learned that the only currency people like Kavanagh trusted was plainness. "You help me. You stay free. You don't write any more letters to anyone without showing me first. And when you find something — and you will find something, because a man is dead in your coat and that didn't happen for no reason — you bring it to me before you bring it to anyone else. Not the coroner. Not the press. Not the canon."

The last word she watched for, and she was not disappointed.

Kavanagh's hands, folded on the table, went very slightly still. Not a flinch. A *settling*, the way a house settles, something structural shifting a fraction of an inch.

"Canon Ellery has been nothing but kind to me," they said.

"I'm sure he has." Okafor turned the recorder on, then off again, just to hear the click — a small ritual of hers, a reminder that everything in this room was one switch away from being evidence. "Kind people are my favorite kind of suspect. They have practice being believed."

* * *

They walked out together, because Okafor wanted to see how Kavanagh moved through the world — whether they flinched at the desk sergeant, whether they looked at the notice board, whether they checked over their shoulder. They did none of those things. They walked like a person in a library they knew intimately, nodding at the duty officer, pausing at the notice board only long enough to read the community safety poster with what Okafor could have sworn was professional disapproval of its typography.

Outside, the rain had thinned to a grey drift. Kavanagh stood at the top of the steps and looked out at the car park as if it were a landscape.

"Three cases," they said. "You said you read everything. Then you know the verdicts all stood."

"I know."

"Then you know that being right changed nothing. Three times." They turned to look at her, and Okafor saw, clearly, the thing underneath the quiet competence: a person who had spent years being the only one awake in a sleeping house. "Why should this time be different?"

"Because this time it's not you writing letters into a void," Okafor said. "It's two of us. And because whoever put that coat on Tomas Reyes made a mistake. They thought you were alone. They thought no one would stand next to you." She pulled her collar up. "I'm standing next to you. Don't make me regret it."

Kavanagh studied her for a long moment, and Okafor recognized the calculation happening behind their eyes because she'd done the same calculation herself, more times than she could count: *how much of myself can I afford to hand this person, and what will they do with it?*

"You said don't go to the canon," Kavanagh said. "Why him specifically? I mentioned him. You picked him out."

"Because you went still," Okafor said. "And because in thirty years of parish records, every alibi that's ever been airtight in this town has been airtight for a reason. I don't know what the reason is yet. But airtight alibis are load-bearing walls, Paloma. You don't get them by accident. Someone builds them."

The rain drifted between them. Down the hill, the cathedral towers stood against the low cloud, grey stone gone darker where the water ran, and Okafor watched Kavanagh look at them — not with the affection she'd have expected from a lifelong archivist, but with the wariness of someone looking at a large animal that has so far declined to bite.

"I'll need access," Kavanagh said. "The 1990s files. Anything on the crypt sealing. And the inquest on the girl — the one from '94. The one nobody talks about."

Okafor felt the case tilt under her, the way cases did when they stopped being about the present and started reaching backward. "Why '94?"

"Because Tomas Reyes was a stonemason's apprentice in 1994," Kavanagh said. "And because whoever drowned him in my coat wanted the past to stay where we buried it. And I've spent my whole life in an archive, Detective. I know what burial looks like. It looks exactly like a locked door that nobody holds a key to."

They went down the steps together, two people who trusted institutions exactly as far as they could each throw one, and Okafor found — to her considerable professional annoyance — that she already believed them.

She just didn't yet know what about.

The answer was waiting in a ledger, in a gap of three weeks in the spring of 1994, and neither of them knew it yet. But the cathedral did.
