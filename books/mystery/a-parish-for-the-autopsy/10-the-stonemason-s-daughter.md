## 10. The Stonemason's Daughter

She arrived on a Tuesday, when the cathedral was emptiest and the light through the west windows fell in long grey bars across the nave floor, and Paloma Kavanagh would later say — to Okafor, to the court, to anyone who asked — that they knew before she spoke a word whose sister she was.

Marisol Reyes did not weep. She did not wring her hands or lower her voice the way mourners did inside St. Aldhelm's, as though grief required a hush. She walked up the centre aisle in a red coat with her chin level and her eyes moving over everything — the pillars, the clerestory, the iron door half-hidden behind the choir screen — like a woman taking inventory of a building she intended to hold accountable.

Paloma was at the archive desk, cataloguing a box of 1970s flower-fund receipts, when the doors opened. They looked up out of habit. Then they stood, because something in the woman's stride said she was not here to pray.

"You're the archivist." Not a question. She had an accent that had travelled — Spanish underneath, softened by years somewhere else, somewhere flat and hot where consonants wore down. "Kavanagh. The one who found my brother."

"I identified the coat," Paloma said carefully. "The police found him."

"The police found him." Marisol reached the desk and set both hands flat on it, and Paloma saw that her knuckles were scarred, the hands of someone who worked stone or plaster or something honest. "And you're the one the police think killed him. That's what the paper in Valencia said. *Local archivist questioned in millrace death.*" Her mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "I flew four thousand miles to meet either a murderer or a scapegoat. I'm hoping for the second one, because I have questions a murderer wouldn't answer."

"Ask them anyway," Paloma said. "I'll answer what I can."

"Tomas Reyes. Thirty-one years old. He left this town when he was nineteen and never once came back, not for Christmas, not for our mother's funeral, nothing. And then eight weeks ago he tells me he's going home. He says it like it's a debt he's finally paying. Two days after he arrives, he's dead in your millrace wearing somebody else's coat." Her voice stayed level but Paloma could hear the effort of it, the way you hear the strain in a rope. "So. Whose coat?"

"Mine. It was stolen from my flat along with a scarf. I never met your brother. I don't know why he took my clothes or why someone put them on him."

"But you've been asking questions anyway. Since before he died." Marisol's eyes narrowed. "The paper mentioned that too. Accidents you didn't believe were accidents. Three of them."

Paloma felt the old reflex, the one that kept their private habit private, rise in their throat. They let it go. The woman had crossed an ocean; she deserved more than discretion.

"I keep the records of this place," Paloma said. "Thirty years of burials, restorations, repairs. When people die here in ways that don't add up, I notice. It's a bad habit."

"It's the only reason I'm talking to you instead of a solicitor." Marisol pulled a chair out without asking and sat, and now that she was still, Paloma saw the exhaustion under the armour — the skin grey at the jaw, the eyes rimmed red but dry, dry the way eyes get when they've run out of water and kept working anyway. "Here's what the paper didn't say. Tomas didn't come back because of a debt. He came back because of letters."

Paloma went very still.

"He got the first one in March. Typed, no signature, postmarked here. He showed me a photograph of it because he thought I'd think he was losing his mind. It said—" she closed her eyes, retrieving it exactly, and Paloma understood she had read it so many times it lived behind her eyelids— "*You were a witness in 1994. You know what is behind the wall. Come back and confess what you saw, or I will tell the town myself.*"

The archive room was quiet enough that Paloma could hear the boiler ticking somewhere below the floor, the cathedral's slow heartbeat.

"There were more," Marisol said. "Four, five — he stopped counting, or stopped showing me. Each one a little more specific. Dates. Names. The last one named the crypt itself. *St. Aldhelm's keeps its dead badly.*" She opened her bag and took out a photograph of a letter, held it out. "He photographed everything. He was a stonemason's son; he trusted stone and paper, not people. He said if anything happened to him, I should bring these to whoever kept the records. Because records don't lie, he said. People lie. Records just sit there being true."

Paloma took the phone with careful fingers and read the words twice. *St. Aldhelm's keeps its dead badly.*

"Did he tell you what he saw?" they asked. "In 1994."

"No." Marisol took the phone back. "That's the thing that's been eating me since Valencia. He wouldn't say. He'd go quiet — this specific quiet, like a door closing — and say, *when I'm back, I'll show you. You'll want to see it yourself.*" Her jaw tightened. "He was coming back to open something. And two days after he landed, he was in the water."

"And the letters stopped?"

"They stopped the day he died." Marisol leaned forward. "Think about that. Whoever wrote them knew he was dead before the police released his name. Or—" and here her voice dropped, not with fear but with contempt, which Paloma found far more frightening— "or there was only ever one letter-writer, and one killer, and they kept office hours together."

* * *

They talked for two hours. Paloma brought out the restoration ledgers, the boxes marked 1994, the thin file the coroner's office had deigned to leave with the cathedral archives: a girl, fourteen, Elodie Marsh, found dead in the crypt stairwell in October of that year, verdict misadventure, crypt sealed by order of the chapter the following spring. Marisol read every page standing up, pacing, as though sitting still with this material would be a kind of surrender.

"A girl died down there," she said finally, "and my brother repaired the wall around her, and thirty years later someone starts writing letters, and everyone who might remember anything has died in convenient little accidents." She looked up. "Your three. Before Tomas. Who were they?"

Paloma told her. The verger, Harold Finch, 2019, the fall on the tower stairs. Mrs. Edith Swan, the former sacristan, 2021, the house fire ruled electrical. And last year, Denis Colm, the retired organist, the river, the verdict accidental drowning despite the fact that Denis had swum the Channel in his fifties and feared nothing on earth except moths.

Marisol listened with her arms folded, and when Paloma finished she said, quietly, "You noticed all of them. Years apart. Alone, in this room, noticing." Something moved across her face that wasn't suspicion — it was recognition, and it seemed to cost her. "My brother was the same. Everyone thought he was strange. He just paid attention."

* * *

It was near dusk when Marisol stopped at the archive door, one hand on the frame, and asked the question Paloma had been waiting for since morning.

"Why are you helping me? They think you killed him. Every hour you spend with me makes that worse for you."

Paloma considered lying, or at least decorating the truth. They had learned, in thirty-eight years of keeping other people's secrets, how to make truth presentable. But Marisol Reyes had flown across an ocean on the strength of photographs of letters, and she would smell decoration.

"Because someone stole my coat and put it on a drowned man," Paloma said. "Which means someone wanted me to carry this. Either as a suspect or as an investigator — I don't know which yet. But either way, it's mine now. Your brother was wearing my life when he died. The least I can do is find out why."

Marisol studied them for a long moment. Then she nodded, once, the way masons nod at sound stone.

"My mother used to say the Reyeses and this town had unfinished business," she said. "I thought she meant money." She pulled her coat tighter. "Tomorrow I'm going to the police. There's a detective — Okafor. The paper said she doesn't trust the cathedral. Good. Neither do I anymore."

When she had gone, Paloma sat alone among the ledgers as the light failed, and turned the phrase over and over until it lost its shape and became only a cold weight in the chest.

*St. Aldhelm's keeps its dead badly.*

Someone had written that sentence knowing it would drive a man four thousand miles home. Someone had known exactly what Tomas Reyes saw in 1994 — and had spent eight weeks sharpening that knowledge into a blade, and then, when he arrived, had used it.

And the letters had stopped the day he died, which meant the writer had not wanted him dead at all, not at first. They had wanted him back. Wanted him close. Wanted him—

Paloma's pen stopped mid-note.

Wanted him where the letters could be found on his body. Wanted the town's returning son discovered with proof in his luggage that he'd come home to blackmail the cathedral about the sealed crypt. Wanted the story already written before the body was cold: a man comes back after thirty years to squeeze money out of an old tragedy, meets the archivist with a reputation for digging, and drowns.

The letters hadn't been threats. They had been stage directions.

Paloma looked up, through the archive doorway, into the darkening nave. Somewhere above, in the tower, a door closed — soft, deliberate, the sound of someone who knew the building's acoustics well enough to place a noise exactly where they wanted it heard.

Paloma wrote one line in the notebook, underlined it twice, and closed it.

*Whoever wrote the letters knew what Tomas saw. Which means the letters weren't from an enemy of the crypt. They were from its keeper.*
