## 11. Evensong Voices

The witness's words followed Okafor home and would not leave.

*A man whose face I never saw. Only his voice at evensong.*

She'd written it down in the car outside the hospice, on the back of a parking receipt, because some part of her brain had already understood what it meant before the rest of her was willing to say it out loud. A voice at evensong. In a cathedral. Thirty years ago. That wasn't a description. That was a list — short, finite, checkable. And Adaeze Okafor had spent twenty-two years learning that the cases that broke open were never the ones with new evidence. They were the ones where somebody finally thought to count.

She counted in the bath that night, steam fogging the window of her flat above the chippy on Cordwell Street. Clergy who sang evensong at St. Aldhelm's in 1994. Not the choir — the choir was boys and men who rotated weekly, dozens of them, impossible to pin down. She meant the officiants. The men whose voices carried alone through the quire when the building emptied: the precentor, the succentor, the canon pastor. The ones who stood in the dark and spoke into it.

Three names. She wrote them on the fogged glass with one finger before she could talk herself out of it.

By morning two of them were dead.

* * *

The cathedral sound archive lived in a room Paloma Kavanagh had shown her once, back in October, during the first uneasy week of their arrangement. Okafor remembered it because Paloma had talked about it the way other people talked about a lover — the temperature-controlled racks, the reel-to-reel machines from the sixties still working, the digitisation project that had consumed four years of evenings. Services recorded since 1958. Every evensong. Every ordination, every funeral, every bishop's visitation, logged and boxed and shelved by date.

"Nobody listens to them," Paloma had said, sliding a box back into its slot with something like tenderness. "That's the point of an archive. It keeps things so nobody has to look."

Okafor hadn't understood then why anyone would keep thirty years of services nobody listened to. She understood now. An archive was an alibi machine. It was also, if you were patient, a confession booth that didn't know it was confessing.

She arrived at eight-fifteen on Tuesday morning, before the cathedral opened to visitors, and found Paloma already there, sleeves rolled, hair tied back with what appeared to be a pencil, standing over a spread of ledger pages like a general over maps.

"You called me at six a.m.," Okafor said.

"I called you at six a.m. because I've been here since five." Paloma didn't look up. "I pulled the service registers for 1994. Do you want the good news or the arithmetic first?"

"Arithmetic."

"Between March and November of 1994 — the window around Edith Marsh's death — there were three clergy regularly officiating at evensong." Paloma laid three index cards on the table, each with a name in careful archival hand. "Precentor Gerald Faussett. Succentor Martin Lowe. And Canon Ambrose Ellery."

Okafor looked at the cards for a long moment. "Faussett died in 2009," she said. "Fell from the tower scaffolding. I remember the file — it came across my desk as a probationer. Ruled accidental. He was seventy-one and everyone said he shouldn't have been up there."

"And Lowe," Paloma said quietly, "drowned in the millrace in 2016. Same stretch of water as Tomas Reyes. Ruled accidental. Heart failure, they said, though he was sixty-four and swam every morning."

The room was very cold and very quiet. Somewhere above them the great clock ticked through its works, patient as sediment.

"So the dying witness hears a voice at evensong," Okafor said slowly. "One of three men. Two of the three are dead of accidents. Which leaves—"

"Don't." Paloma's voice cracked like a whip, and then, immediately, softened into something that sounded almost like apology. "Don't say it yet. Say it in a room with a recording running. Please. I need—" They stopped, pressed the heel of their hand against their eyes. "I've known him since I was fifteen years old, Detective. If we're going to do this, we're going to do it properly. No shortcuts. Because if I'm wrong about him I will have destroyed the only family I ever had, and if I'm right—" They lowered the hand. Their eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. "If I'm right, then everything he ever did for me was a payment plan. And I want to be certain before I collect."

Okafor had interviewed hundreds of witnesses and she knew evasion when she heard it, and this wasn't evasion. This was grief doing arithmetic of its own. She nodded once.

"The recordings," she said. "Where do we start?"

* * *

They started with the reels from the spring of 1994, and it was slow, maddening, meticulous work. The old tape hissed like rain on a tin roof; the organ swelled and buried everything; congregations coughed and shuffled and dropped hymn books. But evensong had a structure, and structure was a gift. Responses, canticles, the office hymn, the anthem, the prayers, the blessing. And threaded through all of it, the officiant's voice — announcing, intoning, reading, praying — alone at the front of a darkening church while the choir rested.

Paloma worked the machines. Okafor worked the notebook. By noon they had a method: log each service, timestamp each spoken passage, note which voice. By two they had a problem.

"They're not distinguishable," Okafor said. "Not to me. Three middle-aged Englishmen intoning the collects. It's like telling apart three different rains."

"To you, maybe." Paloma was feeding a fresh reel onto the machine with hands that moved like they'd done it ten thousand times, which they had. "But listen again. Faussett rushes the ends of lines — hear how he clips 'world without end'? He wants to get home. Lowe drags — he holds the final syllable like he's afraid of silence. And Ellery—" Paloma stopped the tape. Rewound eight seconds. Played it again.

A voice, warm and unhurried, saying: *Lighten our darkness, we beseech thee, O Lord.*

"He breathes between the clauses," Paloma said. "Even when the sentence doesn't want him to. He taught me that. He said a pause is a door you hold open for someone." Their jaw tightened. "He taught me everything about listening. So yes. I can tell them apart."

Okafor watched them and thought: *this is either the best witness I've ever had or the most compromised.* Both, probably. Both at once. She'd built a career on the suspicion that institutions protected themselves before people, and here was the living proof, sitting in the institution's own basement, using the institution's own memory to indict its favourite son.

"Play me everything Ellery says," she said. "Every service. March to November. We build a baseline first. Then we take the witness's account seriously — she heard a voice at evensong, near the crypt, on a night she can't quite date. We find nights Ellery's voice is on tape and cross-reference against the trial timeline."

It took them three days.

* * *

On the second day, Okafor went back to the old files, because tapes told you who spoke but files told you who died, and she wanted the deaths laid out in order, dates aligned, the way a pattern only shows itself when you stop looking at any single piece.

She spread them across her desk at the station, ignoring the raised eyebrow of the duty sergeant, and made herself look at all four at once.

Edith Marsh, fourteen years old, found dead at the foot of the crypt stairs, March 1994. Verdict: accidental fall. The crypt sealed within the month. Trial of Tomas Reyes for trespass and tampering — acquitted, but ruined anyway, left town, stayed gone thirty years until he came back and drowned in the millrace wearing a dead archivist's coat.

Gerald Faussett, precentor, fell from tower scaffolding, June 2009. Accidental.

Martin Lowe, succentor, drowned in the millrace, September 2016. Accidental.

Tomas Reyes, stonemason, drowned in the millrace, last month. Under investigation.

Four deaths. Thirty years. Two stretches of water, one flight of stairs, one scaffold. And threading through all of it, unverifiable, unremarkable, never once questioned: the alibis. She'd read them all now, twice. Faussett's death — Ellery was leading a retreat in the Chilterns, alone, walking. Lowe's — Ellery was at a diocesan conference, his attendance noted at dinner, unaccounted for overnight. Reyes — Ellery had been at the cathedral until late, alone, preparing a sermon no one else ever saw. Even in 1994, the night Edith Marsh died, half the parish swore Ellery was at the hospital visiting a sick parishioner — a parishioner whose name appeared in no record Okafor could find, whose discharge papers had vanished from the hospital archive in what the records office called an administrative purge.

Alone. Always alone. Unverifiable, always. A man built his life so that no one could ever say where he was, and everyone had learned to read that as humility.

Okafor sat back and felt the horror arrive not as a shock but as a slow cold tide, the way she imagined drowning must feel — no single terrible moment, just the gradual understanding that the water was over your head and had been for some time.

Because here was the thing she couldn't stop seeing now that she saw it: the accidents were spaced. 1994. 2009. 2016. Last month. Seven years, fifteen years, eight years. Nobody lives long enough to notice a pattern spaced like that. No detective's career spans it. No journalist remembers. The gaps weren't carelessness — they were design. Whoever this was killed the way a gardener prunes: rarely, precisely, always just enough, and always with seasons in between.

And the pruning had a logic. Faussett ran the music — he'd have known every voice, every rota, every irregularity in who sang when. Lowe was the succentor — deputy, the man who covered services, who'd have known exactly what Ellery did and didn't attend. And both of them, she realised with a fresh chill, would have been able to answer the same question Paloma had answered in an afternoon: *whose voice was that?*

The witness was dying anyway, so killing her bought time rather than silence. But Faussett and Lowe had been killed for what they knew without knowing they knew it. And Tomas Reyes —

Reyes had come back. After thirty years. Something had brought him back, and whatever it was, it had signed his death warrant the day he stepped off the train.

Her phone buzzed. Paloma.

*Found something in the 1994 tapes. Come now. Bring the files.*

* * *

The archive room at midnight smelled of dust and cold metal and, faintly, of incense drifting down from the cathedral above, where even now some curate was locking up after compline. Paloma had three reels cued and a laptop open showing a waveform, and their face in the lamplight was the face of someone who has opened a door they cannot close.

"Tell me what you've got first," Okafor said, dropping the case files on the table. "Then tell me what kept."

"The files can wait thirty seconds." Paloma's hands were not quite steady. "Listen."

They played a clip. Hiss, organ tail-off, and then a voice: *Let us pray.* Warm. Unhurried. Breathing between clauses.

"October the ninth, 1994," Paloma said. "Evensong. Ellery officiating. Now — the trial transcript puts Edith Marsh's death on the eleventh. But the coroner's report—" they slid a photocopied page across "—puts body discovery on the twelfth, and estimates death at least forty-eight hours prior. Which means she likely died on the ninth. Or the tenth. The trial assumed the eleventh because that's when the search started, and nobody ever checked the tapes."

Okafor stared at the waveform. "You're telling me the entire case turned on a date nobody verified."

"I'm telling you the date was wrong, and the wrong date gave everyone an alibi, including—" Paloma's voice caught, steadied. "Including the man who taught me to check dates. Who drilled it into me. *The archive never lies, Paloma, but people quote it lazily.*" They laughed, a small broken sound with nothing in it. "He quoted it lazily. On purpose. Or he let everyone else be lazy, and stood in the gap."

"There's more. You said *kept*."

Paloma turned the laptop toward her. On the screen, a service register entry, photographed from the 1994 volume. October the ninth. Officiant: E. Ellery. And beneath it, in the column for notes, a line in Ellery's own young handwriting, neat as engraving:

*Full quire absent (flu). Said evensong, crypt side chapel, congregation nil. Locked up 7:40pm.*

"Said evensong," Okafor read aloud. "Congregation nil. He was alone in the building with a fourteen-year-old girl who died that night, and he wrote it down himself, and for thirty years that entry sat in a ledger nobody read because—" 

"Because nobody reads archives," Paloma finished. "He used to say the safest place to hide anything was in plain record. I thought it was a joke about bureaucracy." They closed the laptop, gently, the way you close a coffin. "Detective. There are three men who sang evensong that year. Two are dead of accidents. The third one raised me."

The clock above them ticked. Upstairs, a door boomed shut — the last curate leaving, the cathedral settling into its nightly dark.

Okafor looked at the photocopied register entry, at the case files stacked like strata, at the young archivist who had just, with their own hands, dug the grave of the only father they'd ever had. She thought about the millrace, and the coat, and the photograph missing from a flat across town, and the dying woman in the hospice whose interview was scheduled for Friday morning.

"We need to move," she said. "Before he does. Friday, nine a.m., formal interview with the witness. Uniformed protection from tomorrow night. And Paloma—" she hesitated, then said it plainly, because the moment deserved plainness, "—from tonight, you don't stay in your flat. You don't walk anywhere alone. If I'm right about the spacing, about the patience, then the next accident is already being planned, and everything I know about this man says it'll be dressed as grief. Yours."

Paloma nodded slowly. Then, gathering the reels into their arms like something rescued from a fire, they said the thing Okafor would carry with her into every room that followed:

"He locked up at seven-forty. The girl died after that, or he'd never have written the time down. Which means either he lied in the one record he trusted—" Paloma met her eyes. "—or someone else was in the crypt. Someone he was hiding. Detective, I don't think we've been digging toward a murder. I think we've been digging toward whatever he's been guarding down there for thirty years. And I think Tomas Reyes saw it. And I think that's why he's dead."

Outside, somewhere over the city, the first grey suggestion of dawn was still hours away, and the iron door at the bottom of the stair waited with its lock full of thirty years of silence.
