## 12. Accident Number Four

Marisol Reyes had learned to wait in hospital corridors the way other people learned to pray. You didn't choose it. It chose you, and then it never let go.

She'd done it for her mother, three years of Tuesdays and Thursdays in a renal ward in Zaragoza. She'd done it for Tomas — not at the end, when there was nothing left to wait for, but through all the years before that, the years of phone calls from strange cities and long silences that meant her brother was either working or hiding, and she could never tell which. Waiting was a skill. It had a technique. You kept your hands busy. You counted things: ceiling tiles, footsteps, the seconds between one door opening and another.

So she sat in the corridor of St. Aldhelm's parish office with her hands folded around a paper cup of coffee gone cold, counting the seconds since Paloma Kavanagh had gone inside to take a phone call, and she got to four hundred and eleven before the archivist came back out looking like someone had reached into their chest and rearranged something.

"What is it?" Marisol said, standing. "What happened?"

Paloma's mouth opened. Closed. They looked, for a moment, like a person reading a document they couldn't believe was authentic.

"That was Detective Okafor," they said. "Edith Marsh is dead."

The name took a second to land. Edith Marsh — the witness. The woman who had recanted, who had told Okafor she'd lied at the trial thirty years ago, who had agreed to give a formal statement. The dying woman. Marisol had never met her; she'd only heard about her secondhand, in Okafor's clipped summaries and Paloma's careful notes. A voice on a tape recorder. A guilt finally surfacing after three decades underwater.

"Dead how?"

"A fall." Paloma said the word as if tasting it. "Down her own stairs. Last night."

"Last night." Marisol set down the cup because her hands had started to shake and she didn't want to see them shaking. "Her interview was this morning."

"Ten o'clock," Paloma said. "She was found by her home carer at seven-forty."

Marisol did the arithmetic without wanting to. Hours. The woman had been dead for hours while Marisol slept in a guest room at the cathedral close, while Paloma catalogued documents two floors above the crypt, while somewhere in the city a man — or men, or whoever it was — had known exactly what was scheduled to happen at ten o'clock and made sure it never would.

"They knew," she said. "Whoever killed my brother knew she was going to talk, and they—"

"We don't know that it wasn't an accident." Paloma said it flatly, mechanically, the way you recite something you've been told to recite. "She was eighty-one. She had terminal cancer. Falls are—" They stopped. Pressed their lips together. "I'm sorry. I'm saying what the police will say. What they're already saying."

"And what do *you* say?"

Paloma looked at her, and Marisol saw something in their face she hadn't seen before — not the controlled, competent stillness she'd come to expect from the archivist, but something rawer underneath it. Fury, maybe. Or fear wearing fury's coat.

"I say," Paloma said slowly, "that Edith Marsh lived alone, that her stairs were carpeted, that she'd navigated them twice a day for forty years, and that she died twelve hours before giving testimony that would have blown open a thirty-year-old case. And I say that if this is an accident, it is the fourth accident in this parish that has arrived precisely when someone was about to tell the truth."

* * *

They drove to Edith Marsh's house themselves, because Okafor called back an hour later and said, in a voice stripped down to wire, "Don't go near that house," which meant, in the language they had all begun to speak, *go now, before the tape goes up.*

It was a narrow brick terrace on the far side of the millrace, the same water that had taken Tomas, and Marisol stood on the pavement outside it and felt the river's presence like a hand between her shoulder blades. There was already a constable at the door, young, apologetic, holding a clipboard like a shield. Okafor arrived minutes later in an unmarked car, parked badly, and came up the path with her jaw set.

"You shouldn't be here," she said to both of them.

"No," Marisol agreed. "But we are."

Okafor looked at her for a long moment. Something passed over the detective's face — not softness exactly, but recognition. Two people standing outside a door that had closed on the last living witness.

"She fell from the top landing," Okafor said quietly. "Backward. No signs of disturbance downstairs. Her medication was laid out on the kitchen table, half taken. Nothing missing. On its face, it's exactly what it looks like."

"On its face," Marisol repeated.

"On its face." Okafor rubbed her eyes under her glasses. "Except that she called the station yesterday afternoon to confirm the interview. Except that she told the desk sergeant she was 'finally sleeping well' for the first time in weeks. Except that her oncologist says she was frail but steady, used a rail, hadn't fallen once in the six months he'd treated her." She lowered her voice further. "And except that when the first officers arrived, the front door was unlocked. Edith Marsh locked her door even to take out the bins. Her neighbor told me that herself, twenty minutes ago, and then asked me why I was asking, and I couldn't tell her."

The constable shifted his weight. Okafor dismissed him with a glance, and the three of them stood on the small front path while the millrace muttered behind the houses.

"There'll be a post-mortem," Okafor said. "Standard. But I've seen enough of these to know what the report will say. Elderly woman, degenerative illness, possible dizziness from medication, tragic accident. It will be signed off in a week. Nobody will look twice."

"Because nobody ever looks twice," Marisol said. "That's the whole method, isn't it? That's what happened to Tomas. Drowned man, stolen clothes, sad story about a drifter who came back to town. Case closed before the water dried."

"Tomas's case isn't closed," Okafor said. "Not officially. Not anymore."

"But it might as well be. Because whoever is doing this—" Marisol heard her own voice rise and forced it back down. "Whoever is doing this doesn't need to win. They just need us to keep losing witnesses. First the mason who built the crypt wall — dead twenty years, heart attack. Then the verger who signed the restoration invoices — dead eight years ago, fall on ice. Now the woman who lied at the trial and wanted to un-lie. One every time we get close. Do you understand what that means? It means they're not reacting to us. They're *ahead* of us. They've always been ahead of us. Thirty years ahead."

Nobody answered. Down the street, a delivery van idled, and the driver glanced at the house, at the police presence, and drove on without stopping, because that was what people did here. This parish had spent three decades learning not to stop.

* * *

Inside — Okafor let them in against every regulation she'd sworn to uphold, and Marisol suspected the detective had stopped keeping score of those debts some time ago — the house smelled of lavender and disinfectant and something faintly clinical, the smell of a life being managed rather than lived. Edith Marsh had been poor and tidy. Doilies on the arms of the chairs. A television with a lace cloth over it. Photographs on the mantelpiece in cheap frames: a wedding, children grown and gone, grandchildren she'd apparently rarely seen.

On the small table beside her armchair lay a spiral notebook.

Okafor saw it at the same moment Marisol did. Neither of them touched it. Paloma crouched, hands hovering, and read the open page upside down, lips moving slightly.

"It's her statement," they said. "Or the draft of it. She was writing it out by hand before she gave it formally. Look — dates, names. 'The man paid me fifty pounds. He came to the vestry door after evensong. I never saw his face but I knew the voice, everyone knew the voice, you'd know it from the back row of the cathedral.'" Paloma went very still. "There's more. She wrote about the trial. About being coached. 'They told me what I'd seen. I was frightened and the money was real and my husband had just lost his job.'"

"Is there a name?" Marisol asked. "Does she write a name anywhere?"

Paloma turned pages carefully, not touching the ink, using a pencil from their own pocket to lift corners. Marisol watched them work and understood, suddenly, why the police had needed this person all along — not for the archive access, not really. For this. For hands that knew how to treat a dead woman's notebook like scripture.

"No name," Paloma said at last. "She writes around it. Every time she gets to the identity, the sentence breaks off. Here — 'I can't write it down, it wouldn't be safe, but I'll say it tomorrow, I'll say it out loud where it counts.'" They closed the notebook gently. "She was protecting herself until the interview. She thought the interview itself would be the protection. Once it was official, on record, she'd be safe."

"And instead," Marisol said, "she died in the gap between deciding to speak and speaking."

Okafor took the notebook in an evidence bag, though Marisol noticed her hands weren't quite steady doing it. "This changes things," the detective said. "This is a document indicating she intended to identify someone. Combined with the timeline — it gives me grounds to reopen everything. All four deaths. I can request the old files, the post-mortems, the—"

"You can request," Marisol said. "Yes. Request away. And they'll grant it, and the files will arrive, and the post-mortems will say what they always said, because that's the point. That's the whole beautiful machine of it." She heard the bitterness in her voice and didn't try to sand it off. "My brother waited thirty years for someone to look at 1994 again. He came back here because letters were sent to him — threats, promises, whatever they were — and within weeks he was floating in the millrace wearing a dead archivist's coat. And now the one person who could put a face to the man at the vestry door is at the bottom of her own staircase, and the report will call it an accident, and we will stand here in her sitting room holding her handwriting, and it won't matter. It won't matter at all."

"It matters," Paloma said quietly.

Marisol turned on them. "How? Tell me how. Show me the mechanism by which any of this matters, when every person who could testify ends up dead before they can open their mouth. We are excavating a grave while someone shovels it full faster than we dig. Tell me what your archives and your ledgers and your—your *method* actually buy us, except a clearer view of losing."

Paloma didn't flinch. That was the thing about them, Marisol was learning. Everyone else in this parish flinched — from the subject, from the past, from her grief, from each other. Paloma Kavanagh absorbed a blow the way stone absorbs weather, and then, days later, you'd notice the inscription they'd carved into it.

"Because the dead leave records," Paloma said. "Edith Marsh left a notebook. Tomas left letters. The verger left invoices. The mason left mortar lines in a wall that's still standing behind an iron door. Every person they kill becomes a document, and documents don't fall down stairs." They held up the evidence bag, the spiral notebook ghost-pale inside it. "She knew she might not survive to speak. Why else write it all out? Why else date every page? She was building a record. She was doing what I do. She was archiving herself."

The words settled into the little room, into the doilies and the lace-covered television, into the silence of a woman who had carried a lie for thirty years and died trying to set it down.

* * *

They walked back along the millrace in the late afternoon, the three of them, an unlikely procession — the detective, the archivist, the stonemason's daughter — and the water ran brown-green and indifferent beside the path. Marisol kept thinking about the sound of it. Tomas had grown up on a river like this one, in a town whose name he'd stopped saying after he left. Water everywhere in this story. Water taking people and giving back so little.

At the footbridge, Okafor stopped. "There's a briefing tomorrow," she said. "Internal. The chief super wants this wrapped as accidental death, number four in a series of coincidences he'd rather not examine. I'm going to stand up in that room and say otherwise, and I want you both to understand what that costs me, because I don't say things like that lightly." She looked at Paloma. "Your reputation precedes you. Three cases. Three 'accidents' you picked apart. If you'd been wrong even once—"

"I wasn't wrong."

"I know. I checked. That's why I'm standing in a room tomorrow and betting my career on a notebook written by an eighty-one-year-old woman with stage-four cancer." Okafor almost smiled; it didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "Go home. Both of you. Lock your doors. And Paloma —"

"Yes."

"If anything arrives at your flat — anything, a note, a parcel, a photograph — you call me before you touch it. Before you *think* about it. Promise me."

Paloma promised. Marisol watched them make the promise and watched something flicker behind the archivist's eyes, some private calculation they didn't share, and filed it away. Everyone in this parish had rooms in them that stayed shut. Even the ones digging.

She walked on alone toward the cathedral close, where a bed had been made up for her in a guest room that smelled of beeswax and old stone, and as she crossed the green the towers rose above her, gray against a bruised evening sky, and she thought: *you're in there.* Whoever you are, whatever face you wear at evensong, you are inside that building right now, lighting candles, shaking hands, singing. You watched us find the notebook. You'll know by tonight that it exists. And tomorrow you'll begin, patiently, lovingly, to make it disappear too.

Unless we get faster.

That night she couldn't sleep, and near midnight she heard footsteps in the cloister below her window — unhurried, familiar, a walk she'd already begun to recognize in her few days here. She looked down and saw Paloma crossing the moonlit grass toward the cathedral's west door, carrying no light, moving like someone who had walked that path ten thousand times and was walking it now in their sleep.

Marisol dressed, went down, followed.

She found the west door unbolted — unbolted at midnight, in a cathedral terrified of its own history — and stepped into the vast cold dark, and heard, somewhere ahead in the direction of the archive stair, the sound of Paloma Kavanagh's voice, low and urgent, talking to someone.

And the someone answering was a voice Marisol had heard every evening since she arrived, warm as candlelight, gentle as absolution, saying:

*"You should not have gone to that house, child. Come and sit down. Let me explain what you've found."*
