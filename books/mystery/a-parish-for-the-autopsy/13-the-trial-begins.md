## 13. The Trial Begins

The courtroom smelled of lemon polish and old fear.

I noticed the fear second. First I noticed that someone had polished the benches — the same dark oak that had stood in this room since before the cathedral was a tourist attraction, before it was even a cathedral, back when St. Aldhelm's was a parish church with ambitions. Someone had buffed those benches to a shine for me. For my hearing. It struck me as obscene, that care. That anyone had bothered to make the room beautiful for the day they tried to take my life apart in public.

"Kavanagh." A court officer touched my elbow. "You'll be seated at the rear until you're called."

The rear. Where the public sat. Where Marisol Reyes already sat, third row, arms crossed, watching me with an expression I couldn't file under anything. Not forgiveness. Not accusation. Something more exhausting than either: assessment. She'd come to see what I was made of, and she intended to stay until she knew.

Okafor wasn't there. She'd told me she wouldn't be — *conflict of interest, Kavanagh, don't make it worse* — but I caught sight of her anyway, standing at the back corridor door in a coat too heavy for October, pretending to read a notice about jury service. She didn't look at me. She didn't have to. Her being there at all was a sentence, and I read it fine.

Then the doors opened properly, and Canon Ambrose Ellery walked in on the arm of a solicitor, and the room changed temperature.

He wore his collar. Of course he wore his collar. Grey suit, black shirt, the white tab like a held breath. He moved slowly — he was seventy-one, and he let you see every year of it when it served him — and people shifted to let him pass the way water shifts around a stone that has always been in the river. Two parishioners from St. Aldhelm's had come, I realized. Mrs. Okonkwo from the flower rota. Old Denby, who rang bells. They were here for him. They'd come to sit behind their canon while the man who'd grown up in his archives was dissected.

Ellery did not look at me as he passed. That was the discipline of him. Thirty years ago he'd taught me that the archive rewards patience — *the document will wait for you, Paloma; it's the archivist who must learn to wait* — and now he demonstrated it in open court, eyes forward, hands folded, a man with nothing in him but prayer.

I waited too. I was good at it. He'd made sure of that.

* * *

They called the case of *R v. Kavanagh* at ten past ten, and the Crown's barrister — a compact woman named Hargreaves with reading glasses on a chain and a voice like a filing cabinet closing — began not with the body but with the coat.

"The deceased, Tomas Reyes, was recovered from the millrace below Corvey Mill on the fourteenth of June," she said. "He was wearing a wool overcoat. Navy. He was wearing a scarf, grey lambswool. Both items were later identified as belonging to the defendant."

She said it plainly, without theater, which was worse. Theater you could push against. Plainness just settled over you like dust.

"Both items," she repeated, "belonging to the defendant. Who reported them missing only after the body was found. Who could produce no record of when or how they went missing. Who lives alone, keeps no visitors book, and whose flat" — here she paused, and turned a page with terrible gentleness — "whose flat, when searched, yielded a photograph. A photograph of the defendant and one Ambrose Ellery, taken, by the defendant's own admission, some years ago, at the door of the cathedral crypt. A photograph that is now missing from that flat. Missing, members of the panel, in precisely the way the coat and scarf were missing. As though things leave the defendant's possession and end up where the defendant needs them to be."

I wrote that down. I don't know why. My hand does it — some archivist's reflex, transcribe everything, index everything, because a thing written down can be cross-referenced later and a thing only heard becomes whatever the hearer wants. So I sat in the public gallery of my own pretrial hearing and took minutes on my own dismantling, and my handwriting stayed perfectly level, and I hated myself a little for how level it was.

Hargreaves moved on to my history. Of course she did.

"You'll forgive me for taking the panel through some background," she said, "but background matters here. In 2016, the death of Mr. Harold Finch, a fall from the organ loft, ruled accidental. The defendant wrote to the coroner — twice — disputing the finding. In 2019, the drowning of a young woman in the millrace — the same millrace, note — again ruled accidental. Again the defendant wrote. In 2022, the death of Mr. Peter Loxley, carbon monoxide, ruled accidental. The defendant not only wrote to the coroner but obtained, through means we are still examining, copies of the maintenance records for Mr. Loxley's boiler."

Obtained through means we are still examining. I felt the sentence go into the room and lodge there, the way a splinter lodges — small, invisible, and impossible to ignore once named. What she meant was: this person doesn't respect walls. This person reads other people's paper. And she was right, and I had done all of it, and every word of it had felt, at the time, like the only honest thing available to me. Three deaths the town wanted to sleep through, and I'd been the one who lay awake doing arithmetic.

But laid out in a row under courtroom light, without the bodies attached, without the griefs attached, the letters didn't look like vigilance. They looked like appetite. Three accidental deaths, and each time, the same name surfacing in the correspondence like a body in a pond. Mine.

"And so we arrive at June of this year," Hargreaves said. "A fourth death. Another accident, on its face. Another drowning, in fact — the same stretch of water as 2019. And this time, before any letter is written, before any coroner is troubled, the defendant is already there. Already at the millrace. Wearing, I would remind the panel, clothes identical to those on the corpse."

That last part wasn't true and everyone in the building knew it wasn't true — I hadn't been wearing the coat, the coat was on Tomas Reyes — but truth in a hearing is a matter of what survives the retelling, and her version was shorter than mine. Shorter versions win. I'd known that since I was sixteen years old and learned that the difference between a record and a rumor is only who got to write first.

* * *

They called Mrs. Okonkwo after lunch.

She looked terrible up there, and it was my fault she looked terrible, and knowing it was my fault made it worse. She kept smoothing her skirt and addressing her answers to the panel rather than to Hargreaves, as if the panel might protect her, as if anyone in that room would.

"You know the defendant well?"

"Since she was a girl." Mrs. Okonkwo's voice wobbled and steadied itself. "Since the canon took her in. We all — the parish, we all watched her grow up. She used to help me with the lilies at Easter. Good hands. Careful."

"And in recent years? Have you observed changes?"

Objection, sustained, rephrased. But the rephrasing did the same work. Had I seemed obsessive. Had I asked strange questions. Had I, on the Tuesday after Tomas Reyes was pulled from the water, come to the vestry and asked Mrs. Okonkwo — she confirmed this, reluctantly, wringing her hands — whether she remembered anything unusual about the spring of 1994.

"What sort of unusual?" Hargreaves asked.

"She asked about the crypt. Whether it had ever been opened. Whether I remembered the sealing of it. I told her I didn't like to remember that year. I lost a friend that year." Her hands stopped moving. "And Miss Kavanagh — she was kind about it, she was gentle, I want that said — but she kept asking. She has a way of asking that makes you answer. Like the questions aren't really questions. Like she already knows and is only checking."

Like she already knows and is only checking.

I sat very still and thought: that is the most accurate description of my work anyone has ever produced, and she has just handed it to the prosecution gift-wrapped.

Because here is what nobody in that room understood, and here is what I could not say from the gallery without confirming it: yes. I ask like that because I usually do already know. You find the ledger entry first, the date, the discrepancy, the payment for work never done — and then you go to the witness not to discover but to confirm, because confirmation is all a living memory is good for. Memory is the worst archive in the world. It edits. It flatters. It loses whole decades and invents others. But it can still sign its name to a fact, if you bring the fact to it gently enough.

Mrs. Okonkwo stepped down and would not look at me, and I understood that too. She wasn't ashamed of testifying. She was ashamed of having answered honestly, because honesty, in a parish, is a form of betrayal. I had been raised on that grammar. Ellery had taught it to me in a hundred small lessons — *what is said in the vestry stays in the vestry, Paloma; a community runs on discretion, not disclosure* — and now the grammar was being conjugated against me in open court by a woman arranging Easter lilies.

Old Denby followed. He said I'd been seen at the millrace three times in the week before the body was found. True. I'd been walking the water, reading it, the way you reread a page looking for the misprint. He said I'd asked him about the tower keys. Also true. He said, and here his voice dropped into something almost tender, that the canon had told him to keep an eye on me, that I'd been "under a strain."

Under a strain. The phrase went through the room like a current. Every head turned a degree toward me, the way wheat turns. Under a strain — meaning unwell, meaning unreliable, meaning the poor creature isn't responsible, which is the kindest possible way of saying the poor creature cannot be believed.

And I thought, with a clarity that felt like falling: he built this. Not today. Years ago. Every kindness was load-bearing. Every *keep an eye on her*, every gentle word to the parish about my fragility, every time he'd told me I worked too hard, cared too much, dug too deep — it was all scaffolding, erected in advance, waiting for the day the building needed to come down. He had spent thirty years constructing the testimony of everyone who loved me.

The photograph came up last. Hargreaves held it — or rather, held the evidence bag containing the print Okafor had recovered from the curate's desk drawer, the replacement photograph, the staged one, the one planted in my flat during the second theft. Except the panel didn't know it was planted. The panel saw a photograph of a young woman and a younger Ellery at the crypt door, her hand on the iron ring, both of them half-smiling, intimate as family.

"The defendant kept this photograph," Hargreaves said. "In her bedroom. Behind other photographs, as if concealed. A photograph of herself and the chief witness against the theory she has spent thirty years promoting — that something was buried in that crypt worth killing for. Why keep it hidden, unless its existence complicates the story the defendant tells?"

Behind other photographs. Yes. Because it had been taken the day he gave me the archive, the day I turned nineteen, the day I believed I'd been chosen rather than collected. Because I'd hidden it the way you hide anything that hurts to look at — not because it was evidence, but because it was a wound.

I raised my hand. The usher frowned. I raised it higher.

"I'd like to address the panel," I said.

My solicitor — a tired, decent man named Pryce whom Okafor had helped me find — was on his feet saying something about procedure, and the chair was saying something about order, and none of it mattered, because Hargreaves had turned and looked directly at me for the first time all day, and smiled the small professional smile of a woman who has just been handed exactly what she wanted.

"By all means," she said. "Let the defendant speak."

* * *

What I said, I'll set down here exactly, because I've learned that exactness is the only armor that holds.

I said: the photograph in that bag is not the photograph that was stolen from my flat. I said: compare the borders, the fading, the emulsion crack along the lower left corner — the original had one, this doesn't. I said: someone stole my coat, my scarf, and my photograph, and replaced the photograph with a copy, and you do not replace a thing unless the original says something the copy must not say. I said: ask who had access to the original. Ask who appears in it. Ask why a man who claims to barely recall the day it was taken can be seen, in this copy, standing closer to the camera than in any version of events he has given.

The room went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet when a person in the dock stops behaving like a person in the dock.

Hargreaves recovered first. "The defendant is describing a conspiracy involving the theft and forgery of personal effects. Is that your evidence?"

"My evidence," I said, "is that archives exist because people lie, and ledgers exist because people lie with numbers, and I have spent my life learning to tell the difference. Someone in this case has been editing the record. I am simply the first person to have read it closely."

"Or," Hargreaves said, turning back to the panel, spreading her hands, letting them see the whole shape of it, "you are the editor. You are the archivist. You are the one person in this parish with the skill, the access, and — by your own history — the compulsion to rewrite the past. The panel may decide which of us is describing the forger."

And there it was. The whole trap in one sentence, sprung shut. Everything I was — the competence, the patience, the terrible fluency in other people's paperwork — offered to twelve strangers as proof of guilt. He had trained me for thirty years, and the training fit the frame like a key fits a lock, and I understood, sitting there with my level handwriting and my level heart, that this had always been the design. Not the arrest. The unmasking. He didn't need me convicted. He needed me discredited — because the day I was discredited, every letter I'd ever written to a coroner, every question I'd ever asked, every thread I'd ever pulled would unravel backward into noise. The mad archivist. The orphan who never stopped digging. Case closed, all cases, forever.

The chair called the afternoon to a close. People rose and murmured and filed out. Mrs. Okonkwo paused near the aisle, opened her mouth, closed it, and left. Denby left. Ellery rose last, gathered his coat, and only then — the discipline breaking for exactly one second, deliberately, because nothing he did was undeliberate — looked at me across the emptying room.

He didn't smile. He inclined his head, slightly, the way he used to when I'd correctly dated an undated document. *Well reasoned,* the nod said. *Wrong conclusion, but well reasoned.* And then he was gone, into the corridor, into the crowd of parishioners who loved him, and I sat alone in the polished room with my notes and understood that I had just heard his opening argument too.

Marisol Reyes dropped into the seat beside me. She smelled of cigarette smoke and train stations.

"The curate," she said quietly, eyes forward. "The one whose desk the photograph was found in. Okafor's pulling his personnel file tonight. And Paloma—" she finally turned, and her face was furious and alive and the closest thing to hope I'd seen in weeks, "—he looked at you just now like a man checking his watch. Like a man on a schedule. Whatever he's planning next, it's soon."

She was right. I could feel it the way you feel weather coming off water.

The next hearing was set for Thursday.
