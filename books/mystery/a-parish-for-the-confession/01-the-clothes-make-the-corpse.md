## 1. The Clothes Make the Corpse

*From Episode 47 of "Blackwood's Ledger": "The Clothes Make the Corpse, Part One." Recorded on location, Marrow's End, Parish of St. Brigid.*

You know how every town has a smell? Gas stations and cut grass in the suburbs. Salt and diesel on the coast. Well, Marrow's End smells like wet stone and old incense—the kind that's soaked so deep into the walls it can't be scrubbed out, only endured. I'd been in town four hours when I first smelled it properly, standing in the doorway of a church that burned down before some of you were born, and I remember thinking: *this is what guilt smells like when it's had thirty years to cure.*

But I'm getting ahead of myself. I always get ahead of myself. My producer, Dev, says it's my only flaw, which is generous of him, since he also has to listen to me eat.

Let me back up.

I came to Marrow's End for a cold case. That's the brand, if brands are things we're doing—Petra Blackwood, the woman who closes cases nobody else can. Eleven of them now. Eleven families who got answers they didn't necessarily want, delivered in my voice, which people tell me sounds like "a librarian reading a eulogy," which I've decided to take as a compliment. I don't close cases because I'm noble. I close them because an open case is a splinter under the skin of the world, and I am apparently the kind of person who cannot stop picking.

The case was Sister Agnes Doyle, thirty-one years old, last seen on the night of October 14th, 1995, walking toward the confessional at St. Brigid's with her shoes in her hand. She never made it to the booth—or she did, depending on which witness you believed, and there was exactly one witness whose account mattered: a young man named Daniel Voss, who told the parish priest he'd seen a car idling in the churchyard lot, who then recanted, who then left town, who then died in 1995 in circumstances the county recorded as accidental and everyone else recorded as convenient. The church burned two weeks after Agnes vanished. Nobody was ever charged. The file went cold the way files do—not dramatically, just quietly, like a phone call where both parties wait for the other to hang up first.

Thirty years. That's the anniversary I'd come to mark. Thirty years of a town pretending a woman simply walked out of her own life and forgot to write.

I checked into the Hollis House Bed & Breakfast at four o'clock on a Tuesday, a rental cottage actually, one of three the Hollises keep behind the main house for "visiting scholars and such," said Mrs. Hollis, in a tone suggesting visiting scholars were a known nuisance but a paying one. The cottage smelled of lavender and dust. The closet held a row of wooden hangers, empty except for a mothball sachet shaped like a fish, which I found charming and would later find less so.

I unpacked. I set up my recorder on the kitchen table. I did the thing I always do in a new town, which is walk it. You learn more from a town's feet than its face. Marrow's End walks like a man avoiding eye contact: shoulders up, eyes down, moving with purpose toward destinations that don't exist. Main Street has a hardware store, a diner called the Kettle, a pharmacy with a soda fountain nobody uses, and a real estate office whose window display consists entirely of properties marked SOLD, as if the Ashford family—which owns, per the historical society plaque, "a substantial portion of the parish"—wants you to know that everything here is already spoken for. Including, presumably, the truth.

At the Kettle I ordered coffee I didn't want and asked the waitress about St. Brigid's. She said, "Sad thing," and refilled a cup belonging to no one. An old man in the corner booth said, "Which part of sad?" and the waitress said, "Earl," and Earl said nothing else for the entire hour I sat there, though he watched me the way cats watch birds through glass—with patience, and the certainty that the glass won't hold forever.

Here's what I know about being a woman who asks questions in small towns: you become weather. Everyone talks about you; no one talks *to* you. By six o'clock, three separate people had told Mrs. Hollis I was "asking around," and by seven, Mrs. Hollis had told me, sweetly, that dinner was served at seven sharp and the Wi-Fi password was on the fridge, and that if I needed anything at all I should need it quietly.

I laughed. She didn't. Noted.

* * *

The call came at 9:40 p.m.

I want to be precise about this because precision is the only religion I've ever kept. 9:40, my cell, a number I didn't recognize, a voice I'd later learn belonged to Detective Ilsa Reyes of the county sheriff's office, a voice like a door closing politely.

"Ms. Blackwood. Where are you right now?"

"The Hollis House. Why?"

"Is anyone with you?"

"No."

A pause long enough to have furniture in it. "Don't leave the cottage. Don't let anyone in. I'm sending a unit."

"Detective, with respect, you have me at a disadvantage, and I hate disadvantages. What's happened?"

Another pause. This one had a body in it—I could hear her deciding whether to carry it across the line or leave it on her side. She carried it.

"There's been a death. Out past the old quarry road. And there's—" A breath. "There's something at the scene I need you to see before I write my report. Before anyone writes anything."

"Detective, I'm a journalist, not a—"

"I know what you are. Everybody in this county knows what you are. You've got eleven closed cases and a microphone and a following bigger than our whole parish population, and half my colleagues think that makes you a carnival barker." Her voice didn't rise. It lowered, which was worse. "I think it makes you useful. Come at dawn. And Ms. Blackwood—lock your door."

She hung up. I sat in the lavender-and-dust quiet of that rental cottage and felt the particular cold that isn't temperature. It's the cold of realizing a story has noticed *you*.

I locked the door. I didn't sleep. At some point near three a.m. I got up for water and passed the closet, and something made me open it—the way you check a lock twice, the way you look behind the shower curtain in a strange house even though you're thirty-eight years old and embarrassed about it.

Six hangers. Empty. The mothball fish swayed slightly, though the window was shut.

I stood there telling myself the Hollises had collected them for laundering, that everything was fine, that towns like this were merely unfriendly, not uncanny. Then I counted again. When I'd unpacked, there had been eight hangers. Two of mine were missing from my own suitcase side of the rod.

Two hangers. Two garments. Mine.

I'm not going to perform calm for you. My hands shook. I told myself: coincidence, housekeeping, paranoia—the occupational hazard of a woman who makes her living believing the worst until proven otherwise. I took a photo of the closet anyway, timestamped it, emailed it to Dev with the subject line IF I GO MISSING, START HERE, which he has received from me in three counties now and which he has learned to treat as a mood rather than an emergency.

Then I sat up until dawn with the lights on, listening to a house settle around me like something shifting its weight, waiting.

*From Episode 47, continued:*

The quarry road runs northwest out of town through birch woods that go on like a held breath. The scene was already taped off when I arrived at 6:15, and Detective Reyes was waiting at the tape, arms crossed, watching me come up the gravel with the expression of someone who has already regretted a decision and is committed to seeing it through.

She's not what you picture when you picture county detectives. Mid-forties, compact, gray shot through black hair pulled back hard enough to lift her eyebrows, eyes the flat brown of a river in November. She looked at me the way Earl had looked at me in the Kettle, but without the patience.

"You slept?" she said.

"Not really. You?"

"I sleep fine. Guilt-free conscience." She lifted the tape. "Walk where I walk. Touch nothing. And when you see it, you see it once, and then you answer my questions honestly, because I'll know."

"Detective, honesty is literally my business model."

"Your business model," she said, leading me through the birches, "is attention. Honesty is optional equipment."

The clearing opened without warning—a fold in the land where the quarry used to flood, black water ringed with reeds, and near the water's edge, under a tarp the coroner hadn't yet claimed, the shape.

Reyes crouched and pulled the tarp back to the shoulders.

I've seen bodies. I want to say that plainly, because podcast listeners develop a strange parasocial idea that we hosts are hardened, and mostly we are, mostly it's photographs and transcripts and other people's grief at a safe acoustic distance. But I've stood over enough of the actual thing to know the difference between a person and what's left, and what was left here had been a man—young, maybe late twenties, dark hair, face slack with the terrible administrative blankness of the dead.

And he was wearing my clothes.

My gray merino sweater, the one I'd bought in Dublin two years ago, the one with the loose stitch at the left cuff that I always mean to fix. My jeans. Even, God help me, my boots—the low-heeled ones I'd worn on the plane, which had been in my suitcase, which had been in my locked cottage, which had been—

I heard my own breathing go wrong. Reyes watched me do it, cataloguing it, and I understood suddenly why she'd wanted me here before the report: not for my insight. For my reaction. On the record, in her memory, unperformed.

"Those are yours," she said. Not a question.

"Yes." My voice came out level, which surprised us both. "That's my sweater. There's a loose stitch at the cuff. Left side. Check it."

He wasn't wearing them well. They hung on him—he was broader than me through the shoulders, and the jeans had been belted tight to keep them up, cinched with a belt that wasn't mine either. Someone had dressed him. Someone had taken clothes out of my closet, out of my suitcase, out of my life, and put them on a dead stranger in the woods, arranged him near the water, and left him for the morning to find.

"Why?" I said. It came out smaller than I meant it to. "Why dress him like me?"

Reyes straightened, pulling the tarp back over his face with a gentleness that contradicted everything else about her.

"That's the question I was going to ask you," she said. "Because here's the part I couldn't put in a report yet. We ran his prints through the system overnight, standard procedure." She paused, and I watched her decide, again, whether to cross a line. She crossed it. "They came back, Ms. Blackwood. He's got prints on file. From 1995."

The birches stood very still around us. Somewhere in the reeds, the flooded quarry lapped at its own edges, patient as a tongue.

"Daniel Voss," she said. "The witness in your nun's case. The one who died thirty years ago." She looked down at the tarp, then back at me, and for the first time since I'd met her—twelve hours ago, on the phone, in the dark—something moved behind those flat brown eyes that wasn't suspicion. It was almost sympathy, and it frightened me more than the sweater did.

"Except Daniel Voss has been dead since before you started shaving your legs," she said. "So either the county buried the wrong man in '95, or somebody dug him up, dressed him in your clothes, and laid him out here like a message." She clicked the tarp down fully, sealing him in. "And I've been doing this twenty years, Ms. Blackwood. Messages like this? They're never for the dead man."

The wind moved through the birches, and I thought about two empty hangers in a lavender-scented closet, and a fish-shaped sachet swaying in a room with the windows shut, and a voice on the phone at 9:40 p.m. saying *don't let anyone in*, and I understood, with the clarity that only ever arrives too late to be useful, that I hadn't come to Marrow's End to hunt a story.

The story had sent for me.

"So," I said, because my mouth has never once waited for my brain, "when do we start?"

Reyes almost smiled. Almost. "First," she said, "you're going to tell me exactly who knew you were coming to town—and exactly what you packed."
