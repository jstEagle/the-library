## 1. Two Envelopes, One Postmark

The dispatch office at the bottom of Delacroix Street had a way of making time feel like something poured rather than spent. Bram Okonkwo knew this the way she knew the hum of the radio, the particular rattle of the third chair from the window, the way the fluorescent tube above the log books flickered twice before settling — a small stutter of light that happened every night at 11:40 and again just before dawn, as if the building itself were clearing its throat.

It was 11:47 on a Tuesday in October when the mail cart came through.

The night porter, a stooped man named Ferro who had worked the river district longer than Bram had been alive, wheeled his cart past the glass partition and dropped a thin stack onto her counter without breaking stride. Bills, mostly. A circular from a tire wholesaler. And two envelopes that did not belong to any of it.

They were addressed to *B. Okonkwo, Night Dispatcher* — no surname in the addressee line beyond the initial, no company name, no return address. Just the two words in a hand that was careful and old-fashioned, the letters upright and slightly cramped, as though written by someone who had learned penmanship in an era when it was taught as a moral discipline. The ink was black. The paper was heavy cream stock, the kind that cost more than a dispatcher's hourly wage.

Bram looked at them for a long moment before touching them.

Twelve years in this city had taught her to read mail the way other people read faces. She received very little of it, by design. Her lease was month-to-month under a name one letter off from her own. Her utility bills went to a post box she checked at irregular hours. The taxi company's payroll listed her as B. Okonkwo because the state required a name and she had decided, long ago, that hiding inside your own name was safer than inventing another — invented names required maintenance, and maintenance left trails. Bram Okonkwo existed on paper exactly as much as the law demanded and not one syllable more. She had no photographs online, no memberships, no subscriptions, no doctor who could describe her face to a stranger. She was, in the most literal sense available to a living person, unlisted.

And yet here were two envelopes that had found her anyway — found her not at her flat, not at her post box, but at the dispatch desk, routed through the company's general delivery with nothing but her title and an initial.

She put on her reading glasses, which she wore low on her nose so they wouldn't fog near the coffee, and turned the first envelope over. The flap had been sealed with a damp sponge, cleanly, no lick, no tape. The postmark was smudged but legible: *HALLOWAY PARISH STATION*, dated six days ago.

Halloway.

The name landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well. She waited for the sound of it hitting bottom, and it took longer than she expected.

Halloway was the parish where she'd grown up — or whatever you called what she'd done instead of growing up, those years along the river flats where the houses leaned toward the water like conspirators and everyone knew everyone's business except the business that mattered. She had left Halloway at nineteen and had not gone back, not once, not even to stand outside it. She had erased the parish from herself with the same methodical patience she applied to everything: changed her phone number twice, let every childhood acquaintance fall out of contact naturally, never mentioned it on forms. When people asked where she was from — people rarely asked; her manner discouraged asking — she said *upriver*, which was true and meant nothing.

Nobody sent her mail from Halloway. Nobody alive should know to send her mail anywhere at all.

She slit the first envelope with the letter opener Ferro had won at a church raffle in 1987 and kept on the counter like a relic.

Inside was a single sheet, folded in thirds, written in the same upright hand. No salutation. It began mid-thought, as if the writer had been carrying the sentence around for years and finally set it down:

*I was driving the car.*

Bram sat back. The radio crackled beside her — Car 14 requesting a fare check on Mabry Street — and she muted it without looking, her fingers finding the dial by habit while her eyes stayed on the page.

*I have carried this twelve years and I am done carrying it. On the night of the fourteenth of March, twelve years ago, I struck a woman on the causeway road below the old tannery and I did not stop. I want to say I did not see her, but that is a lie I have told so often it has worn smooth as river glass. I saw her. She was wearing a green coat. She had her hand up — not waving, I think now, but shielding her eyes from my headlights, as if she believed the person driving would be someone who would stop.*

*I will not write the rest here. The rest is not mine alone to tell. But if you are reading this, then you are the one person I believe might understand why it has taken me twelve years, and why it must be now, and why it must be you.*

*There is a trial. You may already know. If you do not know, you will.*

*Forgive me for what this letter will cost you. I suspect you have already paid more than your share.*

*— H.*

Bram read it three times. The third time she read it aloud, barely above a whisper, because saying words out loud sometimes shook loose what silence hid, and she wanted to hear whether the sentences sounded like a confession or a trap.

They sounded like both.

The fourteenth of March, twelve years ago. She did the arithmetic without wanting to. She had been twenty-three. She had been living upriver by then, three years gone from Halloway, working her first dispatch job in a windowless room that smelled of burnt coffee and vinyl. Whatever had happened on the causeway road below the old tannery had happened in a parish she'd already amputated, in a life she'd already closed like a door and painted over.

Except.

Except that she remembered the tannery. Of course she remembered the tannery; you didn't grow up on the river flats and forget the smell of it, the sulfur-and-rot stink that rolled off the building on warm nights and made mothers pull children indoors. And she remembered the causeway road, narrow and unlit, running between the tannery ruins and the water, the road every kid in the parish was told never to walk after dark and every kid walked after dark anyway.

Someone had died there. Someone in a green coat, with her hand raised against the headlights.

Bram had no memory of it. That was the thing that unsettled her most — not the letter itself but the blank where the memory should be. Twelve years ago she had still had one foot in Halloway; her mother was buried there; she'd come back for the anniversary of the burial, once, in early spring. Had there been police tape somewhere? Had there been talk in the shops, a name passed around? Or had the parish simply absorbed the death the way the river absorbed everything, silently, without changing course?

She set the page down and looked at the second envelope.

It was the same weight, same cream stock, same handwriting on the face. She held it up to the flickering light and could see nothing through it — good paper, opaque as a held breath. She opened it more slowly than the first, which surprised her; she hadn't thought herself capable of slowness anymore. Caution was supposed to be her native language.

Inside were two pages. The handwriting had changed character — still upright, still careful, but pressed harder into the paper, the pen dragged as though the writer's hand had begun to shake.

*Miss Okonkwo —*

*So you have opened the first one. I knew you would. You are a woman who opens things; it is your profession and perhaps your affliction. You open doors for strangers at all hours. You open lines of communication across a sleeping city. I imagine you open yourself rarely, if ever, and I am sorry to be the one forcing the matter.*

*You do not know me. That is the point. Knowing me would have endangered us both.*

*Here is what I can offer. The man who wrote you the first letter — yes, we correspond, he and I, in the slow way of the guilty — has told you part of the story. There is a civil trial beginning in nine weeks. The family of the woman in the green coat has waited twelve years for someone to be held to account, and they are about to lose, because the man who was truly driving has spent those twelve years becoming respectable, and respectability is armor that juries do not like to pierce.*

*The prosecution — such as it is, in a civil matter — believes their star witness is dead. Drowned, officially, the same spring as the accident. Her name was Ada Voss. She lived on Tannery Row. She saw everything.*

*Ada Voss is dead the way certain women are dead: on paper, in the mouths of men who needed her to be, in the records that were adjusted by hands that were paid to adjust them. But paper is not water, Miss Okonkwo. Paper drowns nothing.*

*Find Ada Voss. Bring her back into the light. Do this, and I will send you the rest of the story — the parts Halloran cannot tell you because he does not know them, the parts that explain why a woman fakes her own death and stays dead for twelve years, the parts about the judge's aide whose name appears nowhere yet but will appear everywhere soon.*

*You are wondering how I know you. You are wondering what a night dispatcher has to do with any of this. Be patient. The answer is coming, and when it comes you will understand that you have been part of this story far longer than you know.*

*One more thing, because I think you will check, because checking is what you do: look at the dispatch logs for the night of the accident. Not your logs — you weren't there yet. The old company, Riverline, before the merger. The fare records from the causeway district, the fourteenth of March. Somewhere in those pages is a booking that should not exist, a ride that was requested and answered, and the reason that ride matters is the reason I am writing to you and not to a lawyer, or a reporter, or the family themselves.*

*Lawyers want testimony. Reporters want headlines. The family wants justice, and deserves it, but they cannot do what needs doing, because what needs doing requires a person whom no record can find.*

*That person is you, Miss Okonkwo. You have spent years making sure no one could find you. Now I am asking you to use it.*

*The rest of the story follows the witness.*

*— A friend of the dead, and of the buried-alive*

Bram lowered the pages. The office was very quiet. Outside the glass, Ferro's cart squeaked away down the corridor, and somewhere above her the city turned over in its sleep, and the radio hissed with the soft static of a hundred empty cabs waiting for fares that wouldn't come until morning.

*Ada Voss.* She said the name silently, testing its weight. Dead twelve years. Alive on paper's failure. Living on Tannery Row, in a parish Bram had spent a decade pretending was a place she'd imagined.

And the last line — *the reason I am writing to you and not to a lawyer* — circled back on itself in her mind like a car circling a block, patient, looking for a place to stop.

A ride that was requested and answered. A booking that should not exist.

Bram pulled the current log book toward her, then stopped. Not these. The old ones. Riverline. Before the merger, before her time — but companies like this one never threw anything away; they boxed it and stacked it in the basement behind the boiler, where the paper slept in the dark for decades, waiting for someone with a flashlight and a reason.

She looked at the clock. 12:31. Four hours until the shift change. Four hours of a slow night, the kind of night she had built her whole life around — nights when nothing happened, when no one looked, when the world forgot she was there.

The letters sat on the counter between her and the basement door, addressed in a hand that knew too much, postmarked from a parish that was supposed to be behind her.

Bram Okonkwo, who had spent twelve years ensuring that no record anywhere could find her, reached beneath the counter for her flashlight, and went looking for a record of her own.
