## 9. The Longest Fare

The night before testimony arrived the way bad weather arrives on the river — not all at once but in a pressure Bram felt in her teeth before she saw a single cloud.

She had spent nine days building the plan the way she had once built her own disappearance: in layers, redundancies, small unremarkable transactions that added up to one impossible thing. A woman who did not exist would walk into a courthouse and exist, loudly, under oath, and then walk out again alive. Every route Bram knew by heart was a tool for vanishing. Now she had to run them backward.

At 11:40 p.m., she pulled the cab up to the safehouse — a shuttered bait shop on the east bank with a rusted sign reading LIVE BAIT / NO REFUNDS — and killed the headlights before the turn, coasting the last hundred yards on momentum and memory. Ada Voss came out of the dark side door carrying a canvas bag and wearing a wig of gray-streaked brown hair that made her look like every tired woman who had ever taken a late cab home from a hospital waiting room. That was the point. Ada had learned to be nobody too; she'd just had twelve more years of practice than Bram.

"You're late," Ada said, folding herself into the back seat out of habit, out of twelve years of habit, before catching herself and moving to the front.

"Deliberately," Bram said. "Everyone watching this building expects punctuality. Punctuality is a signature." She pulled away without lights until the second cross street. "We're not going when they expect or how they expect. We're going boring."

"Boring."

"The most invisible thing in a parish at midnight is a taxi doing something completely ordinary. Not speeding. Not hiding. Just a fare." She glanced over. "You've ridden in cabs for months without being seen. Tonight you're just another fare."

Ada's hands were steady on her knees, which told Bram she was more frightened than she let show; frightened people either shook or went very still, and Ada had gone still as river water in January. "And if it doesn't work?"

"Then tomorrow you testify anyway," Bram said. "I just won't be there to see it."

* * *

The first watcher was where Bram knew he would be, because she had put him there herself — or rather, her own habits had taught her where watchers go. A sedan with fogged windows idled near the pharmacy bench, the spot where Ada had booked rides for months without ever taking them. Bram didn't slow. She took the left onto Ferry Row at exactly the speed limit, signaled even though no one was behind them, and watched the sedan's brake lights bloom in her mirror like a wound opening.

"He's coming," Ada said.

"Of course he is. He's supposed to. That's Delacroix's man, and his job tonight is to follow whoever leaves that building." Bram turned right, then immediately left through the church parking lot, exiting on the far side by the rectory garbage bins — a cut-through she'd used for years to dodge a fare who didn't want to be seen arriving somewhere. "He'll circle the block twice before he figures out we're gone. By then we're on the service road."

The river service roads were Bram's private scripture. Twelve years of night dispatch had carved them into her: the gravel lane behind the old cannery where the asphalt gave way to packed shell; the levee access track that ran between the flood wall and the back fences of houses whose owners had long since stopped looking at the river; the collapsed culvert past the marina that forced every driver but her to turn around. On the map in Delacroix's office — she was certain there was a map in Delacroix's office — those roads were gray lines, unnamed, forgotten. In her head they were sentences she could recite with her eyes closed.

The cab bumped onto the cannery lane and the world went soft and dark. No streetlights here. The river ran invisible on their left, announcing itself only as a smell — mud, diesel, dead fish — and a sound, a slow muscular shifting against the pilings.

"Tell me again why not the highway," Ada said. It wasn't a challenge. It was a woman checking her arithmetic because the sum was her life.

"Because the highway has cameras, and cameras have timestamps, and Delacroix has friends who can pull timestamps. Because the highway is forty minutes of being a shape in a windshield." Bram downshifted around the cannery's loading dock, its corrugated walls breathing rust into the night air. "These roads have no cameras. They have me."

They rode in silence after that, the good kind, the kind two people who had both spent years erasing themselves could share. Once, passing the levee track, Ada said quietly, "I used to walk this. After. When I was deciding whether to stay dead." Her hand rose toward the window, toward the black bulk of the flood wall. "It felt like the one place in the parish that agreed with me about what I was."

"It's not gone," Bram said. "You are. You were never gone."

Ada didn't answer, but in the rearview mirror — the mirror Bram kept angled so she could watch fares without staring — she saw Ada press her knuckles briefly against her mouth, the way people do when grief arrives years late and finds them unprepared.

* * *

The trouble started at the marina.

Bram had planned for the culvert, for the detour through the boatyard, for the dog that always slept in the middle of Shell Road and never moved for anyone. What she hadn't planned for was light — a flashlight beam sweeping the boatyard gate, and behind it, parked nose-out at the fuel dock, a pickup truck she recognized from Halloran's ledger. The ledger had named three men paid to bury the truth. Two were in prison now, testifying. The third, Renny Cade, had been seen exactly once in nine days: outside the courthouse, smoking, watching the doors.

"Renny Cade," Bram breathed, and cut her lights.

"What—"

"Don't talk." She eased the cab off the shell road onto a weed-choked apron behind the boat racks, killing the engine. In the sudden silence the river sounded enormous. Through the windshield, the flashlight beam walked along the fence line, patient, methodical. He wasn't searching. He was guarding. Which meant someone had guessed the service roads after all — or, worse, had simply stationed men at every mouth of every road out of the east bank, and let the net do the work.

"They're not chasing us," Ada whispered. "They're herding us. Toward the bridge."

Bram's stomach dropped, because Ada was right, and because Bram had known it for thirty seconds and hated admitting it. There was only one way off the east bank tonight. Every service road funneled, eventually, to the parish bridge — the narrow iron span where, twelve years ago, a man named Delacroix had driven a car through a woman named Ada Voss and kept driving. Of course they'd hold it. The bridge was where the story had started. Stories like to end where they began.

"Options," Ada said.

"One." Bram stared through the glass at the walking light. "We sit here until dawn and miss your testimony window."

"And?"

"And I get out and introduce myself to Mr. Cade."

The silence in the cab had weight. Then Ada laughed — a short, disbelieving sound with no humor in it. "No."

"Yes."

"Bram. He knows your face is worth nothing and your car is worth everything. You step out of this cab, you're a loose end. Loose ends—" She stopped.

"I've spent my whole adult life making sure I couldn't be a loose end," Bram said. "No records. No lease in my name. No photographs. Do you know what that means? It means there's nothing to find. Nothing to threaten. Nothing to bury." She heard her own voice go strange and calm, the dispatcher voice, the voice that sent cabs into the dark all night and never once got hurt. "Except one thing. And I've been saving it."

"Saving what?"

"My name." She looked at Ada directly for the first time since the pharmacy bench, months ago, when a voice on the radio had ordered a ride she never took. "Nobody in this parish knows it. Tomorrow, half the courtroom will. If Cade takes me seriously — if he believes I'm about to become a person the newspapers will ask questions about — then hurting me stops being easy and starts being evidence. People like him aren't afraid of witnesses. They're afraid of *attention*. I'm about to become attention."

"That's insane."

"It's a fare," Bram said. "The longest one I've ever taken. And the meter's running."

Before Ada could argue again, Bram opened the door and stepped out into the smell of the river.

* * *

The flashlight found her immediately, pinning her against the boat racks like a specimen. Behind the beam, Renny Cade resolved out of the dark: big, unhurried, a man built for standing in doorways. He didn't reach for anything. He didn't need to. His size was the argument.

"Cab's broke?" he said.

"Just the driver changing plans." Bram kept her hands visible, her voice level. "You're wasting your night, Mr. Cade. The woman you're waiting for isn't coming down any of these roads. She's already across the bridge. Has been since ten o'clock, in a church van full of altar flowers. I'm the decoy. My job was to make sure somebody followed *me*, so nobody followed *her*." She let that land, then added, softly, the knife going in: "Which means whatever happens to me tonight gets written down tomorrow, in a courtroom, under oath, next to your name. Halloran's ledger already has you in it once. I read it. Did you?"

Something moved behind Cade's eyes — not fear exactly, but the animal arithmetic of a man realizing the ground had shifted. For twelve years his work had been done on people who stayed dead, stayed quiet, stayed nothing. Bram watched him weigh her: a taxi driver, a nobody, except that nobodies don't know ledgers, don't know names, don't stand in the dark volunteering information with a witness's confidence.

"Who are you?" he said finally.

And here it was — the moment she had been saving, the coin she had hoarded through twelve years of erased leases and unlisted numbers and paychecks signed with initials. Bram Okonkwo, who had not spoken her own full name aloud to a stranger since she was twenty-three, stood in the mud by the river and gave it away like the last thing in her pocket.

"Bram Okonkwo. Night dispatcher, Parish Cab Company. And as of eight o'clock tomorrow morning, sworn witness for the prosecution." She let him see her smile, thin and true. "Look me up. Starting tomorrow, you'll be able to."

Cade studied her for a long five seconds. Then he stepped aside, deliberately, theatrically, and swept the flashlight toward the shell road south — the gesture of a man choosing, this once, to believe the easier story. "Bridge is that way," he said. "Drive careful."

She drove careful. She drove the length of the boatyard with her heart slamming against her ribs like something trying to get out, and only when the marina lights were gone did she realize her hands were shaking on the wheel — shaking, and grinning.

In the passenger seat, Ada exhaled a breath she seemed to have been holding since the accident. "You gave him your name."

"I gave him my name," Bram agreed. And found, to her astonishment, that it didn't feel like losing anything. It felt like setting down luggage she'd carried so long she'd forgotten its weight.

The parish bridge rose ahead of them, iron ribs black against the stars, the river sliding beneath with its patient, indifferent strength. Halfway across, Bram slowed — not from fear, but because some old instinct made her want to look. Below, in the dark water, was the place where a car had struck a woman and kept going, and above, in the cab, was the woman, alive, twelve years later, twenty minutes from the rest of her life.

"Thank you," Ada said quietly. "For getting out of the car."

"Get some sleep," Bram said. "Court's at eight."

But sleep wouldn't come for either of them, and when they reached the safehouse on the west bank — a different one, the one only Father Ambrose knew about — there was a car already waiting in the drive, and a man leaning against it in the starlight, and Bram's blood went cold before she recognized the lean of him: Detective Ruark, hat in his hands, looking like a man carrying news he'd rehearsed all the way over.

"Miss Okonkwo," he said. "I need to tell you about Halloran's ledger. There's a page missing — and I think I know who has it."
