## 10. A Parish for the Living

The courthouse smelled of floor wax and old rain, and Bram Okonkwo sat in the third row with her hands folded in her lap like a woman at a funeral she had finally made peace with.

She had not slept. None of them had. Ada was in the witness box now, upright in a gray coat that still carried the creases of the safehouse drawer it had lived in for twelve years, and her voice — the voice Bram knew only from clipped radio confirmations, *confirmed, corner of Marsh and Tenth*, a fare who never spoke — filled the courtroom like water filling a dry channel.

"I saw the car," Ada said. "I saw it twice. Once on the road, once in the mirror of my own life, every year since."

Bram watched the judge's aide — Callum Reeve, though the papers had started calling him *the defendant* two days ago, when Halloran's ledger went into evidence — watch Ada. He had the face of a man doing arithmetic he was losing. Beside him his lawyers murmured; behind him, the men from the ledger sat in their good coats with the stillness of people who had paid for silence once and were discovering that silence, like everything else, had gone up in price.

Ada testified for four hours. She did not cry until the end, and when she did, she did not hide it.

"The hardest part," she said, looking not at the jury but at the gallery, at the victim's family, at Bram, "wasn't being dead. Being dead was easy. You just stop. The hard part was watching everyone I loved grieve a woman who was sitting in the next room eating cold beans out of a can." She wiped her face with the back of her hand, unashamed. "I'm done being dead. That's all. That's my whole testimony, really. I'm done."

* * *

The verdict came on a Thursday, in weather that couldn't decide between fog and sun.

Guilty. Guilty. Guilty — the word landed seven times, once for each count, and each time it landed Bram felt something shift in her chest, some load-bearing wall coming down that she hadn't known was holding anything up. Reeve was led out past the gallery, and as he passed he looked at Bram — really looked at her, the first person in years to do so — and she found she could hold his eyes without flinching. That was the strange gift of it. Once you'd stood up in a courtroom and said your own name into a microphone, *Bram Okonkwo, dispatcher, night shift, and I drove the witness myself*, there wasn't much left to be afraid of.

The victim's family — the Adeyemis, Mrs. Adeyemi in a green headwrap that seemed to hold all the color the parish had been missing — stopped Bram on the courthouse steps. The old woman took both her hands.

"Twelve years we thought nobody knew," she said. "Twelve years we thought the river parish just... swallowed it. And it was one dying clerk and one taxi woman the whole time."

"Not the whole time," Bram said. "Just at the end."

Mrs. Adeyemi squeezed her hands hard enough to hurt, which Bram understood to be the local dialect for blessing.

* * *

The offers began within the week, which was how Bram learned that visibility, like invisibility, came with paperwork.

The parish council wanted her back first — her *old* parish, the one she'd fled at nineteen with a duffel bag and a name she'd already begun shaving down to initials. There was a ceremony proposed. A plaque. Something about *restored daughter of the community*. The councilman who called her had a voice like warm syrup and used the word *healing* four times in two minutes.

"We'd want you to speak," he said. "About your journey."

"My journey," Bram repeated, watching the dispatch board light up across the office: 4:12 a.m., a nurse off shift at St. Ambrose, a kid stranded outside the bus depot, the usual congregation of the small hours.

"Your return. Your — reclamation."

"I'll think about it," she said, which was what she said to telemarketers, and hung up before he could hear her smile.

The newspaper wanted an interview. A true-crime podcast wanted two hours. A university offered her a fellowship in — she read the letter three times — *narrative erasure and recovery*, which struck her as the kind of thing invented by someone who had never actually erased anything. Erasure wasn't a narrative. It was a thousand small refusals, a signature shortened here, a forwarding address left blank there, until one morning you looked in the mirror and the person looking back was mostly absence wearing your coat.

She put the letters in a drawer. Not the trash — she'd learned that much from Halloran, whose ledger had survived precisely because he was too careful to throw anything away and too frightened to keep it near. The drawer above the radio logs. Let them age. Maybe someday they'd be a story she told on purpose instead of one that happened to her.

* * *

Ada moved in on a Sunday, which was to say: she brought two suitcases and a cast-iron skillet to the flat above the dispatch office and announced, standing in the doorway with the river wind pulling at her scarf, "I've been dead twelve years. I'm not doing apartments alone. Where do you want the skillet?"

Bram, who had spent her adult life ensuring no one knew where she lived, stepped aside and held the door.

The flat was small and smelled of the previous tenant's cigarettes, but by Wednesday it smelled of Ada's cooking — beans and smoked fish and the particular burnt-onion perfume of a woman relearning a kitchen after a decade of safehouse hotplates. By Friday there was a second chair at the table. By the following Tuesday, Bram came down for her shift and found the dispatch office's break room transformed: a proper kettle, a tin of biscuits, a corkboard where drivers pinned photographs of their kids and their dogs and, in one case, a very large cat named Commissioner.

"You're building something," Dezzy said, leaning in the doorway one night, watching Bram reorganize the log shelf by route rather than date. Dezzy had driven the decoy cab the night it was run off the road; her arm was still in a sling, and she wore the sling like a campaign medal. "You know that, right? This isn't a dispatch office anymore. It's got a *kettle*."

"It always had a kettle."

"A sad kettle. A hostage kettle." Dezzy nodded at the corkboard. "That's new. That's a *parish*."

Bram didn't answer, but she didn't take the board down either.

* * *

The night shift taught her things the day never knew. It taught her that the city at 3 a.m. was honest — nobody lied well at 3 a.m.; the drunk wept truthfully, the nurses confessed their doubts, the insomniacs told you their whole lives between corners. It taught her that a dispatcher was a kind of priest, hearing everything and absolving by route. And it taught her, slowly, over months, that the family she'd been avoiding her whole life had been waiting for her in the least family-shaped place imaginable: a room full of radios and tired drivers and one recovering ghost learning to answer to her own name again.

Because that was the work now, the real work. Ada's testimony had ended the trial, but it hadn't ended the twelve years. Some nights Ada woke screaming about headlights, and Bram would find her in the kitchen at 4 a.m. making tea neither of them drank, and they'd sit together while the radio murmured below — *unit fourteen, confirmed, Marsh and Tenth* — and gradually the screaming inside Ada would settle into something more survivable. Other nights Ada laughed, suddenly and enormously, at nothing, at everything, at the fact of beans out of a can tasting better than any meal she'd eaten as a dead woman, and Bram understood that this too was part of it. Resurrection was not an event. It was a practice.

In spring, the parish council tried once more. They sent the plaque anyway — *BRAM OKONKWO, DAUGHTER OF THIS PARISH* — and mounted it on the wall of the old library without asking her. She went to look at it, stood before her own name in brass for a long moment, and felt almost nothing, which was its own kind of miracle. Then she asked them to move it to the dispatch office, because if her name was going to hang anywhere, it should hang where the night shift could see it and know that disappearing wasn't the only way through.

They hung it above the corkboard. Dezzy saluted it daily, ironically, then less ironically.

* * *

On the anniversary of the accident — the real one, the one the newspapers had finally gotten right — Bram and Ada drove out to the river parish bridge at dusk. The same bridge where Bram had once stepped out of a cab and named herself to armed men, trading her invisibility like a coin tossed into dark water. The river ran brown and patient beneath it. Somewhere upstream, the old Halloran house was being sold; his niece had written to say the garden was overgrown but the apple tree still bore.

"He'd have hated the publicity," Ada said. "But he'd have loved being right."

"He was right the whole time," Bram said. "He just wrote it down too late to save himself."

"Not too late," Ada said. "He mailed it. That's not late. That's exactly on time. You just can't always see when the letter lands."

They stood a while longer. Then Ada shivered, and Bram said, "Come on, we've got a pickup at nine," and they walked back to the cab side by side, two women with names, with faces, with a shift to run.

At the office, the board was already lighting up. Dezzy waved from the radio desk. The kettle was on. Above the corkboard, the brass caught the lamplight and threw it around the room like it belonged there.

Bram Okonkwo put on her headset, opened the log book, and wrote her name at the top of the page — full, unhurried, legible — and waited for the night to tell her who needed finding.
