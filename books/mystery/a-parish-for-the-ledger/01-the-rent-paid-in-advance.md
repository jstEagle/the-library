## 1. The Rent Paid in Advance

The rain came in off the Graywater Channel the way it always did in October — sideways, patient, with the air of something that had been sent rather than fallen. It sheeted down the window of Winterbourne's Books in long grey ropes, blurring the harbor lights into smears of amber and rust, and it made the shop feel smaller than it was, the way a coat shrinks when it gets wet.

Silas Winterbourne stood behind the counter with a mug of tea gone cold in her hands and watched the day fail. She was sixty-one years old and had the face of a woman who had spent decades being deliberately unremarkable — a face you'd forget before you finished describing it, which was precisely the point, and had been the point for eleven years now. Grey hair pinned back with whatever was nearest. Cardigan the color of wet slate. Hands that looked like a librarian's and were not.

The shop was dying. She knew it the way you know a tide is going out: not all at once, but by the marks. The waterline of customers crept lower each month. The stock sat heavier on the shelves. Tomás had started leaving his ferry money in the till without being asked, and she'd started letting him, and neither of them had said a word about it, because saying a word about it would have made it real.

She was doing the arithmetic she did every evening — rent, rates, the boiler that coughed like a consumptive — when the bell above the door gave its small brass cough and a man came in out of the rain.

He was gaunt the way winter fields are gaunt. Tall, but folded down into himself, as if he'd spent years trying to take up less space. His coat was good wool gone shiny at the cuffs, and it was soaked through, and he stood in the doorway dripping and looking at her with an expression she recognized from her old life: the look of a man who has rehearsed something in the car and is about to forget all of it.

"We're closing in twenty minutes," Silas said, which was a lie. They'd closed an hour ago. But the shop was hers, and the hours were hers, and lying about small things was how she kept the large things at bay.

"I won't be long," the man said. His voice was dry paper. He came to the counter without browsing, which she noted — people who browse are buying time; people who don't have come for one thing only. "Are you the owner?"

"I'm the shop."

He blinked at that. Then, to her mild surprise, he almost smiled. "That's a good answer."

He set a wet envelope on the counter between them. Not a letter — an envelope, thick, sealed, and heavier than paper had any right to be. He kept two fingers on it as though it might walk off.

"Mrs. Winterbourne. My name is Edmund Vail. I need to hire you."

"I sell books."

"I know what you sell." He glanced at the shelves — the poetry section, the local history, the shelf of maritime curiosities Wren kept dusted and nobody ever bought. "I've been asking about you for three weeks. The ferryman. The woman at the parish office. A man at the Crown who owed me money and paid me in information instead."

The cold tea sat forgotten in Silas's hands. Three weeks. Three weeks of asking about a woman who had spent eleven years making sure nobody had anything to ask. The questions must have been careful ones — Tomás would have deflected, Mrs. Okonkwo would have prayed about it first, Wren would have lied beautifully — but somewhere, someone had said enough.

"What kind of information," Silas said, "does a man at the Crown sell?"

"The useful kind." Vail's fingers tightened on the envelope. "Mrs. Winterbourne, I know what you were. Before. I'm not here to expose you. I'm here because I need someone who knows how to look at a thing and see what it is."

"And what thing is that?"

"A murder," Edmund Vail said. "That hasn't happened yet."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere in the back, a pipe knocked twice against the wall — the boiler's dying percussion — and Wren's cat, a scarred grey thing called Bishop, uncoiled from the windowsill and fixed Vail with the flat yellow appraisal he gave to everyone except Silas.

Silas set down the mug. "That's not a sentence that means anything."

"It means everything," Vail said. "It's the only sentence that means anything anymore. I've been told I'm going to be murdered, Mrs. Winterbourne. Not threatened — told. Written down. Dated. And I want to hire you to prove it isn't going to happen." He leaned in, and up close she could see what the gauntness was made of: weeks of not sleeping, of checking locks, of jumping at the postman. "Because if I can prove it isn't true, then I can stop being afraid. And if I can't—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Then at least I'll know I wasn't mad."

"Written down where?"

He hesitated. It was a long hesitation, the kind with weight behind it, and Silas watched him decide — watched the calculation move behind his eyes like weather. Whatever he was weighing, it was heavier than his fear. That interested her, and she distrusted being interested. Interest was the first crack in a hiding place.

"A ledger," he said finally. "An old parish ledger. From a church that isn't there anymore."

Something moved in Silas's chest, small and cold, like a fish turning under ice. She kept her face still. She was very good at keeping her face still; she'd had practice, once, keeping it still over worse things than a stranger's ravings.

"There's no church at Graywater Point," she said. "There's a ruin. It drowned with the churchyard a century ago."

"The ledger says otherwise." Vail pushed the envelope an inch toward her. "I'll be honest with you, because I think honesty is all I have left. I bought that ledger through this shop. Four months ago. Mail order, from your stock lists. And three weeks ago a copy of one of its pages arrived at my house with my name on it and a date. The eleventh of November. This November."

Silas looked at the envelope. "And you think I sent it."

"I think whoever did used your shop the way a thief uses a doorway. I think you didn't know. But I think you'll want to know. Because I've read that ledger, Mrs. Winterbourne, and the handwriting that wrote my death is the same handwriting that wrote nine other names before mine, going back sixty years, and every one of those people is dead."

Bishop jumped down from the sill and left the room with the affronted dignity of a creature refusing to be present for nonsense. The rain thickened. Down at the harbor, a foghorn spoke once, low and mournful, like the sea clearing its throat before it said something unpleasant.

Silas looked at Edmund Vail for a long moment. He was either mad, or lying, or telling the truth, and the three possibilities wore the same wet coat. But there was one thing she knew about madmen and liars: they rarely looked at you with this much terror and this much courtesy at the same time. Fear made people rude. His fear had made him careful. That was a trained kind of fear. That was a man who had been afraid for a long time and had learned manners around it.

"Open the envelope," she said.

He did. Inside was cash — old notes, counted, banded — and beneath it a folded paper. He slid the cash aside and unfolded the paper and turned it so she could read it. It was a receipt. Her shop's name at the top, her own printed terms of sale, and a line item: *one antique ledger, parish records, Graywater Parish, 19th c. — sold, £140.*

Dated four months ago. Her own stock code in the corner, in her own handwriting.

She remembered it, too. That was the worst of it. She remembered the box it had come in, bought at the Marsh estate sale, one of forty lots she'd never fully catalogued. She remembered Wren complaining about the smell of it. She remembered selling it to a dealer up-country and being glad to see the back of it.

"I didn't send you anything," she said.

"No," Vail agreed. "But it came from here. And the page that came to my house — the one with my death on it — was written on paper that matches this ledger exactly. I had it looked at. The ink is old. The paper is old. Mrs. Winterbourne, either someone has a time machine, or someone has been writing deaths in a hundred-year-old book and making them come true, and either way, I would very much like to pay you to find out which."

Silas was quiet. The shop was quiet. Outside, the tide was turning, and she could hear it — that long, low exhalation of the harbor as the water began its slow withdrawal, uncovering the mudflats, the old mooring posts, the things the sea keeps and the things it gives back.

She thought about the three cases she had solved since coming here, the ones the police had called accidents — the fall from the cliff path, the carbon monoxide in the houseboat, the drowning at the slip. She thought about how each time she had found the truth and folded it away, small and private, like a letter never posted, because the truth is a dangerous thing to own out loud. She had built a life on the principle that the safest place for a dangerous thing is inside a quiet woman in a dying bookshop at the end of the world.

And now a wet, terrified stranger was standing in her shop asking her to take a dangerous thing out and hold it up to the light.

"Say your date again," she said. "The eleventh of November."

"The eleventh of November. Three weeks from today."

"And you came to me now."

"I came to you the day I got the page," Vail said. "But I spent two weeks trying to make myself believe I was imagining it." His laugh was terrible — a small, dry sound with no floor under it. "I'm not imagining it, am I? You believe me. I can see it."

"I believe you're afraid," Silas said. "Fear is evidence of something, but not necessarily of what it claims."

She looked down at the money. A year's rent — she could see it at a glance, she'd been counting rent in her head for eleven years. More than a year's rent. Enough to fix the boiler. Enough to keep the lights on through the dead of winter when nobody buys poetry. Enough to keep the shop alive, and the shop was not only a shop; it was the roof over Wren's attic room and Mrs. Okonkwo's Tuesday readings and Tomás's chair by the fire and all the loose, improbable, beloved machinery of the life she had built out of other people's wreckage.

Taking the money meant being seen. Being seen meant being findable. Every instinct she had, honed across a career that had ended in a way she never spoke of, told her to say no. To tell the man to go to the police. To watch him walk out into the rain and let the tide of ordinary life close over the whole strange episode.

But she had spent three quiet years learning that the police in this town called things accidents when they were not, and she had never once learned why, and the not-knowing had sat in her like a splinter.

"The eleventh of November," she said slowly. "You want me to prove that no murder happens to you on the eleventh of November."

"I want you to prove it *hasn't happened yet*," Vail said. "Those are different things, Mrs. Winterbourne. Anyone can wait and see if I die. I need someone to find out whether it was ever truly going to — whether it's already been *arranged*. Because I've come to believe that in this town, being written down is the same as being dead. I just don't know who holds the pen."

The foghorn spoke again. Bishop reappeared on the stairs and sat regarding them both with the patience of something that had seen strangers come and go for years and expected to go on seeing them.

Silas looked at the money. She looked at the receipt in her own handwriting. She looked at Edmund Vail's hands, trembling very slightly on the counter, and she thought: *this man has already begun to grieve himself, and no one has helped him do anything else.*

She should say no.

She heard herself say: "I'll need the ledger."

"It's at my house. You can come tomorrow. I'll be home all day." He almost smiled again, and for a moment the gauntness fell away and she saw the man he must have been before — someone quick, someone funny, someone who had not yet been made into an envelope of cash and a rehearsed speech. "I'll make tea. It'll be terrible. I'm told that's what you do when you hire a detective."

"I'm not a detective," Silas said. "I'm a bookseller."

"Of course," said Edmund Vail, and there was something in the way he said it — something light and sad and final, like a man setting down a weight he'd carried too far — that she would think about later, much later, when remembering it would do no good at all. "My mistake."

He wrote an address on the flyleaf of a paperback of Tennyson from the sale cart — she'd remember that too, the absurd domestic detail of it, the pencil, the careful block letters — wished her good evening, and went out into the rain. The bell coughed behind him. Through the streaming window she watched him walk down toward the harbor road, hunched into his wet coat, growing smaller and greyer until the dark took him, and she had the odd, unshakeable feeling that she had just watched a man walk out of a story that had already been finished without him.

She stood a long while. Then she picked up the envelope, weighed it in her hand — a year of boiler repairs, a year of Wren's attic, a year of not failing — and carried it to the back room, where the safe was, and the kettle, and the long night's arithmetic.

Wren came down the stairs around ten, all elbows and suspicion, seventeen years old and sharp as a tack in a tin. "Who was the posh ghost?"

"A customer."

"Nobody's a customer at closing time. What did he buy?"

"Nothing," Silas said. "He sold."

Wren waited, the way she did, with her whole body. Silas put the kettle on and did not explain, and Wren did not ask again, because they had built a household on exactly that kind of mutual mercy. But as Silas poured the water she caught her own reflection in the dark window — a grey woman in a grey shop, holding a grey cup — and behind the reflection, faint as a watermark, the harbor lights, and beyond them the black line of Graywater Point, where a drowned church kept its drowned dead.

*Prove that no murder has happened yet.*

She drank her tea and thought about the eleventh of November, three weeks away, and about a ledger sold twice, and about the strange cold feeling — unwelcome, familiar, almost like hunger — that for the first time in eleven years, someone had come to her door with a question she did not already know the answer to.

She should have said no.

She fell asleep in the chair by the fire with the envelope under her hand like a stone, and dreamed, for the first time in years, of water rising.
