## 10. What the Tide Gave Back

The storm came up the coast like something with a grudge.

Tomás Ferro had lived on Graywater his whole life, forty-one years of it, and he could read the sea the way Mrs. Okonkwo read scripture — slowly, with attention to what was being threatened between the lines. By Tuesday evening the barometer at the harbour office had fallen so far the needle looked embarrassed, and the gulls had gone inland in a single grey ribbon, and old Petros Mavromichalis had stood at the end of his pontoon with his hands in his pockets and said, in the flat tone he saved for certainties, "She'll take something this time. She always does, when she blows from the northeast."

Tomás had tied his boat down with double lines anyway, and then a third, because the *Santa Lucia* was the only honest thing he owned and he had learned the hard way that the sea respects nothing so much as a man who assumes the worst.

He had not slept. Nobody in Graywater slept through a northeaster. The wind found every gap in every wall and played the houses like organ pipes, and the rain came sideways, and somewhere past midnight the church bell at St. Brendan's — a bell that had not rung in years, that had no business ringing — began to swing in the gale and toll, arrhythmically, like a heart in trouble. Tomás lay in his narrow bed above the bait shop and listened to it and thought, not for the first time, that the drowned church out at the Point had never really been drowned at all. It had only been waiting.

By dawn the storm had spent itself and gone out to sea the way a drunk leaves a party, loudly and without apology, and the town came out to count its losses. A greenhouse gone from the allotments. The harbour road under two feet of shingle. Petros's pontoon folded like paper. And out at Graywater Point, where the Harrow family had bought the shoreline and fenced it and posted signs that said *PRIVATE — NO LANDING*, the sea had done something the sea does perhaps once in a generation.

It had given something back.

---

Tomás heard about it from Wren, who heard about it from the postman, who had gone out along the coast road at first light because the post does not stop for weather, and had come back white as his own sacks.

"There's a coffin," Wren said, standing in the doorway of the bait shop with her hood still up and rainwater pooling around her boots, her eyes too bright in her thin face. "Out at the Point. On the flats where the old churchyard used to be. The storm pulled the whole bank away and — Tomás, there's a coffin just *sitting* there, and it's open, and it's not — " She stopped. Started again. "It's not what coffins are for."

Tomás was already reaching for his oilskins. "Is Silas out there?"

"Nobody's out there. The road's washed out on the landward side and the Harrow fence is down and there's a sign, but nobody's — " Wren caught his look and her jaw set. "You're going. I'm coming."

"No."

"Yes." She said it the way she said everything, flat and fast and final, a girl who had learned on the streets of three cities that the moment you let someone else decide whether you come along is the moment you get left. "You'll need someone to hold the light. And if it's what I think it is, you'll need someone who can take photographs with her phone before anyone comes to make it disappear."

Tomás looked at her for a long moment. Sixteen years old, all elbows and stubbornness, a runaway Silas had taken in off the shop floor two winters ago and never quite managed to give back. He thought about arguing. He thought about the fact that he had, for eleven months now, been taking money from a man he'd never met to *not* ask questions, and what that money had made of him, and how Wren would look at him if she ever knew.

"Stay behind me on the flats," he said. "The ground out there lies. What looks solid isn't."

"I know," Wren said. "Everything about this town looks solid and isn't."

---

The *Santa Lucia* took them around the headland through a sea still heaving with the storm's leftovers, long grey swells rolling in from the northeast with a mile between their crests, and Tomás ran the engine slow and watched the Point come up out of the haze.

He had ferried Silas out here once before, three weeks ago, and the memory of that crossing sat in his chest like a swallowed stone. She had stood in the bow with her collar up, looking at the drowned church the way you look at a grave you're not sure is empty, and she had asked him, in that quiet voice of hers that made questions feel like knives being laid on a table, *who told you to stop asking about the Point, Tomás?* And he had lied to her, or half-lied, and she had let him, and that was worse.

The church itself had been drowning for a century and more — the sea had eaten the cliff out from under St. Brendan's-by-the-Sea sometime in the 1890s, the story went, and the parish had simply let it go, moved up the hill, built a new one with better drainage. What remained at low water was a suggestion: a few ribs of wall, the stump of a tower, a scatter of stones that might once have been graves, all of it black with weed and only visible for an hour or two on the lowest tides. The locals didn't go. The locals had stopped going long before the Harrows bought the shoreline, which was the thing Tomás had never managed to explain to himself. You don't avoid a ruin for a hundred years unless someone taught you to.

But the storm had redrawn the map. Where the churchyard bank had been, the sea had peeled back the earth like skin off a fruit, and there on the newly bared flats, in the grey morning light, lay the coffin.

It was not a modern coffin. It was lead-lined and oak-cased, the kind they buried sea captains and bishops in, and it had been thrown clear of the eroded ground and left on its side on the shingle, and its lid — heavy, banded with iron gone to lace — had been wrenched off by the same violence that had unearthed it.

And it was full of books.

Tomás beached the boat on the shingle spit above the tide line and stood looking at it, and felt the hair rise on his arms under the oilskin.

"Those aren't books," Wren said beside him. "Tomás. Those are *ledgers*. Parish ledgers. I've been sorting the shop stock for a year, I know what a parish ledger looks like, and there are — " she was counting, her lips moving — "there are dozens."

Dozens was right. The coffin was packed with them, stacked flat like cordwood, spines outward, and even at twenty feet Tomás could see the leather had been treated with something — waxed or tarred — because the sea had been at them and not won. The topmost ledger had fallen open where it lay on the shingle, its pages spread to the sky, and the rain of the retreating storm had soaked it into a dark swollen mass, ink bleeding, but the pages beneath — the ones still pressed under the weight of the stack — would be dry. He knew it the way you know things without wanting to. Someone had prepared this coffin the way sailors prepare a boat. Someone had intended it to survive the water.

"Nobody buries books in a coffin," Wren said. Her voice had gone thin. "You bury *people*. Unless — " She stopped.

"Unless the books are the person," Tomás said. "Unless the books *are* what was alive."

He got the tarpaulin off the boat's locker and they worked fast, because the tide was already turning and the flats at Graywater Point did not forgive. Wren photographed everything first, standing over the coffin with her phone held steady in both hands while her breath shook, shooting the stack, the open ledger, the iron bands, the way the eroded bank behind it showed the clean cross-section of earth where the storm had cut — and Tomás saw, in that cross-section, lower down, the dark shapes of more coffins. Two. Three. A whole row of them, going back into the bank like books on a shelf, like a family crypt that had never had a church above it.

"Tomás." Wren's voice had gone very quiet. "There are more."

"I see them."

"This isn't a grave. It's an *archive*."

He wrapped the topmost ledgers in the tarp — six of them, dry under their waxed covers, heavy as ballast — and they carried them up to the boat in two trips, Wren insisting on the second trip when he told her to stay with the first, and the whole time the back of Tomás's neck crawled with the feeling of being watched. There was nothing to see. The fence was down, the Harrow signs were face-down in the shingle, the headland was empty gorse and gulls. But the feeling did not leave, and it was an old feeling, a childhood feeling — the feeling of standing somewhere the town had agreed, without ever saying so, that you did not stand.

His grandfather had taken him fishing along this shore when he was seven. His grandfather, who would take him anywhere, had stood at the top of the beach at Graywater Point and would not come down onto the flats, and when Tomás had asked why, the old man had looked out at the drowned tower for a long time and said only: *Because the Harrows keep their accounts out here, and it's not healthy to read another man's accounts.* Tomás had thought it was about money. He had been seven. He had thought everything was about money.

He was thirty-one years older now and he understood that his grandfather had meant it exactly, and had meant more.

---

They ran back around the headland with the swells following them like slow grey hounds, and Tomás kept the ledgers wrapped in the tarp in the stern and did not look at them, and Wren sat with her hand on the bundle the whole way like a shepherd with a lamb, and neither of them spoke until the harbour wall was close enough to read the tide gauge on it.

"Silas sold a ledger," Wren said suddenly. "Last month. The antique one, the big parish ledger with the brass corners — a man came in and bought it, cash, and I remember because Silas looked at it for a long time before she let it go, and she doesn't look at stock. And then — " Wren's face changed. Tomás watched the thought arrive in her like weather. "And then it was gone from the buyer's house. Edmund Vail's house. When we went through it, after — it wasn't there. I noticed and I didn't think about it because there was so much else."

"Say the rest," Tomás said, because he could hear that there was a rest.

"The pages were facsimiles. I checked. The paper was too new. Someone bought a ledger out of our shop and put copies back in it and — " She looked down at the bundle in the stern. "And the real one might be in there. With all its brothers. A hundred years of them."

The harbour took them in. The gulls screamed. Tomás tied off the *Santa Lucia* with hands that were steadier than he had any right to expect them to be, and lifted the bundle, and it was heavier than ballast, heavier than rope, heavier than anything he'd carried across this water in eleven years of ferrying. It was the weight of a town's whole secret, and it had been sitting under the sea at the end of a beach his family had known to avoid for three generations, and now it was in his arms in broad daylight.

He thought about the money. Eleven months of it, in cash, in an envelope left in his bait bucket on the first Tuesday of every month, for a service that had never once been described to him in words — only in a look, in a handshake, in the phrase *you're a sensible man, Ferro, and sensible men don't ask about the Point.* He thought about what Silas would say, in her quiet way that was not quiet at all. He thought about his grandfather on the top of the beach.

He carried the ledgers up the harbour steps and did not put them down.

---

The bookshop smelled of rain and old paper and the paraffin heater Silas refused to replace. She was behind the counter with her reading glasses pushed up into her greying hair, and when Tomás set the bundle on the counter and unwrapped the tarp, she went very still — that particular stillness he had learned to fear and trust in equal measure, the stillness of a woman whose mind had just left the room at speed.

"Where," she said.

"Graywater Point. The storm opened the churchyard bank. There's a coffin — " his voice caught on it and he made himself finish — "there's a coffin full of these. And more coffins behind it in the earth, a whole row. We took six. There could be thirty. Fifty."

Silas took off her glasses. She looked at the ledgers the way she looked at everything — as if reading a sentence written across them that only she could see — and then she opened the top one, and turned pages with a gentleness that did not match her face at all, and Tomás watched the colour leave her hands.

"Wren," she said. "Get the hymnal. And the lamp. And lock the door — the front one and the back one."

Wren was already moving.

Silas smoothed a page flat with two fingers. The entries were in the same cramped clerical hand Tomás had glimpsed once before, in the ledger that had come and gone through this shop like a ghost, and beside the dates and the names were columns of figures and strings of small coded marks, patient as stitching. She read for a long time. The heater ticked. Outside, the town went about its business, sweeping up shingle, righting fences, saying *terrible storm, terrible* to one another in the warm ordinary voices of people who did not know what had come up out of the water.

"Sixty years," Silas said at last, to no one, to the page. "Maybe more. They didn't stop. They never stopped." She looked up, and her eyes were the coldest Tomás had ever seen them, colder than the sea that morning. "Tomás. This one is dated 1974. And the entry beside the name — the one that's not a name, the coded one — I've seen that mark before. It's the mark that was next to Edmund Vail."

The lamp hissed. Wren stood in the doorway with the hymnal hugged to her chest, and Tomás saw her look at the ledger, and look at Silas, and understand — the way you understand a wave is coming, before it comes — that whatever was written in those books was not finished being written.

"They kept their accounts," Tomás said. "My grandfather used to say it. I thought he meant money."

"He meant everything," Silas said. "Get the lamp closer. We're going to read every page before anyone comes to take them back — and Tomás — " she paused, and the pause had a knife in it — "someone will come. The storm took the fence down and the whole town saw that beach. We have until dark. Maybe until lunch."

Wren locked the door. The lamp came closer. And the three of them bent over a dead family's memory, while outside the tide crept back in over Graywater Point to cover what they had left behind — and somewhere across the harbour, Tomás would learn later, a well-dressed man was already making a telephone call.
