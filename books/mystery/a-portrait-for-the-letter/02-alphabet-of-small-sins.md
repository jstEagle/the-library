## 2. Alphabet of Small Sins

The envelope stayed in my bottom drawer overnight, which was itself a small sin. Dispatch protocol said anything unusual got logged, escalated, passed up the chain within the hour. But I had learned — twenty years of night shifts will teach you this if nothing else — that the chain exists mostly to pass things down until they land on whoever can be blamed for them. And the envelope had been addressed to me. Not to Hollowmere Taxi & Car Service. Not to *the night dispatcher*. To Ada Oyelaran, in handwriting so careful it looked printed.

So at 6:14 a.m., when the day driver came in smelling of coffee and complaint, and the office belonged to me for one more hour, I took the envelope out and put it on my desk under the lamp, and I did what I do with everything.

I filed it.

Not officially. Officially it went into the incident log as "prepaid booking — special instructions — pending review," which is a sentence designed to make any supervisor's eyes slide off it like rain off waxed canvas. The real filing happened in the ledger I keep in the second drawer down, the one with no label, the one that has never appeared on an inventory list because as far as the company knows, it doesn't exist.

Twenty years of ledgers. Twenty years of nights. Every call that came through this office, logged twice — once for the company, once for me. The company's version records what Hollowmere wants remembered. Mine records what actually happened. The gap between those two versions is where I have lived my entire adult life.

I started with Calder Row.

There are fourteen houses on it, though only eleven have been occupied in my time, and the street dies into scrubland past number 12, so anything numbered 14 is either new or a lie. My ledger's index is alphabetical by street, because streets don't change their minds about their names the way people do. Calder Row ran from 1998 forward in my own cramped hand, and I ran my finger down the entries the way you'd run a hand along a fence in the dark, counting posts.

Most of it was nothing. A pickup from number 3 at two in the morning, 2004, fare paid in coins counted out twice. A standing weekly booking from number 9, an old man going to dialysis, four years of Tuesdays until the Tuesdays stopped. Domestic disputes called in from number 7 that were really requests for someone to just be present while a woman packed a bag. I'd driven some of those myself, back before I stopped driving and started dispatching. You learn what a house sounds like from its calls. Number 7 sounded like a kettle always just about to boil.

Number 14 appeared exactly once before last week.

March 2019. A call at 11:52 p.m., a woman's voice, low and fast, asking for a car to the bus station, no luggage, cash on arrival. The company log says the call was disconnected after forty seconds. My ledger says otherwise. My ledger says the call lasted six minutes and eleven seconds, because I kept the line open, because something in the woman's voice made me want to keep her talking, and because keeping people talking is the closest thing to rescue I know how to perform from behind a desk.

She hadn't given a name. She'd given a destination — the 5:40 coach south — and then she'd said, right before hanging up, in a voice I have replayed maybe a thousand times since: *"If anyone asks, I was never here."*

And then, three days later, Nell Barrow died.

* * *

Everyone in Hollowmere remembers the Barrow fire the way towns remember things — loosely, sentimentally, wrong. A space heater, they say. An old house on the edge of the industrial quarter, a young woman who didn't make it out. There was a collection plate at St. Brigid's. There was a paragraph in the *Hollowmere Courier* between the church notices and the classifieds, because Nell Barrow was nobody important, and dead nobodies get paragraphs, not headlines.

But here is what my ledger says, and my ledger does not care what a town prefers to remember:

April 2nd, 2019, 11:52 p.m. — call from 14 Calder Row. Female caller. Six minutes eleven seconds.

April 5th, 2019 — Nell Barrow reported deceased. Fire at the Calder Row property. Cause listed as accidental.

And then — and this is the part I sat looking at for a long time, long enough that the lamp buzzed and the morning shift arrived and left again and the light outside went from grey to white without my noticing —

May 14th, 2019, 1:03 a.m. — call placed to our dispatch line from a payphone at the depot. Caller declined to give a name. Asked one question: *"Did anyone come asking about a woman who called in March?"* Then hung up.

August 30th, 2020, 11:47 p.m. — same payphone. Same voice, older by a year and a half, rougher. No question this time. Just breathing for nine seconds, and then: *"You still there?"* And I said yes, I'm always here, and she said, *"Good,"* and hung up.

February 21st, 2022, 2:15 a.m. — same payphone. Silence for thirty seconds. Then, almost too quiet to record even in my own memory: *"Don't look for me."*

Three calls. Three years apart, then two, then — nothing. Until now. Until an envelope with exact-change instructions and a murder scheduled like a dentist appointment.

Nell Barrow had been dead for three years, officially, irreversibly, with a paragraph in the paper and a plate passed at church. And she had called me from a payphone forty feet from my own window, twice, maybe three times, and I had never once connected the voice to the name, because the name was filed under D for Deceased, and the dead don't call dispatch at two in the morning.

Except they do. They did. She did.

* * *

I want to explain what that felt like, because it wasn't fear, not at first. It was the thing I live for and would deny living for if you asked me directly: the click. The moment when twenty separate facts that have been sitting in separate drawers of separate cabinets suddenly align, and a pattern appears that was there all along, invisible only because no one — including me — had ever laid the pages side by side.

No one else could have seen it. That's not vanity; it's arithmetic. The company log says the March call was forty seconds. Only my ledger knows it was six minutes. The company log lists the fire as accidental. Only my ledger holds the voice that came back from the ashes three times to check whether anyone was listening. The police file, whatever it contains, sits in a building I've never had reason to enter. The newspaper paragraph is microfiche now, fading. Every thread of this ran through one pair of hands, and the hands were mine.

For about ten minutes I felt like the smartest woman in Hollowmere.

Then I felt the other thing. The thing underneath.

Because the pattern didn't end with the phone calls. I pulled the thread further, the way you do, and the way you shouldn't. I went back through my ledgers to the weeks after the fire, cross-referencing, and found that in April 2019 — the month Nell Barrow burned — our little company had taken on a contract we'd never had before: a monthly retainer from Voss Industrial Holdings, for "priority transport services." Edmund Voss. The benefactor. The man whose name is on the library wing and the children's playground and the new wing of the clinic, the man who stood at the memorial for the warehouse workers two winters ago with his head bowed just low enough to be photographed.

Voss Industrial Holdings paid us eleven hundred pounds a month, every month, for five years, and in all that time I could find — I checked, entry by entry, night by night — perhaps a dozen actual fares charged against the account. A car to the airport once. A car from the golf club twice. The rest of it, the eleven hundred pounds a month, bought nothing I could see.

Or bought something I couldn't see. Something that needed a taxi company on retainer the way a burglar needs a getaway driver who doesn't ask questions. And my employer — my mild, tired, overextended employer, Gerry, who signs the rota and complains about fuel prices — had accepted that money without once, in five years, mentioning it to me, the woman who reconciles every invoice that crosses this desk.

Gerry knew. Or Gerry didn't want to know. In a town like Hollowmere those are the same thing.

* * *

By noon I had the ledgers spread across the back room, the good ones, the private ones, and I was building a timeline on the back of a duty roster with a red pen, and I had forgotten to eat, which never happens, because eating at regular intervals is one of the load-bearing walls of my invisibility. Routine is armor. I was out of uniform.

The timeline read like this:

A woman calls from 14 Calder Row and asks to disappear. Three days later she burns. The property belongs — I checked the rate book, the one listing commercial accounts — to a shell company that traces back, through two names I don't recognize and one I do, to Voss. The town mourns briefly and moves on. Voss's fortune, which was already large, becomes enormous; the warehouse expansion breaks ground eight months later on land adjacent to the Calder Row lot. A taxi company starts receiving money for services it doesn't render. And the dead woman calls a payphone near the depot to ask whether anyone came looking.

Someone had come looking. Someone always comes looking. The question the envelope was really asking me — the question underneath the prepaid fare and the precise instructions — was whether I was the kind of person who looked, or the kind who filed and forgot.

I had spent twenty years being the second kind. It had kept me safe. It had kept me *nothing*, too, but I'd made my peace with nothing; nothing and I had an arrangement.

I looked at the timeline. At the top of the page, in my own red ink, I had written the date of the scheduled ride — ten days from now — and beneath it, the address: 14 Calder Row. And beneath that, because my hand apparently knew something my mind hadn't caught up to yet, I had written a single word:

*Nell.*

The door to the front office opened. Gerry's footsteps, and then Gerry's voice, casual in the way that isn't casual at all:

"Ada? You got a minute? There's something I need to talk to you about."

I closed the ledger before he reached the doorway.
