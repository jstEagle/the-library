## 9. Every Call Recorded

The radio on Ada's hip had been live since the moment she turned the ignition.

She'd done it without ceremony, the way she did everything — a small toggle of the channel selector, a flick of her thumb over the transmit lock so it stayed open. Channel four. The old all-call frequency that every patrol car, every depot mechanic, every insomniac night worker in Hollowmere kept murmuring in the background like a second heartbeat. Nobody monitored it closely anymore; it was mostly static and weather and the occasional drunk calling in a lost dog. But it was open. It was always open. And for twenty years Ada Oyelaran had been the only person who understood what that meant: that anything spoken into it was not heard by one person but witnessed by a town.

Voss's man — his name was Grell, and he smelled of expensive cologne laid over cheap sweat — sat in the passenger seat with a pistol resting against his thigh, casual as a folded newspaper. Nell was in the back, hands zip-tied in front of her, breathing in the small controlled rhythm Ada recognized from her own mirror. *We are calm. We are invisible. We will survive this.*

"Fourteen Calder Row," Grell said. "Straight there. No detours."

"Of course," Ada said. Her voice came out level, dispatcher-flat, the voice she'd used ten thousand times to tell frightened people that help was coming. She pulled away from the depot into the wet dark. Rain needled the windshield. The wipers kept their patient count — one, two, one, two — and beneath them, faintly, the radio hissed.

She wondered who was listening tonight. Okafor in the patrol car on Route 9, probably, nursing his thermos. Marcy at the hospital switchboard, who never turned her set off because the static reminded her of her father's workshop. The kid who ran the all-night counter at the Texaco. Hollowmere's night shift, scattered across the dark like embers nobody had bothered to stamp out.

*Listen,* she thought at them. *Please, whoever you are. Listen.*

* * *

Nell Barrow had died once already, and she remembered every second of it.

She remembered the smoke coming under the door of the records office at Voss Freight, thick and sweet, and Edmund Voss himself turning the key in the outer door — she'd seen it through the wired glass, his face lit orange and utterly serene, a man closing up shop for the evening. She remembered clawing the window until her nails split. She remembered the fire brigade pulling out a body burned past recognition and the coroner, three weeks later, signing a name that was not hers onto a death certificate, because everyone who could contradict him had been paid or frightened or both.

Three years of being no one. Three years of names like coats, changed seasonally. And now here she was in the back of a taxi, bound, being driven by the quiet dispatcher whose ledgers she'd once called from a payphone in Ashford just to hear a human voice say *Hollowmere* — driven to the house where Edmund Voss intended to finish his bookkeeping.

She should have run when she felt the net tightening. She'd felt it for weeks — the man asking questions at the laundromat, the car parked too long outside her rooming house. Instead she'd come to the depot, because something in the dispatcher's flat careful voice had sounded like a person who also counted locks at night.

Now Nell watched the back of Ada Oyelaran's head and saw what Grell did not: the woman's left hand, resting on the gearshift, thumb pressed against the transmit button in slow, deliberate pulses. Tap. Pause. Tap-tap. The old depot signal code, the one every driver learned in their first week and every dispatcher used to say *all units acknowledge* without saying it.

Ada was talking to the town in the only language the town still spoke.

* * *

At 14 Calder Row, the rain had stopped and the house stood lit from within, gold and hospitable, like every lie Edmund Voss had ever told.

He met them on the porch himself. That was the thing about powerful men, Nell thought — they grew tired of intermediaries right at the moment it became fatal to them. He was seventy-one and straight-backed, wearing a cardigan the color of oatmeal, and he looked at Nell with an expression she had spent three years trying to forget: mild regret, the look of a man about to put down a dog he'd once been fond of.

"Miss Barrow." He said it gently. "You've caused a great deal of trouble for a dead woman."

Grell walked her up the steps. Ada followed, hands visible, moving like a woman made of apology. Voss barely glanced at her. That was the mistake. That was the whole mistake, right there, in the space between the porch light and the front door: he looked through Ada Oyelaran the way everyone in Hollowmere had looked through her for twenty years, the way you look through furniture, through weather, through the night itself.

Inside, the hallway smelled of lemon polish and old money. Voss gestured toward the study with the weary courtesy of a host.

"I want to understand," he said, "how it went wrong. Indulge an old man."

"You locked the doors," Nell said. Her voice didn't shake. She'd rehearsed this sentence in eleven rented rooms. "October the ninth. You locked the doors yourself. I watched you do it through the glass, and then you stood there and watched the windows go black."

Voss sighed, folding his hands. "I built a town," he said. "A library. A clinic. A hockey rink with your name on a plaque, Miss Barrow — well, not your name, obviously. Do you know what this valley was before me? Nothing. A place people left. I gave it everything, and all I asked—" his voice frayed, just slightly, the first honest thread in the weave— "was that one fire stay a fire. One woman stay dead. Is that so much?"

"No," said a third voice, flat and clear and coming from everywhere at once. "It isn't."

Everyone turned. Ada stood by the study door with the radio lifted from her belt, held up like a small black lantern.

"It isn't so much," she repeated, "and that's why everyone's listening."

* * *

In the patrol car on Route 9, Officer Okafor sat up so fast his thermos went over.

He knew the voice. Everyone knew the voice — twenty years of *unit twelve, proceed to Mill Street*, twenty years of the calmest woman in Hollowmere reading out the town's worst nights. But she had never once, not once, spoken on the open channel like this, unbidden, unlogged, her voice filling the cab of his cruiser as if she were sitting in the passenger seat.

"—confessing now, on record, October ninth warehouse fire, Voss Freight, victim Nell Barrow, alive and present, subject Edmund Voss stating intent—"

Okafor grabbed his handset. Then stopped. Because he understood, with the sudden cold clarity of a man handed something breakable, that if he keyed his mic, if he interrupted, he might shatter whatever spell she was weaving — and that every other unit, every depot hand, every night nurse and gas station clerk in the valley was hearing exactly what he was hearing.

He put the handset down. He drove. Fast, lights off, toward Calder Row, and behind him he could hear — distantly, impossibly — other engines starting. The Texaco kid's truck. The ambulance crew at the station. Marcy at the hospital, her voice low and steady on the line: *"All units, be advised, dispatch is transmitting from an off-site unit, stand by."*

Stand by. As if the whole town had been waiting twenty years for permission.

* * *

"You're broadcasting," Voss said. He hadn't raised his voice. Powerful men rarely needed to. "That's a crime, I believe. Misuse of emergency frequencies."

"So is murder," Ada said. "But mine has witnesses."

She saw it happen in his face then — the calculation running and failing, running and failing, the arithmetic of a lifetime suddenly refusing to balance. Twenty years she had studied voices the way other people studied faces, and she heard the exact moment Edmund Voss understood that the room had changed shape around him. That the walls of his study had become, in some incorporeal way, the walls of every kitchen and cruiser and depot bunkroom in Hollowmere.

"Grell," he said quietly.

"Don't," Ada said, and her voice did something it had never done in two decades of service: it cracked, and she let it. "Grell. You're on the channel too. Everything you do next is going out to forty people, fifty, maybe more, and every one of them is writing down the time. I know how my town works at night, Mr. Voss. I've filed it for twenty years. Nobody in Hollowmere does a bad thing after midnight anymore without wondering if I heard. Well. They heard."

Grell's gun hand drifted downward, degree by degree, like a man lowering a heavy suitcase he'd finally admitted he couldn't carry.

And then the headlights came.

They swept across the study windows one after another — cruiser, ambulance, the Texaco truck, a pickup with a snowplow blade that belonged to nobody official at all — pulling up the drive in a loose silent procession, engines ticking, doors opening, boots on gravel. Ordinary people in ordinary jackets, standing in the wet dark, looking up at the lit windows of the benefactor's house.

Nell Barrow stepped forward to the window. She wanted them to see her face. She had been a body in a file, a signature on a certificate, a rumor, a ghost, a name scratched off a payroll — and now she stood in full light, alive, in front of the whole watching town, and raised her tied hands slightly, like a woman waving across a street.

"That's her," someone said outside, loud enough to carry. "That's Nell Barrow. My dad worked that fire. We thought—" The voice broke off.

Edmund Voss sat down slowly in his own leather chair, in his own study, in the house his fortune had bought with a locked door and a struck match, and put his face in his hands.

Ada lowered the radio. Her arm was shaking. Twenty years of steadiness, spent in nine minutes.

Okafor appeared in the doorway, breathless, handset in hand, and looked at her with an expression she would remember for the rest of her life — not gratitude exactly, and not surprise, but recognition. The look of a man seeing, for the first time, the person who had always been there.

"Dispatch," he said. "We're going to need a statement."

"Yes," said Ada Oyelaran. "Under my own name."

Outside, the rain began again, soft on the gravel, and somewhere in the crowd a phone camera light came on, then another, then another — small white stars rising all over the dark lawn of 14 Calder Row, and Nell Barrow, officially dead for three years, walked out onto the porch into all of them at once.
