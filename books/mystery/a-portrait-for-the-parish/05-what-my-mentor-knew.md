## 5. What My Mentor Knew

The train from Bodmin Parkway crawled along the valley like something ashamed to arrive. Rosalind watched the hedgerows slide past — blackthorn in ragged bloom, a heron standing its ground in a flooded field — and tried to compose the conversation he was about to have. He had drafted three openings in his notebook and disliked all of them.

*Alistair. I need to ask you about Penhallow.*

*Alistair. Do you remember the summer of '94?*

*Alistair. Did you know she was alive?*

None of them would survive contact with the man himself. Alistair Grebe had taught him conveyancing, probate, and the finer art of never putting anything in writing that could be read aloud in court. He had taught him how to hold a client's hand across a desk while quietly ruining them with a clause they hadn't understood. Rosalind owed him his career, or at least its architecture — the load-bearing walls of discretion, the windows placed exactly where light was wanted and nowhere else.

He also owed him, he was beginning to suspect, a great deal more than gratitude. Perhaps an apology. Perhaps a reckoning.

Truro arrived in drizzle. Grebe had retired to a narrow terraced house on a street where every door was the same colour and the sea was a rumour on clear days. Rosalind stood before number eleven for a long moment, rain gathering on his shoulders, before he knocked.

It took nearly two minutes for the door to open. When it did, the man in the gap was not quite the man Rosalind remembered.

Alistair Grebe had always been built like a church warden — tall, spare, upright, with a voice pitched for reading lessons and hands that smelled faintly of ink no matter how often he washed them. The man before him now seemed to have been folded. His cardigan hung off shoulders that had learned to curve inward, as if he'd spent thirty years trying to take up less room in the world. His eyes went past Rosalind's face, down the empty street, and back again, twice, before he spoke.

"Rosalind." Not a greeting. An identification, flat as a stamp.

"Alistair. May I come in?"

"I expect you will whether I say so or not." But he stepped aside, and there was something almost relieved in the surrender, the way a man looks when the knock he has been dreading finally comes and he can stop listening for it.

* * *

The sitting room smelled of cold tea and old paper. Every surface held documents — not files, exactly, but loose sheets, envelopes, clippings folded into quarters, arranged in drifts that suggested a mind still working even as the body around it slowed. On the mantelpiece stood a photograph Rosalind remembered: Grebe's late wife, Margaret, laughing on a beach at Polzeath, her hair full of wind. Beside it, incongruously, a small brass bell of the kind used on shop counters.

Grebe saw him looking at it. "For the nurse," he said. "She comes Thursdays. I don't always hear the door."

"You were expecting someone else, then."

"I wasn't expecting anyone." He lowered himself into an armchair with the care of a man handling cracked porcelain. "But I've been expecting *this*. Since the telephone call came from St. Merryn last month. Since Mrs. Tresidder wrote to me in March. Sit down, boy. You'll want to sit down."

Rosalind sat. The word *boy* landed strangely — Grebe had called him that for twenty years, but tonight it sounded less like affection than like a man reaching for the last familiar thing in a flooding room.

"I'm here about Edith Penhallow," Rosalind said. "Her death certificate is dated June 1994. Her will is dated April of this year. Both bear signatures in the same hand, and one of them — the later one — was witnessed by your firm. Your initials are on the attestation clause, Alistair. You certified a document for a woman who had been dead for thirty years."

"Yes," said Grebe.

Not *I don't know what you mean.* Not *there must be some mistake.* Just *yes*, quiet and immediate, and Rosalind felt the floor of his profession tilt beneath him. In forty years of practice, Grebe had never once conceded a point in the first exchange. It was the first lesson he'd ever taught: *never agree too quickly, Rosalind; agreement is a currency, spend it slowly.*

"And yet," Rosalind continued carefully, "you're going to tell me you did nothing wrong."

"I did nothing *illegal*." Grebe's hands found each other in his lap and gripped. "That is not the same thing, and you know it better than most."

"Then explain it to me."

The old man was silent for a long moment. Rain ticked against the window. Somewhere down the street a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"She walked into my office," Grebe said at last, "on the fourth of October, 1994. I remember the date because it was Margaret's birthday and I was meant to be taking her to dinner. The woman asked me to certify a power of attorney. Standard instrument. She presented identification — a passport, a driving licence, both in order, both genuine as far as any examination could make them. The name on those documents was Edith Mary Penhallow. I checked against the electoral roll. I checked against the parish register extract. Everything aligned."

"But she was dead. You'd have known. You drafted the original estate papers yourself — you'd have known her face, her signature—"

"I knew her *signature*," Grebe said, "because I had taught her to sign it. She was seventeen and her father wanted the estate settled into trust before the boy came into it. Tobias Chenoweth." He said the name like a stone set down gently on a table. "Edith Penhallow sat across from me in this very chair — well, in my Truro office, then — and practised her signature four times because she thought it looked childish. So yes. When a woman signed that name in front of me in October 1994, I knew her hand the way a father knows a child's handwriting on a school note."

Rosalind leaned forward. "And?"

"And it was the same hand." Grebe closed his eyes. "Every loop, every hesitation before the crossbar of the H. Thirty years of practice could not have improved upon it, because it needed no improvement. It was hers. Or—" his voice caught, corrected itself, "—or it was the hand of someone who had signed that name ten thousand times since."

"The twin," Rosalind said. "Morwenna."

Something moved through the old man's face — not surprise. Confirmation. The look of a man who has carried a weight so long that hearing another speak its name feels less like exposure than like setting it down.

"Who told you about Morwenna?"

"The publican at the Lugger. Photographs in the parish hall. Two identical girls, one of whom 'went away.' Alistair — who walked into your office?"

"No."

"Alistair—"

"*No*." The word cracked like ice underfoot. Grebe's hands had gone white where they gripped each other. "You may write down everything else I've said. Put it in an affidavit if you must. But that — who came through my door — that I will carry out of this world in my own pocket, and you will not have it from me, and you should thank God you won't, because the moment you have it you become what I am."

"What are you?" Rosalind asked, and immediately wished he hadn't, because the question was crueler than he'd intended and the answer was already visible in the ruined geometry of the man before him.

"Complicit," Grebe said simply. "Thirty years complicit. And so, my dear boy, are you — though you don't know it yet, which is the only mercy either of us has been shown in this business."

* * *

Later — much later, after the tea Grebe poured and didn't drink, after the fire had been coaxed and abandoned — Rosalind sat in the window seat and let the memory rise, because it had been rising all evening, patient as tide.

*Truro, 1994. He was twenty-six, the youngest clerk Grebe had ever taken on, and proud of it in the way young men are proud of things that have not yet cost them anything.*

*It was a Thursday in October. He remembered the rain that day too — Cornish rain, horizontal, personal. Grebe had gone out to lunch with a client and left instructions: file the Chenoweth correspondence, lock the strongroom, nothing else crosses his desk until Monday.*

*Rosalind filed the correspondence. And then, because he was twenty-six and the strongroom door was heavy and satisfying, and because Grebe had once told him that a solicitor who doesn't understand his own archive understands nothing, he went inside.*

*The Penhallow box was open on the table. That was the first wrong thing — Grebe never left boxes open. Inside lay a death certificate, fresh-inked, awaiting registration. And beneath it, a draft letter in Grebe's own hand, addressed to the district registrar, requesting correction of an administrative error.*

*An error. As though a woman's death were a misfiled deed, a crossed cheque, a thing amendable by form and fee.*

*He heard footsteps and slid the box shut with his heart hammering, and when Grebe came in — earlier than expected, coat wet, face grey — Rosalind was standing in the doorway of the strongroom holding a volume of statutes he had no reason to be holding.*

*"Everything in order?" Grebe had asked.*

*"Yes, sir."*

*And Grebe had looked at him — one long, level, terrible look, the look of a man measuring a distance — and said, "Good lad," and that was all. They never spoke of it. Not once in twenty years of partnership, of shared offices, of Christmas lunches and late-night probate emergencies. The silence between them had a shape after that day, a specific shape, like a keyhole, and Rosalind had learned to walk around it without looking down.*

*In return, Grebe made him partner at thirty-four. Younger than anyone in the firm's history. Rosalind had told himself for years that it was merit. He was only now, sitting in a damp Truro sitting room watching his mentor's hands tremble, allowing himself to count the years and do the arithmetic of what the promotion had actually purchased.*

*His discretion. His silence. Him.*

*He had spent his whole career believing himself the clean instrument of other people's messy affairs — the man who tidied estates, who translated grief into grant letters, who stood outside the mess with clean hands. And all the while he had been bought, early and cheaply, with a title and a corner office, and he had signed the receipt without reading it.*

*His invisibility, he understood now, had never been an accident of temperament. It had been a transaction.*

* * *

"You knew I was in the strongroom," Rosalind said aloud. "That October. You came back and I was standing there with a book in my hands and you knew."

Grebe did not pretend otherwise. He looked into the fire for a long moment, and when he answered, his voice had lost its courtroom steel entirely. It was just an old man's voice, tired and thin.

"I knew. And I made a choice, right or wrong, that a young man with your future ought not to be asked to carry what I was carrying. So I gave you something else instead. A career. A name in the firm. And I told myself for thirty years that it was kindness." He laughed — a small, dreadful sound. "Kindness. You see how the vocabulary rots, Rosalind? First you call it discretion. Then prudence. Then kindness. And one morning you wake up and you cannot find the word for what it actually was, because you buried it so deep you've forgotten the shape of it."

"What *was* it?"

"Fear," said Grebe. "And love, if you can believe it. Both at once, in equal measure, which is the worst possible recipe for any decision." He turned to face Rosalind fully, and his eyes were wet. "You want to know who walked into my office in 1994. I'll tell you what I can tell you, and not a syllable more. She was frightened. Frightened in a way I had never seen in thirty years of practice — not guilty-frightened, not running-from-the-law frightened. Grieving-frightened. Like a woman carrying something dead inside her that she couldn't put down. She asked me to certify papers that would let her live a life that was not hers. And she told me — she *made* me understand — that the alternative was a truth that would destroy people who had done nothing wrong. A whole parish, Rosalind. A whole parish of innocent people, and one grave, and one grief, and one lie that held them all together like mortar holds stone."

"One grave," Rosalind repeated. "Tobias Chenoweth's grave is in the churchyard at St. Merryn. Marked. Dated 1994."

"Is it?" Something flickered behind Grebe's eyes — not surprise this time, but a kind of horror, deep and old. "Then you know less than I feared and more than I hoped. Listen to me." He leaned forward, gripping the arms of his chair, and for a moment the old churchwarden's spine reasserted itself, and he was almost the man from the Truro office again. "There are documents in this affair that were never filed. There is a page missing from a coroner's file, and I know it's missing because I was asked, in the spring of 1995, whether my firm had ever held it, and I lied, and the person who asked me believed me, and that lie has sat in my chest for thirty years like a swallowed stone. And there is a woman — whoever she is, whichever sister she is — who has spent three decades doing penance in a parish that thinks she's a saint, and she asked me for one thing besides the certifications."

"What thing?"

"To forget her." Grebe sank back. "To forget her so thoroughly that no one could ever dig her up. And I have been forgetting her, professionally, diligently, for thirty years. Until your letter came. Until I saw your name on the letterhead and knew that the past had sent the one man alive who could recognise my handwriting in the dark."

Rosalind stood. His legs felt strange, borrowed. At the window, the drizzle had thickened into proper rain, erasing the street, the opposite houses, the whole ordinary world beyond the glass.

"The coroner's file," he said. "The missing page. Whose hand removed it?"

"I told you. No names."

"Then tell me this. If I find it — if I find the page, or the grave, or the truth about which sister is buried under which name — will you testify? Will you stand in a room full of people and say what you've said to me tonight?"

Grebe was silent for so long that Rosalind thought he would refuse. Then the old man reached to the mantelpiece, took down the photograph of Margaret on the beach, and held it in both hands as though warming them.

"When Margaret died," he said, "I promised her I would die clean. No unfinished business, no unsaid truths. I have failed her for thirty years, Rosalind, one careful day at a time." He set the photograph down, facing the wall. "Find your page. Find your grave. And when you're ready — truly ready, knowing what it costs — send for me. I'll stand up. God help us both, I'll stand up."

* * *

Rosalind walked back to the station through the rain without hurrying. The streetlamps came on early, sodium-orange, and the wet pavements threw the light back up at him like something offered.

At the station buffet, waiting for the delayed train, he took out his notebook and wrote down everything Grebe had said, verbatim, in the shorthand he used for witness statements. When he reached the phrase *one grave, and one grief, and one lie that held them all together*, his pen stopped.

Because Grebe had said *a grave* — but he had flinched at the mention of Tobias Chenoweth's marked grave in the churchyard. Flinched, Rosalind was almost certain, not at the name but at the *location*. As though the grave that mattered were somewhere else. Somewhere unmarked. Somewhere a sexton might have dug at midnight, thirty years ago, and told no one.

He turned to a fresh page and wrote a single line:

*What did the vicar give him to bury?*

He didn't know yet why Father Callum Reeve's name had risen unbidden into his mind — the soft-spoken vicar with the watchful eyes, who had stood too close at the reading of the will and looked at Rosalind as though counting the questions behind his questions.

But the name was there now, inked in his own hand, and it would not be unwritten.

The train drew in, lights blazing through the rain, and Rosalind Brightwater boarded it carrying, for the first time in his life, a secret of his own — and the growing certainty that in St. Merryn, secrets were not kept.

They were buried.

And graves, however carefully dug, had a way of surfacing.
