## 14. Two Sisters, One Life

The letters were arranged on Rosalind's desk in the order he had found them, which was to say, in no order at all — a shoebox of correspondence rescued from Morwenna's room, wrapped in a 1994 edition of the *Western Morning News* and smelling of camphor and dust and, faintly, of lavender that had given up its scent decades ago. He had read them twice through the night. By dawn he had stopped taking notes, because there was nothing left to note. There was only the story, whole and terrible, laid out in two identical hands that differed only in pressure — Edith's writing firm as a signature on a deed, Morwenna's thinning month by month until the last letters looked as though they had been written by someone dissolving into the paper.

He made coffee he did not drink and began, as a lawyer must, at the beginning.

---

**12 March 1994**

*My dearest Edith,*

*Dr. Pascoe came again today. He has stopped pretending it is a chill. He says "months" now instead of "weeks," which I suppose is his way of being kind, though I would rather he said weeks. Months is a country I don't want a map of.*

*I am not writing to make you weep. I am writing because I have thought of something, and once I thought of it I could not stop thinking of it, and you are the only person alive I can say it to.*

*You know what Mother arranged. You know what Tobias expects in June, and what Father's debts expect sooner. You watched me try on that dress last week and you saw what I saw in your face — that you would rather be buried in it than married in it. Well. Here is my thought, and you may call it the fever talking:*

*Why should only one of us die?*

*Take my name, Edith. Take my passport, my bank book, my locket, my life. I will be dead on paper by summer anyway; let the paper be true a little early. You go where I cannot — anywhere, everywhere — and I will stay here in this house I love and be looked after, and when the end comes they will bury Morwenna Penhallow, who died of her illness, and no one will ever have cause to look twice. The parish already half believes we are one person. Let us make the belief useful.*

*You would be free of him. That is the whole of my argument and it needs no other. You never loved him. You told me so at fourteen and you have never once unsaid it.*

*Think of it as inheritance. Not of money — of breath.*

*Your twin (as if you needed telling),*
*Morwenna*

---

Rosalind set the letter down and walked to the window. St. Merryn lay below in the grey morning light, chimneys smoking, gulls arguing over the harbor wall, a place performing its own ordinariness with the practiced ease of thirty years' rehearsal. He tried to imagine the woman who had written that letter — dying, twenty-six years old perhaps, watching her healthy sister dread a wedding — and found he could not hold her as a stranger. She sounded like someone he might have known. She sounded, uncomfortably, like the young clerk he had once been, weighing silence against ruin and finding the arithmetic agreeable.

He returned to the desk.

---

**30 March 1994**

*M.,*

*You are mad, and I have read your letter eleven times, and I am going to do it.*

*Don't ask me yet how I feel. I don't know how I feel. When I say your name aloud in my room it sounds like someone else's voice saying it, and perhaps that is the point. Perhaps that is exactly the point.*

*Tobias came to tea today. He talked about the boat he means to buy with Father's settlement money, and about Bristol, where we are to live, and he took my hand — yours, he thinks — and I let him, and I felt nothing but the cold of his ring. He is not cruel, Morwenna. That is the worst of it. He is simply certain, the way weather is certain, and I am to be the field he rains on.*

*Tell me what to do first. Tell me everything. I am frightened of the paperwork more than of the sin, if it is a sin. Grebe will need convincing —*

*No. Don't tell me. I'll manage Grebe. I've watched you manage men all our lives; surely some of it transferred with the face.*

*One condition. Only one. When I go, you write to me. Under whatever name you like, through whatever channel you like, but write. If I am to be you for forty years I will want to know what you did with my Tuesdays.*

*E.*

---

There was a gap then — six weeks with nothing — and Rosalind understood why before he found the next letter. Six weeks was time for documents. Time for a solicitor in Truro to be persuaded or paid or shamed. Time for a death certificate to be drafted in advance, dated for a future that had not yet happened, waiting in a drawer like a loaded gun. He knew that drawer. He had, in another life, in another office, helped keep such a drawer shut.

His own name appeared in the next one, and he read it with the sensation of walking downstairs in the dark and finding one stair missing.

---

**19 May 1994**

*E.,*

*Grebe has agreed. I won't put in writing how he was persuaded — burn this page if you're wise, though I know you keep everything, you magpie — but he has agreed, and the young clerk who witnessed the papers asked no questions worth answering. A boy with careful eyes. Grebe called him "discreet." I called him expensive. We settled on discreet.*

*The certificate is drawn. Dated the 14th of June. Father Reeve has the register ready and the ground reserved beside the family stone — he believes, as I believe, that a lie told inside a church is still a lie, but that some lies are load-bearing walls, and you pull them out and the whole house comes down on the people you love.*

*Reeve. I want to say something about Reeve and I hardly know how. He looks at you — at me, when I wear your coat — the way sailors look at the horizon. He has loved you since the harvest fete when we were nineteen and you beat him at quoits and laughed at him for a full minute. He knows which sister he loves. Do you understand? Even now, even with the faces, he knows. He told me yesterday, in the vestry, hands shaking around a cup of tea: "I don't care what name she carries. I'd know her heartbeat in a crowd."*

*Be kind to him, Edith. Whatever else you do with my life, be kind to him. He is giving up his God for you, or very nearly, and men who give up God for love either become saints or become ghosts, and I would rather you kept him a saint.*

*M.*

---

Rosalind sat back. The fire had gone down; he fed it a log without looking away from the letters. *A boy with careful eyes.* Thirty years ago Alistair Grebe had called him discreet, and he had worn the word like a medal, and here it was again from the other side of the transaction — *expensive*, she had joked, but the joke had a ledger under it. Everyone in this story had been paid. In money, in silence, in love withheld. He himself had been paid in a career.

He turned to the June letters, and the tone changed the way the light changes when a cloud crosses the sun.

---

**11 June 1994**

*Edith — come home. Come tonight, whatever you've arranged, wherever you've got to. I've done something and I can't undo it alone.*

*I told him.*

*Not everything. I meant to tell him nothing. But he came to see me — he thinks I'm convalescent, he brings me oranges like it's 1950 — and he said, "When we're married I want Edith to live with us. It isn't right, her rattling round that house alone," and something in me broke, some small bone of honesty I didn't know I had, and I heard myself say: "She doesn't want to marry you, Tobias. She never wanted to marry you. She's doing it for Father's debts, and you know it, and you've always known it."*

*He went very quiet. Then he smiled — that patient smile — and said, "Morwenna, you're ill. You shouldn't upset yourself." As if the truth were a symptom.*

*But he stayed an hour longer than he needed to, and he asked questions, small ones, sideways ones, and I am frightened, Edith. Not of him. Of what he'll find if he starts pulling threads. The certificate. Grebe. The register. Come home and we'll decide together what he knows and what he must never know.*

*Burn this.*

*(I know you won't.)*

---

The next letter was not from Morwenna. It was in Edith's hand, but the pressure had changed — the firm strokes gone ragged, the pen pressed hard enough in places to tear. Rosalind had handled enough confessions, professional and otherwise, to know what a hand looks like when it is shaking. This one shook on every line.

It was undated. He guessed it had been written within days of the eleventh of June, and guessed further that it had been written many times before it was written finally, and that this was the version that survived because it was the version that told the truth all the way down.

---

*He knows.*

*I came home on the 13th and the house was wrong — you know how a house is wrong, the way a room is wrong when someone has cried in it — and Morwenna wouldn't say what had happened, only that Tobias had been, and had left "understanding everything," and that she had sent for Father Reeve.*

*We went down to the cove to meet him. Both of us. Morwenna insisted; she said if Tobias was going to unmake us he would have to unmake us to our faces, and she was dying anyway and past fear, and I went because I have followed her into every trouble since the womb and I wasn't going to start refusing now.*

*He was already there. On the rocks below the cliff path, where the boys dive in August. He had been drinking — not drunk, but drinking, the flask in his pocket, and his voice had that terrible reasonableness drink gives certain men.*

*He said he had been to Truro. He said he had seen the file in Grebe's office — he wouldn't say how, and I have never learned how, and it doesn't matter now — and he said the words "death certificate" and "fraud" and "prison" as calmly as a man reading a shopping list. And then he said the thing that ended everything. He said: "I'll keep your secret, Edith. Both your secrets. On terms."*

*And Morwenna — God forgive me, God forgive both of us — Morwenna laughed at him. She was so near the end and so far past him, past all of it, and she laughed and said, "Terms? Tobias Chenoweth, you couldn't afford the interest."*

*He grabbed her. I think — I have spent thirty years thinking — that he meant only to shake her, to stop the laughing, to make her take him seriously. But the rocks were wet and she was light, lighter than she'd been in months, illness had pared her down to almost nothing, and she went backward off the ledge into the pool beneath and the sea took her the way the sea takes things, quickly and completely and without any opinion on the matter.*

*I went in after her. I went in in my coat and boots. I found her. I held her. She was already gone — Dr. Pascoe would tell me later it would have taken her by August regardless, and I have built what shelter I could out of that sentence, and it has never once kept out the rain.*

*Tobias was on the rocks. He hadn't moved. He was looking at the water and making a sound I will not describe even here, even to you, whoever you are, whoever finds this.*

*And then he said: "Say it was an accident. Say she slipped. No one will question it — she was ill, everyone knows she wandered, she loved that cove." And I understood that he was offering me the same bargain he had offered before, except now the price was different. Now the price was that we would carry it together, the two of us, forever, and he would be safe inside my silence the way I was supposed to be safe inside his.*

*I said yes.*

*What else does the record say I should have done? Call for help, for a drowned woman who was dying anyway, and hang us all — Grebe, Reeve, the parish, myself — on the truth of a fraud she invented out of love for me? I said yes. He climbed up. He walked away along the cliff path toward the village, and I stayed in the water holding my sister, and the tide came in, and somewhere above us the church bell rang the hour as if the world were an ordinary place.*

*They found him three weeks later under the north cliffs. The sea gives back what it takes when it's finished, and it had finished with him. They said he lost his footing. They said grief, or drink, or both. The coroner wrote "misadventure" and everyone in the parish nodded, because everyone in the parish had already agreed to nod — Reeve saw to that, quietly, gently, a word here and a word there — and the file closed, minus one page, and the village began the long work of forgetting in unison.*

*But I know what I know. I stood on those rocks and I watched him walk the path north, and I have stood on them a hundred times since, and I will tell you what I believe and let the sea argue with me: he did not slip. He walked to the edge where she went in, and he looked down at the water that had her, and whatever lived in him that could offer bargains could not survive having caused that, and he stepped off. Or he leaned too far into looking. I have never decided, and I have decided a thousand times, and both decisions are true, and neither one lets me sleep.*

*So here is the confession entire, for whoever it falls to:*

*I, Edith Penhallow, drowned no one. But I buried my sister under my own name, and I let a man die rather than speak, and I have lived thirty years as a ghost wearing my sister's face, paying for it in penance the way other women pay rent — monthly, punctually, without hope of the debt ending.*

*If you are reading this, then either I am dead at last, truly, or you have dug deeper than anyone was meant to dig. Either way: the fortune is not payment for silence. It is payment for witness. Someone must know all of it and carry it kindly, or the carrying has been for nothing.*

*Her name was Morwenna. Say it sometimes. That is all the monument she wanted.*

---

Rosalind read the final lines three times. Outside, the rain had begun, soft against the glass, and the harbor lights smeared into long yellow wounds across the water.

*Payment for witness.* Not hush money. Not gratitude. An inheritance contingent on proof — on someone learning her real name — because Edith Penhallow had spent three decades unable to bear her own, and had wanted, more than freedom, more than absolution, one living soul to hold the whole weight of it and not drop it.

He thought of Mrs. Tresidder and her trustees, fattening comfortably on ambiguity. He thought of Father Reeve keeping watch like a lighthouse nobody credited. He thought of the unmarked grave beside the Penhallow stone, and of the second body the sea had kept, and of the coroner's file with its missing page — and he understood suddenly, with a coldness that started in his stomach and spread outward, that the missing page might not have been removed to hide Tobias's death.

It might have been removed because Tobias's death was never misadventure at all — because someone, sometime, had written down what really happened on those rocks, and that someone had not been Edith.

Tobias had walked north along the cliff path. Edith swore to it. But the path passed the coastguard station, and the station kept a log, and logs get copied, and copies get filed.

Rosalind reached for his coat. If there was a page missing from the coroner's file, then somewhere in Truro there was a duplicate — and whoever had pulled the original, thirty years ago, had known exactly what it said.

* * *

The county archive at Truro opened at nine. Rosalind was at the door at five past, rain still dripping from his shoulders, and the archivist — a young woman with the patient manner of someone guarding paper against the public — fetched the Chenoweth file without comment. He had seen it before, in the first days, when the drowning was only a thread. Now he turned to the end, to the gap where the page had been cut, and asked the question he should have asked weeks ago:

"Who requested this file between 1994 and now?"

She checked the register. Her finger ran down the columns and stopped.

"Twice," she said. "Once in 2009 — a Mrs. Tresidder, estate business, quite proper." She tapped the second line, and Rosalind felt the floor tilt gently beneath his chair. "And once in June 1994. Three weeks after the body was found. Collected in person."

"By whom?"

She turned the register toward him, and there it was in faded ink, a name he knew, written in a hand he knew better than his own.

Alistair Grebe.
