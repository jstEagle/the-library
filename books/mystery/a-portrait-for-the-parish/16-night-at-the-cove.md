## 16. Night at the Cove

The light came at half past eleven, a thin yellow pulse against the black shoulder of Penhallow Head, and Rosalind saw it from his window at the Pilchard's Rest as though it had been lit for him alone.

He had not been sleeping. Sleep had become a country he could no longer find on any map. For three nights he had lain in the narrow bed listening to the sea work at the rocks below the harbour wall, turning over the same questions like stones: *Who took the letters? Who put young Pascoe on the floor of the strongroom? And why does every road in this parish lead back to the same door?* The police had come that afternoon with their soft questions about his employment history, about the year he'd spent unaccounted for between Grebe's office and his articles, and he had answered them with the smoothness of long practice while something inside him quietly bled.

So when the light appeared — one flash, then another, deliberate as a signal — he did not hesitate. He pulled on his coat, took the torch from the chest of drawers, and let himself out into the dark without telling anyone, because there was no one to tell.

The path down to Penhallow Cove was older than any map of it. It dropped through gorse and bracken in switchbacks worn slick by two centuries of fishermen's boots, and the wind came up off the water carrying salt and the cold mineral smell of the caves below the cliff. Rosalind went carefully, his torch beam jumping ahead of him, and all the while he was aware of the cove opening below — a crescent of shingle pale as bone under the starlight, the sea sliding in and out of it with a sound like slow breathing.

He had read the coroner's file enough times now to know this place by heart. Tobias Chenoweth had been found here, or just beyond it, beneath the cliffs where the tide kept what it took. Weeks before Edith Penhallow died on paper. A man betrothed, a woman grieving, a parish that closed around both facts like a fist.

And now a light, burning at the water's edge, at half past eleven on a night when no honest man had business there.

Rosalind came down the last stretch of path and stopped among the rocks at the head of the shingle, and he saw him.

Father Callum Reeve stood at the tideline, his cassock dark against the paler stones, holding a storm lantern in one hand. In the other — and Rosalind felt his heart stop and start again — he held two envelopes, white and ghostly in the lantern light, tied with the solicitor's green ribbon that Rosalind himself had catalogued only days ago in the strongroom at Truro.

"You saw me," Reeve said, without turning. His voice carried easily over the water; the cove was built for carrying voices, for shouting over engines and gales. "I thought you might. You've been watching my window too, Mr. Brightwater. We've developed certain habits, you and I."

"The letters were taken from the strongroom four nights ago," Rosalind said. He came forward onto the shingle, each step loud, wanting his steps to be loud. "A clerk was hurt. Badly. Those letters are evidence in an open inquiry."

"I know." Reeve turned then, and the lantern threw his face into relief — the deep lines, the eyes that were older than the rest of him, eyes that had been watching this cove for thirty years. "I know what they are. I've known since before you were born, Mr. Brightwater. I wrote part of them."

The wind moved through the gorse above them. Somewhere out at sea, a buoy bell tolled once, twice, patient as a heartbeat.

"Sit down if you like," the vicar said. "Or stand. But hear me out, because I have waited three decades for someone to ask me the right questions, and you are the first who has come close, and I am tired, Rosalind Brightwater. I am so very tired."

"I'm not here for confession," Rosalind said. "I'm here for the truth."

"They are frequently the same thing." Reeve lowered himself onto a flat rock near the tideline, setting the lantern between his feet, and after a moment Rosalind sat opposite him, close enough to see the letters in the vicar's lap, close enough to see that his hands were shaking. "You want to know how I got them. I'll tell you plainly: I didn't take them. God is my witness, and He has witnessed enough of my sins that He would surely have mentioned this one."

"Then who—"

"Someone left them in the church porch. Tuesday morning, before Matins. Wrapped in this ribbon, with no note. I had been praying — literally praying — for their return since I heard they were gone, because I knew what would happen if they fell into the wrong hands, and then God, or someone wearing His coat, set them on my doorstep like milk." He looked down at the envelopes. "I don't believe in coincidences anymore. I stopped believing in them in 1994. But I confess I cannot explain this one, and it frightens me more than anything else in this affair."

Rosalind studied him. Thirty years of interviewing witnesses had taught him to read faces the way other men read tide tables, and Reeve's face said grief, exhaustion, and fear — but not guilt. Not the particular flatness of a man constructing lies. Still.

"You'll forgive me if I don't take your word."

"I would think less of you if you did." The vicar almost smiled. "Ask me, then. Ask me everything. That's why I lit the lantern — so you'd come. So we could do this properly, in the place where it happened, instead of across a desk with a tape recorder running."

"Where what happened?"

Reeve looked past him, out at the black water. When he spoke again, his voice had changed — lower, stripped of the pulpit's music, down to the bare timber of the man underneath.

"Tobias Chenoweth died here. On this shingle, or in that water, I have never been entirely sure which, and neither was she. Edith didn't kill him, whatever her letter says. But she believed she had, and belief is heavier than fact. Belief is what you carry for the rest of your life."

"You loved her."

It was not a question, and Reeve did not treat it as one. "From the age of nineteen. She was promised to Chenoweth by her father — money, land, the usual arithmetic — and she never loved him, and everyone knew it, and no one said it, because that is what parishes do with truths too large to bury. And Morwenna—" his voice caught, and he mastered it "—Morwenna was dying. Slowly, in that room in the east wing, while the doctors used words like *months*. And she made Edith promise. Take my name. Take my life. Escape him. Live."

"And you helped."

"I helped." He said it the way a man says *I killed him*, flat and final. "I knew a doctor in Falmouth who owed me. I certified the death — Morwenna's death, which was real, which was coming whether we lied or not — under Edith's name. I buried Morwenna in the plot beside the family stone, and I stood over that grave at midnight with the sexton, and I told God it was necessary, and God, being merciful or indifferent, said nothing. And Edith walked out of this parish as Morwenna Penhallow, and she lived thirty years, and I watched her live them from behind a pulpit, and I never once touched her hand again."

The lantern flame guttered in a gust off the water. Reeve steadied it with a hand that was not quite steady itself.

"Do you understand what that was, Mr. Brightwater? To stand in that church every Sunday, forty feet from the woman you loved, calling her by her sister's name, hearing her answer to it? To visit her — as a vicar visits the sick, as a vicar visits anyone — and sit in her kitchen drinking tea while every word between us was a lie we had agreed upon? She asked me once, near the end, whether I regretted it. I told her the truth. I said: every day, and I would do it again tomorrow. She said that was the most honest sermon I'd ever preached."

Rosalind found he could not speak for a moment. The cove breathed around them, in and out.

"The letter," he managed at last. "The confession. She wrote that she drowned him."

"She wrote what she believed. There was a struggle — he had discovered the substitution, you understand, he'd worked it out, and he came here to confront her, to drag her back, to expose the whole arrangement before it was a week old. They argued. He grabbed her. She fought him off — she was stronger than anyone gave her credit for, all that farm work — and he went down on these stones, here, where we're sitting, and he struck his head, and the tide was coming in, and she ran for help, and by the time anyone came back the sea had him." Reeve's jaw tightened. "Was it murder? The coroner would have said accident. Edith said murder, in her own heart, for thirty years. I say it was a terrible night that made three liars of us and a corpse of him, and that God can sort the categories better than any of us."

"Why keep the letters at all? Why not burn them?"

"Because she asked me not to." Simple as that. "She said a lie that leaves no record isn't survived, it's just repeated. She wanted the truth to exist somewhere, even sealed, even unread. She wanted—" his voice broke, and he pressed the backs of his hands against his eyes for a moment before continuing, "—she wanted someone, someday, to know who she really was. That's the second letter, Mr. Brightwater. The inheritance. It wasn't a bribe. It was a plea. *Find my name. Say it over my grave.*"

The wind rose. Above them the cliff loomed, and Rosalind thought of the room in the east wing, the wedding dress, the third letter bearing his own name, and felt the whole architecture of the last thirty years settle around him like weather.

"If you didn't take the letters from the strongroom," he said slowly, "and whoever did returned them to your porch — then someone knows what you are. Someone is playing with you. Or protecting you. Or setting you up."

"Yes," Reeve said quietly. "I've considered all three. I like none of them."

"The police will ask where you were the night the clerk was hurt."

"At the hospital, sitting with a dying parishioner, with twelve nurses as witnesses. My alibi is embarrassingly good, which will make it look rehearsed. I've thought of that too." He gathered the letters into his lap and looked at them for a long moment. Then he held them out across the space between them. "Take them."

Rosalind stared. "They're evidence—"

"They're hers. And you are the man she chose — she named you in that third letter, did she not? Don't look surprised; she told me she'd written to you, though not what was in it. She trusted you before she ever met you, God knows why. Perhaps because you were Grebe's, and Grebe was ours, and she believed loyalty runs in institutions like blood in families." The vicar's arm stayed extended, steady now. "Take them, read them, and do what she asked. Say her name. Because I am an old man, and I have kept this watch for thirty years, and I am done keeping it alone."

Rosalind took the letters. They were lighter than they should have been, somehow — two envelopes, a few sheets of paper, thirty years of a human life.

"One more thing," Reeve said as Rosalind rose. The lantern light caught his face, and for a moment he looked not like a vicar but like a man standing at his own graveside. "Whoever left these on my step wanted me to have them back. Think about what that means. Someone in this parish knows everything — the grave, the name, the letters — and is moving pieces on the board. And the last person who tried to profit from what they knew ended up at the foot of the church tower."

The buoy bell tolled far out in the dark, and Rosalind, standing on the shingle where a man had died and a lie had been born, felt the cold of the coming chapter settle into his bones.

"Mrs. Tresidder," he said.

Reeve did not answer. He only bent, and turned down the lantern, and the cove went dark around them both, and somewhere above, faint on the wind, came the sound of the church clock striking midnight — the hour, the sexton had told him, when things were buried.
