## 19. The Confession Read Aloud

The inquest was held in the schoolroom of St. Merryn's village hall, because the coroner's court at Bodmin had been deemed too far and too formal for a matter the diocese still insisted was a clerical irregularity and nothing more. Rosalind thought that choice told its own story. A small room held fewer people who could be trusted to stay silent; a small room could be filled with the right people.

It was filled now.

Folding chairs scraped against a floor that still smelled faintly of chalk and childhood. The parish had turned out in its Sunday clothes — the publican from the Anchor with his collar pressed and his eyes carefully neutral, the fisherman's widow Mrs. Tregose in a coat she had clearly worn to two funerals and a christening, the sexton old Pascoe sitting so far back he was nearly in the coat rack, his grandson's name pinned like a bruise to every conversation in the room. At the front, beneath a map of Cornwall left over from some long-ago geography lesson, sat the coroner — a dry, precise man named Dr. Lanyon who had driven down from Truro and whose expression suggested he had already written half his verdict in his head and did not intend for anyone in this damp little parish to change it.

Mrs. Tresidder occupied the front row as though it were a throne. She wore grey wool and pearls and the patient face of a woman prepared to endure an inconvenience. Beside her, the trustees arranged themselves like caryatids: Mr. Nance, who kept the estate accounts; Mr. Polglase, retired from Falmouth and mostly decorative. Father Reeve sat apart, in the second row, his hands folded on his knee, his face the colour of weathered slate. He had not looked at Rosalind since they had stood together in the dark at Penhallow Cove three nights ago, and Rosalind understood why. Whatever passed between them there had used up all the looking they could safely do.

Rosalind sat where he always sat in rooms like this — near the door, slightly behind the action, the man nobody remembered inviting. For thirty years that invisibility had been his profession and his shelter. This morning he intended to burn it down.

"Mr. Brightwater." Dr. Lanyon consulted a card. "You are entered here as counsel to the estate. You will appreciate that this is an inquiry into the death of a young man found at Penhallow Cove in October 1994, and into certain... irregularities arising from documents filed in connection with the late Miss Edith Penhallow. It is not," and here his gaze flicked, briefly, toward Mrs. Tresidder, "a theatre."

"No, Doctor," Rosalind said, rising. "It is a churchyard."

A murmur went through the room. Mrs. Tresidder's pearls caught the light as she turned her head a quarter-inch, no more.

"I beg your pardon?"

"There is a grave in St. Merryn churchyard," Rosalind said, "dug in November 1994, beside the Penhallow stone, never opened for viewing, never marked. Old Pascoe dug it at midnight because the vicar asked him to, and what the vicar gave him to put in it weighed, by his own account, rather less than a grown woman should." He let that stand in the chalk-smelling air. "There is also a death certificate signed by the same vicar, certifying the death of Edith Penhallow thirty years before she actually died. And there is a letter, written in her own hand, which explains both."

Dr. Lanyon's pen had stopped moving. "You are not here to give evidence about graves, Mr. Brightwater. You are here because you hold letters that were reported stolen from a solicitor's strongroom in Truro, and the police would very much like to know how they came into your possession."

"They came into my possession," Rosalind said, "because Edith Penhallow addressed one of them to me by name, eleven years before I ever heard of St. Merryn, and arranged through her solicitor for it to reach me when the time was right. She knew, Doctor. She knew that someday someone would come asking questions, and she chose who that someone would be. That is not theft. That is testamentary instruction, and I am bound to honour it."

He reached into his briefcase and drew out the letter.

It was a plain envelope, softened by decades, bearing his name in ink gone the brown of old tea. He had read it perhaps forty times since the night at the cove, when Father Reeve had pressed it into his hands along with its twin — *I didn't take them from the strongroom,* Reeve had said, *but God forgive me, I know who did, and I'll not say the name until you've read what she wrote.* Rosalind had read it. He had wept over it, privately, once, in his lodgings above the Anchor, and then he had stopped weeping and started preparing.

"Mr. Brightwater." Mrs. Tresidder's voice was mild as milk. "Before you do anything theatrical, you might consider what you're holding. A document of unknown provenance, unsigned by any witness, produced by a man the police have twice interviewed regarding a break-in and a dead boy at the foot of our church tower. If you read that aloud in this room, you will be reading your own indictment."

The room had gone very quiet. Somewhere outside, gulls were working the harbour wind, and their cries came through the tall windows like laughter at a funeral.

"You mistake me, Mrs. Tresidder," Rosalind said pleasantly. "I am not reading my indictment. I am reading hers — the confession of a woman who spent thirty years doing penance for something that was half a sin and half a mercy, and all of it a tragedy. And I am reading it because the law has failed her twice: once when no one investigated the drowning of Tobias Chenoweth, and once when no one questioned a death certificate filed by a man who was standing next to the living woman at the time. The law is in this room today. So the truth may as well be."

He broke the seal.

* * *

The paper was thin as onion skin and had gone soft at the folds. His voice, when he began, sounded strange to him — too loud in the little room, too ordinary for the words.

*"If you are reading this, then I am dead at last, properly dead, and the sea can have what it has been owed since October of 1994.*

*"My name is Edith Penhallow. Not Morwenna. Morwenna Penhallow died in the spring of 1994, in this house, of the illness that was eating her, and she died in my arms with her hand in mine, and the last thing she said to me was: take my name, Edie. Take it and run. He can't make you marry him if you're already married to a ghost.*

*"You must understand what she was saving me from. Tobias Chenoweth was not a monster — I want that written somewhere, because everyone in this parish will make him one or make me one, and he was neither. He was a proud, frightened man who had loved my sister first and settled for me when she fell ill, and who could not bear the idea that the Penhallow money and the Penhallow name might slip through his fingers because the last Penhallow girl refused him. My father had promised us to each other before he died, and Tobias held to promises the way drowning men hold to wreckage. There was no loving him after Morwenna. There was only the tide coming in, slow, every day, a little closer.*

*"So when my sister begged me with her dying breath to become her, I said yes. It seemed a small sin set against a large one. Grebe drew the papers — God forgive him, he was kind and frightened and paid, all three. Callum — Father Reeve — wrote the certificate, because he loved me, though I never deserved it and never let myself deserve it, and that refusal is the one clean thing in this whole filthy arrangement. I buried Morwenna under my own name in the plot by the family stone, and I walked out of my own life wearing hers, and for eleven years I lived quietly in Falmouth as a widow woman, and no one ever looked at me twice, because being invisible is a thing you can learn, like French."*

Rosalind paused. In the second row, Father Reeve had closed his eyes. His lips moved — whether in prayer or in memory, Rosalind could not tell.

*"But Tobias would not let go. He learned what we had done — I never knew how; perhaps he saw us at the station, perhaps Morwenna wrote to him before the end, hoping he'd grieve instead of rage. He came to the house in October and said he would go to the police unless I came back and married him and let the whole thing be a joke between the four of us: the living sister, the dead sister, the priest, the lawyer. He said it as if it were reasonable. He said it while standing at the top of the cliff path at Penhallow Cove, and I followed him down because I thought if I could just get him away from the road, away from witnesses, I could talk him round.*

*"We argued on the rocks below the cliff. The sea was up. I told him the truth — that I had never loved him, that Morwenna had died begging me to escape him, that the marriage he wanted was a cage and I would die in it before I entered it. And he grabbed me. Not to kill me — I believe that with everything in me, I have had thirty years to examine it and I believe it — but to shake me, to make me stop saying those things, and the rock was wet, and he stumbled, and I — "*

The handwriting changed here. Rosalind had noticed it on his first reading: the letters grew larger, looser, as though the hand that made them had begun to shake, or to weep, or both.

*"— I put out my hands to catch my own balance and he went past me. He went off the ledge into the water and I heard him hit the rocks below and I did not go in after him. I stood on that ledge in the rain and I listened to the sea take him and I did not go in. I tell you honestly, whoever you are: I do not know if I could have reached him. I do not know if I tried hard enough to find out. That is my sin. Not murder. But something next door to it, and I have paid rent on that house every day for thirty years.*

*"I carried him up the path myself, bit by bit, in the dark. Callum helped me. We put him in the ground by the family stone because I could not bear the thought of him in the pauper rows, and because I wanted him near me, near us, near the sisters who broke him. Then Callum certified my death and I became a ghost in earnest, and ghosts, I find, keep better accounts than the living.*

*"To whoever holds this: I ask nothing except that the record be set straight. Let them dig if they must. Let them write on the stone what she was. I have been two women for thirty years and neither of them has been allowed to rest."*

Rosalind lowered the page.

* * *

For a moment the schoolroom was absolutely still — the particular stillness of forty people breathing carefully. Then it broke all at once, the way ice breaks, in a hundred directions at once.

Mrs. Tregose was crying without noise, her handkerchief crushed in her fist. The publican had gone grey. Old Pascoe rose halfway from his chair as if to flee and sat again, gripping his knees. Someone near the back said, aloud, "the locket — she wore the locket, I saw it, I *told* them—" and was shushed violently by three neighbours at once.

"Forgery."

Mrs. Tresidder's voice cut through it like a blade through wet canvas. She had risen. The pearls moved at her throat with the quickness of her breath, the only sign that anything beneath the grey wool was disturbed.

"That document is a forgery, and Mr. Brightwater is its forger. Consider the convenience of it, ladies and gentlemen. A letter that arrives — when? After a break-in. After a boy falls from our church tower. After this gentleman's own professional history begins to attract attention." She turned to the coroner, spreading her hands, the picture of wounded reasonableness. "Doctor, this man was interviewed by the police not ten days ago. His mentor, Grebe, is dying and cannot be cross-examined. Every living witness to whatever he claims is either dead, senile, or — " a glance, swift and surgical, at Father Reeve — "compromised. And we are asked to accept, on the strength of a piece of paper he alone controls, that an entire parish conspired for thirty years to conceal a death?"

"Not the parish," Rosalind said. "Five people. And you were one of them."

The words landed in the room like a dropped glass.

Mrs. Tresidder went very still. It lasted perhaps two seconds — long enough for Rosalind, who had spent his career watching faces across negotiating tables, to see everything: not guilt exactly, not yet, but the calculation of a woman discovering that the ground plan of the building had changed without her consent.

"I have no notion what you mean," she said.

"Then let me refresh you." Rosalind took a folded paper from his briefcase — not the confession, but the trustee minutes, the ones he had obtained from the diocesan registry on Tuesday morning with a subpoena and a great deal of patience. "In December 1994, two months after a woman named Edith Penhallow was legally buried, the Penhallow estate trustees convened an emergency session. The minutes record that the chair — yourself, Mrs. Tresidder — presented 'correspondence of a private nature' establishing that Miss Penhallow had 'settled her affairs informally prior to her decease,' and recommending that the estate be administered in trust 'pending clarification.' Pending clarification. For thirty years, Doctor. Do you know what the Penhallow rents amount to over thirty years? I do. I've done the arithmetic. It comes to rather more than the estate was worth on the day she died — which means the trust has been paying itself, year upon year, out of the estate of a woman who was alive in Falmouth the entire time, drawing her own pension under her sister's name, while four trustees divided the difference."

"That is slander—"

"It is arithmetic," Rosalind said. "Slander requires falsehood. Show me where I'm wrong. Take the minutes. Read them aloud. I'll wait."

She did not take the minutes. She stood in the chalky light with her hands at her sides and her chin high, and around her the room rearranged itself — Rosalind could feel it happening, the slow tectonic shift of forty villagers deciding, all at once and independently, whom they had been protecting all these years and whether he was worth protecting any longer. The publican looked at his shoes. Mr. Nance, the accountant-trustee, looked at Mrs. Tresidder with the expression of a man watching the tide come in around his ankles.

"The letter is genuine," Rosalind said more quietly. "You know it is, because you knew about the swap from the beginning. Grebe told me enough before he lost his nerve, and the diocesan registry told me the rest. You didn't forge the death, Mrs. Tresidder. You merely profited from it — and when a boy found the unmarked grave and started asking you for money to keep quiet about it, you found that profitable too, right up until the night he fell from the tower."

"I pushed no one." It came out fast, unguarded, the first true sentence she had spoken all morning, and she heard it land and closed her mouth over it a heartbeat too late.

Dr. Lanyon's pen was moving again. "Mrs. Tresidder," he said, in the tone of a man lifting a stone to see what lives beneath, "perhaps you'd care to rephrase that."

The silence stretched. Outside, the gulls wheeled and cried over the harbour, and the wind came through the tall windows carrying salt and the smell of the sea that had taken Tobias Chenoweth and been promised, thirty years ago, a woman it never got.

Mrs. Tresidder smoothed the front of her grey skirt with both hands, a small domestic gesture, absurdly calm, and when she raised her head her eyes had gone bright and hard as coins.

"I will rephrase nothing," she said, "until my solicitor is present. And when he arrives, Mr. Brightwater, I promise you — every document you have read today will be examined, every signature compared, and every person in this room who helped you build your little fiction will answer for it. Beginning with the man who wrote a death certificate for a woman he was sleeping with."

Every head turned to the second row.

Father Reeve opened his eyes. He looked, Rosalind thought, not afraid but relieved — the look of a man who has carried a coffin a very long way and has finally been given permission to set it down. He rose, buttoned his cassock, and said, in a voice that carried to the back of the room and out through the windows to the sea:

"Doctor, I'd like to amend my statement. All of it. Everything I told the police, everything I told the diocese, everything I've told anyone since October of 1994." He glanced at Rosalind — one look, at last, steady and tired and free. "Because Mr. Brightwater has read only part of what Edith wrote. There's a second letter. And there's a third hand in this business that hasn't shown itself yet — and before this inquiry goes another hour, I think it's time we talked about what Mrs. Tresidder's solicitor was doing at Penhallow House the night that boy died."
