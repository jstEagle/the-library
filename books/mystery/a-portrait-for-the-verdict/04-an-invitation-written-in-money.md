## 4. An Invitation Written in Money

There is a particular pleasure in burning paper at nine in the morning. It is the pleasure of a woman who has earned her fire — who has paid for the grate, for the oak it came from, for the whole cold house that holds it. Anyone can burn a letter at midnight, furtively, like a schoolgirl with a diary. It takes real position to burn correspondence in daylight, in full view of the gardeners, and to feel nothing but tidiness about it.

I was three pages into the morning's offerings when my lawyer arrived, which meant I was two pages into ash and one page into something more interesting.

The study at Ravenmoor faces east, because I prefer to watch the light come in over the water rather than watch it leave. Augustin chose that room for himself once — his father's room before him — and when he drowned, I did not move out of it. People expected me to. Grief is supposed to drive you from the dead man's chair, and I have always found grief to be an excellent seat. The leather has taken the shape of me now. That is more than most families manage.

"Madame Reyner." Vasseur stood in the doorway with his briefcase and his face arranged into the expression lawyers wear when they have bad news they intend to charge for. "You're burning things."

"I'm curating," I said. "There's a difference, and the difference is intent."

He crossed the room, set the case down on the low table — never on the desk; he knew better than to put anything on that desk — and waited while I fed another envelope to the flames. Bank correspondence. A charity wanting a wing named after us. A journalist whose curiosity had begun to cost him subscribers. All of it went the way of everything else in this family: quietly, completely, and without witnesses.

Then I opened the last letter, and stopped being bored.

*Dear Madame Reyner,* it began, in handwriting so carefully neutral it might have been typed by a machine designed to produce politeness. *Forgive the intrusion of a stranger. I am Odile Halloran, concierge of the Hôtel Verlaine in —*

I read it twice. The first time as a hostess reads, noting tone. The second time as I actually live, which is to say noting threat.

It was a beautiful letter. That was what made it dangerous. It asked nothing — or appeared to ask nothing. It mentioned, in passing, with the delicacy of someone setting down a wine glass at exactly the right moment, that the Verlaine had recently received correspondence concerning events at Ravenmoor some twelve years past, and that Madame Reyner would wish to know that her family's privacy was respected by establishments such as ours, and that should any clarification be useful, I remained entirely at your disposal.

Twelve years past. Events. Correspondence.

"You've gone still," Vasseur observed.

"I'm often still. It's how I think." I held the page toward the light, as though the ink might confess something the words had not. "Vasseur. What do you know about the Hôtel Verlaine?"

"The hotel? Old place, good reputation. Discreet. Kings have stayed there — or people who believe they were kings, which amounts to the same thing on an invoice." He frowned. "Why?"

"Because its concierge writes letters like a duelist writes apologies: courteously, and with the blade already through you." I laid the page flat on the desk, smoothing it with two fingers, the way one smooths a cat that may bite. "Look at the construction. No question anywhere. Not one interrogative in the entire document. Do you know what that means?"

"That she doesn't want answers."

"It means she already has them, or believes she does, and is giving me the opportunity to confirm her accuracy before she does something irreversible with it. This isn't an inquiry. It's a courtesy call from a firing squad." I sat back. "Who is she? Halloran. Find out everything. Where she comes from, what she's paid, whom she loves, what she drinks, whether she has debts, whether she has vices, whether she has anyone. I want to know if she can be bought, frightened, flattered, or married off. In ascending order of difficulty."

Vasseur had been with the family long enough that he no longer pretended not to understand instructions like these. He simply took out his notebook. "And the letter itself?"

I looked at the grate. The fire had settled into its working mood, all orange patience.

"Nothing," I said. "We answer nothing. We do not confirm, deny, correct, or console. If this Halloran creature has received a letter about twelve years ago, then someone is writing letters, and the last thing we do is teach them that writing letters produces results." I paused, and allowed myself the small honesty I permit myself perhaps twice a year. "Although."

"Although?"

"Although silence has a texture too. Silence says *guilty* to people who listen for it, and this one listens. You don't write a letter that careful unless you've spent years listening to what people don't say." I turned the page toward him. "So we won't be silent either. We'll be generous. There's a difference between saying nothing and having nothing to say, and only one of those is innocent."

* * *

Here is what everyone believes about me, and I have never once corrected them, because a reputation misunderstood in your favor is worth more than a fortune honestly explained.

They believe Camille Reyner is ice. Camille Reyner is marble. Camille Reyner felt nothing when her brother drowned, signed the condolence book in a hand steadier than the priest's, wore grey to the funeral instead of black because black is for people who need to announce their feelings. They believe the inheritance sat lightly on her, that she stepped into Ravenmoor like a woman stepping into shoes made for her all along.

All true, as far as it goes. What they miss is that ice is not the absence of temperature. Ice is water that has decided, very firmly, what it wants to be.

Augustin was the warm one. Everyone said so. Augustin laughed in rooms, Augustin remembered the gardener's daughter's name, Augustin would talk to anyone — drivers, waiters, the sort of people Father called *background*. And I loved him. I want that on the record somewhere, even if the record is only this: I loved my brother the way you love weather, helplessly and from a distance, admiring it right up until it floods your house.

He was going to ruin us. That is the sentence I carry, and I have carried it so long it has worn smooth as a river stone. He was going to take everything our grandfather built, everything Father defended like a border war, everything I had spent my entire life maintaining — the shipping lines, the land, the name — and he was going to give it away. Not squander it. Squandering I could have managed; squandering is at least a family vice. He was going to *give* it. To causes, to strangers, to a girl with ink-stained fingers who looked at him as though he were a person instead of an institution.

An institution is what keeps four hundred people employed. An institution is what pays for the roof over the village, the boats in the harbor, the school with our name carved over the door. Love is lovely, but love has never once met a payroll.

I told him so. Repeatedly. Over years. And in the end, on a night in October with rain coming in off the water, I told him so once more at the jetty, and I will say this much for myself: I did not plan what happened. Planning implies premeditation, and premeditation implies doubt — time enough to reconsider. There was no time. There was his voice, patient and terrible, explaining that the will was already drawn, and there was my hand, and there was the water.

Except.

Except that the body they pulled from the bay three days later was not quite the right weight. Except that the doctor who certified the housekeeper's death — the girl who vanished the same week, the girl who saw — had hands that shook when he signed. Except that every year, on the anniversary, something in this house goes quiet in a way that is not grief.

But I am getting ahead of myself, and a woman who gets ahead of herself ends up confessing to firelight, and firelight repeats everything.

* * *

By afternoon, Vasseur returned with a folder thin enough to be insulting and thick enough to be alarming.

"Odile Halloran," he said, laying it down. "Concierge of the Verlaine for nineteen years. Before that, nothing. No birth record we could find in the usual registries — the name appears fully formed around age twenty-five, like a stage actor taking a role. No spouse. No property beyond a flat above the hotel, rent controlled, held under a lease older than most governments. No debts." He said this last with the disappointment of a man reporting a locked door. "No vices we could purchase. Colleagues describe — well. Listen to this one. From a former guest, a countess, unverified: 'Halloran knows what you did before you know you've done it, and charges nothing for the knowledge, which is somehow worse.'"

"How poetic."

"There's more. The staff would die for her. Literally — there was an incident some years ago, a burglar, and three separate employees gave statements that contradicted each other so thoroughly the police concluded no burglary occurred. The burglar was never found. Neither was his van."

I turned a page. A photograph, grainy, taken from across a street: a figure at the hotel entrance in a dark coat, receiving a guest's luggage with both hands, head inclined at the angle of someone listening to a confession. The face was indistinct. The posture was not. You could have cut that silhouette out of paper and hung it in a gallery titled *Attention*.

"No family," I said slowly. "No leverage. No price."

"Not one we've found."

"Everyone has a price, Vasseur. Sometimes it's money. Sometimes it's a person. Sometimes it's only the truth, which is the most expensive thing there is, because you can't counterfeit it and you can't get it back once you've spent it." I closed the folder. "What does she want?"

"She says she wants to protect your family's privacy."

"And what does she *want*?"

Vasseur hesitated. He is a careful man, my lawyer, careful the way a man is careful when he has watched his employer burn things for twenty years. "The letter offers discretion," he said. "Which means she anticipates needing to withhold something. Which means she has something. Which means—" 

"—which means she is not selling silence. She's announcing it. Holding it up to the window so I can see the merchandise." I rose and went to the window. Below, the gardeners were cutting back the roses for winter, reducing the bushes to thorns and geometry, and I found the sight restful. Everything in this garden blooms only because someone decides annually what may live. "She wants to be invited. That's the whole game. She wrote a letter too polite to ignore and too pointed to answer, because she knows that the only response left is hospitality. She wants inside Ravenmoor, Vasseur, and she has engineered it so that I must open the door myself and smile while doing it."

"Then we don't invite her."

"Oh, we invite her." I watched the roses fall. "If we refuse, she digs. She has ledgers and postmarks and God knows what else, and she'll dig with the patience of the unemployed. If we invite her, she comes here, into this house, where every room is furnished with my decisions and every corridor leads where I choose. A spy in the field is dangerous. A spy at your dinner table is a guest. And guests—" I smiled, and felt my face do it properly, the way it was trained to, "—guests can be watched while they eat."

"You want to know what she knows."

"I want to know who sent her what. Because here is the part that interests me, Vasseur, the part I want you to hold onto: she said the Verlaine *received correspondence*. Plural possibilities in that word. Someone wrote to a hotel concierge about my brother's death, and I want to know who has suddenly grown a conscience after twelve years, and what they think they remember, and — most importantly — who they think is still alive to be remembered *by*."

The fire had burned down to a red arithmetic of coals. On the desk lay the last item of the morning's post, the one I had not yet fed to the flames: a plain envelope, unstamped, hand-delivered, containing a single sheet of good paper and nothing else. No signature. No message. Only a small pressed flower — sea lavender, the kind that grows wild along the jetty path — and beneath it, in handwriting I had last seen on a will I was assured no longer existed, five words:

*I remember the tide.*

I had burned bank statements, journalists, and charity. I had burned twelve years of other people's curiosity. This I folded, once, twice, and placed inside the lining of my desk drawer, behind the false back, next to the annual envelopes I intercept every October and have intercepted for twelve years, and I did not burn it because you do not burn evidence of a ghost. You keep it where you can see it. Fear is only useful if it stays sharp.

"Draft the invitation," I told Vasseur. "Consultation regarding the estate archives. Full courtesy. Car to meet her train. Tell her we welcome any friend of discretion."

"And if she asks what we wish to consult about?"

"We'll tell her the truth, or near enough. Tell her the family is reviewing its records. Tell her history requires a custodian." I picked up my pen and began, in my own hand, on my own paper, the note that would accompany the formal invitation — because a typed invitation is a summons, but a handwritten one is a lure. "Tell her, Vasseur, that Ravenmoor honors those who honor the dead."

Outside, the gardeners finished with the roses, and the beds stood bare and obedient, and the tide came in over the jetty exactly as it always did, covering everything it covered, keeping everything it kept.

Somewhere in the east, a train was carrying my guest toward me. I hoped she liked fish.
