## 8. A Death Certificate Too Clean

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in municipal records offices, and it is not the peaceful kind. It is the silence of paper that has not been touched in years — the silence of a filing system that has outlived everyone who understood it. I had spent three days earning my way into that silence, and by Thursday morning I was sitting in a basement room in the coastal prefecture with a cup of coffee that tasted like punishment and a clerk named Madame Ferrand who looked at me the way one looks at weather: with resignation and a certain grudging respect.

"You want a death record from 2013," she said.

"I do."

"Twelve years ago."

"Time is cruel that way."

She pulled off her glasses and studied me over them. "You're the concierge from the Verlaine. The one who wrote to us about the Reyner estate."

Word travels. It always does; I have built a career on the fact, so it would be hypocritical to complain when the current flowed the other direction. "The same," I said. "Though I'd be grateful if we kept my profession out of this. I'm here as a private citizen with an inconvenient curiosity."

"Private citizens don't request certified copies of records sealed under an estate dispute." She let that sit for a moment, then reached for her keyboard. "But you're not quite private either, are you? Your letter said you were retained as a consultant to the family."

I had written no such thing. Camille's lawyer had written *me*, using language carefully vague enough that I could neither confirm nor deny a retainer existed. Apparently someone at the prefecture had read the correspondence more generously than I had. I decided, in the space of half a second, not to correct the error. This is the sort of moral bookkeeping a concierge learns to do without flinching: I did not lie; I simply declined to interrupt a lie already in progress. The distinction matters to me more than it should.

"Consultant," Madame Ferrand repeated, typing. "Reyner estate. Name of the deceased?"

"Sylvie Arnaud." The name came out of me easily now. I had been saying it to myself for days, testing its weight. *Sylvie Arnaud, housekeeper, aged twenty-four, vanished the week Augustin Reyner drowned.* Everyone in the village had a version of her: the girl who ran off with a deckhand, the girl who stole silver, the girl who was never mentioned at all because mentioning her was bad luck, like whistling in a sickroom.

The printer groaned. Madame Ferrand retrieved the page, glanced at it, glanced again, and frowned — a small frown, professional rather than alarmed, the frown of a woman who has spent thirty years noticing things and has learned to keep most of them to herself.

"Huh," she said.

I have learned to fear "huh." In my experience, "huh" precedes everything interesting in this world.

* * *

The document itself was beautiful. That was my first thought, and it should have been my warning. Death certificates are not beautiful documents. They are bureaucratic confessions — cramped forms, hasty signatures, dates corrected in different inks, causes of death rendered in doctors' handwriting that looks like a seismograph during an earthquake. I have seen enough of them through the hotel's long acquaintance with grief to know their texture. They are human documents. They are messy the way dying is messy.

This one was immaculate.

Sylvie Arnaud, died the fourteenth of September, 2013, cause: drowning, place: the coastal waters off Ravenmoor. Certified by Dr. Émile Fournier, license number, address, signature. Every field complete. Every field *correct*. No corrections, no marginal notes, no hesitation marks where a pen had paused. The ink was uniform, the hand steady, the whole thing executed with the serene confidence of a form filled out by someone who knew exactly what the answer to every question would be before the questions were asked.

"Clean," I murmured.

"Very," said Madame Ferrand. "Too clean, if you want my opinion, which nobody ever does." She tapped the page. "Drowning certifications usually come through the maritime authority or the coroner, especially for open water. This went straight through a GP. That happens — small villages, everyone knows everyone — but it's rare enough to notice."

"And the doctor?"

"Fournier." She typed a little. "Émile Fournier, general practice, Saint-Aubin-les-Flots. Deceased himself, March 2014. Heart failure, age sixty-one." She shrugged. "He can't answer your questions."

"No," I agreed. "But his pen might."

She looked at me over her glasses again, longer this time. "You know what I tell people like you? People who come down here with polite letters and inconvenient curiosity?" She didn't wait for me to guess. "I tell them: the record is the record. It says what it says. If it says a woman died, then a woman died, and the fact that she died tidily is not evidence of anything except a tidy doctor."

"Of course," I said.

"But," she said, and here she lowered her voice as though the filing cabinets might inform on her, "if I were a tidy doctor certifying a drowning I hadn't witnessed, I'd still make a mistake somewhere. Everyone makes mistakes. I've never once seen a death certificate with nothing wrong with it. Not in thirty years." She slid the copy across the desk to me, stamped and certified, official as scripture. "So if you find something wrong with that one, you come back and tell me, because I'd like to know I haven't lost my touch."

I promised her I would. I am careful about promises made in basements; they tend to be the ones I actually keep.

* * *

Back at the Verlaine, the afternoon performed its usual theater — a departing opera singer who required eleven pieces of luggage and two apologies, a honeymooning couple who wanted a restaurant recommendation and got one along with a reservation they didn't ask for, Basile holding the door with the gravity of a man guarding a national border. I moved through all of it with the smooth surface of competence while underneath, the certificate sat in my mind like a stone in a shoe.

Immaculate. Too clean. Madame Ferrand had never seen such a certificate in thirty years, and she was right — I had handled dozens through the years, condolence letters to write, insurance queries to field, and every single one bore some small scar of haste or doubt. A doctor's hand trembles. A date gets smudged. Someone writes "approx." somewhere, because death refuses to be punctual even on paper.

Someone had manufactured Sylvie Arnaud's death with the care of a jeweler setting a stone.

That evening, after the lobby emptied and Marguerite took up her post at the night auditor's desk with her reading lamp and her infinite patience, I spread my materials on the back office table. I am not naturally a conspiracy theorist. Suspicion is a tool I use the way I use a corkscrew — precisely, and only when there's a cork. But I laid out what I had: the confession letter, unsigned, mailed from the Reyner village, naming "the witness in the portrait." The promise-of-payment letter, also from the village, offering a fortune for finding that same witness. And now the death certificate, clean as a lie told slowly.

One thread connected them all: handwriting.

The confession letter had been written in a careful, upright script — the writing of someone controlling themselves letter by letter. I had photographed the certificate's signature block on the train home, and now I set the images side by side under the desk lamp.

Dr. Émile Fournier's signature on Sylvie Arnaud's death certificate was a masterpiece of legibility. *E. Fournier,* each letter formed, each stroke confident, the sort of signature a man produces when he wants to be read.

Doctors' signatures are never legible. This is not a quirk; it is practically a medical condition. Half the correspondence that crosses a hotel desk involves deciphering some physician's scrawl on a guest's letter or prescription. So before concluding anything, I did what any reasonable person would do: I spent two hours on the telephone being transferred between offices, and then I did something slightly less reasonable. I called the regional medical council, identified myself as a consultant reviewing historical records — technically true, if one stretches the definition of both "consultant" and "reviewing" — and requested examples of Dr. Fournier's signature from other documents he had signed during his career. Prescriptions, referrals, vaccination records, anything.

The clerk there was less charmed than Madame Ferrand, but bureaucracy has its own gravity, and by Friday morning a fax arrived — yes, a fax; the medical council exists in a state of dignified timelessness — containing copies of four documents bearing Émile Fournier's signature across a span of nine years.

I laid them beside the death certificate.

They were not the same hand.

Not close. Not a matter of age or illness or a bad pen day. The real Fournier wrote with a forward slant, a heavy initial stroke, a distinctive crossed loop in the F that looked like a fence rail. The signature on Sylvie Arnaud's certificate stood perfectly upright, light-handed, the F uncrossed, the final r trailing into a small precise flourish that belonged to no one — because it wasn't a habit, it was a performance. Whoever signed that certificate had studied Fournier's name and reproduced it the way an actor reproduces a face: convincingly at a distance, fatally upon inspection.

And then I saw the detail that turned my stomach from interest into dread.

The flourish. That small trailing r. I had seen it before — twice, in fact, in the last week. Once in the confession letter, at the end of the word *murder*. Once in the promise-of-payment letter, at the end of the word *fortune*. The same little flourish, tucked into the same terminal position, the tic of a hand that doesn't know it has a tic.

Two letters, written in what appeared to be entirely different hands — one cramped and urgent, one flowing and businesslike — sharing a single unconscious gesture at the ends of words. And that gesture matching the forged signature on a dead woman's death certificate.

Someone had killed Sylvie Arnaud on paper. And whoever held the pen had written both of my letters.

I sat very still in the back office. Through the door I could hear the lobby's evening sounds — Basile's low voice, the lift's patient hum, a spoon against porcelain at the bar. The hotel going about its eternal business, unaware that one of its own was sitting in the dark holding a piece of paper that meant a woman had been erased so thoroughly that even her death had been counterfeited to fit.

Here is the arithmetic that kept me awake that night. If Sylvie Arnaud's death was fabricated, then she may never have died at all — which aligned, uncomfortably neatly, with the confession letter's claim that a witness survived. But if she lived, someone had gone to extraordinary lengths to bury her: a forged certificate, a dead doctor whose signature could never be compared, twelve years of silence maintained by — what? Money, certainly. Fear, probably. And the second letter, promising a fortune for finding her, suddenly looked less like generosity and more like a search warrant written in perfume. Someone wanted that woman found. Found by whom, delivered to whom, and for what purpose — those were the questions the letters declined to answer.

And one more thing, the thing I turned over last, the way one saves the bitterest bite for the end. Dr. Fournier had died thirteen months after signing. Heart failure, sixty-one years old. Perhaps coincidence. Villages are full of coincidences. But I have noticed, in my years behind the desk, that people who facilitate convenient deaths have a way of having them.

Marguerite found me at midnight, standing at the window overlooking the empty street, the certificates fanned out on the table behind me like a losing hand.

"You've got the look," she said. She poured two fingers of something amber without asking and set it near my elbow. "The look you get when a guest's problem stops being a puzzle and starts being a person."

"It was never a puzzle," I said. "Puzzles are fair. Someone built this."

She looked at the papers, and I watched her read the way she reads ledgers — fast, sideways, missing nothing. Her eyes stopped on the flourish comparison. She was quiet for a long moment.

"Odile," she said finally, and she almost never uses my name, "when the night auditor notices a discrepancy, there are two kinds. The kind you report, and the kind you report and then start locking your office." She tapped the forged signature once, lightly. "This is the second kind. Whatever you think you've found — a housekeeper who didn't die, a doctor who signed his own forgery, letters from a ghost — understand what it means. Somebody committed murder on paper and got away with it for twelve years. People who manage that don't forgive auditors."

"I know," I said.

"Do you?" She refilled my glass, which was her way of ending an argument she expected to lose. "Because I've watched you chase trouble before, and you always come back with the story and give it away for free. This one isn't free. This one has a price tag, and I don't think it's printed yet."

She left me with the whiskey and the dark window. Below, the street lay quiet, lamplit, ordinary. Somewhere inland, in a village that smelled of salt and old money, a woman who officially died twelve years ago was either dust or breathing, and someone with a practiced hand and a fatal flourish was waiting to learn which.

I gathered the papers, locked them in the office safe — the one guests believe holds spare passports, and which actually holds everything the Hotel Verlaine has ever needed to survive — and stood a moment with my hand on the dial.

Tomorrow I would go back to Ravenmoor. There was an archive there, and an archivist who wouldn't look up, and a gallery with a crooked portrait and empty hooks. And now there was a question I could not unask, ringing in me like a struck bell:

If Sylvie Arnaud never died —

who buried her alive?
