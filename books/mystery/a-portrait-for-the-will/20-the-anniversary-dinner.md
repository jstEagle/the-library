## 20. The Anniversary Dinner

Ashcombe Hall had been dressed for dinner the way a corpse is dressed for a funeral: beautifully, and by people who knew it would never notice.

Noor stood at the edge of the great drawing room with a glass of untouched champagne and watched two hundred years of the town's history arrange itself around candlelight. The founding family's portraits had been taken down from the walls for cleaning — all except one, the old patriarch in his cracked varnish, who hung above the fireplace like a chaperone who disapproved of everyone present. Beneath him, the heirs of Ashcombe mingled with the servants of Ashcombe, the lawyers of Ashcombe, and the hangers-on who had learned to pronounce *Ashcombe* with the correct softness on the second syllable.

She had chosen her dress the way she chose her opening moves in chess: unremarkable, forgettable, impossible to read. Dark green. Pockets. A woman with pockets is a woman with options.

"You look like you're about to arrest someone," Imogen Hale murmured, materializing at her elbow in police-issue formalwear that fooled no one. Her badge was somewhere it shouldn't have been, and her eyes were doing arithmetic over Noor's shoulder.

"I'm holding champagne. That's practically a costume."

"The false codicil went into the estate file at four o'clock. Vane's office confirmed he requested a copy within the hour."

"Then he knows the will names *him*." Noor turned the glass slowly. "Which means tonight he has exactly one problem left."

"Which is?"

"Me." She smiled at someone across the room — old Mrs. Penhale, who smiled back with the cautious warmth of a woman who'd heard rumors. "The genealogist is dead and blamed. The codicil is forged and planted. The only loose thread is a retired bookseller who keeps asking questions nobody hired her to ask. If I were Edmund Vane, I'd want me dead before the toast."

"And if you were Edmund Vane," Hale said, "you'd also want to be standing next to me when it happened, so I could watch your face."

They looked at each other.

"He's by the window," Hale said. "Green waistcoat. Talking to Julian Ashcombe like they're old friends."

"They're not. Julian met him for the first time three days ago. I checked." Noor sipped her champagne at last. It was excellent, which annoyed her; she wanted nothing about this evening to be pleasant. "That's the trick of him, Imogen. He doesn't insert himself into families. He lets them think they invited him. Twenty years of practice. He was Aldous Wren's protégé before Wren vanished — he learned everything from the ledger man himself. How to stage an accident. How to wait. How to make grief do the paperwork."

Across the room, Edmund Vane laughed at something Julian said — a warm, modest laugh, the laugh of a man delighted to be included. He was handsome in the way furniture is handsome: well-made, well-kept, designed not to draw the eye. He wore the family friend role like the coat he'd once borrowed from Noor's closet. Everything fit because he had studied the measurements.

"He's been watching us since we came in," Hale said.

"Of course. He wants to see whether we know. And we want him to think we don't. So we're going to stand here being bad actors until the toast."

"God, I hate theater."

"You're a detective. Theater is most of the job."

Hale snorted into her own glass. For a moment they were just two women at a party, and Noor let herself have the moment, because she suspected it would be the last easy one for a long time.

* * *

Dinner was served at eight. Noor found herself seated between Julian Ashcombe and a distant cousin who talked exclusively about drainage.

"You're staring at Vane again," Julian said quietly, under the soup.

"I'm staring at the window. He happens to be near it."

"Noor." He set down his spoon. His hands were steady but his voice wasn't quite. "Whatever you're doing tonight — whatever you and that detective are doing — I need you to tell me if it touches my family. All of them. Even the ones I haven't forgiven."

It was, she thought, the first entirely honest thing anyone had said to her in weeks. The disinherited son, back from twenty years abroad, asking after the family that had cut him out. Grief, not guilt. Her instincts had said so in his book-lined flat, surrounded by photographs of a mother who never wrote. Instincts had lied to her before. But not, she believed, about Julian.

"It touches everyone at this table," she said. "And before this evening ends, some of them are going to learn things about themselves they won't thank me for. When it starts — and it will start at the toast — I need you to keep your seat. Whatever happens. Can you do that?"

He studied her for a long moment. Candlelight moved in his eyes.

"My mother used to say you were the only person in this town who saw clearly and pretended not to," he said. "I thought she was being romantic." He picked up his spoon. "Keep your seat. Fine. But if anyone points a gun at you, all bets are off."

"That's the most romantic thing anyone's said to me in years."

The cousin leaned across. "Did you know the causeway drains were redone in '94? Terrible work. Terrible."

* * *

Between the fish course and the meat, Noor excused herself and walked the long gallery alone.

This was the part of the plan she hadn't told Hale in full, because Hale would have forbidden it. The gallery ran along the east wing, past the portraits of every Ashcombe who had ever mattered, and at its end stood the small study where the estate papers were kept. Where, at four o'clock that afternoon, a forged codicil had been slipped into a folder that Edmund Vane had already requested a copy of — a codicil naming *him*, illegitimate son of Aldous Wren's arrangement, heir to everything.

A lie, of course. A beautiful, poisoned lie. The real truth — the fragment the genealogist had swallowed rather than surrender — named no one Vane expected. But Vane didn't know that yet. Vane only knew that the paper trail now pointed at him, and that the woman who'd planted it was in the building.

He would come for her before the toast. He had to. A dead witness couldn't testify.

She stood in the dark gallery and listened to the dinner sounds drifting down the corridor — laughter, silverware, the cousin's voice rising on the subject of culverts — and she felt, unexpectedly, calm. This was the room she'd lived in for thirty years: the quiet just before something terrible, the stillness where she did her best work. She had spent a decade making herself invisible. Tonight invisibility was a weapon.

Footsteps. Soft ones, unhurried, coming up the gallery.

She stepped behind the portrait of Reginald Ashcombe, third of his name, a man whose painted eyes had seen worse than her.

Edmund Vane passed close enough to touch. He carried no weapon she could see. He paused at the study door, worked something in the lock — a key, of course; he'd have copied it months ago — and slipped inside.

Noor counted to sixty. Then she crossed the corridor to the service door Hale had shown her that afternoon, the one that opened into the study's adjoining closet, and she let herself into the dark among the dust sheets, and she waited.

Through the closet's slatted door she watched Vane move through the study by the light of a small torch. He went straight to the estate files — no searching, no hesitation — and drew out the folder. She heard paper. She heard him stop breathing for a moment.

Then she heard him laugh. Quietly. To himself. A laugh with no warmth in it at all, the furniture-handsome face stripped down to the frame.

"Clever," he murmured, to the empty room or to her or to the ghost of Aldous Wren. "Clever, clever bookseller."

He took out his phone. Photographed the codicil. Then, methodically, he fed the pages into the cold fireplace, produced a lighter, and burned them — burning evidence that implicated no one, destroying a forgery as though it were the genuine article, tidying the record the way he'd tidied three deaths and planned a fourth.

When the fire had eaten everything, he straightened his waistcoat, checked his reflection in the darkened window, and left.

Noor stayed in the closet another full minute, heart drumming, and permitted herself one small, fierce smile.

He'd taken the bait. He believed the codicil was real, believed the net was closing, believed he had perhaps an hour before Hale moved on him. Which meant the toast would come early. Which meant everything would happen now.

* * *

She returned to the ballroom as coffee was being poured. The patriarch's portrait glowered above the fireplace. Candles guttered in their hundreds. Old Mrs. Ashcombe — ninety-one, sharp as a hatpin — rose from her chair at the head of the table with the help of a grandson, and the room began to settle into the hush that precedes toasts.

Vane was beside her almost immediately. "You've been exploring," he said pleasantly.

"I get lost in big houses. It's a bookseller thing. We're used to small rooms."

"Mm. You know, Miss Winterbourne, I feel I know you better than our brief acquaintance suggests. Your shop. Your habits. Your taste in coats." His smile was flawless. "I've admired your mind for some time. Aldous spoke of it often, near the end."

"Aldous Wren spoke of my mind?"

"He spoke of everyone's minds. It was how he collected them." He offered his arm toward the table. "Shall we? The toast is about to begin, and I should hate for anything to interrupt it."

There it was. The invitation with a knife in it. She took his arm anyway, because the plan required her to be exactly where he wanted her, and because her skin crawled and she refused to let it show.

They reached the table. Mrs. Ashcombe lifted her glass. Two hundred faces turned toward her, candlelit and expectant.

"To two hundred years," the old woman began, her voice thin but carrying, "and to the family that—"

"Forgive me." Vane's voice slid into the pause, smooth as poured cream. "Before we drink — there's a matter of the estate that should be settled tonight, while we're all together. A codicil has come to light."

The hush changed texture. Noor felt the whole room lean.

"A codicil," repeated Mrs. Ashcombe, dry as August.

"Discovered among Aldous Wren's papers. It concerns... legitimacy. An heir the family never acknowledged." He let the word *heir* hang in the candlelight, and Noor watched heads turn toward Julian, watched the old wounds open on schedule. "I believe the document may name—" he paused, savoring it, "—me."

Gasps, properly distributed. He had rehearsed this; she could hear the rehearsal in the rhythm. In one sentence he had made himself the story, claimed the inheritance publicly before any court could question it, and positioned the coming death of the meddling bookseller as the desperate act of a cornered fraud.

"But surely," Vane continued, turning to Noor with sorrowful magnanimity, "Miss Winterbourne can speak to this. She's been investigating so diligently. Tell them what you found in the study, Miss Winterbourne. Tell them what you burned."

Ah. There it was — the reversal, pre-loaded. He'd burn the codicil, then claim *she* had destroyed it. Her word against a beloved family friend's. And soon, if his plan held, a dead woman's word against no one at all.

Noor set down her glass. The sound of it on the tablecloth was very small and very loud.

"What I found in the study," she said, "was you, Mr. Vane, photographing a document and then burning it. What I found in the mill gallery was chalk marks in my own notation system. What I found in my own closet was a wardrobe missing the clothes a drowned man was wearing. And what I found in Aldous Wren's hidden ledger — the real one, not the one you kept for him — was twenty years of coaching notes. Four accidents. One author."

The room had gone absolutely still. Somewhere a candle spat.

"You're confused," Vane said gently, to the room, to the family, the picture of patience. "The poor woman has been under a strain—"

"Imogen," Noor said, "now, please."

Hale stepped out of the crowd with two uniformed officers who had been passing champagne all evening, and behind her came the hall's housekeeper carrying a small wooden box, and behind *her* — to Noor's genuine surprise — came Julian Ashcombe, keeping his seat at the table exactly as promised, white-knuckled and upright, watching everything.

"Edmund Vane," Hale said, "I'm arresting you for the murder of Tobias Finch, and for conspiracy in the deaths of Harriet Ashcombe, Daniel Croft, and Peter Lyle. We recovered the ledger yesterday from the safe deposit box you opened under Wren's name in March. Your handwriting is in it. So is your plan for tonight."

For one moment — one long, lit, suspended moment — Edmund Vane simply looked at Noor. And the mask didn't drop so much as dissolve, the way frost leaves a window: gradually, then all at once, leaving the plain cold glass beneath. What looked out of his face was not rage. It was disappointment, almost scholarly, the look of a craftsman watching an apprentice ruin good work.

"The ledger was a decoy," he said softly. Only Noor heard it. "You know it was. I wrote that ledger *for* whoever found it. Just like I wrote your cases for you, Miss Winterbourne. Every page. Every chalk mark. You've been reading my manuscript all year and calling it your investigation."

"Then here's my review," Noor said. "The ending's derivative. I solved the first draft thirty years ago."

His hand moved — toward his pocket, toward something — and three things happened at once: Hale stepped in, an officer caught his arm, and Julian Ashcombe finally broke his promise and rose from his chair, knocking it over with a crack that rang through the hall like a verdict.

But Vane wasn't reaching for a weapon. He was reaching for his glass.

He raised it — handcuffs closing on his wrist, officers at both shoulders, two hundred guests frozen mid-gasp — and he toasted Noor Winterbourne across the wreckage of his perfect design, with a smile that was almost, horribly, sincere.

"To the invisible woman," he said. "Enjoy the visibility. It costs more than you think."

They led him out between the candles. The flames bent in the draft of the opening doors and stood up straight again.

Mrs. Ashcombe, ninety-one and unmoved, looked around the shattered room, lifted her glass higher, and said, "To two hundred years. We've survived worse. Sit *down*, all of you, the coffee's getting cold."

And the extraordinary thing — the thing Noor would remember afterward, in the weeks of statements and testimony and a town discovering her name — was that they did. They sat down. They drank. Life, at Ashcombe Hall, insisted on itself.

Only Julian remained standing. He came to her through the candlelight, past the overturned chair, and stopped close enough that she could see his hands shaking now that it was over.

"The ledger was a decoy," he repeated slowly. "He said it was a decoy. Noor — if the ledger was fake, then what did the genealogist actually find? What did he die with?"

Noor looked at the patriarch's portrait, at the old man's cracked varnish and painted eyes, and felt the night tilt beneath her feet.

"That," she said, "is a very good question. And I'm afraid the answer is still inside a dead man — unless the coroner's fragment says what I think it says. Imogen needs to decode it tonight. Because if I'm right about what that parchment names..."

She looked at Julian's face, at the family resemblance he couldn't help, and finished quietly:

"...then the heir Edmund Vane killed for isn't in this room. And the person the true will protects has been sitting at this table all along — and Vane's protégés aren't the only ones who read other people's mail."
