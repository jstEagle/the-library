## 3. Postmarked Yesterday

The morning mail came to the coroner's office the way most things came to Marrow Creek: late, damp, and without apology.

Juniper Hartwell heard the mail truck's engine cough at the corner of Ash and Second—hearing it the way he heard everything, with the particular attention of a man who had spent thirty years listening for what people did not say. He was at his desk with a cup of coffee that had gone cold an hour ago, which was how he preferred it. Hot coffee encouraged haste. Cold coffee encouraged thought, and thought was the only part of his job the county had never thought to budget for.

He signed for the death certificates first—two, both routine, both honest work—and then the funeral home invoices, and then a flyer from the feed store announcing a sale on fencing posts that Juniper read with more interest than he would ever admit to, because a man who lived alone had to take his pleasures where he found them.

At the bottom of the stack was a plain white envelope.

No return address. His name typed in the center of the face—*JUNIPER HARTWELL, CORONER, MARROW CREEK*—in letters that had come off a machine, even and unbothered, the way letters looked when someone wanted them to look like no one at all had written them. He turned it over. The postmark was smudged at one edge but legible, and when he read it he sat very still for a moment, the way he did at scenes, the way he'd taught every deputy who'd ever shadowed him: *don't touch anything until you've understood what you're touching.*

The postmark was dated the day of Edith Calloway's funeral.

Seven days ago. The mail did not sit in Marrow Creek for a week. The mail in Marrow Creek was slow, but it was not *that* slow. Which meant the letter had been mailed and then held—or mailed from somewhere that held it—or, and this was the possibility that made Juniper set down his cold coffee with a small deliberate click, mailed on time and delivered on time and then kept, by someone, for six days, before being slipped into the stack where it would do the most work.

He put on his glasses. He took a pair of cotton gloves from the drawer—he kept them for handling evidence, and also, though he'd have denied it, because his grandmother had raised him to respect paper—and he opened the envelope with a letter opener shaped like a heron, a gift from a widow whose husband's autopsy he'd handled with what she'd called "unusual tenderness."

One page. Typed. He read it twice before he let himself breathe.

*To the coroner of Marrow Creek, and no other.*

*I killed Thomas Vane on the fourteenth of June, 1994. I drowned him in Miller's Bend with my hands and my weight and my will, and the county called it an accident because the county wanted to. I am the only one who knows this, and now you are the only one who knows that I know.*

*You will want to know why I am telling you. Because you are the only man in this town who has ever looked at a dead body and asked it the right questions. I have watched you do it. You asked Edith Calloway's body questions at the funeral, standing over her in the ground, and I saw you not believe what you were told.*

*You will find my name at the bottom of this letter. You will not believe it. That is correct. Do not believe it. Believe the date instead, and the river, and the way his left shoe was never found.*

*—Dolores Finch*

Juniper read it a third time. Then a fourth. Then he took off his glasses, cleaned them on his shirttail, put them back on, and read it a fifth time, because in thirty years of this work he had learned that shock is just information arriving faster than the mind's furniture can accommodate it, and the remedy is to slow the information down until the furniture catches up.

The furniture was not catching up.

Thomas Vane. June 14, 1994. Juniper had been a young deputy then, not yet the coroner, not yet the man the town brought its dead to the way they brought their sick to Doc Pell's back door. He remembered the case the way you remember weather from your childhood—not the details, but the feeling of it. A boy drowned at Miller's Bend. Seventeen years old. The whole town had worn its grief like a borrowed coat, too big, not quite theirs. The sheriff at the time—Sheriff Boone, a good man with a tired heart—had ruled it accidental, and nobody had argued, and Juniper himself had stood at the edge of the water that June and felt the case close around him like a door.

And the left shoe.

He had not thought of the left shoe in thirty years. Thomas Vane's body had come out of the water with one shoe on and one shoe gone, and the river had been dragged twice and the shoe had never surfaced, and Sheriff Boone had said rivers keep what rivers keep, and everyone had nodded, and Juniper—twenty-six years old and certain that older men knew things he didn't—had nodded too.

The letter knew about the shoe.

Not the kind of thing that appeared in the newspaper account. Not the kind of thing a person remembered from gossip, which had concerned itself, in that season, with who cried at the funeral and who didn't. The shoe was a detail from the recovery report. A detail Juniper himself had written into the file, in his own careful deputy's hand, thirty years ago.

*Believe the date, and the river, and the way his left shoe was never found.*

Juniper sat in the quiet of his office and felt the cold arrive. Not the cold of fear—not yet. The cold of a pattern completing itself, the way a chord completes when the last note is finally struck. Edith Calloway, drowned, with bruising on her shoulders that the sheriff preferred not to see. Thomas Vane, drowned, thirty years and eleven months before her. And now a confession, arriving postmarked the day of her burial, naming Juniper Hartwell as its only witness.

Somebody wanted him to look. Somebody wanted him to look *now*, before the ground over Edith settled, before the town's attention moved on the way the town's attention always moved on, gentle and merciless as water.

Or—and Juniper turned this thought over too, because he turned everything over, it was his one vice and his one virtue—somebody wanted him to look *away* from something else. A letter could be a window or it could be a curtain. He had learned, over four decades of the dead, that the difference was usually discoverable, if you were patient, if you were honest, if you were willing to be wrong in public.

He was willing. He'd had practice.

* * *

He did what he always did with a document that mattered: he made a copy, he locked the original in the steel cabinet that had come with the office in 1989 and had never once been broken into, largely because everyone in Marrow Creek knew Juniper kept a shotgun behind it and everyone in Marrow Creek also knew Juniper had never once raised his voice, and the combination of the two facts was more deterrent than either alone.

Then he called Dolores Finch.

There was no answer at her house on Cordwainer Lane, which did not surprise him. Dolores Finch was eighty-one years old and had lived alone since her sister passed, and her sister had been the one who answered the phone. Juniper left a message in the voice he used for the living, which was warmer than the one he used for the dead, though not by much—Dolores had once told him he sounded like a man reading a grocery list to a jury, and he'd taken it as the compliment it was half meant to be.

Then he called the Pines, the care facility out past the quarry road, and got a young man named Theo who said yes, Mrs. Finch was a resident, yes, she was having a good day, and did the coroner want him to put her on?

"Please."

There was a pause with shuffling in it, and then Dolores's voice, thin and clear, like a bell heard across a field.

"Juniper Hartwell. Well. I didn't think you'd call about the casserole."

"What casserole?"

"The one I sent after Edith's funeral. You didn't come to the funeral, you know. I looked for you."

"I was there," Juniper said. "I don't stand with the family. I stand at the back, where I can see everybody."

There was a silence on the line, and in it he heard something shift—something small and careful, the sound of an old woman recalibrating. "And what did you see at the back, Juniper?"

"Mostly I saw who wasn't crying," he said. "But I'm not calling about the funeral, Dolores. I'm calling because I received a letter this morning. It's signed with your name."

The silence that followed was longer. When Dolores spoke again, her voice had changed—not thinner, but tighter, like wire drawn taut.

"Did it say anything about a shoe?"

Juniper closed his eyes. The cold in his chest went a degree deeper. "It did."

"Then come and see me," she said. "Not on the phone. Come and see me, and bring the letter, and don't bring the sheriff, and don't tell the sheriff, and Juniper—" her voice caught on something, an old stone in an old stream, "—don't tell *anyone*. Not yet. There's a man in the county jail who shouldn't be there, and there's a person walking around this town who has killed four times, and if you say my name out loud where the walls are thin, I will be the fifth."

The line went quiet. Juniper held the phone against his ear and listened to the nothing, which was not nothing at all; it was an old woman's breath, and a care facility's distant television, and underneath both, the sound of thirty years of silence beginning, at last, to crack.

"Dolores," he said gently. "Four times?"

"Come and see me," she said. "Tomorrow. Ten o'clock. And Juniper—eat the casserole. It's got too much sage in it. I always put too much sage in. Ruth Ann used to say—"

She stopped. The stop had a shape to it, the shape of a name swallowed whole.

"Ten o'clock," Juniper said. "I'll be there."

* * *

He did not sleep well that night, which was not unusual, but he slept badly in a new way, which was. He lay in the narrow bed in the narrow house he'd lived in since his wife left him twenty-two years ago—she'd said he loved the dead more than the living, and he'd never found a way to argue, because the dead were easier; they never needed him to be anything but attentive—and he listened to the house tick and settle and he turned the letter over in his mind the way he'd turned Edith's bruising over, looking for the angle of the blow.

*Four times.* Dolores had said four. Edith made four if you counted her. Which meant three before her, and Juniper lay in the dark and did the arithmetic he had spent his whole career doing without letting himself do it: the deaths he had quietly, privately, in the locked drawer of his own conscience, concluded were not accidents.

Old Man Weller, 2009, at the bottom of his own cellar stairs, with the bruise on his hip that didn't match a fall. Marnie Kettle, 2016, in her garage with the engine running, whose wrists had shown the hesitation marks of a woman who had changed her mind and been made to stay. And now Edith. Three. He had filed his doubts and buried his conclusions and told himself, each time, that a coroner's job was the body and not the verdict, and each time the telling-himself had cost him something he didn't get back.

If Dolores was right—if there were four, and they were connected, and they reached back to a drowned boy in 1994—then Juniper had spent three decades standing in a room where a slow murder was happening, one death per decade, patient as farming, and he had seen the furniture move and called it settling.

He got up at four in the morning and made coffee he did not drink hot and sat at his kitchen table with the letter's copy in front of him and read it again by the light over the stove.

*I have watched you do it.*

Someone had watched him at Edith's graveside. He had stood at the back of the cemetery, as he always did, and someone had stood where they could see *him*, and had seen the thing he believed no one saw: that he did not believe. That was a specific kind of attention. Not the town's fond, vague attention—*old Juniper, good man, odd duck*—but close attention, patient attention, the attention of someone who had been watching for a long time and had finally seen what they were waiting for.

And the letter had been held for six days. Mailed the day of the burial, delivered a week late. Juniper turned that over too. If you wanted a letter to arrive at a particular moment—after the funeral, yes, but why the delay?—you either held it yourself, or you gave it to someone to hold, or you mailed it from somewhere the mail was slow.

Or you mailed it and then took it back out of the box, which was a federal offense and also, in Marrow Creek, a thing a person could do, because the mail carrier was Earl Pruitt and Earl Pruitt left his truck running with the door open while he bought his morning biscuits, and half the town had seen him do it, and half the town had, at one time or another, been tempted.

Juniper wrote *EARL* on a legal pad and underlined it, and then, feeling slightly ashamed of suspecting a man he'd known since Earl was a boy stealing apples, wrote *or someone who knows Earl's habits* and underlined that instead.

At dawn he stood at his kitchen window with the cold coffee and watched the light come up over the creek, and he thought about Dolores Finch's voice on the telephone, the way it had tightened around the word *shoe* like a hand around a rope.

*There's a person walking around this town who has killed four times.*

He believed her. That was the thing that frightened him—not the letter, not the watching, not the four deaths arranged across thirty years like stations of a terrible cross. What frightened him was how easily he believed her. Because some part of him, the part that had filed three sets of doubts in a locked drawer and called it duty, had been waiting for this letter for thirty years. Waiting for someone to tell him he hadn't been imagining it. Waiting for permission.

The letter was permission. And permission, Juniper knew from long experience with the dead, was the most dangerous thing a person could hand you.

He rinsed the cup. He put on his good coat—the brown one, the one Dolores had once said made him look like a schoolmaster who'd lost his school—and he took the locked copy of the letter out of the cabinet, and he drove out past the quarry road toward the Pines, toward an eighty-one-year-old woman with too much sage in her casserole and thirty years of a dead boy's secret in her keeping.

The morning was bright and ordinary. The town looked the way it always looked, kind and closed, its windows washed, its secrets kept.

Somewhere in it, Juniper thought, a person was walking around who had killed four times—and who had chosen *him*, of all people, to be the witness.

He meant to find out why.

He meant to find out who.

And as he turned onto the quarry road, he noticed, in his rearview mirror, a gray sedan that had been behind him since Ash and Second, keeping a polite, patient, unhurried distance—the distance of someone who was not following him at all, and wanted very much for him to think so.
