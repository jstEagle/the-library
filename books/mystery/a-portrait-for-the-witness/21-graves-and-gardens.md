## 21. Graves and Gardens

The first Saturday of May arrived in Marrow Creek the way forgiveness does—quietly, and after a long wait.

Juniper Hartwell had been awake since five, not from insomnia but from anticipation, which was a novelty he intended to savor. For thirty years his Saturdays had belonged to the cemetery: mowing around the stones he knew by name, straightening the ones that leaned, talking to the dead in the low, unhurried voice he'd never quite learned to use with the living. He had tended these graves the way some men tend stamp collections—meticulously, privately, with no expectation that anyone would ever notice or care.

Today, forty-one people were going to show up with trowels.

He'd counted the RSVPs twice. Dolores had organized them from her wheelchair at the care facility's garden table, armed with a clipboard, a seed catalog, and the imperious energy of a woman who had spent eight months nearly being murdered and had decided that if death wanted her, it would have to get in line behind the petunias.

Juniper loaded his truck bed at dawn: bags of potting soil, six flats of marigolds, four of black-eyed Susans, a coil of hose, and the hand tools he'd inherited from his father, whose handles were worn to the shape of Hartwell palms going back three generations. He paused with his hand on the tailgate and looked up at a sky scrubbed clean blue, and allowed himself one full minute of something he didn't have a better word for than *gratitude*.

Then he drove to the cemetery where he'd spent more of his life than any church.

* * *

They came in ones and twos at first, the way people do when they're not sure they've been invited all the way. Mrs. Tandy from the pharmacy, who had slipped Juniper copies of thirty-year-old prescription ledgers back when doing so could have cost her. The Okafor brothers, who ran the hardware store and had "accidentally" left a crowbar in Juniper's truck the night he'd gone looking for the boathouse wall. Old Walt Merrick, retired deputy, who had spent four decades pretending not to know what everyone in Marrow Creek pretended not to know, and who now stood at the gate holding a shovel like a man reporting for duty he'd owed a long time.

By nine there were children, which undid Juniper more than anything. The Pruitt kids, chasing each other between the rows until their mother caught them by the collars; the little Vasquez girl solemnly carrying a watering can half her size. A cemetery on a spring morning with children running through it—not for a funeral, but for flowers. He stood very still and let that fact settle into him like rain into dry ground.

"You're going to cry," said Dolores, arriving in the van the facility sent, Harlan pushing her chair over the grass with the exaggerated care of a man handling nitroglycerin. "I can see it happening. Your ears go pink first."

"My ears do not go pink."

"Thirty years of correspondence, Juniper. I know your ears."

Harlan Pike looked thinner than before his arrest, grayer, but his eyes had come back from wherever they'd gone during those weeks in the county jail—the weeks he'd sat silent, refusing to defend himself against a murder charge because some debts, he'd believed, had to be paid even wrongly. Now he wore a flannel shirt with the sleeves rolled and dirt already under his nails, and when Juniper met his eyes, Harlan nodded once, the nod of a man who had been forgiven and was still learning how to carry it.

"Four graves," Harlan said. "You want them done proper?"

"I want them done like somebody loved them," Juniper said.

"Somebody did," Dolores said quietly. "That was always the whole tragedy of it."

* * *

They started with Edith Calloway, because Edith had been last, and because her sister had driven down from Ohio for the morning—a small sharp-eyed woman named Carol who shook Juniper's hand and held onto it a beat too long.

"The sheriff told me accident," she said. "You told me otherwise. You were the only one." She looked out at the gathering crowd, the wheelbarrows, the trays of seedlings. "All these people. For my sister."

"They came for the truth," Juniper said. "Your sister was part of it."

Carol knelt at the fresh-turned earth and pressed a marigold in with both hands, and Juniper turned away to give her privacy, finding instead the Pruitt twins attempting to bury each other in mulch.

He worked the rows through the morning, moving from grave to grave as the sun climbed: Edith Calloway, drowned in water that had been made to look like an ending. Before her, the three deaths he'd quietly proved murders across the years and filed away in the locked drawer of his conscience—each one a thread, though he hadn't known the loom. And beneath all of it, thirty years deep, Thomas Vane, seventeen years old, in the river, while five friends carried the secret of him like a stone passed hand to hand until it crushed them one by one.

Walt Merrick dug alongside him for a while without talking, which was how Walt preferred most things.

"You know," Walt said finally, leaning on the shovel, "I worked under Sheriff Dill for twenty-two years. Never once opened the '94 file. Told myself it was closed, case done, nothing to see." He spat thoughtfully into the grass. "Wasn't that I didn't wonder. Was that wondering felt disloyal."

"And now?"

"Now I'm planting flowers on a boy's grave thirty years late, so I reckon loyalty and truth weren't the same animal after all." He resumed digging. "Took me sixty-eight years to learn that. You learned it faster. Don't let it go to your head."

"It went to my head approximately eleven months ago," Juniper said. "It hasn't left."

* * *

At noon they broke for food, because no gathering in Marrow Creek had ever survived past noon without it. Folding tables appeared as if conjured—Mrs. Tandy's fried chicken, the Okafors' jollof rice that reduced grown men to silence, a lemonade situation that the Vasquez girl supervised with terrifying authority. Someone had brought a pie labeled simply FOR THE WITNESS, which Juniper suspected was less a tribute than a warning that the whole town still remembered every word of his testimony.

He ate sitting on the tailgate of his truck, and found himself surrounded without anyone deciding to surround him. Dolores angled her chair close. Harlan settled on the grass. The teacher, Miss Ellery, retired forty years now, who had been the one to say *I saw Ruth Ann Vane's car at the river that night, and there were two people inside*—she sat on a folding chair someone brought her and fanned herself with a paper plate.

"Do you know," Miss Ellery said, "I taught all five of them. Dolores, Edith, Harlan, Amos Creed, Ruth Ann. Fifth grade." She shook her head slowly. "Ruth Ann used to organize the other children into games with rules nobody else understood. She'd appoint herself scorekeeper. And the children would just... accept it. Even then."

"She kept score her whole life," Dolores said. "We just never asked what game."

Juniper listened and said little. This was another thing he was learning late: that being among people did not require performance. That you could sit on a tailgate with pie on a paper plate and be, without effort or explanation, one of them.

Eight months ago he had eaten every meal alone in a house with one chair pulled up to the table, because a second chair seemed like an accusation of hope.

Now there wasn't room on the tailgate and he didn't mind.

* * *

In the afternoon they planted Thomas's grave last, by design. It lay near the river side of the cemetery, under a sycamore, and it had spent thirty years bearing only a flat military-style marker ordered by a county clerk: THOMAS R. VANE, 1977–1994. No epitaph. No flowers. His sister the mayor had never visited it—or so the groundskeepers said—and Juniper had privately mowed around it for decades feeling the particular grief of tending a grave nobody claimed.

That had changed. Ruth Ann Vane was in the county jail awaiting her own trial for obstruction and the cover-up, having stood in open court and taken the whole weight of it onto herself—*the concealment was mine, the silence was mine*—and whatever else she had been, whatever velvet menace she'd once turned on Juniper in her too-perfect living room, she had shielded him with that confession, and shielded Harlan, and no one in Marrow Creek entirely knew what to feel about her. Juniper had visited her once. She'd asked about the flowers at Thomas's grave. He'd told her black-eyed Susans, because they grew wild along the river, and she had looked at the wall of the visiting room for a long moment and said, "He hated weeding those. He said they were weeds that got ambitious."

So: black-eyed Susans.

Dolores insisted on placing the first one herself. Harlan lifted her from the chair and she stood, swaying, gripping his arm, and knelt—actually knelt, to the horror of the care facility nurse who'd come along—at the head of the grave and pressed the root ball into the earth with hands that shook.

"I wrote you a letter, Tommy," she said, not loudly, but everyone heard. "Took me fifty years to write it. I'm sorry it was late. I'm sorry we let you be a secret." She patted the soil twice, the way you'd pat a shoulder. "You're not a secret anymore."

Nobody clapped. Nobody spoke. But Juniper saw Walt Merrick take off his hat, and saw Miss Ellery reach for Mrs. Tandy's hand, and understood that this—forty-one people standing silent over a boy's grave in the sun—was the thing the town had owed since 1994, and was paying at last, with interest.

* * *

Late in the afternoon, as the crowd thinned and the light went long and gold, Juniper walked the row alone with his watering can. Four graves, four beds of new color: marigolds for Edith, black-eyed Susans for Thomas, and for the other three—the ones from the drawer, the quiet murders that had been threads before he knew the pattern—whatever Dolores had chosen off the seed catalog with a flourish: phlox, coneflowers, something called *autumn joy* that Juniper suspected was a lie botanically but not emotionally.

He stopped at the last one and stood a while.

Justice outside the law. He'd done it, and he was still doing it, and the cost was still coming due—he had a hearing next month, a formal review of his conduct, a prosecutor's letter using phrases like *withholding material evidence*. He'd face it. He'd tell the truth of it, all of it, under oath again if they made him. But standing here in the smell of turned earth and cut grass, watching the Vasquez girl water a flower that did not need watering because she'd watered it four times already, Juniper found he could not summon a single regret large enough to hold.

The brother awaited trial in the fall. Dale Mercer, the quiet man from Ruth Ann's office, the hit-and-run victim's surviving brother, who had watched from hiding by the river in 1994 and let a boy drown rather than expose himself, and who had spent thirty years collecting his reckoning one "accident" at a time. In the fall, a jury would hear about the night on the county road, the girl on the bicycle, the family that never got a day in court. They would get their day at last. It was late justice. It was still justice. Juniper had learned the difference mattered.

"Coroner Hartwell."

He turned. The Vasquez girl stood beside him, watering can dripping, regarding him with the frank appraisal of the seven-year-old.

"You're the one who fixed everything," she said.

"I didn't fix everything. A lot of people helped."

"But you're the main one." She considered. "My mom says you live all alone in that house by the graveyard."

"That's right."

"That's sad."

"It used to be," Juniper said, and found it was simply true. "It isn't anymore."

She nodded as though this confirmed data she'd already collected, and trotted off, and Juniper stood among the graves and the gardens and the last of his strange, assembled, unasked-for family, and watched the light come down the hill toward the river.

Then his phone buzzed in his pocket. He almost didn't check it—Saturdays, he'd decided, belonged to the living—but the number was the county jail.

"Mr. Hartwell," said the voice on the line, careful and unfamiliar. "This is Deputy Alvarez, up at the county facility. I'm calling because—" a pause, papers shuffling— "because Ruth Ann Vane is asking for you. Says it's urgent. Says you'll want to hear it before Monday." Another pause, longer. "She says to tell you: *there's a second journal.*"

Juniper looked out over the four graves, the flowers nodding in the evening wind, the town packing up its tables and its trowels behind him, and felt the old cold move up his spine like water finding a crack.

"Tell her," he said, "I'll be there in the morning."
