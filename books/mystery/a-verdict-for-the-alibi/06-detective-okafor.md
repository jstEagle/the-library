## 6. Detective Okafor

The anonymous tip came in on a Tuesday, which Amara Okafor would later think of as the day the file opened its eyes.

She was at her desk at 6:40 in the morning, earlier than anyone, because mornings were the only hours Ashenmoor's police department felt like hers. The building had been a bank before it had been a precinct, and it still wore its history like a bad suit — marble floors scarred by decades of boot heels, a vault door at the back that nobody could remove, now used to store evidence boxes nobody wanted. The Hale case lived there. Box 114. Eleven years of dust and sealed testimony, sitting behind steel while the town above it went on pretending the whole thing had been settled.

The tip was one sentence, typed, no return address: *Ask who signed the death certificate.*

Amara read it three times with her coffee going cold beside her keyboard. She was forty-three years old, sixteen years on the job, eleven of them in Ashenmoor, and she had learned that anonymous tips were like river ice — most of them wouldn't hold your weight, but you never knew until you stepped out. This one held something. She could feel it in the way the words were chosen. Not *check* the death certificate. *Ask who signed it.* A question shaped for someone who already suspected the answer.

She pulled Box 114 from the vault herself, signing the log with the small private satisfaction of a woman doing something she'd wanted to do for years.

The file smelled like the inside of old paper, that sweet-rot smell of cellulose quietly eating itself. She spread it across two desks because one wasn't enough. Trial transcripts. Photographs of a riverbank. The statement of Marcus Hale, then twenty-six, his handwriting jumping on the page the way handwriting does when the hand can't stop shaking. The confession of Aldous Hale — recanted within the month, but recantations don't unring bells, not in towns like this one. And there, clipped to the medical examiner's report, the death certificate of Elena Marie Hale, signed by Dr. Edmund Voss.

Amara sat with that name for a long time.

Edmund Voss had been dead four years. She remembered the obituary, because everyone in Ashenmoor remembered obituaries; they were the closest thing the town had to a newspaper. What she hadn't remembered — what she checked twice, then a third time, then called the county records office in Corvane County about on her lunch break — was that Edmund Voss had not been licensed to practice medicine in this state at the time Elena Hale died. His license had lapsed fourteen months earlier over an unpaid renewal fee, a clerical embarrassment he'd been too proud to fix. He'd been practicing anyway, quietly, out of a converted farmhouse office, treating people who trusted him more than they trusted the clinic two towns over. Everyone had known. Nobody had made it a problem.

Which meant the death certificate of Elena Marie Hale was signed by a man who legally did not exist as a doctor on the day he signed it.

It didn't prove anything. Amara knew that better than anyone. It was a thread, and threads in Ashenmoor had a way of leading back into the same wall. But she was a careful woman, and careful women know that walls are built by someone, and someone who builds a wall once will build it again if you give him reason.

She reopened the file that afternoon. Her captain signed off without reading past the first page, because the captain signed off on everything Amara asked for, having learned years ago that refusing her cost more energy than permitting her. She told herself it was routine review. She told herself a lot of things that week.

* * *

The stranger arrived on Thursday.

She heard about him before she saw him, because in Ashenmoor information moved faster than weather. Dell at the Gas-N-Go mentioned a rental car with out-of-county plates. Ruthie at the Bluebird Motel called her sister, who called the dispatcher, who mentioned to Amara in the tone of voice people used when they wanted credit for news without responsibility for it: a man checked in under the name *R. Villalobos*, paid cash for the first week, said he was here on estate business.

Estate business. In a town where the biggest estate question in a generation had been buried along with a box of river stones.

Amara ran the name through the state database that evening, sitting in her kitchen with a bowl of soup she kept forgetting to eat. Rosalind Villalobos. Attorney-at-law, licensed nine years, clean record, admitted to practice in three states. Address in a city she'd visited once for a conference and remembered mostly for its airport. There was even a photograph on the bar association page — a lean man with dark hair going silver early at the temples, a face built for listening rather than talking, eyes that looked at the camera the way people look at doors they're calculating the distance to.

Everything checked out.

That was the problem. Amara had run thousands of identities in her career, and real ones were always slightly wrong somewhere. A middle initial missing from the DMV record. A voter registration from a county the license didn't match. A gap of eight months somewhere that meant a bad year, a divorce, a stint in a hospital, a stretch of living out of a car. Real lives were ragged at the edges, worn like old coins. Villalobos's life was crisp. Every document dovetailed with every other document. His law school graduation date matched his admission date matched his first employment record. Nine years of professional history with no gaps, no moves that didn't have paperwork attached, no loose threads anywhere.

It read less like a biography than like a brief — an argument constructed to withstand scrutiny.

She'd seen that kind of perfection exactly twice before. Once on a fugitive who'd bought himself a dead man's childhood. Once on a federal witness whose new identity had been assembled by people very good at their jobs. Both times, the flawlessness had been the tell.

But knowing a thing was false and proving it were different animals entirely, and Amara Okafor did not move on animals she couldn't yet see clearly. So she did what careful people do. She waited, and she watched, and she let the stranger come to her.

He came on Saturday morning.

* * *

The interview room at the Ashenmoor PD was a repurposed storage closet with a table, three chairs, and a window looking out onto the parking lot, because Chief Bramlett believed windows humanized interrogations and Amara privately believed he just liked watching people watch the lot. She brought two coffees anyway, in paper cups, because the ritual mattered. Coffee said *this is a conversation*. Handcuffs said other things. You chose your props the way a novelist chooses furniture.

Villalobos rose when she entered. That was the first note in her book, figuratively speaking — she hadn't brought a notebook, deliberately. He was taller than his photograph suggested, or perhaps the photograph had been taken by someone who resented him. His handshake was dry, measured, exactly firm enough. His suit was good but not city-good, chosen, she guessed, by a man who understood precisely how much money a town like Ashenmoor distrusted.

"Detective Okafor." His voice was low and unhurried. "I appreciate you making time."

"You're the one driving out to a cemetery in November," she said. "I figure I owe you hospitality."

Something flickered behind his eyes — not surprise exactly, but the recalibration of a man discovering the board was already set. "You've been keeping track of me."

"It's a small town. We keep track of everything. Weather, gossip, strangers." She set the coffees down and took the chair facing him, leaving the third empty between them like a guest neither wanted. "Mr. Villalobos. Estate business. Whose estate?"

"Marcus Hale retained me last month." No hesitation. Good. Or rehearsed. With men like this, those were often the same thing. "He wants his sister's will probated."

"His sister's been dead eleven years."

"Yes."

"And probate requires—" she let the silence do a little work— "a decedent."

"Probate requires a valid will and a testator's death. Whether the person named as beneficiary is alive is, frankly, outside my remit. The will names Elena Hale. If she's deceased, the estate passes per the contingency clause. If she's not—" he shrugged, a small economical motion— "the law has opinions about that, and none of them require me to speculate."

"Your client does, though. Speculate."

"I'm not in the business of my clients' speculations. Only their filings."

Amara drank her coffee and studied him over the rim. He met her gaze without effort, without challenge, the way a man meets the gaze of someone he's decided is not currently a threat — and she noticed that he let her look just long enough to seem open, then glanced away toward the window, toward the parking lot, giving her privacy to study his profile. It was a courteous maneuver. It was also a controlled one. Everything this man did was controlled, down to the angle at which he held the paper cup, thumb positioned so the logo faced outward, a tiny unconscious courtesy to whoever might be reading the room.

"So walk me through it," she said. "A man hires you to probate a will for a woman declared legally dead at trial. Eleven years ago. Why now?"

"Grief has its own calendar."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the truest one I have." He turned the cup a quarter-turn on the table. "Mr. Hale is dying, Detective. Pancreatic. Months, maybe weeks. Dying men want their affairs in order, and their affairs include a sister whose grave may or may not contain his sister. He wants certainty. Certainty is a service I provide."

"You provide certainty."

"I provide documents that make uncertainty administratively irrelevant. It's less poetic than it sounds."

And there it was again — the polish. Amara had interviewed hundreds of people, guilty and innocent and everything between, and innocence stammered. Innocence over-explained, contradicted itself, got defensive, cried, laughed at the wrong moment. Innocence was messy because truth was messy. Villalobos answered like a deposition transcript prepared in advance by a very expensive attorney: accurate, complete, and frictionless. Every answer landed exactly where a question wanted it, no more, no less. He gave her nothing to pull on because he'd already trimmed every thread.

Except.

Except that he'd driven out to the cemetery on his second day in town, alone, in the rain, and stood at Elena Hale's grave for forty minutes according to Ruthie's sister's neighbor, who walked her dog there. Except that a man providing *administrative services* did not need forty minutes at a graveside. Except that when she'd said *your client does speculate*, his thumb had pressed the cup hard enough to dent it — a half-second of pressure, immediately released, invisible to anyone not trained to watch hands.

"Tell me about the death certificate," she said.

This time the pause was real. Brief — a breath and a half — but real, the first unpolished thing he'd given her.

"What about it?"

"Dr. Voss signed it. You've read the trial file; I assume that's why you drove to the cemetery. Did you notice anything about Dr. Voss?"

"I noticed he's dead."

"He is. Died four years ago. Here's what the obituary doesn't say." She leaned forward, elbows on the table, and watched his face with the patience of a woman who had all morning. "At the time Elena Hale's death certificate was signed, Edmund Voss had no valid medical license. None. Lapsed over a fee dispute with the state board. Which means the sole legal documentation of Elena Hale's death rests on the signature of a man who, in the eyes of the state, was not a doctor that day."

Villalobos was quiet. When he spoke, his voice had changed register — lower, slower, the courtroom voice of a man choosing his footing on uncertain ground. "That's a significant irregularity."

"It is."

"One that would presumably interest the court."

"One that interests me." She sat back. "Here's my problem, Mr. Villalobos. An anonymous tip tells me to ask who signed the certificate. Two days later, a stranger arrives asking questions about the same certificate, staying at the Bluebird under a name that checks out so cleanly it reads like fiction. And I'm left wondering whether I'm talking to the answer or the question."

"If I'm the question," he said mildly, "what's the answer?"

"That's what I intend to find out."

They looked at each other across the table, and Amara felt the strange pleasure of recognition — the particular jolt of meeting someone built like yourself. Careful. Watchful. Polite the way duels are polite. He knew she knew something was wrong with him. She knew he knew. Neither of them could say it, because saying it would cost them both the position they'd spent the conversation constructing. So they sat in the wreckage of mutual suspicion like two chess players who had both spotted the same trap and were now politely waiting to see who would step in it first.

He stood, eventually, and thanked her for the coffee, and said he hoped he hadn't taken up too much of her Saturday, and offered his card — heavy stock, letterpress, the kind of card a man orders when he wants the object to argue for him. At the door he paused.

"For what it's worth, Detective," he said, not turning around, "I hope the certificate is wrong. I hope there's a living woman somewhere who needed a dead one. Some deaths are the kindest thing a person can arrange." Then he was gone, footsteps fading down the hallway, the front door sighing shut behind him.

Amara sat alone in the little room for a long time, turning his card over in her fingers.

Then she went back to her desk, opened the state database, and requested the full workup on Rosalind Villalobos — not the summary screen, the deep pull, birth records forward, every document the state had ever touched with his name on it. If his life was a construction, somewhere in the foundation there would be a seam.

While she waited, she wrote on a legal pad, in her small square handwriting:

*Villalobos — too clean. Who benefits? Who pays? Who's afraid?*

And beneath it, after a moment:

*Who tipped me?*

Because that was the question she kept circling back to, the one that wouldn't sit still. The tip had come Tuesday. Villalobos had arrived Thursday. Unless the timing was coincidence — and Amara Okafor had stopped believing in coincidences somewhere around her tenth year on the job — then either the tipper had known he was coming, or he had sent it himself, walking into her sightline on purpose, daring her to look closer.

People who wanted to be looked at closely usually had a reason. People who engineered their own scrutiny were either arrogant or desperate, and the difference between arrogance and desperation was, in her experience, the difference between a predator and prey wearing armor.

She thought about the dent his thumb had left in the paper cup. About the pause before *What about it?* About a man who hoped a death certificate was wrong.

The database results landed in her inbox at 4:52 p.m., and Amara opened them the way she opened all potentially devastating documents — slowly, with both feet flat on the floor.

Birth certificate: Rosalind Mateo Villalobos, born St. Ambrose Hospital, registered four days later. School records. Immunization records. A pediatrician's signature. A mother's maiden name that traced back three generations in the same county. It was all there. All of it. A complete childhood, documented, verifiable, boring.

And yet.

She zoomed in on the birth certificate scan until the pixels broke apart, studying the registrar's stamp, the ink notation in the corner, the archival number. She'd processed enough vital records in eleven years of detective work to know their anatomy the way a butcher knows a carcass. This one was beautiful. This one was flawless.

This one had an archival control number that didn't match the county's numbering sequence for that year — off by a full series block, the kind of discrepancy that happened when a document was created retroactively and assigned a number from a batch issued later. A forgery so good it had fooled everyone, including, possibly, the database itself. Someone hadn't just invented a man. Someone had gone back and built him a cradle, a hospital, a mother's maiden name, a pediatrician's pen.

Amara sat back in her chair. Outside her window, the streetlights of Ashenmoor were flickering on one by one, and somewhere out there in the blue dusk, a man who did not exist was walking back to a motel room under a name he'd chosen like a coat.

She picked up her phone, hesitated, and dialed the county records office in Corvane instead — the same clerk she'd spoken to about Voss's license.

"Question for you," she said. "Birth certificates. Say somebody wanted to verify one from, oh, thirty-some years back. Is there any way to check the original ledger? Paper, I mean. Not the digitized stuff."

A pause on the line, the sound of a drawer opening somewhere in another county. "Depends on the year. Ledgers get archived, some get lost. What name are we looking at?"

Amara looked at the legal pad, at her own neat handwriting staring back at her.

"Villalobos," she said. "Rosalind Villalobos. Born at St. Ambrose."

Another pause, longer this time, and when the clerk spoke again her voice had shifted — the careful, lowered tone of someone who has just realized a phone call might matter later, in a room with worse lighting.

"Honey," the clerk said, "you want to come look at this one yourself."
