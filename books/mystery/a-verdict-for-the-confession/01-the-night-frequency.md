## 1. The Night Frequency

The dispatch board at Meridian Taxi glows the way it always does between two and four in the morning: mostly green, occasionally amber, and once—memorably, catastrophically—the night Denny Kowalczyk drove his cab into the retention pond on Route 9, a solid block of red that I still think about when I can't sleep.

Green means a driver is available. Amber means occupied. Red means trouble, and trouble at 3 a.m. is usually just a flat tire or a fare who wouldn't stop crying, but my brain has never learned to leave that distinction alone. My brain treats every red like a door left open in a house where someone died.

"Dispatch, this is Marisol, I'm dropping at St. Anthony's, then I'm dark for twenty."

"Copy, Marisol. Get some sleep. Tell your mother I said hi."

"She says you work too much."

"Your mother is a wise woman with no proof."

That's the shape of the night. That's most nights. I've been doing the graveyard shift at Meridian for six years now, and people hear "taxi dispatcher" and picture somebody chewing gum and yelling into a headset, and sure, there's some of that, but what they don't picture is that I am functionally the last person awake in this city who knows where everybody is. Every driver's location, every pickup, every drop-off, timestamped and logged whether anyone asks for it or not. The police have systems. I have *Marisol always takes the long way past the harbor because she likes the water*, and *Theo won't drive east of Delmore after dark since his brother's accident*, and *the man on Fenwick Street who calls every Thursday at 2:15 to go to the bus station and comes back Sunday mornings smelling like someone else's perfume*.

I don't mean to notice these things. Or—I do, but not the way people assume. It's not nosiness. It's more like tinnitus. A frequency I can't turn off. Patterns arrive in my head already assembled, and all I do is fail to un-notice them.

It's gotten me in trouble exactly three times.

The first was Walter Pembridge, found dead at the bottom of his own basement stairs, ruled a fall by a man who'd had a hip replacement and hadn't been cleared to climb ladders, except the ladder was down from the attic and the attic light was on and Walter was afraid of attics—his neighbor told me so, unprompted, while I waited in a cab outside for a fare who never came out. I called the tip line. I didn't leave a name. Six weeks later they arrested the nephew, and I went back to the board.

The second was Lorraine Duffy. Hit by a car on a straight road in clear weather, ruled a tragic accident, except the skid marks started forty feet *after* the point of impact according to the photos in the paper, which meant the driver braked late, which meant either he didn't see her—and you see a person on a straight road in clear weather—or he wasn't braking for her at all. I did the math on a napkin during a slow Tuesday. The tip line again. They reopened it. The husband had a new life insurance policy, eleven days old.

The third one I don't talk about, even in my own head, more than necessary. The third one took eleven months and cost me a friendship, because the friend turned out to be the reason. But the pattern held, and the pattern was right, and somewhere in the middle of all that I understood something about myself that I keep in a drawer with the napkin: I am very good at noticing, and noticing is only useful if nobody notices you noticing.

Anonymity isn't cowardice. Okay, it's maybe ten percent cowardice. Mostly it's craft. A dispatcher is furniture. Furniture hears everything.

* * *

At 4:12 a.m., the city does its one honest thing: it goes quiet. Even the drunks have been deposited somewhere. The board settles into green, and I let myself do the thing I do on slow nights, which is read the log backward, the way other people reread old letters.

Tonight's entries are ordinary. A bachelorette party fragment abandoned on Corliss Avenue. A nurse. A man who tipped in quarters counted out one at a time, which Marisol reported with the weary reverence of a war story. And at 1:38, a pickup on Ashford Road—east side, industrial stretch, mostly shuttered since the foundry closed—that ran long. Forty minutes from pickup to drop-off, for a route that should take twelve.

I pull up the details because the frequency in my head has started humming, quiet, like a refrigerator in another room. Pickup: Ashford Road near the old gatehouse. Drop-off: the Kessler Street bridge. Passenger: female, paid cash, asked the driver to wait twice.

Kessler Street. The bridge over the canal. There's nothing at the Kessler Street bridge at 2 a.m. There's nothing there at any time; it's the kind of place the city forgot to finish thinking about. Why would a woman get picked up at the ruins of a foundry and ask to be driven to an empty bridge, and why would she need the driver to wait?

I'm still looking at it when the radio crackles.

"Dispatch, this is Renata, I'm at Fifth and Halloway, there's—hold on." A pause. Renata Okafor has driven nights longer than I've worked here, and she doesn't say *hold on* unless something needs holding. "There's police activity at the Kessler bridge. Lot of lights. Dispatch, are we flagged off that block?"

I check the board as if the board would know before me. "Nothing official yet. What are you seeing?"

"Ambulance, two cruisers, and—" She stops. When she starts again her voice has changed registers, dropped down into the flat careful place drivers go when they've seen something on a sidewalk they'll be describing to each other for weeks. "They're putting up tape. Yara, there's a body. Under the bridge, near the water. They're saying—well, the officer at the tape is telling people to move along, which means it's bad."

"Copy," I say, and my voice is doing its job voice, level and bored, while the rest of me leans toward the radio like a plant toward light. "Stay clear, Renata. Log your position and keep rolling."

She keeps rolling. I don't.

Here's the thing about being furniture: furniture develops opinions. And my opinion, formed over six years and three anonymous phone calls, is that bodies under bridges at 2 a.m. in cities this size are rarely strangers to anybody. Somebody drove her there. Somebody knew the bridge. And a woman who gets picked up at the Ashford gatehouse and asks a taxi to wait while she looks at black water—that woman wasn't sightseeing.

I pull the full trip record. Driver was Sal Petraglia, tonight driving car fourteen. Sal is sixty-one, reads westerns on his breaks, and has the observational skills of a security camera with feelings. If anyone noticed anything, Sal did.

I dial his cell, because protocol says route non-emergency questions through the morning supervisor, and protocol can wait until the sun is up.

"Sal. It's Yara. You awake?"

"I'm eating a sandwich, which is the same thing. What's wrong?"

"Your 1:38 pickup. Ashford Road. The woman going to Kessler."

A pause long enough that I can hear him chewing stop.

"What about her?" he says, and the chewiness is gone.

"Tell me about her."

Another pause. Then, quieter, the sound of a truck door closing, him sealing himself into the small privacy of his cab. "Young-ish. Thirty-five, maybe. Nervous—not jumpy nervous. *Decided* nervous. Like she'd already argued herself into whatever she was doing." He exhales. "She asked me to wait at the bridge. Ten minutes. Then she got out, walked halfway across, stopped, and came back. Got in and told me to take her back to Ashford."

"Back to the gatehouse?"

"Yeah. Same spot. Paid me double and said she'd changed her mind." He hesitates. "Yara, she was crying. Not loud. Just—leaking. And when I dropped her, there was a car parked up the road with its lights off. I clocked it because lights-off on Ashford at 2 a.m. is how you get robbed. Dark sedan. I want to say gray. Plate was mud-covered or taped. She got out and stood there a second, looking at it, and then she walked toward it like a person walking toward a dentist."

"You remember anything else? Anything at all."

He's quiet, and I can hear him deciding how much of himself to spend. "Her coat," he says finally. "Nice coat. Wool, kind of a rust color, with a scarf—patterned, lots of little shapes, looked hand-knitted, the good kind of ugly. And she kept touching the scarf. Like it wasn't hers. Like she was checking it was still there."

The frequency in my head stops humming and goes sharp.

"Sal, I need you to write down everything you just told me. Everything. Time-stamped, tonight, while it's fresh. Don't show anyone, don't tell anyone, just write it and keep it."

"This about the body at the bridge?"

"It might be. Don't listen to the scanner, Sal. Write your statement."

I hang up and sit with the board glowing green around me, and I make myself do the boring professional things: note the times, flag the trip record, print the log page for the shift file. Routine. Furniture behavior. Meanwhile the rest of me is running the arithmetic I can't help running: a woman leaves the dead foundry twice, can't jump off a bridge, walks back toward a waiting car like a prisoner returning to a cell, and four hours later she's under that same bridge with the whole city's worth of police lights pointed at her.

And a scarf. Patterned, hand-knitted, the good kind of ugly.

I know that scarf. I knitted it myself, badly, during a winter when my hands needed something to do besides hover over a radio. It lives—or lived—on the hook by my apartment door, next to a wool coat in a color I'd call rust if I were feeling generous.

Except it hasn't lived there for nine days. Nine days ago I came home from a shift and the hook was empty and the closet door was open one inch wider than I leave it, and I stood in my own doorway doing the math everyone does—did I lend it, did I lose it, am I losing my mind—and then decided, in the great tradition of people who value their anonymity, to report nothing. To be furniture even in my own home. Whoever took a scarf and a coat took them from a building where half the tenants don't lock their doors anyway, and I told myself it was random, petty, forgettable.

The radio crackles again. Not Renata. The police band I'm not supposed to have on the second speaker, low enough to be deniable.

"—victim's identity pending notification. Responding unit notes the deceased was dressed warmly, distinctive clothing, possible—"

Possible. Possible what? Finish the sentence. But the band moves on to a noise complaint on Delmore, and I'm alone with the unfinished sentence and the cold spreading through my chest in a way that has nothing to do with the radiator I keep meaning to bleed.

Because here is what I haven't said yet, even to myself, even in the drawer where I keep the napkin: the coat and the scarf weren't taken by someone who wanted a coat and a scarf. I've had nine days to think about it, in the wordless way you think about a wrongness you can't name. The break-in took nothing else. Not the laptop sitting in plain view. Not the rent money in the coffee tin labeled COFFEE, which has fooled exactly no one including me. Just the coat. Just the scarf. Things that would look right on a woman of roughly my build, seen from a distance, at night, by a driver like Sal.

Things that would make a witness say, later, *it was her*. Things that could put me at a bridge I've never stood on, at a foundry I've never visited, inside a story I've never heard.

Or—colder thought, arriving now, wearing its own little hand-knitted scarf—things that would make a killer feel clever. A message wrapped in wool. *I know where you live. I've been inside your life. I can dress a dead woman in your clothes and watch you realize it.*

The board pings. Amber, amber, green. The city waking up its first early risers. Somewhere out there, detectives are standing over a woman in my coat, and one of them is going to run the victim's last known movements, and one of those movements is a taxi ride logged under my dispatch code, and sooner or later—because patterns arrive in my head already assembled, and this one is assembling fast—someone is going to trace that ride back to the woman who dispatched it.

Invisibility has rules. Rule one: never be the interesting part of anyone's night.

I look at the printed log page, at the 1:38 pickup on Ashford Road, and I understand that rule one broke itself sometime around 2 a.m. tonight, without my permission, in a rust-colored coat.

Renata's voice comes over the radio, casual, tired, oblivious: "Dispatch, I'm clear of Fifth and Halloway, heading toward the yard. Quiet night, huh?"

"Real quiet," I say.

Then I pick up the phone and call the one number I swore I'd never call from this desk, and ask the police tip line, for the fourth time in my life, to connect me to whoever's in charge of the body on Kessler Street.

This time, I give them my name.
