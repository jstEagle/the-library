## 6. What Was Taken

The thing about being robbed is that you're the last to know.

I'd been living with the theft for three days before I understood it as a theft. That's the occupational hazard of invisibility: when something leaves your life quietly, your first instinct is to assume you misremembered. You check the same shelf twice. You look behind the same stack of dispatch logs. You tell yourself the jacket is at the dry cleaner, the scarf is in the winter bin, the gloves are in the other coat—the one you never wear because it makes you look like a detective in a show about detectives.

By Thursday morning I'd stopped lying to myself.

* * *

Let me walk you through my closet, because the geography of it matters, and because I've spent enough nights mapping other people's movements that reconstructing my own came embarrassingly easy.

Top rod: work clothes, the gray and black uniform of someone who doesn't want to be remembered at 3 a.m. Second rod: the good stuff, such as it is—two dresses, a blazer with ambitions above its station, and the jacket. Charcoal wool, collar like folded wings, bought four years ago from a consignment shop on Delmore Street by a woman who told me it "had presence." I'd laughed. Presence was not something I'd ever shopped for.

Bottom shelf, left: scarves. Bottom shelf, right: the winter bin, and inside it, the gloves—leather, cashmere-lined, a birthday gift from my mother back when she still sent gifts instead of guilt.

All of it gone. Jacket, scarf, gloves. A coordinated outfit, essentially. Someone hadn't robbed me; someone had *dressed* me.

That's the sentence that kept me up past sunrise, staring at the ceiling while the day shift's cars rattled over the potholes outside. You don't burgle an apartment for an outfit. You don't risk felony charges for charcoal wool and a scarf the color of dried blood. Unless the outfit isn't for you. Unless the outfit is a costume, and the costume needs to fit a specific role, and the role is—

Me.

Dana Voss had died wearing my clothes. The responding officer had said "distinctive" over the radio, and I'd felt the word land somewhere under my ribs before I knew why. Distinctive. Present. *Recognizable.* Someone had put a dead woman in Yara Hartwell's jacket and walked her into a story where Yara Hartwell belonged.

The question wasn't why anymore. The question was how they got in.

* * *

I did what I always do with a puzzle too big to hold: I broke it into pieces small enough to fit in my hands.

Piece one: timing. I work nights, four on, two off, ten to six. My building knows this schedule better than my family does. Anyone watching the lobby—or anyone who works in the lobby—could tell you exactly which ninety-minute windows my apartment sat empty. Tuesday night into Wednesday morning, per the last time I definitively remembered seeing the jacket, there were two such windows.

Piece two: entry. My door locks fine but isn't precious; it's a builder-grade deadbolt on a door that predates both of us. But here's the detail that mattered: no forced entry. No scratched paint, no splintered jamb, no credit-card marks. I checked twice, then a third time with a flashlight held sideways, the way Ruiz probably would have, if I'd been fool enough to call her. Nothing. Whoever came in either picked the lock with real skill or used a key.

Piece three—and this is where the floor tilted—access.

My building has eleven units and one super: Mr. Okafor, sixty-something, patient, keeps the boiler alive through sheer stubbornness and prayer. He has keys to everything. He also has a brother who helps him out sometimes, a big quiet man named Emeka whom I've seen maybe six times in three years, always in a gray work jacket, always nodding, never speaking much. Two men with legitimate access. One face I barely know.

And here's the thought I didn't want, the one that arrived uninvited around noon while I stood in my closet holding an empty hanger like evidence: what does a super's uniform actually look like? Gray coveralls, a tool bag, a lanyard. What does it cost to fake? Forty dollars and a trip to any hardware store. What does it buy? Every locked door in a building full of people trained by city life not to look at maintenance workers. We are a species that makes eye contact with dogs more readily than with the man fixing the radiator.

Someone had come into my home wearing permission.

* * *

I spent the afternoon doing something I'm not proud of and would do again: I interviewed my neighbors the way I take calls—warm, unhurried, asking questions that sound like small talk until you play them back.

Mrs. Ferreira in 3B remembered "the repair man" coming Tuesday evening about a leak in the ceiling. There was no leak in her ceiling. She remembered because he'd been polite, shown her a work order even, and she'd felt bad later that she couldn't recall his face—"they all look alike in those jackets, don't they, honey?" She said he'd gone up toward the fourth floor. Toward me.

Mr. Okafor himself was harder. I found him in the basement wrestling with the water heater, and I watched his face go through confusion, then genuine distress, then something worse—recognition of a kind. He swore he hadn't sent anyone Tuesday. He swore he hadn't been out of his apartment after eight. And then, quieter, turning the wrench over in his hands like it might explain things: "Emeka borrowed my coveralls last week. Said he had a side job."

A side job. I smiled and thanked him and climbed the stairs with my pulse tapping at my throat like a driver radioing in. Maybe it really was Emeka. Maybe some stranger paid Emeka two hundred dollars to borrow a uniform and a key ring. Or maybe Emeka doesn't exist in quite the shape Mr. Okafor believes, and "borrowing the coveralls" was the whole plan wearing a family member's face.

Either way, the picture was complete: someone with access, or dressed as access, entered my apartment during a known empty window, took only the items that could dress a body in my silhouette, and left the door undamaged. This was not a burglary. It was a fitting.

* * *

The photo arrived at 9:47 p.m., while I was back on shift, headset on, board half-lit.

It came to my personal email—the one I use for nothing but utility bills and my sister's forwarded jokes—which meant whoever sent it had done more than break into my closet. They'd looked at my life long enough to find the quiet channels. That should have frightened me most, and it did, but not as much as the image itself.

Me. In the jacket, the scarf, the gloves. Standing on Kessler Street, near where Dana Voss died, looking directly into the camera with an expression I have never made in my life—a small, satisfied almost-smile, the face of someone pleased with their work. It was my face, unmistakably, but composited, assembled, grafted onto a body standing at an angle my shoulders don't make. Bad Photoshop, honestly. Sloppy shadow work. The kind of forgery that wouldn't survive five minutes with a forensic examiner.

But it wasn't made to survive an examiner. It was made to survive a news cycle. A tip line. A panicked detective's first glance.

Attached, a single line of text, no subject:

**You wear it well.**

I sat with the headset humming against my ear and read it four times. Around me the night went on—Unit 12 requesting a pickup on Halloway, a drunk singing somewhere in the background of a call, the ordinary symphony of a city that doesn't know what's happening inside one dispatcher's chest.

Here's what I understood, sitting in that pool of lamplight: the photo wasn't a threat in the way I first read it. Threats say *stop*. This said *continue*. You don't frame someone and then warn them off—you frame them and wait for the machinery to close. Unless the frame is bait. Unless someone wants me scared, and scared people run, and running people get found, but digging people—

Digging people dig toward things.

Dana Voss had recanted privately two years ago and then gone silent. Dana Voss had died in my clothes. And now a dead woman's killer—or her killer's employer—was nudging me along a trail like bread crumbs, wanting me frightened enough to be predictable and curious enough to be useful. They wanted me digging. Just not where I'd choose to dig.

Fine. Then I'd be the worst kind of quarry: the kind that reads the hunter's map and walks the other direction.

I printed the email. I wrote the timestamp on the back in pen. And when Unit 12 signed off at midnight, I asked Marisol—twenty-two years driving nights, sharp as broken glass—if she still had that cousin who worked building security downtown.

Because if someone wanted to dress me in a story, I intended to find out who owned the wardrobe. And the next place that thread led wasn't my building at all.

It was the Ashford Foundry—and the man whose grief the whole city had spent seven years believing.
