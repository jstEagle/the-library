## 8. Dana's Last Shift

There's a particular kind of quiet that settles over a dispatch board at four in the morning, and I've come to think of it as the sound of the city telling the truth. Nobody performs at 4 a.m. The drunks are honest, the insomniacs are honest, the drivers on my board are too tired to lie about where they've been. It's the hour when the paper trail warms up, if you know how to listen.

I didn't sleep. That's not dramatic; it's arithmetic. Dana Voss was wearing my clothes when she died, which meant someone had been in my apartment, and every hour I spent horizontal was an hour that person spent being smarter than me. So instead of sleeping, I did what I do best: I pulled logs.

The taxi company's archive goes back eleven years. Every call, every pickup, every fare, timestamped and driver-coded, sitting in a database nobody audits because nobody thinks a cab company's records matter. That's the beautiful thing about unglamorous infrastructure—it remembers everything precisely because no one expects it to.

Dana Voss died on a Tuesday in October. I went back to the Monday before it.

Her name wasn't in the system under Voss. But I'd learned her address from the news coverage—Maple Row, the apartment over the dry cleaner—and I ran the address through the call log instead of the name. People lie with names. Addresses are harder.

One hit. 11:47 p.m., Tuesday night. The night she died.

Pickup location: 14 Ashford Lane.

I stared at that address for a long time, long enough that the screen saver kicked in and I had to jiggle the mouse like a detective in a bad movie. Because 14 Ashford Lane is not an apartment over a dry cleaner. 14 Ashford Lane is the Hale estate—the big stone place on the hill above the river, where Marcus Hale has lived since before the fire, back when he was just a foundry owner instead of the city's favorite grieving philanthropist.

And here's the thing. Dana Voss had told everyone—told reporters, told the police, told anyone who'd hold still—that she hadn't set foot on that property in years. She said it in interviews. She said it in the one public statement she ever gave after the fire closed. *I have nothing to do with Marcus Hale or his family. I haven't been there since the funerals.* Her voice had that flat, rehearsed quality of someone reciting lines they'd been made to memorize.

But at 11:47 p.m. on the last night of her life, she called a cab from his front drive.

* * *

The driver code on the fare was R-114. Ray Okafor. Twenty-two years on the night shift, drives like a man reading scripture aloud—slow, deliberate, no surprises. If anyone remembered a fare from three weeks ago, it would be Ray.

He came into the depot at 5:15 for his shift change, and I bought him a coffee from the machine that dispenses something technically legal but morally gray, and I slid into the passenger seat of his cab while he drank it.

"Ray. The eleventh of October. You picked up a woman at 14 Ashford Lane, around midnight."

He didn't even blink. "Voss woman. Yeah." He took another sip. "Been waiting for somebody to ask me about that."

My spine went cold in a way I didn't love. "Why?"

"Because it was strange, Yara. And strange things don't usually stay strange. They come back around." He set the cup down. "She called the dispatch line herself. Not the app—she called, voice mail, you know how the old system works. Sounded calm. Said she needed a ride to Maple Row, no rush. I get there, she's standing by the gatehouse, not the main door. Side gate. Like she didn't want to be seen leaving the front."

"Was anyone with her?"

Ray was quiet for a moment, choosing his words the way he chose his routes—carefully, avoiding anything that might blow up. "Here's where it gets strange. When I pull up, there's a man walking her out. Tall guy, gray coat, stays maybe ten feet behind her the whole way. Doesn't help with her bag. Doesn't say goodbye. Just watches her get in the car."

"You know him?"

"I know him now," Ray said, and the past tense landed wrong in my chest. "Because after—" He stopped. Started again. "After they found her, I did what you do. I looked at pictures. News sites, the memorial pages, all that. And that man was in one of them. Standing behind Marcus Hale at some charity thing. Caption said Colin Merrick. Some kind of assistant."

Colin Merrick. The name slid into place next to everything else I'd collected, clicking like a tumbler in a lock. Hale's right hand. The man who handled things. I'd seen the name twice already this week—in a seven-year-old article about the fire settlement, listed as "estate representative," and once more in Dana's own file, in a police report footnote so minor it might as well have been invisible: *Ms. Voss's statements were facilitated by Mr. C. Merrick, acting for the estate.*

Facilitated. What a soft word for whatever he actually did.

"What was her mood?" I asked. "In the car."

Ray's hands rested on the wheel at ten and two, even parked, even off duty. A man built for waiting. "Quiet at first. Then about halfway down the hill she starts talking—not to me exactly, more to the windshield. She says, 'Tell them I'm done. Tell him I'm done.' Over and over. Like a woman practicing a speech."

"Tell who?"

"I figured her family. Grief does that, makes people talk in circles." He finally turned and looked at me, and his eyes were tired in the old way, the way men get tired when they've seen too many fares end badly. "But then near Maple Row she says one more thing. Clear as church bells. She says, 'If anything happens to me, it wasn't an accident.'"

The coffee in my hand went cold, or my hand went cold around the coffee—one of the two. "Did you tell the police?"

"I called the tip line the morning they found her. Left a message. Nobody called back." He shrugged, a small, defeated motion. "They had their story already. Woman alone, bad stairs, sad ending. Who needs a cabdriver's memory getting in the way?"

Who indeed. I thought of Ruiz, of her careful notebook and her suspicion worn like armor, and decided that Detective Imogen Ruiz was about to get a phone call she couldn't ignore.

"One more thing, Ray. The man in the gray coat. When you drove away—did he follow?"

Ray nodded slowly. "Black SUV, pulled out of the side gate maybe thirty seconds behind me. Followed us all the way to Maple Row. Parked across the street." He paused. "I watched it in the mirror until I turned the corner. It was still there."

So Dana Voss left the Hale estate at midnight with Merrick's eyes on her back, delivered a warning to a stranger in a cab, and was dead by morning. And the official record said she hadn't been near that house in years.

Lies stack, I've learned. They don't collapse all at once—they lean, and lean, and then one day a dispatcher pulls a log file and the whole wall finds its angle.

* * *

Ruiz answered on the second ring, which told me she was also awake, also not sleeping, also doing arithmetic.

"Ashford Lane," I said, without hello. "Dana called a cab from the Hale estate the night she died. Eleven forty-seven p.m. Driver confirms a man walked her out—Colin Merrick. And she told the driver, word for word, 'If anything happens to me, it wasn't an accident.'"

Silence on the line. Then the sound of a pen clicking, twice, fast—the sound of Ruiz thinking in shorthand.

"The report says she claimed she hadn't been there in years," Ruiz said finally.

"The report also says she fell down her own stairs sober, which her tox screen contradicts, but who's counting."

"How do you know about the tox screen?"

"I read footnotes, Detective. It's a hobby."

Another click of the pen. "You're saying Hale lied about the timeline. Or Dana did."

"I'm saying Dana told the truth to a cabdriver and a lie to everyone else, and the difference between those two audiences is a man named Colin Merrick." I let that sit. "She was at that house the night she died, Ruiz. Whatever she went there to say—'tell him I'm done'—it got her killed within hours."

"And the message she left you," Ruiz said quietly. "The clothes. Your clothes."

"What about it?"

"It's the same sentence, isn't it? *It wasn't an accident.* Whoever killed Dana is telling you they know what you do. That you find the accidents that aren't." The pen clicked again. "Which means we're not looking for a killer who's hiding anymore. We're looking for one who's inviting."

I hung up and sat in the empty depot with the board glowing green in front of me, calls trickling in from the honest corners of the city, and I understood something with terrible clarity: Dana Voss had spent her last ride rehearsing a confession she never got to make. And somewhere out there, the man in the gray coat was watching to see whether I'd finish it for her.

My phone buzzed. Unknown number. One line:

*Ask Theo Brandt what the checks were for.*

I looked at the message for a long time. Then I did the bravest and stupidest thing available to me at 6 a.m.

I looked up Theo Brandt's address.
