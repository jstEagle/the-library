## 9. Found Family, Fraying

There are five of us left, which is a terrible way to start a dinner, but it's the only way we know how to start one.

The Ashford Foundry Survivors' Monthly Supper Club — we've never called it that out loud, but the name lives in the walls of the back room at Kowalczyk's Tavern the way smoke lives in a coat you can't bring yourself to throw away. First Thursday of every month, seven o'clock, the corner booth with the wobbly table leg Marcus Hale paid to have fixed and then never fixed, because the check got lost between his foundation and his accountant, the way most things do. We order too many pierogi and not enough beer, and we talk about everything except the thing.

Seven years of practice. You'd think we'd be good at it by now. We are. That's the problem.

* * *

I got there early, the way I always do. Habit from the foundry — I was the one who clocked in twenty minutes ahead of shift, checked the gauges, walked the floor. The night of the fire I was in the locker room changing out of my coveralls when the third shift alarm went off, which is the only reason I'm alive to have a monthly dinner about it. Twenty minutes early. Forty percent of my body. There's a math joke in there somewhere, and I've never been able to find it.

The booth was cold. Kowalczyk's is always cold in the corner; the radiator gave up the ghost around the same time we did. I sat with my back to the wall, which is another habit I picked up from the fire, or from what came after, and I watched the door.

Priya came in at seven sharp, shaking snow off her coat, her phone already in her hand because her son Arjun's school had sent four messages that afternoon about a fever going around and she reads them like scripture. She slid in across from me and didn't look at me, which was normal. We don't look at each other much, the five of us. We look at our plates, our drinks, our hands. Hands are safe. Hands tell the truth about what they've survived without telling anything else.

"Arjun's fever broke," she said, to the table.

"Good," I said, to the table.

That was our whole conversation for the first ten minutes. It was a good one, as our conversations go.

Dana used to come to these dinners. That's the thing nobody's said out loud yet, three weeks after they pulled her out of the river with her lungs full of it and her pockets empty of it. Dana Voss, who signed the confession that let us all keep our settlements, who sat in this booth every first Thursday and ordered the mushroom pierogi and laughed at my jokes the way you laugh at a man who's been burned over forty percent of his body — generously, out of pity, out of something that might have been love. She was the only one of us who never worked the floor. She was the office clerk. She saw the maintenance logs. She saw what Marcus Hale saw, and what he ordered, and what he signed, and then she stood in front of the state investigator and said the fire was an electrical fault, no negligence indicated, and she came to this booth afterward and drank until she cried and we all pretended that was grief for the dead and not for herself.

And now she was dead. Ruled accidental. Of course it was. Everything we touch gets ruled accidental.

* * *

The others arrived in their usual order. Sal Mercer first after us — Sal's lungs are shot, seven years of scar tissue doing the work his alveoli used to do, and he breathes like a man climbing stairs even when he's sitting down. He's the one who loves Marcus. Says it flat out, no shame. "The man paid for my pulmonary rehab. The man sends a card every Christmas with a personal note. You want me to hate him?" Sal's loyalty is the honest kind, which makes it the hardest kind to argue with. He was a crane operator for twenty-two years. He believes in chains of command. The order came down the chain, and it wasn't from Hale, and if you keep pulling that thread, Sal, I always want to say, you'll find the chain is made of paper and every link has Hale's signature on it. But I've never said it. We don't say things at this table. That's the whole architecture of the thing.

Marta Kessler came next, alone as always, her husband having died — actually died, medically died, no asterisk — three years ago of a heart attack that had nothing to do with anything, probably. Marta is terrified. You can see it in the way she checks the windows. She was the one who got the phone call, back in year two, the one with no voice on the other end, just breathing, and then a click. After that she started parking two blocks from the restaurant and walking a different route each time. She thinks none of us notice. We all notice. That's the other thing about our family: we notice everything and acknowledge nothing.

And then the door opened and the stranger walked in, and I knew her before Sal did, before Marta did, before Priya's whole body went still the way prey goes still.

Yara Hartwell. The dispatcher. I'd heard the name the way you hear a name in a town this size — attached to stories. Three deaths the police called accidents, three anonymous tips that turned them into something else. The drivers at the cab company talk about her like she's a weather pattern. She sees things. She notices. Which is, if you're one of the five people at this table with something to hide, not a comforting reputation.

"I'm sorry to intrude," she said, and her voice had that dispatcher flatness to it, the calm they train into you or the calm the night shift burns into you, hard to say which. "I'm looking for Theo Brandt. Someone at the cab company said he might be here."

Sal looked at me. Priya looked at her plate. Marta looked at the window.

"That's me," I said. "You found me."

"I was hoping I could ask you a few questions. About Dana Voss." She didn't sit. She stood there with her hands in the pockets of a coat that had seen better decades, and she let the silence do its work, and I'll tell you what — the silence worked. Because Dana's name in this room was a lit match dropped into a room full of what we were all pretending was air.

"You can ask," I said. "I can't promise answers."

"That's fair. That's more than the police gave me."

Sal cleared his throat, the wet rattle of it. "Who are you, exactly? You a reporter?"

"I dispatch taxis," she said. "Nights."

"And you're asking about Dana Voss because—"

"Because she called a cab the night she died. From a place she told everyone she hadn't been in years. And because somebody wants me to stop asking." She said it plainly, no drama in it, and I watched it land on each of them differently — Sal bristling, Marta going gray, Priya's hand moving to her phone like it was a rosary. "I'm sorry. I'll go. Theo, can I walk you out when you're done? No pressure."

And then, God help me, the family did what the family does. Sal said, "You shouldn't be here," and Marta said, "This is a private dinner," and Priya said nothing at all, and the stranger nodded like she'd expected exactly that, because she had, because she'd read us in ten seconds the way I used to read a gauge bank, and she turned to go.

"Wait," I said. And I don't know why I said it. I do know why. I said it because Dana sat in that booth for seven years and never once told us the truth, and because the truth had just walked in wearing a thrift-store coat and offered itself to us for free, and because forty percent of my body has been on fire for seven years and the only thing that ever put any of it out was the thought that maybe, someday, somebody would say out loud what actually happened in that building.

"Ten minutes," I said. "Outside, after. Let me finish my dinner."

* * *

We finished the dinner. That sounds like nothing. It was the hardest hour of my life, and I've had skin grafts.

Sal talked about his granddaughter's soccer tournament. Marta talked about a recipe. Priya refilled everyone's water glasses like it was a sacrament, and twice her eyes came up and met mine across the table, and both times there was something in them I'd never seen before, or something I'd seen and refused to name. Fear, yes, but fear with a direction. Fear aimed at something specific. I've been afraid of a lot of things since the fire — of open flame, of closed doors, of the sound of a phone ringing after midnight — but Priya's fear had a return address.

"Marcus called me last week," Sal said, apropos of nothing, apropos of everything. "About the memorial. Seventh anniversary. He wants to do it right this time. At the foundry itself — you know he restored it? Turned it into some kind of event space. He wants us all up front. Honored guests."

"Honored guests," Marta repeated, and her voice did something strange on the second word, something thin.

"He's a good man," Sal said. "Whatever people are whispering now that Dana's gone. He's a good man who had a bad night seven years ago, same as the rest of us."

"Same as the rest of us," I said, and I heard how it came out, flat and cold, and Sal looked at me like I'd slapped him.

"You got something to say, Theo?"

"No," I said. Which was true. I had something to *not* say, and I'd been not saying it for seven years, and my not-saying had a settlement attached to it and a pulmonary rehab and a Christmas card with a personal note, and I was so tired of the weight of all that silence that the table seemed to bend under it.

We paid. We always split it evenly, down to the cent, another piece of the architecture — fairness, the appearance of it, the ritual. And then the stranger was waiting outside under Kowalczyk's awning with the snow coming down around her like the town was trying to bury the whole conversation, and I buttoned my coat over my ruined skin, which hates the cold worse than it hates fire now, funny how that works, and I went out to meet her.

* * *

She didn't waste my time. I'll give her that. She told me about the jacket — my stomach dropped through the sidewalk when she said it, because I'd seen the photos of the scene, everyone in this town had seen them, and that scarf had looked familiar to me in a way I'd refused to examine — and about Dana's recantation, the one Dana made to her two years ago, private, terrified, and then Dana went silent, and then Dana went dead.

"She told you she lied," I said.

"She told me she was coerced. She didn't say by who. She didn't have to."

"Say it anyway."

She looked at me. Snow in her hair. "Marcus Hale."

And there it was, out loud, in the open air of a public street, and the sky didn't fall, and the ground didn't open, and no black car came around the corner, and it was almost disappointing, how much nothing happened, after seven years of everything depending on it not being said.

"You don't understand what he did for us," I said, and I heard my own voice doing the thing Sal's voice does, the loyal thing, the grateful thing, and I hated it. "The settlements. My rehab. Priya's son's coverage — that kid has a congenital heart condition, and the foundation pays for everything, everything. You tear Hale down, the foundation goes with him. You know what happens to Priya's kid?"

"I know what happened to Dana Voss."

"She lied," I said. "She lied for seven years and she got paid for it same as the rest of us, and maybe somebody decided the bill came due. That's not my table. That's not my war."

"Then why did you come outside?"

I didn't have an answer. I stood there in the snow with my hands in my pockets and my scars aching the way they do when the truth gets close, some sympathetic magic in the nerve endings, and I thought about Dana in the booth ordering her mushroom pierogi, and about the phone call Marta got, the one with just the breathing in it, and about the night of the fire, which I don't think about, which I have trained myself not to think about, and which I thought about anyway, standing there: the alarm, the heat coming down the corridor like a living thing, and — no. Not that part. Not the part I've never told anyone, the part about the maintenance log I saw three days before the fire, the work order for the kiln seals that got countersigned and then vanished from the system.

"Theo." Her voice, quieter now. "Somebody stole my clothes and put them on a dead woman to send me a message. Whoever it is knows I was asking about the fire. Which means whoever it is has been watching me, which means they're watching everyone who talks to me." She paused. "Including you. Including everyone in that restaurant."

"I know how settlements work," I said. "I signed one."

"Then you know they're not just money. They're a leash. And somebody's holding all five of yours."

* * *

I went back inside for my gloves — I'd left them on the seat, my hands ache without them, the grafts, the cold — and the booth was empty, the others gone out the back the way we sometimes do, the way we've done since year two, and Priya was waiting by the coat rack, and she did the thing.

She did it fast, the way you'd pass contraband in a prison yard, which I suppose is what it was. Her hand brushed mine, and the paper was there, folded small, small enough to hide in a palm, in a fist, in a pocket, in seven years of silence, and she didn't look at me, and she said, "Get home safe, Theo," in a voice like a closed door, and she walked out into the snow.

I stood there with the note against my palm and my heart going like the old foundry press, and I didn't open it. Not there. Kowalczyk's back room had felt safe for seven years and it had just stopped being safe in the space of one dinner, and I have learned — forty percent of me has learned — to respect that kind of information.

I walked home the long way. Checked behind me twice. Felt ridiculous, then felt ridiculous for feeling ridiculous, then stopped feeling anything except the paper in my pocket, which seemed to get heavier with every block, the way guilt does, the way truth does.

Inside, under the kitchen light, with the deadbolt thrown and the curtains drawn, I opened it.

Her handwriting, small and careful, the handwriting of a woman who has practiced being precise because precision is the only armor they let her keep:

*Theo. I saw Colin Merrick's car in the foundry lot at 11:40 the night of the fire. The official timeline says the building was empty and locked by 10. I never told the investigators. I never told anyone. I have a son. You understand. But Dana deserved better than the river, and so did the four of them, and I can't carry it alone anymore. Burn this. Or don't. — P.*

I sat at my kitchen table for a long time. The radiator ticked. Somewhere down the block a plow went by, scraping the night clean, making it look like nothing had happened.

Colin Merrick. Hale's fixer. The man who delivered the settlement checks personally, who shook each of our hands, who remembered our kids' names, who was so *kind*, God help us, the kindness of a man who knows exactly how much you're worth and what it costs to keep you.

And the foundry lot, at 11:40. Forty minutes before the alarm.

I got out my phone. The dispatcher's card was in my coat pocket, where she'd put it, where I'd let her put it, and I looked at it for a long time before I did anything, because I knew what the call meant. It meant the end of the dinners. It meant the end of the architecture. It meant Sal's Christmas cards and Priya's son's coverage and Marta's two-block walks, all of it, everything the silence bought us, on the table, in the fire, gone.

Then I thought about Dana's face the last time I saw her, three weeks ago in this same booth, laughing at one of my jokes with that generous pity in her eyes, and how she'd gripped my hand on the way out — harder than usual, longer than usual, a grip with a message in it that I'd been too much of a coward to read.

I dialed.

"Dispatch, this is Yara."

"It's Brandt," I said. "Theo Brandt. You wanted ten minutes. I've got more than that." I looked down at the note in my hand, at the name that was going to burn down everything the five of us had left. "And I think Priya Nair does too."
