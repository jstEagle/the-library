## 11. Break-In, Second Time

The first time someone broke into my apartment, I didn't notice for three days.

That's the kind of sentence that sounds like a confession at a support group, but it's just the truth of my life: I own very little, I misplace most of it, and the geography of my apartment is less "organized home" and more "sediment layers." The burglary—my burglary, the one I never reported because who reports a theft where nothing's missing?—happened while I was on shift, and by the time I came home and felt that faint wrongness in the air, like a room that had been breathed in by someone else, I'd already talked myself out of it. Drafts. Memory. The general entropy of a woman who lives alone and works nights.

This time was different. This time I noticed before I got the key all the way into the lock.

* * *

It started with Theo Brandt calling me at four in the afternoon, which for a man who'd spent seven years perfecting the art of not calling anyone was practically a fireworks display.

"You need to leave wherever you are," he said. No hello. His voice had the flat, careful quality of someone holding a hot pan by the handle.

"Theo—"

"Don't argue. Don't go home. Don't go to your car if it's parked outside your building. Just—walk somewhere public. Call Ruiz."

"Theo, what happened?"

A pause long enough that I could hear him breathing through his nose, the way he does when he's deciding how much truth he can afford. "Priya called me. She said a man was asking questions at the clinic where she takes Dev—not about her, about you. Showed her a photo." Another breath. "Yara, she said the photo was of you wearing clothes that weren't yours."

I stood in the break room of the dispatch office with a cooling cup of vending machine coffee and felt the temperature of my whole life drop about ten degrees. The staged photo. The one that had arrived in my inbox two nights ago from an address made of random letters, showing a woman in my burgundy jacket and gray scarf, face turned away, standing on Kessler Street near where Dana Voss died. I'd stared at it so long the pixels started to swim. I'd told myself it was a threat, which was bad enough. It hadn't occurred to me until that moment that it was also an *advertisement*. Someone wasn't just scaring me. They were introducing me—to Priya, to Theo, to whoever else would look—as a character in a story they were writing.

"Did you call Ruiz?" I asked.

"I'm calling you first," Theo said, and there was something raw under the flatness. "Because last time I let the system handle something, Dana ended up in a chalk outline. You're the system now, as far as I can tell. So go be it somewhere with witnesses."

I went. I called Ruiz from the diner on Corliss Avenue, wedged into a booth with a view of the door because apparently paranoia is contagious and I'd caught it wholesale. She picked up on the second ring, listened without interrupting—which for Imogen Ruiz is roughly equivalent to a standing ovation—and said, "Stay there. I'll meet you. And Yara?" A beat. "Don't go home alone. Not tonight, not tomorrow. If someone wants you to walk into your apartment, we're going to make them wait."

We waited forty-eight hours, which is its own special torture when your home is the thing in question. Ruiz wanted a warrant for a formal search; I pointed out that technically nothing had been reported stolen, ever, and the paperwork on my life wouldn't survive contact with a magistrate. What we did instead was dumber and better: on Saturday morning, Ruiz walked me in with two uniformed officers she described as "borrowed," and we documented everything ourselves, photograph by photograph, before I touched a single thing.

I want to tell you what it feels like to have your apartment photographed by police while you stand in your own doorway like a stranger. It feels like being read aloud in a language you don't speak. Every object suddenly had to explain itself. The chipped mug. The dispatch schedules taped above my desk. The second pillow on the bed that no one had used in months but that I kept anyway, out of some stubborn arithmetic of hope. The officer taking pictures paused at that one, and I watched him decide not to ask, and I loved him a little for it.

Nothing was stolen. That was the first finding, and Ruiz said it out loud like a diagnosis: "Nothing's missing."

Everything had been photographed. That was the second finding, and it took longer to see, because the evidence was absence-shaped: faint dust rings where picture frames had been lifted off hooks and set back down a millimeter off-true. My desk drawers closed in the wrong order—the top one over the bottom one, when I always do bottom-first, a habit so ingrained I do it half-asleep. My bookshelf reshelved by height instead of by the private logic of which books I reach for at 3 a.m. Someone had gone through my life the way I go through other people's lives: thoroughly, patiently, looking for patterns.

"They weren't looking for something specific," I said, mostly to myself. "They were building a file."

Ruiz crouched by my desk, gloved, turning over a stack of taxi logs I'd copied from the archive—the ones showing Dana Voss's last ride. They were still there. Of course they were still there. "Or they already have the file," she said, "and this was maintenance. An update." She looked up at me. "Which means they expect you to keep finding things. Which means either they're sloppy—"

"—or they're feeding me," I finished.

* * *

Here's the thing about being a dispatcher: you spend years listening to people describe rooms you've never seen. You learn that everyone's memory of a space is a reconstruction, and that the seams show if you know where to look. So when I finally walked my own apartment after the documentation was done, I read it like a witness statement, and the statement had a hole in it.

The bedroom closet.

My clothes had been stolen once already—jacket, scarf, a pair of boots, small things, things that could be worn by someone my size. After the photo arrived, I'd assumed the thief kept them, wore them, displayed them like trophies. But standing in front of the closet Saturday morning, I counted hangers twice, and then a third time, and then I sat down on the edge of my bed and laughed, one dry note with no humor anywhere in it.

The jacket was back. The scarf too, folded on the shelf, neat as a returned library book.

"They brought them back," I told Ruiz. She came and stood beside me and looked at the burgundy jacket like it might testify.

"When?"

"I don't know. Sometime between the first break-in and now. Maybe today. Maybe during this visit." I picked up the scarf with two fingers, the way you handle evidence even when nobody's asked you to. It smelled faintly of cold air and something chemical underneath—dry cleaning, maybe, or smoke scrubbed almost clean. Almost. "They took my clothes to dress a dead woman in them, and then they gave them back."

"That's not remorse," Ruiz said quietly.

"No. That's a message with a return receipt."

She bagged the scarf anyway, and the jacket, though we both knew the lab would find what labs always find: nothing clean, nothing conclusive, a man careful enough to be worth fearing.

* * *

It was Ruiz who found the journal page, and I will say, for the record, that she found it fair and square, because I'd have missed it.

Dana Voss had kept a journal—I knew that from her sister, Marisol, whom I'd tracked down through a paper trail of utility bills and grief. Marisol had given me photocopies of what she had: eleven pages, dated across the last year of Dana's life, the handwriting getting smaller and more slanted as it went, like the words were trying to take up less room in the world. One page was missing from the sequence. Page nine jumped to page eleven, and Marisol swore page ten had existed—she remembered it, remembered Dana reading part of it aloud once, remembered it ending abruptly. We'd looked. It was gone. I'd filed its absence alongside the stolen clothes, another subtraction in the arithmetic of a life being edited by someone else.

Page ten was lying on my kitchen table.

Not hidden. Not tucked into anything. Placed dead center, squared to the table's edge, weighted down by—of all things—one of my own coffee mugs, the chipped one, as if the visitor had made themselves at home and left a tip. The handwriting was Dana's. I'd spent enough hours with those photocopies to know it the way you know a voice on the phone: the cramped leftward lean, the way she crossed her sevens.

I didn't touch it for a full minute. Then I put on gloves that Ruiz handed me without being asked, because we'd stopped needing to explain ourselves to each other somewhere around the third crime scene, and I read.

*Drove past the foundry tonight. Couldn't make myself stop. Seven years and I still hear the sound the roof made.*

*I keep the checks. I hate that I keep the checks. Theo says we all do what we have to. But Theo doesn't know what I know.*

*C.M. was there that night. Before. He told me what to say and he told me what it would cost if I said anything else, and he was right, God help me, he was right, because I said what he told me to say and I'm alive.*

*But the fire had a second cause. Everyone looks at the wiring, the old panels, Hale's corner-cutting—that's real, that's true, Marcus cut every corner he could find. But the fire started early. Before the shift change. Before anyone should have been in the kiln room at all. Someone set it to move up the timeline, and C.M. knows who, because C.M. arranged it. Insurance was only half of it. Ask what the night crew was supposed to be moving out at midnight. Ask why the manifest changed.*

*I wrote it all down. Where I wrote it down doesn't matter. What matters is that if I die, it wasn't an accident, and it wasn't me. I'm not brave enough to do anything else. I just needed it to exist somewhere outside my head.*

That was the end of the page. The ink pressed hard on the last line, the pen nearly through the paper, a woman bearing down on the world with everything she had left.

I read it three times. On the third pass, my hands were steady, which surprised me, because inside I was doing what I imagine a house does in an earthquake—all the familiar load-bearing walls moving at once.

"C.M.," Ruiz said. "Colin Merrick."

"Merrick." The name had been circling us for days now, a bird getting lower. Hale's fixer. The man the driver remembered climbing into the cab behind Dana on her last night, the man whose name appeared nowhere in any official document about the fire, the man who existed, professionally speaking, in the negative space around Marcus Hale. "And 'the manifest changed.' Something was supposed to be moved out of the foundry the night of the fire. Something worth burning four people's deaths on top of."

"Insurance fraud plus cargo," Ruiz said. "Whatever was in that building at midnight, Hale couldn't let it be found. So the fire moved up." She was pacing my narrow kitchen, three steps each way, a metronome winding itself tight. "This page should be in a storage unit in Merrick's name along with the rest of Dana's real confession. Instead it's on your table."

"Because he wants me to have it."

"He *left* it, Yara. There's a difference."

"Is there?" I looked at the page again, at the mug sitting where it had been placed, deliberate as a signature. "Think about the sequence. First they steal my clothes and use them on Dana—a message that says *we see you, we can reach you, we can wear you.* Then the staged photo goes out to the survivors, introducing me as a problem. Then they search my place and photograph everything—cataloguing what I know, what I've found. And now they give me the one piece I was missing. The page that points harder at Merrick. That gives me a new question—the manifest, the cargo, the second cause." I pulled out a chair and sat down, because my legs had opinions about remaining vertical. "That's not a burglar's trail. That's a curriculum."

Ruiz stopped pacing. "You think someone's steering you."

"I think someone's been steering me since Kessler Street. Every clue arrives right on schedule. Every door opens exactly far enough." I tapped the page, gently, through the plastic sleeve Ruiz had produced from her coat like a magician. "Dana wrote that she hid the rest of her confession somewhere that 'doesn't matter.' Whoever has this page has probably been hunting the rest of it for seven years—or since Dana started talking again. And they've decided the fastest way to find it isn't to follow me." I looked up at her. "It's to *be* me. Let me do the work. Let me pull the threads. And when I finally lead them to whatever's left of Dana's real confession—"

"—they take it, and they take you, and it looks like the obsessed amateur dispatcher wandered too deep." Ruiz's jaw was doing the thing it does when she's angry in a professional capacity, which is to say it looked carved out of wood. "It's elegant. I hate it."

"It's a leash," I said. "Long enough to feel like freedom."

* * *

After Ruiz left—with the journal page, over my objection and then with my grudging agreement, because she was right that a page naming C.M. belonged in an evidence locker and not pinned above my dispatch board, however much I wanted it there—I sat in my apartment and did the thing I do best, which is listen to a room.

The silence had changed texture. That's the honest truth of it, the thing I'd never say out loud to anyone: for seven years my apartment had been the one place shaped exactly like my solitude, and now it was shaped like someone else's attention. I kept catching myself checking the dust rings on the walls, straightening books I hadn't touched, re-closing the top drawer first just to spite a man who'd never see it. Violation, it turns out, isn't a single event. It's a climate.

But underneath the violation, colder and quieter, was the thought I kept returning to, the one that felt like a stone in my shoe all evening:

He wants me digging. He's invested in my digging. Which means everything I've found so far—the logs, the driver, the ledger thread Ruiz was pulling—he already knew I'd find. Which means the real shape of this thing, the cargo, the manifest, the reason four people died and one woman was dressed in my clothes and left on Kessler Street, is still sitting somewhere ahead of me, past the edge of the map he's drawn.

And that meant the anniversary memorial at the restored foundry—the one Hale had announced with press releases and candle graphics—wasn't just a memorial. It was the last page of the curriculum. The field trip. He was going to gather every survivor, every reporter, every loose end into the one building where this all began, and close the book.

Unless the book closed differently.

My phone buzzed. A text from an unknown number, which these days is less a surprise than a genre. I braced for another photo.

It was a single line, no sender, no signature:

*Ask Priya Nair what she saw the night of the fire. Before someone asks her first.*

I stared at it for a long time. A gift or a tripwire—maybe both, maybe always both, with this man. But Priya had slipped me a note at the survivors' dinner, folded small as a prayer, and I hadn't yet worked up the courage to open it, because I'd understood instinctively that whatever was inside would cost somebody something they couldn't afford.

I unfolded it now, there in my violated kitchen, and read it twice.

Then I reached for my coat—the burgundy one, back on its hanger like nothing had happened—and called the number for the night-shift cab rotation. Some habits are load-bearing.

"Priya Nair," I said when the driver picked up. "And drive slow. I want to see everything on the way."
