## 12. Merrick's Ledger

Detectives are taught, early, that money doesn't lie. It just mumbles. Your job is to stand close enough to hear it.

I'd been standing close to Colin Merrick's finances for six hours, and by hour four the mumbling had turned into something close to a sermon.

The trick with men like Merrick is that they're careful, but not patient. Careful men hide their money in shell companies and offshore accounts. Patient men would have waited out the statute of limitations and lived like monks for a decade. Merrick was careful the way a burglar is careful—gloves, alibis, a bland face for every camera. But he still had to *pay* people. Money is the one confession a fixer can't withhold. Every hush dollar leaves a fingerprint, and fingerprints don't take the Fifth.

I started with the shell companies because starting with shell companies is like starting with the onion's outer layer: unpleasant, but you can't get to the center without peeling it. Merrick had three. Ashford Holdings LLC, which sounded like a man who'd named his own alibi. Kestrel Property Group, which held—of all things—a lease on a self-storage facility in the industrial flats, Unit 214. And a third, Meridian Consulting, which existed, as far as I could tell, to pay one person: a forensic fire investigator named Dale Ostrander.

Ostrander. The name rang a bell the way a dead man rings a bell—dully, from far away. I pulled the file on the Ashford Foundry fire and there he was, listed in the appendix of the official report as an independent consultant whose analysis "supported the finding of electrical fault." Independent. Paid through a shell company controlled by the man whose negligence he'd just cleared. That's not independence. That's a ventriloquist act with a lab coat.

I called the state licensing board. Dale Ostrander's fire investigator license had lapsed four years ago. I called the number on his old business card, out of habit, and got a voicemail that said the mailbox was full. I called the county morgue and asked a clerk I owed a favor to whether the name meant anything. It did. Dale Ostrander, deceased, eighteen months back. Liver failure, per the death certificate. He'd been forty-six.

Liver failure at forty-six is possible. Liver failure at forty-six in a man whose consulting fees dried up right around the time his usefulness did—that's a pattern. I wrote the date of his last Meridian payment in my notebook and the date of his death next to it, and the gap between them was eleven months. Eleven months of a man drinking himself to death on the money he'd taken to bury four workers under the word "fault."

I sat with that for a while. It's important, in this job, to sit with things. The sitting is where the anger gets organized.

* * *

The survivors' payments were almost elegant in their simplicity. Merrick hadn't used the shells for those—he'd used something better. An annuity. Structured settlements, disbursed monthly, administered by a legitimate financial firm that had no idea what the money was for. Five accounts. Five survivors of the Ashford fire, each receiving a monthly deposit that, over seven years, added up to enough to buy silence from people who couldn't have afforded to refuse it.

I pulled the disbursement records. Theo Brandt. Priya Nair. And three names I didn't know yet—Marisol Vega, Edmund Cho, and a fifth account that had gone dormant two years ago.

Dana Voss.

Her account had stopped receiving deposits twenty-six months ago. I checked the termination paperwork. The closure was requested by the account holder, per the firm's records. But the signature on the closure request was Dana Voss's, and I'd spent enough of the last week with Dana Voss's signature—on her confession, on her tax forms, on her lease—to know that the woman signed her name like she was apologizing for taking up space. The signature on the closure request was confident. Clean. A signature written by someone who'd never met Dana Voss, or by someone who'd practiced her name until he could forge it in his sleep.

Twenty-six months. Dana had gone silent twenty-six months ago. The annuity—the money that kept her quiet—had been cut off twenty-six months ago.

You don't have to be a genius to do that arithmetic. You just have to be willing to do it.

Either Dana had tried to walk away from the money, and someone had cut her off to punish her, or someone had cut her off because she'd stopped being quiet and started being dangerous. Either way, the timeline said the same thing: the silence had a price tag, and when Dana stopped accepting the payment, she'd signed her own death warrant in a hand that wasn't hers.

I put the disbursement records in a folder. Then I put the folder in my desk, then took it out again, because my desk had a way of eating things, and put it in my car instead. Under the seat. Call me paranoid. Call me a woman who'd read too many case files about evidence that walked out of evidence rooms. I've been called worse by better people.

* * *

Unit 214.

The storage facility sat in the industrial flats like a monument to other people's secrets—rows of orange roll-up doors, a chain-link fence, a manager's office that smelled like burnt coffee and despair. I'd brought a warrant. I'd also brought Yara Hartwell's number, which I hadn't told anyone about, and which I'd typed into my phone under a contact name that wouldn't raise eyebrows if anyone glanced at my screen: "Dispatch."

I'm not proud of that. I'm also not sorry.

The manager was a man named Gus who looked like he'd been carved out of a barstool. He checked my warrant with the enthusiasm of a man checking a lottery ticket he already knew had lost, then led me down the corridor to Unit 214 with a rolling gate key and a running commentary on the facility's security ("cameras got wiped in the storm last March, insurance says new ones by summer, you know how it is").

The storm last March. I made a note. If the cameras had been wiped in March, then anything that had happened at Unit 214 since then had happened off the record. Convenient. The kind of convenient that costs money.

Gus rolled up the door.

The unit was ten by twenty, climate-controlled—Merrick had paid extra for climate control, which told me whatever was inside mattered to him—and mostly empty. A workbench along one wall. A tarp on the floor, folded with military precision. And on the workbench, arranged like a man's idea of a shrine:

Six accelerant cans. Empty, but with residue in the seams—I could smell it, that sweet chemical ghost that never quite leaves. The same accelerant, I'd have bet my pension, that the original fire investigators had found traces of and then explained away as "solvent storage consistent with foundry operations."

A box of latex gloves. A funnel. A copy of the foundry's floor plan, laminated, with the electrical room circled in red marker.

And a fireproof document box, unlocked, sitting at the back like it had been waiting for me.

Gus had the good sense to wait by the gate. I put on gloves—I had a box in my trunk, because I'd started carrying a box in my trunk around the time I started trusting my own department less than I trusted a taxi dispatcher—and opened it.

Inside: a manila envelope, thick with pages. And a cassette tape, in a case labeled in ballpoint pen, in handwriting I recognized from Dana Voss's lease application—the handwriting that apologized for existing.

*For Theo,* the label said. *If anything happens to me. He'll know what to do with it. He won't want to, but he'll know.*

I stood in that storage unit for a long time, holding a dead woman's voice in a plastic case, and I thought about the word *if.* Dana had written *if anything happens to me* the way you write a will—knowing it's a formality, hoping it stays one. Except Dana hadn't been hoping. Dana had known. You don't hide your real confession in a fireproof box in a storage unit leased by your murderer's shell company unless you understand, on some cellular level, that the man who owns the shell company intends for you to stop existing.

Wait. I had to stop and go back to that. The storage unit was leased by Kestrel Property Group. Merrick's shell. Dana had hidden her insurance policy inside the killer's own lockbox, because the safest place to hide something is inside the thing that's hunting you. She'd gambled that no one would ever look there—that the unit was Merrick's blind spot, his private archive of sins, and that a man who keeps trophies doesn't inventory them.

She'd been right for two years. She'd been wrong by one week.

I sat down on the workbench—evidence be damned, my knees had opinions—and read the pages. Dana's real confession. Not the one she'd given the police, the one that absolved Marcus Hale and closed the case and let a philanthropist be born. This one was dated three weeks before her death, and it was addressed, in its opening line, to "whoever finally asks."

*My name is Dana Voss and everything I told the police about the fire was a lie.*

I read it twice. Then a third time, because the third time is when the details stop being words and start being pictures. Dana described the night of the fire: the maintenance logs that had been altered, the inspection that had been scheduled and then quietly cancelled, the phone call she'd overheard between Hale and Merrick two days before the blaze—*Colin saying, "It'll be clean, Marcus. Ostrander's already on it. Nobody's going to look twice at a foundry."* She described being called into Hale's office a week after the funerals, and Merrick sliding a paper across the desk, and Hale saying, with tears in his eyes that she would later realize were the best performance she'd ever seen, "We need to protect the workers' families from a lawsuit that would leave them with nothing. This is mercy, Dana. You're doing a kindness."

And then the last page. The accusation, in Dana's own hand, plain as a headstone:

*Marcus Hale ordered the fire. Colin Merrick set it. Dale Ostrander buried the cause. And I looked at the paper they gave me, and I signed it, because I was twenty-four years old and they told me it was mercy.*

I photographed every page. I photographed the cassette. I photographed the cans, the gloves, the floor plan, the tarp. I photographed the unit number and the lease agreement Gus pulled for me, and I photographed the wiped cameras, because sometimes the absence of a record is a record.

Then I did the thing that the Ruiz of seven years ago would never have done, and the Ruiz of today did without hesitating: I called Yara Hartwell from the parking lot, and I told her everything.

She was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "The tape. You need to hear the tape before you log it. If it's what I think it is, you can't let it sit in an evidence room."

"I know."

"And Ruiz? If Merrick's ledger led you to that unit, Merrick knows the ledger exists. And Merrick knows what's in the unit."

"I know that too."

"Then you should also know," Yara said, in the flat, calm voice of a woman who reads emergency traffic for a living and has stopped being surprised by any of it, "that the unit's lease renewed eleven days ago. Somebody paid for another year on a storage unit they were about to empty. That's not housekeeping. That's a man keeping a room ready."

I looked back at Unit 214. At the tarp folded with military precision. At the workbench with room on it for six more cans.

A room kept ready.

I thanked her and hung up, and I sat in my car in the storage facility parking lot with a fireproof box on the passenger seat and the feeling—cold, certain, familiar from three case files I'd never been able to close—that I was no longer investigating a murder.

I was scheduled for one.
