## 14. The Anniversary

The invitation came in a cream envelope, heavy as a headstone, and I knew before I opened it what it would say. You always know, in this family of ours. Bad news arrives on good paper.

*Marcus Hale cordially invites you to commemorate the seventh anniversary of the Ashford Foundry fire. A memorial service will be held at the restored foundry, followed by the unveiling of the Workers' Memorial Garden. Press in attendance.*

Press in attendance. Three words doing the work of a paragraph.

I sat at my kitchen table with the envelope and my coffee going cold, and I did the arithmetic I've been doing for seven years: what it costs to say yes, what it costs to say no. The annuity lands on the first of every month like a small, polite bribe. Priya's son's coverage rides on hers. Mine bought me skin grafts and silence in equal measure, and I have never been able to tell you which one I needed more.

The restored foundry. That was the part that made me laugh, actually laugh, alone in my kitchen at seven in the morning like a man who'd finally tipped over. Seven years ago that building ate four people and half my body, and now Marcus Hale wants us to come back so the cameras can watch us grieve beautifully in a place he's had sandblasted and repointed until even the ghosts look renovated.

I should have thrown the invitation away. Instead I called Yara Hartwell, because apparently that's who I am now—a man who calls the dispatcher instead of the priest.

* * *

Yara's apartment smells like burnt coffee and old radio equipment, which is to say it smells like someone who listens for a living. There's a dispatch board on the wall—not the official one, a homemade one, index cards and colored pins tracking drivers and routes—and above it, taped up where anyone could see, a single page of handwriting that I recognized before Yara said a word about it. Dana's handwriting. I'd seen enough of her notes across the break room table to know the way she crossed her sevens.

"You got one too," I said, holding up my envelope.

"Mine came by courier." Yara didn't smile. Yara doesn't waste smiles on things that aren't funny. "Hand-delivered. Which means he wanted to know exactly when I received it."

"He invited you? You weren't even—" I stopped. Recalculated. "You weren't anything back then."

"I'm something now." Yara turned the envelope over in those quick dispatcher hands, the ones that move like they're always three calls ahead. "He dressed a dead woman in my clothes, Theo. I'm not attending his memorial as a guest. I'm attending as a message he already sent."

The journal page hung there between us, that single page Yara swears was left behind deliberately, the way you leave a trail of breadcrumbs when you want the bird to follow you somewhere specific. *C.M.* A second cause. I'd read it once and felt like I'd touched a live wire.

"So we don't go," I said. It came out faster than I meant it to, and Yara looked at me with that flat, patient attention that makes you hear your own cowardice in stereo.

"Theo."

"It's a trap." I stood up because sitting still felt like agreeing. "You think a man who kills a witness and dresses her in your clothes is inviting the press out of sentiment? He's staging something. He stages everything—the grief, the burn wards, the garden. He's going to stand in front of cameras in the building where four people died and tell the story where he comes out clean, and if any of us open our mouths, we're crazy grieving people ruining a memorial. Or worse."

"Or," Yara said quietly, "it's the first time in seven years he'll be standing in a room with all of you, on camera, with microphones, while you tell the truth."

"That's not how microphones work for people like us."

"No," Yara agreed. "That's how they work for people like him. Unless somebody changes the channel."

I paced the length of the apartment, three steps, turn, three steps. My leg aches in cold weather—nerve damage, they told me, the fire's parting gift—and it was aching now, which is stupid, because it was warm in there. Bodies keep their own calendars.

"Dana recanted," Yara said, when I'd worn a groove in the floorboards. "Two years ago. Privately. She told someone she lied in her confession, and then she went quiet, and then she died wearing my jacket. You told me yourself she said she lied to survive. So did you. So did Priya. So did Marisol and Ed." Yara tapped the table once, a dispatcher ending a call. "Hale thinks he's inviting an audience. He's actually inviting witnesses. The only question is whether we show up as audience or witnesses."

"And if we get it wrong?"

Yara didn't answer, which was its own answer.

* * *

We held the vote at Marisol's, because Marisol's kitchen has always been where this family does its hardest talking. Seven years of monthly dinners and the geometry never changes: Marisol at the stove because her hands need occupation, Ed in the corner chair because his lungs can't handle the walk from the door anymore—the smoke took a permanent deposit on them—Priya at the end of the table closest to the exit, which nobody comments on but everybody notices, and me wherever there's room, usually standing, because sitting still has never been my disease.

Marisol set out food nobody ate. That's tradition too.

"They restored the kiln room," Ed said. He'd seen the photos online, because Ed monitors everything Hale does the way other men check weather reports. "Poured new floors. Put up a plaque with the names. Four names, brass, right where the shift board used to hang." His voice did something complicated on *four*. "You go take a look at the comments. Half of them call Hale a saint. Man built a chapel out of our worst day and we're supposed to bring flowers."

"He did build the burn wing," Marisol said, not looking up from the pot she was stirring though nothing needed stirring. "Priya's son was treated there. My niece too, after her accident. Say what you want about why he did it—"

"I will," I said. "He did it to build a wall of gratitude tall enough that nobody asks questions. Every kid healed in that ward is another reason to call us ungrateful if we ever speak."

"That's not fair." Marisol finally turned around. She's a small woman with forearms like dock rope, and she was holding a wooden spoon like it might become relevant. "Some of that guilt is real. You don't know what it costs him—"

"I know exactly what it costs him, Mari. It costs him nothing. That's the whole design." I heard my own voice getting loud and couldn't stop it. Seven years of dinners where we passed the bread and swallowed the rest, and something in me had come loose since Dana turned up dead, some seam that wasn't going to resew itself. "Dana is dead. Does everyone understand that? Not moved away, not sick—dead, with her confession still sitting in a file cabinet like a headstone with the wrong name on it. And the man who owns that file just invited us to a party."

Nobody said anything. The spoon stopped moving.

Then Priya spoke, from her chair by the exit, in the voice she uses maybe twice a year, the quiet one that stops rooms. "I'll go."

Everyone looked at her. Priya doesn't volunteer for things. Priya's survival strategy for seven years has been beautiful, meticulous invisibility—she sits near exits, she keeps receipts, she never says a word against Hale within earshot of anyone outside this kitchen.

"Priya," Marisol started gently.

"My son asked me last week why we never talk about the fire." Her hands were folded on the table, perfectly still, which is how I knew it was costing her. "He's fourteen. He googled it. He read Dana Voss's confession—the official one—and he asked me if it was true. If it was really an electrical fault, if the alarms were really working." She looked at each of us in turn, and I have been burned before, actual fire, actual skin, and I have never felt heat like that look. "And I lied to my child's face. In my own kitchen. Because I'm paid to."

"Priya, if you speak publicly, the settlement's void. All of it. Arjun's coverage—"

"I know the terms, Theo. I wrote them out myself after the lawyers left. I've had seven years to memorize the price list." Something flickered across her face then, fast, gone—I'd learn later what it was, what she'd already seen and carried alone—but at the time I read it as fear, and fear was the currency we all traded in. "I'm not saying I'll give a speech. I'm saying I'll be in that room. And I think—" she hesitated, and here was the woman who plans exits, choosing her words like steps on ice— "I think we should hear what Yara has to say before we decide anything else."

* * *

So we heard it. Yara came to Marisol's the next evening with Ruiz's blessing and no badge, just that notebook and a calm that I've decided is either courage or a very well-managed lack of self-preservation.

Yara laid it out plain. The memorial was a stage, yes—but stages run both directions. Hale expected a controlled room: his eulogy, his plaque, his press, our silent bowed heads as set dressing. What he couldn't control, Yara argued, was sequencing. Who spoke, and when, and whether the microphones stayed live. Ruiz would be there. Drivers Yara trusted would be there, parked where they could see every exit. And the survivors—if any of us chose to speak—would do it knowing we weren't alone in the room for the first time in seven years.

"You keep using that word," Ed said. "*Choose.* Like it's free."

"Nothing's free," Yara said. "But you've been paying interest for seven years. This is a chance to pay principal."

Marisol cried a little, quietly, into a dish towel, the way she does everything—competently, without asking to be comforted. Ed stared at his hands. And I stood at the window watching the streetlight come on and thought about Dana Voss at the monthly dinner two years ago, the last one she attended, laughing at Ed's terrible jokes with her sleeve pulled down over her wrist like always, and none of us knowing—or all of us suspecting and none of us saying—that she was already carrying the weight that would eventually be dropped on her from a great height.

She'd told me once, drunk on Marisol's cheap wine, *Theo, you know what the difference is between us and Hale? He thinks the fire ended. We know it didn't. It's still burning. It's just underground now, like a coal seam.* I'd laughed and told her she was morbid. She'd smiled and said, *One day it'll come up through the floor. Question is whose house it's under.*

Her house, it turned out. First.

"All right," I said, turning around. Five faces looked at me—four survivors and one dispatcher who belonged at that table more than any of us deserved to admit. "Here's my vote. I'm done being a man who gets invited to his friends' graves and thanks the killer for the flowers. I'll go. And if the moment comes—" I looked at Priya, at Marisol, at Ed— "if the moment comes, I'll take it. Whatever it costs. I've been spending seven years buying safety with other people's deaths, and the exchange rate is obscene."

Marisol put down the spoon. "If I speak, they'll dig into the settlement. They'll say I was paid off, that anything I say is bought and bitter either way."

"Let them," Yara said. "Ruiz has the financial records. The payments cut both ways—they prove the silence was purchased, which proves there was something worth purchasing."

Ed laughed, a dry rustle of a sound. "The accountant's nightmare. Silence with a paper trail."

"Every empire keeps books," Yara said. "That's how you burn them down. Figuratively," Yara added, catching my expression. "Mostly figuratively."

It wasn't unanimous. Nothing in this family ever is. Marisol abstained for a long time, stirring nothing, and then said she'd attend but wouldn't promise her mouth, which from Marisol is practically a battle cry. Ed said he'd come and hold the door, literally, that his job was to stand between us and the exits and make sure nobody got rushed or cornered. Priya said nothing more at all, which frightened me more than any speech would have.

Before we broke up, Yara pinned a photocopy of Dana's journal page to Marisol's refrigerator, next to the grocery list and Arjun's school photo, right there among the ordinary magnets of an ordinary kitchen.

"C.M.," Ed read aloud. "Second cause." He looked around the table. "Colin Merrick."

"Hale's fixer," I said. "The man who delivered the settlement checks personally. Never mailed, always hand-delivered, always with a little speech about how grateful we should be." I'd watched Merrick's face across a hundred doorways. A man built entirely of patience and shoe leather. "He scouted the foundry last week. Yara's people saw him. Specifically the kiln room."

The kitchen went very quiet. Outside, a car went past, headlights sweeping the ceiling like a searchlight.

"Why the kiln room?" Marisol whispered.

"Because that's where the second ignition point was," Yara said. "According to the forensic consultant Hale's company paid off. According to Dana's real confession—the one that isn't in the file yet." Yara looked at each of us, and for the first time since I'd known this strange, relentless person, I saw something underneath the calm. Not fear exactly. Readiness. The look of a dispatcher who has finally routed every unit to the scene. "He's planning something for that room. Maybe a display. Maybe an accident. Either way, we'll be standing inside his plan when it happens."

"Then we change the plan," Priya said.

We all turned. She hadn't moved from her chair by the exit, but her chin was up, and her hands were unfolded, flat on the table like a woman setting down something heavy she'd carried too far.

"He expects four silent survivors and a grieving hero," she said. "So we give him the eulogy he rehearsed—right up until we don't."

* * *

I walked home the long way, past the foundry, because I'm a fool with scar tissue and I wanted to see it lit up. Scaffolding off, fresh mortar pale as scar skin, floodlights washing the brick the color of bone. A banner over the loading dock where they used to clock in: *SEVEN YEARS OF REMEMBRANCE — HONORING THE FALLEN OF ASHFORD FOUNDRY, A GIFT FROM THE HALE FAMILY FOUNDATION.*

A gift. From the man who locked the alarm panel in his office because the false alarms were annoying him. From the man whose night manager skipped the maintenance inspection because Marcus Hale wanted the order shipped on schedule. None of that is in the official record. All of it is in ours.

Four names on a brass plaque. Reyes. Okafor. Lindqvist. Gutierrez. I say them to myself sometimes, walking, like a rosary with no church attached. Big Manuel Reyes who sang boleros on the line. Sam Okafor who was saving for his daughter's tuition. Astrid Lindqvist, three weeks from retirement. Little Ray Gutierrez, nineteen years old, who'd been on the job eleven days.

Seven years ago I crawled out of that building on my elbows with my back on fire, and the last thing I saw before the medics covered my eyes was the loading dock light swinging on its cable, throwing shadows that looked like people running.

Now there's a banner there instead. And in six days, cameras, and Marcus Hale in a charcoal coat telling the world a story with himself in the role of the wounded.

Unless the story changes channels.

My phone buzzed. A text from Yara, no greeting, dispatcher to the bone:

*Kiln room checked again tonight. Fresh padlock. Bring your key memories Thursday—we're going to need every door in that building mapped. And Theo—when Priya stands up, whatever happens, don't let anyone pull her down. Not even her.*

I stood under the banner a long moment, reading the word *gift* until it stopped meaning anything, the way words do when you stare at a lie long enough.

Then I texted back: *Copy that. Nobody pulls anybody down. Thursday we map the doors. Sunday we open them.*
