## 16. Rehearsal for a Confession

Ruiz spread the foundry's floor plan across my kitchen table like a tarot reading she already knew was bad news.

"Main hall here," she said, tapping a rectangle the size of a basketball court. "Stage for the memorial there. Press seating—Hale's people are calling it 'the media riser,' which tells you everything about who this event is actually for. Restored kilns along the east wall, behind glass now, very tasteful, very museum."

"And the kiln room?"

"Basement. Original structure. They kept it because Hale likes to tell the story of where he 'pulled men from the fire.'" She looked up at me. "He didn't pull anyone from anything. But it photographs well."

I poured coffee into two mugs that had seen better decades and sat down across from her. Outside my window, the city did its gray morning business—a delivery truck double-parked, someone's dog losing an argument with a squirrel. Ordinary things. I'd learned to hold onto ordinary things lately, the way you grip a railing in rough water.

"Okay," I said. "Walk me through how we turn a memorial service into a confession booth."

* * *

The planning took three days, and if you'd watched us from outside—two women hunched over maps and printouts, arguing in low voices over cold takeout—you might have thought we were planning a heist. In a way, we were. The thing we meant to steal was seven years old and had been locked in Marcus Hale's pocket since the night four men died breathing smoke.

"Recorders first," Ruiz said on day two, lining up three devices on my table like soldiers at inspection. "Priya gets one in her jacket—the left inside pocket, wired to run ninety minutes. Theo takes a second one. And the third goes somewhere fixed, near the stage, because Priya and Theo are going to be nervous, and nervous people check their pockets."

"You've done this before," I said.

"I've imagined doing this before," Ruiz said. "There's a difference. Imagining doesn't get you fired." She turned one of the recorders over in her fingers. "Legally, Massachusetts is a two-party consent state. Everything we record without signatures is evidence of our optimism, not admissible proof."

"So why bother?"

"Because Hale won't know that. Because Merrick won't know that. Because a man who hears his own voice played back to him in a room full of witnesses will say almost anything to make the tape stop mattering." She set it down. "And because if everything goes sideways, the recordings still tell the truth to somebody. Eventually. That's not nothing."

I thought about Dana Voss, whose truth had eventually reached exactly no one while she was alive. "It's not nothing," I agreed.

The sequencing was my contribution. That surprised Ruiz, I think—she expected me to be good at voices on a radio and patterns in logs, and I am, but dispatch work is really just choreography. You learn that a crisis isn't solved by the loudest voice or the fastest responder. It's solved by whoever arranges events in the right order so that panic has nowhere to land.

"Hale controls rooms," I told her, drawing arrows on the floor plan. "That's his whole talent. He decides who speaks when, he interrupts with grief, he reframes. So we don't give him a room to control. We give him a script he thinks is his."

I laid out the order: opening remarks by the memorial committee—Hale's own people, harmless. Then a candle lighting, which would soften everyone, including the press. Then Theo, speaking about the dead as friends, no accusations, pure elegy—Hale would relax, maybe even tear up on cue. Then Priya, last, with the microphone already in her hand and nowhere for Hale to hide the moment.

"By the time Priya says Merrick's name," I said, "the program will be over. There'll be no 'next speaker' for Hale to invoke. The only move left to him is interruption, and interrupting a grieving woman mid-testimony in front of twelve cameras makes him the story in the worst way."

"And if he does it anyway?"

"Then he confirms everything by reflex." I capped my pen. "Men like him can't stand silence. It's the one room they can't decorate."

Ruiz studied the arrows for a long moment. "You've thought about getting fired a lot less than I have."

"I got fired from invisibility weeks ago," I said. "The benefits package was terrible anyway."

* * *

The taxi drivers were the part I loved most, and I'm not ashamed of it.

I called them one at a time from the dispatch board, using the coded phrasing we'd developed over years of night shifts—"special fare, long wait, cash consideration"—and one by one they said yes before I'd finished explaining. Marisol, who'd driven nights since before I hired on and who once talked a suicidal passenger out of the East River bridge by ordering him dinner. Deacon, ex-Army, unflappable, the man you send when the address scares you. The Okafor brothers, who communicated in a shorthand so efficient it was nearly telepathy. Old Sam, who claimed to be retired but showed up every time the board lit up ugly.

Six drivers. Six cars. Positioned around the restored foundry's perimeter like a rosary said over the building.

"You're treating this like an incident response," Ruiz observed, watching me mark positions on the map.

"I'm treating this like the only kind of operation I know how to run." I tapped the north lot. "Marisol here, engine running, sightline to the main doors. If anyone leaves that building in distress—or gets carried out—she's forty seconds closer than any patrol car. Deacon covers the loading dock, because that's how you move a body out of a venue without crossing the cameras. Sam floats."

"And what do I tell my lieutenant when he asks why six taxis are idling around a memorial service?"

"Tell him the truth. Nobody will believe it."

She laughed—short, reluctant, real. It occurred to me, watching her laugh at my table with coffee rings mapping our negotiations, that this was what love looked like for people like us. Not declarations. Logistics. Ruiz showing up at my door with floor plans instead of flowers. Me memorizing her brake pressure and her GPS pings the way other people memorize birthdays. We were two women who expressed devotion through contingency plans, and if that sounded cold to anyone else, they'd never waited for a radio to crackle with news of whether someone they—whether someone made it out.

"We need a signal," I said. "Between you and me, day-of. Something deniable."

"A phone call asking me to pick up dry cleaning," she said immediately. "If I answer 'the gray suit,' it means I see Merrick. If I say 'the gray suit needs pressing,' it means he's near you."

"You've thought about this."

"I've imagined it," she said again, but softer this time, and I understood she meant: *I've lain awake imagining the ways this ends badly, and I planned past all of them so I could keep standing next to you.* I handed her more coffee. Neither of us called it what it was. The logistics spoke fluently enough for both of us.

* * *

Thursday night, two days out, Marisol called the board on a hunch.

"He's there," she said, no greeting, which from Marisol meant hurry. "Gray coat, alone, walking the perimeter of the foundry like he owns the lease. Been circling twenty minutes. Now he's at the service entrance talking to a maintenance guy."

I pulled the foundry's site plan up on my second monitor. "Which entrance?"

"The Kiln Street side. He's pointing at the ground. Showing the guy something on his phone."

Kiln Street. My stomach performed a slow, professional drop. "Stay with him. Don't get close. Call me if he moves toward the basement access."

I patched Ruiz in on three-way. She listened to Marisol's running commentary for four minutes—Merrick walking the east wall, Merrick checking a door handle, Merrick standing too long at the grate that vented the old kiln room—then said, quietly, in the voice she used for crime scenes: "He's picking his exit. Or his stage."

"Both," I said. "Fire needs air. That grate is the kiln room's lung."

We sat with that for a moment, the three of us connected by radio waves over a sleeping city, listening to a man rehearse our deaths in advance.

"Options," Ruiz said finally. "One: we cancel. Two: we move the memorial. Three:"

"Three: we let him think the kiln room is his ace," I finished. "Because a man with an exit plan stops looking for new problems. And because on Saturday, that room is going to be the most surveilled fifty square meters in the state."

Silence on the line. Then Marisol, dry as August: "Dispatcher's got a mean streak. I like her."

"It's not a streak," I said. "It's choreography."

* * *

Friday evening, the survivors came to my apartment—not all of them, but enough. Theo arrived first, moving carefully the way burned bodies always move, even seven years healed, carrying a grocery bag of food nobody had asked for because Theo fed people the way other people flinched. Priya came late, hood up, son dropped at her sister's, eyes red-rimmed and steady. Two others came and left early; I didn't write down their names and Ruiz never asked. Courage rations itself.

We ran it like a table read. Theo stood in my living room and delivered his eulogy to my bookshelf, and halfway through he stopped, and none of us told him to keep going, because we all understood he needed the pause more than the practice.

"They were funny," he said finally, to the bookshelf, to the dead. "Raj used to do impressions of the foreman. Everybody laughed. Even the foreman." His hands trembled. "Seven years, and that's what I remember. An impression. A joke in a break room that doesn't exist anymore."

"That's what you'll say Saturday," I told him gently. "Exactly that. The joke. Let them laugh with you, and then let them go quiet, and then Priya speaks into the quiet."

Priya practiced once, sitting down, voice barely above the radiator's hum. "I saw Colin Merrick at the foundry that night. Before the fire. I have said this to no one for seven years because saying it cost more than I could pay." She looked up. "Is that true? What I'll lose?"

Ruiz answered before I could, and she didn't soften it, because Priya hadn't asked for softening. "Your settlement's voided the moment you speak. Your NDA's breached. Hale's lawyers will come after you within the week." She held Priya's gaze. "But there are pro bono attorneys already briefed, a fund being structured from frozen assets, and a prosecutor who's been waiting seven years for a living witness. You won't be alone in it. That's the whole offer. It's not safety. It's company."

Priya nodded slowly, like a woman signing something. "Company," she repeated. "After seven years of the opposite, that sounds like a fortune."

They left near midnight. Theo paused at my door, bag of leftovers pressed into my hands, and said, "You know what you're doing, dispatcher?" It wasn't quite a question. "You're giving us a rehearsal. Nobody ever gave us a rehearsal. Seven years ago they handed us scripts and a pen and pointed at the line that said *accident*, and that was the whole ceremony."

"This time you write your own lines," I said.

He smiled with half his face, the way fire survivors smile, and went down the stairs slow and careful, alive.

* * *

Saturday, 4:47 a.m. I hadn't slept. I stood at my window with coffee going cold and watched the dark do its last hour of business, and I reviewed the board in my head the way I did before every bad shift: recorders checked twice. Drivers positioned by dawn. Ruiz meeting the prosecutor at six. Priya's statement memorized and backed up on paper in three places. Theo steady. The press list confirmed—twelve outlets, two national. Hale expecting a coronation.

Everything sequenced. Every arrow drawn. Panic given nowhere to land.

My phone buzzed. Marisol.

"Dispatcher." Her voice was flat, which from Marisol was a five-alarm tone. "I stayed on the foundry overnight. Off the clock. Don't lecture me."

"Lecture pending. Talk."

"Forty minutes ago, a box truck pulls into the loading dock. No markings. Two guys unload drums—small ones, the five-gallon kind—and carry them down the basement ramp. Ten minutes later they leave, empty-handed." A breath. "And dispatcher? The maintenance guy from Thursday? He unlocked the kiln room door for them. Left it unlocked after."

I closed my eyes. Somewhere under that restored, tasteful, museum-glass building, accelerant was waiting in the dark beside the lungs of the old fire, and Colin Merrick believed he had already written Saturday's ending.

"Get clear of the building," I said. "Now. And Marisol—thank you."

"Yeah, yeah." A pause, and then, gentler: "See you at your position, dispatcher. Forty seconds closer than anybody."

I called Ruiz. She picked up on the first ring, awake, already dressed—I could hear it in her breathing, the readiness of her.

"I know," she said, before I could speak. "I've been watching the dock cam feed since five. The drums are down there. Which means he's committed, which means he's predictable, which means—" she exhaled, and I heard the wolfish calm settle over her, the one I'd only heard once before, on an icy grade with failed brakes, "—which means we don't change a single thing. We let him walk into his own rehearsal."

"And if the script flips?"

"Then six taxis, three recorders, and one stubborn dispatcher rewrite it live." A beat. "Yara. Whatever happens in that building today—when it's over, there's a conversation we're going to finish. Non-logistically."

Despite everything—despite the drums and the unlocked door and the man downstairs from history's worst intentions—I smiled at the dark window.

"Copy that, Detective," I said. "Scheduled after close of business."

Outside, the sky was turning the color of a fresh page.
