## 7. What the Fire Kept

The dream came back on a Tuesday, which was wrong, because it always came on Sundays.

Emrys Iversen had kept a log of it for eleven years—not out of sentiment but out of professional habit. Recurring dreams were data. She'd read somewhere that the brain filed unresolved material at night, and she'd wanted to know what her own mind considered unresolved. So there was a notebook in her bottom desk drawer, spine cracked, entries in her small even hand: *Sunday. Same dream. No variation.* Forty-one entries over eleven years, every one dated a Sunday, until this one arrived on a Tuesday and sat on her desk like a document with a forged signature.

She knew what was in it before sleep had fully released her. She could have recited it the way she recited insurance policies—verbatim, complete, indifferent to whether anyone wanted to hear it. That was the trouble with a photographic memory. It didn't distinguish between the things you chose to keep and the things that kept you.

*The boathouse is burning.*

She sat up in bed. Grey light through the blinds, gulls somewhere over the harbor, the radiator ticking. Ordinary morning sounds. But the dream was still open behind her eyes like a file someone had left on her desk overnight, and for the first time in eleven years she didn't close it. She let it run.

*The boathouse is burning, and the fire is orange at the top and blue where it meets the water, and the heat has a sound, a low animal hum, and I am very small. Small enough that the dock boards are wide as doors under my bare feet. There is a woman singing. Not screaming—singing. A song with no words I know, rising above the fire's hum, steady as a tide chart. The singing is the most frightening part. Grown-ups scream at fires. This woman sings to one.*

Emrys got up. Made coffee she didn't drink. Stood at the window with the mug going cold in her hands while the dream unspooled further than it ever had in sleep, because in sleep it always cut off at the same frame—the hand—and now, awake, with Harrowgate's ash still under her fingernails from three days in Greyhollow, the frame held.

*A hand comes out of the black water. Not reaching for me. Reaching past me. And then it finds me anyway, closes around my wrist, and pulls, and the water takes me the way fire was taking the boathouse—completely, without negotiation. The last thing before the cold is the singing, and the singing stops mid-note. Not fading. Stopping. The way a door stops a voice.*

She set the mug down. Her hands were steady. Her hands were always steady; that was the joke everyone at the firm made, that Emrys Iversen's pulse probably ran in a straight line. But steadiness of hand was not steadiness of anything else, and standing in her kitchen at six in the morning she felt something she hadn't felt since she was a child being handed between caseworkers: vertigo. Not fear of falling. Fear of discovering there had never been any floor.

Because here was the thing she had never written in the notebook, never said aloud, never let herself think in full sentences: the dream had details. Dreams were supposed to be fog. Hers had grain. The specific rust-orange of rusted iron cleats on the dock. The way the burning tar smell mixed with salt. The exact pitch of the singing—a low alto, unhurried, the second phrase a minor third below the first. She could hum it. She had never heard the song anywhere else in thirty-one years, and she could hum it note-perfect.

Dreams didn't come with perfect pitch. Memories did.

* * *

She went to the office early, before anyone who might ask questions, and pulled her own file.

That sounded simpler than it was. Emrys Iversen had no childhood records in any ordinary sense. There was a folder at the state archive—she'd requested it once, at twenty-two, and received forty-one pages, most of them boilerplate. Found child, female, approx. age four, recovered near Greyhollow, no identification. Placed in temporary care. Transferred. Transferred again. Adopted at seven by the Iversens, a accountant and a schoolteacher two hundred miles inland who had wanted a quiet child and gotten one. The file's only photograph was a intake snapshot: a small girl with wet hair combed flat, staring into the camera with an expression the caseworker had described, in the file's single moment of humanity, as *"watchful beyond her years."*

Approximate age four. Recovered near Greyhollow.

She had read those words at twenty-two and felt nothing she could name, which she'd later understood was itself a feeling—the particular numbness of a lock meeting the wrong key. Greyhollow had been a word on a form. Now it was a town with a burned estate and a fenced memorial and an archivist named Pell who quoted coroner's reports that had never been made public. Now it was the place where Marek Voss had drowned without dying.

Now it was the place where, twenty-seven years ago, someone had pulled a four-year-old girl out of the water the same week a fire took the Harrowgate boathouse.

She had not let herself write that sentence until this morning. She wrote it now, in the dream-log notebook, under the Tuesday date, and looked at it for a long time. Then she turned back forty-one entries and began, entry by entry, doing the thing she did better than anyone alive: reading for contradictions.

There weren't any. That was the horror of it. Eleven years of Sundays, and the dream never varied—no detail added, none lost, none softened the way real dreams soften. Real dreams decay. Hers had the fidelity of a recording. Which meant one of two things: either her mind was a machine of impossible precision even asleep, or the dream wasn't a dream at all. It was playback. A memory stored whole and replayed on a schedule, Sunday after Sunday, like a file set to auto-open, waiting for her to finally read it.

Why Sundays? She almost smiled. Even her subconscious had administrative habits.

* * *

The song was the thread she followed first, because songs could be checked.

She hummed it into her phone on the walk to work, ran it through every recognition app she trusted, got nothing. She described it to a musicologist she'd once used on a fraud case involving a forged Stradivarius provenance, and the woman called back within the hour, intrigued. "It's not a standard hymn or folk tune in any catalogued tradition," she said. "But the structure—it's a lullaby form. Call and response, but the response line is missing. Whoever sang it was improvising the second half, or teaching it, or—" a pause—"or making it up to soothe someone. Lullabies get invented all the time. They just don't usually survive."

Survived. Twenty-seven years in the head of a woman who couldn't forget anything. Emrys thanked her and hung up and stood in the stairwell of her office building with her forehead against the cool concrete, humming four notes to herself like a madwoman, like a child.

A housekeeper would sing to a child. The thought arrived without permission. A housekeeper on a great coastal estate, fired the week before a fire, would have— 

No. She stopped it there. That was chapter thirteen of a story she hadn't earned yet, and she was a professional, and professionals followed evidence in order. The evidence said: a girl of about four was pulled from the water near Greyhollow by an unknown hand, on or around the night the Harrowgate boathouse burned. The evidence said: that girl remembered the fire with photographic fidelity and had spent her adult life filing it under *dreams* because filing things was how she survived everything else.

The evidence also said something she liked less. The hand in the dream reached past her first. Reaching past her—for whom? For another child? For the singing woman? And the singing stopped mid-note when the water closed over them both, which meant the singer had still been in the boathouse, or on the dock, or in the water, when Emrys was carried away. The dream ended where her knowledge ended. Everything after the cold water was blank—not forgotten, just never recorded. She had been hauled out of one catastrophe and into a procession of caseworkers' cars, and whatever happened to the singer, to the reaching hand, to whoever else had been pulled or not pulled from that water, had happened in the dark on the far side of her memory's edge.

She had built a whole life in that blank space. That was the vertigo talking now. Every year of the Iversens' tidy inland household, every report card, every closed case at the firm—all of it constructed on a foundation she'd never inspected, and the inspection was starting, and the building creaked.

* * *

Pell answered on the second ring, though it was barely eight.

"The archivist," he said, in the tone of a man who had expected the call and dreaded being right. "I wondered when the letters would catch up to you properly."

"They've caught up." She kept her voice level, professional, a voice for taking statements. "I have a question that isn't about the fire."

"A question about the water, then." He said it too quickly, and she marked it. Pell knew something about the water.

"There was a child recovered near Greyhollow the week of the fire. Female, approximately four. No identification. Does the name mean anything to you?"

Silence long enough that she checked the call hadn't dropped. When he spoke again his voice had changed register—lower, careful, the voice of a man choosing each plank before stepping on it. "Where did you get that?"

"State archive. Intake record."

"And why," Pell said, "does an insurance investigator from two hundred miles away know her own intake record?"

The stairwell was very quiet. Somewhere below, a door opened and shut. Emrys discovered she was gripping the railing with her free hand, hard enough to whiten the knuckles, and made herself let go.

"You're assuming things," she said.

"I'm assuming nothing. I'm remembering." A breath. "There were two children that night. Everyone in this town old enough to remember remembers two. One was accounted for. One wasn't—or was, depending on which version of the paperwork you believe, and the paperwork in this town believes whatever the Ashcombes pay it to believe." Another breath, longer. "You asked if the name means anything. Names don't. Faces do. Come back to Greyhollow, Miss Iversen, and bring a photograph of yourself at four years old, if such a thing exists."

"It exists. It's in the file. Wet hair, flat expression."

"I don't need the description," Pell said quietly. "I need to see whether I recognize you. Because I have spent twenty-seven years wondering what happened to the second Voss girl, and I am beginning to suspect I'm speaking to her."

The line didn't go dead so much as Emrys stopped hearing it. *Voss.* The name landed in her chest like a coin dropped down a well—she tracked its falling through every layer of herself, waiting for the bottom, hearing nothing hit.

Marek Voss. The witness everyone swore was dead. Drowned weeks after the fire, body never recovered, alive at a shuttered lighthouse feeding gulls. And now an archivist saying *the second Voss girl* in a voice worn smooth from turning the words over for decades.

"That's not possible," she said, and heard how young she sounded, and hated it.

"No," Pell agreed. "Probably not. Probably you're an orphan from nowhere whose recurring nightmare happens to match a fire I've thought about every day for half my life, down to the tide and the dock and the singing. Probably." He let the word sit. "Come back to Greyhollow. Bring the photograph. And Miss Iversen—don't tell the Ashcombes you're coming. Last time someone started asking about the second girl, he disappeared into the sea, officially, and into a lighthouse, actually. I'd rather that didn't happen to you."

He hung up. Emrys stood in the stairwell holding a phone that had gone warm in her hand, and above her, through the high window, the gulls wheeled over the harbor crying their one syllable over and over, and she understood suddenly why the sound had always unsettled her—why, since childhood, since before she could name the reason, the cry of gulls had made her look over her shoulder for a fire that wasn't there yet.

She opened the dream-log notebook to a fresh page. At the top, in her small even hand, she wrote the date—a Tuesday—and beneath it, for the first time in eleven years, she did not write *same dream.*

She wrote: *Not a dream. Evidence.*

And beneath that, because her hand seemed to know before she did, she wrote a second line, smaller, the handwriting less even:

*Marek Voss saw who set the fire. Marek Voss may have seen who I was.*

She closed the notebook, booked a train to Greyhollow for the evening, and went upstairs to her desk to face the day's ordinary fraud cases with the particular calm of a woman whose own file had just become the largest one on her desk.
