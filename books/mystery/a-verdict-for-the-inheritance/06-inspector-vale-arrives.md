## 6. Inspector Vale Arrives

The inspector arrived on a Tuesday, which felt fitting. Tuesdays were the day I filed the cathedral's correspondence, and there was something about the bureaucratic rhythm of it that suited a woman who would spend the next week dismantling my life with a notebook and a pair of unimpressed eyes.

I saw her before she saw me—or rather, I saw her car, a county-plate sedan that parked at the lychgate with the precise, slightly aggressive geometry of someone who had never once worried about scratching a bumper. She got out, stood in the drizzle without opening her umbrella, and looked up at the cathedral spire the way a surgeon looks at an X-ray: not admiring it, exactly. Reading it.

Inspector Vale. I knew the name from the county constabulary's occasional letters, the ones requesting archived records for cold cases. Her signature was small and slanted forward, like handwriting in a hurry to be somewhere else. I had filed three of her requests over the years. She had never once requested anything from Marrowfield.

Until now.

I watched from the archive window as she shook rain from her coat, produced a warrant folder from inside it—dry, of course; she'd have kept it against her body—and walked toward the deanery with the unhurried gait of a person who believed that everything worth finding was already waiting for her, patient as dust.

My first instinct was the old one. The good one. The one that had kept me employed and unnoticed for thirty-one years: *become furniture*. Archivists are furniture. We are shelving with a pulse. No investigator in history has ever looked twice at a man whose entire professional identity is organized around not being looked at.

But the coffin was empty. And empty coffins do not permit furniture.

* * *

She came to the archive at half past ten, escorted by Dean Aldercott, who wore the expression of a man introducing a bailiff to his own sitting room.

"Mr. Blackwood," the dean said, "Inspector Vale, county criminal division. She'll need access to... well. To everything, I'm afraid."

"Everything is rather the point of an archive," I said. "Welcome."

Vale did not smile. She was perhaps forty-five, grey at the temples in a way that suggested either age or a great deal of thinking done under poor lighting, and she carried no briefcase—only a slim notebook and, tucked into her coat pocket, a pencil. A pencil. Everyone else in the world of official inquiry had gone to recorders and phones years ago. She wrote in pencil because pencil can be erased, or because pencil does not fail in the rain, or because she liked knowing things that could not be subpoenaed out of a device. I found myself already constructing theories about her pencil, which was, I realized, precisely what she wanted. A suspect who theorizes about your stationery is a suspect who has stopped watching his own hands.

"Mr. Blackwood." She said my name once, tasting it. "You found the seal."

"I catalogued it. The sexton found it."

"The sexton opened the coffin. You were standing closest when he lifted the lid." It was not a question, and it was accurate, which annoyed me more than it should have. "Describe it for me. In your own words. Not the report's words—your own."

"In my own words," I said, "it was a message. Wax seals are correspondence, Inspector. Nobody seals a document they intend to keep private forever. Somebody put that seal in that coffin expecting it to be read."

Something flickered behind her eyes—not surprise, but recalibration. She had expected me to say *curious* or *disturbing* or some other word chosen for its harmlessness. Instead I had said *message*, and now she had to decide whether I was clever or careless.

"And who," she asked, "would you send a message like that to?"

"Whoever exhumed him. Which is to say—all of us."

She wrote nothing. That was its own kind of answer. People write down what they want to quote later; she was still deciding whether anything I said would hold up in quotation marks.

"I'll need the archive's full holdings for 1983 through 1990," she said. "Founding family correspondence, mill records, burial registers, and any private papers relating to Aldous Verity."

"You'll need a ladder and a strong back as well. The Verity papers alone run to eleven crates."

"I've carried heavier."

"I doubt it," I said, and immediately regretted how much of myself that sentence gave away. She noticed. Of course she noticed. She wrote down *doubt it*, and I understood that I had just been quoted after all.

* * *

Here is what Inspector Vale did not know, and what I spent the afternoon deciding whether to tell her:

Three times in my life, I have solved a murder. Not assisted. Solved. The first was a fall from the bell tower that was actually a push—the dead man's boot heels were scuffed backward, and a man falling voluntarily does not scrape stone climbing away from his own death. The second was a drowning in the millrace, a woman who could swim, found with pond water in lungs that should have held river water. The third was a heart attack in a locked vestry, except the man's own doctor swore his heart was sound, and the tea things held the faint bitterness of foxglove.

I documented all three. Quietly. Thoroughly. In files kept where only I could find them, because every time I brought my findings to the authorities, the findings were received the way one receives a bill: paid late, resented, and eventually forgotten. The deaths were ruled accidents. The families involved were founding families. The town preferred its accidents, and I learned—I genuinely learned—that truth without power is just gossip with evidence.

So I became invisible instead. It seemed like wisdom. For thirty years I called it wisdom.

And here was the thing that made my hands unsteady as I shelved the morning's returns: none of those three deaths appeared anywhere in Inspector Vale's file. I knew this because I had checked, years ago, in a fit of vanity I am still not proud of. My name appears in no report, no deposition, no margin note. The man who solved three murders officially solved none of them. To Inspector Vale, arriving with her warrant and her pencil, Edmund Blackwood was a blank page.

And blank pages, in my experience, make investigators nervous. They start asking why the page is blank. They start wondering who held the pen.

* * *

The interlude that follows I reconstructed later—from her own testimony, from the dean's account, and from a single page of her notebook that surfaced, much later, in a place I will describe when the time comes. I include it here because a confession requires it. This is what she thought of me, in those first hours, while I was deciding whether to lie to her:

*Subject: Blackwood, E., archivist, cathedral of St. Ambrose, Marrowfield. Age approx. 60. Unmarried. No dependents. Employment continuous 31 yrs.*

*Note: ran the name through county records. Nothing. Three deaths in this parish in the relevant window—Tolliver (fall, '85), Marsh (drowning, '87), Crane (cardiac, '89)—all ruled accidental, all closed without inquest, and the archivist's name appears nowhere. Not as witness. Not as correspondent. A man who works twenty feet from the burial registers of three suspicious deaths and never once puts himself on paper.*

*Either he sees nothing—which no archivist alive manages—or he sees everything and writes none of it down.*

*Second note: he described the seal as "a message" within four seconds of being asked. He's been thinking about who sent it longer than he admits.*

*Third note: watch the hands. He catalogs people the way he catalogs paper. I've seen it twice today—he filed the dean, then filed me. Question is what drawer he keeps himself in.*

She was right about all of it. That is the worst part. I have spent this whole account insisting on my own cleverness, so let the record show that within six hours, a stranger with a pencil had read me more accurately than my own town had managed in thirty years.

* * *

She returned at four o'clock, alone, and found me where she knew she would find me, because I am a predictable piece of furniture.

"Mr. Blackwood. Do you know a family called Harrow?"

"Councilman Harrow chairs the bicentennial committee. His grandfather signed the mill insurance documents of 1984."

"I didn't ask what his grandfather signed. I asked if you know him."

"Everyone in Marrowfield knows everyone. It's the town's principal industry."

She almost smiled. Almost. It was the kind of almost-smile that costs the smiler something, and I understood she was showing it to me deliberately—a coin spent to buy rapport—and that she knew I knew, and that we were both going to keep pretending otherwise because pretending is cheaper than honesty in this town and always has been.

"Here's my problem," she said, sitting down uninvited on the edge of my worktable, which no one had done in thirty-one years, and looking at the ledger open before me upside down, which she read without difficulty, which told me something else about her. "An empty coffin is either vandalism, a clerical error two centuries deep, or a message. You think it's a message. Fine. Messages have senders. Senders have reasons. So I go looking through the parish records for anyone connected to Verity who might have a reason, and do you know what I find?"

"Silence," I said.

"Suspiciously tidy silence. Six workers died in a fire at the Verity mill in '84, and the parish register lists them, and then—" she tapped the ledger, "—the next entry is in different ink. Same year. Different ink, Mr. Blackwood. Someone went back into this book. Someone with access."

The room was very quiet. Rain ticked against the archive window. Somewhere above us, the bells practiced for the bicentennial, practicing their one reliable skill, which was announcing things to people who already knew them.

"Archives are open to researchers," I said.

"Not at midnight." She let that sit. "The lock on the crypt door has fresh scratches. The Verity crates were re-shelved out of order—crates seven through nine, reversed sequence, and only a man who knows the collection would notice, and only a man who works alone would risk it. And the sexton tells me you asked him last week whether the coffin lead was original or a Victorian replacement. Before the exhumation was even announced."

I had, in fact, asked him that. I had forgotten asking. This is the occupational hazard of a memory built for other people's secrets: your own questions get misfiled.

"So here is my question," Vale said, and took out her pencil, and did not write anything, which meant the question mattered. "Thirty-one years in this building. Three convenient deaths. A tampered register. An empty coffin. And a man whose name is on none of it. Are you the archivist who notices everything and says nothing? Or are you the reason there's nothing to say?"

And there it was. The door I had spent three decades keeping shut, held open by a woman with a pencil, and beyond it the choice I had been avoiding since Tuesday morning: speak, and become visible, and become vulnerable, and possibly become useful; or lie, and stay safe, and stay small, and stay exactly the man who watched three murderers walk home in the rain.

I thought about Ruth Verity. I did not yet know her name—that would come later—but I thought about whoever had sealed that wax and dropped it into the dark and waited forty years for someone to lift a lid. Someone had spoken, once, in the only language available to the powerless. Someone had made a message and trusted it to time.

And I thought about the simplest, oldest truth of my profession: archives exist because somebody, someday, will come asking. Every record is a letter to the future. I had simply never expected the future to arrive wearing a county badge and reading my ledgers upside down.

"I'm the archivist who notices everything," I said. "It's the job description."

"And the saying nothing?"

"That's the pension plan."

She studied me for a long moment. Then she closed her notebook—not in defeat; in filing. I could see her filing me, and I could not see the drawer.

"For now," she said, "you're a witness, Mr. Blackwood. Witnesses who volunteer observations are more useful than witnesses who require extraction. Consider volunteering something. Anything. Before I have to start treating tidiness as evidence."

She left. Her footsteps on the stone stairs were unhurried, patient, certain. Everything worth finding was already waiting for her.

I sat for a long while in the lamplight, listening to the bells, and then I did the thing I am least proud of in this entire account, prouder men would refuse to confess it, but this account is a verdict and verdicts require the whole of the evidence:

I lied to her.

Not loudly. Not dramatically. I simply did not mention the three files. When she came back the next morning and asked, casually, whether I had ever noticed anything irregular in the parish deaths of the eighties, I said, "Irregular is my native climate, Inspector. You'll have to be specific." And she named no names, because she was testing whether I'd offer them, and I offered nothing, and we both smiled our expensive smiles, and the moment passed.

I told myself it was strategy. That the files were my leverage, my protection, my one card, and one does not lay one's card on the table because a stranger admired one's shelving. I told myself that invisibility was still armor.

But that night, locking the archive, I caught my reflection in the dark window—grey man, grey cardigan, grey life—and for the first time in thirty-one years the reflection looked less like armor and more like a cage, and I recognized the shape of it, because I had built it myself, plank by plank, verdict by undelivered verdict.

Somebody had left a message in a coffin for me. And I had just lied to the one person sent to help read it.

The glassblower's shop on Tanner's Lane opened at nine. I intended to be there at five past.
