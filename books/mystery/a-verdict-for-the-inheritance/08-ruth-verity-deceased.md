## 8. Ruth Verity, Deceased

There is a particular cruelty in the way a town records its dead. Not malice, exactly—Marrowfield has never been malicious so much as *tidy*, which is worse. Malice at least leaves fingerprints. Tidiness leaves nothing but a line in a ledger and the assumption that the line was written by someone who cared.

I spent the morning with the parish death registers, and I will confess that by the third hour I had developed a low-grade headache and a high-grade suspicion. The registers are my oldest companions in this archive. I know their smells—the 1840s volume smells of pipe smoke and mildew, the 1860s of candle wax, the 1880s of something I have never been able to identify and have privately named "regret." I have read them the way other men read scripture: devotionally, repeatedly, looking for what isn't there.

What wasn't there, on the fourteenth of October in the year of the mill fire, was any entry at all.

The fire itself is recorded, of course. Six names, entered in a careful clerical hand, each followed by the notation *perished in the Verity mill conflagration*. Six workers. The town mourned them for exactly as long as it takes to carve six names into a millstone and then stop talking about it.

But Ruth Verity's name appears nowhere in that month. Nor the next. Nor anywhere in the register until—

Here. Page 211. Eleven months after the fire, entered without ceremony:

*Ruth Verity, daughter of Aldous Verity, founder. Aged nineteen years. Cause: fever. Witness to interment: none.*

No witness. No attending physician. No burial plot number. In a register where the parish clerk had dutifully recorded, three lines above, that a farmwife named Prudence Ottley died of "a surfeit of plums" with two witnesses and a doctor's signature, Ruth Verity—a founder's daughter, a girl whose funeral should have shut down half the town—was buried by nobody, seen by nobody, mourned by nobody.

Dead at nineteen, on paper. Alive, if an old glassblower's memory could be trusted, long enough to leave town with a child.

I sat back in my chair and let that arithmetic settle over me like cold water. A girl declared dead eleven months after her father was declared dead. Two deaths, both convenient, both unwitnessed, both filed away by the same tidy hand that had cut pages out of the founding ledgers.

You begin to see the shape of it. Or rather, you begin to see the shape of the *absence* of it, which is how Marrowfield does its murder work. This town has never once committed a crime in the open. It commits crimes in the margins, in the missing pages, in the entries that were never made. It kills people with stationery.

* * *

Mrs. Halloway—the glassblower, though her hands no longer held the pipe—received me in the back room of her shop on Cordwainer Lane, surrounded by forty years of dust and the ghosts of beautiful things. Her kiln was cold. Her shelves were not empty but nearly, holding only a few pieces she'd apparently kept for herself: a blue vase with a flaw running through it like a scar, a paperweight with a bubble suspended in its heart like a trapped breath.

"You're the archivist," she said. Not a question. "Edmund Blackwood. The one who finds things."

"I find documents," I said. "Whether they're true is another matter."

She snorted, which I took as agreement. "Sit, then. My legs are done for the day and my opinions aren't." She lowered herself into a chair by the cold kiln with the groan of a woman who had earned every syllable of it. "You asked about the seal."

"The wax. You said your grandmother supplied it."

"My grandmother supplied everything to that man." Something moved across her face—not fondness, not quite anger. Recognition, maybe. The look you give a ghost you'd stopped expecting to see. "Aldous Verity came into this shop every month for twenty years. Seals for his letters, seals for his ledger boxes, seals for—" She paused. "Seals for things he didn't want opened by anyone but himself. He'd bring the matrix himself, that signet ring of his, and he'd watch my grandmother pour the wax like a hawk watches a field mouse. Never let her handle the ring. Never told her what went under the seals."

"And the word 'witness'?"

"That stamp was separate. He had it made special, maybe ten years before the end. Came in one day with a second matrix, smaller, and had my grandmother cast it in iron because brass wears. He never explained it. Men like him don't explain; they instruct." She folded her hands. "But that's not why you came back, is it. You came back about the woman."

I hadn't mentioned a woman yet. I kept my face still, which is the one skill forty years in an archive teaches you better than any other. "What woman?"

"The quiet one." Mrs. Halloway looked at the cold kiln as if reading something in its brickwork. "After the fire. After they put Aldous in the ground—or said they did—there was a woman started coming round. Bought candles, mostly. Church candles, which we didn't sell, but my grandmother kept a stock just for her. Dark hair, always covered. Young, but carried herself old, like the weight was already fitted to her shoulders and she'd stopped trying to shrug it off. She never gave a surname. Just said 'Ruth.'"

"When?"

"Spring after the fire. Then again in autumn. Then once more, the following year—and that last time she had a baby with her. Wrapped up tight against the cold, little fist poking out of the blanket like it was shaking that whole town's hand in defiance." The old woman's mouth twitched. "My grandmother held her. Said she was the warmest thing she'd touched in a decade."

"Where did Ruth go?"

"She didn't say. They never do, the ones who leave." Mrs. Halloway turned her eyes on me, and they were sharper than her hands. "But I'll tell you what my grandmother told *me*, when I was young enough to still ask questions. She said that girl looked over her shoulder the whole time she was in this shop. Not nervous-like. *Certain*-like. Like she knew exactly what was behind her and was checking whether it had found her yet. And when she left the last time, she left through the back door onto Tanner's Row instead of the front, and my grandmother watched from the window as she walked past the mill ruins without so much as turning her head toward them."

"Why not turn her head?"

"Because," Mrs. Halloway said simply, "you don't look at the thing that killed everyone you loved. Not if you mean to stay alive."

The shop was very quiet then. Dust drifted in the light from the single window, unhurried, indifferent—the way dust has been drifting since before Aldous Verity ever drew breath, and would go on drifting after the last of us was filed, unwitnessed, into some register or other.

"Mrs. Halloway," I said carefully, "the parish record says Ruth Verity died of fever eleven months after the fire."

"I know what the record says."

"It says she was buried with no witnesses."

"I know that too." She leaned forward, and her voice dropped into something almost gentle, which frightened me more than any threat had all week. "Young man, my grandmother buried three husbands and two children, and she signed her name as witness on every grave in this town that would have her. She remembered that girl sixty years later down to the chipped tooth and the way she counted her coins twice. Whatever they wrote in your books—" she tapped the air toward my satchel, toward the whole paper skeleton of Marrowfield's official memory— "that girl walked out of here alive. And somebody in this town decided she'd be easier to mourn than to find."

* * *

I did not go straight back to the archive. I walked. It was evening by then, and Marrowfield in the evening is a town performing innocence: lamplighters making their rounds, the cathedral bells counting out the hour over rooftops that have heard them count for two hundred years, smoke rising from chimneys in domestic little columns that say *nothing is wrong here, nothing has ever been wrong here.*

I walked past the mill ruins. Everyone walks past the mill ruins; they sit at the bottom of Founders' Hill like a pulled tooth the town refuses to have replaced. The stone is black still, after all these decades. Six names carved into the gatepost, weathered soft. I stood there longer than I meant to.

Nineteen years old. I tried to hold that number properly, the way you're supposed to hold numbers about the dead, and found I couldn't. Nineteen is a season, not an age—it's the year you still believe the adult world will make sense if you just learn its rules. Ruth Verity had learned its rules instead. The rule being: if the founding families decide you are dead, you are dead, and the living thing to do is agree with them, take your child, and walk past the ruins without turning your head.

Eleven months. Someone had waited eleven months after the fire before filing her death—long enough for grief to fade into paperwork, short enough that no one would ask questions. And whoever filed it had known the clerk's habits, known that a fever death needed no inquest, known that a founder's daughter with no mother and a father freshly in the ground had no one left to demand witnesses.

It was family. It had to be. Only family could erase her that cleanly, and only family would need to.

I thought about what that meant. It meant that somewhere in Marrowfield's founding houses—Harrow, Crane, Pell, Ashcombe, the rest of the gilded roster that would be seating themselves at the anniversary dinner in a few days' time—someone had sat down with a pen eleven months after burning six people alive, and had written *fever* beside a living girl's name, and had slept afterward. Had slept *well*, probably. Tidy men sleep well.

And it meant she had run. Forty years. A lifetime spent being dead—changing towns, changing names, teaching a child to be afraid of his own surname. I thought of the baby's fist poking out of the blanket, shaking its hand at the world, and wondered what that hand looked like now. Whether it had learned to write a false name smoothly. Whether it flinched at church registers.

Here is the thing I understood standing in front of those blackened stones, and I understood it with the peculiar clarity of a man who has just realized his life's work has been rearranging furniture in a burning house: I had spent forty years solving small deaths because small deaths were solvable. Three accidents, three careful files, three verdicts delivered to no one. I had called it humility. I had called it knowing my place. But Mrs. Halloway's grandmother had watched a murdered girl walk past these ruins without looking, and I had catalogued six names in a fire ledger without once asking who lit the match, and we were both doing the same thing—looking away from the thing that killed everyone, because looking is dangerous and not-looking is survivable.

Invisibility, I had told myself for four decades, was wisdom.

Standing there, I finally admitted what it actually was. It was the same trick the founding families used, scaled down to one man. They erased Ruth with a ledger line; I erased myself with modesty. We were all of us curators of our own disappearances.

Well. Enough.

Ruth Verity was declared dead in this town's books, and dead women cannot testify, which was precisely the point of declaring her. So the task was plain, and it was not archival, and it was not safe: find the living woman underneath the dead one. Find where a frightened girl with a baby and a chipped tooth would run, when running from the five richest families in the county.

Not alone, though. Not this time. There was a photograph, if Mrs. Halloway's memory served—and grandmothers' memories, in my experience, serve better than parish registers. A woman, a child, a moment caught in silver before the erasure began. A child grows into a face. Faces can be traced. Even across forty years. Even across a false name.

I turned back toward the archive, walking faster now, my headache gone, replaced by something hotter and considerably less comfortable.

Somewhere out there was a boy who had shaken his fist at Marrowfield before he could walk. I intended to find out what he'd grown into—and I intended to find him before the anniversary dinner, before Harrow sealed that crypt lid, before the town finished tidying away the last loose page of Aldous Verity's story.

Starting, tomorrow morning, with whatever photographs an old glassblower had kept in a dusty shop on Cordwainer Lane.
