## 16. The Anniversary Guest List

There is a particular kind of document that tells you more about a town than any ledger of births or deaths ever could: the seating chart.

A census tells you who lives in Marrowfield. A seating chart tells you who matters, and in what order, and precisely how many chairs of humiliation have been set aside for everyone else. I had spent thirty years among documents, and I will tell you plainly that the founding families' anniversary dinner chart was the most honest piece of paper the town had produced in two centuries.

I did not steal it. I want that on the record, wherever records still mean anything. I *copied* it, which in archivist terms is a sacrament, not a crime.

* * *

The chart lived, as all such documents do, in the least guarded place imaginable: the office of Mrs. Pettibone, the cathedral's events coordinator, a woman whose organizational genius was matched only by her conviction that no one under seventy could possibly be interested in her work. She kept it in a manila folder labeled — I am not inventing this — "DINNER (FINAL) (REVISED) (ACTUAL FINAL)" on top of a cabinet beside the tea things.

Getting to it required only patience and a plausible errand. On Thursday morning I carried a box of bicentennial correspondence into her office, complained at length about the misfiling of the Harrow family's RSVPs — a complaint so tedious that Mrs. Pettibone herself suggested I go make tea while she sorted it — and read every name on that chart twice before the kettle sang.

Forty-two covers at the long table in the great hall. The founding families arranged by seniority: Harrow at the head, naturally, with the mayor to his right and Bishop's representative to his left. The Ashcombes, the Fenwicks, the Larkins, the Mordaunts — names that had been carved into pew ends and millstones and, if my recent education was correct, into other people's graves. Then the lesser gentry, the donors, the newspaper man from the county seat carefully seated next to the wine steward so he'd be too far from Harrow to ask questions until the third course had softened him.

And there, near the foot of the table but present, unmistakable: *E. Blackwood, Cathedral Archive.* Someone had written me in by hand, in pencil, as though my presence were provisional. A footnote at my own town's table. I confess it stung less than it once would have. Invisibility, I was learning, has a way of becoming comfortable right up until the moment it becomes fatal.

But it wasn't the seating that stopped my breath. It was what was printed along the bottom margin, in Mrs. Pettibone's tidy hand, as an addendum:

*After dinner: private ceremony, crypt level. Family members and clergy only. Coffin reinterment, 10:30 p.m. New lid (Harrow commission, delivered Tuesday). Locks changed following morning.*

New lid.

Locks changed.

I stood in Mrs. Pettibone's office holding a teacup I had no memory of picking up, and understood with perfect clarity that I was looking at a funeral for a lie. They were going to lower that empty coffin back into the earth behind a brand-new sealed lid, change the locks on the crypt, and let two hundred years of marble settle over the question like snow. No investigation. No answers. Just a ceremony, a blessing, and a door that would never open again in anyone's lifetime.

Four days. The dinner was Sunday night. Today was Wednesday.

The kettle screamed. I made the tea. My hands were steady, which surprised me; inside, something was running down a corridor shouting.

* * *

I want to say something about clocks, because that week taught me what clocks actually are.

We think of them as instruments. They are not. They are verdicts delivered in advance, one second at a time, each tick a small announcement: *less remains than did a moment ago.* For most of my life I had lived without hearing them. An archive keeps its own time — geological, patient, indifferent. Paper does not rot on a schedule you can feel in your chest. But now, walking back across the close with the seating chart burned into my memory, I heard ticking everywhere. In the cathedral bell marking the quarter hour. In the creak of the gate hinge. In my own pulse, which had become, without my permission, a metronome counting down four days.

Because here is what the chart told me, between its lines: they were not waiting for the question to die. They were going to *kill* it. Sunday night, after the toasts, after the town had gone home warm and full and satisfied, forty-odd people would descend into the crypt and rebury the most famous empty coffin in England, and Monday morning a locksmith would arrive, and by noon the past would have a new lock on it, and men like Edmund Blackwood would be left standing outside with their photographs and their suspicions and their furious, useless patience.

Unless someone put a body in the coffin's story first. A living one.

Ruth Verity.

* * *

That afternoon I went to see Inspector Vale, which will surprise those who have followed this account, given our history of circling each other like two cats pretending the room is large enough for both of us. But the glassblower's death had rearranged something in me. Edith Marsh — I had learned her name properly by then, Edith Marsh, seventy-eight years old, hands that had shaped glass for sixty years — was dead because I had knocked on her door. The conspiracy had stopped being subtle, and subtlety was the only currency I had ever traded in. It was time, I decided, to spend something else.

Vale was at the Rose & Crown, in the corner room the constabulary had commandeered, surrounded by the paperwork of a town that had spent two hundred years generating paperwork specifically to prevent this exact moment. He looked up when I came in with the expression of a man who has been expecting a bill.

"Blackwood."

"Inspector. I've brought you something you'll want."

"I've brought myself nothing but trouble so far," he said, "so forgive me if I brace." But he cleared a space on the table, which from Vale was practically an embrace.

I told him about the seating chart. The addendum. The new lid, commissioned and already delivered — I had confirmed that much with a carpenter's apprentice who owed me a favor for twenty years of quiet help with his grandfather's pension papers. The locks scheduled for Monday morning. I laid it out flat and chronological, the way I'd lay out a provenance chain, and watched Vale's face do what faces do when a theory becomes a timetable.

"They're closing it," he said finally. "Not solving it. Closing it."

"Sealing it. There's a difference, and the difference is a lid."

He stood and walked to the window, hands behind his back, looking out at the close where tourists were photographing the cathedral as though it were merely old instead of guilty. "You understand how this looks from where I sit. An anonymous exhumation message. Three cold cases with your fingerprints all over the solutions nobody knows about. A break-in at Harrow's house the same week — don't look surprised, Blackwood, Harrow reported a disturbed panel and you're the only man in town who reads architecture the way other men read letters. And now you come to me with a conspiracy theory wrapped in a seating chart."

"Is it a theory if the lid has already been built?"

"It's a motive with excellent documentation," Vale said. He turned. "Here's my problem. Everything you've brought me is true. That's what makes you dangerous. Liars I can handle. You keep handing me pieces of the truth one at a time, always the piece that serves whatever you're doing, and I'm meant to be grateful."

"What are you doing?" I asked. "Genuinely. Because from where I stand, you arrived, you suspected me, you interviewed half the town, and the only person who's died since you got here died *after* you got here, in a shop she'd have been safe in if the town's oldest secret hadn't started bleeding through the floorboards. You're investigating the wrong crime, Inspector. The murder isn't forty years old. It's four days in the future, and its victim is the truth."

Silence. Somewhere below us, the pub noise rose and fell like weather.

"You said the witness might be alive," Vale said at last. "In the crypt, the night we were locked in — you said it. Ruth Verity. Declared dead at nineteen. If she's alive, she's evidence. If she's alive, she's also a target, and whoever hid her has spent four decades getting very good at hiding her." He looked at me with those flat grey eyes that missed nothing and forgave less. "Do you know where she is?"

"No."

"Do you know how to find her?"

"Yes."

"Then find her," Vale said, "and bring her to me before Sunday, or bring yourself to me instead, because I promise you, Blackwood, if that coffin goes back in the ground behind a new lid, the next thing sealed up will be you. Not in a coffin." He almost smiled. "In a cell. Obstruction, trespass, theft of county records — I can build it out of what I already know, and what I already know would fill a smaller room than this one."

"That's not protection. That's a hostage exchange."

"It's both," Vale said. "Everything worth having is both." He sat back down and picked up his pen, dismissing me the way a judge dismisses court. "Four days, Blackwood. I'd start running."

* * *

I did not go straight back to the archive. I walked.

Marrowfield in the evening light is a beautiful liar. The millstone by the bridge, worn smooth, that schoolchildren are told ground the founder's first grain — it ground something else too, if the ledgers' torn pages were to be believed, though stone keeps no confessions. The row of cottages called Workers' Row, quaint now, geraniums in the windows, built by Verity's mill for the six families whose names the town had somehow misplaced for two hundred years. The cathedral rising over everything, gorgeous and complicit, its crypt holding its breath beneath ten thousand tons of nave.

People nodded to me as I passed. They always had. The archivist, the quiet man, the one who knows where everything is filed. I had spent a lifetime being nodded at, and I had told myself it was peace. Walking those streets with four days on the clock, I finally admitted what it actually was: it was being furniture. Useful, durable, and incapable of testimony.

Edith Marsh had not been furniture. She had been a witness, and witnesses get buried — sometimes in coffins, sometimes in paperwork, sometimes in the long polite silence of a town that agrees not to remember. The founding families had managed it for two centuries with fire and torn pages and staged accidents. Now they proposed to manage it with a dinner and a new lid and a locksmith's invoice, and the whole operation would take one evening, catered.

I stopped on the bridge. Below, the millstream — the same water that had turned the Verity wheel — went on about its business, indifferent as arithmetic.

Four days. Find Ruth Verity, a woman who had spent forty years being dead, who had raised a son under another name, who had every reason on earth to trust no one — least of all a nodding archivist who had already gotten one old woman killed. Persuade her to walk back into the town that had erased her, into a hall full of the descendants of the men who had torched her father's mill and murdered her life on paper, and testify. Guarantee — I remembered my own promise, made in a kitchen smelling of bread, a promise I had no earthly means of keeping — guarantee that she walked out alive.

Against me: the most powerful families in the county, an institution I loved and could no longer believe in, a clock, and my own long-trained instinct for silence.

With me: an inspector who distrusted me and was right to, a dead woman's recollections, a photograph of a boy, and a name two towns over.

It occurred to me, standing on that bridge with the water talking underneath, that for the first time in my life I was not afraid of being seen. I was afraid of being *too late*. The distinction felt like being born.

* * *

Thomas Ashcombe — no. Thomas *Fenwick*, the name on the cottage door two towns east, the name of a family that had perhaps lent itself kindly or perhaps been paid, I didn't yet know which — answered on the second knock, and when he saw me his face went through recognition, fear, and resolve in the space of a breath, like weather over the moors.

"You shouldn't have come back," he said.

"The glassblower is dead," I said. "They killed her the day after I found her. Cruder than the others. They're done hiding, Thomas. Sunday night they seal the crypt with a new lid, and Monday morning they change the locks, and whatever chance your mother has of ever mattering gets buried with an empty coffin." I held his eyes. "I need to speak with her. Tonight. Before the four days run out."

"She'll say no."

"Then she'll say it to my face, and I'll carry the no back to Marrowfield and open my mouth anyway, because some verdicts—" I heard the stolen words leave my own mouth, the ones they'd pinned to my ransacked desk, and finished them deliberately— "some verdicts are mine to deliver after all. I've just decided whose they belong to."

Thomas looked past me down the lane, at the hedges and the evening and the forty years of hiding stacked behind his mother's front door. His hand tightened on the frame.

"She's been expecting someone," he said quietly. "Since the news of the exhumation. Every knock, she listens. Last night she told me: 'If they send a coward, turn him away. If they send a believer—'" He stepped back from the doorway. "Come in, Mr. Blackwood. Mind the third stair. She doesn't like surprises, and neither, anymore, does the house."

I crossed the threshold, and somewhere behind my ribs, faint but unmistakable, the clock began to count faster.
