## 2. Crane's Hollow

The bus from the county seat took forty minutes through country that got prettier the closer it came to lying to you.

Lena Villalobos watched from the window as the hills rolled green and patient, pastures stitched with stone walls older than any deed she'd ever filed, a creek flashing silver between sycamores. It was the kind of landscape that made people believe in providence — that some places were simply blessed, and the families who owned them simply chosen. She'd grown up in country like this, on the wrong side of its blessings, and she had learned early that prettiness was often a form of architecture. Something built. Something maintained. Something paid for.

The sign at the town line said WELCOME TO CRANE'S HOLLOW — EST. 1861 — A TOWN BUILT TO LAST. The letters were fresh gold on black, repainted recently enough that the smell of enamel still hung faintly in the air at the pull-off. Somebody cared about that sign. Somebody budgeted for it.

The bus let her off in the square, and the square was exactly what she expected and somehow worse: immaculate. Brick paving swept clean of leaves though it was October and every tree around the perimeter was shedding like a molting bird. Benches painted the same deep green. Flower boxes still blooming out of season — pansies, which meant someone replaced them weekly. And in the center, on a granite pedestal taller than the courthouse door, Augustus Crane stood in bronze.

He was rendered younger than the photographs, broad-chested, one hand resting on a plow and the other extended toward the horizon as if offering the valley itself to whoever looked up at him. His face wore the expression sculptors give founders: visionary, benevolent, slightly amused by the future he can already see. At his feet, chiseled into the pedestal in letters you could read from the second story of any building on the square:

AUGUSTUS CRANE — FATHER OF THE HOLLOW — GAVE THE LAND, BUILT THE MILL, KEPT THE FAITH.

*Kept the faith.* Lena set her bag down on a bench and studied the statue the way she'd study a hostile witness. Bronze Augustus gazed back over her head toward the mountains, magnanimous and mute, and she found herself thinking about what a man would have to do to earn a statue in life and an empty coffin after death. Not yet. She didn't know about the coffin yet. All she knew was a will dated three years after a man's certified death, signed in a hand the document examiner called authentic beyond reasonable doubt, witnessed by a woman the county swore had been dead since 1974.

But standing under the statue, she felt the first thing she would come to recognize later as the town's signature move: the sense of being gently, courteously watched.

"Visiting?"

The man who asked was sixty-ish, silver-haired, wearing a cardigan despite the mild morning and a smile calibrated to put strangers at ease. He had a canvas tote from the hardware store and the unhurried gait of someone who had never once been made to feel unwelcome here.

"Lena Villalobos." She offered her hand. "Attorney. I'm here on estate business."

"Estate business." He said it warmly, the way another man might say *fishing trip*. "Crane business?"

"The Crane estate, yes."

"Well." He shook her hand with both of his, briefly, like a benediction. "Walt Hale. Councilman — though I'm mostly retired these days. Anything you need in the Hollow, folks will point you my way eventually, so we may as well meet now." His eyes were pale blue and perfectly friendly and did not blink quite as often as they should have. "The family's always been good to this town. Good to everybody."

"So I've heard."

"You'll hear it a lot." He laughed, easy and full. "We're proud of our history here. Some towns have a founder; we have a *father*. There's a difference." He shifted the tote on his shoulder. "You'll want the Historical Society, probably. Mrs. Whitcomb keeps wonderful records. Or the church — Pastor Ames does the walking tour on Saturdays. People come from three counties."

"I actually came for the county clerk's office."

Something happened then, so small that a less attentive person would have missed it entirely. Walt Hale's smile stayed exactly where it was — same width, same warmth — but it stopped moving. It became, for perhaps two seconds, a thing he was holding up rather than a thing he was feeling. Then it resumed its life.

"Clerk's office is in the annex, behind the courthouse. Miss Ondine will take care of you." He patted her arm once, lightly, a gesture that managed to be both grandfatherly and proprietary. "Enjoy the Hollow, Ms. Villalobos. It grows on people. Most people never want to leave."

He walked on toward the hardware store, unhurried, and Lena noticed that two other men on the square — one sweeping brick dust outside the diner, one idly reading a newspaper on a bench — had both turned their heads, just slightly, to track him as he passed. Not deference exactly. Orientation. The way sunflowers face the light.

She picked up her bag and went to find the annex.

* * *

The county records annex smelled of toner and old paper and, beneath both, something floral — potpourri, or a plug-in air freshener working hard against the building's true age. It was a low brick building connected to the courthouse by a covered walkway, and it was colder inside than the morning justified, the air conditioning running in October as if the thermostat answered to someone other than the weather.

Miss Ondine sat behind a counter of polished oak, a small woman somewhere north of seventy with white hair pinned in a coil and reading glasses on a chain of tiny gold links. Her nameplate said ONDINE PRYCE, COUNTY CLERK, and beneath it, in smaller letters someone had added by hand: *and keeper of same.*

"Ms. Villalobos." Lena hadn't given her name yet. "You're the one asking after the Crane estate papers."

"That's right. I called Friday—"

"You did. Left a message with my girl Tammy." Miss Ondine folded her hands on the counter. Her nails were manicured, a soft pearl color. "I'm afraid I have some disappointing news, and I do hate delivering disappointing news before lunch."

"I'm requesting certified copies of the probate file for Augustus Crane, deceased 1995. Death certificate, letters testamentary, the inventory, and any subsequent filings against the estate."

"Mm." Miss Ondine rose without hurry. "Let me show you something while we talk. Come around — mind the gate, it sticks."

Lena came around the counter. The clerk led her down an aisle of bound volumes, trailing one fingertip along their spines like a pianist finding her place, and stopped at a shelf near the back where the light from the window fell just so.

"This," she said, "is the probate record for the County of Ashford, 1995 through 2001. Everything public, everything orderly. Estate of Augustus Crane, opened March of '95, executed per the will of record, closed November of '97 with distribution complete. Heirs satisfied, taxes paid, no contests." She smiled up at Lena with genuine, almost maternal regret. "It's all there, dear. Neat as a hymnal. Which means there's nothing left to request. A closed estate is a closed book."

"I'd still like copies. The death certificate especially, and the will itself."

"The will of record is a matter of public file, yes." Miss Ondine's finger moved along the shelf and tapped a volume. "And I could make you copies, certainly, at fifty cents a page, if the file were here."

"If."

"It's been pulled for review." The clerk said it smoothly, without a flicker. "Periodic audit. State sends a team every few years, they take certain files back to the regional office, bring them home when they're done. Could be weeks. Could be months. I never know until they tell me, and they haven't told me."

"When was it pulled?"

"Oh — recently."

"Recently meaning?"

"Meaning before your call on Friday, which I imagine is why Tammy told you to come in person rather than promising anything over the phone. We try not to disappoint people twice." Miss Ondine removed her glasses and polished them on the hem of her cardigan, unhurried, giving Lena the full benefit of her attention. Her eyes, behind the smudged lenses, were quick and gray and entirely unfoolish. "You're the lawyer with the strange will. The one dated after he died."

Word traveled fast in a town with one square. "I am."

"Mm. And now you want to see what we already probated, to compare it against what somebody sent you." She hung the glasses back on their chain. "I'll tell you what I told the last fellow who came sniffing — and he was from the state, so no offense meant, dear, but you're smaller fish than he was. That file has been open, examined, and closed by judges better credentialed than either of us. Augustus Crane died in March of 1995 at his home on the hill, attended by Dr. Pruett, buried in the cemetery on the ridge with half the county weeping. Whatever paper you're carrying, it isn't from this office, and this office's books balance."

"May I at least see the death certificate? You keep a duplicate index—"

"Certified copies route through vital records at the state level now." A shrug, dainty and final. "Privacy provisions. Since 2011."

"Death certificates are public record in this state, Miss Pryce. I've filed for them in eleven counties."

"And gotten them, I don't doubt." The old woman's voice stayed kind, which somehow made it worse. "But this is Crane's Hollow, and things are done a certain way here. Now." She clapped her hands together softly, once, like a teacher closing a lesson. "Can I interest you in the historical collection instead? Photographs of the mill race when it ran. The founding quilt. Mrs. Whitcomb donated the original land grant with Augustus's own signature — beautiful penmanship, you'd enjoy comparing it to whatever you've got in your briefcase."

The offer landed with a precision that told Lena it wasn't an offer at all. It was a message: *we know what you brought, we know why you brought it, and we'd rather you look at the pretty version.*

"Not today," Lena said. "Thank you."

"Of course." Miss Ondine walked her back to the counter, pressed a peppermint into her hand at the gate — actually pressed it into her palm, closing Lena's fingers over it like a communion — and smiled her smooth, disappointing smile. "Do come back whenever. The Hollow loves company. We just prefer it well-behaved."

Outside, the cold of the annex peeled away and the autumn sun took over, warm on the brick square, and Lena stood for a moment eating the peppermint because refusing it had felt suddenly dangerous in a way she couldn't articulate. It was excellent peppermint. Of course it was.

* * *

She spent the afternoon doing what lawyers do when documents hide: she talked to people.

The diner on the square — the Crane Room, naturally, its walls papered with sepia photographs of the mill, the founding families, harvest festivals four generations deep — served her coffee in a mug with the Crane Foundation logo and pie that a waitress named Dot described, unprompted, as "Augustus's mother's recipe, from the old country book." Everyone Lena spoke to mentioned Augustus within the first five minutes, unprompted, the way people in other towns mention the weather. The barber. The feed store owner. Two elderly women playing cards by the window who interrupted their game to tell her, in overlapping chorus, that Mr. Crane had personally paid the hospital bill for one of their brothers in 1968 and never told a soul, though of course everyone knew.

"He built the school," the barber said, scissors snipping. "Built the church. Built the levee after the flood of '52. Man gave everything."

"Everything except himself," Lena said, testing.

The scissors paused — half a beat, no more — and resumed. "Well. He earned his rest."

At the cemetery on the ridge, she found the grave easily. It was the best-kept plot in the yard, granite polished to a dark mirror, flowers fresh, the name AUGUSTUS CRANE cut deep and re-cut recently enough that the grooves still showed pale stone underneath. Someone had power-washed it within the month. She knelt and photographed it from four angles, noting the maintenance, noting the sightline — the grave faced the town, the whole valley laid out below it, the statue in the square visible even from here, a bronze speck keeping watch. Founders get the view, she thought. Fathers watch over their children.

An old man in coveralls was raking leaves two rows over, slow and methodical. When she straightened up, he was looking at her.

"You're the lawyer," he said. Not a question. Word traveled fast in a town with one square.

"I'm looking into the estate. Routine verification."

"Nobody comes up here to verify." He leaned on the rake. He had a work-worn face and eyes the color of creek water, and he looked at the grave the way a man looks at a dog that once bit him. "They come up here to pay respects. Forty years I've kept this ground. Nobody verifies."

"What's your name?"

"Tomás." He said nothing else for a moment, raking a small clean circle, then: "You'll want to talk to the clerk, I expect. Everybody does."

"I did. The file's out for audit."

Tomás nodded slowly, unsurprised, and Lena watched something pass across his face — not fear exactly, but the muscular weariness of a man who has carried a weight so long his shoulders have changed shape. "Files go out for audit," he agreed. "Flowers get changed. Graves get washed." He looked at her then, directly, and held it a beat longer than the town's courtesy allowed. "Everything in this town gets taken care of, ma'am. That's what people mean when they say he kept the faith. Somebody's still keeping it for him."

"Who pays you?" she asked. "For the flowers. The washing. Is it the town?"

"The Foundation," Tomás said. "There's a fund. There's always a fund." He went back to his raking, and the conversation was over, though as she turned to go he added, quietly, without looking up: "Careful driving back down. Ridge road takes a curve too fast if you're not from here."

* * *

By dusk she had filled twelve pages of her notebook and confirmed three things. First: the town's veneration of Augustus Crane was not folklore but infrastructure — funded, curated, refreshed, the statue's patina waxed, the sign regilded, the pie recipes attributed. Second: her arrival was known to the square's entire population within hours, and her questions were being reported somewhere, because at the motel the desk clerk had already upgraded her room, unasked, "compliments of the Foundation," a fruit basket on the dresser with a card reading *Welcome to the Hollow — we hope your stay is productive.* Third: nobody, not one soul among the dozens she'd spoken to, had volunteered a single unguarded sentence about the man himself. Every anecdote was a donation. Every memory was a monument.

She ate dinner at the diner because there was nowhere else, and because she wanted to sit in the room with the photographs and look at them properly. That was where she found it — third row, above the coat hooks, a picture she suspected most patrons never really saw. The dedication of the statue, 1974 by the plaque's date. Augustus in bronze unveiled to a crowd of several hundred, bunting on the storefronts, a high school band with tarnished horns. Standard ceremonial photograph, professionally composed.

Except for the edges.

In the left foreground, half-cropped by the frame, stood a young woman in a maid's uniform holding a covered dish, caught mid-turn toward the camera. Dark hair, sharp cheekbones, an expression that had nothing to do with celebration — wary, watchful, looking at the statue the way Tomás looked at the grave. Lena leaned close, breath fogging the glass, and read the handwritten caption on the matting: *Founders Day, June 1974.*

Under the woman's feet, in a different hand, smaller, someone had written a single word long ago in ballpoint ink, half worn away:

*Marisol.*

Lena stood in the emptying diner for a long time, the coffee going cold in her hand, while Dot wiped tables and the neon OPEN sign buzzed and outside the window the statue of Augustus Crane caught the last of the light and threw its long shadow across the square — across the benches, the flower boxes, the swept bricks, all the way to the door of the county annex where the files went out for audits and came back, presumably, neat as hymnals.

Every record said Marisol Reyes died in 1974.

Someone in this town had known her well enough to write her name under her own photograph, in a frame that had hung undisturbed for decades, in a diner that served pie from the founder's mother's recipe — and had let the name stay.

Lena photographed the picture, the caption, the ink. Then she went back to her complimentary room, sat on the complimentary bed, and dialed the number for the county vital records office at the state capital, because Miss Pryce had lied to her. Death certificates were public record in this state, and there was no privacy provision dated 2011, and tomorrow morning she intended to prove it.

The line rang twice before a recorded voice told her the office was closed and to call back during business hours.

Fine. Tomorrow.

She was setting the phone down when she noticed the fruit basket again — really noticed it — and lifted out the card a second time. *Welcome to the Hollow — we hope your stay is productive.* Beneath the printed line, in fresh blue ink, someone had added a postscript in a small, tidy, unhurried hand:

*Some questions are best asked from home, Ms. Villalobos. — W.H.*

She looked out the window. Across the street, under the square's lone lamp, a man was sweeping brick dust that had already been swept that morning, working the broom slowly back and forth, back and forth, not cleaning anything at all — just watching her lit window, patient as the statue, in no hurry whatsoever.

Lena closed the curtains, and began making a list of everyone whose smoothness had shown her the rot underneath.
