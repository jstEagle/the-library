## 9. Statute Clock

The calendar on Lena's office wall was the kind lawyers bought to impress clients—thick cream stock, gold corners, a different engraving of the county courthouse for every month. She had never once written on it. Her real calendar lived in her head and in three overlapping systems: the firm's docketing software, a leather daybook her mentor Delia Marsh had given her when she passed the bar, and a legal pad she kept face-down on her desk like a confession.

On the morning after the exhumation, she turned the legal pad over.

She wrote the date at the top. Then she worked backward, the way she'd been taught—not from hope toward deadline, but from deadline toward what remained. The will contest window under state probate code ran one hundred eighty days from the date a will was first presented for probate. Presented, not filed; presented, not validated. The clock had started the moment she'd walked the document into Judge Ferris's chambers and asked, in her most reasonable voice, that it be noticed for hearing.

That had been fourteen days ago.

Which left one hundred sixty-six days on paper—and far fewer in practice. Because the contest window wasn't the only clock running. The estate itself sat in a trust administered by the Crane Foundation, and the Foundation's trustees had already filed their motion to dismiss on standing grounds, arguing that no named beneficiary had come forward to defend the instrument. If no beneficiary appeared by the hearing date, the will would be dismissed as abandoned—a dead document naming a ghost, struck from the record with prejudice. And if it was dismissed with prejudice, no future petition could revive it. Not ever.

Lena did the arithmetic twice. Then a third time, because the answer kept refusing to feel real.

Thirty-four days.

Thirty-four days until the Foundation's motion would be heard. Thirty-four days to produce a living, breathing, competent witness who could authenticate a signature everyone in Crane's Hollow swore belonged to a woman thirty years in her grave. Thirty-four days to find Marisol Reyes—or watch the only honest piece of paper Augustus Crane had ever signed dissolve into procedural dust.

She set down the pen. Outside her window, the city went about its morning with the indifference cities have, traffic moving in long silver ribbons, a window washer swaying on his rig against the glass of the tower across the street. Somewhere down the block a jackhammer stuttered through concrete. The world, announcing that it did not care about her deadline.

* * *

Her partner found her at nine, carrying two coffees and the expression he wore when he was about to say something he'd rehearsed.

"Marisol Reyes," Ray Okonkwo said, setting a cup on her desk. "You're still looking."

"I'm still looking."

"Lena." He lowered himself into the chair across from her, all six-foot-four of him folding down with the weary grace of a man who'd spent twenty years watching idealists arrive at his door. "Walk with me through this. You've got a will signed by a dead man, witnessed by a dead woman, contested by a foundation with more money than God, in a town where the sheriff's office is staffed by the same three families that appear in your client's—your *non-client's*—private ledger. And you want to find the one person whose testimony could blow all of it open. Before a deadline. In thirty-four days."

"When you say it like that—"

"Because I said it like that so you'd hear it." He leaned forward. "I've been doing this long enough to know what happens next. First they ignore you. Then they offer you money—that already happened, Hale tried it. Then they make it personal. Then they make it criminal. You're at stage three, maybe four. And you're doing it alone, for a beneficiary who doesn't know she exists yet, on behalf of a man who faked his own death to escape prosecution."

"For a woman who was murdered," Lena said quietly. "Elena Duarte. That's who this is for. Crane just happened to sign the paperwork."

Ray looked at her for a long moment. She watched him decide something—the same small surrender she'd seen from him before, the moment where his pragmatism lost another round to whatever it was he actually believed about why they practiced law.

"Fine," he said. "Then do it right. No freelancing. Everything through counsel of record. And Lena—if someone offers you evidence out of nowhere, too clean, too convenient, you bring it to me before you touch it. People who are being hunted get handed bait. Understand?"

"Understood."

"And take an investigator. A real one. Not yourself at two a.m. with a phone book."

After he left, she sat with the coffee going cold and thought about what he hadn't said, which was: *you can still stop.* He hadn't said it because he knew her well enough to know it would land as insult rather than advice. Fourteen days ago she'd been a lawyer with a respectable practice and a mentor who loved her. Now she was a name on a councilman's list, a photograph in someone's file, a woman who had made forty people gasp at a graveside and could not unmake it.

Thirty-four days. She wrote the number large across the top of the pad and boxed it.

* * *

The reward notice cost her four hundred dollars to place and took eleven drafts.

The problem was tone. Too official and it read like law enforcement—which would spook anyone hiding, and alert Hale's people instantly. Too personal and it read like desperation, which invited cranks and worse. Too specific and it told the town exactly how much she knew. Too vague and nobody would connect it to anything worth money.

In the end she settled on language that was almost aggressively bland:

> **INFORMATION SOUGHT — MARISOL REYES**
> Last known residence: Crane's Hollow. Formerly employed at the Crane estate. Any information regarding her whereabouts or circumstances since 1974. Substantial reward. Confidentiality assured. All inquiries handled by licensed attorney. Reply to P.O. Box 1147.

No photograph—she didn't have one newer than 1974, and the black-and-white image of a young woman with a serious mouth and hair pinned back told the town nothing it didn't already claim to know. No mention of the will, the estate, or any sum. *Substantial reward* was a phrase designed to be read twice.

She placed it in the *Crane's Hollow Courier*, the regional weekly, and the classified sections of three papers within driving distance. Then she drove up herself, on a gray Thursday with low clouds snagging on the ridgelines, and spent the afternoon taping index cards to the bulletin boards of the laundromat, the feed store, the diner off Route 9, and the little market by the river bridge. Each card carried the same text. Each time she pressed a thumbtack into corkboard, she felt the eyes of the room adjust around her, the way water adjusts around a dropped stone.

At the diner, the waitress refilled her coffee without being asked and said, not looking at her, "You're the one that opened the box."

"I'm the one that opened the box."

"My mother used to clean up there. At the big house. Before they closed the wing." The waitress tore the check and laid it down. "She said the housekeeper's room still had a coat on its hook. Forty years, and a coat on its hook. Like everybody agreed not to see it." She moved off before Lena could ask anything else, and when Lena looked up, the woman was wiping down a booth that was already clean, her back a closed door.

Everyone knew. That was the thing about Crane's Hollow she understood better each visit—it wasn't a town with a secret. It was a town *made* of a secret, the way a pearl is made of an irritation. Everyone knew, and everyone had arranged their lives around not saying, and the arrangement had held for fifty years because the alternative had always been worse than silence.

Until now. Until an empty coffin and a legal pad with a number boxed at the top.

* * *

The calls began the second evening, and they were exactly what Ray had predicted.

A man who claimed Marisol Reyes was living in Reno under an assumed name and would confirm it for five hundred dollars cash, mailed ahead. A woman who wept and said Marisol had been her aunt's best friend and died in 1976, buried in a pauper's field, and then couldn't say which aunt. A teenager who'd seen the card at the market and wanted to know if this was for a school project, could she interview Lena, it was for civics class. A voice that whispered *you don't know what you're digging up* and hung up before the trace software could catch a prefix.

Lena logged them all in a spiral notebook, time-stamped each entry, and felt the days draining away like sand through her fingers. Day twenty-nine remaining. Twenty-eight. Twenty-six. The Foundation's attorneys filed a supplemental brief arguing that even if a beneficiary materialized, the will's execution was presumptively invalid given the testator's documented death three years prior—citing, with what Lena considered breathtaking nerve, the very dental records she herself had introduced to prove the coffin empty. They were using her own exhumation as a weapon. Of course they were. It was good lawyering. She hated how good it was.

She was at her desk on the twenty-fourth night—day ten remaining, though she'd stopped writing the countdown and now simply felt it, a weight behind her sternum like a stone swallowed—when the phone rang at 11:47 p.m.

Not the office line. Her cell. The number she'd printed on nothing, listed nowhere, given to no one except Ray, Delia, and the answering service.

She stared at it. Unknown caller. Local exchange—Crane's Hollow's own area code, the one she'd been dialing all month.

She answered without speaking.

For a moment there was nothing but the connection itself: a faint electrical hush, the sound of a line open between two points in the dark. Then breathing. Slow, deliberate, unhurried breathing—not the ragged pull of fear or the theatrical rasp of a prankster, but the calm respiration of someone who had decided to call and was taking their time about what came next. Someone old, maybe. Or someone very controlled. The breath drew in, held, and released, and Lena found herself holding her own breath in imitation, as if the rhythm were contagious, as if she could synchronize herself with whoever stood at the other end of the dark.

"Don't hang up," she said softly. "Please. Whoever you are—I'm not the police. I'm not the Foundation. I'm the one who opened the coffin, and I need to talk to Marisol Reyes. That's all. Just talk."

The breathing paused. In the pause she heard—or thought she heard—something beneath it. A room. The particular quality of silence that meant distance from roads, from neighbors, from everything. Water maybe, faintly, moving. And underneath that, so faint it might have been imagination or the line's static wearing a shape, something rhythmic and small: a page turning. Paper against paper.

Then a voice—not words, not quite, but the ghost of one, a single syllable shaped and released like a coin dropped down a well:

"*...busque...*"

And the dial tone came, flat and final, the way dial tones always sound like a verdict.

Lena sat motionless in the dark of her office for a long time, the phone still pressed to her ear, listening to the dead line as though it might reconsider. *Busque.* Search. Look for. An imperative, or the fragment of one—*búsquemela*, search for her, look for me? Her Spanish was good enough to catch the shape of it and not good enough to be sure, and the uncertainty was unbearable, a hook set just below the skin.

Whoever it was had called her cell. Had known the number. Had breathed at her for eleven seconds and offered one syllable of Spanish and a turning page—and had not spoken, because speaking was the thing that got people in Crane's Hollow disappeared, declared dead by affidavit, written out of the record while still warm.

But they had called. After fifty years of silence, someone had picked up a phone.

Lena set the handset down with hands that were not entirely steady, pulled the legal pad toward her, and wrote beneath the boxed number a single line:

*Day 10. She knows my number.*

Outside, the city slept on, indifferent, silver-lit, loud with its jackhammers and its traffic. Inside, Lena Villalobos sat alone with a cold cup of coffee and a heartbeat that wouldn't slow, understanding suddenly and completely that the clock she'd been counting down was not the only one running.

Somewhere upriver, in a room far from roads, someone had waited fifty years for a lawyer honest enough to help—and tonight, finally, she had dialed.
