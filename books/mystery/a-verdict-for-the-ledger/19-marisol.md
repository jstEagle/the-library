## 19. Marisol

The balcony of the First Congregational Church had not been built for comfort. Marisol Reyes knew this because she had been sitting in the same corner of it, on and off, for fifty years — first on a hard pew bench that smelled of varnish and old hymnals, later in a folding chair she carried up herself, and now in a padded rocker she'd earned through sheer stubbornness and the pastor's willful blindness. Pastor Ellery was the fourth man to hold that title since she'd come back to Crane's Hollow, and he was the only one who had never asked her name. He simply nodded to her each Sunday as if she were a fixture, like the bell rope or the water stain shaped like a dove above the east window, and let her climb the narrow stair with her bad knee and her good book.

She was seventy-nine years old, and she had learned to be furniture.

Below her, through the slats of the balcony rail, the town went about its business of forgetting. She could see the square from here — the whole geometry of it, the statue of Augustus Crane with his hand extended over the fountain as though blessing the water, the bank, the diner, the courthouse with its cupola like a small tin crown. On market days the stalls went up around the statue's base and people sold honey and quilts beneath the stone gaze of a murderer, and nobody flinched, because nobody knew. That was the miracle Marisol had watched for half a century: not that a town could keep a secret, but that a town could keep a secret *from itself*. They didn't lie about Augustus Crane. They simply never thought about him at all, the way you don't think about the foundation your house stands on until it cracks.

The morning light came through the east window in long amber panes and lay across her lap like something warm and living. In her hands, wrapped in a dish towel gone soft with washing, was a photograph she had looked at ten thousand times: Elena Duarte, twenty-two years old, laughing at something off-camera, one hand raised mid-gesture as if she were in the middle of saying something important and expected the whole world to lean in and listen.

Elena had been like that. The world should have leaned in.

Marisol turned the photograph over, out of habit, though there was nothing written on the back anymore. There had been, once. She had burned the writing and kept the face.

*Fifty years,* she thought. *Fifty years of Sundays.*

She had counted them the way other people count rosaries. Four hundred and some Sundays a year, minus the winters when ice made the church stairs treacherous and she stayed home in the cabin upriver with the wood stove and the radio and the ledger pages hidden in the flour tin, reciting them to herself in the dark like scripture, because memory is the only vault no one can drill open. She had recited those numbers so many times they had worn smooth inside her head, the way a river wears a stone — July 9th, four thousand dollars; July 16th, six thousand; July 23rd, the entry that mattered most, the one she would carry into whatever grave finally took her: *the Duarte matter resolved.*

Resolved. As if Elena had been a dispute over a fence line. As if a girl who wanted clean water for the migrant camps had been a *matter*, filed and closed.

Marisol closed her eyes and let the light press warm against her lids.

*You are being dramatic again, vieja,* she told herself, in her mother's voice, which had never entirely left her. *Dramatic and old and sitting in a church when you could be baking bread.*

But drama was the truth of it, and she had earned the right to name it. She had been twenty-nine years old in the summer of 1974, a housekeeper with quick hands and quicker eyes, and she had seen everything — everything — and she had run, and she had been declared dead by a notary public with soft hands and frightened eyes, a young woman named Delia Marsh whose signature Marisol had studied so many times over the decades that she felt she knew the girl better than God did. Delia Marsh, who must be seventy by now, an old lioness of the county bar, and who had signed away Marisol's existence with a shaking pen. Marisol had forgiven her long ago. Fear was a language everyone in Crane's Hollow spoke fluently; it was practically the town's second tongue, after money.

And then, three weeks ago, the woman had come.

* * *

Lena Villalobos. Marisol had watched her for three weeks the way she watched everything — patiently, from edges, from balconies, from the passenger seat of Rosa's rattling pickup with a blanket over her knees. She had watched the lawyer walk the square with her chin up and her questions out in the open, no apology in her stride, and she had thought: *either this one is honest or this one is very good at pretending, and I have fifty years of practice telling the difference.*

The pretending kind smiled too much. This one didn't smile enough, if anything. She walked past the statue of Augustus Crane without looking up at it, which Marisol found more telling than any document. People who believed in the Crane legend looked up. People who were hunting it kept their eyes level, on doors and faces.

She had watched her argue with the county clerk through the glass, watched her stand in the cemetery in the rain while men in coveralls worked around the opened earth, watched her sit alone in the diner booth with a legal pad and a cup of coffee going cold, writing in that tight, slanted hand. And last Sunday, she had watched her slip into the back pew of this very church, late, quiet, wearing a dark coat, and sit through the entire service with her eyes fixed on the stained glass and her jaw set like a woman listening for something underneath the sermon.

Marisol had been in the balcony, ten feet above her, close enough to have dropped a hymnal on her head.

She hadn't dropped anything. She had rocked, very slightly, in her chair, and prayed — not the church prayers, but her own, the ones she'd been saying since 1974: *Let her be real. Let her be real. Let this one be real.*

Because here was the thing Marisol Reyes had learned about waiting: it does not make you patient. It makes you precise. Fifty years of watching had taught her to read a person the way she once read laundry marks — fast, total, no second pass. And Lena Villalobos read true. There was a hunger in her that wasn't for money or standing; Marisol had seen plenty of that hunger and knew its smell. This was a different appetite. This was a woman who needed the world to add up, who would rather burn her own career than let a false number stand in a ledger. Marisol had met perhaps four such people in her long life, and two of them were dead, and one was Elena.

So she had decided, somewhere between the second hymn and the benediction last Sunday, that she would let the lawyer find her.

Not yet. Not today. But soon. Because the clock was running — she knew about the statute, Rosa had told her, thirty-four days then, fewer now — and because she was dying. That was the plain fact she kept folded away like the photograph: the cough that wouldn't leave, the weight sliding off her bones no matter how much bread she baked and ate. Dr. Prasad in Corbett had used the word *months* in the gentle way doctors do, and Marisol had thanked him and driven home and baked bread, because what else was there to do with months except make them smell good?

She would not die with Elena's name still buried. That was the whole of her remaining ambition, and it was large enough to fill a coffin, even an empty one.

* * *

Today the lawyer came back to the church.

It was Wednesday, a nothing day, the square nearly empty, heat shimmering off the flagstones. Marisol heard the door below — that heavy oak door with its iron hinge, a sound she knew better than her own heartbeat — and she rose, quietly, and moved to the rail.

Lena Villalobos stood in the nave alone. No coat this time; just a blouse the color of cream and dark trousers, a leather bag over her shoulder. She didn't go to the pews. She stood in the center aisle and turned slowly, taking in the sanctuary the way she must take in every room — cataloguing, weighing. Her gaze climbed the pulpit, the choir loft stairs, the memorial plaques on the side wall where the founding families' names marched in brass: CRANE, HALE, WHITCOMB, MARSH.

There it was. Marisol saw her stop at the plaques, saw her lean closer, saw her finger rise almost to touch the name MARSH before falling back to her side. So the mentor's name was already in her mouth. Good. A lawyer who hadn't yet suspected her own family was no use to anyone.

Then Lena did something unexpected. She sat down — not in a pew near the door, escape within reach, but three rows from the front, directly beneath the pulpit, in full view of whatever might be watching. She set her bag beside her, folded her hands, and waited. It was the posture of someone who had come to be found.

Marisol's breath went thin.

*She knows,* Marisol thought, and then corrected herself: *No. She hopes.* There was a difference, and the difference was everything. The girl couldn't know — no one alive knew Marisol was in the church on Wednesdays except Rosa, and Rosa would die before telling. But the lawyer had reasoned her way here, the way she apparently reasoned her way everywhere: the church was the one building in Crane's Hollow the Foundation didn't own, the one place with a balcony and a history of letting strangers sit unnoticed. Or maybe it was simpler than that. Maybe she had just come to think, and thinking had led her to God's house the way trouble always eventually does.

Either way, she was sitting in the light, asking to be seen.

Marisol stood at the rail with her hands on the wood, and the wood was cool, and her heart — that old unreliable drum — was going like she was twenty-nine again, like it was the night she crouched in the pantry with the study door ajar and heard the sounds she had spent fifty years trying to unhear.

*Decide, vieja. You have decided everything else in your life alone. Decide this.*

She thought of Elena's mother, who had died in 1981 in Corbett without ever learning where her daughter's body was. She thought of Ana — Elena's granddaughter, the nurse, the one Marisol paid tuition to through a post office box and a false name, watching from a distance as the girl grew tall and serious and good. She thought of the flour tin, and the pages, and the phrase worn smooth as river stone: *the Duarte matter resolved.*

She thought of the empty coffin, and the sandbags, and the forty witnesses gasping, and the gray face of Councilman Hale, and she almost laughed — a dry sound with no air behind it. Augustus Crane had spent fifty years and a fortune making his death the most examined event in county history, and it had taken one stubborn woman with a petition and a shovel to crack it open like an egg.

If the coffin could be opened, so could everything else.

"Miss Villalobos," Marisol said.

Her voice came out rusty, lower than she intended, and it fell down through the amber light into the nave and landed in the silence like a coin dropped in a well.

Lena Villalobos went absolutely still. Then, slowly, she turned — not toward the pulpit, not toward the doors, but upward, her chin lifting, her eyes climbing the balcony rail until they found the shape of an old woman standing in the shadow of the east window with a dish towel still draped forgotten over one arm.

For a long moment neither of them moved. The dust drifted in the light shafts between them. Somewhere outside, a truck changed gears on the county road, and the sound passed over the church and was gone.

"You're supposed to be dead," Lena said at last. Her voice was steady, but Marisol, connoisseur of voices, heard the tremor under the steel. "Every record in this county says you died in 1974."

"The records," Marisol said, "were signed by a frightened girl with soft hands. Records are only paper, Miss Villalobos. Paper lies when people are afraid." She gripped the rail. "I have spent fifty years watching this town from up here, waiting for one person honest enough to hear what I know. One person. Do you understand how long a person can wait?"

"How do you know my name?"

"I know everyone's name. That has been my work." Marisol stepped forward, out of the shadow, into the amber light, and let the old woman's face be seen at last — the deep lines, the black eyes still sharp as awls, the mouth that had kept fifty years of secrets pressed shut as a ledger. "I am Marisol Reyes. I was Augustus Crane's housekeeper. I saw what he did to Elena Duarte in the summer of 1974, and I saw what six families did to hide it, and I have the proof memorized down to the dates and the dollar amounts."

Below her, the lawyer's hands had gone white on the pew back.

"And I have decided," Marisol said, "that you are the one I have been waiting for. So come up the stairs, Miss Villalobos, and bring your legal pad. We have thirty days, and I have fifty years, and we are going to need every hour of both."

Lena Villalobos rose from the pew without a word — and Marisol saw that she was already reaching for the stairwell door, already moving, already hers.

*Elena,* Marisol thought, turning toward the narrow stairs, one hand on the cool wall. *Mija. After fifty years, someone is finally coming to listen.*
