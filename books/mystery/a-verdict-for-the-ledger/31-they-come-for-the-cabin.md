## 31. They Come for the Cabin

The call came at 4:17 in the morning, and Lena knew before she answered.

She had learned, over the past two months, that bad news in Crane's Hollow kept office hours the way the town's founders had kept their ledgers — irregularly, and always with an eye toward what could be hidden. But a fire was different. A fire announced itself. She heard it in Tomás's voice before he said a word: a flatness, a hollowness, like a man reading his own obituary off a card someone else had written.

"The cabin," he said. "It's gone."

Lena sat up in the dark of her motel room, phone pressed to her ear, and felt the floor tilt beneath her even though she hadn't moved. "Marisol—"

"Not there." A breath, ragged at the edges. "She hasn't been up there since we moved her to the mansion. Thank God for that, at least. But the place — Lena, I've been keeping that cabin ready for her for thirty years. Firewood stacked. The stove oiled. Fresh linens every spring, because I told myself someday she'd come back and I'd want it right." His voice caught and steadied itself with visible effort, the way an old man catches himself on a fence rail. "Somebody burned it down last night. Burned it *clean*."

"Are you there now?"

"I'm standing in the road looking at it. You should see this. No — don't see this. Stay away. There are county trucks all over, and half of them belong to men who drink beer with Hale on Fridays." He paused. "But you need to know one thing. Before they tarp it over or whatever they're going to do. Come before eight, and bring your camera."

* * *

The cabin sat at the end of a fire road above the river bend, a quarter mile from where the pavement gave up and became gravel and then became nothing much at all. Lena had walked that road once before, at dawn, following signs of life — fresh bread, reading glasses, the torn ledger pages waiting on a table like a confession. Now she followed the smell instead: wet ash and char and something chemical underneath, sharp and wrong, that made her think of accelerant rather than accident.

The fire had taken the roof and most of the east wall. What remained stood black against the graying sky like a rib cage picked clean. Snow from two weeks ago had melted into gray slush around the foundation, and steam still rose faintly from the deepest of the ruins, breathing out of the wreckage as though the structure itself were exhaling its last heat into the cold morning.

Tomás waited by his truck, hands jammed in the pockets of a canvas coat older than she was. He nodded toward the ruin without greeting.

"Fire department logged it as accidental," he said. "Electrical. Nobody's lived there full-time in decades, but they'll say somebody ran a space heater, something like that. There's no electrical service to this cabin, Lena. Hasn't been since the nineties. But the report will say electrical anyway, because the report gets written by people who get told what to write."

Lena raised her camera and began to shoot.

She worked methodically, the way Delia had taught her twenty years ago in a windowless records room: wide establishing frames first, then mid-range, then close detail. The melted aluminum of a window frame, frozen mid-drip. A door hinge, blackened but intact, hanging open toward the river. And then, near the center of what had been the main room, a shape that stopped her lens mid-air.

A chair.

Someone had dragged a metal folding chair out ahead of time — or placed it there ahead of time — and set it facing the cabin, ten yards back, far enough to be safe from the flames, close enough to watch. It sat upright in the mud, untouched by fire, unburned, patient. Whoever set the blaze had wanted a seat for the show.

"They watched it burn," Lena said quietly.

"Somebody did." Tomás crouched beside the chair without touching it. "And they left this on the seat." He held up a clear evidence bag — not an official one; a sandwich bag from the general store — and inside it lay a scrap of charred paper, folded once. "I found it snagged on the porch nail. Figured you'd want it before the county 'lost' it."

Lena gloved her hand, took the bag, and opened it carefully against her knee. Inside, scorched brown at the edges but legible in the center, was a page torn from a telephone directory. On it, printed in block letters in grease pencil:

**YOU CAN'T PROBATE ASHES.**

Below that, smaller, almost gentle in its malice:

**Last warning.**

Lena looked at the chair, at the ruined cabin, at the river sliding past below them, indifferent and silver in the early light. Her hands were steady. She noticed that about herself with a kind of clinical surprise — after weeks of threats, tailgating pickups, a windshield note, an arson at the annex, her body had apparently decided that fear was a luxury it could no longer afford to process in real time. It would bill her later. It always did.

"Thirty years you kept this place ready for her," Lena said.

Tomás's jaw worked. "My father kept it before me. He tended that cabin until the day he died, and he never once told me who it was for. Just said, *keep the wood dry, keep the door oiled, and never ask.*" He stared at the wreckage. "I asked myself a thousand times if I was a fool for it. Keeping a promise to a ghost. And now some son of a bitch burns it down in one night, and you know what I feel?" He shook his head slowly. "Relieved. That's the shameful part. Relieved that Marisol wasn't in it. Everything else is just property. Property can be rebuilt. People—" He stopped.

"People can't," Lena finished.

They stood together in the smoke-smell silence. Downriver, a kingfisher broke the water and came up flashing.

"There's something else," Tomás said. "Tracks. Two sets, coming up the fire road from the north side, away from town. Boot prints, big ones, and tire tracks from a heavy pickup — dual rear wheels, loaded. They drove in from the county line side so nobody from town would see them pass." He met her eyes. "That's not passion, Ms. Villalobos. That's logistics. Somebody planned this the way you plan a court filing."

* * *

By nine o'clock, the story was already on the local news, and by noon it was everywhere.

Lena watched it in the diner off the square, on the television mounted above the pie case, between the specials board and a faded photograph of Augustus Crane shaking hands with a governor dead forty years. The anchor — a pleasant, professionally concerned woman from the regional station — introduced the segment with footage of the burning cabin shot from across the river, orange light pulsing in the pre-dawn dark like a second sunrise nobody had asked for.

Then Councilman Everett Hale stepped up to a bank of microphones outside the county administration building, and Lena set down her coffee and made herself watch.

Hale had dressed for it. Charcoal overcoat, blue tie, the small flag pin on his lapel that he wore to funerals and budget hearings alike. Behind him, arranged with careful casualness, stood three members of the town council and the volunteer fire chief, whose face had the particular blankness of a man reciting lines written by someone else.

"This is a tragedy," Hale said, and his voice was warm, resonant, calibrated to carry sincerity the way a church organ carries hymns. "An old structure with no historical significance, lost to what appears to be an electrical fault. We're grateful no one was hurt. I want to be absolutely clear: any suggestion that this fire was anything other than an accident is irresponsible speculation. This community does not tolerate arson, and it certainly does not tolerate the kind of conspiracy-mongering that certain outside attorneys have been spreading in order to advance a meritless probate claim."

He paused there, let the cameras drink, and then delivered the line Lena knew had been written for exactly this moment, probably days ago, probably rehearsed in a mirror.

"I would remind everyone that the Crane Foundation has served this town faithfully for fifty years. Our record speaks for itself. And I'd caution those trafficking in innuendo to remember that making false accusations is itself a serious matter — one that carries real legal consequences." A thin smile. "We will be pursuing all available remedies."

The anchor cut back in, thanking the councilman, noting that the state fire marshal had been requested but had not yet arrived.

Lena sat very still, her coffee cooling, and listened to the diner around her. Conversations had dropped to murmurs. A table of men in feed-store jackets glanced at her, then away, then at each other. The waitress refilled her cup without meeting her eyes — though Lena noticed, and filed away, that the refill was free, and that the girl's hand trembled slightly as she poured.

Nobody spoke to her. Nobody had spoken to her in the diner in three weeks. But nobody had asked her to leave, either, and in Crane's Hollow that arithmetic meant something.

Her phone buzzed. A text from Ana Duarte, the nurse two towns over who had agreed to stand as petitioner: **Saw the news. Are you okay? Do you need me to do anything?**

Lena typed back: **Stay reachable. Don't come to town alone. We're seven days out.**

Seven days. She had said it aloud to herself every morning like a dose of medicine. Seven days until the probate motion was filed, until everything she had — the will, the ledger transcription Marisol had recited from fifty-year-old memory, the dental records proving the coffin held sandbags, the photographed pages rescued from the annex fire — went into the public record where it could never be fully buried again. Seven days was nothing. Seven days was a geological age.

The question was what Hale intended to do with them.

* * *

She got her answer that afternoon.

Lena had moved her working files out of her motel room days ago — scattered them, actually, across three locations, because she had learned from the annex fire that paper was only safe when it was everywhere and nowhere at once. But she still kept an office in town, a rented storefront suite on Cordwainer Street with her name stenciled on the glass, mostly because leaving town entirely would look like retreat, and retreat, in a fight like this one, was indistinguishable from surrender.

She was at the window at four o'clock when she saw the first one: a gray F-250, parked across the street with its engine running, positioned so its mirrors caught the front of her building. A man sat behind the wheel. He didn't read, didn't eat, didn't check his phone. He sat the way furniture sits.

At five, the truck was relieved by a black Silverado, and the new driver got out once, leaned against the fender, and ate a sandwich while looking directly at her window. Not at the building. At her window. He held her gaze from across the street for a long moment, then tipped an imaginary hat and got back in the cab.

Lena closed her blinds halfway — enough to work, not enough to hide — and called Rosa.

"You've seen them too?" The girl's voice was tight, fast. Rosa had been staying with her aunt since the detention scare, since Lena had traded a partial ledger page for her release, a transaction that still sat in Lena's chest like a swallowed stone. "There's been a truck on our street since last night. My aunt wants to call the sheriff, but—" She stopped. They both knew what calling the Whitcomb sheriff's line would accomplish, which was nothing, and possibly worse than nothing.

"Don't call anyone," Lena said. "Keep your doors locked. Keep your phone charged. In seven days none of this matters anymore, because everything goes into the record and the record doesn't burn."

"And until then?"

Until then. Lena looked through the gap in her blinds at the black truck, at the man watching her building with the patience of a man being paid by the hour, and understood that this was the message, delivered not in grease pencil but in idle engines: *we know where you sleep, where you work, where the girl lives, where the old woman hides. We can reach all of it. The only reason we haven't is that seven days from now it won't matter — or because we're deciding whether it will.*

"Until then," Lena said, "you trust me. Can you do that?"

A pause. Then, quieter: "Marisol says trust is a debt you pay forward. She says everybody in this town owes her some." A ghost of Rosa's old defiance came back into her voice. "I guess I'm paying hers."

After Lena hung up, she sat for a long time in the half-dark of her borrowed office, listening to the idling engine across the street, and allowed herself — briefly, privately, in the one place no camera could follow — to feel the weight of what she was carrying. The cabin was gone. The annex was gone. Delia was in holding, having taken the fall deliberately, and Lena hadn't been able to stop it. Rosa had been detained once already and would be again if Hale thought it useful. Every person who had helped her was now a target, and every target was a pressure point aimed at her, and the man sending the pressure had gone on television that morning and smiled.

*You can't probate ashes.*

No, Lena thought. But you can transcribe them. Marisol had carried July 1974 in her memory for fifty years — dates, amounts, the phrase *the Duarte matter resolved* — and memory, unlike paper, did not burn. That was the thing Hale had never understood, the thing a man who solved problems with gasoline could not comprehend: the ledger was never the box in the bank or the pages in the annex or the cabin upriver. The ledger was a seventy-nine-year-old woman who had watched everything and refused, for half a century, to forget.

The engine across the street revved once, deliberately, and settled back to its idle.

Lena turned on her desk lamp, opened her laptop, and went back to the motion. Page twelve. The section on continuous concealment. Outside, the dark came down over Crane's Hollow, and the truck's headlights carved two pale blades across her blinds, and somewhere upriver the ashes of a cabin that had waited thirty years for a woman who never needed it cooled slowly in the snowmelt.

At 9:40 that night, her phone rang. Unknown number.

She answered on the third ring, the way she always did now, saying nothing — letting the caller spend their silence first.

This time there was no breathing. No dial tone. Instead, a voice she recognized, low and roughened, pitched carefully beneath the register of any recording device: Tomás.

"They took my truck apart today," he said. "Inspection sticker, brake lights, registration — wrote me three citations in an hour. Next it'll be something they can arrest me for." A pause. "I'm telling you because tomorrow morning, when I don't answer my phone, I need you to know it isn't because I changed sides."

"Tomás—"

"One more thing." His voice dropped further. "Before the fire, somebody was up at that cabin. Days before. There were footprints under the back window, small ones, and a jar with flowers on the sill — wildflowers, the yellow kind that grow down by the creek. Marisol used to pick those for Elena." His breath caught. "Somebody was saying goodbye to that cabin, Ms. Villalobos. And it wasn't the man who burned it."

The line went dead.

Lena lowered the phone and sat in the cone of lamplight, the motion glowing unfinished on her screen, the headlights cutting their pale bars across the blinds, and thought about yellow wildflowers on a windowsill — grief, laid down gently, days before the match was ever struck.

Marisol had known the cabin was going to burn.

Which meant Marisol knew something else, too — something she hadn't yet told her — and with six days left on the clock, Lena intended to find out what.
