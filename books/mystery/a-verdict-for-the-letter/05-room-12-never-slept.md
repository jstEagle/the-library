## 5. Room 12 Never Slept

The Gullwing's archive lived in the basement, behind a door that most guests assumed led to the boiler. It did not. The boiler had been decommissioned in 1987 and replaced by a wall of steel shelving that hummed faintly in the damp, holding thirty years of the hotel's memory in manila folders and green ledger books — occupancy registers, folio cards, deposit slips, the small bureaucratic sediment of every life that had passed through the lobby above.

Kestrel went down on the morning after her interviews with the founding families, carrying a tray of tea she would not drink and a pencil she would.

She had learned long ago that you did not interview people with a pen. A pen invited signatures, commitments, things that could be produced later in a courtroom or a quarrel. A pencil was provisional. A pencil said *I'm only thinking out loud.* People told pencils everything.

She set the tea on a shelf cleared of dust by the simple expedient of never letting dust settle there, pulled the stool from beneath the register shelf, and opened the first ledger: July 1994.

The summer Verity Marsh drowned.

* * *

Reading an old occupancy register is like reading a seismograph of a town's soul. You can see the season breathe. In July 1994 the Gullwing had been full — regatta week, the Vance garden party, the Merrick clambake that ended, as it always ended, with someone's uncle weeping on the terrace. The entries marched down the page in the confident hands of the desk staff of that era: Mrs. Tregowan's upright copperplate, young Kestrel's own mother's smaller, quicker script beside the word *paid* more often than any other clerk's, because her mother had hated debt the way some people hate weather.

Kestrel ran her finger down the column of names. Halloway, party of six, rooms 3 through 8. Vance, party of four. Pell. Merrick. The great houses had taken the hotel over that week the way they took over everything — not by booking it, exactly, but by assuming it was theirs to fill.

And then, at the bottom of the page, dated the fourteenth of July:

**Room 12. Mr. E. Salter. 1 guest. Paid cash.**

Kestrel sat back on the stool.

Room 12 was the corner room on the top floor, the one with the window that looked out over the harbour wall and down into the water. It was the room the brochures called *the lookout*. It was also, in Kestrel's private taxonomy of the building, the room that never slept — not because of ghosts, though Mrs. Tregowan had told guests stories about a sea captain when the tips were good, but because of something stranger: in all her years behind the desk, Kestrel could not remember ever having cleaned it out after a departure and found it truly empty. There was always something. A hairpin. A bus ticket. Once, a child's shoe, single, salt-stiffened, which she had put in lost property and which no one had ever claimed.

She turned the page. August 1994. No Room 12. She flipped forward through autumn, winter, spring. Nothing. Then July 1995:

**Room 12. Mr. D. Penhale. 1 guest. Paid cash.**

Different name. Same room. Same dates — the second week of July, seven nights. Same notation in the margin, in the same small hand: *no service required.*

Kestrel reached for 1996. **Mr. J. Curnow.** 1997: **Mrs. M. Rosevear.** 1998: **Mr. T. Bligh.**

The names changed like weather. The handwriting did not.

It was a narrow, forward-leaning script, economical to the point of secrecy, the letters tall and close together as if they were keeping each other company against the dark. The same hand had written *E. Salter* and *D. Penhale* and *M. Rosevear*, had drawn the same little horizontal tick through the crossbar of every *t*, had made the same distinctive open-bowled *b* that looked almost like a *6*. Thirty years of aliases, and every one of them signed by the same person.

Kestrel pulled ledgers off the shelf until the stool disappeared under her and the tea went cold and forgotten. 2001: **Mr. W. Nance.** 2005: **Ms. A. Trevithick.** 2010: **Mr. S. Old.** 2016: **Mr. P. Grey.** Every July. Second week. Seven nights. Cash. *No service required.* The maid had knocked once on the first morning of each stay and never again; there were standing instructions in the housekeeping file, renewed year upon year in that same hand: *linens changed before arrival, do not enter during occupancy, do not disturb.*

Thirty years. Someone had come to Saltmere every July of Kestrel's working life, taken the room with the window over the water, paid in cash, refused to be seen, and left. And no one — not Mrs. Tregowan, not the night porters, not the seasonal girls who changed the linens — had ever thought it strange enough to mention, because the booking was always made properly, always paid, always quiet. The perfect guest. The guest who was a hole in the shape of a person.

Kestrel knew what Ivo Marsh would say. He would say: *someone was watching.* Someone had stood at that window every July for three decades, looking down at the harbour mouth, at the black slot of the cave entrance below the cliff, waiting for something. Counting, perhaps. Three decades is ten years short of a full turn of whatever clock this town kept. But someone had started watching the year Verity died, and had never stopped.

She wrote the initials and dates in her notebook — the real one, the one that lived in her head and nowhere else — and then she did the thing she was actually good at, the thing that had made her the concierge she was instead of merely a woman with a desk. She stopped looking at the names and looked at the hand.

Handwriting is a face you can't shave. People disguise their names; they rarely disguise their letters. And this hand was not disguised so much as disciplined — the writing of someone who had taught themselves to write small and fast and unremarkably, the way a person learns to walk quietly. Except in one place. On the oldest pages, 1994 and 1995, before the habit had fully set, the discipline slipped. The *y* in *Rosevear* carried a tail that looped back under itself, a flourish too pretty for the rest of the hand, a leftover from whoever this person had been before they became nobody.

Kestrel stared at that looping *y* for a long time.

There was a feeling gathering in her chest, low and cold, like water finding a crack in a wall. Not recognition, exactly. Recognition would have been worse and she knew it, because some part of her mind was already reaching toward the feeling, testing it, the way a tongue finds the socket of a lost tooth. She had seen this handwriting before. Somewhere safe. Somewhere so thoroughly filed away in her own past that the filing system itself refused to produce it.

She did what she always did when her own memory became a locked room. She went to work on someone else's.

* * *

At noon she climbed to the third floor with the housekeeping master key and let herself into Room 12.

Unoccupied since last July. The bed was made with the hospital corners she insisted on; the curtains hung straight; the air held the faint mineral smell the old plaster gave off in summer, like a stone warmed by sun. She crossed to the window.

From here the harbour was a painting: the masts, the crab boats, the seawall, and beyond it the shingle beach running down to the cliffs where the tidal caves opened their mouths twice a day and swallowed the sea. From here, with good glass or patient eyes, you could watch the caves the way you'd watch a door.

On the sill, in the corner where the paint had blistered, someone had scratched a mark. Small. Old. Kestrel bent close. It was a tally — five strokes and a diagonal, the way you count to six — scratched and re-scratched until the paint was gouged to bare wood, then painted over, then scratched again. Six. Over and over, six.

Five came out. That was what Mrs. Pell had said, in her drawing room full of orchids and euphemism: *six children went in, five came out.* But the window said six too, insisted on it, counted it thirty times over.

Kestrel photographed nothing. Photographs could be seized, subpoenaed, seen. She drew the mark in her notebook instead, and below it she wrote the only question that mattered: *who counts to six for thirty years?*

* * *

In the afternoon she did something she had done perhaps five times in her career, and never comfortably: she went into the personal files. Not the guests'. Her own.

Every concierge who lasts keeps a private archive, and Kestrel's was the best in the county, though its existence was known to no one. Decades of confessions, filed A to Z. Adulterers under A, thieves under T, the man who poisoned his brother's dogs under P for poison and cross-filed under D for dogs. It was not blackmail material. She had never used it that way, not once. It was simply the truth, kept somewhere, because a town like Saltmere would drown the truth if you left it lying on the beach.

But there was a section of the basement shelves that was not alphabetical. A single box, unlabelled, containing the papers that had come to her eleven years ago in a solicitor's envelope after her mother's death: a marriage certificate, a prayer book, a ration-book-era recipe card for saffron buns, a bundle of household accounts kept in a school exercise book, and a photograph of a woman on the harbour steps squinting into the sun, laughing at whoever held the camera.

Her mother had been dead eleven years and Kestrel still could not say the word *died* about her without hearing the coroner's flat voice underneath it: *misadventure. Found at the base of the east cliff. No suspicious circumstances.* Her mother who walked that cliff path every evening of her life. Her mother whose death had been, like Verity Marsh's drowning, closed quickly and cleanly by the town, filed and forgotten, the tide taking what the tide takes.

Kestrel opened the exercise book of household accounts.

Her mother's hand flowed up at her off the page — *flour, 38p; bootlaces; church envelope* — quick, narrow, forward-leaning, the letters tall and pressed close together, the crossbars of the *t*s struck with a small horizontal tick, the *b*s open-bowled like little sixes.

The world went very quiet, the way it does underwater.

No. No, that wasn't — everyone's handwriting from that generation looked like that, schoolroom copperplate, they'd all been beaten into the same slants and loops, it proved nothing, it was coincidence, it was —

It was the *y*. The tail of the *y* looping back under itself, too pretty for the rest of the hand, the leftover flourish of a girl who'd once written love letters to a fisherman's son. There on the account page, in *yesterday*: the identical loop. Stroke for stroke. Tick for tick. The hand that had booked Room 12 every July for thirty years was the hand that had totted up flour and bootlaces at the kitchen table while Kestrel did her homework across from her.

Her mother had been the guest. Her mother had taken the corner room with the window over the water, every July, under a different name each year, and watched the caves.

Kestrel set the exercise book down very carefully, as if it were sleeping.

The cold feeling in her chest cracked open, and what came through it was not understanding but memory — real memory, finally surfacing, the way drowned things surface when the tide is right. She was small. Four, maybe five. A July evening, the light going bronze. Her mother dressing her in a coat too warm for the season, saying *we're going to see Auntie Vera,* which was what her mother said when she didn't want to say where they were really going. Standing in this building — she knew it was this building, knew it by the smell of the lobby, beeswax and brine — while her mother spoke to someone behind a desk, low and urgent, and the someone said, *you can't keep doing this, Enid,* and her mother said, *I have to know they haven't touched her. I have to look at the water every year or I'll go mad.*

*Touched her.* Whom? Her? Little Kestrel, eating a biscuit on the lobby sofa with her shoes swinging?

Or the other girl. The one from the caves. The one whose brother had just checked into the Gullwing and paid a year in advance and asked her, with his hungry grey eyes, to prove a murder that hadn't happened yet.

Kestrel sat in the basement among the humming shelves with her mother's account book open in front of her, and understood, with the particular horror of a professional recognizing amateur work, that she had been investigating a mystery for two weeks without noticing that the mystery had been investigating her. Ivo Marsh had not chosen her despite her being nobody. He had chosen her because of who her mother was. Because Eastwick blood — maid's blood, invisible blood — was somehow a key. Because the sixth child who went into the caves in 1994 and came out again, the one even Mrs. Pell had spoken of only as *the hotel maid's daughter*, had grown up to become the one person in Saltmere who noticed everything and was noticed by no one.

Perfect cover. Perfect witness. Chosen before she was born, almost.

She closed the exercise book. Her hands were steady — she made them be steady; steadiness was a skill like any other — and she filed her mother's box back on its shelf, and she stood for a moment in the dark with the tea gone completely cold beside her.

Then she took out her notebook and wrote, in her own careful hand, a new heading. Not a name. Not yet. Just the fact, plain as a ledger line:

*Room 12 was never booked by strangers. It was booked by my mother. Every July. Until the July she died.*

Eleven years ago. She checked the register to be sure, though she already knew. The bookings stopped in the summer her mother fell from the east cliff. The year the watcher stopped watching, the watcher died.

Which meant one of two things, and both of them were unbearable: either her mother's death had been exactly what the coroner called it, misadventure, grief or guilt or the long weight of thirty Julys finally pulling her off the cliff path —

— or someone had decided that the woman who counted to six at the window every year had counted one time too many.

Kestrel blew out the basement bulb, climbed the stairs, and stood in the lobby with the evening guests murmuring around her like water around a stone. Behind the front desk, in the wall by the pigeonholes, there was a panel she had never had reason to open, a cavity the builders had left for pipes and the hotel had used for nothing, as far as she knew, in fifty years.

As far as she knew.

She looked at it for a long moment, her mother's looping *y* burning behind her eyes, and reached for the edge of the panel.
