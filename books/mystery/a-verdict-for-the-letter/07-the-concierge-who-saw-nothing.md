## 7. The Concierge Who Saw Nothing

There was a correct way to receive bad news, and there was a correct way to receive good news, and in both cases the procedure was identical: you thanked the person for telling you, you expressed a suitable sentiment, and you did not, under any circumstances, let your face become a room other people could walk into.

Edith Pell had learned this at her mother's knee, which was to say she had learned it at the age of six, standing on a chair so that she could reach the basin, while her mother explained — patiently, the way one explains long division — that a lady's face was like the front hall of a great house. Guests might be admitted. Servants might be admitted. But no one, ever, was admitted past the hall without an invitation, and grief, Edith's mother had said, rinsing the china with hands that did not tremble, grief is the worst sort of guest. It arrives uninvited and it rearranges the furniture.

Edith Pell was eighty-one years old now, and her front hall had been swept every morning of her life.

She was sweeping it this morning too, metaphorically speaking, though the actual activity was tea. She made it properly — water off the boil but not boiling, four minutes exactly, the pot warmed first — because the ritual of it steadied her, and because she required steadying. The girl from the Gullwing had telephoned the evening before. Kestrel Eastwick. A name Edith had turned over in the dark like a coin whose date she could not quite read. The concierge wished to call on her, regarding what she called "some old hotel records," and would eleven o'clock suit?

Eleven o'clock suited. Eleven o'clock always suited. That was rather the point of being the sort of woman to whom eleven o'clock might suit.

* * *

The Gullwing girl arrived at two minutes past, which Edith noted and forgave — the tide road floods early these days, and young people drove as though the sea were a rumor. She was a slight creature, dark-haired, quiet-footed, with the particular stillness of staff who have learned to stand in doorways without occupying them. Edith recognized the quality instantly. She had been surrounded by such people all her life: housemaids, chauffeurs, secretaries, all of them gifted in the art of being present and invisible at once. It was a skill Edith had never needed to acquire, having been born on the other side of the doorway, and she had spent most of her life not thinking about that fact, which is its own kind of labor.

"Mrs. Pell. Thank you for seeing me." The girl — Kestrel — did not offer her hand until Edith extended hers, which showed breeding, or training; the distinction had blurred over the decades and Edith had stopped pretending it mattered. "I won't take much of your time."

"You'll take tea first," Edith said. "One cannot interrogate a person on an empty pot."

Something flickered across the girl's face — surprise, quickly filed away. She sat where she was pointed, on the edge of the settee, spine straight, hands folded. A maid's posture, Edith thought, and then wondered why she had thought it, and then decided not to pursue the thought, in the way one decides not to pursue a smell of gas.

"I'm researching the summer of 1994," Kestrel said, when the tea had been poured and approved. "For Mr. Marsh. His sister died that July, below the caves at Saltmere Point. Verity Marsh."

"Yes," Edith said. "A sad business."

She said it the way she said *how kind of you to come* and *the weather is turning* — a phrase kept polished in the drawer with the others, brought out on occasion, put back clean.

"A sad business," Kestrel repeated. Not mocking. Testing. "You knew her?"

"I knew of her. The Marsh family kept to themselves. The father was — well. He drank, I believe, though one didn't say so. And the children ran wild along the shore all summer, as children do." Edith sipped. "I was not, you understand, closely acquainted with the family. My involvement was limited to — " she searched for the word, and found it waiting, as words always waited for her — "hospitality."

"Hospitality," Kestrel said.

"The town hosted a regatta that summer. The last one, as it happened. There was a dinner at the Merricks'. A dance. The children were everywhere, underfoot, delighted. It was a good summer, before it wasn't." Edith set down her cup with a small click, precise as punctuation. "You must understand, Miss Eastwick, that when something dreadful happens to a child, the whole town becomes a kind of — bereaved household. Everyone grieves. Everyone helps. One does what one can."

"What did you do?"

The question was impertinent, and Edith admired it, and hated it, in roughly equal measure.

"I wrote letters," she said. "To the family. To the coroner's office, offering any assistance. I organized the flowers. Someone must organize the flowers, or they arrive in a dispiriting jumble, chrysanthemums and plastic ribbon, and the whole occasion looks cheap instead of tragic. Tragedy has standards, Miss Eastwick. That is what nobody understands about tragedy. It requires management."

"And after? After the verdict?"

"After the verdict, one lets people heal." Edith smiled, and was aware of smiling, and let it happen anyway, because a smile properly deployed ends more conversations than it begins. "That is what the verdict is *for*, my dear. A closed verdict is a kind of mercy. It allows everyone to stop asking questions that have no kind answers."

The girl did not stop. Edith watched her decide not to stop — saw it happen behind her eyes, some small internal door opening onto a corridor Edith preferred not to imagine.

"Six children went into the caves that night," Kestrel said quietly. "Didn't they."

The clock on the mantel ticked. Outside, a gull said something ugly and wheeled away.

"I couldn't speak to that," Edith said. "I wasn't there."

"No," Kestrel agreed. "But you counted them afterward. At the Merricks' dinner. You were the one who noticed."

And here is the thing about a lifetime of composure: it holds, it holds, it holds — and then one morning a stranger sets a teacup down wrong, half an inch from the saucer's center, and your hand wants to reach out and correct it, and correcting it means admitting you noticed, and noticing is the whole confession, isn't it, the entire architecture of guilt built on the foundation of *I saw*.

Edith reached out and centered the cup. Her hand did not tremble. She was rather proud of that, later, in the way one is proud of a wall that held during a flood, even knowing the flood was coming from inside the house.

"Children are difficult to count," she said. "They move. They double back. They hide from their elders precisely when counting matters. On the night in question there was a great deal of movement — the storm coming up, the parents calling from the cliff path, lanterns going back and forth. Five children came up off the shingle when the alarm was raised. Wet, frightened, half-drowned themselves, poor lambs. Edmund Vance had to be carried. And the Marsh girl did not come up at all." She paused. "If there was confusion earlier in the evening about numbers — if someone believed six had gone down and five came up — well. Confusion is the sea's native language. It speaks nothing else."

"Who believed six?" Kestrel asked. "Who counted six?"

Edith looked at her then, really looked, past the concierge's uniform of plainness, past the careful stillness, and saw — or thought she saw, and the thought was a cold key turning somewhere below stairs — a child's face. Four years old, perhaps five. Dark-haired. Standing in a doorway.

"You have your mother's patience," Edith said, before she could stop herself.

The girl went very still. Stillness, in Edith's long experience, was what people did instead of screaming.

"My mother was a chambermaid at the Gullwing," Kestrel said. "Ruth Eastwick. You'd have known her, I suppose. Everyone knew the staff."

"One knows the staff," Edith agreed, "in the way one knows the wallpaper. Fondly. Without attention." She rose, because rising ended interviews; it had ended interviews for forty years. "Miss Eastwick, I will tell you what I told the coroner, thirty years ago, and what I have told no one since, because it is not the sort of thing one tells anyone. That night, at the dinner, Ruth Eastwick came to me in a state I can only describe as — undone. She was a sensible girl, Ruth. Sensible girls are not undone by weather. She said her daughter had gone down to the caves with the others. She said — " and here Edith's voice performed the smallest hesitation, a hesitation she had rehearsed for three decades without ever once performing it aloud — "she said she had counted them herself, from the kitchen window. Six silhouettes on the shingle. And when they came back up, five."

The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs a window had been left open against instructions, and the curtain breathed in and out like something sleeping.

"And you told the coroner this," Kestrel said. It was not a question, and it was not quite an accusation, and Edith understood that the girl already knew the answer, and was making her say it, and despised her a little for the elegance of it.

"I told the coroner that Mrs. Eastwick was distraught," Edith said, "and that distraught persons miscount. I told him that the official number was five, that five was consistent with the testimony of the surviving children, all of whom — independently, unprompted — recalled five. I told him that suggesting otherwise to grieving families would be cruelty without purpose." She folded her hands. "He agreed. Judges usually agree, when one gives them permission to."

"Judge Halloway."

"The same. He was very kind about it. He said, *Edith, the town needs this to be simple,* and he was right. It did need to be simple. Towns are like houses, Miss Eastwick. They stand only if the load-bearing walls stay where you put them." She moved toward the door, unhurried, a woman concluding a pleasant call. "Verity Marsh drowned. It was a tragedy. The sea took her, as the sea takes things, without malice and without witnesses worth the name. If a tired maid miscounted shadows on a beach in a rising wind, that is not a conspiracy. It is arithmetic failing under pressure, which arithmetic does."

"Then why," Kestrel said, also rising, also unhurried, "are you telling me about the sixth silhouette at all?"

Because, Edith thought, because you have your mother's face and your mother's patience and your mother's way of standing in a doorway as though the doorway belongs to you; because I am eighty-one years old and I have swept this hall every morning for thirty years and lately, in the night, I find the sweepings will not stay swept; because Ruth Eastwick came to me twice more before she died, and the second time I did not open the door, and the third time there was no door to open, because Ruth Eastwick was dead in a manner the town found convenient and I found — 

— and there she stopped, at the threshold of her own mind, and stepped back, and closed it.

"Because you asked politely," she said aloud, "and I am old, and old women ramble. Good day, Miss Eastwick. Do give Mr. Marsh my condolences. Thirty years late, but sincere."

* * *

Afterward she stood at the window and watched the girl walk down the drive, small and straight-backed, pausing once at the gate to look back at the house — not at Edith, Edith was sure of it, at the *house*, as though reading it — and then go on toward the village, and the sea beyond it, gray and patient as an unpaid debt.

Edith went to her writing desk. She did not write anything. She simply opened the middle drawer, where she kept the correspondence that was not correspondence: the sympathy cards from 1994, the coroner's courteous note, the single sheet in Ruth Eastwick's round, uneducated hand that Edith had received the week before the maid's death and had never answered, and had never destroyed, because destroying things required deciding they were disposable, and Edith had never been able to decide that.

*You know what they did,* the letter said. Or rather, it began that way, and Edith had never managed to read past the beginning, all those years, the way one does not step on the loose stair.

She read past the beginning now.

Outside, the tide was coming in over the flats, covering the cave mouths one by one, and Edith Pell sat in her neat bright room with her mother's rules dissolving around her like sugar in warm water, and understood — finally, fully, with the clarity she had spent a lifetime mistaking for virtue — that she had not failed to see the truth in 1994.

She had employed it.
