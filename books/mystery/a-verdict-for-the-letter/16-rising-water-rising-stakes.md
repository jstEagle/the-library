## 16. Rising Water, Rising Stakes

The tide tables did not lie. Ivo had checked them three times that morning, once against the almanac in the Gullwing's back office and twice against the battered brass instrument the harbourmaster kept bolted to the seawall, as though Saltmere itself might try to argue with arithmetic. The equinox was nine days out. On the twenty-first of September, at 4:17 in the afternoon, the sea would climb higher than it had climbed since the last ceremony — higher than it had climbed on the night Verity went into the water.

He stood on the shingle below the cave mouth with his trouser cuffs darkened by spray and felt the old familiar pull in his chest, the one that was not quite grief and not quite hunger. Thirty years ago he had been dragged from this same stretch of beach, coughing salt, nine years old, while men in oilskins shouted his name and no one shouted hers. He remembered the cold. He remembered the light going strange above him, green and wavering, like looking up through a bottle. What he did not remember — what he had spent a year of lodging fees and a lifetime of sleepless nights trying to recover — was the sound of his sister's voice in the dark before the water took her.

*You know more than you told her.* That was what Kestrel Eastwick would say if she were standing here. She had said something very like it two nights ago, in the hotel bar, over a glass of nothing because she didn't drink on duty. *You hired me to prove a murder hasn't happened yet, Mr. Marsh. Nobody hires a concierge for that unless they already believe it will.*

She was right, of course. She was right the way a blade is right.

Ivo crouched and pressed his palm flat to the wet stone at the cave mouth. The chalk marks were still there — five vertical strokes, four of them crossed through with names in a hand too careful to be hurried: VANCE. MERRICK. PELL. HALLOWAY. And beneath them, the fifth stroke, uncrossed, unmarked, waiting. He had found them eleven days ago along with the wax-sealed envelope, and every day since he had come back to look at them the way a man looks at a diagnosis he is not yet ready to read aloud.

The tide was coming in behind him. It always came in behind him here; there was no other direction for it to come from. He watched a wave slide up the shingle, gather the small stones into its grip, and let them go with a rattle like distant applause.

Nine days.

He turned and walked back toward the town, and as he climbed the coast road he saw what he had been seeing all week: Saltmere preparing itself the way a body prepares for fever. The Vance house had scaffolding on its seaward face — ostensibly repairs, but the workmen arrived at dawn and left by noon, and Ivo had watched Edmund Vance himself carry a long wooden crate from the boathouse to the cellar steps, moving with the furtive energy of a man doing penance in advance. At Merrick Hall, the gates that had rusted open for a decade now swung shut behind a new padlock. Old Mrs. Pell had taken to sitting in her front window every evening between six and seven, perfectly still, hands folded, like a woman keeping an appointment she could not cancel.

And Judge Halloway — Halloway had begun attending church again.

That was the detail that frightened Ivo most. A man who believed in nothing did not return to St. Brendan's after thirty years of absence unless he wanted something from whatever might be listening.

* * *

He reached the Gullwing just after four, coming through the service entrance because the front steps had become a stage he preferred not to stand on. Kestrel was in the back office, and the sight of her stopped him in the doorway longer than he intended.

She had the ledgers out. All of them — the full run of the hotel's records going back past her mother's time, stacked in towers on the desk with slips of paper protruding from them like the spines of some sleeping animal. Her reading glasses were pushed up into her hair. There was ink on the side of her right hand where she'd braced it against the page. She looked, he thought, like exactly what she was: the most dangerous person in Saltmere, disguised as the person nobody saw.

"You're dripping on my carpet," she said, without looking up.

"It's not your carpet."

"It will be in nine days if Halloway has his way." She pulled off the glasses and finally looked at him, and he saw the thing in her face that had been growing there for a week — a kind of bruised clarity. "I've been reading marginalia, Mr. Marsh. My mother wrote in the margins of these ledgers like other people write diaries. Weather notes. Guest notes. Little codes." She tapped the top ledger. "And in July of 1994, the handwriting changes. It gets smaller. Faster. Like someone writing with one eye on the door."

"What does it say?"

"That's the interesting question." She stood, and he noticed how tired she was — not the tiredness of late nights but of something heavier, carried for weeks. "It says almost nothing. Which is what it says when someone is being careful. But there's a sequence of numbers repeated down the margin of three separate pages, and I finally worked out they're shelf references. Locations in this room." She gestured at the office, at the walls of shelving holding forty years of the hotel's memory. "Whatever she hid, she hid it here. In the building. Under everyone's nose, filed like everything else."

"Have you found it?"

"Not yet." Something moved across her face — quickly, but not quickly enough. "There's a section of shelving behind the guest register case where the wood doesn't match. Newer timber, older varnish. Someone's been at it."

Ivo felt the floor tilt slightly under him, the way it did when the truth got close enough to have gravity. "Kestrel—"

"Don't." She held up the ink-stained hand. "Don't tell me you didn't know about my mother. Don't tell me you chose this hotel at random. You paid twelve months in advance for a room you barely sleep in, and you asked me to prove a murder hasn't happened yet, and you knew — you *knew* — whose daughter works the front desk." Her voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "I'm not asking you to confess. I'm telling you I've worked it out, so we can stop pretending the pretense matters."

He should have lied. Some part of him, the part that had survived the water at nine years old, argued for the lie with real force. But he looked at her standing among her mother's ledgers with her mother's ink on her hand, and he heard himself say:

"I knew the maid survived. I didn't know she was your mother until I walked into this lobby and saw you file a key behind the desk with your left hand crossed over your right, exactly the way Verity used to do it when she thought no one was watching."

Kestrel went very still.

"She learned it somewhere," Ivo said. "People learn their hands from people who love them. I've been watching for that gesture for thirty years in every woman under fifty who ever touched a hotel counter." He swallowed. "You were four years old, Kestrel. You were the sixth child in those caves. Whatever happened that night, you were there, and you came out, and you don't remember — or you remember and you've spent your whole life filing it away alphabetically like everything else."

For a moment neither of them spoke. Somewhere upstairs a vacuum cleaner droned; a guest laughed at something on a telephone. The ordinary sounds of the Gullwing, which had always sounded to Ivo like safety, now sounded like a held breath.

"The fifth name," Kestrel said at last. Her voice had gone quiet and precise, the voice she used for delivering bad news to families. "On the chalk. The blank line."

"Yes."

"It's mine."

"It's yours," he said. "The ritual needs the one who escaped. That's why the envelope was sealed and dated. That's why they've waited thirty years. They don't need a victim, Kestrel. They need a *return*."

* * *

They were interrupted at half past five, and later Ivo would understand that the interruption was not accidental, because nothing in Saltmere was ever accidental.

The knock on the front door was formal — three measured strikes, evenly spaced. Through the lobby glass Ivo saw Judge Ambrose Halloway standing on the steps in a grey suit, holding a document wallet, flanked by a young solicitor Ivo didn't recognize who had the particular blankness of a man being well paid not to think.

Kestrel opened the door herself. She had put her glasses back on and smoothed her expression into perfect professional neutrality, and Ivo, watching from the office doorway, admired it even as it broke his heart: thirty years of invisibility deployed like armor.

"Judge Halloway. We're not expecting you."

"No," Halloway agreed pleasantly. "One rarely expects the law. May I?" He did not wait for permission. He stepped into the lobby, and the young solicitor followed, and the door closed behind them with a click that sounded, in the quiet of the Gullwing, like a lock engaging.

Halloway was seventy-three years old and carried it the way he carried everything — lightly, expensively, with the confidence of a man who had never once lost an argument he cared about. His hair was silver and immaculate. His eyes, when they found Ivo in the office doorway, registered him with mild, dismissive interest, the way one registers furniture.

"Miss Eastwick." He produced the documents from the wallet with a small flourish. "I regret the necessity. This concerns the tenancy of the property known as the Gullwing Hotel."

"This property has been in my family for forty years," Kestrel said.

"And therein lies the difficulty." Halloway handed her the papers. "You'll find the ownership chain documented. Upon the death of your mother — my condolences, belatedly — the freehold passed into trust, administered by the estate's executors. I hold proxy for two of the three. The trust has elected to exercise its right of repossession, citing — " he consulted a page with distaste, as though the law itself had disappointed him — "structural deficiencies, insurance non-compliance, and failure to maintain habitable standards. The notice period, I'm afraid, expired Tuesday."

"Expired Tuesday," Kestrel repeated. "This notice is dated August the second."

"The notice was served on August the second," Halloway corrected gently. "By registered post. To this address. Perhaps it was misfiled."

Ivo saw it land — saw Kestrel understand, in the space of a heartbeat, what was being done to her. The notice had never existed until this morning. It had been drafted last night, backdated with elegant legality, witnessed by a solicitor too young and too well-paid to ask questions, and mailed to a hotel whose post was sorted by the very woman it was designed to dispossess. Any letter arriving in early August would have passed across her own desk. She would have filed it herself. If she had found it, it would have looked like her own negligence.

The machinery didn't need to break the law. It only needed to be the law.

"You can't do this," Kestrel said. Not a protest — an assessment. Testing the sentence for weight.

"I can and I have," Halloway said. "You'll have fourteen days to vacate, per the trust's discretion, though I'm empowered to extend it should circumstances warrant." He smiled, and it was almost warm. "Circumstances often warrant, Miss Eastwick, for those who conduct themselves reasonably. Saltmere takes care of its own. It always has." His gaze drifted, deliberately, to the office doorway. "Mr. Marsh. You've been with us nearly a year, haven't you? A long visit. I hope the town has been — hospitable."

"Hospitable enough," Ivo said.

"Mm. Do give my regards to your family's memory. Verity was a lovely girl. Terrible shame about the verdict — accidental drowning, the coroner was quite thorough." He settled his hat. "Well. I'll leave you to your packing, such as it is. Fourteen days, Miss Eastwick. Though I'd take it as a personal kindness if the premises were left tidy. Ledgers especially. Hotels accumulate so much *paper*."

The word landed like a scalpel. *Ledgers.* He knew. Of course he knew — he had known since the chalk marks appeared, since the envelope was sealed, perhaps since 1994 itself. The eviction wasn't about the hotel. It was about the shelves in the back office, the mismatched timber behind the register case, the marginalia in a dead woman's hand. They meant to take the building apart before the tide came in, board by board, page by page, and bury whatever they found in whatever landfill received the possessions of inconvenient women.

Halloway paused at the door, one hand on the frame, and turned back with the air of a man remembering a courtesy.

"Oh — one more thing. The caves below the point are closed to the public as of Monday. Coastal erosion. Safety assessment. All quite routine." He smiled at Kestrel. "You'll want to keep your guests away from the water, Miss Eastwick. The sea can be terribly possessive of what it's owed."

Then he was gone, and the solicitor with him, and the door clicked shut, and the lobby was so quiet Ivo could hear the grandfather clock in the hall counting off seconds that suddenly felt rationed.

Kestrel stood holding the papers. She read them once, all the way through, with the terrible patience of a professional — and then she set them on the front desk, squared their edges precisely, and filed them, Ivo realized with a chill, under *H*.

"He signed it," she said. "His actual signature. On a backdated instrument. That's forgery, fraud, and abuse of office in one document." She looked up, and her eyes were bright and hard and more alive than he had ever seen them. "He couldn't help himself. He had to make it *legal*. Even now. Even with nine days left, he needed the paperwork to be beautiful."

"Kestrel—"

"They're going to demolish the office," she said. "Or 'renovate' it. Whatever they call it. They'll strip the shelving, burn the ledgers, and by the time anyone asks questions, the Gullwing will be a boutique development with a plaque about local heritage." She pulled the reading glasses from her hair and folded them slowly. "Which means I have fourteen days to find what my mother hid behind that wall — except they'll move before then. Halloway doesn't leave things to chance. He'll send the workmen the moment the tide tables say the water's high enough to cover the noise."

Ivo came out of the office doorway at last and stood beside her at the desk, close enough to see the ink still staining the side of her hand.

"Then we go tonight," he said. "Low water's at eleven-forty. The wall first — your mother's hiding place. And then the caves, before they're 'closed for erosion.'" He hesitated, and then gave her the last piece of honesty he had left. "Kestrel — the envelope I found. The instructions. There's a line I haven't told you. It says the fifth name must arrive *willingly*, and it says the witness must be present to see it done. Their word. Witness."

Kestrel was silent for a long moment. Outside, the light was failing over the harbour, and the first lamps of Saltmere were coming on one by one, gentle and golden, a town tucking itself in around its secrets.

"Witness," she repeated. Then, quietly, almost to herself: "My mother wrote in the margin of the 1994 ledger — the last thing she ever wrote there. One word. I thought it was a date." She looked up at him. "It was *witness*. She knew they'd need one. She knew they'd come back for her daughter."

The clock struck six. Down in the town, unseen, the tide had already turned, and the water was beginning its long patient climb toward the caves.

"We open the wall tonight," Kestrel said.
