## 4. Three Families, One Roof

The rain came to Bellhaven the way gossip did—sideways, persistent, and finding every crack in a person's defenses. Marguerite drove through it with her recorder on the passenger seat like a sleeping animal she didn't quite trust, past the cannery ruins with their ribs of rusted steel, past the church whose steeple had leaned two degrees off true for as long as anyone could remember and which the parishioners had long ago decided was a matter of character rather than engineering.

Three interviews today. Three families. She had spent the night before laying their names out on her kitchen table like tarot cards: Reeve, Halloran—no, not Halloran—the deacon's line, the Vances. Thirty years of intermarriage and godparentage and shared casseroles at shared funerals, until the three family trees had grown together into something less like a forest than a single root system, one organism wearing three crowns.

She had learned, in seven years of this work, that towns don't keep secrets. Towns keep *alibis*. And alibis, unlike secrets, are always built by committee.

* * *

The Reeve cousins received her in the house on Pilchard Lane where Thomas had lived until the night he pedaled his bike toward evening Mass and became a story instead of a boy. The house smelled of beeswax and old carpet and, beneath both, the faint mineral cold of a home that had been closed to children for a generation.

Dorothy Reeve-Pike poured tea she clearly didn't expect to be drunk. Her cousin Gerald sat by the window with his hands folded, a man shaped by decades of sitting very still in pews.

"You're the podcast woman," Dorothy said. Not unkindly. The way one identifies weather.

"I'm the podcast woman," Marguerite agreed, and let the recorder's red light be seen, because honesty about the machine bought honesty from the mouth. Sometimes.

"Thomas would be forty-three," Gerald said to the window. "I do the arithmetic every year. It's a habit. Like winding a clock."

"What do you remember about that night?"

What followed was, word for word, nearly what she'd been told by the second cousin in 2019, and by the grandmother before that. Thomas left at six-forty. He was excited—he'd been chosen to carry the processional cross. The fog was coming in off the water. They assumed he'd gone straight to the church, because Thomas always went straight to the church.

Every detail polished smooth as sea glass. That was the thing about rehearsed memory: it lost its edges. Real recollection was jagged, full of irrelevant debris—a dog barking, a wrong number, the taste of dinner. This account had no debris. It had been swept.

"And you never doubted he went directly there?"

Dorothy's teacup paused an inch above its saucer. A pause so small most people would have missed it. Marguerite had built a career on missing nothing.

"He was a good boy," Dorothy said. "Good boys go where they say they're going."

"And where they're sent," Gerald added softly, still to the window.

Marguerite waited. The rain ticked against the glass. Neither cousin filled the silence, because neither cousin had ever needed to; thirty years of practice had taught them that silence, held in company, is load-bearing.

At the door, Dorothy pressed a foil-wrapped slice of lemon loaf into her hands—an old ritual, hospitality as border control—and said, "You know, the Vances were very good to us, after. Very good. You should talk to them. And to Peter." A beat. "Peter Ashcombe. The deacon's son. He was Thomas's age."

Marguerite smiled and understood she had just been handed her itinerary by people who wanted to make sure she took it in the right order.

* * *

Peter Ashcombe lived eleven miles up the coast in a house that had once been handsome and had stopped trying around the time of his divorce, if the state of the gutters meant anything. He answered the door in a cardigan that had known better decades, and Marguerite smelled the day's drinking on him before she smelled anything else—rye and tonic, the perfume of men who manage their grief or their guilt in measured doses and have begun losing count.

He was charming, in the way of men who had been handsome boys in small towns and never fully retired from the post. He put her in the sunroom where the light flattered no one and told her about Thomas with real warmth, real laughter even—"Tommy could make Father Ambrose laugh during the consecration, just by breathing"—and then, when she asked where *he* had been that night, the warmth shut like a drawer.

"I was home sick."

"Sick how?"

"Sick the way twelve-year-olds get sick when they want to skip altar practice." He smiled. The smile didn't reach anywhere near his eyes. "My father vouched for me. Deacons don't lie, Ms.—Halloran, is it? So you can take that to the bank."

There it was again. The tidy edge. The alibi with no debris clinging to it. Sick, vouched, banked. Filed and cross-filed.

She asked him about the sexton. Aldous Wren. The handyman who rang the bells and fixed the boilers and, in Marguerite's own childhood, had carried her on his shoulders through the Fourth of July fair while her mother laughed from a folding chair.

Something moved through Peter's face then—not guilt exactly, but its fossil. The shape guilt leaves in stone.

"Aldous," he said, and refilled his glass with a hand that was almost steady. "Now there's a man who stayed. Everyone else got out or got buried, and Aldous just... stayed. Thirty years ringing the same bells over the same hole."

"What hole?"

"The hole under everything, obviously." He laughed, and the laugh had a crack down the middle of it. "That's what Bellhaven is, Ms. Halloran. A roof. One roof. The Reeves are shingles, the Vances are shingles, my father was the ridge beam. You put enough shingles over a thing and pretty soon nobody remembers there was ever anything underneath. You just remember the roof. You love the roof. You'd die for the roof."

His voice had risen. He noticed, and lowered it, and looked at his glass as if it had betrayed him.

"Your father," Marguerite said carefully. "Deacon Ashcombe. What did he do, after Thomas disappeared?"

Peter was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the rain thickened, drumming on the sunroom glass like fingers testing every pane for weakness.

"My father," he said at last, slowly, each word set down like a man crossing ice, "took care of the sexton's problem."

The room went very still. Marguerite kept her face pleasant and empty, a held breath made flesh.

"What problem was that?"

But Peter had heard himself. She watched it land—the words arriving back in his own ears a half-second too late, the way a dropped plate announces itself only after the sound. His whole body performed a small, terrible recalibration. He set down the glass. He stood. He became, with visible effort, the sober man he had not been for hours.

"That was a figure of speech," he said. "Parish talk. My father handled problems. Leaky roofs. Broken boilers. Sextons who talked too much after a few beers at the Legion hall." He walked her to the door with terrible courtesy. "Aldous Wren drank, you know. Still drinks, I hear. Men who drink say things. Men like my father cleaned up the things they said. That's all. That's all it was."

"Mr. Ashcombe—"

"Thank you for coming," Peter Ashcombe said, and closed the door with the gentleness of a man lowering a coffin lid.

* * *

The Vance Funeral Home occupied a gray shingled building on Harbor Street, and Marguerite arrived as the light was failing, the harbor going the color of pewter. Marion Vance III—third of his name, keeper of the town's dead for fifty years combined across generations—met her in the arrangement room among the velvet chairs and the tasteful lamps designed to make grief feel upholstered.

Of the three interviews, this one frightened her most, because Marion was not drunk and not grieving and not old enough to hide behind foggy memory. He was sixty, precise, and kind in the way of men who handle other people's worst days professionally.

"Thomas Reeve," he said, when she raised the name. "Yes. We didn't have him. No body, no service. There was a memorial, of course. My father did it. Beautiful service. Packed house." He shook his head slowly. "Bellhaven turned out for that boy like he was a drowned fisherman. Whole town. Whole roof."

"You've used that word yourself, then. Roof."

Marion smiled faintly. "Small towns, Ms. Halloran. Everything holds everything else up. You pull one board out to see what's underneath, you find out fast whether you're standing on a floor or a ceiling." He folded his hands. "What is it you want to know?"

"About the night he disappeared. About who came here, who called, who asked questions."

And here Marion Vance did something she had never seen a subject do in seven years of interviews. He answered a question she hadn't asked.

"The phone log from October 1994 doesn't exist anymore," he said. "Paper logs, back then. My father burned them in '95. Spring of '95. I know because I was sixteen and I helped carry the box out to the incinerator, and I asked him why, and he said, 'Because some doors are better shut, and we are in the business of closing them.'" He met her eyes steadily. "I've thought about that sentence every day for thirty years. I'm telling you this because your show has gotten people talking, and talking, in my experience, is how bodies surface. I'd rather hand you the truth in pieces than watch it come up all at once."

"Why did your father burn the logs? Who called?"

"I don't know who called. But I know who came." Marion's voice dropped, and the funeral-home quiet seemed to lean in around them. "The deacon. Ashcombe. Came the next morning, before we'd opened, with his collar on and his hat in his hands, and asked my father what the procedure was for a death without a body. Asked it twice. Like he was checking the answer against something."

"Against what?"

"Against what someone had already told him." Marion stood, ending the interview the way he ended viewings—gently, finally. "One more thing, since we're being honest. Aldous Wren came in that same week. Bought a plot in St. Brigid's yard. Paid cash. For himself, we assumed." He paused at the door. "He was twenty-eight years old, Ms. Halloran. Nobody buys their grave at twenty-eight unless somebody's promised them one."

* * *

She sat in the car outside the funeral home as the streetlights came on, the lemon loaf going stale on the seat beside her, and laid the day out in her mind like evidence on a table.

The Reeves, sweeping their memory clean. Peter Ashcombe, drunk, saying *the sexton's problem* and then drowning it in corrections. Marion Vance, sober, handing her a burned phone log and a grave bought at twenty-eight. Three families, one roof—and beneath the shingles, a shape she could now feel through the boards. A deacon asking procedure for a death without a body. A young sexton purchasing his own burial ground. A problem that had been *taken care of*.

Her phone buzzed against the dashboard. An email, routed through the show's anonymous tip address, no subject line, sent four minutes ago—from someone who apparently knew exactly where she'd spent her afternoon.

*You're asking the right houses,* it read. *Ask the wrong one next. Ask who kept the parish records, and ask what happened to the girl who worked the front office in 1994.*

Marguerite stared at the screen until the words blurred. The girl who worked the front office. She knew that name. She had known it her whole life, sung into lullabies, written on school emergency cards in her mother's careful hand.

Evelyn Yarrow. Her mother.

The rain struck the windshield, patient as a knock at a door, and Marguerite understood that whoever was writing to her wasn't pointing her toward the truth.

They were pointing her toward home.
