## 5. The Client Returns

The money had a smell to it, Ulyana decided on the third morning — not the honest smell of crumpled notes passed over a counter, but something institutional, like the inside of a bank vault or the paper stock of court documents. She'd left the envelope untouched in her cash drawer for two days, telling herself it was evidence, then telling herself it was nothing, then telling herself it was evidence again. On the third morning she took it to Harrow & Finch, the accountants on Cordwainer Lane who handled the bookshop's dismal ledgers, and asked old Finch to trace the cheque that sat beneath the cash.

"A month's rent," she'd said. "Paid by cheque. I want to know whose hand signed it."

Finch had peered at her over his spectacles with the expression of a man who has spent forty years learning not to ask questions. "That's not strictly how it works, Mrs. Eriksen."

"Then work it however it does work. I'll pay for your time."

He'd worked it in a day and a half, and what he brought back was a single photocopied page and a look of mild alarm. The cheque cleared through a company called Meridian Correspondence Services Ltd., registered in an office park outside Ashcombe, an address that turned out to be a mail drop above a shuttered chandlery. The company had one director listed: a solicitor's clerk named T. Rudge, of whom no further record existed anywhere Finch could find. The account had been opened eleven weeks ago with a deposit of exactly four thousand pounds and had touched nothing since except this payment.

"It's a shell," Ulyana said.

"It's a shell," Finch agreed. "A very tidy one. Whoever built it didn't want their name anywhere near it, but they weren't clever enough to be truly invisible. There's a maintenance fee paid quarterly from a second account — personal, at the Marrow's End branch of Coastal Mutual." He tapped the photocopy. "I couldn't get you that account. But I can tell you the branch."

She knew the branch. She'd walked past it every day for three years. And she knew, with the flat certainty that used to serve her in interview rooms, that whoever maintained a shell account through the local Coastal Mutual was someone who lived here, worked here, moved through the parish like anyone else — someone close enough to be careless about geography and confident enough not to care.

* * *

She spent the afternoon behind the till with the parish electoral roll open beside the ledger, doing arithmetic she hated herself for. Cross-referencing was detective work, and she had sworn off detective work the way some people swear off drink — sincerely, repeatedly, and without much conviction. By closing time she had narrowed four thousand pounds and a mail-drop company to seven plausible names, and all seven made her feel slightly ill, because they were neighbours. The widow Prentice, who bought large-print westerns. The harbourmaster. Two of the churchwardens. She put the list away and did not sleep.

In the morning there was a letter waiting under the shop door, typed, unsigned, on the same stationery as the first page. It said only: *You've found the money. Now find me. Ask for Rudge at the Chandlery House, Ashcombe, Thursday, 4 p.m.*

Which meant her client was watching the shop. Which meant her client knew she was looking. Which meant, she thought as she stood in the doorway holding the page with hands that would not quite steady, that she was being managed — drawn along a line like a kite, and someone else holding the string.

She went anyway. Of course she went.

* * *

Chandlery House was a Georgian pile gone grey at the edges, its ground floor still bearing the ghost-lettering of a marine supplier long dead. The door stood ajar. Inside, the hall smelled of dust and cold plaster, and a single voice came from the back room, level and unhurried:

"The door locks behind you, if you'd close it. Old habit. I don't like draughts."

Ulyana closed it. She walked down the corridor with her heart going hard against her ribs, and into a room where a man sat at a trestle table with his back to the window, so that his face was in shadow and the light fell instead on his hands — long, careful, teacher's hands, folded neatly on the wood.

She knew those hands. She had watched them give evidence for six hours across two days, three years ago, in Court One. She had watched them tremble once, only once, when the foreman read the verdict, and she had told herself at the time that the trembling was guilt.

"Elias Vane," she said.

"Mrs. Eriksen." He did not rise. "Please sit down. I imagine you have questions, and I'd rather answer them sitting. Standing confrontations have never gone well for me."

She did not sit. "You hired me. You — the man I—" The sentence wouldn't finish itself. She started again. "You paid me a month's rent to investigate a murder prediction, and you let me believe my client was a stranger."

"You never asked who I was." His voice stayed even, but she heard the effort in it now, the way a floorboard sounds when weight is placed carefully upon it. "You took the money because you wanted to be needed, and I took advantage of that, and I'm sorry for it. But if I'd walked into your shop with my face, you'd have thrown me out. Or worse — you'd have called Sorrel, and Sorrel would have told you I was manipulating you, and you'd have believed him, because he's your partner and I'm your conviction."

"My conviction," she repeated. The words tasted like copper.

"Yes." He finally turned his head, letting the light find him, and she saw what three years had done: the hair gone thin at the temples, the eyes ringed, the mouth set in a line of permanent guardedness. A man who had learned in prison to keep his face still. "Yours. You built the case, Mrs. Eriksen. You interviewed me twice. You found the timeline persuasive. You testified at trial that my account of the evening was inconsistent. You were good at it. That's not an accusation — it's a compliment, and I mean it as one. You were the best witness against me."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere above them a gull cried and was answered.

"Why?" she managed. "Why hire me?"

"Because I received a letter too." He drew a folded page from his jacket and laid it on the table between them, not pushing it toward her, just setting it down, as though anything he offered might be refused. "Typed. Same stationery. Same typewriter, I'd wager — look at the worn 'e', the way it prints faint on the left side. It says a murder will occur on March the fourteenth, and it says—" his voice tightened almost imperceptibly— "it says the murder will be *the one I was convicted of*. Restaged. In Marrow's End. With the organist."

"And you didn't go to the police."

"I went to the police." For the first time something cracked in the evenness, and underneath it was three years of fury held at a temperature just below boiling. "I wrote to Sorrel from prison eight months ago, before my release was even approved, telling him I'd received this. He filed it. I know he filed it because I made a subject access request and there it is — logged, dated, ignored. So then I thought: who do I go to? Not the police. Not a solicitor; mine are busy preparing the retrial. Someone with the skills, someone outside the force, someone who owes—" he paused, chose the word deliberately— "*something* to the truth. That's you, Mrs. Eriksen. Whether you know it yet or not."

"The date on your letter," she said slowly. "When did it arrive?"

"Eleven weeks ago. Before the Parole Board sat. Before my release was approved by a single day." He leaned forward, and the shadow left his face entirely, and what she saw in it frightened her more than anger would have — it was certainty, cold and total. "Do you understand what that means? Whoever wrote it knew I'd be released before the decision was made. Either they could see the future, or they could see the paperwork. And paperwork, Mrs. Eriksen, is written by people."

She thought of the case file in her back room, the shifted dates, the inked-over name. She thought of Ambrose Pell's neat transcription, pages she had signed off without reading closely, because the trial was over and she was tired and the verdict had been the right one, everyone said so. A cold thread began to work its way down her spine.

"There's something else," Vane said. "The letter I received predicted March the fourteenth. Your client's page — the one you showed round the parish asking questions, yes, I hear things — predicted the same. Two letters, same hand, same prophecy. One sent to the convicted man, one to the detective who convicted him." He spread his hands. "We're not employer and employee, Mrs. Eriksen. We're both characters in someone else's document. The only question is whether we compare our copies."

For a long moment she stood in the dusty light of the dead chandlery, looking at the man whose life she had helped dismantle, and felt the strange vertigo of discovering that the ground she'd stood on for three years — the solid, righteous ground of a job well done — had been hollow all along. She should hate him. Part of her did, reflexively, the way you hate a mirror. But underneath the reflex was something older and more durable: the instinct that had made her a good detective before it made her a guilty one. The instinct that said *this man is telling the truth*, and had perhaps been saying it three years ago in Court One while she looked elsewhere.

"If we do this," she said at last, "we do it properly. No more shells, no more envelopes, no more watching my shop. You tell me everything, in order, and I decide what Sorrel hears and when."

"Sorrel," Vane said, and something flickered across his face — not quite distrust, but its cousin. "Your former partner is the one who filed my letter and forgot it."

"He's also the one who'll keep the official investigation pointed anywhere but at us while we work. We may need him." She picked up the letter from the table, turned it over, examined the faint impression of typing on the reverse — as she should have done with the first page, weeks ago. "And Vane — if I find out you're playing me, if any part of this is theatre—"

"I know," he said quietly. "You'll build a case against me. You're very good at it."

It wasn't a threat, the way he said it. It was almost faith.

She tucked the letter into her coat and walked back up the corridor, and at the door she stopped, because there was one more thing, and it had been sitting in her chest since the moment she saw his hands.

"The night of the verdict," she said, not turning around. "You trembled. When they read it out. I always assumed—"

"Guilt?" His voice came from the dark room behind her. "No, Mrs. Eriksen. Relief. Because whatever happened next, at least it was over." A pause. "I've since learned it wasn't."

She stepped out into the grey afternoon, and the sea wind hit her face, and she stood on the steps of Chandlery House thinking about paperwork and the people who write it — and about the fact that of all the names on her list of seven plausible neighbours, she had never once written down the name of the man who typed every word of the trial.

Not because she hadn't considered him.

Because she had, briefly, and then — she could not now remember why — she had crossed him out.
