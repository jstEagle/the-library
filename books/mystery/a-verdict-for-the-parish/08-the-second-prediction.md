## 8. The Second Prediction

The returns bin had always been Ulyana Eriksen's least favourite piece of furniture in Marrow's End Books & Sundries. It sat by the door like an apology — a wicker basket with a hand-lettered card reading *NO QUESTIONS ASKED*, into which the parish deposited its literary regrets: water-swollen paperbacks, romances with cracked spines, once a complete set of encyclopedias missing only the letter *M*. She had never once found anything in it that frightened her.

Until the morning she found a typed page folded in thirds, resting on top of a dog-eared copy of *The Pilgrim's Progress* like a bookmark placed by someone who wanted to be sure she'd open the book at that exact page.

She knew before she unfolded it. Her body knew first — the cold starting at the base of her skull, the way her hands went very still on the wicker rim. Three years of homicide work had taught her body to recognize the shape of a thing before her mind consented to look at it.

She looked.

*THE TEACHER WILL DIE BEFORE THE TRIAL OPENS. THE RECORD MUST STAND.*

No signature. No date this time — and that absence was worse than any date, because it meant the deadline was not a day on a calendar. It was a countdown with an unknown denominator. The retrial was scheduled to open in nine days. Which meant, at most, nine. At most.

The paper was the same heavy cream stock as the first page, the same police-stationery watermark that had made her stomach drop in the shop's back room six weeks ago. The typeface was the same tired Courier, the same slight lift on the ascenders where the ribbon had begun to fail — a detail she had noted in her casebook with the obsessive care of a woman building a case she couldn't yet name. Same machine. Same hands.

She carried the page to the counter and laid it flat beneath the lamp, as though light might reveal something the darkness had hidden. It revealed only what she already knew: the killer had been inside her shop. Not the first page — that had come by post, postmarked two parishes over. But this one had been placed here, in the returns bin, among the parish's discarded reading, by a hand that had walked through her door, browsed or pretended to browse, and left a death sentence folded in thirds like a hymn programme.

*The record must stand.*

She read it a fourth time, and the meaning finally assembled itself with the slow, horrible click of a lock turning. The first prediction had been theatre — a demonstration, a proof of authorship. *I wrote the murder once; I can write it again.* But this was not theatre. This was necessity. Elias Vane had been released pending retrial because she had found the altered pages, because she had gone to Sorrel, because the machinery she had once trusted had begun, grindingly, to turn. And if the retrial opened — if the transcripts were read aloud in a courtroom, if clerks and barristers and twelve sworn parishioners compared page against page — then the errors would surface. The shifted dates. The inked-over name. The marginalia of a man who had typed the record and then, somewhere along three years of quiet evenings, begun to believe the record was his to correct.

Ambrose Pell needed Vane dead the way a forger needs the original document burned. Not out of appetite. Out of arithmetic.

Ulyana sat down on the stool behind the counter and became, for a long moment, simply a woman in a cold shop with a piece of paper in front of her.

She had spent three years learning to be a bookseller the way other people learned foreign languages — diligently, with a slight accent, never quite native. She had alphabetized and dusted and learned which customers wanted conversation and which wanted only the transaction. She had told herself it was retirement. She had told herself it was peace. Now, holding the second page, she understood that it had been neither. It had been a hiding place, and the hider had been found.

* * *

She did not telephone Sorrel. Not yet. Sorrel was drowning in the official investigation — the organist's widow, the coroner, the Chief Constable's insistence that a drowned man in a locked vestry was a tragic accident until proven otherwise, and Sorrel's own slow, unwilling conversion to the truth of what Ulyana had shown him. Every hour Ulyana gave the official machinery was an hour Pell could use. She was beginning to think of the man in clerical terms she'd absorbed from the parish around her: not as a killer but as a scrivener, and scriveners worked fastest when unobserved.

Instead she went to see Vane.

He was lodging above the chandlery on Harbour Row, in a room the width of a bed and a chair, because no one in Marrow's End would rent to him anything better and no one would rent to him anything worse, either — the parish's conscience had settled on a precise and cruel altitude. She climbed the stairs that smelled of tar and old rope and knocked, and when he opened the door he looked at her the way he had looked at her in the shop two weeks ago: not with forgiveness, not yet, but with the wary attention of a man deciding, daily and afresh, whether she was an ally or simply the most articulate of his accusers.

"You've seen it," he said. He was holding his own copy. Of course he was. He had folded it in quarters and kept it in his breast pocket, over the heart, like a letter from a hostile correspondent he couldn't stop rereading.

"Returns bin," Ulyana said. "Yours?"

"Under my door. He wanted me to know he could reach me." Vane stepped back to let her in, which required nothing of the room, since there was nowhere in it he could not be touched by someone standing in the doorway. "He wanted me to know the trial doesn't have to open for me to be finished. Ulyana — he signed it with the record. *The record must stand.* That's not a threat to me. That's a thesis statement."

"He's right," she said. "That's what makes him dangerous. He's not mad. He's consistent. Three years ago he typed a verdict into the transcript that the evidence didn't support — or he corrected the evidence to support the verdict, I still don't know which came first — and ever since, the record and the truth have been diverging, and every day the gap grows, and every day the risk that someone reads both versions grows with it. The organist wasn't random. Mr. Aldous had been receiving anonymous transcripts of the proceedings. He was reading the record. He was the gap, walking around the parish with legs on."

"And now I'm the gap," Vane said. He sat on the bed, which put him lower than her, and he seemed to find the geometry appropriate. "Nine days."

"Fewer. He's accelerating. The first murder took him three years of preparation. This one has a deadline, and deadlines make people improvisational, and improvisation makes mistakes." She heard herself and almost laughed — the old litany, the catechism of the homicide squad. *Pressure produces error; error produces evidence.* "I used to say that to my sergeants. I'd forgotten I knew it."

"You haven't forgotten anything," Vane said quietly. "That's the trouble with you, Mrs. Eriksen. You remember everything except the things you've decided not to."

She let that sit. It deserved to sit.

"I need you to do something," she said. "It may be dangerous, and I have no right to ask."

"You have every right," Vane said. "You built the case against me. You're entitled to unbuild it." He said it without bitterness, which was somehow worse than bitterness would have been. "What is it?"

"Stay visible. Walk the parish at the hours you always walk it. The harbour path at seven, the churchyard at noon, the shop — my shop — at four. Be predictable. Be a man with a schedule."

He stared at her. "You're asking me to be bait."

"I'm asking you to be legible," Ulyana said. "He has spent three years reading this parish out of documents. He knows us as testimony, as transcript, as typed pages in cream-coloured folders. He knows us better than we know ourselves in some ways — and worse in the ways that matter. He has never had to read us *live*. A man who works from a script can be broken by an actor who changes the blocking. I want to know when he deviates from his own pattern. I want him to come off the page."

"And if he comes off the page toward me?"

"Then he comes toward both of us," she said, "and for the first time in three years, someone will be watching who knows what she's looking at."

* * *

She walked home along the cliff road in a wind that had teeth in it, the sea below working itself grey and furious against the stacks, and she made herself think the thought she had been circling for two days the way a tongue circles a broken tooth.

Pell had been inside her shop.

Not once. The returns bin page proved the most recent visit, but she found herself replaying the past six weeks — every customer, every browser, every figure in a rain coat who had lingered too long over the true-crime shelf without buying. She had served a man a cup of the terrible coffee she kept for regulars and he had asked, pleasantly, whether she stocked local history, and she had directed him to the shelf where — God help her — her own trial notes had sat for one afternoon while she reorganized the back room. He had held her handwriting in his hands and smiled at her across the counter, and she had thought: *how nice, someone who reads.*

The wind pushed at her back like a hand between the shoulder blades. Nine days. Fewer. And the parish around her went on with its ordinary business — nets mended, bread baked, the church bell practising its cracked note — all of it reciting, as Maren had said, a script none of them knew they were reading. She thought of Maren in her cold house with her husband's anonymous transcripts stacked on the kitchen table, and of the promise she had made her: *I will find who did this.* She had made the promise as a detective. She was beginning to understand she would have to keep it as something else — as a neighbour, as a penitent, as a woman whose silence had once been worth more to a killer than any alibi.

Back at the shop, she locked the door behind her, drew the blinds, and set the two typed pages side by side on the counter under the lamp. Then she took out her old casebook — the one she had kept off the books, the parallel record she and Sorrel were building — and began to write.

At the top of a fresh page she printed the deadline in block capitals: *RETRIAL OPENS — 9 DAYS.* Beneath it, the arithmetic of the hunt: Pell's access to stationery, his hours at the courthouse, the archive room's faulty lock she had noted last week and not yet understood why she'd noted it. And beneath that, the thing that had been forming in her since the cliff road, since Vane's narrow room, since the returns bin:

*He corrects. He cannot bear an error to stand. It is not a weakness of method — it is the method. So give him an error.*

She stopped writing. Her pen hovered. Somewhere below the level of conscious thought, the shape of a trap was assembling itself out of the killer's own compulsion, out of the very perfectionism that had kept the false verdict standing for three years. A transcript with a flaw in it. A flaw only one man in the parish would be unable to leave alone.

The bell above the shop door rang.

Ulyana's pen went still. She had locked that door. She was certain — she could feel the key's teeth in her memory, the stiff half-turn of the old lock. She closed the casebook, slid it beneath the counter, and picked up the nearest object with weight and edges, a brass bookend shaped like an owl.

From the shadows at the end of the biography aisle, a voice she knew from three years of courtrooms said, pleasantly:

"Don't get up, Mrs. Eriksen. I've only come to return a book."
