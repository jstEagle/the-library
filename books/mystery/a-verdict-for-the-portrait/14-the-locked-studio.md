## 14. The Locked Studio

The lock on Vivienne Ashcombe's studio had not been changed since 1994.

I knew this because Theo told me, and I knew why he told me—guilt is a solvent, and thirty years of it had eaten through him like rust through a pipe. He didn't say *break in*. He said, over lukewarm coffee in a service station off the A-road, "Nobody's been in there since she died. Marguerite keeps the key. She says." The way he said *she says* was the whole sentence.

So at 11:40 on a Thursday night, with the house dark except for one lamp on the third floor that never seemed to go off—the lamp I'd started thinking of as Marguerite's vigil—I stood in the service corridor behind the kitchen with a credit card, a flashlight with the lens taped to narrow the beam, and the particular nausea of a person about to become exactly what her enemies have accused her of being.

A trespasser. An opportunist. A woman who breaks into other people's grief.

I'd left my phone recording in my coat pocket. Old habit now. Everything is evidence; everything is episode. Sometimes I worry the habit has eaten the woman, that if you peeled me open you'd find not organs but microphones.

* * *

The corridor smelled of cold fat and lemon polish, the smell of a kitchen that cooks for one person out of obligation. My breath fogged. The heating in Ashcombe House, I'd learned during my first visit, ran only on the floors Marguerite used—a woman who rationed warmth the way she rationed truth, giving it only where she intended to live.

The studio door was oak, painted over so many times the panels had gone soft-edged, like something dissolving. Three locks: a deadbolt, a chain, and an old iron latch older than both. Theo had said the deadbolt was original. He hadn't said anything about the chain.

The card failed twice. On the third try I stopped thinking of it as a door and thought of it as a statement—*nothing here has been touched, nothing here matters*—and statements, unlike doors, can be argued with. The tongue gave with a sound like a knuckle cracking. The chain I reached through the gap with a bent hanger from my bag, hooked it, and worked it back and forth until the screw anchoring it to the frame, starved by three decades of paint layers, pulled free of the wood with a soft sigh.

Then the latch, which opened as if it had only been waiting.

The door swung inward six inches and stopped against something heavy on the far side. I put my shoulder to it. It moved grudgingly, grinding, and the smell that came out stopped me with my foot still in the corridor.

Turpentine. Linseed. Dust. And under all of it, faint as a watermark, the ghost of cigarettes—Vivienne Ashcombe smoked forty a day according to every interview ever given, and thirty years later her studio still remembered it. Rooms hold breath longer than people do. That's the thing nobody tells you about houses. They're not containers. They're witnesses.

I slipped inside and eased the door shut behind me.

* * *

My beam found canvases first.

They were stacked against the walls in ranks, some facing in, some facing out, dozens of them—landscapes mostly, the Cornish coast where Vivienne had spent her last decade, cliffs and water and the particular bruised light of that coastline rendered over and over as if she were trying to get one painting exactly right and couldn't stop until she did. A worktable under a window, its surface drifted with dried brushes standing in jars like flowers that had given up. A chair. An easel, folded flat against the wall, its tray empty.

And above the worktable, on the north wall where the light would have fallen cleanest—

The frame.

It was enormous, gilt, ornate, the kind of frame that costs more than the car most people drive. It hung exactly where the estate inventory said *Untitled (Final Work)* hung. I knew the dimensions from the file: 183 by 152 centimeters. I knew the description from the 1995 probate appraisal: *portrait of the artist, unfinished.*

The frame was empty.

Not damaged-empty. Not stripped or looted. Empty the way a stage is empty between performances—deliberately, formally, with intention. And the wall inside the frame—

I crossed the room before I decided to, my boots loud on boards that should have creaked and didn't, as if the floor had learned silence the way everything else in this house had learned it. I raised the light to the rectangle of wall framed in gilt.

Clean. Unfaded. A perfect pale rectangle, the plaster around it yellowed by thirty years of daylight while the space behind the canvas stayed the color of the day it was covered. No dust shadow. no ghost of pigment bleeding at the edges. Whoever took the portrait down had done it recently, carefully, and had either cleaned the wall or—my stomach turned as I understood it—had taken the painting down and *put something back*. A backing board, maybe. Something cut to size, primed to match, fitted into the frame so that anyone glancing through the studio doorway—and nobody glanced, because nobody came—would see a canvas hanging where a canvas should hang.

Except they'd removed even that. Now there was nothing but the negative space, the absence itself on display, like a body missing from a coffin with the lid left open.

Why take the false panel out? Why leave the frame empty?

Unless someone else had gotten here first. Unless the second thief had found the first thief's trick and stripped it, and what I was looking at wasn't neglect but evidence of a war conducted entirely within these four walls, generation after generation, everyone stealing from the dead and hiding the theft from each other.

Or unless Marguerite had come herself. Tonight. Recently enough that the air still held the disturbance of it.

I played the beam along the floor beneath the frame. Dust lay thick everywhere—except two shallow arcs in front of the wall, scuffed clean, the width of a person standing and turning, standing and stepping back to look at their work.

Someone had stood here. Recently. Looking at what they'd done.

I stood in those scuff marks myself, because apparently I wanted to know how it felt, and it felt like standing inside a confession that had been edited.

* * *

Here is what I knew about the final portrait, assembled from thirty years of clippings, probate documents, and one line in Camille's journals that I still hadn't fully unpacked:

Vivienne Ashcombe worked on it for eleven months before her death. She refused to show it. She refused to name it. Two weeks before she died, she had it crated—crated, which painters do for shipping or for storage, and she was doing neither—and then, per the testimony of the housekeeper at the trial, she uncrated it again and hung it herself, on the north wall, and sat looking at it for an entire afternoon with the door locked.

The housekeeper's testimony was ruled hearsay. The housekeeper left England in 1996 and could not be located for appeal. I had found her last year living outside Porto; she was ninety-one and sharp as broken glass, and when I asked her on the phone what Vivienne had looked like that afternoon, she said, "Like a woman who had finished saying something and was waiting to see who heard it."

The handyman, Daniel Okafor, was convicted partly because prosecutors argued the portrait proved no struggle—he'd supposedly strangled her elsewhere and staged the studio—but the portrait was never produced at trial. Never photographed for evidence. Never examined. The family's solicitor, the same solicitor whose fingerprints were on every alibi, informed the court the work was "the private property of the estate and of no evidentiary value."

Of no evidentiary value. A painting the victim had crated, uncrated, hung, and stared at for an afternoon, twelve days before she was killed.

I'd spent twenty years listening to what people chose not to say. The legal system spends its whole life listening to what documents choose not to say. *No evidentiary value* might be the loudest sentence I'd ever read.

Camille's journal, the entry from March 2003, the one I'd transcribed four times trying to hear it right:

*M. says the portrait was ruined by damp and destroyed years ago. M. says a great many things. Grandmother painted the truth underneath the paint. She taught me that when I was nine. You can say a thing so it looks like one thing and means another. She called it the honest lie. I think she finally painted one too honest to hang.*

Underpainting. Every conservator I'd called had told me it was possible, common even—artists revising, concealing, composing over earlier attempts. Dr. Ilsa Ren had told me something else, though, when I'd shown her the study sketch Marguerite had given me, the little gift wrapped in charm: "This isn't revision," she'd said, tapping the X-ray plate. "Revision is messy. This is deliberate. Someone built a second picture and buried it alive."

Buried alive. Like the case itself. Like Okafor's name. Like Camille's suspicions, dying with her in a house fire the coroner called accidental, the week before the trust's final review.

And now the portrait—the one object that could prove any of it—was gone from a room that was supposed to be untouched.

* * *

I made myself slow down. This was the discipline, the one thing twenty years of cold cases had actually taught me: when the adrenaline says run, walk. When it says grab, look.

I went through the studio the way I'd want an investigator to go through mine if I ever ended up a file in someone else's season.

The crates were stacked in the corner, the ones Vivienne had packed and unpacked. Open, lids off, empty—but the packing straw inside them was disturbed, recent, compressed in patterns that suggested weight had been lifted out, not put in. I shone the light into the deepest crate. In the corner, caught in the straw, a scrap of stiff paper. I teased it out with two fingers.

An auction tag. Christie's, South Kensington. Lot number handwritten in ballpoint. Dated—my throat tightened—eleven days ago.

Eleven days ago, someone had tagged this crate for auction. Which meant the portrait hadn't been stolen. It was being *sold*. Quietly, privately, through channels—because a genuine Ashcombe final work, surfacing thirty years after the murder, would raise questions the family had spent three decades and several fortunes keeping closed. Unless it was sold as something else. Unless the underpainting had already been dealt with.

Unless someone was destroying the evidence and monetizing the frame.

I photographed the tag, the crates, the scuff marks, the empty frame, the unfaded rectangle. My hands were steady. I remember noticing that, and remembering being surprised, and understanding distantly that the surprise itself was a bad sign—that the part of me calibrated for fear had been recalibrated for this, for dark rooms and other people's secrets, and I didn't know when that had happened or whether I'd get it back.

That was when I heard the footsteps.

* * *

Above me. Not close—through two floors of old joices and settled plaster, muffled, almost deniable. But rhythmical. Deliberate. Crossing the third-floor landing, pausing, crossing again.

Marguerite's lamp. The vigil light. Except the vigil was walking.

I killed my flashlight and stood in the dark with my heart going like a trapped bird, doing the arithmetic I should have done at the door. One road out, past the kitchen, past the servants' stair that connected this wing to hers. A window over the worktable, sash-painted shut by the look of it. And somewhere above me, an eighty-year-old woman moving through her house at midnight with the unhurried tread of someone who has never once needed to hurry, because the house itself does the hurrying for her—the locks, the lawyers, the lies, all of it arranged around her like furniture.

The footsteps stopped directly overhead.

I counted my own heartbeats. Ten. Twenty. The house ticked and settled around me, breathing its turpentine-and-cigarette breath. Then, faintly, through the floor, a sound I couldn't place—metallic, small, repeated. A drawer opening and closing? A latch?

Then nothing. The footsteps resumed, moved away toward the east end of the house, and faded.

I gave it five full minutes. Then I did the thing I'd been avoiding: I took out my phone, opened the recorder, and spoke into the dark, barely above a whisper, timestamping it.

"Studio interior, 12:51 a.m. Final portrait absent from documented location. Wall shows recent removal. Auction tag, Christie's, dated within the last two weeks, recovered from shipping crate. Footsteps audible on the third floor during search. Subject unidentified."

A pause. And then, because the dark makes honest, I added the thing I hadn't planned to say:

"Whoever she is, she was here tonight. She's always one room ahead of me. And I'm starting to understand that maybe I'm not investigating this house."

Another breath.

"Maybe the house is investigating me."

I pocketed the phone, eased the door open onto the cold corridor, and froze.

At the far end of the passage, by the window where moonlight fell through the glass in a long pale stripe, something lay on the floorboards that had not been there when I came in.

A single long-stemmed rose. Red, fresh, dew still on it, laid across the threshold of the studio door like an offering—or a marker. Like a flower dropped on a grave.

And pinned beneath its stem, a folded note in handwriting I recognized instantly, because I'd seen it on thirty years of letters, ledgers, and alibis, elegant and upright and utterly controlled:

*You should have taken the money, Miss Lockridge.*
