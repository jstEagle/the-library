## 1. The Fourth Accident

The crypt smelled of salt and old stone, the way it always did after a wet winter — as though the sea had been down there in the dark, breathing against the walls, and had only just left.

Yara Okonkwo came down the spiral stair with a lamp in one hand and a clipboard under her arm, and paused at the bottom to let her eyes adjust. The lower crypt of St. Brannoc's was not on the public tour. It was not, strictly speaking, on any tour. It had been sealed since the flood of 1979, when the harbor burst its banks and the tide climbed the town's throat far enough to drown the lowest chambers of the cathedral, and when the waters went out they took the money for restoration with them. What remained below was a museum of neglect: shelving warped into question marks, ledgers swollen fat with brine, their spines furred white with salt bloom like something growing rather than decaying.

She unlocked the gate with the long iron key Father Ambrose kept on a nail in the vestry, signed the access book out of habit even though no one checked it, and set her lamp on the trestle table she'd claimed three weeks ago. The work was tedious and she loved it. That was the thing about cataloguing that nobody understood who hadn't done it: it wasn't dusting. It was listening. Every ledger was a voice that had stopped mid-sentence, and her job was to write down where it stopped.

"Parish accounts, 1946 through 1958," she said aloud, to the dark, because the dark was better company than silence. "Water damage, forty percent legible. Binding failed." She wrote it on the card, slid the ledger into its acid-free sleeve, and reached for the next box.

That was when she saw the reliquary.

It stood at the back of the third alcove, past the flooded section where the shelving gave up entirely — a small oak casket on a stone plinth, its brass fittings gone green, its lid carved with saints whose faces the damp had eaten smooth. She'd catalogued it weeks ago: *Reliquary, oak, provenance uncertain, contents unverified.* She'd noted then that it sat oddly, angled away from the wall, as if someone had moved it in a hurry and never come back to straighten it. She'd straightened it herself, once, tidying the alcove.

Now it was angled again. Wedged behind it, visible only from this exact angle, in this exact light, was the corner of a leather book.

Yara stood very still.

There were two explanations, and she made herself consider both with the flat professional calm she'd learned to wear like a coat. One: the book had been there all along, hidden by the reliquary's angle, and she'd missed it. Possible. The alcove was dim; she worked by lamplight; archivists missed things, that was half of why re-cataloguing existed. Two: the book had not been there before, which meant someone had put it there recently, which meant someone else had a key to a locked crypt that was supposed to have exactly one key.

She didn't decide between them. She simply crossed the floor, knelt on the cold flagstones — the damp came up through her trousers immediately, inevitable as weather — and drew the book out with both hands, the way you'd draw a splinter.

A diary. Leather, cracked at the spine, a rubber band around it gone brittle and snapped at her touch. On the flyleaf, in faded ballpoint: *Property of Edith Marsh. If found, please return to 14 Chapel Row.*

Yara sat back on her heels.

Edith Marsh. Everyone in the parish knew the name now, though most of them had known the woman first — the small, upright figure in the third pew from the front, the one who ran the flower rota and corrected the bulletin typos with a pencil so sharp it was practically a weapon. Edith Marsh had vanished twenty-three days ago. Walked out of her cottage on Chapel Row on a Tuesday evening in her good coat, according to the neighbors, and hadn't come home. No body, no signs of struggle, no bank activity, no bus tickets. The police had canvassed, searched the cliffs, dragged nothing out of the harbor, and concluded what police conclude when an elderly woman walks into the sea fog without a note: misadventure, probably. An open verdict pending. A woman who wandered off. It happens.

Except Yara had spent two years learning that in this town, things happened for reasons.

She turned the diary over. The leather was dry — dry, here, in a chamber where everything swelled and rotted — and the pages, when she opened them carefully at the back, were crisp. Not flood-damaged. Not fifty years old. This book had come down here recently, and recently, someone had wanted it not to be found — or wanted it found by exactly the right person, at exactly the right time, and Yara was honest enough with herself to know she couldn't tell which frightened her more.

She should take it upstairs. Log it, photograph it, call DS Hale, hand it over, let procedure do what procedure did. That was the correct sequence. She knew the correct sequence the way she knew the fire exits.

Instead she read the last entry.

*Tuesday. Told Marianne I'd stop asking questions and she almost believed me, which shows how little any of them know me. Went to the sea wall at dusk. Left the packet. If anything happens to me, whoever finds this should know it wasn't the water that took Nell Pryce, and it wasn't the water that will take me either. It was the Vane vault and everyone who keeps it shut. Ask Ambrose why he signed a burial certificate for a woman with no grave. Ask him about the warden. Ask him—*

The entry stopped. Mid-sentence. The ink ended in a small blunt period, as if the pen had been pressed down hard and lifted, and beneath it the rest of the page was blank, and the page after that, and the page after that, all the way to the back cover.

Yara became aware, distantly, that her heart was going too fast, and that her hands were perfectly steady, and that these two facts together meant something about her that she preferred not to examine.

*Marianne.* Marianne Ashcombe, who poured tea at the memorial fund meetings and remembered everyone's grandchildren's names. *Nell Pryce,* a name Yara didn't know at all, filed away instantly for later. *The Vane vault. The warden.* And Ambrose — *ask Ambrose* — Ambrose, who had taught her to read marginalia, who brought her ginger nuts with the archive tea, who had given her the key to this crypt himself and told her, smiling, *the dead keep better order than the living, Yara, see that you don't disturb them.*

She looked at the reliquary. Saints with eaten faces. A box built to hold bones of the holy, sitting guard over a book that named the living.

Professional curiosity, she told herself. That was all this was. A discrepancy in the record. A document recovered from a restricted area, requiring verification of provenance, chain of custody, authorship. There was a form for this. There was a whole drawer of forms.

But underneath the form-filling voice, another voice said: *three weeks.* Edith Marsh had been gone three weeks, and her diary had been hidden in a locked crypt, and the last entry was a sentence cut off like a rope, and somewhere above her head four hundred people were singing the noon service as though the world were a place where old women simply wandered off into fog.

Yara photographed every page with her phone, flash harsh against the damp stone, then wrapped the diary in an archival sleeve and carried it up the spiral stair with both hands, the way you carry something that might still be alive.

At the top, she paused. Below her, the lower crypt lay in its lamplit dark, patient as a held breath. Somewhere in those drowned chambers was a room the plans didn't show — she'd find that out later, though she didn't know it yet — and somewhere in this town was a woman named Nell Pryce whom the sea was supposed to have taken in 1979, and somewhere in the vestry Father Ambrose was putting away his vestments, humming, kind.

She locked the gate. Signed the access book. Wrote, in her neat archivist's hand: *Nothing further recovered today.*

Then she carried the diary to her office, locked it in the bottom drawer, and sat for a long moment with her palms flat on the desk, listening to the cathedral breathe above her — timber and stone and eight hundred years of people telling each other that everything was fine.

The final entry named a name she recognized. It had also, she realized now, named her — or nearly. *Whoever finds this.* Edith Marsh had hidden that book where only the archivist would ever look, in the one place in St. Brannoc's that belonged to Yara alone.

It wasn't a message left behind.

It was a letter, addressed and delivered.

And tomorrow, whether anyone believed her or not, Yara Okonkwo intended to open it properly.

* * *

That night she dreamed of water climbing the cathedral steps, patient and unhurried, and woke at three a.m. with the certainty — cold, complete, and utterly unprofessional — that Edith Marsh had not walked into the sea at all.

Someone had wanted the town to think she had.

And they had made one mistake: they'd hidden the proof in Yara's crypt.
