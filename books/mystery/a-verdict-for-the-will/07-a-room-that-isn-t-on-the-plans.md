## 7. A Room That Isn't on the Plans

The blueprints arrived on a Tuesday morning, delivered to the archive in a cardboard tube that had once held a poster of the Bayeux Tapestry and now smelled faintly of the surveyor's office where they had spent forty years forgotten.

Yara signed for them with the archivist's pen — her pen, the one she'd bought herself the day she took the post, because the cathedral provided everything except the tools for truth — and carried them to the long table beneath the north window, where the light came down clean and grey and honest.

She had asked for them twice before. The first time, the diocesan office had lost the request. The second time, a helpful voice on the telephone had suggested that the 1890s plans were of purely historical interest, that the cathedral had been surveyed properly in 1998, that surely the modern drawings would serve her better. She had agreed pleasantly, hung up, and requested the 1890s plans again by letter, citing the Conservation Charter, section four, which required original documentation to be made available to any officer of the cathedral upon written application.

Officer of the cathedral. She had signed herself that without irony, and the irony had arrived later, sitting in her chest like a stone swallowed wrong.

Now she unrolled them, weighted the corners with the brass paperweights shaped like eagles — St. Brannoc's emblem, the bird that was said to have flown out of the sea carrying the saint's book in its beak — and began, methodically, with the kind of patience that had once been called a virtue and was now, she suspected, simply a survival trait, to measure the past against itself.

The lower crypt plan was drawn in sepia ink on linen-backed paper, the hand of the surveyor — one T. R. Ellacombe, whose flourish on the signature suggested a man pleased with his own precision — crisp and confident. The crypt ran the full length of the nave above it, a corridor of squat pillars and low vaulting, with the reliquary niches along the south wall and the ossuary recess at the east end. She had walked it a hundred times. She had catalogued it by candlelight during the flood-damage assessment, her wellies in an inch of standing brine, her headlamp throwing shadows that moved like things with intentions.

She measured.

The scale bar said one inch to eight feet. She checked it twice, then a third time, because the first discrepancy was so small she assumed it was her error — the paper stretching, the ink bleeding, the hundred and thirty years doing their slow work on linen and size.

But the discrepancy repeated. And repeated. And repeated.

The corridor as drawn was sixty-two feet from the west stair to the ossuary recess. The pillars were drawn at even intervals — seven of them, each bay exactly eight feet ten inches on the plan. She measured each bay on the drawing with the steel rule she kept for exactly this kind of work, the rule that had never once lied to her, and converted, and wrote the figures in her notebook in her small, unlovely, unforgeable hand.

Then she took the modern survey — the 1998 drawings, which the helpful voice had been so eager to give her — and laid it alongside.

The modern survey agreed with the 1890s plan. Bay for bay, pillar for pillar, the two documents, separated by a century, told the same story.

It was the crypt itself that disagreed with both of them.

She had measured the lower crypt herself in November, during the flood assessment, because that was what an archivist did: she measured, she recorded, she left a record that could not be argued with. She had paced the bays with a laser measure borrowed from the stonemason's yard — from the stonemason who was now dead, she thought, and pushed the thought down like a lid on a well — and she had noted the figures in the condition report, a report that now lived in a folder in her office, in a drawer that locked.

The bays in the actual crypt were not eight feet ten inches.

Six of them were. The seventh — the bay between the fifth and sixth pillars, the one nearest the ossuary recess, the one where the standing water had always been deepest and the air had always smelled most of the sea — was nine feet six inches.

Eight inches too long. Eight inches, over a bay that should have been identical to its six brothers, in a building where the medieval masons had worked to tolerances of a quarter inch.

She sat back. The eagles on the paperweights gleamed. Somewhere above her, through three floors of stone, the Tuesday service was letting out, and the sound of the congregation came down the old walls like sand through an hourglass.

Eight inches. She had missed it in November because she had been measuring for damp, not for truth, and because the laser had read the back wall of the bay and she had written down what it said without asking what the wall was standing in front of.

But a wall could stand in front of something.

She pulled the 1890s plan closer and looked at it the way she had learned to look at documents — not at what they showed, but at what they had been made to show. Ellacombe's ink was confident everywhere except one place. Along the south wall of the seventh bay, where the other bays showed the reliquary niches in careful section — each niche drawn with its depth, its hood moulding, its sill height — the seventh bay showed nothing. Blank stone. And the blankness had been drawn with a slightly heavier hand, as if the pen had been pressed down to make the absence look deliberate, architectural, uninteresting.

Yara had spent eleven years learning to read the silences in archives. The destroyed page. The index entry that pointed to a folder that no longer existed. The burial register with a line ruled through a name and no note of reinterment. Documents lied most fluently in the places where they tried hardest to sound boring.

The blank wall was trying very hard to sound boring.

* * *

She went down at four o'clock, when the last of the volunteers had gone and the vergers were doing the upper rooms, because the lower crypt was hers alone — hers and, apparently, whatever the eighth inches were hiding.

The stair was narrow and the air changed halfway down, the way it always did, from candle-wax and stone to something older and wetter, the smell of a place that had once been under the sea and had never entirely been given back. The flood of 1979 had sealed the lower crypt; the tide still came partway up the outer passage on spring tides; the diocese had ruled it structurally sound and spiritually irrelevant, which in Yara's experience was the church's way of saying *we have decided not to look at this*.

Her headlamp cut a cone through the gloom. Water lay in the channel down the corridor's centre, an inch of it, black and still, reflecting her light back up at her like a second, darker archivist walking below the floor.

She reached the seventh bay and stopped.

Up close, the south wall was just a wall — dressed granite, the same as the rest, the blocks laid in courses, the mortar between them grey and crumbling in the old way. She put her palm against it. Cold. Colder than the walls on either side, cold in a way that had nothing to do with the season.

She measured the bay anyway, because she was who she was. Nine feet six inches, wall to wall. She measured the wall's thickness at the doorway of the sixth bay, where the plan showed the wall continuing: two feet four inches.

Then she stood in the seventh bay and did the arithmetic that had been waiting for her all day.

The crypt's outer wall, on the plan, at this point, was four feet thick. The bay was nine feet six. Two walls of two feet four each, plus the outer wall of four feet, came to eight feet eight inches of stone — and the bay was nine feet six. There were eight inches of the bay that belonged to nothing on any drawing. Eight inches that were not corridor, not wall, not crypt.

Eight inches was nothing. Eight inches was the thickness of a single skin of brickwork laid flat. Eight inches was a wall built in front of a wall.

Her heart was doing something she didn't authorize. She made herself breathe the way the meditation app on her phone suggested — in for four, hold for four — and then she took the trowel from her bag, the small one, the one for lifting flaking paint samples, and she knelt in the standing water with her trousers soaking and she began, gently, at the lowest course, at the joint where two blocks met, to sound the wall.

The trowel blade went into the first joint and stopped at two inches. Old mortar, hard as iron.

She moved along. Two inches. Two and a half. Then, at the third block from the corner — the block at knee height, half-hidden by the water line — the blade slid in and kept sliding, four inches, five, six, and the resistance changed, and the sound the trowel made against the block behind it was not the dull knock of granite on granite.

It was a hollow sound.

Yara knelt very still in the black water and listened to the hollow sound die away, and the crypt listened with her, and above her, faint through the floors, the cathedral bell rang the quarter hour, and the vibration came down through the stone and she felt it in her knees, in her teeth, in the trowel.

She worked the joint wider with a patience that cost her everything she had. Mortar crumbled out in dark crumbs, and then in lighter crumbs, and then in crumbs that were not grey at all but pale, almost white, almost new.

Fresh mortar.

Not 1890s mortar. Not 1979 flood-mortar, which would have been rough and rushed and full of grit. This was pale and fine and evenly mixed, and when she rubbed it between her fingers it still had a faint tack, the ghost of a cure that was not yet years old.

Someone had bricked this wall recently. Someone had stood in this bay — in the water, in the dark, in the cathedral's most forgotten room — and laid a skin of brick across the older stone, and pointed it, and made it look like every other wall in the crypt, and gone away, and let thirty years of everyone's not-looking do the rest.

She got the block loose at last. It came out with a grinding suck of water and a breath of air from behind it — and Yara Okonkwo, who had spent her life in archives, who knew the smell of every kind of old paper from vellum to newsprint, who could date a document by its decay, closed her eyes because she didn't need the light to tell her what was behind the wall.

Salt and old paper.

The smell came out of the gap like a voice, like a hand reaching through eight inches of brick and a hundred and thirty years of silence, and it was the smell of documents stored where the damp could reach them, the smell of a place where paper had been kept by someone who needed it kept and could not keep it properly, and under the salt and the paper there was something else, faint and human and terrible: the smell of a room that had been lived in, or at least visited, for a very long time.

She got her headlamp to the hole and looked through.

Darkness. A space beyond the wall — not a cavity, not a crawl, but a room, the beam of her light swallowed after six feet by a blackness that had texture, that had corners, that was the blackness of a chamber and not a gap. The floor beyond was dry. Dry, when the crypt she knelt in stood in an inch of the sea. Whatever lay behind the wall was above the flood line, or protected from it, or both.

And against the far edge of the hole, where her light just grazed it, she saw the edge of a shelf. Wooden. Handmade. And on it, the corner of something that might have been a box, or a ledger, or a life.

Yara sat back on her heels in the cold water and made herself think.

She thought about Edith Marsh, three weeks missing, her diary wedged behind a reliquary with entries that continued past the end of her. She thought about the three accidents, and Detective Sergeant Hale's face when he closed the files too fast, and the way the founding families' warmth had curdled at the word *flood*. She thought about Father Ambrose, who had taught her everything she knew about reading the silences in a record, and who had gone quiet — a specific, careful quiet — when she'd mentioned the lower crypt.

And she understood, with the vertigo of a woman standing on a threshold that is also a cliff edge, that the room behind the wall was not a room that had been forgotten.

It was a room that was being kept.

The mortar was fresh. Which meant someone had sealed it — recently. Which meant someone might come back to it. Which meant that every minute she knelt here in the water with a hole in the wall and her fingerprints in the mortar crumbs was a minute borrowed from someone who had killed at least three people to keep this room a secret.

She could widen the hole. She could be through it in an hour, maybe two, and whatever was on that shelf — whatever Edith Marsh had died, or vanished, or hidden herself to protect — could be in her hands tonight.

Or she could put the block back. Point the joint with wet mortar from the channel floor, the old grey kind, and leave the wall looking exactly as she had found it, and go away, and come back with Hale, with witnesses, with the law at her shoulder instead of alone in the dark with a trowel.

The sensible archivist put the block back.

Yara looked through the hole one more time. Her light moved — and stopped.

Because the shelf was not the only thing against the far wall. Below it, half in shadow, was a chair. A plain wooden chair, the kind that came in stacks of twelve from any church supplier in the county. And on the chair, folded with a care that seemed almost tender, was a coat. A woman's coat. Dark wool, out of fashion by decades, its shoulders dusted with the fine grey fall of plaster and time.

It had been waiting there a long time. And it had not been left by someone who intended never to come back.

Yara's hand was shaking as she eased the granite block back into place. She pointed the joint with the old wet mortar and smoothed it, and smoothed it again, and the wall closed its mouth and became, once more, the most boring wall in the cathedral.

She climbed the stair on legs that did not feel like hers. At the top, in the sudden ordinary light of the cloister walk, she took out her phone and typed a message to Hale with thumbs that would not quite obey her:

*The lower crypt. The seventh bay. There's a room that isn't on the plans. I need you here. Tonight.*

She stood a moment, watching the message send, watching the little delivered tick appear.

Then, across the cloister garth, beneath the yew that had stood there before the cathedral, Father Ambrose came out of the vestry door, saw her standing there with wet knees and mortar dust on her hands, and stopped.

He looked at her for a long moment. And then he said, in a voice as gentle and as terrible as the smell of salt and old paper:

"Yara. What have you found?"
