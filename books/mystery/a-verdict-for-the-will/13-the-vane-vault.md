## 13. The Vane Vault

The Vane family had kept their dead the way they kept everything else: behind iron, under lock, and with the assumption that no one would ever dare ask.

Yara stood across the lane from the Vane mausoleum at half past eleven on a Tuesday night, her breath fogging in the cold, and told herself she was not breaking in. Not yet. She was *observing*. The distinction mattered to her, though she suspected it would matter very little to a magistrate.

The mausoleum sat in the corner of the old municipal cemetery like a small arrogant house — granite faced, slate roofed, its door a slab of blackened bronze worked with the Vane arms: three gulls over water. A newer structure crouched beside it, added sometime in the sixties by a Vane who had wanted to be buried near his ancestors but not *under* them; that was where the records office was, because the family had never trusted the cathedral archive or the county registry to hold anything they cared about. Marianne Ashcombe — born Vane, married Ashcombe, and never once let anyone forget either — kept the keys.

Which meant Yara could not use keys. She would have to use patience instead, and patience was the one tool she owned outright.

She had spent two evenings establishing the pattern. The caretaker, a stooped man named Tredinnick who nodded to her every Sunday as though archivists were clergy-adjacent and therefore harmless, locked up at nine and walked home along the coast road. The cemetery's perimeter wall was Victorian and generous with handholds. The records room's only window faced the sea side of the building, away from the lane, and its frame had been painted shut sometime during Yara's own childhood — she knew this because she'd tried to open it once, years ago, chasing a misfiled burial register for the Friends of St. Brannoc's. She'd given up then. She was not going to give up now.

*Three deaths,* she thought, watching the moon slide behind cloud. *One pattern. And now this.*

The idea had come to her three days ago, in the blue-lit quiet of the county records annex, when she'd pulled the Vane charitable trust filings on a hunch so thin she hadn't dared name it even to herself. The trust paid for burials. It had paid for burials since 1911 — pauper interments, unclaimed bodies, "special arrangements" for parishioners of modest means whose families the Vanes wished to assist. A charity, everyone said. Generosity, everyone said.

But Edith Marsh's diary had used a phrase Yara couldn't shake. *He signs them all.* Written in the entry dated the fourth of February, three weeks before Edith vanished. Yara had assumed it referred to the warden, or to Ambrose's endless liturgical paperwork. Now, staring at the trust's expenditure ledgers — line after line of burial fees, plot fees, stone-cutting fees, all authorized by a single countersignature — she understood it differently.

*He signs them all.* Every burial the trust funded carried the same authorizing signature. And if some of those burials were lies — if people who died in the flood of '79, people like Nell Pryce, had been declared dead without graves — then somewhere there would be paper proving it. Paper signed by the same hand.

Paper kept where the family kept things they didn't want found.

* * *

The window gave on the third try, the paint cracking along the seam with a sound like a knuckle popping. Yara froze, counted to sixty, listened to nothing but the sea working at the shingle below the cemetery wall. Then she slid the sash up, hooked her elbows over the sill, and pulled herself through into a room that smelled of dust, camphor, and forty years of someone's pipe smoke soaked into the woodwork.

Her penlight found steel shelving, ledger boxes in acid-free sleeves — the Vanes had at least hired competent archivists, whoever they'd been — and a desk with a brass lamp and a blotter worn smooth at one corner, where generations of some clerk's elbow had rested. On the wall hung a framed photograph of the mausoleum's interior, taken at some dedication: marble shelves, lead-lined coffins, and in the foreground a man in a dark coat whose face was turned just enough toward the camera that Yara felt her stomach drop.

The warden. Younger, but unmistakable. Standing at the Vane family's shoulder the way he now stood at the cathedral's.

"Of course you're here," she whispered. "You've always been here."

She started with the most recent boxes and worked backward, because method was the only prayer she had left. Burial certificates, carbon copies, each one bearing the trust's letterhead — a gull over waves — and the particulars of the deceased: name, age, parish, cause of death, plot number, officiant. The officiating signature varied; local curates came and went. But the authorizing signature did not. It appeared on every certificate from 1962 onward, a small, controlled, slightly old-fashioned hand with a distinctive crossed *t*:

*A. R. Ambrose.*

Yara sat down on the floor among the boxes and stared at the name until the letters stopped meaning anything.

He'd been signing these since 1962. He'd been a young priest then, newly arrived, already trusted by the founding families with their paperwork — with the machinery of death itself. Forty years of certificates. Hundreds of them. Most, surely, honest: real deaths, real plots, real grieving families the trust had genuinely helped. That was what made it work. That was what made it *invisible*. You couldn't audit generosity. No one checks the graves of the poor.

But she wasn't here for the honest ones.

She worked faster now, pulling certificates whose details snagged against something — a missing plot number, a cause of death written in a different ink, an officiant's line left blank. She built a pile. Eleven certificates by midnight. Twelve. Thirteen.

Then she found Nell Pryce.

*Eleanor Mary Pryce. Age 31. Parish of St. Brannoc's. Cause of death: drowning, flood of January 1979. Interred: Vane Trust plot, Section F, Row 3, Grave 19.* Signed, authorized, countersigned, filed. Dated the ninth of February, 1979.

Yara's hands were shaking. She made herself go back through the box, one certificate at a time, until she found the interment register for Section F — the actual ground-level record, the book that said what was actually *in the earth*. Row 3 ran from Grave 12 to Grave 24.

There was no Grave 19.

The row simply skipped it. The numbering jumped from 18 to 20, and between them the register noted, in a clerk's tidy hand: *plot reserved — structural.* Structural. As if the ground itself had declined to hold Eleanor Pryce.

No body had ever been recovered from the flood. Yara had learned that in chapter after fruitless hour of the 1979 inquest file. The coroner had declared Nell Pryce dead on the strength of witness statements and the presumption of drowning — and someone, within ten days, had produced a burial certificate placing her body in a grave that did not exist, signed by a priest who was still signing such documents today.

A dead woman with a death certificate and no grave. A living woman, if the ferryman's memory held, who had been taking the night boat under another name for years. And between those two facts, a signature — Ambrose's signature — dated *after* the date everyone swore she died.

Because that was the thing Yara saw now, laid out in the penlight's cold circle: the certificate was dated the ninth of February. The flood had been the second of January. Five weeks. Enough time for a body to wash ashore, be identified, be processed. Or enough time for something else entirely — for a woman who hadn't drowned to be erased on paper, quietly, officially, with the church's blessing and the family's money.

Fury rose in her like water in a lock, steady and total, and she let it — for exactly one minute. She watched the penlight beam tremble with her breathing and thought: *this is what they do. They make you feel it alone, in the dark, with no one to tell.* Detective Sergeant Hale would want this photographed properly, chain-of-custody intact, and Hale could not know she was here tonight. Marianne Ashcombe would offer her a salary. Father Ambrose would ask her, gently, with love in his voice, to stop.

So she stopped feeling it. She took out her phone and began to photograph.

Every certificate in the pile. The register page with its missing Grave 19. The letterhead. The signatures. Close-ups of the crossed *t*, the date lines, the countersignature of the trust secretary — a name she recognized from the memorial tea, one of the Ashcombe cousins, dead now fifteen years. She photographed until her storage warning blinked, then culled the blurry shots and retook them, and by the time she finished her fury had cooled into something harder and more useful.

Method.

She copied the eleven other anomalous certificates' names onto a card from her pocketbook. Ten names besides Nell Pryce, spanning 1963 to 1994. Ten more people buried in graves that might not exist. She would check every one against the ground registers tomorrow — today, she corrected herself, glancing at her watch, it was nearly two in the morning — and if even half of them were hollow, then the Vane vault records weren't a scandal. They were a machine. A machine for making people disappear, dressed as charity, oiled by the church, and operated — she thought of the young man in the photograph, standing at the family's shoulder — by hands that had never once changed.

She repacked the boxes exactly as she'd found them, aligning the spines by feel, restoring the dust shadows. She closed the register. She wiped the desk edge and the window latch with her sleeve. Then she paused, penlight off, listening to the dark.

Somewhere outside, gravel had shifted.

Not the sea. The sea was rhythm; this had been weight — a footfall, or the settling of something heavy on loose stone. She killed the light completely and stood motionless by the shelf, counting her own heartbeats, and heard nothing further. Wind in the yew trees. A dog barking twice, far off toward the town. Nothing else.

Still, she left the way she'd come rather than risk the door, easing through the window and down into the shadow of the wall, and she did not walk back along the lane. She cut through the old quarry path above the cemetery, moving fast, and only when she reached the coast road with the town lights below her did she stop and breathe.

Below, the sea was turning. She could feel it more than see it — that slow gathering pressure the estuary made twice a day, the tide beginning its long pull toward the spring equinox. Toward whatever the water had kept, and whatever it was about to give back.

She took out the card with eleven names on it and read them once by the light of a passing car, then folded it away. Tomorrow she would verify the graves. Tonight she had something better than proof: she had a signature, a date, and a grave that wasn't there.

Ambrose had signed Nell Pryce into the ground five weeks after the flood. Which meant that on the ninth of February, 1979, someone in this town had known — known certainly, known officially — that Nell Pryce was not in the water.

And if they knew she wasn't dead, Yara thought, starting down the hill toward the sleeping town, then they had known all along where she went.

The question that had haunted her for weeks turned over like a stone in the tide and showed her its underside:

Who had helped her disappear — and who had spent forty years making sure no one ever asked?
