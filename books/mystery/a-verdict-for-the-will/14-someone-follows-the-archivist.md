## 14. Someone Follows the Archivist

The cloisters had a rhythm to them, and Yara had learned to read it the way she read a ledger — by what was absent.

For eleven years she had walked the stone arcade between the chapter house and the refectory at the same hour each morning, and in all that time the rhythm had never varied: the verger's keys at seven, the choir's warm-up drifting down from the triforium at half past, the first tourists shuffling in at nine with their folded maps and hushed voices. The cloisters were a metronome. She had stopped hearing it years ago, the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat.

Now she heard it again, because something had been added to it.

Footsteps. Not hers. Not the verger's — Weller's gait had a hitch in it, a lifetime of stone stairs written into his left knee. Not the dean's, not the organ scholar's, not any of the half-dozen regulars whose movements she could identify blind. These steps were measured, unhurried, and they kept a fixed distance behind her — never closing, never falling away. When she stopped at the window that overlooked the garth to pretend interest in the frost-burned yew, the steps stopped too, three arches back, in the blind spot where the pillar's shadow pooled.

She did not turn. Turning was a confession. Turning said *I know you're there*, and whoever it was — and she had her suspicions, narrowing by the day to a single heavy-set silhouette in a dark coat — would simply melt into the tourist current and return tomorrow, patient as water.

Instead she took out her phone and, cradling it as if reading messages, angled the black screen toward the glass of the cloister window. The reflection was smeared, ghosted, useless for faces — but it was enough to confirm the coat, the build, the stillness. He stood with his hands clasped behind his back, the posture of a man admiring architecture. The posture of the cathedral warden at every fundraising dinner she'd ever worked, holding doors, pouring wine, watching everything.

*Mr. Trennick.* She let the name settle in her mind without letting it reach her face. The warden. Keeper of keys, keeper of the fabric of the building, keeper — if Edith's diary was to be believed, if the pattern she'd mapped in her own hand-drawn chart was to be believed — of the silences.

She resumed walking. The footsteps resumed behind her.

There was a strange comfort in it, once the first cold wash of fear had passed through her and drained away. Being followed meant she was right. Being followed meant the diary, the dates, the three accidents aligned against Nell Pryce's name — all of it had finally frightened someone powerful enough to spend resources on her. You did not put a man on a woman who was wrong.

She climbed the steps to the archive, unlocked the door with hands that were almost steady, and did not look back. The footsteps faded below, unhurried, satisfied. *Let him report that I noticed nothing. Let him think the archivist is a woman who lives entirely in her papers.*

She locked the door, drew the blind, and sat in the dark for a full minute, listening to her own pulse.

Then she took out the diary, and Edith Marsh's small, disciplined handwriting, and began again at the beginning.

* * *

The sea wall road east of the cathedral was a single lane most of its length, hedged on the landward side and open to the mudflats on the other, running the last mile from the village of Porthmear to the old ferry slip. Yara drove it at dusk because dusk was when the tide tables in her head told her the flats would be at their most revealing — channels braiding silver through the mud, the skeleton of the old ferry steps emerging from the silt like ribs. Edith's final known evening had ended somewhere along this stretch. Yara had walked it twice in daylight. She wanted to see it as Edith had seen it, with the light going and the water coming in.

The cracked thing happened in the space of four seconds, and afterward she would replay those seconds so many times they wore smooth, like a stone handled in a pocket.

Headlights behind her. High beams, wrong for the road — there was nothing ahead of them but her and the hedge, no oncoming traffic to justify full beam, and the light sat too high and too wide to be an ordinary car. It filled her mirror, turned the road into a tunnel of white. She touched the brake gently, meaning to let it pass.

It didn't pass. It came on faster.

The impact, when it came, was not the cinematic shunt she'd half-expected — no scream of metal, no spinning. It was a shove, a heavy shoulder against her rear bumper, the kind of nudge a farmer gives a stuck tractor. Her car fishtailed. She corrected. The headlights fell back, then surged again, and the second impact caught her quarter-panel and put her left wheels onto the grass verge, and the verge was soft with winter rain, and the sea wall was three feet to her right, and beyond the wall the flats lay black and glistening under a sky the color of wet slate.

She did the only thing the geography allowed. She turned *into* the wall.

Not into it — along it. She wrestled the car parallel, tires hissing on wet grass, and brought it to a stop with the driver's door six inches from the stone. Behind her the headlights stopped too, hung there, a held breath of white light. She could see nothing through the glare but the shape of the grille. The engine idled. Hers ticked and steamed.

For ten seconds — she counted them, she would always count them — nothing moved. Then the high beams clicked off, and in the sudden dark she caught the other car reversing, unhurried, until it reached the bend, where it paused. Paused. As if to let her read the plate.

The plate was caked in mud. Of course it was.

Then it was gone, and the road was just a road, and the tide was coming in over the flats with a sound like a long exhale, and Yara sat with her hands welded to the wheel and discovered she was shaking so hard her teeth chattered.

She got out. Her legs held, which surprised her. The rear of the car was crumpled — the bumper pushed in, the boot lid sprung so it no longer sat flush. And the windshield — she saw it when she came around to the driver's side, in the light of her phone — the windshield had cracked. A single impact star low on the passenger side, a bloom of white fractures the size of a saucer, thrown when something — a stone, she supposed, or a piece of the hedge, or a fist — had struck it. She had no memory of that. Her body had filed it somewhere she couldn't reach.

The flats were filling. She could see the first channels losing their edges, the mud going soft and dark, the water walking in with the patience of something that had never once been in a hurry. If the second shove had landed a half-second later, if her correction had come a half-second later, if the verge had been a foot softer — she stood at the wall and looked down at the place where the water was beginning to lick the stone, and she understood, with a clarity that felt almost like calm, that she was alive because of arithmetic. Tide tables. The same tables she'd been reading for weeks. The water that would have taken her had not yet arrived.

*They knew the tide too,* she thought. *They just counted it differently.*

She drove back to town at thirty miles an hour with the cracked windshield spreading its white bloom an inch at a time, and she did not stop at the police station.

* * *

Hale would have come. She knew that. She stood in her kitchen with the kettle going and her phone in her hand, DS Hale's number already scrolled to, her thumb hovering, and she thought about what would happen if she pressed it.

He would come. He'd take photos of the bumper, the panel, the star in the glass. He'd ask her, in his flat careful way, whether anyone had reason to want to frighten her, and she would have to decide, standing in her own kitchen, how much to tell him — and whatever she told him would go into a report, and the report would go up the chain, and the chain, as Hale himself had told her with a redacted file and a face like a man apologizing to a stranger, ended somewhere above him in rooms she would never see. The cathedral's founding families had been funding half the town's institutions since before either of them was born. A report about a bump on a dark road, with no witness, no plate, no injury — it would be filed under *traffic, minor*, and it would tell Trennick exactly one thing: that the archivist had gone running to the police, and could be handled the same way the verger and the stonemason and the solicitor had been handled. Quietly. Administratively. With paperwork where the violence should be.

Or she could say nothing, and keep the only advantage she had, which was that they did not know how much she knew.

The kettle clicked off. She made the tea she didn't want, because her hands needed the work, and she sat at her kitchen table with the diary in front of her and the phone face-down beside it, and she made the decision the way she made all her decisions now — slowly, and then all at once.

She would not report it.

But she would not pretend it hadn't happened, either. She took a fresh sheet of acid-free paper from her work bag — archivist's habit; if it mattered enough to keep, it mattered enough to keep properly — and she wrote down the time, the place, the two impacts, the pause at the bend, the mud-caked plate, the star-shaped crack in the glass. She described the headlights and the height of them. She wrote *Trennick?* and then, after a moment, crossed nothing out, because an archive records what is, not what is comfortable. She signed and dated it, folded it into an envelope, and wrote on the front, in her most formal hand: *To be opened in the event of my death or disappearance. — Y. Okonkwo.*

Then she sealed it and put it in the bottom of the biscuit tin where she kept her passports, because the best hiding place in any house is the one that looks like it belongs to someone boring.

The windshield she left alone. She cleaned the mud off the bumper, because leaving mud on it felt like letting them mark her, but the crack stayed. In the morning she would drive to work with the fracture blooming in her sightline, a white star at the edge of everything she looked at, and every time the light caught it she would feel the shove again — and she would let herself feel it, all of it, because fear was fuel if you refused to let it become paralysis. They had wanted her frightened off. Instead they had given her a daily exhibit, mounted in glass at the edge of her vision: *this is what the truth costs. Pay it.*

She opened the diary to the page she'd been turning over all week — the entry from the 11th of October, the one that read, in Edith's neat liturgical shorthand: *Feria secunda. The Lady chapel, after Compline. N. says the ledger must go out before the Dedication feast or it never will.* And beneath it, almost as an afterthought, the line that had kept Yara awake: *He watches the cloisters now. He is not clever, only patient. Patience is worse.*

*He watches the cloisters now.*

Edith had been watched too. Edith had written about it in the same flat, precise hand she used for everything, and then she had gone on writing for three more weeks — which meant Edith had done what Yara had just decided to do. She had kept working with the watcher at her back. She had let the fear sit in her chest like a coal and had used its heat.

Yara looked at the entry for a long time. Then she turned back through the diary, page by page, faster now, the pages whispering, looking for the pattern she had half-seen and never named. The liturgical references. *Feria secunda.* *Within the octave.* *After Compline.* *Before the Dedication.* She'd read them as Edith's habit, the calendar of a devout woman marking her days. But Edith had been an accountant before she'd been a parishioner. Accountants didn't decorate their notes. They coded them.

Yara pulled the cathedral's ordo — the annual liturgical calendar — from her bag, spread it beside the diary, and began to lay the dates side by side.

Outside, the wind had come up off the water, and somewhere below her window the town was closing its shutters for the night. In the white star of the cracked windshield, propped against the kitchen wall where she could see it from the table, the fractures caught the lamplight and held it — and Yara, with the calendar open and Edith's code half-broken in her head, understood suddenly what the Dedication feast had meant in October, and which ledger Edith had meant, and why three people had died in the weeks after it.

Her hand went still on the page.

*Oh, Edith,* she thought. *You didn't disappear at all.*
