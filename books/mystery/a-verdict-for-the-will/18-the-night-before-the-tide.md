## 18. The Night Before the Tide

The postcard had been on my desk for six days, and I had read it so many times the ink was beginning to look less like print and more like something I'd dreamed. No words. Just a tide table for the spring equinox — high water at 4:12 in the morning, low at 10:26 — and beneath it, in a hand I did not know but trusted anyway because it had crossed forty years of silence to reach me, a single date.

Tomorrow.

I stood at my office window above the cloister garth and watched the light go out of the cathedral stone by stone, the way it does in March, the west face holding its gold long after everything else has gone grey. Somewhere under that stone was a chamber that wasn't on any plan, and behind fresh mortar, and beyond that a passage the sea opened twice a year like a mouth deciding whether to speak. Tomorrow morning, at low water, it would open. And Nell Pryce — scarred, old, alive against every record and every grave certificate Ambrose had ever signed — would walk out of it carrying documents that could bury the founding families of St. Brannoc's.

If she made it. If I made it. If whoever had run me off the road two weeks ago didn't decide that tomorrow the tide should take two more bodies instead of giving one back.

I picked up the telephone before I could talk myself out of it.

* * *

Hale answered on the fourth ring, and I knew from the pause before he spoke that he'd been waiting for this call the way you wait for weather.

"It's Yara."

"I know who it is." A chair creaked somewhere on his end. "You've got the postcard."

Not a question. "How?"

"Because I've got one too." He said it flatly, but I heard what was underneath it — the same thing that had been underneath him since he'd handed me the redacted file in his car with the engine running, an ally who could only afford to be one in the dark. "Came to the station yesterday. No return address. My superintendent thinks it's a prank."

"And what do you think?"

Another pause. Through the receiver I could hear the sounds of his house — a television murmuring, a kettle beginning its climb toward a whistle. The ordinary furniture of a life being lived while we discussed exhumations.

"I think," Hale said, "that if a woman declared dead in 1979 walks out of a flooded crypt at dawn tomorrow, I would very much prefer to be standing there when it happens than reading about it Monday." The kettle began to whistle; he moved away from it, voice dropping. "Sea wall. Below the cathedral steps. Low water's what — half ten? I'll be there at ten. Not before. If anyone asks, I'm walking the coastal path. Alone. That part will even be true until it isn't."

"Hale." I pressed my forehead against the cold glass of the window. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me yet." And then, quieter, the closest he had ever come to saying what he actually meant: "Yara. Whatever's down there — don't go into the passage before I arrive. Promise me."

I looked across the garth at the dark bulk of the tower, at the one lit window of the vestry where Ambrose was almost certainly still awake, still wrestling whatever angel had been sitting on his chest since I showed him the opened chamber and watched forty years of complicity crack across his face.

"I promise," I said, which was true, and hung up before I could add *unless*.

* * *

The knock came at nine-forty, soft, the way you knock when you hope no one answers.

I opened the door of my lodgings on Chapel Lane and found no one there. The lane was empty in both directions, lamplight lying in pale coins on the wet cobbles, the smell of the tide coming up off the harbour. For a moment I thought I'd imagined it — I'd been sleeping badly enough that imagination and memory had started sharing a wardrobe — and then I looked down.

On the mat, laid parallel to the threshold as precisely as something in a reliquary case, lay a key.

The crypt key. Heavy brass, older than either of us, worn smooth at the bow by fifty years of Ambrose's thumb. I knew it the way I knew my own handwriting. I had watched him turn it in the lock a hundred times, had borrowed it a dozen times under his supervision, had returned it each time to the hook inside the vestry cupboard where it lived among the cassocks like a small black secret.

He had not knocked again. He was not waiting at the corner. The lane gave me nothing but wind and the far-off complaint of a gull, and I understood that the absence of him was the message. He couldn't give it to me in daylight. He couldn't hand it over with his eyes open, or say aloud what the key said for him: *I can't stop you, and God forgive me, I'm not sure I want to.*

I picked it up. It was warm. He must have been carrying it in his pocket, walking here and standing outside my door working up to the moment, and the metal had taken his heat, and now it took mine.

Ambrose had taught me to catalogue. Had taught me that a record is a moral act, that every entry is a small promise made to the future. He had also, I now knew, signed burial certificates for people with no graves, had accepted the founding families' money to keep the flood's dead "at rest," had let three deaths be called accidents because the alternative was a reckoning he could not survive. Love and betrayal in the same breath — I had learned that phrase about him weeks ago and still hadn't decided which word carried more weight.

But he had left the key.

I closed the door and turned the lock and stood a long moment in my narrow hall with the brass cold against my palm, and then I went to my desk, because there was one more thing to do before morning, and it was the thing I was best at in all the world.

* * *

I wrote through the night.

Not the diary — Edith's diary stayed wrapped in acid-free tissue in the strongbox, its evidence intact, untouchable, the one object in this whole affair whose integrity I would defend with my life. What I wrote was mine. My account. My verdict, though I didn't let myself call it that until nearly the end.

I started with the verger, Thomas Renwick, because he was first. October the ninth. The fall from the bell tower scaffolding that the coroner called a misjudged step in poor light. I wrote down what I'd found when I finally pulled the maintenance ledger: the scaffold inspection signed off two days before his death by a hand that hadn't inspected anything, and the entry in Edith's diary eleven days earlier — *N.P.'s name came up again tonight. T.R. went white as ash. He says he'll deal with it. I don't believe him.* Deal with it. Or be dealt with.

Then the stonemason, Gideon Farrow, February the fourteenth, crushed under a lintel that had hung safely over the north door for a century. The rope that frayed exactly where fraying wouldn't show. The diary entry eight days prior: *G.F. knows where things were put. He's frightened. He asked me outright if I was passing papers out. I lied to him and hated myself for it.*

Then the solicitor, Peter Quillan, drowned in the harbour shallows in April with no alcohol in his blood and no reason on earth to have been on that slipway at midnight. *P.Q. has the Vane letters. He wants money for them. Money won't keep him quiet forever.*

And then Edith herself — not dead, hidden, vanished into her own disappearance with the precision of a woman who had spent months learning how this place buried things. I wrote what I believed: that she had walked out of her life on the second of May and into the chamber below the crypt, or out along the sea wall to wherever she'd been going ever since, leaving the diary to continue without her in a hand indistinguishable from her own because it *was* her own, written ahead, planted, a fuse burning slowly toward the truth. She hadn't disappeared. She'd deployed herself.

Four deaths. One pattern. Each preceded by an entry naming Nell Pryce, each ruled accident, each investigated just long enough to close. And behind all of it, the warden — I wrote his name, and my pen pressed hard enough to emboss the next three pages — the cathedral's own warden, the founding families' quiet man with the ring of keys, seen once at the top of the cloister stairs watching me with the patience of someone measuring a coffin lid.

When I finished the narrative I sat back and read it through twice, archivist's discipline overriding exhaustion, checking every date against the diary, every claim against the redacted file Hale had given me, every inference marked as inference. Then I added the last page, separate, and this one I wrote slowly:

*I, Yara Okonkwo, archivist of St. Brannoc's Cathedral, make this statement freely and in full knowledge that I may not survive to repeat it.*

It sounded melodramatic written down. It was also simply accurate, and accuracy has never cared how it sounds.

I sealed the pages in two envelopes. The first I addressed to Detective Sergeant Hale, care of the station, and wrote on the back: *To be opened only if I fail to contact you within 48 hours of the spring equinox.* The second I addressed to myself, care of the county records office, with instructions to hold unopened pending my collection — a letter that would sit in an official queue, timestamped, undeniable, a splinter of paper lodged where no friendly hand could reach it without leaving a mark.

Insurance. That was the honest word for it. Not courage — courage would be walking to the sea wall tomorrow with nothing but my name. This was what an archivist does instead of praying: she makes copies, and she distributes them, and she trusts the system of records more than she trusts the mercy of men.

I posted the first envelope through the station's night slot myself, at one in the morning, standing in the yellow wash of the exterior lamp while the town slept around me and every parked car looked like the kind of car that follows an archivist home. Nothing moved. The slot swallowed my envelope with a sound like a held breath released.

The second would go out with the morning post. Which meant that between now and dawn, it would sit in my coat pocket, over my heart, the way people used to carry letters they couldn't bear to send.

* * *

I didn't sleep. I tried — lay down fully clothed at two, listened to the wind work at the loose slate above my window, got up again at twenty past. Instead I made tea I didn't drink and stood at the window watching the town's few lights and thinking about belonging.

That was the word underneath everything, I realized. Not justice, not truth, not even fear. Belonging. Three years in this town, and I had built my life inside the cathedral's gravity the way a moon builds its orbit — gratefully, without examining the terms. Ambrose's approval. The memorial teas where the Ashcombes and the Vanes smiled at me with warmth that curdled the moment I said the word *flood*. The sense of being *kept*, which is a different thing entirely from being welcome, and which I had mistaken for the same thing because being kept felt so much like home.

Tomorrow I would walk out of that orbit. Whatever happened at the sea wall — whether Nell Pryce came out of the mist with her forty years of documents, whether the warden's headlights swept the shingle, whether Hale arrived in time or arrived too late — after tomorrow I would belong here differently, or not at all. There was no version of the morning in which the founding families forgave me for pulling their knot apart in public. There was only the version where I stopped needing them to.

I found, examining the thought honestly, that it didn't hurt the way I'd expected. It hurt the way a tooth stops hurting when the nerve finally dies — a strange relief inside the loss.

At some point near four I unpacked the diary from the strongbox, not to read it but to say goodbye to it, because tomorrow it would become evidence, handled by strangers, photographed under institutional lights, its private griefs made public property. I touched the cover once, the worn leather Edith's hands had polished, and told her — silently, feeling foolish, doing it anyway — *you hid yourself well, and I found you, and tomorrow the water gives the rest of it back.*

Then I rewrapped it, relocked the box, and dressed for the cold.

* * *

The town was still asleep when I left, but the sea was already awake. I heard it before I reached the bottom of Chapel Lane — a long, rhythmic exhale, the sound of something enormous turning over in preparation. The sky in the east had not yet decided between grey and gold.

Halfway down Fore Street I passed the cathedral close, and I made myself look. The vestry window was dark now; Ambrose had finally slept, or finally hadn't. But on the step of the south porch, placed where the early light would find it, someone had set a single candle in a jar, lit, sheltered from the wind, burning steadily toward morning.

A vigil. The oldest kind there is. Not absolution — I had meant what I intended to tell him, that honesty is not the same as forgiveness — but a man kneeling in the only language he had left, saying *I know what wakes today, and I am sorry, and I am watching.*

I did not stop. I walked past it, down toward the water, the envelope riding over my heart and the crypt key heavy in my pocket, and behind me the candle burned, and ahead of me the tide was already drawing back off the shingle, further and further, baring the stones it had kept for forty years —

— and at the sea wall, half lost in the pre-dawn mist, a figure was already standing where Hale had promised to be, an hour too early, facing the water as if he'd been sent to meet it.
