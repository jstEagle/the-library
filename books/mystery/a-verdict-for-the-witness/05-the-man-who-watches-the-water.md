## 5. The Man Who Watches the Water

The tide at Gull Point went out on a Tuesday, and Elodie went with it.

She told herself it was procedure. The drowned man — John Doe, no name yet, no dental match in the county system — had surfaced at the base of the rocks below the point, and the water that gave him up would have taken other things with it when it left: a shoe, a phone, a scrap of something that could tell her who he'd been before the sea finished its work with him. Coroner's habit. Walk the tideline after a recovery, see what the water surrendered late.

But she knew, in the honest part of herself that she usually kept locked in the same drawer as her father's watch, that it was more than procedure. The clothes had been hers. The coat — the gray wool one with the torn pocket lining she'd never mended — had been hers, and the flannel shirt she'd bought at the hardware store because it was the only one long enough in the sleeve, and the boots she'd resoled twice rather than replace. And the watch. Her father's watch, with the hairline crack across the crystal she'd been looking at since she was nine years old, since the morning he didn't come home from the *Marrow Queen* and the harbor master had handed her his effects in a plastic bag.

She had identified all of it in her own morgue, standing over a stranger's drowned body dressed in her own grief, and Sheriff Dane Rook had looked at her with those tired, kind eyes and said, *Clothes prove nothing, Elodie. Somebody could've taken them off a line. Off your porch. You ought to get a lock.*

She had gone home and inventoried her closet like a woman counting her teeth. Everything was there except the things on the corpse. Which meant someone had been inside her cottage. Someone had opened her drawers, lifted the watch from the dish on her nightstand where she kept it, and carried her life out into the night and put it on a dead man.

*Clothes prove nothing.* She turned the phrase over as she walked the flats, her boots sinking into the gray-brown sand that the low tide exposed for a quarter mile, all the way down to where the kelp lay in dark ropes and the crabs went about their small, indifferent business. Rook wasn't a fool. She'd known him since they were children — he'd been the boy who pulled her hair in the fourth grade and then, the same year, punched Teddy Marsh for saying her father's drowning was a drunk's drowning. He was a decent man wearing down like a shoreline. And decent men wearing down said what the pressure wanted them to say.

*Clothes prove nothing.* Which meant someone wanted him to say it.

She worked the tideline methodically, the way she worked a body, from the known to the unknown. A gull, dead, its wings folded wrong. A child's sandal, sun-bleached to the color of bone, no child missing in Marrow Bay that she knew of. A length of polypropylene rope, frayed, the kind sold at Harrow Marine Supply in fifty-yard coils. She catalogued each in the notebook she carried — she catalogued everything; it was the only prayer she knew — and when she reached the rocks at the base of the point, where the water had taken the man and given him back, she stopped.

There was someone on the bluff above her.

He was standing at the edge where the grass gave way to the crumbling clay, and he was holding a camera — not a phone, an actual camera, old and black and heavy-looking, with a lens he was adjusting with slow, deliberate turns. He was gaunt the way winter is gaunt, all edges and angles, a tall man in a canvas coat the color of driftwood, and he was photographing the shoreline. Not the water. The shoreline. The exact stretch of it she was walking, the rocks, the tideline, the place where the body had come ashore.

Elodie did not move. She had spent eleven years in a morgue learning the discipline of stillness — the dead don't mind being watched, but the living do, and she had learned to watch the living the way she watched the dead: without disturbing them, without letting them know they were being seen.

He was too far away for her to read his face, but she didn't need to. She read his body the way she read a chart. The hunch of the shoulders wasn't casual. He wasn't a birdwatcher or a tourist — Marrow Bay got maybe six tourists a year and none of them came to Gull Point in February to photograph rocks. The way he stood, weight forward on the balls of his feet, the camera held close to his chest between shots like something he was protecting — that was a man doing a job he'd done ten thousand times. A man who watched the water the way she watched her tables. Professionally. Religiously. As if something owed him an answer.

She took one step forward, gravel shifting under her boot, and the sound — small as it was, swallowed almost entirely by the wind coming off the water — reached him like a gunshot.

He turned. For one instant, across two hundred yards of cold air, their eyes met. She couldn't have described his face afterward if she'd tried; distance and the flat gray light erased everything except an impression, a paleness, a stillness that matched her own. But she felt the look land on her like a hand on her shoulder. He knew her. She was certain of it the way she was certain of a cause of death — not from any single finding, but from the whole of the body of evidence. He knew exactly who she was, and he had known she would be here, and he had been photographing the shore where her clothes had walked without her.

Then he was gone.

It happened so fast it was almost funny — a man that tall, that thin, folding himself down and back over the crest of the bluff in three seconds flat, like a shadow when a cloud passes. She stood in the wet sand with her heart going hard against her ribs and told herself to run after him, and her legs, sensibly, refused. She was fifty-one years old and alone on a beach where a man had recently washed up dressed in her skin, and whatever instinct had kept her alive through three years of digging into deaths that powerful people wanted called accidents was telling her, very clearly, *not that way. Not yet.*

Instead she did what she always did. She observed. She recorded.

The bluff path was a goat track of a thing, up through the gorse, and she climbed it slowly, cataloguing as she went: a boot print in the clay, deep at the toe, a heavy man's tread though the man himself had been thin — worn boots, heels struck down. A single long gray hair caught on the gorse, not hers. And at the top, where the path met the old coast road, the grass was pressed flat in a narrow line leading north, toward the harbor, toward town, and she followed it with her eyes until the drizzle took it.

She stood there a long time, the wind pushing at her back like a hand between her shoulder blades, and thought: *He wasn't photographing me. He was photographing the place before I got to it. Or after. He's documenting.*

So was she. It was, absurdly, the first moment in three years that she had felt less alone.

* * *

She went back the next morning, and the morning after that. This was her nature, and she had stopped apologizing for it sometime in her forties. The dead taught patience; you couldn't rush a body into telling you what it knew, and you couldn't rush a watcher into showing himself either. On the third morning she brought field glasses and sat in her car at the pull-off above Gull Point with a thermocouple of coffee going cold beside her, and she watched the water do its patient work and waited.

He didn't come. But she noticed other things, because noticing was the whole of her trade.

She noticed that the mooring cleat at the base of the point — an old iron cleat bolted into the rock, a relic from when fishing boats had worked the lee side of the point before the marina was built — was not the way she'd left it. She had walked past it three times in as many days. It was the kind of object she saw the way she saw everything: completely, without effort, the way other people breathed. Someone had moved it. The rust ring on the rock around its base had been disturbed, a crescent of clean stone showing where the iron had shifted a quarter inch, and the marine growth on the shank was scraped.

She crouched beside it. The cleat was cold and furred with lichen, and she worked it loose with both hands, the bolts groaning, and underneath, folded into a rectangle the size of a matchbook, waterproofed in a sleeve cut from a freezer bag, was a note.

Not a note. A cipher.

She sat back on her heels and stared at it for a full minute before she touched it. The paper was graph paper, torn small, and across it in a fine, precise hand — an engineer's hand, a draftsman's hand — ran rows of characters. Not letters. Numbers and symbols, columns of them, arranged in groups of three and four, with small tick marks between the groups. At the bottom, underlined twice, a sequence of paired digits: *07-14. 09-02. 11-19.*

She knew those numbers. Her hands went cold around the paper, colder than the morning had made them.

She had been staring at numbers like those for two weeks, penciled in the margins of the battered tide table book that lived in the drawer of the morgue's front desk, the book somebody — she no longer believed it was coincidence, and she was no longer sure she believed it was malice either — had been keeping as a ledger of the dead. Three names, three dates, three deaths the police had ruled accidents and she had ruled otherwise, each one annotated in that same fine, precise hand with paired digits she hadn't yet broken.

Whoever wrote the tide-table ledger had just written to her. Had known she would walk the tideline — of course he had known, he'd photographed the shore, he'd been watching her the way she watched everything — and had known she was the one person in Marrow Bay who noticed a mooring cleat out of true, and had left her a message in the only language they apparently shared: things written where only the careful would find them.

She put the cipher in her coat's inner pocket, over her breast, the way you'd carry a letter from someone you loved, and then she sat in her car for an hour with the engine off, watching the point, feeling the paper against her ribs like a second heartbeat.

She should be afraid. Some part of her registered that, distantly, the way you register a weather report for somewhere else. The drowned man had worn her clothes. Someone had entered her home, her morgue, her life. And now a gaunt stranger was leaving coded messages under hardware on the shoreline, and for all she knew the cipher was a lure, the cleat was a trap, the whole arrangement was designed to draw her somewhere at some hour when the tide would do to her what it had done to the man in her coat.

But she read people the way she read bodies, and the body of this — the worn boots, the old camera, the quarter-inch shift of a mooring cleat, the graph paper and the draftsman's hand, the *patience* of it, a message left to be found by the one person who'd find it, no threat, no demand, no name — did not read as malice. Malice was loud. Malice had come to her already, wearing her father's watch. This was something else. This was the handwriting of a man who had been keeping records for a very long time, alone, for an audience of no one, and who had finally, desperately, decided his records needed a second keeper.

*He communicates the way I do,* she thought. *In things left unsaid. In things left where only the dead's friends will look.*

She started the car. The heater coughed and caught. Out past the point, the water lay flat and pewter-colored, keeping its accounts the way it always had, and Elodie Crane drove back toward town with a stranger's cipher against her heart and, for the first time since the man in her coat had washed ashore, something in her chest that was not fear.

It felt dangerously like hope. She didn't trust it. She'd learned not to trust anything in Marrow Bay that came in on the tide.

But that night, in the morgue after hours, with the tide table book open on her desk under the green lamp and the cipher flattened beside it, she began to match the paired digits to the penciled dates — and three lines in, her breath stopped, because the numbers weren't dates at all.

They were page references. And the first one pointed to an entry she had never been able to read: a line in the old ledger, smudged past recovery, dated the spring of 1989.

The night the *Corinne Mae* went down.
