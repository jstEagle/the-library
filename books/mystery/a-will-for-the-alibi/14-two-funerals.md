## 14. Two Funerals

There are two funerals at every memorial service. The one on the program — hymns, eulogies, a photograph of the deceased looking younger and kinder than he ever was — and the one happening in the corners, in the pauses between casseroles, in the way certain people refuse to stand near each other.

I came to Aldous Voss's memorial for the second funeral.

The venue was a community hall in Gullwrack with water-stained ceiling tiles and folding chairs that had been arranged by someone who understood grief as logistics: family in front, acquaintances behind, an aisle wide enough for a casket that wasn't there because Aldous had been cremated three weeks ago and his ashes were, according to the program, "at sea." I found that phrase interesting. *At sea.* The man had spent thirty years putting things at sea. It seemed only fair the sea got him back.

I signed the guest book under a name I use for occasions like this — Ruth Calloway, freelance archivist — and took a seat near the back where I could watch the room without being watched back. Old habit from my days covering courtrooms. The best place to observe people is always the place they've stopped performing for.

Except nobody here had stopped performing. That was the thing about this room. Everyone in it was acting.

* * *

I'd gotten the invitation through Dev Lindqvist's sister, who ran the hall's booking calendar and whom I'd bought two coffees and forty minutes of conversation the week before. *Memorial for Aldous Voss, open to the community, he was on the harbor board for years.* She'd said it the way you'd announce a bake sale. Whatever the circle knew about Aldous Voss — whatever Maren's cropped photographs and Ilsa's sealed lips and Dev's warm, frightened warnings were protecting me from — the town itself remembered him as a fixture. A benefactor. A man whose name was on a bench by the seawall.

Aldous Voss had died in hospice care, of what his obituary called complications following a long illness. He was seventy-nine. He had outlived the brother-in-law he may or may not have murdered by exactly three decades, and he had outlived his wife's fear by — well, that was the question, wasn't it. Because Maren was still afraid. I'd sat across from her in that care home with its photographs missing one face, and I'd watched her deflect every mention of her husband with the smoothness of long practice, and underneath the calm I'd felt something else. Not grief. Anticipation. Or dread wearing anticipation's coat.

Now the whole circle was in one room for the first time since 1995, and I intended to watch them like weather.

They arrived separately, which told me everything before anyone said a word.

Halvard Brandt came first, the fisherman, filling a doorway in a suit that fit like an apology. He shook hands with strangers too firmly and stood too close to the coffee urn, a man more comfortable with ropes than rooms. His eyes went to the front table where the framed photograph of Aldous sat propped against a spray of carnations, and his jaw did something complicated, and then he looked away and didn't look back. Guilt looks different on every face. On Halvard's it looked like indigestion.

Ilsa Berg arrived ten minutes later in a nurse's posture even out of uniform — spine straight, hands folded, scanning exits. She'd retired from the regional medical center six years ago; I'd confirmed that much through the licensing board. She took a seat in the second row, not the first, and when the pastor began his remarks she closed her eyes during the parts about Aldous's generosity. You can tell a lot from how someone receives praise of a person they knew. Some people flinch. Some people go very still. Ilsa went very still, the way you do when you're listening for something under the words.

Petra Ondine, the teacher — no. Wait. Let me get this right, because getting it right is the whole job. Petra Ondine was the teacher. Corwin Hale was the other one. I'll come back to Corwin Hale, because the chapter of this story that belongs to Corwin Hale doesn't start until later, and I want to be precise about when I noticed him, because precision is the difference between an accusation and a slander.

Petra arrived with her husband and stayed near the door, checking her phone, checking the time, a woman attending a duty rather than a death. She'd taught fourth grade in Gullwrack for thirty-one years. Half the town had been her students. She hugged Halvard briefly, kissed Ilsa's cheek, and then positioned herself — and I mean *positioned*, deliberately, the way you'd arrange furniture — equidistant from everyone else in the circle. A woman managing sightlines. A woman who had rehearsed this.

And Dev.

Dev Lindqvist came in last, wet from rain, holding his cap in both hands like a man entering a church he'd been excommunicated from. He scanned the room once, fast, the way you check a tide chart — and his eyes snagged on me for half a second before sliding off. He knew Ruth Calloway wasn't real. I watched him decide, visibly, not to blow my cover, and I watched what it cost him. Then he went and stood beside Halvard at the coffee urn, and the two men said nothing to each other, and their silence was louder than the pastor's entire eulogy.

Four witnesses. One dead man's ashes. One absent brother. And somewhere in this room, if Maren's terror was worth the fortune she'd paid me, one person who had spent thirty years waiting for the tide to hand him a murder.

* * *

The eulogies were a masterclass in what people don't say.

The harbor board chairman spoke about Aldous's decades of service and never once mentioned Tomas Voss, which was like delivering a eulogy for a lighthouse without mentioning the rocks. A nephew from the mainland spoke about Aldous's model ships, how patient he'd been teaching a boy to rig the tiny sails, and I thought: *patient.* There's a kind of patience that builds things and a kind that waits for them to break, and I was beginning to suspect Aldous Voss had possessed both.

Then the pastor invited anyone who wished to share a memory, and the room did that thing rooms do — the collective inhale, the negotiation of who will move first — and Corwin Hale stood up.

I need to describe Corwin Hale properly, because for most of this investigation he was a footnote in my notes. *Retired educator. Moved to Gullwrack in the late eighties. Close to the Voss household.* When I'd finally tracked down the full roster of the circle — fisherman, nurse, teacher, restorer — Corwin hadn't made the list. He wasn't one of the four witnesses whose alibis matched down to weather details no honest memory could hold. He was adjacent. Family-adjacent, Ilsa would later say, though she wouldn't say it to me for another month. He'd been Aldous's protégé, his chess partner, the young teacher who'd come to town and attached himself to the great man's orbit with the devotion of a moon to a planet.

He was sixty now, silver at the temples, dressed in a charcoal blazer that cost more than Halvard's boat earned in a season. He walked to the front the way men walk when they've given speeches before — unhurried, claiming the space, making the microphone wait for him.

"I wasn't going to speak," he said, and smiled, and the room smiled back, because some faces are built to be believed. "Aldous hated fuss. But I've learned that funerals aren't for the dead. They're for the living. And the living need to hear that a difficult man was also a good one."

*A difficult man.* I wrote the phrase in my notebook and underlined it twice. In thirty years of true crime, I have never once heard a genuinely good man described at his own funeral as *difficult.* Difficult is the word you use when the list of true adjectives would clear the room.

Corwin spoke for four minutes. He spoke about Aldous's loyalty — *he protected the people he loved, sometimes from themselves* — and about storms, how Aldous used to say you couldn't stop the weather, you could only build better seawalls. He spoke about the Voss family with such tender familiarity that several older attendees nodded along, and he mentioned Maren — "who carries more than any of us know, and has carried it with a grace I hope someday to understand" — and the room went soft, and I went cold.

Because that sentence was a message. Delivered in public, deniable, wrapped in sympathy, but a message all the same. *I know what you carry, Maren. I understand it better than you think. And I am watching.*

Maren wasn't there, of course. She was ninety minutes up the coast in her care home, too frail to travel, or so the program said. But Corwin looked directly at the empty chair reserved for family when he said it, and I have sat across from enough killers and enough liars to know the difference between a man addressing an absence and a man addressing someone he believes is listening.

Then he returned to his seat, accepted two pats on the arm and a handshake from the harbor board chairman, and became again a mild retired teacher with kind eyes and excellent posture.

I put down my pen. My hand wasn't quite steady, and I wanted the next entry to be legible.

* * *

After the service, the hall filled with the shuffling economy of funeral receptions — trays of sandwiches, coffee in urns, the low murmur of a town trading thirty years of news in forty minutes. I worked the edges.

Ruth Calloway, freelance archivist, turned out to be useful. Archivists are the safest strangers in the world; everyone assumes you're interested in paper, not people. An elderly woman named Grethe told me about the flood of '98 and pointed out photographs on a side table — Aldous at regattas, Aldous at board meetings, Aldous shaking hands with a regional minister. No Tomas anywhere. No Maren either, except one shot from some long-ago summer picnic where a young woman stood at the frame's edge, half-turned away, her face angled so the camera caught mostly hair. Even in the town's archive, Maren had been edited out.

"Sad business, the brother," Grethe said, unprompted, in the way small-town women deliver intelligence they've been sitting on for decades. "Drowned off Gullwrack Point before you'd have been born, dear. They never found him."

"Do people still talk about it?"

"Oh, every storm." She lowered her voice with relish. "Old Rook, the harbor master — before his time, mind — he always said the boat was anchored. Who anchors a boat to drown?"

"Maybe he anchored to swim," I offered.

"In October?" Grethe said, and pressed a sandwich into my hand as if feeding the archivist were also my job, and moved on to fresher gossip.

Across the room, the circle had formed its constellation. Halvard and Dev at the window, shoulder to shoulder, speaking in the low continuous tone of men discussing engines — or alibis. Ilsa seated, untouched coffee, watching the door. Petra circulating with her husband, laughing at intervals that were slightly too regular, a metronome of normalcy. And Corwin Hale at the center of a small orbit of admirers, telling a story I couldn't hear but could read perfectly from the audience: heads tipping back, laughter, one woman touching his sleeve.

At one point he excused himself and crossed to the side table of photographs. He stood before the image of Aldous for a long moment — longer than grief, shorter than suspicion, calibrated precisely in between — and then he straightened the frame. It hadn't been crooked. He straightened it anyway, a quarter inch, squaring it to the table's edge with two fingers, and I thought: *there it is.*

You can learn a man from how he handles objects that belong to the dead. Most people touch them gently, apologetically. Some avoid them entirely. But a man who adjusts a photograph that isn't crooked — a man who needs the dead arranged just so — that's a man who has been keeping things in order for a very long time. Curating. Maintaining an exhibit.

Thirty years is a long time to dust a display case.

* * *

Dev caught me at the coat rack.

Not by accident. I'd watched him disengage from Halvard, cross the room on a path that looked casual and wasn't, and arrive at my elbow just as I reached for a coat that wasn't mine. In a hall full of witnesses, he chose the one spot with a wall at our backs and the crowd's noise as cover.

"Ruth, is it?" His voice was warm. His eyes weren't. Warmth and warning, braided together, exactly as they'd been in his workshop when he told me to let the past lie where the sea had put it.

"That's right."

"You do archival work." Not a question. "Funny. You've got the look of someone who reads people the way I read hulls. Looking for the soft spot."

"People are mostly soft spot," I said. "It's the frames that interest me. What holds them up."

Something moved behind his face — recognition, maybe, or regret. "Aldous Voss held a lot of things up," he said quietly. "For a lot of years. Whatever else he was."

"And whatever else he was — what was that?"

Dev looked at me for a long moment. Around us the reception murmured on, Petra's metronome laugh ticking, the urn hissing, the ordinary machinery of mourning. Somewhere in that noise, Corwin Hale was accepting condolences with the serenity of a man whose long vigil had entered its final act.

"You should go home, Miss Calloway," Dev said softly. "Gullwrack's no place for archives. Everything important here was never written down."

"Then how does anyone remember it?"

"We don't," he said. "That's the point. We agreed."

*We.* He heard himself say it a half-second after it left his mouth, and I watched the word land in him like a hook set deep. He turned away before I could answer, collected his cap, and walked out into the rain — and as the door swung shut behind him, I caught the reflection in the hall's fogged glass window: Corwin Hale, across the room, watching Dev leave.

Watching him the way a man watches a clock he wound himself.

I stayed twenty more minutes. Long enough to confirm what I already knew. Ilsa left without dessert. Petra checked her phone eleven times. Halvard drank three coffees and touched nothing else. And Corwin Hale gave a small speech to the harbor board chairman about possibly funding a new bench by the seawall — *Aldous would have liked that, something lasting, something that stands against the water* — while the storm the forecasters had been promising all week built itself out over the channel, black and patient, walking toward shore.

Two funerals, I thought, pulling on my coat. One for the man in the photograph. And one still pending — thirty years deferred, kept waiting, kept tidy, kept like a photograph squared to the edge of a table.

Someone in that room believed the second funeral was overdue. And as I stepped out into the rising wind and looked back through the glass at Corwin Hale shaking hands, serene and silver-templed, already saying his goodnights like a host ending a party —

— I realized he wasn't grieving Aldous at all.

He was counting.
