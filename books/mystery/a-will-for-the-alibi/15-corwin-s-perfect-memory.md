## 15. Corwin's Perfect Memory

Corwin Hale taught history for thirty-one years at the same coastal high school where he'd once taught Tomas Voss, and he retired the way he did everything — precisely, on schedule, with a small party in the staff room and a framed certificate he reportedly hung in his study facing the door so visitors would see it first.

I knew all this before I knocked on his door because I'd spent two days learning it, and because Corwin Hale was a man who left footprints on purpose. His public life was curated like an exhibit: the teaching awards, the local history column he wrote for the Gullwrack Gazette under the byline "C.H.," the volunteer hours at the maritime museum where he'd catalogued, of all things, the 1995 storm season. He wanted to be known. That interested me. People who want to be known are usually hiding one specific thing and hoping you'll be dazzled enough not to look behind it.

He answered the door before my second knock, which told me he'd been watching me come up the walk.

"Ms. Nystrom." He smiled the way a docent smiles at a guest who has arrived slightly early. "I wondered when you'd get to me."

"You wondered."

"I listen to your show." He stepped back to let me in. "Episode forty-one, the one about the lighthouse keeper's widow. Very good work. You have a talent for making people say more than they meant to."

"And yet here you are, inviting me inside."

"Here I am." He gestured toward a sitting room that looked like a museum had been compressed into a bungalow: ship models, tide tables framed behind glass, photographs of Gullwrack Point in every season. On the wall above the fireplace hung a class photo from 1994, and if you looked — and I looked — you could find Tomas Voss in the third row, seventeen years old and squinting into the sun, and beside him a younger Corwin Hale with his hand on the boy's shoulder like a claim.

I sat where he pointed. He poured tea without asking whether I wanted any, which was either hospitality or control, and handed me the cup with the handle turned exactly right.

"Mr. Hale—"

"Corwin."

"Corwin. I've been reconstructing the night of October ninth, 1995."

"Have you." He settled into the chair across from me, ankles crossed, hands folded on one knee. A man prepared to enjoy himself.

"Four witnesses placed Tomas Voss at the harbor that evening. Dev Marchetti saw him loading gear onto the *Kestrel*. Ilsa Brandt saw him buy fuel at the marina kiosk. Ruth Okafor — the teacher before you took over her classroom — saw him walking the seawall road around seven. And you saw him last of all, near the point, just after eight."

"That's all correct." No hesitation. Not even the performative kind. Just smooth, rehearsed-smooth agreement.

"Here's what interests me," I said. "The four accounts don't just align. They *interlock*. Dev mentions the fuel run, Ilsa confirms it with a time. Ruth's sighting explains why Dev lost sight of him between the dock and the road. Your sighting closes the gap between the road and the water. It's not four memories. It's one story told in four voices."

"People remember what they remember."

"People misremember," I said. "That's the whole science of it. Memory doesn't interlock. It frays. Two honest witnesses to the same event will disagree about the color of a coat, the order of events, whether it rained. Four independent accounts of one evening should be a mess of contradictions. Yours is a dovetail joint."

He sipped his tea. "You've clearly thought about this a great deal."

"I've also checked the weather." I let that sit. "October ninth, 1995. The official record says fog moved in off the water around nine p.m., visibility dropping to under fifty meters by ten. But that's the *official* record — the harbor log, written up the next morning. Here's the thing about fog, Corwin. It doesn't arrive everywhere at once. It rolls. The point can be socked in while the harbor sits clear as glass. So tell me how four witnesses, scattered along two miles of coastline, all described the same thing: fog rolling in 'just after eight,' thick enough to swallow a man on the water."

"They compared notes afterward, surely. Grief does that. People talk."

"Grief talks," I agreed. "But grief doesn't agree on the *minute*. And there's one more problem." I set down my cup. "Ruth Okafor died in 2019. Before she died, she gave a statement to a cold-case review — a formality, nobody expected anything. In it she says, quote, 'It was the kind of fog that comes in ahead of the wind shift, from the southeast. You could feel the temperature drop.' Unquote."

"A precise woman."

"She was a geography teacher, actually. But here's my question, Corwin, and I'd love your help with it." I leaned forward. "The southeast wind shift didn't begin until 9:40 p.m. We know that from the weather station at the light — the automated readings, timestamped, not written by any human hand. Ruth saw Tomas at seven. So either she remembered weather that hadn't happened yet —"

"Memory is imprecise—"

"—or she wasn't describing October ninth at all. She was describing a different night. A rehearsal. Someone walked those witnesses through their stories using a real evening as the template, and they picked the wrong one. Or the right one, depending on how you look at it." I watched his face. "Which means the alibi wasn't built from memory. It was built from a script. And scripts have authors."

For exactly one second, Corwin Hale stopped smiling.

It wasn't much. A stillness around the eyes, the teacup pausing half an inch above its saucer, the small muscles of his jaw going quiet. If you weren't watching for it, you'd have missed it entirely, and I understood suddenly why this man had held a secret for thirty years: he was very good, and most people are not watching.

Then the smile came back, warm as a lamp, and he set the cup down with a soft click.

"You should write a book," he said. "You have a novelist's instinct for pattern. Finding shapes in noise. It's a gift." He rose, crossed to the window, adjusted a blind that didn't need adjusting. "But let me offer a historian's caution. The past resists tidy narratives. You want the four of us to have conspired, because conspiracy is a *story*, and stories sell. The truth is duller. We were young, we loved the Voss family, we talked about that night for years afterward — at memorials, at anniversaries, over drinks. Of course our accounts converged. Convergence is what communities do with trauma. They sand the edges until everyone holds the same stone."

"It's a beautiful theory."

"Thank you."

"One flaw." I stood too, slowly, giving him no reason to feel cornered. "You said 'the four of us.' I named three witnesses before I got to you. I never said there were four."

The room went very quiet. Out on the water, somewhere beyond the blinds, a gull cried once and quit.

Corwin turned from the window with his eyebrows raised, mild as milk. "Dev, Ilsa, Ruth, myself. You listed them all not five minutes ago. I'm merely summarizing."

"I listed them," I said. "In a different order. And I never used the word 'four.'"

"Ms. Nystrom." He laughed — genuinely, I think, or such a perfect copy of genuine that the difference stopped mattering. "You're interviewing a retired history teacher about a thirty-year-old tragedy. Numbers are my native language. Dates, counts, casualties. Forgive me for keeping track." He walked me to the door himself, unhurried, and opened it onto the gray afternoon. "I do hope you'll come back. I have boxes of materials from that year — clippings, photographs, the school records. Tomas was my student. I'd like whatever happened to him to finally have an ending. Even a sad one."

"Especially a sad one?" I asked.

His smile widened by a degree. "Sad endings are the only ones people believe."

* * *

I sat in the car outside his house for eleven minutes, doing what I always do after an interview: replaying it, frame by frame, looking for the seam.

The slip was real. I'd have staked the show on it. "The four of us" — spoken before I'd given him a number, offered up like a gift he assumed I wouldn't notice because nobody notices arithmetic in conversation. Except I notice everything, that's the job, and Corwin Hale knew that better than anyone, because he listened to my show. Which meant the slip wasn't carelessness.

It was bait.

That was the thought that followed me down the coast highway, past the harbor and the shuttered kiosk and the road that ran out toward Gullwrack Point. A man that controlled doesn't misspeak. A man that controlled *plants*. He'd handed me the number four the way a magician palms a coin — visibly, daring you to catch it, wanting you to feel clever when you did. Because a listener who catches the slip stops listening and starts congratulating herself.

Or — second possibility, worse — he wanted me to leave that interview certain of him. Certain enough to move openly. Certain enough to tell Maren I was closing in, so the news would travel through the circle the way news always traveled through that circle: fast, and straight to the person who needed it counted.

Either way, I'd shown him my hand, and he'd shown me exactly what he chose to show.

There was a third thing, though. Something he couldn't have scripted, because I doubted he knew it existed. When I'd quoted Ruth Okafor's statement — the southeast wind, the temperature drop — his eyes had gone not to me but to the wall. To the class photo. To his own younger hand on Tomas Voss's shoulder.

Grief looks like that sometimes. So does ownership.

Back at the studio I spread the timeline across the corkboard wall, the way I do when a case starts talking back. October ninth, 1995, in four colored threads. Seven-ten, Dev at the dock. Seven-forty, Ilsa at the kiosk. Eight o'clock, Ruth on the seawall road. Eight-fifteen, Corwin near the point. Then nothing. Fog. Water. A boat found anchored two days later, and a family told to grieve.

I pinned one new card at the end of the chain, blank except for a question mark, and beneath it I wrote the sentence that had been forming since Corwin's door closed behind me:

*Every script needs a reason the story ends in the water.*

Because that was the detail I kept circling and couldn't land. The coached alibi didn't just place Tomas at the harbor — it marched him there step by step, witness by witness, each sighting closer to the sea than the last. It was choreography with a destination. Whoever built it hadn't wanted to obscure where Tomas went that night. They'd wanted to *prove* it, beyond doubt, to any future investigator: he boarded his boat, he sailed into the fog, the sea took him. Case closed. Case *kept* closed.

Thirty years closed.

My phone buzzed against the desk. Unknown number, local prefix. I answered on speaker.

"Ms. Nystrom." A woman's voice, older, careful. "This is Ilsa Brandt. I understand you've been to see Corwin today." A pause long enough to hear her breathing. "I think you should know something before he comes to see you. About the hospice records. About Aldous Voss."

She stopped. In the background of the call, faint but unmistakable, a car door shut.

"He's already outside my gate," Ilsa whispered. "Please. Tomorrow morning. Come before he—"

The line went dead.

I looked at the corkboard, at the question mark, at the neat chain of sightings marching a man into the sea. Then I picked up my keys, because tomorrow morning suddenly felt like a luxury neither of us had been promised.
