## 21. Off the Record

The studio smelled like burnt coffee and old carpet, the way it always did before a deadline, and I sat in the dark with my hand hovering over the record button for a full minute before I pressed it.

Not because I didn't know what to say. Because I knew exactly what to say, and every version of it cost someone something.

The red light came on. The waveform crawled across the screen like a pulse.

"This is *A Will for the Alibi*, episode forty-seven," I said. "I'm Isandro Nystrom. If you've been following this season, you know I've spent four months inside a thirty-year-old disappearance off Gullwrack Point. You've heard the harbor master's silence. The coached alibis. The locker with the dry-bagged satchel. You've heard me say that this case wasn't history — it was a countdown.

"Tonight is the last episode of the season. And I have to tell you up front: it doesn't end the way you want it to."

I paused there. Let the tape run. Silence is a tool; I'd learned that from Maren Voss, who could fill a room with everything she refused to say.

"The man at the center of this story is alive," I said. "He was never drowned. He walked away from his own death on a cold night in 1995 so that his sister could walk away from something worse — a marriage that was killing her by inches, in a house where no one would ever have believed her over him. Four people helped stage it. A fifth person spent three decades waiting to undo it.

"I'm not going to name him. I'm not going to name her, or any of the people who loved them enough to lie for them. And before you write in — yes, I know what I'm doing. I know what it costs a story to withhold its ending. But this show has one rule above all the others: an alibi is a story, and stories age badly. This one has aged thirty years into something fragile and living, and I will not be the storm that breaks it open for ratings."

My voice held. I was almost surprised.

"What I can tell you is this. There was a man — call him the Keeper — who attached himself to that family young, who built his whole identity on being their memory, their archive, their devoted witness. When the brother vanished, the Keeper didn't grieve. He curated. He kept the alibi polished. He coached witnesses until their stories matched down to weather they couldn't have seen. And when the husband finally died — died in a hospice bed, quietly, the way abusers rarely get to die — the Keeper couldn't let the story rest. Because if the drowned man ever surfaced, the Keeper's thirty years of faithfulness would curdle into thirty years of complicity in a lie. So he waited by the water. He altered records. He planted bones. And last week, at a decommissioned lighthouse, he tried to add a podcaster to the body count."

I touched the scar along my hairline where the stitches had come out two days ago. Six of them. Small price, Dev had said, and meant it as comfort.

"He failed," I said. "Because someone who loved that family more than he feared it followed his conscience up those stairs in the middle of a storm. What happened after that is a matter for the courts now. The Keeper faces charges for falsifying medical records and for what he did to me. The rest of it — the staged drowning, the forged certificate, the exile — belongs to no jury. It belongs to a brother and a sister who paid thirty years for a freedom most of us get at birth.

"Some of you will call this a betrayal of the format. Maybe it is. But I started this show on a simple premise: I don't take a case I can't close. And closing a case doesn't mean handing everyone to the law. Sometimes it means deciding what justice actually looks like — and having the spine to choose it on purpose, with your eyes open, knowing you'll live with it.

"This case is closed. Thank you for listening."

I stopped the recording. Sat in the hum of the equipment for a long time.

Then I saved the file under a name Maren would appreciate: *The Tide Gives Back*.

* * *

Corwin Hale's arraignment was held in a county courtroom that smelled of floor wax and rain-damp wool, and he arrived looking like a man attending someone else's wedding — charcoal coat, silver hair combed back, posture straight as a mast.

He found me in the second row and smiled. That was the thing about Corwin. Even cuffed, even charged, he smiled like we were sharing a private joke, and maybe we were: the joke being that he had nearly beaten a woman to death on a lighthouse stair and still looked like the most respectable man in the building.

"You cut the episode short," he said, when the bailiff wasn't close enough to care. "No names. No town. I listened twice."

"You listen to my show?"

"I've listened since the first letter I sent you." His eyes were calm, pale, bottomless. "You should know, Isandro — I'm not sorry about the lighthouse. I'm sorry it didn't work. There's a difference, and you of all people understand the difference."

"Do I?"

"You do. You're airing a lie of omission right now and calling it mercy. We're the same animal. The only difference between us is that your story ends with everyone forgiven." He leaned slightly closer, voice dropping to something almost gentle. "Tomas Voss left his sister alone with Aldous for six years before he ran. Whatever peace you think you've bought — she paid for it long before I ever picked up a pen."

"That's not yours to weigh anymore," I said. "It never was."

The bailiff called his name. Corwin rose, straightened his coat with his free hand, and gave me one last look — not hatred, not quite. Inventory. The look of a man filing me away in the archive where he kept everything.

"They'll convict me of paperwork and a broken railing," he said. "And somewhere out there, a man who let his sister bleed for six years will grow old in the sun. Tell me again which of us the story punished."

They took him out. I sat through the whole proceeding anyway, every procedural minute of it, because I'd promised myself I would watch this part with my eyes open too.

Hospice falsification. Aggravated assault. Attempted — the prosecutor had fought for that word and gotten it. Corwin's lawyer entered a plea of not guilty, and the judge set a date, and none of it touched Tomas Voss or Maren or Dev or Ilsa or the fisherman or anyone else who had carried the secret across three decades. The law got its piece. Only its piece. I had made sure of that, in long careful conversations with a prosecutor who owed me a favor and understood, eventually, that some witnesses are load-bearing walls — pull them out and the whole structure comes down on the innocent.

Justice outside the law isn't a loophole. It's a choice, made deliberately, and you carry it.

I intended to carry it fine.

* * *

Maren watched the episode drop from her room at the coastal home, propped up in her chair with her tablet balanced on a blanket, and afterward she called me.

"You said his name wrong," she said. No hello. Her voice was stronger than it had been a month ago; whatever the truth had taken out of her, it had also put something back.

"I didn't say his name at all."

"Exactly wrong." I could hear the smile in it. "You said nothing, and it was the truest thing anyone's said about him in thirty years."

"How are you feeling?"

"Tired. Terrible. Wonderful." A pause, the sound of her settling deeper into the pillows. "Dev brought the phone yesterday. We talked for forty minutes. He sounds old, Isandro. He sounds like our father." Another pause, longer. "He cried. Tomas never cried. Not once, not even the night we planned it, not even when I begged him to take me with him instead of leaving me behind to be the widow of a living man. He cried, and then he said, 'Tell Mare the tide kept its word.'"

I wrote nothing down. Some things you keep only in the body.

"What happens now?" she asked.

"Now? Now Corwin's lawyer discovers that juries dislike men who alter dying women's charts and shove podcasters off lighthouses. Now the hospice settles quietly. Now the rest of you go on pretending to be strangers, which you've had practice at."

"And you? What happens to the woman who closes cases?"

I looked out the studio window at the gray street, at the wet roofs, at the far smudge of coast beyond them where Gullwrack Point stood its long vigil against the water.

"I start listening to new letters," I said.

* * *

There was one more thing, though. There is always one more thing.

It came three days later, an envelope with no return address, postmarked from a town I'd never heard of, in a country I had to look up. Inside was a single sheet, handwritten in a careful, weathered hand, and a photograph.

The photograph showed a small boatyard under enormous sky. An older man stood beside a half-restored hull, sawdust on his sleeves, squinting into the sun. He had Tomas Voss's jaw and nobody else's anything — thirty years of salt and distance had remade him entirely. On the back, in the same handwriting: *He says thank you. He says he'll sleep tonight for the first time since the light went out.*

The letter itself was shorter.

*Ms. Nystrom —*

*I heard the episode. Of course I did; Dev told me to, the moment it aired, and for the first time in thirty years I did what Dev told me. You kept my name out of it. You kept hers out of it. I don't know what that cost you, and I suspect you don't either yet, but you will, someday, and I wanted you to hear this before that day comes: it was worth it. She was worth it. Every year of it.*

*I won't come back while Corwin's trial is live. After — I don't know. Maybe a phone call. Maybe a beach. Maybe just this letter, which is already more than I ever believed I'd get to send.*

*You asked on air once whether the sea keeps its promises. It does. It kept mine. Keep yours.*

*T.V.*

I read it twice. Then I did something I almost never do with evidence.

I burned it over the studio sink, and while the paper curled and went to ash, I thought about what Corwin had said in the courtroom — *we're the same animal* — and how good it had felt, for one ugly second, to believe him. Because believing him would have been easy. Believing him would have excused everything.

But Corwin kept stories to own them. I'd just burned one to set it free.

That was the difference. That was the whole difference, and I would spend the rest of my life being sure of it, and I could live with that.

The ash went down the drain. Outside, the rain had stopped without my noticing, and the light coming through the window had turned the color of clean water.

My phone buzzed on the desk. Unknown number, local prefix, Gullwrack exchange.

I picked it up.

"Isandro Nystrom," said a voice I'd only ever heard through thirty years of other people's memories, older and rougher than any of them, but unmistakable — the voice of a man the tide had given back. "I'm told you're the woman who closes cases. I have one more thing you need to hear. And then I have a favor to ask — the kind that can't go on any record at all."
