## 20. Verdicts

I wore the blue dress. The one Odette pressed for me the night before, hanging on the closet door like a dare. I hadn't owned a sober occasion in so long that I'd forgotten what it felt like to stand in a courtroom with a clear head and a clean tongue and nothing between me and the morning but the morning itself. Day forty-one. My hands only shook a little, and I told myself that was the cold, though it was June and the courthouse smelled like floor wax and old paper and the particular dread of a town about to hear its own name read out loud.

Corliss Vane sat at the defense table in a gray suit that fit him like an apology. Eleven months of hiding inside the town's paperwork had thinned him down to something almost transparent — a man who had learned that the best way to not exist is to be the one holding the eraser. When the verdict came back, he didn't flinch. He'd rehearsed for it, I think. What he hadn't rehearsed for was the sound.

Guilty. On all three counts. The foreman, a feed-store owner named Halvorsen who once sold me a bottle of rye I never paid for, said it in a voice like he was reading a grain price, and the room made a noise I will carry to my grave — not a gasp, not a cheer. A kind of collective exhale, like a house settling. Forty years of Harrowgate letting things slide, and here was one thing, one single thing, that refused to stay filed.

Isandro didn't move. He sat two rows behind the prosecution with his hands folded and his eyes on Corliss the way he looks at everything — like a page he's already read and will never forget. I watched him watch the man who had tried to kill him on those cellar stairs, and I thought: that boy was raised by liars, and he turned out to be the only honest thing in this county.

Then I corrected myself. *We* raised him. And he turned out honest anyway. It's the closest thing to a miracle I've ever witnessed, and I was sober for it.

* * *

The sentencing came down the same afternoon — twenty-five years, no parole eligibility until the fifteenth, the judge reading the aggravating factors in a voice that made even the stenographer sit straighter. Aldous Vane, sixty-eight years old, beaten at the top of his own cellar stairs by the brother he'd spent a decade believing was dead, all because a will was being rewritten and a trust was being built and Corliss Vane had decided that the town his family had bled for did not deserve a single dollar of it.

Inheritance rage. That's what the prosecution called it. I kept thinking about the photograph Benny found in Odette's drawer — four faces cut out, one of them Corliss's, back when the five of them were young and the fire of 2011 was still a thing that hadn't happened yet. You can cut a face out of a photograph. You can't cut it out of a man.

Maren Duval resigned in a letter delivered to the chief's desk at 7:14 that morning, timestamped, typed, and signed — because of course she typed it, of course she signed it, Maren Duval never did anything halfway except the lying. The DA had offered her a deal: cooperate fully on the 2011 fire cover-up, testify to everything, and the obstruction charges would be held in abeyance pending her good behavior. She took the deal and then, characteristically, went one step further. She resigned rather than wear a badge she no longer believed she'd earned.

I went to see her at the station that last afternoon. She was clearing out her desk the way you'd clear out a stranger's — no boxes, just a canvas bag, everything in it fitting into one armful. Twenty-two years of service, one bag.

"You could've fought it," I said. "The deal was generous."

She set a framed citation facedown on the desk, the way you do with a photograph of someone you're not ready to look at yet. "The deal was a door, Ruth. I'm choosing to walk through it instead of standing in the frame pretending it's a wall." She looked at me then, and her eyes were dry and terrible and honest. "You wrote the original report. Vane's death. Before it was retyped."

"I did."

"I read both versions for three years and told myself the differences didn't matter." She zipped the bag. "That's the job, isn't it? Telling yourself the differences don't matter until there's a body."

"There were two bodies," I said. "One of them just left town."

"Tomas Reyes is coming home Thursday," she said. "I read the file. Isandro's file." She almost smiled. "The boy files things in his head the way I used to file them in cabinets. Except his never get misfiled, do they."

"No," I said. "They don't. That's the whole problem with him."

She carried her bag out past the front desk, past the dispatch board Odette used to work before her hands got bad, and she didn't look back, and I understood that too. Some exits you have to make without looking back or you'll never make them at all.

* * *

The town split the way a river splits around a stone.

You could feel it at the diner within a day of the verdict. On one side of the room: the people who came up to Isandro and shook his hand and said *thank you, thank you, my brother worked for Aldous, we never believed it was the stairs.* On the other side: the ones who wouldn't look at him, who paid their checks in exact change and left, who had spent three decades in a town where the Vane name meant payroll and charity and the annual fund that kept the volunteer department running, and who now had to sit with the fact that the money had a body under it.

And under both of those, quieter, the third current: the ones angry not at Corliss but at Isandro. Because Isandro hadn't just caught a killer. He'd pulled the thread, and the thread had unraveled the fire of 2011, and the fire of 2011 had unraveled the cover-up, and the cover-up had unraveled the names — Duval, Odette, and others, small names, names of people who'd only buried one small thing one time and had spent fourteen years being decent citizens on top of it. The *Courier* ran the list. Not the whole list. Enough of it.

I found Isandro at the records office — the new one, the one Vane's trust was already funding, still smelling of paint, shelves half-built, Benny on a ladder with a level and an expression of profound suspicion toward the level. Isandro was at the center table with the confession letter in a mylar sleeve, cataloguing it. He does that when the world gets loud. He files.

"Half the town wants to buy you dinner," I said, "and the other half wants to key your car."

"I don't own a car."

"Then they'll key Benny's."

"Hey," said Benny, from the ladder, without looking up.

Isandro set down the sleeve. He looked tired in a way I recognized, because I'd worn that tiredness for twenty years — the tiredness of a person who has done the right thing and discovered that the right thing has a bill attached. "Mrs. Okafor crossed the street when she saw me coming," he said. "She used to give me butterscotch when I was fifteen. Before Odette, even. She was the first person in Harrowgate who was kind to me."

"And now?"

"Now she crosses the street." He said it without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "Her husband's name was on the fire list, Ruth. Not for setting it. For signing off on the inspection that never happened. He's dead four years. She's going to spend the rest of her life explaining a signature she never made."

I sat down across from him. The paint smell was doing something to my head, or maybe that was just being awake at four in the afternoon with nothing in my bloodstream, which still felt like a medical event some days.

"Can I tell you something about the list?" I said.

He waited. He's good at waiting. It's the archivist in him — he lets the document finish.

"Every name on it, I could have caught," I said. "Not the murder. Not Corliss. But the fire, the retyped reports, the inspection — I was a detective for nineteen years and I drank through the last nine of them, and I *saw* things. Small wrongnesses. A date that didn't sit right. A signature that looked traced. And every time, I told myself it was easier not to look, and every time, the not-looking got easier, until it wasn't even a decision anymore. It was just me." I turned my coffee cup — decaf, God help me — in a circle on the table. "Corliss Vane edited files because he was a murderer. The rest of us edited them by leaving them alone. Same archive. Different pens."

Isandro was quiet for a long moment. Then he said, "Odette took me in because of the fire. Because of my father. I know that now." He looked at the letter in its sleeve. "She spent fourteen years doing penance by raising me, and I spent three years suspecting her of murder, and neither of us was wrong about what the other one was."

"That's family," I said. "Nobody's wrong and everybody's sorry."

Benny came down off the ladder. "For the record," he said, "I'm on the dinner half of the town. Also the butterscotch lady will come around. Mrs. Okafor once forgave a man who backed a truck into her greenhouse. She's slow, not gone."

* * *

I did it on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays were the day I used to start drinking again, back when I was quitting every Monday. I walked into St. Ambrose's basement hall where the meeting happens — the one I'd sat in the back of twice in three years, close enough to smell the coffee, far enough to leave before the talking started — and I stood up when they asked if anyone was new, and I said my name, and I said the number.

Ninety-one days. Say it out loud and it becomes real in a way the counting never was.

I'd tried sobriety before. Four times. Each time it was a performance for someone — for the chief, for a doctor, for a version of myself I was auditioning to become. This time it wasn't for anyone. This time it was because I had pulled Vane's original incident report out of the archive and read my own typing and known, in my hands, in the specific rhythm of my keystrokes, that the pages that followed were lies, and I had done nothing, and the nothing had a body count. You don't drink over that. You can't. Drinking is how you get there in the first place.

After the meeting, I found Isandro on the courthouse steps, because he was always on the courthouse steps in those weeks, going over his testimony in his head, though the inquest was over and the trial was over and there was nothing left to rehearse. Old habit. The archive doesn't close just because the case does.

"I want to show you something," I said, and handed him the folder.

He opened it. Inside was a list, handwritten, forty-one names. Case numbers. Dates. My cases — the ones I'd fumbled, the ones where the file sat wrong in my hands and I'd looked away. A stolen tractor in 2013 where the serial number in the report didn't match the serial number on the evidence tag. A burglary on Corbett Lane where the responding officer's timeline was impossible and I'd signed off anyway. An assault behind the Vane mill in 2016 that got downgraded to a fall. Forty-one of them, and I could have made the list longer, and the fact that I stopped at forty-one was itself a kind of lie I hadn't sorted out yet.

"What's this?" he asked, though I could see him reading it, could see the names filing themselves into that head of his where nothing ever leaves.

"My caseload," I said. "The honest version. I want to reopen every one. Formally. With the DA's blessing or without it." I made myself look at him. "And I can't do it alone, because I'm the one who broke them. I need someone who reads a file and can't un-read it. I need the archive."

He looked at the list for a long time. Forty-one names. I watched him reach the bottom and then, I swear, start over from the top, because that's what he does — he reads things twice, once to know them and once to keep them.

"You understand what this costs," he said. "The Okafor thing, the mill thing — half these names are people who'll hate you for it. Half are people who'll hate *me* for it."

"I spent nine years buying peace with whiskey," I said. "It's the most expensive thing I ever purchased and it didn't work. I'm done buying peace."

He closed the folder and held it a moment, like a man weighing a document's admissibility. Then he handed it back.

"Monday," he said. "The records office opens Monday. Benny's already built the shelves. We'll start with the tractor — the serial number's been bothering me since you mentioned it, and I remember the evidence tag." He almost smiled, that dry Isandro smile that means something enormous is happening underneath. "Ruth. Nobody's ever asked me for my memory before. They've only ever asked me to lose it."

I walked home the long way, past the diner, past the mill, past the Vane house with its FOR SALE sign and its cellar windows dark. The evening was doing that thing June evenings do in Harrowgate — going gold and slow, the whole town lit up like something worth saving, which is the cruelest trick a place can play on you, because it makes you want to save it.

My hands were steady. Day ninety-one, and steady.

I was halfway up Odette's walk when I saw the envelope taped to the boarding house door. No stamp. Hand-delivered. My name on it in handwriting I didn't know, and Isandro's name underneath mine, and one line visible through the paper where the ink had pressed through:

*You should check the 2009 files. Both of you. Before someone else does.*

I stood on the porch with the evening going dark around me, holding the thing every instinct I'd spent nineteen years sharpening told me was either a beginning or a trap, and in Harrowgate, as I had finally learned, it was always both.
