## 2. The Anniversary Clause

Marisol Vane had rehearsed her grief so many times that it no longer felt like acting. That was the trouble with prophecy, she thought, watching the investigator's car crawl up the cypress-lined drive: if you imagined a thing long enough, with enough detail and enough love, it stopped being imagination and became a kind of memory — a memory of something that hadn't happened yet, sitting in the chest like a stone swallowed whole.

She met him in the morning room because the light there was honest. The rest of the house lied beautifully — the portraits flattered, the mirrors softened, the long gallery of Vane ancestors gazed down with painted eyes that had witnessed everything and confessed nothing. But the morning room faced east, and at nine o'clock the sun came through the leaded glass without mercy, showing every crack in the china, every thread pulled loose in the upholstery, every line on a face that had spent thirty years practicing stillness.

Quentin Almeida stood when she entered, as she'd known he would. He was younger than his reputation suggested, narrow-shouldered, with the careful hands of a man who handled other people's secrets for a living. He did not look around the room the way most visitors did, cataloging the silver and the oil paintings for their resale value. He looked at *her*, and then he sat down exactly where she indicated, and folded those careful hands, and waited.

He had brought the retainer with him. She saw it in the inside pocket of his jacket, a corner of the envelope visible like a white tooth. He hadn't deposited it yet. A cautious man. Good. Cautious men asked better questions.

"Mrs. Vane," he said. "I've been thinking about your request."

"I imagine you have. I imagine you've thought about very little else since Tuesday." She smiled to take the sting out of it, and watched him decide not to be flattered or offended. "You want to know why a woman would pay an insurance investigator to prove her husband has not been murdered."

"The thought crossed my mind."

"And what conclusion did you reach?"

"That either you're mad, or someone has told you something terrible, and you don't know yet whether to believe them."

Marisol turned her teacup a quarter-turn on its saucer. The porcelain made a small sound, a chime almost too high to hear. *There it is,* she thought. *The mind behind the eyes. Not guessing — deducing.* She had chosen him for exactly this, from a file she'd assembled over eleven months, but knowing a thing and watching it happen were different animals entirely.

"Both," she said. "And neither. Mr. Almeida, do you know what an anniversary is? Truly?"

"A commemoration."

"A wound that we bandage once a year and call healing." Her voice came out steadier than she expected. Thirty years of practice. "My husband and I will celebrate our thirtieth wedding anniversary on the fourteenth of June. There will be dinner. There will be thirty guests, one for each year, because Emilio finds symmetry charming. There will be toasts. And at some point between the soup and the dessert — I don't know when, no one ever knows when — my husband will be murdered."

She let the words sit in the sunlight between them. Quentin Almeida did not blink. She gave him credit for that.

"You say this," he said slowly, "as though reading from a schedule."

"Because I am." She rose, joints announcing themselves the way they did now, and went to the writing desk beneath the window. The drawer stuck; it always stuck; she had made sure of it, because a lock that opened easily invited inspection, and inspection was the enemy of everything she'd built. Inside lay a folder of dove-gray leather, soft as skin. She carried it back and laid it on the table between them but did not open it. Not yet.

"Emilio redrew his will last spring," she said. "After his diagnosis. You'll find nothing unusual in that — dying men tidy up. But there is a clause. Article Nine. His attorney calls it the Anniversary Provision, because Emilio insisted on the name himself, insisted with that laugh of his, that wonderful terrible laugh." Her hand rested on the folder. "It states that if Emilio Vane dies on the date of our thirtieth anniversary — dies *that night*, between six o'clock and midnight — the entire estate passes not to our children, not to me, but into a trust whose terms are sealed until such time as a court of competent jurisdiction determines the manner of his death."

Quentin's stillness changed quality. It was the difference between a man sitting and a man aiming. "Sealed how?"

"Sealed completely. No beneficiary named. No executor named. Nothing released to anyone — not the heirs, not the tax authorities, not me — until the question of murder is answered definitively. If he dies naturally that night, the trust dissolves and everything proceeds as written. If he dies any other night of the year, same. Only that night, only that window." She finally opened the folder and turned it toward him. "Read it. Take your time. I know you won't need much."

She watched him read. It was extraordinary, watching him read — his eyes moved down the page once, unhurried, and never returned to the top, and she understood that he had taken the whole of it in a single pass, every subclause, every cross-reference, the way other men took a breath. Eleven months she had spent learning about him. The photographic memory. The years with the county records office before he went private. The reputation among attorneys: *Almeida doesn't forget anything, which means Almeida forgives nothing.*

"The drafting is sound," he said at last. "Deliberately convoluted, but sound. Whoever wrote this wanted the estate frozen the moment the coroner signs a certificate dated June fourteenth." He looked up. "Mrs. Vane, a clause like this doesn't prevent a murder. If anything—"

"If anything, it guarantees one," Marisol finished. "Yes. I know. I told Emilio the same. I said, *darling, you've written an invitation.* And he laughed and poured more wine and said, *then whoever comes will finally show us who they are.*"

*And that,* she thought, *was the first night I understood my husband knew exactly what he was doing.*

"Tell me about the murder," Quentin said. "Not whether. How. What do you see?"

And here was the moment she had dreaded for eleven months, the moment she had rehearsed alone in this room at dawn, saying the words to the empty chairs. She made herself look at the east light while she spoke, because looking at him would be unbearable.

"I see the dining room. Thirty-one places set — thirty guests and the two of us make thirty-two, no, I miscounted, I always miscount now, grief does that even when the person isn't dead yet. I see the candles. Emilio stands to give the toast, because he always gives the toast, he has given the toast at every anniversary since our first, when we were poor and the wine came in a jug. And I see—" Her breath caught, precisely where it always caught, in the rehearsal and now in the telling. "I see a glass raised. And I see that after the toast, one glass on that table will be wrong. Poisoned, broken, overturned — I don't know which. I only know that by midnight my husband will be dead and everyone in that room will already know why, and none of them will say it aloud."

Silence. The clock in the hall counted its patient seconds.

"That's not a premonition," Quentin said quietly. "That's a reconstruction. Mrs. Vane — people don't reconstruct crimes that haven't happened. They reconstruct crimes that happened once already."

*Oh, you beautiful, dangerous man.* She had known he would arrive there eventually; she had prayed it wouldn't be today. She closed the folder with fingers that trembled only slightly, and allowed herself one small lie, delivered flawlessly after thirty years of practice:

"Then perhaps you should find out what crime it is they're all remembering," she said. "You're the investigator. Investigate. Prove me wrong, Mr. Almeida. Prove that on the fourteenth of June, my husband will pour the wine, drink the toast, and live. Prove that I am a foolish old woman who has frightened herself with ghosts. I will pay you double for the pleasure of being humiliated."

He stood. At the door he paused, and she saw the question form behind his eyes — she could nearly read it off his face the way he read pages: *Why does a woman rehearse widowhood with such precision unless she has already buried someone?*

He didn't ask it. He thanked her for her time and said he would begin with the will's history, the prior drafts, the attorney's files. Of course he would. He would pull the thread of paper first, because paper was his native language.

*But the thread doesn't start with paper,* Marisol thought, listening to his car recede down the drive. *It starts with a girl who came to clean our house thirty years ago and never left it again — not alive, anyway. And every alibi in this family was born the night she died.*

She remained in the morning room long after the engine sound faded, the dove-gray folder open on her lap, her thumb moving over Article Nine like a rosary bead. Outside, the cypress trees threw their long shadows westward, toward the town, toward the records office, toward all the neatly filed secrets she was paying Quentin Almeida to drag into the light.

On the fourteenth of June, she thought, someone at that table would raise a poisoned glass. She had seen it too many times to doubt it now.

What she had never managed to see — not in thirty years of rehearsals — was whose hand held it.

* * *

Three miles away, in a house that smelled of lemon polish and old money, Theodore Vane ended a phone call and stood for a moment at his study window, smiling at nothing.

"Dad's will," he said to the empty room, conversationally. "Article Nine."

He picked up the receiver again and dialed a number he knew by heart.
