## 4. The Heir Who Smiles

Theodore Vane's office smelled of leather and cold money — old leather, new money, the particular cologne of a man who had never once been told no. Quentin Almeida noticed these things the way other men noticed weather. He noticed the desk positioned so that visitors had to cross the entire room before sitting, a geography of deference. He noticed the framed photograph on the credenza: the Vane family, thirty years younger, posed on the estate's south lawn, everyone smiling with the strained brightness of people who had just learned to perform happiness. Everyone except one figure at the edge of the frame, slightly out of focus, a woman in a gray uniform holding a silver tray as if she wished it were a shield.

He noticed, too, that the photograph had been cropped. There was a pale rectangle where a second figure had once stood, and the scissors had been neither careful nor ashamed.

"Mr. Almeida." Theodore rose from behind the desk with the unhurried grace of a man who owned the floor, the building, and probably the street outside it. He was sixty-one but carried it like fifty, silver at the temples arranged rather than grown, a navy blazer that fit the way things fit when you could afford to have the world altered to your measurements. His handshake was warm, brief, calibrated. "My mother said she'd hired an investigator. I confess I assumed she was indulging one of her fancies. She does that now. Age is a kind of permission."

"Your mother doesn't strike me as a woman who indulges anything," Quentin said.

"No." Theodore smiled, and it was a beautiful smile — Quentin's memory filed it automatically, the exact width of it, the half-second delay before it reached the eyes. "She doesn't, does she. Can I offer you something? The house pours a decent scotch, though I keep the good bottle in the bottom drawer, a habit my father taught me. He always said the best things in a house should be hidden."

"Water's fine."

"Water." Theodore said the word the way a sommelier might say *box wine*, but he poured it himself, in a crystal tumbler, and handed it across the desk with both hands, a small ceremony of respect. "So. You're investigating my father's murder."

"Your father isn't dead, Mr. Vane."

"Not yet." Theodore sat, laced his fingers over his waistcoat, and regarded Quentin with the frank, amused patience of a man watching a dog perform a trick it didn't know it knew. "That's the remarkable thing about my mother's request, isn't it? She's paying you to prevent a crime that hasn't been committed, on the testimony of a woman whose doctors have told her, in writing, that her mind is going. I could have that letter. I have most of her letters."

"Then you know why I'm here."

"I know what she told you. I'm curious what you think." He leaned back. "Between us — and I mean that, Mr. Almeida, what's said in this room stays in this room — my mother has spent thirty years building a cathedral out of one bad night. The anniversary. The dinner. The prophecy. She's rehearsed my father's death so many times that when he finally goes — of his heart, at ninety-one, in his sleep, surrounded by grandchildren — she'll be certain one of us did it. And she'll have hired someone like you to prove it." He spread his hands. "I'd like to spare you the embarrassment."

Quentin drank his water. "Which one of you is she certain about?"

The laugh that came out of Theodore was genuine, and that was the most frightening thing about it. "All of us. None of us. Whichever hurts most." He stood, crossed to the window, and looked out over the lawn where the anniversary tent was already going up — white canvas, string lights, a dance floor being leveled by men on their knees. "You know what I remember about my parents' marriage, Mr. Almeida? Nothing. That's the trick of it. Thirty years of dinners and toasts and photographs and I could not tell you a single true thing I witnessed between them. Whatever they had, they kept it behind glass, like the good silver. We were raised in the display case." He turned. "So when my mother tells you a murder is coming, I don't doubt her. I doubt only that it hasn't already happened."

The words hung there, offered like a gift, like a trap.

"Meaning what?"

"Meaning nothing. Meaning everything. Meaning I'm a man who has learned that in this family, the truth is a load-bearing wall — you don't remove it, you just hang the right paintings on it." He checked his watch, a gesture so smooth it might have been rehearsed. "I have a call. Stay as long as you like. The house is yours. My mother insisted, and I've learned not to fight her when she's holding a checkbook."

At the door, Theodore paused. "One thing. The basement records — the old ledgers, the household accounts. They're being catalogued this week. My archivist is very thorough, and very proprietary. I'd hate for there to be a misunderstanding about what belongs to the estate."

"Everything belongs to the estate," Quentin said. "Until it doesn't."

Theodore's smile widened a fraction of an inch. "My mother was right about you. You're going to be a great deal of trouble."

* * *

Theodore watched the investigator cross the hall toward the east wing, and only when the man's footsteps faded did he pick up the phone.

"He's in the house," he said. "Gray suit, carries a notebook he never writes in. Watch what he touches."

He set the receiver down and stood for a moment in the quiet of his office, listening to the house breathe. He had grown up inside this breathing — the boiler's sigh, the settling of the third-floor beams, his father's voice rising and falling behind study doors like weather. A house this old didn't keep secrets. It kept *inventory*. Everything in it was catalogued, insured, appraised, and Theodore had spent his whole life learning the catalogue, because the catalogue was power, and power was the only inheritance that never diminished.

His mother, of course, had never understood that. Marisol Vane believed in truth the way other women believed in saints — fervently, uselessly, with a devotion that required no results. She had spent three decades trying to make the family confess to something, Theodore had never been entirely sure what, and the family had spent three decades perfecting the art of not hearing her. It was a marriage of sorts, that silence. They had all entered into it.

He crossed to the window and watched Quentin Almeida pause at the top of the basement stairs. The man stood there a long moment, head tilted, the way a man tilts his head at a sound. Then he descended.

"Tell me what he finds," Theodore said into the phone again, softly. "Not before."

It wasn't fear he felt. He wanted to be honest with himself about that, because honesty, in small doses, in private, was a luxury he permitted. What he felt was the old familiar arithmetic. His mother had hired a man with a reputation — the investigator who never forgot — and a man who never forgot was a weapon that could not be recalled. Whatever Almeida found in the basement, in the ledgers, in the crooked seams of thirty years of alibis, he would find it completely. There would be no half-measures, no convenient gaps.

Which meant the only question left was the one Theodore had been answering his entire life, in boardrooms and courtrooms and quiet rooms like this one: not *what is true*, but *who will the truth belong to when it arrives*.

He returned to his desk and opened the bottom drawer, past the good scotch, to the folder he kept beneath it. Inside: a photograph of the south lawn, uncropped. He looked at it the way he looked at all the family's relics — briefly, without warmth, with a kind of professional respect. The gray-uniformed woman stood at the edge of the frame, tray held high, and beside her a second figure had been cut away. He ran his thumb along the scissors' edge.

*You were supposed to stay invisible*, he thought. *That was the arrangement. Invisibility for silence.*

But his mother had gone and hired a professional rememberer, and now the invisible woman's handwriting was sitting in a basement two floors below him, waiting in the dark like a witness who had finally been subpoenaed.

Theodore closed the drawer. He smiled — the full smile, the one that reached his eyes on the second beat, the one that had closed a hundred deals and buried a hundred questions. Then he lifted the phone one last time.

"Change of instruction," he said. "If he finds the ledger — and he will, he's the type — don't stop him." A pause, while somewhere on the line a man breathed and waited. "Let him read it. Every page. And then find out which page he looks at longest. That's the page we burn."

* * *

Quentin felt the surveillance before he saw it — a prickle at the neck, the house's attention settling on him like dust. Two staff members had crossed his path in ten minutes, both too casual, both remembering his face a half-second too long. In the basement's record room, among the shelved decades of the Vane household accounts, he found the evidence he'd expected: the archivist's cataloguing was recent, thorough, and incomplete in a very particular way. The 1990s volumes were present. The 1980s were present. But the boxes for the year the housekeeper vanished had been re-shelved out of order — deliberately, he was almost certain, the way a man hides a letter in a stack of bills.

He was still standing there, mapping the disorder in his head, when he found it: a ledger bound in green cloth, unmarked on the spine, its pages filled with a small, upright, disciplined hand. Not the estate's hand. Not the archivist's hand.

A housekeeper's hand.

He opened it to the first page, and the first line stopped his breath:

*If you are reading this, then someone finally looked.*
