## 4. What the Archive Keeps

The county records annex smelled the way all government buildings eventually come to smell: of toner, old carpet glue, and the faint despair of fluorescent lighting. Ada Fairweather had spent enough evenings in that smell to consider it almost companionable. Almost. It was not, however, a smell she was supposed to be enjoying at nine-forty on a Tuesday night, alone, with a coat hanger bent into a tool of questionable legality in her gloved hand.

"You could have just asked for a key," Ray Okafor had said that afternoon, arms crossed in her office doorway, radiating the particular weariness of a man who has learned that asking Ada Fairweather to be reasonable is like asking the tide to file paperwork.

"And you'd have handed me one, would you? With a little bow?"

"I'd have told you no."

"Exactly." She'd smiled at him over her reading glasses. "So here we are."

Here they were indeed — except Okafor was currently parked two blocks away in his personal truck, pretending to fill out a fishing license application he had no intention of submitting, while Ada let herself into the annex through the loading door whose lock she'd defeated with a paperclip and thirty years of stubbornness.

The truth was she hadn't wanted a witness. Not yet. If what she suspected was true — if someone had been rearranging the Pryce file the way a child rearranges furniture when their parents aren't home — then she needed to see it with her own eyes before anyone else's interpretation got between her and the pages.

She clicked on her penlight and stood a moment in the dark, letting the building settle around her. Somewhere a furnace ticked. Somewhere else, a refrigerator hummed its small bureaucratic hymn. The annex at night felt less like an archive than a body at rest — which was, she supposed, how she thought about most rooms.

* * *

The Pryce file lived in drawer 114-B, third row from the window, under a label some long-departed clerk had typed as PRYCE, D. — DROWNING (ACC.). That final parenthetical had always struck Ada as unnecessary. Files didn't need editorializing. Verdicts were hers to make, not the alphabet's.

She pulled the folder out and laid it flat on the reading table beneath the penlight, and for a moment she simply looked at it, the way she'd look at a face before beginning an examination. You learned things that way. You learned where the wrongness lived.

It took four minutes.

Ada kept meticulous records of her own files — not copies, exactly, but a private index, a map of each document's order and condition, made the day she closed any case. Habit, mostly. Pride, partly. Three unofficial solves had taught her that cases were rarely lost in the field; they were lost in the filing, in the slow sediment of mislabeled pages and well-meaning tidying. So she'd mapped the Pryce file in October 2019, page by page, and she had that map in her head now, and the file did not match it.

The tide tables were out of sequence. In her original index they'd sat between the harbor master's statement and the first responder log. Now they came after the autopsy summary — moved forward, given prominence, as if someone wanted them read *first*, wanted them to do their lying early, before a reader's mind had set.

And there was more. She turned the tide table toward the light and saw them: pencil marks. Faint, careful, erasable-but-not-erased annotations in a small, precise hand. A tiny check beside the 6:14 p.m. high tide. A question mark beside the notation for tidal flow direction. And in the margin, written so lightly it might have been breath:

*in or out?*

Ada sat down slowly on the reading chair, the vinyl exhaling its familiar complaint.

"In or out," she murmured. "Yes. That is rather the question, isn't it."

Because that was the discrepancy she'd found only last week, the one that had brought her here with a coat hanger and a guilty conscience: Daniel Pryce had drowned with seawater in his lungs, but the tide tables said the water at the base of the sea wall where he'd been found was barely ankle-deep at the time he must have gone in. Either the tables were wrong, or the water in his lungs had come from somewhere else — somewhere deeper, somewhere the report never mentioned. Someone had drowned him and walked him — or dragged him — to the sea wall afterward. And Ada had signed the autopsy that said otherwise.

Her signature. Her lie. Engineered by someone else's hands, or so she desperately hoped, because the alternative — that she'd been careless, that she'd missed what a pencil annotator had caught — was a wound she wasn't ready to look at directly.

But it was the *nature* of the annotations that chilled her most, sitting there in the humming dark. They weren't corrections. They weren't concealment. Whoever had marked these pages hadn't hidden the discrepancy — they'd *highlighted* it. They'd moved the tide tables forward like a docent arranging an exhibit, hung a question mark on the wall, and left the room.

The killer — if that's what this person was — wasn't hiding the truth.

They were curating it.

* * *

She worked through the rest of the folder with the patience of a woman defusing something. Photocopies, she noticed now — several pages were photocopies substituted for originals, good ones, but photocopies nonetheless, the telltale slight shrinkage visible along the edges if you knew to look. Someone had taken the originals out, copied them, annotated the copies, and returned everything in a new order. A museum installation. *The Death of Daniel Pryce: A Reconsideration.*

Only three people had regular access to this drawer without leaving a request slip. She counted them off like ribs.

Deputy Ray Okafor, who had helped her carry the Pryce file to her car in 2019 and who had, two days ago, warned her off reopening it with genuine worry in his face. Worry, she'd assumed, for her. But worry could wear many coats.

District Attorney Warren Pell, who wanted this trial clean, quiet, and over — a man whose relationship with inconvenient documents was famously transactional.

And Ruth Ambrose, the file clerk, who had kept this annex for twenty-six years, who knew every drawer the way a shepherd knows individual sheep, and who had died fourteen months ago of a heart that finally declined to keep filing.

Ruth. Ada turned the name over. Ruth Ambrose, who had signed off on the transfer of evidence in 2019, whose neat initials appeared on the intake form. Ruth, who had once brought Ada ginger cookies during the Halloway case and asked, with shy earnestness, whether dead people could really tell you things. "Oh yes," Ada had said. "They talk constantly. The trick is listening."

Had Ruth listened to Daniel Pryce? Had she known?

Or — and here the cold moved up Ada's spine and settled between her shoulder blades — had Ruth left behind someone who listened for her? A son, she seemed to recall. A daughter. Grief was a strange archivist; it reordered everything, kept some pages, burned others, and sometimes it sent people back to the drawers of the dead looking for answers, or absolution, or instructions.

She photographed every page with her phone, replaced the folder exactly as she'd found it — *exactly*, down to the angle of the tide tables, because if the curator visited again she wanted to know — and sat back in the dark to think.

The furnace ticked. The refrigerator hummed. And Ada Fairweather, coroner of Marrow Bay, keeper of three unofficial solves and one official mistake, admitted to the empty room what she'd been circling for two days:

Someone had been watching her work for years. Reading her reports. Following her signatures through the drawers. And now they were arranging the evidence of her worst error like flowers on a grave — not to bury it, but to make sure she *saw* it.

This wasn't a confession. The letter in her desk drawer, that handwritten apology for a drowning, was part of the exhibit too. Everything was. She was being walked through a gallery by an invisible hand, and the next room was already waiting.

Her phone buzzed against the table. Okafor.

"Well?" he said, before she'd even said hello.

"The file's been touched. Rearranged. Annotated in pencil." She paused. "Ray, whoever did it wants the discrepancy found. They're not covering anything. They're *presenting*."

A long silence on the line, long enough for her to hear him breathing, hear the truck cab creak. Then, carefully: "Three people had access, Ada."

"I know."

"One of them is me."

"I know that too." She looked at the drawer, at the neat typed label, at the parenthetical verdict that wasn't hers. "Which leaves us a dead woman and a district attorney, and I don't love either option."

"Ruth Ambrose wouldn't—" He stopped himself. Even over the phone, she could hear him reorganizing the sentence, the way men did when loyalty collided with arithmetic. "Her kids still live around here. Elias, and the daughter — Nora. Nora works at Hollis & Crane. Defense side."

Ada went very still.

"Defense side," she repeated. "Of *which* trial, Ray?"

Another silence. Longer this time. Outside, far off toward the water, a foghorn sounded its one patient note, and Ada thought: *the gavel falls Thursday.*

"Hollis & Crane is representing Cole Brannick," Okafor said quietly. "The Pryce trial isn't the only one coming. But Ada — the star witness in the Brannick case is Maren Kettle. And Maren Kettle was the deputy clerk in this annex in 2019. She'd have known this drawer too."

Ada stood, knees protesting, and gathered her penlight. The exhibit, she understood suddenly, had a fourth room. And the promised body — *one more before the gavel falls* — had a name, an address, and a habit, Ada would bet her license, of counting backward.

"Go home, Ray," she said. "And then tomorrow, don't go home at all. Find Maren Kettle and stand somewhere she can see you."

"Why? What do you think's going to—"

"I think," Ada said, clicking off the penlight and letting the dark take the archive back, "that our curator isn't finished hanging things on walls."
