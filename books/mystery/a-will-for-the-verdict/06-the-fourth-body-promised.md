## 6. The Fourth Body, Promised

The phone rang at 11:47 on the night before Maren Kettle's deposition, and Ada Fairweather knew before she answered it that the world had tilted.

She had been asleep for exactly forty minutes — a personal record this week — and when she fumbled the receiver off its cradle, her first coherent thought was not *who* but *finally*. The second letter had promised a fourth body before the gavel fell, and bodies, in Ada's experience, kept appointments the way trains in Marrow Bay did: late, but never absent.

"Fairweather." Her voice came out gravelled.

"It's Ray." Deputy Okafor's voice was too controlled, which for Ray meant frantic. "Maren Kettle didn't come home. Her neighbor called it in an hour ago. Porch light on, door unlocked, teakettle boiled dry on the stove."

Ada sat up in the dark. "Boiled dry."

"Dry as a sermon on tithing. She'd never. That woman descaled that kettle like it was a family member."

"I'm coming in."

"You're coming in," he agreed, "and Ada — the DA's already been called. Hollis wants to send everyone to the harbor, because somebody reported a boat."

"What boat?"

"A boat that may or may not exist. I told dispatch we had a credible sighting of a gray sedan headed up the coast road."

"There is no gray sedan."

"Nope." A pause, and then, with the weariness of a man confessing a small sin in a large church: "There is now. I need you gone from your house and looking at crime scenes while I keep Hollis pointed at fog."

Ada was already pulling on her boots one-handed. "Ray Okafor, you are fabricating evidence."

"I am fabricating *direction*. There's a difference. It's a difference I'll explain to my conscience later, if I can find it under all these fabricated leads."

* * *

Fog in Marrow Bay at midnight was not weather so much as a decision the town made about itself — a closing of curtains, a folding of hands. Ada drove through it with her wipers off because there was nothing to wipe, only wet wool pressing against the windshield, streetlights blooming into soft yellow halos like bruises healing in fast-forward.

She thought about Maren Kettle counting backward.

That was the thing Maren did — Ada had watched her do it in the records room, years ago, when they'd both been younger and the town less complicated. Faced with any problem, Maren started at the end and walked it back to the beginning, step by step, like a woman retracing her own footprints across fresh snow. *The verdict depends on the testimony. The testimony depends on the memory. The memory depends on the night.* It had always struck Ada as a kind of courage, counting backward toward the worst moment instead of away from it.

And now someone had taken that habit and turned it inside out. The phone calls — reciting Maren's own testimony back to her in a stranger's level voice. The gate left open. The footprints in her garden matching her dead husband's boots. Whoever it was wasn't trying to frighten Maren into silence. They were rehearsing her fear, running lines with her, until the performance itself became the point.

Which meant Maren was probably alive. You don't rehearse a corpse.

Ada held onto that thought the way you hold onto a rail in rough water, and she drove.

They found the first clue without meaning to: Maren's Buick, parked neatly at the old cannery overlook, driver's door unlocked, her handbag on the passenger seat with her wallet still in it. Robbery had never been on the table, but its absence still landed strangely, like a handshake from someone wearing gloves.

"She drove herself," Ada said, crouching by the open door. "Or she was made to think she was driving. Look — seat pushed back too far for her. She's five-foot-one; she complained about it for twenty years at the clerk's office. Someone tall sat here last."

Okafor shone his flashlight through the fog, which was roughly as useful as shining it through milk. "Cannery's a quarter mile down the path. Tide's coming in."

"Then we walk fast and talk quietly."

"Those are my two best skills."

"Liar."

"Third-best skill," he conceded.

* * *

The path down to the cannery was gravel and rot, and the fog swallowed their flashlights at ten feet so that they moved inside two small private worlds that occasionally overlapped. Ada counted her own footsteps out of habit — thirty-eight steps from the car to the rusted gate — and it occurred to her, with a cold little click, that she was doing exactly what Maren did. Counting backward. Walking the scene from the end toward the beginning.

*The body would be found here. So the body was brought here. So the car was driven here. So the kettle was left boiling so the neighbor would notice, but not too soon.*

No. Not the body. Please, not the body.

The cannery itself was a long shed of corrugated iron that the town had been meaning to demolish since roughly the Nixon administration. Its windows were broken teeth. Inside, the fog followed them through the doorway like a dog that hadn't been invited.

"Maren?" Okafor called, and his voice came back at him doubled, tripled, off the iron walls. "Mrs. Kettle! Police!"

Nothing. Then — faintly, absurdly — a sound Ada took a full three seconds to identify: a wind-up clock ticking somewhere in the dark. Ticking steadily, patiently, like it had all the time in the world and wanted everyone to know it.

"This way," she whispered.

They found her in the old foreman's office at the far end, behind a door hanging by one hinge. Maren Kettle lay on a camp cot — a *cot*, someone had carried in a cot — covered to the chin with a wool blanket, her silver hair arranged on the pillow, her hands folded on her breast. For one lurching heartbeat Ada thought: *laid out*. Like a viewing. Like something out of her grandmother's funeral parlor.

Then Maren snored.

Ada's knees nearly went. She pressed two fingers to the woman's throat and found a pulse, slow and deep and unbothered, and Maren's breath smelled sweetish, chemical — sedated, not harmed. On the floor beside the cot, squared to its edges with a precision that made Ada's skin prickle, lay a manila folder.

She knew what it was before she opened it. She knew by the weight of it, by the way her own name seemed to rise up through the paper like damp through plaster.

*Autopsy Report — Daniel Pryce, 2019. Cause of death: accidental drowning.* Her signature at the bottom, young and confident and wrong. And in the margin, in pencil, in that same small neat hand she'd seen annotating the archive copies, a single line:

**You signed it. Now watch what it costs.**

Not a threat against Maren. Maren was the envelope, not the letter. This was addressed to *her*. To Ada Fairweather, coroner, keeper of signatures, the woman who had ruled a drowning accidental because the tide tables said the water should have been there — except it shouldn't have, and someone had known that for six years, and now they wanted her standing in the dark holding her own mistake like a lantern.

"Fury," Okafor said quietly, beside her. He'd read over her shoulder. "That's the word for your face right now. In case you were wondering."

"I'm fine."

"You've got the look you had with the Delgado case. Right before you made three grown men cry in an interview room using only a spreadsheet."

"They cried because the spreadsheet was *correct*, Ray."

"Sure." He crouched to check the cot's frame, the blanket, the clock. "Whoever this is carried her down that path. Sedative in her tea, probably — the kettle. Carried a hundred and ten pounds of retired court clerk a quarter mile through fog, set her up comfortable, wound a clock, and left a folder square to the corners." He shook his head slowly. "This isn't rage, Ada. Rage throws people off bridges. This is — staging. Theater. Somebody who wants the audience in the front row."

"And the front row," Ada said, "is me."

The clock ticked on. Outside, the tide worried at the pilings, patient as everything else in this case. Ada looked down at Maren's sleeping face — indignant even unconscious, mouth pursed as though mid-objection — and felt the fury in her chest stop churning and start hardening, the way sea glass does. Smooth. Dense. Something you could build with, or break a window with.

The killer didn't want Maren dead. Dead witnesses testified at nobody. The killer wanted Maren *found* — found frightened, found fragile, found unreliable, her deposition shaken loose from her like fruit from a branch. Or else the killer simply wanted Ada to run this exact race tonight, through this exact fog, to arrive at this exact folder, breathless, on schedule.

Either way, they were being directed. Every step since the second letter had been choreography.

"Then we change the dance," Ada said aloud.

Okafor glanced up. "Ma'am?"

"Nobody finds her tonight. No radio call, no report, nothing until sunrise. She wakes up in her own bed with a headache and a story about sleeping at her sister's, and our director reads about it in the paper wondering why the scene didn't play." She tucked the autopsy copy inside her coat, against her heart, where it felt less like evidence than like a debt. "And tomorrow, Ray, while Hollis is busy congratulating himself on the harbor, you and I are going to give the courthouse archive a very thorough spring cleaning."

"That sounds ominous."

"It should. I'm going to file some paperwork."

"In the annex?"

"In the annex." Ada pulled the blanket higher over Maren's shoulders, gently, the way you'd close a book you intended to finish. "Paperwork that says exactly what I want a certain reader to believe. If our friend has spent six years curating the truth, it's past time somebody curated a lie."

The clock ticked. The fog pressed at the broken windows. Somewhere out in the dark, Ada was certain, someone was already waiting to see how the scene ended — and for the first time since the letters arrived, she smiled.

Let them wait. The next act was hers to write.

* * *

By dawn, Maren Kettle was home in her own bed with a plausible headache and an implausible sister, the cannery stood empty and unreported, and Ada Fairweather sat at her desk with a blank legal pad, writing four versions of the same timeline — each one true except for a single, carefully chosen detail.

Three of them were bait.

One of them was a hook.

She sharpened her pencil and began.
