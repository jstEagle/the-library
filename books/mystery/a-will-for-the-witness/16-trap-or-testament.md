## 16. Trap or Testament

Rhea Calloway had been a cop for nineteen years, and in all that time she had never once sat in her own car outside a suspect's house with the engine off and the dome light on, reading evidence she had no right to possess.

The printout lay on the passenger seat like something contraband. Which, technically, it was. She'd photographed eleven pages of Oscar Dunmore's father's ledger on her phone at two in the morning, printed them at the twenty-four-hour copy shop on Delmore Road because she didn't trust the precinct printer, and now here they were: initials, dates, amounts. H—$4,000, quarterly, 1994 through 2003. P—$6,500, same cadence. And starting in 2011, a new column of entries with no initials at all, just a dash where the name should be, as if whoever kept the books had decided some clients were better left unnamed.

She knew whose handwriting it was anyway. She'd spent three days comparing it to the signature on Dale Voss's campaign finance disclosures.

Her phone buzzed. A text from Oscar Dunmore, the fourth since yesterday:

*He came back. Second visit. Brought coffee like we're friends.*

Rhea typed, deleted, typed again. *Don't let him in again. Don't record him yourself. If this goes wrong I need clean hands on your end, not dirtier ones.*

The reply came fast. *My hands are already dirty. My whole podcast is dirty. That's the point of me.*

Rhea put the phone face-down on the console and looked up at Voss's house—the big colonial on Kestrel Lane with the American flag out front and the campaign signs stacked in the garage, visible through the window because he wanted them visible. Sheriff-elect. Two weeks from swearing-in. Every hour she sat here was an hour she could be charged with surveillance off-book, evidence tampering, conspiracy.

Her captain's name was in that ledger too. Captain Merrick, $1,200 a quarter going back nine years, filed under M with the same neat clerk's hand. Rhea had reported to the man for six years. He'd signed off on her commendation for the Pell case intake. He'd told her, just last week, that the body in Miller's Hollow was "probably a transient thing, don't overbuild it."

Probably a transient thing.

She started the car and drove, not toward home, not toward the precinct, but toward the one place in Hollow Creek she'd sworn she'd never go after dark: the recording studio above the old pharmacy on Vine Street.

* * *

Oscar opened the door before Rhea knocked. Up close she looked worse than on air—thin, sleepless, a woman running on caffeine and purpose—but her eyes were steady, and steady eyes were rarer than Rhea liked to admit.

"You texted me eleven times," Rhea said, stepping inside. "I counted."

"I wanted you counting." Oscar closed the door and turned the deadbolt. The studio was small, cluttered, warm—a mixing board, two microphones under foam windscreens, walls papered with index cards in Oscar's cramped handwriting. Rhea read a few without meaning to. *Pell audit 1989 — county contract. Ada Voss — relocated? or hidden? Lily Marsh — who signed the burial permit?* The last card had been circled twice.

"Captain Merrick," Rhea said. "That's what you want to talk about."

"That's one of the things I want to talk about." Oscar pulled out a chair at the mixing desk but didn't sit herself. She stood behind it like a lecturer, or a defendant. "Here's my problem, Detective. I have a ledger. I have Ada Voss's testimony—I recorded it, she gave it freely, she wants it heard. I have a photograph of Martin Pell alive and well having lunch with Dale Voss six weeks before Pell died. What I don't have is any way to get any of it into a courtroom, because every door between me and a courtroom has somebody's initials on it in my father's handwriting."

"Merrick would recuse," Rhea said. "He'd have to."

"Would he? Or would he bury the file in a drawer until Voss is sworn in, and then the file gets lost, and then I get audited, and then Ada's son gets a visit from a deputy asking health-and-safety questions?" Oscar's voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "You've seen what he does to people who talk. Thirty years of it. He doesn't need to kill everyone. He just needs them to believe he might."

Rhea walked the length of the room, looking at the index cards, not answering. This was the conversation she'd driven across town to avoid having, and here she was having it.

"There's a version of this," Rhea said finally, "where I take everything you have to the state attorney general. Outside the county. Clean chain of custody, sworn statements, the whole apparatus."

"And how long does that apparatus take?"

"Months."

"Dale Voss takes office in twelve days."

"Yeah." Rhea turned around. "I did the math too."

"So we're past the apparatus." Oscar came around the desk. "Which brings me to the part you're going to hate."

"I already hate it. Go ahead."

"A public confession. On the record, on the air, unambiguous, from his own mouth. Nothing else survives what he can buy. But a confession—" Oscar tapped the microphone on the desk, once, gently, like checking a pulse. "A confession is the one piece of evidence he can't shred, because the moment it exists, it exists in ten thousand places at once. You can burn a file. You can lose a drawer. You can't un-hear forty thousand people listening live."

Rhea laughed, short and humorless. "You're describing entrapment. You're describing a civilian luring an elected official into saying something incriminating on a broadcast, and hoping it holds up."

"I'm describing the only courtroom he can't reach. And I'm not doing it alone—that's why you're here. I need one cop willing to fall. Someone inside who'll stand up afterward and say: yes, I knew, yes, I helped, and here's why, and here's the evidence, properly logged, timestamped, witnessed. Because if it's just me, I'm a grieving podcaster with a grudge and a dead father. If it's you and me, it's a law enforcement officer corroborating civilian evidence under oath." She paused. "It needs to be both of us or it's neither."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere below, the pharmacy's refrigeration unit cycled on with a low mechanical hum.

"You understand what you're asking me to give up," Rhea said. "Not the badge. The badge I might survive losing. I mean the thing underneath it. Nineteen years. Every arrest I ever made, every conviction, every defense attorney who can now say Detective Calloway conspired with a podcaster to manufacture evidence. Everything I've done becomes questionable retroactively. That's what you're asking."

"Yes," Oscar said. No flinch, no softening. "And I'm asking because I watched you in that interview room when I identified the scarf. You wrote down things other people would have smoothed over. You asked about the monogram twice. You're either honest or you're the best liar in three counties, and if you're the best liar in three counties, then you're exactly the person I need anyway, and I'll take my chances."

For a moment Rhea almost smiled. Almost.

"The confession won't come easy," she said. "Voss is careful. He's spent thirty years being careful. He won't say anything damning on a stage unless he believes he's already won."

"He won't come to confess," Oscar agreed. "He'll come to control. That's his whole architecture—he can't leave a narrative standing that he doesn't own. I announce a finale episode. 'The Confession of Hollow Creek.' I invite him on as a guest to respond to the allegations. His campaign people will tell him it's a trap. He'll come anyway, because not coming is a story too, and he'd rather write the story than refuse it." She slid a printed page across the desk. "And while he's writing it, I feed him the ledger, line by line, and Ada's voice, and Pell's last voicemail—which, by the way, I have, because Pell called my tip line in September and nobody listened until it was too late."

Rhea picked up the page. It was a run sheet. Segment timings. Cue points. At the bottom, in Oscar's handwriting: *If he walks, he walks into whatever's outside. Make sure what's outside is you.*

"This is a plan to make a man destroy himself in public," Rhea said.

"It's a plan to stop him destroying anyone else in private. Those aren't the same thing, but only one of them is available." Oscar met her eyes. "You can still walk out. I'll never contact you again, and I'll die on this hill alone if I have to. But I'm asking you not to walk out. I'm asking you to be the witness. It's literally your job title. Detective. The one who detects. Be the detection."

Rhea looked down at the run sheet. Twelve days until the swearing-in. Nine days, if Oscar's timeline held, until the broadcast. Her captain's initials in a dead clerk's ledger. A girl named Lily Marsh under Harrow farmland, waiting thirty years for someone to say her name out loud.

"If we do this," Rhea said slowly, "we do it my way on the parts that matter. One: nothing illegal happens in that studio. He threatens you, he incriminates himself, fine—but nobody provokes him past what a jury would call fair. Two: I record everything independently, separate device, separate custody. When this breaks, I walk into the state attorney's office within the hour with my copy, before Merrick or anyone else can touch it. Three—" she hesitated, then finished it, "—you tell Ada what you're planning. She's given enough without being asked. She gets to consent to the next thing too."

Something moved through Oscar's face—not surprise exactly. Recognition, maybe. The look of someone who'd expected to fight and found a colleague instead.

"She already knows," Oscar said quietly. "She said—she said she's been silent for thirty years, and silence is a debt, and she'd like to start paying it back. Her words."

Rhea exhaled. It sounded, in the small room, like something structural giving way.

"Then I'm in," she said. "God help us both."

She held out her hand. Oscar took it. The grip was brief and hard, two women sealing a contract that had no paper and no precedent and no guarantee except each other.

"One more thing," Rhea said at the door. "When this goes wrong—and something always goes wrong—you don't protect me. You protect the recording. The recording is the testimony. We're just the people carrying it."

Oscar nodded. Then, as Rhea stepped onto the landing, she added, so softly Rhea almost missed it:

"Episode forty-seven was the last one about the dead. Forty-eight will be about the living. I intend to still be here to record it."

Rhea went down the stairs into the cold, and drove home the long way, past the courthouse with its lit windows, past Kestrel Lane where the flag hung motionless over the garage full of campaign signs. Her phone buzzed at a red light. A message from an unknown number:

*Nice visit to Vine Street tonight, Detective. Hope the coffee was good.*

She stared at it for a long moment, heart hammering, thumb hovering. Then she did the only thing she could do.

She screenshotted it, timestamped it, backed it up to two drives, and started drafting her resignation letter—one she hoped she'd never have to send, dated nine days out.

Nine days.
