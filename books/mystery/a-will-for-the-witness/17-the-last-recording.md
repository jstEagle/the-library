## 17. The Last Recording

I wrote the announcement at three in the morning, the way I write everything that matters—alone, in the dark, with my father's ledger open on the desk beside me like a patient on a table.

*Episode Forty-Eight: The Confession of Hollow Creek.*

One episode. Live. No edits, no cuts, no safety net.

I read it back four times and each time my hands shook a little less, which I took for courage and knew was really just numbness setting in. There's a difference between being brave and being so frightened your body stops reporting in. I'd learned that distinction from thirty years of case files. The witnesses who sounded calmest were always the ones closest to breaking.

The hosting company had cut me off eleven days ago. The podcast platforms had followed within hours, each one citing vague terms-of-service violations, each one quietly holding Harrow money somewhere in its cap table—Rhea had shown me the threads, her face gray with a disgust she couldn't afford to act on yet. My archive of forty-seven episodes, three years of work, existed now only as printed transcripts in my father's old card catalog and in the heads of about sixty thousand people who had listened before the lights went out.

Sixty thousand people who still remembered.

So when I posted the announcement—not to a platform but directly, through a mailing list I'd built episode by episode, name by name, the way you build anything that lasts—it landed in inboxes like a match dropped into dry grass.

*For three years I have told you the story of Lily Marsh in pieces. On Thursday night, at eight o'clock, I'm going to tell it whole. Live. Unedited. And I've invited the man who can end this case with a single sentence to sit across from me while I do it.*

*Sheriff-elect Dale Voss has been asked to appear. He has been asked publicly, so that his silence will be an answer too.*

*The Confession of Hollow Creek. One night only.*

I hit send and sat very still in the studio, listening to the building settle around me—the tick of the space heater, the hum of the old mixing board my father had bought secondhand when I was nineteen because he believed every house should contain one room where the truth got said out loud. Ironic, given what he'd spent thirty years filing away in the other rooms.

Then I did the thing I'd been avoiding since the idea first formed whole in my chest two nights ago, fully grown, like something that had been gestating since Miller's Hollow.

I called Dale Voss's campaign office.

* * *

"Oscar Dunmore." His voice came warm through the phone, unhurried, the voice of a man who had never once been interrupted in his life. "I wondered how long it would take you to call me direct."

"You saw the announcement."

"I make a point of reading everything written about me." A pause with a smile in it. "Even the suicide notes."

The word went through me like cold water through cloth. I'd prepared for charm, for menace, for the careful lawyerly hedging he'd deployed over tea in my studio three weeks ago, when he'd sat in my guest chair with his knees crossed and told me, in the gentlest possible terms, that my father had debts and debts were inherited. I had not prepared for him to say *suicide note* out loud, and the fact that I hadn't prepared for it told me something important: he wanted me to know he'd already decided how this ended for me. He wasn't weighing options. He was picking music.

"It's not a suicide note," I said. "It's an invitation."

"To what? A lynching?" He laughed, low and easy. "You're going to play your little recordings, wave your little papers, and I'm going to sit there and smile. And then Thursday turns to Friday, and Friday turns to election day, and I win by fourteen points, and you become a footnote in a story nobody finished. That's the shape of it, isn't it? You've thought it through. You're smart like your father was smart."

"Don't talk about my father."

"He talked about you, near the end." Softly now, almost kind, which was worse. "Did you know that? He'd come to me worried about you. All those tapes, all those dead girls filed in alphabetical order. He said, *Dale, I don't know where she gets it.*"

He let that sit. Somewhere behind his voice I could hear a television, campaign coverage, the low murmur of a state that had already made up its mind about him.

"So you're declining," I said. My voice came out steadier than I felt. Three years of recording grief had taught me that trick: the voice holds even when nothing else does.

"I didn't say that."

The sentence hung there, and I understood—with the particular vertigo of realizing you've been outmaneuvered by someone doing exactly what you predicted—that I had miscalculated one thing. I'd assumed Voss would stay away. That his absence would be its own confession, that a live broadcast naming him with no one to answer would be enough to force Rhea's hand, force the FBI, force *something*. I'd built the trap around his caution.

But Dale Voss had never once in thirty years left a narrative unattended. That was the whole architecture of him. He didn't kill Martin Pell because Pell was dangerous; he killed him because Pell's laptop was a story someone else might tell first. He hadn't deplatformed me because he feared my voice—he'd done it because silence, unmanaged, develops echoes.

He was going to come. He was going to walk into my studio, shake my hand under the hot lights, and spend ninety minutes performing innocence so thoroughly that half the county would hang up convinced the podcaster woman was unhinged and the sheriff-elect was a saint who'd endured it graciously.

And I was going to let him. Because a man who comes to control the story has to stand inside the microphone to do it.

"Eight o'clock Thursday," I said. "My studio. Live. No questions submitted in advance, no review of materials. You get equal time. Anything you want to say, you say it on air, to everyone at once."

"And if I say no?"

"Then I do the episode alone, and your name is in every sentence."

Another laugh, softer. "You sound like him sometimes. Your father. That clerk's patience—you file things, wait, file more." The television murmured. A door closed somewhere on his end, and when he spoke again his voice had dropped its warmth entirely, and what was underneath was flat and old and terribly calm. "Thursday, then. But Oscar—one courtesy between professionals. Whatever you think you have, whatever Ada told you, whatever you dug out of that card catalog—hear me clearly. If you go past the point of no return, there is no version of Thursday night after which you are safe. Not during, not after, not ever. People who trade their invisibility for justice always imagine they're buying something. They're selling. Ask yourself what you're selling and who's buying, and whether the price is one you can pay in installments."

"I'll take my chances."

"Yes," he said. "I rather thought you would. Your father said that too, once. About Lily Marsh's mother, when she kept calling the courthouse." A breath. "Eight o'clock. I'll bring my own water. I don't drink things other people pour."

The line went dead.

I set the phone down on the mixing board and discovered I was standing, though I had no memory of getting up, and that my hand was pressed flat against the fader panel hard enough to leave the skin white. Terror and purpose braided together—I could feel them wound around each other in my chest like wire, impossible to tell which was holding which up.

*Ask yourself what you're selling and who's buying.*

I knew exactly what I was selling. I'd known since the night I found Lily Marsh's grave on Harrow farmland and knelt down in the frost and finally, finally cried for a girl I'd only ever known as a folder tab. I was selling the last quiet thing I owned: the anonymous life, the archivist's booth, the safety of watching without being watched. Dale Voss thought he was threatening me with exposure.

He was offering me the deal I'd already accepted.

* * *

Wednesday was preparation, and preparation meant Ada.

She came to the studio at dusk, wrapped in a coat too heavy for October, carrying a casserole dish she set on my desk without explanation—"you'll forget to eat, Rhea says you forget"—and then stood in the middle of the room looking at the two microphones like they were headstones.

"One for you," I said. "One for him."

"And if I freeze?"

"Then you freeze on air, and thirty years of freezing ends in front of six hundred people instead of in a kitchen at two a.m. Either way it's the last time."

Ada laughed—a small, cracked sound, but real—and sat down in the guest chair Voss had occupied three weeks ago, and it struck me how differently fear sits in different bodies. Voss wore fear like a tailored suit; you'd never see it unless he turned too fast. Ada wore hers like weather. It moved through her constantly, changed her temperature, bent her posture. And still she'd come. Still she'd signed the sworn statement Rhea had typed up at her kitchen table, and still she'd agreed to speak the names out loud, live, knowing what it had cost her son every year for three decades just to keep her mouth shut.

"Dale called me Tuesday," she said suddenly. "After he talked to you."

The room went very quiet. "What did he say?"

"He quoted scripture." Her hands were folded in her lap, knuckles pale. "Psalm fifty-five. *Death stalks about my house morning and evening.* That's how he talks when he's done pretending. He wanted me to know he knows about Thursday. He wanted me to know that Tommy's new address is—" she stopped, swallowed, started again, "—that he knows Tommy's new address. He reads addresses the way you read index cards, Oscar. It's how he's always said *I see you* without saying it."

"Rhea can put protection on Tommy tonight."

"Rhea is one detective whose captain is on the ledger," Ada said gently. "Protection means a cruiser outside the door until election day. Then what?" She looked at the microphones again, and something in her face settled, the way water settles when it finally finds its level. "No. This is the only protection that works. He's spent thirty years making sure everything stays quiet. So we stop being quiet, all of us, at the same time, where he can't reach it. You thought of that before I did. I'm just catching up."

I wanted to tell her she was braver than anyone I'd ever archived. What I said was, "We run the sequence twice more tonight. When he interrupts—and he will interrupt—you keep talking. Don't stop for him. The audience hears a man shouting over a witness, and that tells them everything before we play a single tape."

"And if he doesn't shout? If he's charming? He's so *charming*, Oscar. It's how he got all of us. Half the town will listen to him and hear their neighbor."

"Then we let him be charming right up until the moment we aren't." I pulled the printed index cards from the drawer and laid them out in broadcast order: the ledger entries, 1989 through 1994, initials and amounts in my father's clerk's hand. The recording of Martin Pell's voicemail, recovered from a backup drive Rhea had pried out of the evidence locker before her captain could bury it. Ada's sworn statement. And last, at the bottom of the stack, the thing I'd saved for the final ten minutes, the thing Voss didn't know existed: a recording of Dale Voss himself, three weeks ago, sitting in that same guest chair, telling me my father's debts were mine now. He'd thought we were speaking off the record.

I'd never told him the red light was on. It was always on. That was the first rule of the archive, and I'd learned it from him, in a way—power keeps records; it just assumes no one else will.

Ada studied the cards a long moment. "Your father's handwriting," she said. "He filed us all, didn't he. Every payment, every year. Like church minutes."

"Like inventory."

"And tomorrow you sell it all." She reached over and touched the top card, lightly, the way you touch a grave marker. "Good," she said. "Sell it loud."

* * *

Thursday, 7:41 p.m. Nineteen minutes to air.

The studio had never held so many people. Rhea stood by the door in civilian clothes, badge on her belt anyway, because she'd told me that afternoon, "If this goes wrong, I want the paperwork to show I was here as myself, not undercover. I'm done hiding inside the department." Two volunteers from the mailing list ran the streaming rig off three laptops and a generator, because I didn't trust the building's power to anyone with a Harrow in their org chart. Six hundred and forty listeners were already connected, the counter climbing in slow heartbeats. Local news vans idled out front—Voss's campaign had leaked his attendance an hour ago, framing it as *courage under attack*, and the cameras had come like gulls.

I checked the levels. Checked them again. Checked the backup recorder, the battery bank, the printed scripts, the fire exit.

At 7:52, Dale Voss arrived.

He came in without an entourage, which was itself a performance—*I need no one; what could frighten me here*—wearing a charcoal suit and the expression of a man arriving at a ribbon-cutting. He shook Rhea's hand. He complimented the studio. He looked at the two microphones, at Ada seated beside them with her spine straight and her hands still, at me behind the board, and he smiled the smile from the tea, the one that had a ledger of its own behind it.

"Miss Dunmore. Mrs.—forgive me, I'll use your current name on air, of course." He hung his coat on the rack, unhurried. "Lovely setup. My father had a radio like this board. He used to say a microphone is just a confessional that pays for itself."

"Two minutes," I said, and pointed him to his chair.

He sat. He arranged his water bottle—he'd brought his own, as promised, and I understood the message: *I trust nothing you touch, and I want your audience to wonder why.* At 7:58 he leaned toward me slightly, and for a moment, just a moment, the charm thinned and I saw the thing underneath, the flat old calm from the phone call, patient as a courthouse.

"Last chance to be sensible," he said quietly. "We could do a nice episode. Lily Marsh, God rest her, unsolved, tragic. The town heals. You get your ending."

"This is the ending," I said.

The counter ticked past seven hundred. Rhea gave me the nod. Ada closed her eyes for one breath, opened them, and looked straight ahead at nothing, the way witnesses do when they've decided to tell the truth to the room and not to any face in it.

I keyed the microphone. The red light came on—always on—and went out over the airwaves to seven hundred strangers, one detective, one witness, and the man who had spent thirty years buying the silence of a town, sitting six feet away with his own water bottle and his father's smile.

"Good evening. You're listening to the final episode of this program. My name is Oscar Dunmore, and tonight—for the first time in three years—I'm saying it out loud.

"This is *The Confession of Hollow Creek*. And my first guest needs no introduction, because he's spent thirty years making sure of it. Sheriff-elect Dale Voss, welcome. Let's begin with the ledger."

Voss smiled for the microphone, opened his mouth to charm six hundred people—

—and I pressed play on the first tape.
