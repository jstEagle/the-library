## 20. The Fall of Dale Voss

The courthouse smelled the way old buildings do when they've been left to rot from the inside—dust and damp plaster and something faintly sweet underneath, like flowers kept too long in a closed room. Oscar stood in the doorway of the rotunda with her hands raised, palms out, the way you approach a frightened animal or a man with a gun.

Dale Voss had Ada by the arm. He'd dragged her up the marble steps from the parking lot, past the bronze plaque listing the county's founding families, and now he stood beneath the dome where the light came down gray through a skylight furred with grime. His suit jacket was gone. His tie hung loose. The charm that had carried him through two debates, three fundraisers, and one very polite cup of tea in her studio was stripped away, and what was left was a man doing arithmetic he didn't like.

"Put it down," he said. "The ledger. The originals. You walk them over here, and she walks out."

Oscar had walked into this building a hundred times as a child, trailing her father on his way to file some document or other. She knew the acoustics. She knew how sound pooled under the dome and came back doubled. She knew there was a service corridor behind the east wall, because she'd hidden in it once during hide-and-seek while her father pretended not to see her.

She also knew Rhea Calloway was somewhere behind her, moving slow, and that every second Voss spent talking was a second his confession streamed to however many thousands of people were still listening to a broadcast that was supposed to have ended forty minutes ago.

"The originals are gone," Oscar said.

"Liar."

"I published them." She let that land. Watched it land. "Twenty minutes ago. Every page. Every initial. Every payment from 1991 to last month. It's live right now, Dale. On servers in four countries. You can't burn it, can't buy it, can't delete it. It's done."

Something moved behind his eyes—the flicker of a man checking his accounts and finding them emptied. His grip on Ada's arm tightened, and Ada made a small sound, and Oscar took a step forward before she could think better of it.

"Let her go," Oscar said. "You don't need her. You never needed her. That was always your problem—you thought silence was something you could own. But silence doesn't belong to anyone. It just waits."

"Your father understood that." Voss's voice dropped, went almost gentle, which was worse. "Thirty years, and he never once got sentimental. He filed things. He kept his mouth shut. He understood that a town is just a machine for keeping quiet, and he was the best part it ever had."

"My father is dead."

"Yeah. Heart attack, wasn't it?" A thin smile. "He was getting old. Old men start wanting to unburden themselves. Start leaving little indexes around for their clever daughters to find."

The words went into her like a blade finding the gap between ribs. She'd wondered. In the dark hours since the library, since the card catalog with its alphabet of initials and sums, she had wondered whether her father's death had been natural, and she had decided—deliberately, the way she'd once filed grief in alphabetical order—not to know. Now Voss handed her the question back, gift-wrapped, and she couldn't tell if he was confessing or needling or simply lying, because cornered men will say anything, and dead men can't be asked twice.

"You're stalling," she said. "And so am I. So let's both stop pretending."

Voss laughed—a short, ugly bark that bounced off the dome. "You think anyone's coming? I *am* the law here. As of Tuesday, I'm the elected sheriff of this county. Whatever Calloway told you, whatever deal you think you struck—"

The doors opened behind him.

Not the big ones. The service corridor, the one behind the east wall, the one her father had shown her when she was eight years old—"in case you're ever here late, baby girl, you go out this way"—and Rhea Calloway came through it with her weapon drawn and her badge held high and her voice carrying all the way to the rafters.

"Dale Voss! Sheriff's department! Let the woman go and put your hands where I can see them!"

For one suspended second, nobody moved. The dome held the echo of Rhea's voice like a bell that wouldn't finish ringing. And then Dale Voss did the thing that ended him—not the murder, not the thirty years of payments and threats and graves dug on Harrow farmland, but this: he looked at the door, at the badge, at the phone in Oscar's hand still streaming red, and he pushed Ada away from him and ran.

He made it six steps across the rotunda. Rhea took him down hard against the marble, knee between his shoulder blades, cuffs clicking home with a sound Oscar would hear in her sleep for the rest of her life. Ada stumbled into Oscar's arms, and they held each other up, two women who had spent decades being quiet, watching a man who had bought their silence get his face pressed to the floor of the building where the silence had been signed.

"It's over," Oscar said—to Ada, to herself, to the microphone, to whoever was listening. "It's over."

* * *

They put him in a cruiser with Rhea driving and Oscar riding shotgun, because Rhea said later she didn't trust anyone else in the building not to open the door. The drive to the county jail took eleven minutes. For ten of them, Voss said nothing. He sat in the back with his cuffed hands in his lap like a man waiting at a bus stop, and the streetlights strobed across his face, and Hollow Creek slid by outside the windows—the diner, the hardware store, the war memorial with its list of names going back to the founding.

On the eleventh minute, he started talking.

Not to Rhea. To Oscar. He leaned forward as far as the seatbelt allowed, and his voice had found its charm again, or the ghost of it, worn smooth as river stone.

"You want to know the funny thing about your father?" he said. "He didn't sell Lily Marsh's silence because we made him. We offered. He named his price."

"Don't," Oscar said.

"He sat right there in that clerk's office the week after it happened, and Harrow's lawyer laid out the arrangement—monthly retainer, discretion clause, standard protection structure—and your father looked at it, and he said, 'I'll need more than that.' Not no. Not *how could you*. He haggled, Miss Dunmore. He was the only one at that table who haggled."

"Stop the car," Oscar said.

Rhea didn't. Her eyes stayed on the road, her knuckles white on the wheel, but she said, quietly, "Let him talk. Everything he says is on the record now. Everything."

"Oh, I'll talk," Voss said. "I've got thirty years of talking saved up. Here's the thing your podcast never figured out, archivist: Lily Marsh wasn't taken. Lily Marsh was *paid for*. Same as the witness. Same as your father's conscience. Everybody in this town has a price, and the ones who say they don't just haven't been quoted yet. Your dad knew that better than anybody. He built the filing system for it. Alphabetical. Very tidy. You should be proud—he taught you everything you know."

Oscar turned around in her seat and looked at him. Really looked. And what she felt, to her surprise, wasn't rage. It was something colder and more useful: recognition. This was a man who needed everyone to be guilty, because if everyone was guilty, then he was only first among equals, and the ledger proved otherwise. The ledger proved there had been people who said no. People like Ada, who paid with thirty years of fear instead of money. People like Martin Pell, who audited the accounts and died for the arithmetic.

"You're wrong about one thing," she said. "My father haggled. And then he spent the rest of his life writing down every dollar he hated himself for taking. He indexed his own guilt, Dale. Card by card. He left me the map to bury you. That's not complicity. That's a confession thirty years long, and I just read it aloud."

Voss's smile stayed fixed, but something behind it shifted, the way ice shifts on a river before it goes.

* * *

The subpoenas started before midnight.

Rhea made the calls herself from the jail's back office, having already resigned herself—Oscar heard her say it down the line, flat and final—to being the detective who arrested her own captain, her own incoming sheriff, and half the chamber of commerce in a single week. The state attorney general's office picked up on the fourth ring. By 1 a.m., a prosecutor named Okafor was awake and taking notes, and by 6 a.m., process servers were knocking on doors along Harrow Lane and Pemberton Hill with envelopes that would take apart the town's founding families the way Oscar's father had once taken apart a court docket: methodically, alphabetically, without mercy.

Marcus Harrow was served at breakfast. Caroline Pemberton's son was served on the steps of the First Methodist Church, mid-handshake with a state senator. The hosting company that had deplatformed Oscar's show received a records preservation order that made its general counsel cancel a golf weekend. It happened fast, the way rot happens fast once you break the skin—thirty years of careful architecture collapsing in a matter of hours, and all of it streaming, because Oscar had never ended the broadcast, and the internet had never stopped listening.

By noon, #HollowCreekLedger was trending in nine countries. By evening, there were camera trucks on Main Street, parked outside the diner like vultures who'd learned about catering.

Oscar watched none of it. She sat in the studio—the ransacked studio, the one with the broken lock and the missing drives—and she listened to the raw audio of the arrest on headphones, over and over, because she was an archivist, and archiving was how she breathed. The click of the cuffs. Ada's breath catching. Rhea's voice, steady as bedrock: *Dale Voss, you're under arrest for the murders of Martin Pell and Lily Marsh.*

Lily Marsh. Said out loud, finally, officially, in a police record that would exist forever. A girl with a name, restored to the record after thirty-four years.

Ada found her there around dusk. She came in without knocking—the door didn't lock anymore anyway—and lowered herself into the guest chair with the careful movements of a woman whose body had finally been allowed to feel thirty years of exhaustion all at once.

"They're saying he might take a plea," Ada said. "Avoid trial. Spare the town."

"Okafor won't offer one worth taking. Not with the recording of him threatening Pell. Not with my testimony. Not with yours."

Ada nodded slowly. Then, after a silence: "What he said to you. In the car. About your father."

Oscar took off the headphones. Set them down carefully, aligned, the way her father would have.

"He lied," Oscar said. "Or he told the truth. I don't know which, and I've decided I'm going to find out—not from him. From the files. My father kept duplicates. There's a box in the county archive, accession number 1991-114, that was never entered into the index. I checked the archive database this afternoon while everyone was busy arresting each other." She paused. "There's a deposition in it. Sealed. My father requested the seal himself."

Ada went very still. "A deposition from who?"

"That's the thing." Oscar pulled up the scan on her monitor and turned the screen toward Ada, and watched the color leave the older woman's face for the second time in a week. "It's dated November 4th, 1991. Nine days after Lily disappeared. The deponent is listed as L.M.—initials only, sealed by court order, witnessed by one E. Dunmore, deputy clerk."

The initials glowed on the screen in the dark studio. L.M. Lily Marsh.

"Not a body," Oscar said quietly. "A voice. If that deposition is what it looks like—if Lily Marsh gave testimony nine days after the whole world stopped looking for her—then she wasn't just alive that week. She talked. And someone sealed it so thoroughly that even my father's index couldn't point to it without giving the game away." She met Ada's eyes. "Which means the grave we found on Harrow farmland might not be the end of this story. It might be the beginning of the lie."

Outside, the camera trucks lit up Main Street one by one, and somewhere across town, in a cell he'd built with thirty years of other people's silence, Dale Voss was learning what it cost when the silence finally spoke back.
