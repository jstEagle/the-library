## 6. Celeste's Ledger

The rain had followed Astrid from the coast road all the way back to the village, and by the time she reached the Grey Heron Inn her coat was a second skin of cold. She hung it dripping in the narrow vestibule, stamped the wet from her boots, and was reaching for the key to her room when Mrs. Tallow, the innkeeper, appeared at the end of the corridor with the particular expression of a woman who has been asked to deliver something she'd rather not touch.

"You've a visitor," Mrs. Tallow said. "In the sitting room. She wouldn't give a name, but—" a delicate pause, the kind that cost extra— "she's wearing pearls indoors on a Tuesday."

Astrid knew before she opened the door.

Celeste Ashcombe sat with her ankles crossed and her hands folded in her lap, positioned in the wingback chair nearest the fire as though the room had been arranged around her, which Astrid suspected it had been, through some alchemy of posture and expectation that certain women carried like perfume. The pearls were real. The grief was not — or rather, it had been worn so long and so often that it had lost whatever original substance it possessed and become pure surface, lacquered smooth.

"Mrs. Blackwood." Celeste rose, extended a hand cool and dry as porcelain. "I hope you don't mind. I telephoned the hall and they said you'd gone out. I took the liberty."

"The liberty of what, exactly?"

"Of waiting." A small smile. "I find solicitors respond better to patience than to pressure. You're not a solicitor, of course. But you're reading the will, which makes you worse than one, or better, depending on who's asking."

Astrid did not sit. This was deliberate. Standing gave her the high ground over the firelight, let her watch Celeste's face from above without the flattering glow catching it directly. Twenty years of estate law had taught her that the most important conversations happened in rooms like this one — small, overheated, smelling of old upholstery and someone else's secrets — and that the person who wanted something always revealed more than they intended in the first ninety seconds.

Celeste lasted forty.

"I'll come to the point," she said, and her voice dropped half a register, shedding its drawing-room polish like a coat shrugged off indoors. "You've read the will. You know what it claims. Edmund Ashcombe has been dead for twenty years. Whatever document my mother-in-law signed in her final days is either the product of an addled mind or a cruel joke, and either way it cannot stand. The family needs you to understand that."

"The family," Astrid said, "or you?"

Something flickered behind Celeste's eyes — quick, involuntary, like a fish turning under ice. "I beg your pardon?"

"You said *the family* twice in two sentences. People repeat themselves when they're reciting. They only stumble when they're improvising." Astrid moved to the window, partly for the light, partly because it forced Celeste to turn, to keep presenting her face, to keep performing. Rain crawled down the glass in silver threads. Beyond it, the village street lay drowned and empty, lamplight pooling in the puddles like spilled honey. "So let's not rehearse. Tell me why you came."

For a moment Astrid thought the woman would simply leave — gather her dignity like her coat and walk back out into the rain to report to Marcus that the lawyer was impossible. But desperation is a patient animal. It waits beneath dignity the way rot waits beneath paint, and given one honest question it will push through.

Celeste sat back down. Slowly. As if her knees had made the decision without consulting her pride.

"May I be frank?"

"You may try."

"My mother-in-law was ninety-one years old. She signed that will eleven days after her last lucid conversation with any member of this family. Gerald Pyle will tell you — Gerald Pyle *has* told us — that the document bears irregularities. And even setting aside the absurdity of naming a dead man, the terms are punitive. Everything to Edmund, or to his issue, should he be deceased. Nothing to Marcus. Nothing to Wren. Nothing to me, naturally; I'm only married to the name." Her laugh was thin and bright and entirely without humor. "Twenty years of service to this house, and I am to be turned out of it by a ghost."

"That's a grievance," Astrid said. "Grievances don't require house calls. What do you want from me?"

Celeste's hands, folded in her lap, tightened until the knuckles went pale. When she spoke again, the voice was lower still, stripped now of everything but the thing underneath.

"I want you to declare it invalid."

* * *

Later — much later, after the offer had been made and refused and remade and refused again, after Celeste had swept out into the rain with her pearls dry under her umbrella while Astrid's conscience dripped — Astrid would sit at the little desk in her room and reconstruct the conversation the way she reconstructed everything: line by line, motive by motive, looking for the seam where the truth showed through the stitching.

But first there had been the ledger.

Because Celeste had not come with charm alone. Charm was the wrapping paper. Halfway through her plea, she had reached into her handbag and produced a leather-bound book, slim, navy, corners worn soft, and laid it on the table between them with the care of a woman setting down something both precious and contaminated.

"Do you know what Thornfield costs?" she asked. "Per year. To keep standing?"

Astrid didn't answer. Celeste hadn't expected her to.

"Roof alone, last survey: forty thousand. The east wing hasn't been properly heated since 2019 because the boiler would need replacing and the replacement would need the chimneys rebuilt, and the chimneys are listed, so it requires specialists, and specialists charge what specialists charge. The grounds staff is down to two men who are both past retirement age and whom we cannot afford to replace. There is a mortgage — yes, a mortgage, on a house people assume has been freehold for three centuries — taken out by Vivienne herself eight years ago, quietly, through a lender in Jersey, and I found the statements after the funeral because Gerald certainly wasn't going to show them to anyone." She opened the ledger. Pages of figures, dense and immaculate, in a small controlled hand. "Three years of accounts. Every debt. Every payment deferred. Every creditor who has begun, in the last six months, to write letters instead of reminders."

Astrid looked down at the columns. She had seen ledgers like this before, in other houses with other names — the arithmetic of slow collapse, each entry reasonable on its own, the sum ruinous. Estates died the way people did, usually: not of one wound but of accumulation.

"This is why the will matters," Celeste said. "Not sentiment. Not legacy. If the will stands as written — if everything passes to a dead man or his phantom heirs — the estate goes into probate limbo. Years of it. And the creditors won't wait years. They'll force a sale. Auction. The house, the land, the contents. Everything my husband's family built, sold off to a developer who'll gut Thornfield into flats and put a car park where the rose garden is." Her voice cracked on the last word, and Astrid noted — professionally, coldly — that the crack arrived precisely at the sentimental object, precisely where a jury or a judge or a soft-hearted lawyer would be most likely to feel it. Whether the crack was manufactured or genuine was almost beside the point. Both kinds of woman cried at rose gardens.

"And if the will is invalidated?" Astrid asked. "Then what? The earlier will takes effect. Marcus inherits. Does Marcus have a plan for these debts, or does he have the same plan you do, which is to inherit fast enough to borrow against the difference?"

The silence that followed told Astrid she had guessed correctly, and Celeste's face confirmed it — the flinch, quickly mastered, of a woman whose contingency plans had just been read aloud.

"There is money," Celeste said carefully. "If the estate settles cleanly. There is enough to save the house and enough left over to see the children provided for, and I am prepared to share that provision with the person who makes it possible."

And there it was. Not dressed up, not quite. Dressed, certainly — in the language of fairness, of compensation, of *a retainer for professional judgment rendered* — but naked underneath.

"How much?" Astrid asked, because she wanted to hear the number. Numbers were confessions in disguise.

"Forty thousand pounds," Celeste said. "On settlement. Plus expenses."

Forty thousand. Enough to be serious. Not enough to be insulting. Someone had done market research on Astrid Blackwood's integrity, and this was the price band it occupied in their estimation.

"No," Astrid said.

"Astrid." First use of her Christian name, warm, confiding, a hand on the arm that never actually touched. "You misunderstand me. This isn't bribery. This is a fee for expert opinion. You're being asked to review a document of dubious provenance and render your professional judgment. If your judgment is that the will is invalid — which any competent practitioner would conclude — then you'd be compensated for arriving at the correct answer. That's not corruption. That's how the world works."

"It's how *your* world works," Astrid said. "I've seen it work before. Three times." She picked up the ledger, weighed it in her hand — it was heavier than its pages justified, or perhaps that was imagination — and held it out. "Keep your accounts, Mrs. Ashcombe. And keep your forty thousand. Because here's what I know about buying judgments: the buyer never stops at one. Next year it'll be sixty, and the year after that you'll own me outright, and the day I'm no longer useful you'll wonder what else I can be paid to forget. I don't sell. Not opinions, not silences, not myself."

Celeste stood. The porcelain returned, layer by layer, the voice climbing back up to its drawing-room register as though the last ten minutes had been a costume change in reverse. "You'll regret this," she said pleasantly. "When the creditors come, and the house goes, and everyone asks why the lawyer couldn't see what was plainly in front of her — you'll wish you'd been kinder to a widow in a rainstorm."

"You're not a widow."

"No," Celeste agreed, at the door, with a smile that finally, briefly, showed the true architecture underneath. "But I know exactly what's coming. Do you?"

She left. The rain took her.

* * *

In her room, with the lamp turned low and the rain still working at the window like something trying to get in, Astrid set the evening out in pieces.

The ledger was real. That was the first conclusion, and it mattered. Fabricated evidence collapses under scrutiny, and Celeste had handed the book over too easily, too willingly, for it to be anything but genuine — or at least genuine enough that she believed it would survive inspection. Which meant the debts were real, which meant the desperation was real, which meant the motive was real.

Motive was the currency Astrid traded in. In three previous cases — three deaths, three tidy accident reports, three truths she had excavated and then, deliberately, buried again — motive had been the load-bearing wall of every structure she'd brought down. Grief doesn't kill. Anger rarely does. But debt — debt kills quietly, in kitchens and sickrooms and boathouses, and it kills respectable people most efficiently of all, because respectable people have the most to lose and the best practice at lying.

*Thornfield must not be sold.* Four words, and under them, an entire architecture of possibility.

The second conclusion was less comfortable. Celeste had come alone. No Marcus, no Gerald, no family counsel hovering at her shoulder. Either her husband didn't know she'd made the approach — possible, but Astrid doubted it; Marcus struck her as a man who noticed nothing except what he was afraid of, and he was afraid of everything — or the approach was sanctioned and deniable, a feeler sent out so that if Astrid ever talked, Marcus could claim ignorance with a straight face. Deniability had a smell to it. This room smelled of it.

And the third conclusion, the one Astrid turned over longest, was the strangest: Celeste had been *afraid*. Not of Astrid — of the conversation itself. Twice, mid-speech, her eyes had gone to the door. Once, when Astrid mentioned Wren, her whole face had performed a small controlled implosion, grief and fury and something sharper compressed into a single blink. There was history there, older than the debts. Debts were new; fear like that was furniture.

Astrid opened her notebook and wrote, in the small backward-slanting hand she used for things she didn't want casually read:

*C. wants will dead. Money real, desperation real. Offer = confession of need. Who taught her to be this frightened? Watch the daughter. The mother flinches at the child.*

She underlined the last line twice.

Outside, the rain thickened toward something with intent. Downstairs, Mrs. Tallow locked the front bar, and the inn settled into its creaks. Astrid was preparing for bed — toothbrush in hand, mind already running tomorrow's errands, the coroner's office, the parish records, the long drive back toward the coast — when her phone lit on the nightstand.

An email. From an address she didn't recognize, routed through some anonymizing service, subject line blank.

She almost deleted it. Then she saw the first line, and the toothbrush stayed in its cup, and she sat down slowly on the edge of the bed with the phone held close, the way one holds a letter that might be from the dead.

*I know what you're doing at Thornfield,* the message began. *My grandmother wrote about you. She said if anyone ever started asking questions, it would be a woman, and she said I shouldn't trust the ones who knock politely.*

*Please stop digging. Please. You'll make them finish what they started.*

It was unsigned. But the message ended with a single line that raised every hair on Astrid's arms, because it was dated — the sender had thought to date it, or had wanted her to know —

*Wren.*
