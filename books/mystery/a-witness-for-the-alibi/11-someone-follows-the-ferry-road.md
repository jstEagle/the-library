## 11. Someone Follows the Ferry Road

The village was called Greywater Ness, and it had the look of a place the sea had tried to take and half succeeded.

Astrid drove the last ten miles with the windows cracked despite the cold, because the air off the water smelled of salt and kelp and something older — tar, maybe, or the memory of tar — and it cleared the head in a way that no amount of coffee could. The road narrowed as it descended, hedged on one side by gorse gone feral and on the other by a low stone wall beyond which the land simply gave up, dropping away in grey-green terraces to a harbour that looked painted rather than built: a crescent of slate roofs, a stone quay, a dozen boats knocking their hulls against their moorings like impatient horses.

Twenty years ago, according to the ferry company's records — such as they were — an Edmund Ashcombe had boarded the morning crossing from this quay to the mainland, one of forty-one passengers on a Tuesday in October. He had never disembarked at the far terminal. No record of him existed after that. The official story, stitched together by police who had already closed the boathouse fire and wanted very much to keep it closed, was that he had changed his mind mid-crossing, or missed the boat entirely, or that the manifest itself was in error. A dead man's name on a passenger list was an anomaly, not a mystery. Anomalies got filed. Mysteries got worked.

Astrid had spent three days persuading the ferry company's records office to admit the original manifests still existed, bound in ledgers, stored in a damp annex behind the ticket office. The clerk — a young woman with chapped hands and the permanent apology of someone who worked for a failing institution — had confirmed over the phone that the October ledger for that year was present and accounted for.

*Present and accounted for.* Astrid turned the phrase over now as the car bumped down into the village, past a shuttered chandlery and a pub called The Drowned Rat whose sign depicted, with grim local humour, a rat swimming for its life. Present and accounted for. In Astrid's experience, documents were only ever described that way by people who hadn't actually looked at them recently.

The ticket office occupied the ground floor of a building that leaned slightly seaward, as if craning to watch the boats come in. Inside it smelled of paper and mildew and the ghost of pipe smoke. The same clerk from the phone — her name tag read *P. TULLOCH* — looked up from a crossword and did not quite manage to hide her disappointment at having a customer.

"Ms. Blackwood? You rang about the old manifests." She said it as though Astrid had rung about a leaking roof. "They're through the back. I'll have to fetch them. We don't let people browse unsupervised — insurance."

"Of course."

She disappeared through a door marked *PRIVATE*, and Astrid waited, and while waiting, did what Astrid always did in new rooms: catalogued the exits, noted the sightlines, and read the room the way other people read newspapers. A wall of framed photographs — the quay in 1974, the quay in flood, the quay under snow. A corkboard of faded flyers: regattas, missing cats, a charity swim. And pinned near the bottom, yellowed almost to translucence, a newspaper clipping from twenty years ago: **LOCAL MAN LOST IN THORNFIELD BOATHOUSE BLAZE**, with a photograph of Edmund Ashcombe that made Astrid lean closer.

He had been handsome in the careless way of men who had never needed to try — dark hair falling over his forehead, a smile that suggested he knew something funny he'd never bother to share. Vivienne's letters had described him as *my north star, my difficult, brilliant boy*. Standing here in the village where he had supposedly vanished, Astrid felt the letters change weight. They had been written by a woman posting words into darkness. Now the darkness had a face.

"Here we are." Tulloch reappeared hauling two enormous ledgers, setting them on the counter with a thump that raised dust. "October, year you wanted. What is it exactly you're looking for?"

"A passenger list. The morning crossing on the fourteenth."

Tulloch licked her thumb, found the month, began turning pages. The manifests were handwritten in a variety of hands — different clerks, different days — names in columns, fares in shillings converted untidily to pounds. She ran her finger down the fourteenth.

And stopped.

Astrid saw it before Tulloch spoke: the wrongness of the page. Every leaf in the open ledger showed the same pattern — ruled lines, dense columns, the ink-brown patina of age. But the page for the fourteenth of October was different. Not missing. *Replaced.* A newer sheet had been pasted or tipped in over the original, its paper whiter, its ruling fainter, and on it a neat modern hand had transcribed a partial list — perhaps fifteen names where there should have been forty-one — ending abruptly halfway down the page in a line of dashes, as if the copyist had grown bored or been interrupted.

Or as if the transcription were a forgery designed to be found instead of the original.

"The page," Astrid said quietly. "It's been replaced."

Tulloch frowned, touched the edge of the newer sheet, and peeled it back — and Astrid's stomach dropped, because beneath it there was nothing. Not the original page. Not even stubs where the original had been cut out. The underlying leaf had been razored flush to the binding, so cleanly that only a hairline ridge of glue remained, like a scar where something had been removed by someone who knew exactly what they were doing and had all the time in the world.

"That's—" Tulloch's voice went thin. "That wasn't like that when I checked the inventory. I *inventoried* these, last spring. I'd have noticed—"

"When was the last time anyone opened this ledger?"

"I don't— people don't ask for these. Years. Maybe since before I started." She was looking at the mutilated binding the way one looks at a wound on a pet. "Who would *do* that?"

Someone who knew I was coming, Astrid thought. Someone who has been ahead of me since the funeral. Or — worse, and more likely — someone who had been ahead of everyone for twenty years, and had only now returned to tidy up.

"May I photograph what's here?" Astrid asked. "The replacement sheet?"

Tulloch nodded numbly. Astrid took the pictures — the transcribed names, the dashes, the clean-cut absence beneath — and then stood for a long moment with the ledger open, feeling the particular chill that came not from fear but from recognition. This was not the act of a guilty man covering tracks in panic. This was archival work. Patient, careful, done with a blade and fresh paper and time. Whoever had gutted this manifest had done it calmly, recently, and with access to information they should not have had: namely, that Astrid Blackwood was asking questions about the fourteenth of October.

Unless they hadn't needed the information. Unless they had simply known, all along, that someday someone would come looking, and had kept the wound pre-made, ready to be discovered, a decoy laid decades in advance.

Either possibility meant the same thing. The killer — Vivienne's killer, Edmund's attempted killer, the hand behind the fire — was not resting. Was not safe in some distant city living on old sins. Was here, or near here, or watching.

"Thank you," Astrid said to Tulloch, closing the ledger gently, as if it could feel pain. "You've been more help than you know."

"I haven't helped at all," Tulloch said miserably. "There's nothing here."

"There's everything here," Astrid said. "An absence is evidence too. It's just evidence that had to travel further to reach us."

Outside, the light had begun to fail. The harbour lay in that flat pewter calm that precedes a change of weather, and the first lamps along the quay were buzzing on, halos of orange in the salt haze. Astrid walked to the car, unlocked it, sat behind the wheel without starting the engine, and let the day settle.

That was when the discipline took over — the habit built across three cases that had never appeared in any newspaper, three quiet graves given their true names in private. *Observe before you move. Move before you're observed moving.* Astrid checked the mirror.

The van was still there.

* * *

It was a white panel van, unremarkable in the way of every tradesman's vehicle on every coast road in the country, parked fifty yards up the quay beside a fishmonger's that had clearly closed hours ago. Astrid had noticed it on arrival without noticing it — the way one notices wallpaper. But it had been there when Astrid parked, and it was there now, and in forty minutes no one had loaded or unloaded anything from it, and its engine ticked with the residual heat of recent running.

Coincidence, said the reasonable voice. Coastal villages are full of vans.

Reasonable voices had gotten better people than Astrid killed. Astrid started the engine, pulled out of the space at an unhurried pace, and drove the length of the quay past the van without looking at it — a deliberate exercise in not-looking, eyes fixed on the road ahead, counting in the periphery. As the car passed, the van's wing mirror angled. Just slightly. Just enough.

Astrid drove out of the village on the coast road, climbed through the gorse, and at the crest of the hill — where the road bent inland around a knoll and a driver's view of the rear window would be blocked for perhaps six seconds — took the turn fast, swung the car into a farm gate's recessed pull-off that some considerate farmer had gravelled years ago, killed the lights, and waited.

Ninety seconds later the van came over the crest. Too fast for the bend. It braked hard, its nose dipping, and for a moment it hung there at the top of the hill, silhouetted against the last bruised band of sunset, and Astrid understood that it was scanning the road below — looking for a car that had ceased to exist.

Then it rolled slowly onward, down toward Greywater Ness, and was gone.

Astrid sat in the darkening car with both hands on the wheel and let the adrenaline arrive properly, because suppressing it only meant paying interest later. Heart rate elevated. Hands steady enough. Mind — the mind was doing what it always did, which was worse than fear: it was *filing*. The van had followed from the village or before it. Which meant it had been waiting, or it had been summoned. Summoned how? Astrid's enquiries had been made by telephone, from the hotel, thirty miles inland. The records clerk had been told only a name and a date. Unless the clerk had mentioned the call to someone — a supervisor, a local historian, a husband — the leak was elsewhere. The hotel. The solicitor's office. Gerald Pyle's firm, through which Astrid's appointment at Thornfield had been arranged and therefore logged.

Or the family itself. Marcus, brittle and guilt-ridden. Celeste, whose desperation wore dignity like a borrowed coat. Wren, who flinched at nothing so completely that the flinching had become its own kind of scream.

Or someone none of them suspected. Someone who had gutted a ferry ledger with a razor blade and left a forgery in its place, patient as rot.

"You're being hunted," Astrid said aloud to the empty car, testing the shape of it. The words hung in the cold. They didn't frighten, exactly. They *focused*. There was a difference between digging into a closed case and being dug for, and the difference was this electric clarity, this narrowing of the world to essentials. Three times before, Astrid had crossed this line — from the one asking questions to the one the answers endangered. Three times before, the crossing had ended with the truth extracted and the danger survived and the whole affair folded away unspoken, sealed in the vault where Astrid kept the things that could not be told without destroying people who deserved destruction less than they deserved mercy.

This time felt different. This time the hunter had a head start measured in decades.

Astrid turned the car around and drove back toward the mainland road, taking a route that added twenty minutes and shed any tail — through a forestry track, past a churchyard where the stones leaned like tired mourners, onto the main road with its honest double lines and its stream of ordinary traffic. Twice, Astrid checked the mirror for headlights that held too steady. None came. Either the van had given up, or its driver was better than the first impression suggested, and Astrid genuinely did not know which prospect was worse.

* * *

Back at the hotel — a grey granite pile above the estuary, all creaking floors and radiators that clanked like distant machinery — Astrid spread the day's harvest across the writing desk: photographs of the forged manifest page, notes on timings, a sketch of the van and its position on the quay. Then, because the mind needed a problem it could actually solve tonight, Astrid turned again to the photocopies of Vivienne's unsent letters, reading them not for grief this time but for geography.

*You always loved the crossing,* Vivienne had written, in a letter dated eleven years ago. *I think of you on the water somewhere, and I make myself believe the sea keeps you safer than this house ever did.*

The crossing. The ferry. Vivienne had known — had believed, at least — that Edmund had left by sea. Had she known about Greywater Ness specifically? Astrid flipped forward through the copies, hunting. Two letters later:

*Maren wrote to me once. Only once, and unsigned, but I knew her hand. She said the lighthouse was kind to them. I have never dared write back. If they are alive, my silence is the last gift I can give them.*

Astrid set the page down very carefully, as though it might startle.

*Them.* Maren Ostley, the housekeeper who had vanished the same week as the fire, whose locket had surfaced in the ashes — the locket now wrapped in a sock in Astrid's suitcase, the one object the ransackers of the hotel room had conspicuously left behind. *The lighthouse was kind to them.* A lighthouse on this coast. A keeper who had sheltered two fugitives, or so Vivienne had believed, secondhand, through a single unsigned letter.

And Astrid had driven right past the headland on the way in — had seen it in the failing light, a white tower on the black shoulder of the point, two miles north of the village, its lamp sweeping the water with the patience of something that had been keeping the same vigil for a century.

The pieces were no longer scattered. They were converging, and convergence was dangerous, because Astrid was not the only one converging. The van on the quay. The gutted ledger. Someone else was reading the same map, walking the same trail, and they had twenty years' familiarity with the ground.

A knock at the door made Astrid's hand close instinctively around the letter-knife lying on the desk — an absurd weapon, but the hand does what it knows.

"Yes?"

"Room service, miss. The tea you ordered." A pause. "I don't think you ordered tea, actually. But there's a pot going spare in the kitchen, and it seemed a shame to waste it."

Astrid exhaled, crossed the room, and opened the door on a teenage girl with a tray and a grin, took the tea, tipped her, and locked the door behind her. Then Astrid stood in the middle of the quiet room, listening to the radiator clank, and made the decision that had been assembling itself all evening.

Tomorrow, before dawn, before anyone who might be watching the roads had finished their breakfast — tomorrow Astrid would drive to the lighthouse. Not along the coast road, where a white van could idle and wait, but across country, the long way, the way a farmer would drive.

Because Vivienne Ashcombe had spent thirty years writing letters to a son the world insisted was dead, and her last unfinished letter had named the only person left who might have seen the truth with their own eyes. Somewhere on that black headland, an old keeper had kept a secret for twenty years — and secrets, Astrid had learned, were like the tide. You could hold them back for a while.

But sooner or later, something always came to disturb the water.

Astrid packed the locket, the letters, and the photographs into the lining of a coat that would not leave their shoulders, set the alarm for five, and slept — or lay in the dark listening to the sea, which amounted to the same thing — while two miles north, unseen, the lighthouse swept its beam across the water, over and over, like a question no one had yet answered.
