## 3. Margins and Ciphers

The will of Edmund Voss weighed four pounds and eleven ounces, and Rafael Kavanagh knew this because they had weighed it, because weighing things was what Rafael did instead of sleeping.

It was the third night since the reading, and the conference room at Kavanagh & Associates had been reduced to its essential furniture: one desk lamp, one pot of coffee going bitter on the warmer, and one estate lawyer sitting in a pool of yellow light like a specimen under glass. Outside, Port Merrow did what Port Merrow did after ten o'clock, which was nothing, loudly. A foghorn somewhere out past the breakwater kept time with the rain.

Rafael had spent twenty-six years being the person nobody remembered introducing. It was a craft. Halbrook had taught it to them the way other mentors taught golf: *You are not the story, Kavanagh. You are the binding. Nobody thanks the binding.* Rafael had taken the lesson to heart with the devotion of the genuinely suited, and for two and a half decades the trick had worked flawlessly — until a dead shipping magnate had gone and made them visible in front of nine beneficiaries and a stenographer, and now Rafael could not walk into the harborside bakery without the woman at the till saying *you're the Voss lawyer* as though announcing a diagnosis.

So: nights. Nights were safe. Nights were when the invisible did their best work.

Rafael turned to page six of the will and looked again at the margins.

They had noticed them at the reading, or almost noticed — the way you notice a draft without locating its source. Faint pencil marks running down the outer edges of the document, so light that under the conference room's chandelier they'd read as smudges, as the ghost of an eraser. But under the desk lamp, raked sideways by the light, they resolved into something deliberate. Small numerals. Ticks and strokes. A notation system of some kind, marching down the margin of every page like ants following a seam.

Rafael put on the white cotton gloves — habit, not necessity; the will was theirs to handle now, executor's privilege — and began cataloguing.

The marks came in clusters. Each cluster sat beside a clause, and each cluster contained between three and seven characters: digits mostly, occasionally a letter, once what appeared to be a small drawn anchor, which Rafael chose initially to interpret as whimsy. Beside Clause 4 — the disposition of the Harrow Street properties — the margin read *11-3 / 7-2 / V*. Beside Clause 9, the trust distributions, a longer string: *4-19 · 4-20 · 5-1 · 5-2*. Beside the residuary clause, the longest cluster of all, fourteen characters deep, ending in something that might have been *FDRY* if the writer had been in a hurry, and someone had been in a hurry; the last strokes were scored hard enough to dent the page.

Rafael sat back and let the coffee go cold and thought about what lawyers think when they find a pattern in a document they didn't draft, which is: *who*, and then, immediately and less comfortably, *why not me*.

Because here was the thing that had been sitting in Rafael's chest since the reading like a swallowed stone: Rafael Dunmore had not written this will. Every lawyer in Port Merrow assumed otherwise — of course Voss's will was Kavanagh's work, who else — and Rafael had stood at the head of the table reading their own supposed handiwork and understood within two paragraphs that they were reading a stranger. The drafting was competent but wrong. The clauses turned in directions Rafael would never have turned them. And the execution date was three years old, which meant Edmund Voss had walked into some other office, or sat down at some other table, and arranged his affairs without the one lawyer in the county whose entire reputation rested on knowing where everyone's bodies were filed.

And then he'd named Rafael executor anyway.

*You'll understand why,* Voss had supposedly said, according to Prentiss, the family solicitor, who had delivered the document with the apologetic air of a man handing over someone else's baby. *Or you won't. He said either way, you're the only one who'll do it properly.*

Flattery, Rafael had decided at the time. The oldest tool in the testamentary toolbox. But nights change your mind about things. Nights make flattery look like instruction.

Rafael pulled the Voss Shipping ledgers from the document carton — five volumes, leather-spined, delivered with the estate papers in a crate that smelled of bilge and old rope — and opened Volume Four to a random page. Dates in the left column. Entries in the right. Account numbers down the middle margin, three digits each, running from roughly 100 to 640 across the volume's span.

Rafael looked at the ledger's account numbers.

Then at the marginal clusters in the will.

Then back.

*11-3.*

Volume Eleven didn't exist. But entry 113 did — no, wait. Rafael tried it the other way: page 11, line 3. Page eleven of Volume Four, third entry down: *Harbormaster's assessment, Q1 — £240.* Nothing. A nothing entry, the kind of entry that existed purely to be boring.

Rafael tried Volume Five, page 11, line 3. *Coal tar, deck sealant — £86.* Also nothing.

The coffee had gone fully cold. Rafael poured it anyway, drank it anyway, and stood at the window watching rain crawl down the glass while the back of their mind did what the back of Rafael's mind always did, which was keep working after the front of it had gone for a walk. *You are not the story,* Halbrook's voice said fondly from wherever dead mentors kept their offices. *You are the binding.* And bindings, Rafael thought, bindings hold pages in order. Bindings know the sequence.

Sequence. Not location. The clusters weren't addresses; they were instructions. Read me in this order.

Rafael went back to the will and copied out every marginal cluster onto a legal pad, in document order, and then — because the numbers ranged up into the twenties and there were only five ledger volumes — tried the obvious: treating each pair as *volume-page* across a continuous run. 11-3 became Volume One, page thirteen? No. Became — Rafael counted on their fingers like a schoolchild and hated themselves for it — the eleventh *folio group*, third entry. The ledgers were paginated in fives, folio groups of five entries each, an old coastal accounting convention Halbrook had once called "the harbormaster's stubbornness."

Eleventh folio group. Third entry. Volume Four.

Page fifty-six, line three: *FDRY — Graywater Foundry & Chain Works — special fabrication — £310 — acct. 441.*

Rafael stared at it.

Graywater Foundry. Everyone in Port Merrow knew Graywater; it stamped the mooring chains and dredge weights that hung off half the boats in the harbor, and its owner, Teague, was the kind of man who sponsored the regatta and got photographed doing it. There was nothing strange about a shipping company paying its foundry. That was the trouble. That was precisely the trouble. The entry was perfectly ordinary, except that it was dated the eleventh of October —

— and Edmund Voss had vanished from his boat on the night of October fourteenth, three years ago, and the sea had declined to give him back.

Rafael wrote the date on the legal pad and drew a box around it and then, feeling slightly ridiculous, drew a second box around the first, the way one does when circling something changes it from a fact into a wound.

*Special fabrication.* Three hundred and ten pounds, in a week when the *Merrow Queen* — Voss's beloved ketch, the boat he'd been found without — was in for routine maintenance, per the maintenance logs Rafael had inventoried on day one because inventorying was breathing. Routine maintenance did not require special fabrications from a chain works. Routine maintenance required paint and caulking and, if the owner was fussy, new cleats.

Rafael decoded the next cluster. Then the next. The lamp hummed. The foghorn groaned. Somewhere around two in the morning the pad held eleven lines, and Rafael stopped reading them as entries and started reading them as sentences:

A payment to Graywater Foundry, the week before the disappearance.
A payment — *retainer, professional services* — to a firm whose initials Rafael recognized and refused, for the moment, to recognize.
Two cash withdrawals routed through the trust, flagged in the ledger only by a code — *D-series* — that appeared nowhere in any legitimate chart of accounts Rafael had ever seen.
And at the bottom, in the longest cluster, the one ending in the dented *FDRY*: a figure. Forty pounds. Followed by the word, spelled out in the cipher's letter-substitutions, *WEIGHT*.

Rafael set down the pen very carefully, the way you set down something when your hands have opinions of their own.

The room was cold. Rafael realized they'd been sweating anyway. The thrill was still there — God help them, the thrill was real, the clean hot pleasure of a lock yielding, twenty-six years of competence finally pointed at something worthy of it — but underneath the thrill, arriving late and staying longer, was the other thing. The curdling thing. Because knowledge, Rafael had explained to a hundred clients over brandy they were billing for, is not a possession. It is a position. Once you know, you are standing somewhere, and the ground does not care whether you volunteered to stand there.

Edmund Voss had bought forty pounds of iron from a foundry six days before he sailed out of Port Merrow for the last time, and he had hidden the purchase inside a will he'd entrusted to the one lawyer in the county too careful to lose it and too honest, presumably, to bury it.

*You'll understand why. Or you won't.*

"Oh, I understand," Rafael said aloud to the empty room, and was unsettled by how much their voice sounded like Halbrook's.

* * *

Sleep declined the invitation. Rafael tried — lay down at three, lay awake at four, gave up at half past — and by five was back at the desk with the shades still down, working through the D-series codes, because a mind that has found a seam will pick at it until the whole garment confesses.

The D-series entries totaled, across three years of ledgers, just under nineteen thousand pounds. Nineteen thousand pounds in unexplained cash movements through a family trust, coded in a private shorthand, in a company whose books were otherwise immaculate — immaculate in the specific way that books are immaculate when someone has gone through them removing everything interesting. Rafael had audited enough estates to know the difference between clean and scoured. These ledgers were scoured. Except whoever had done the scouring hadn't known about the will's margins, because the margins weren't part of the ledgers. They were the key to the ledgers, kept separate, kept *outside*, kept in the one document guaranteed by law to survive and by custom never to be read closely.

Voss hadn't hidden the record. He'd *published* it, in a font of one reader.

But which reader? Rafael, specifically — named executor against all sense — or whoever happened to hold the office? No. The margins predated the appointment; the handwriting in the margins, Rafael was now nearly certain, matched the will's own drafting hand, and the will was three years old. Voss had built the lock before he died. He'd simply trusted the profession itself to eventually turn the key. Or he'd trusted one particular member of it.

Rafael thought about the second letter. It was in the drawer, in an envelope, in a plastic sleeve, because Rafael had begun keeping evidence in plastic sleeves sometime around midnight and had noted the transition without approving of it. *Burn the ledger and there will be no more letters.* Someone out there believed Rafael had already decoded enough to be dangerous. Which meant someone out there knew what the margins were. Which meant the circle of people who understood this document was not, in fact, a circle of one.

Unless — and Rafael turned this over with the delicacy of a man handling a live wire — unless the letters' author wanted Rafael to believe the circle was larger than it was. Fear was cheaper than bribery and more reliable than flattery. If you wanted a careful person to destroy something, you didn't need to catch them; you only needed to convince them they'd been caught.

Rafael looked at the legal pad. Eleven lines. A foundry payment dated six days before a drowning. Forty pounds of weight. Nineteen thousand pounds of silence money. A firm of solicitors whose initials Rafael had recognized and refused.

There was a version of the next hour in which Rafael closed the pad, locked the will in the fireproof cabinet, and telephoned no one, and waited to see whether the letters stopped coming, and told themselves forever afterward that discretion had been the correct professional judgment. Rafael was intimately familiar with that version. Rafael had watched Halbrook live inside that version for thirty years, comfortable and respected and unreadable, and had admired it right up until this week.

Instead, Rafael did the thing that could not be undone. They took out a fresh sheet, transcribed all eleven decoded lines in full, dated it, signed it, and placed it in a sealed envelope marked *Executor's Working Notes — Confidential*, which they addressed to themselves and dropped in the office mail tray for morning collection. Not the police. Not yet. Just paper, traveling through the ordinary machinery of the post, so that by Friday there would exist outside these walls a record that Rafael Dunmore had known, and when.

It was, Rafael reflected, stamping the envelope with more force than the stamp required, a strange kind of insurance: mailing yourself the proof of your own complicity. Because that was the word for it now, wasn't it. Complicity. Not for the drowning — Rafael had touched nothing but paper — but for the knowing. Every hour the ledger stayed undecoded, Rafael had been innocent. The innocence had lasted exactly as long as the cipher had.

Outside, the rain had stopped. The sky over the harbor was doing the bruised gray thing it did before dawn, and the foghorn had finally shut up, and Rafael stood at the window with cold coffee and hotter hands and understood that the door behind them had quietly locked.

The intercom buzzed.

Rafael startled badly enough to slosh coffee — the office phone did not ring at six a.m., the office phone had *never* rung at six a.m., invisibility had perks and one of them was silence. They pressed the button. "Kavanagh."

"Morning." The voice was female, clipped, unfamiliar. "This is Okafor's service — I'm calling on behalf of Mrs. Voss. She'd like to retain counsel. Estate-adjacent matters." A pause with texture in it. "She asked specifically for you. Said you were the only one Edmund trusted."

Rafael looked at the legal pad, face-up on the desk, eleven lines of drowned men's arithmetic catching the first gray light.

"Tell Mrs. Voss," Rafael heard themselves say, calm as a ledger line, "I'll call her office at nine."

They hung up and stood very still in the empty room, listening to the building tick as the radiators woke, aware of two facts with total clarity: that the widow of the drowned man wanted her lawyer close enough to watch; and that Rafael had just agreed to stand, once more, exactly where everyone could see them.

The mail tray sat by the door. The envelope lay in it, addressed in their own hand, holding the only copy in the world of what Edmund Voss had paid forty pounds of iron to say.

Nine o'clock suddenly felt very far away.
