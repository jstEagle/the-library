## 15. Okafor Changes Sides

I have been lied to by better people than Marisol Voss. That's not a compliment to her; it's a compliment to the profession of lying, which attracts talent the way a harbor lamp attracts moths—inevitably, and mostly to their ruin.

It started with the weather.

I'd written in my report of the twelfth that the chandlery owner, Pell, had told me about fog on the morning Voss sailed out and never came back. Thick fog, Pell said, the kind that swallows a boat before she clears the breakwater. I put it down the way I put everything down: plainly, with dates, with quotation marks where quotation marks were owed. Then, three days later, over tea I was not offered but given anyway, Marisol said to me, "At least it was a clear morning. He loved a clear morning on the water."

Clear. Not foggy. Clear as a bell.

Now, people misremember weather all the time. Grief rearranges the furniture of memory; I've seen widows swear their husbands died in summer when the coroner's report says November. But Marisol wasn't misremembering. She said *clear* the way you say a line you've rehearsed in front of a mirror—smoothed flat, sanded down, no splinters left. And my report said fog.

Which meant one of two things. Either she hadn't read my reports, which was possible—she paid me to know things so she wouldn't have to—or she had read them, or rather someone had read them for her, and what reached her was not what I wrote.

I did what any sensible man does when he suspects his mail is being opened: I tested it.

* * *

The test was childish and I'm not proud of it, but pride is a luxury for people whose clients don't lie to them. In my next report—the routine one, Rafael Dunmore's movements logged hour by hour, the visit to the foundry noted with the foundry's address spelled correctly—I embedded an error. A deliberate one. I wrote that Dunmore had met at the harbor master's office with a man I described as "a dredging contractor named Ferro." There is no Ferro. There is no such meeting. The harbor master's calendar for that day shows nothing but a complaint about gulls nesting in the pilot boat.

If Marisol read my reports herself, the fiction would pass through her like water through a net. If someone else read them first—if someone edited, summarized, softened—then Ferro might survive the trip. A invented name is a canary in a coal mine. It dies quietly, and you learn something about the air.

I waited nine days. Nine days of tailing Dunmore through rain that seemed personally offended by the town, of drinking coffee at the chandlery until Pell started leaving the good biscuits out for me, of watching the tide do its slow bookkeeping on the shingle beach. And then, at our weekly sit-down, Marisol set down her cup and said, "Mr. Okafor, tell me again about this contractor. Ferro. The one consulting with the lawyer about the dredging."

The canary was dead.

I kept my face doing what faces are supposed to do—attentive, mildly bored—and said, "There's no contractor named Ferro, Mrs. Voss. I never wrote that name."

She blinked. For half a second something moved behind her eyes, quick as a fish under ice, and then the composure came down over it like a shopfront shutter. "Then I must have misunderstood," she said. "Someone mentioned it."

"Who?"

"It hardly matters."

"It matters to me," I said, "because it means someone is reading my reports besides you. And whoever it is changed what I wrote before it got to you."

Silence. The clock on her mantel ticked with the smugness of clocks in expensive rooms. Outside, the harbor did its gray business.

"You're paid to follow the lawyer," she said finally, "not to audit my household."

"I'm paid to find the truth," I said. "You told me that yourself, the first day. You said you wanted to know who killed your husband, or whether anyone had." I leaned forward. "So let me ask you plainly, ma'am, because I've earned plainness: do you want the truth? Or do you want a version of it that's been through somebody's hands first?"

And here is the thing I will remember about Marisol Voss long after this case is done and I've spent whatever's left of my fee on something foolish: she didn't answer. She looked at me with those still, deep-water eyes, and she didn't say *the truth*, and she didn't say *get out*, and she didn't fire me, though God knows I'd handed her the scissors. She just said, "You may go, Mr. Okafor," in a voice like a door closing softly rather than slamming, and somehow that was worse than either.

I went. But I took my ledger with me, and I'd already taken copies of everything else.

* * *

Here's what I knew by then, laid out in my office among the cold coffee rings:

One. My reports were being intercepted and altered between my desk and Marisol's reading glasses. The Ferro test proved it. Whoever did the altering was literate enough to edit smoothly and confident enough to think I'd never check.

Two. The alterations all ran the same direction. Nothing was added that pointed toward guilt; things were subtracted that pointed anywhere at all. My notes on the two men who helped cast off Voss's boat—Pell's contradiction, the detail that gnawed at me like a dog at a bone—had been trimmed from the summary Marisol received. I know because I asked her, casually, whether she'd heard anything about dockhands helping her husband aboard, and she said, "He always cast off alone. He was particular about it." Particular. Alone. When the last man to see him alive told me otherwise with his own mouth.

Three. The person doing the editing had access to Marisol's correspondence and her confidence. That's a short list in a house like hers. Family solicitor. Housekeeper. Daughter. The widow herself, if she was directing it—which I no longer believed, because a woman running a cover-up doesn't get caught out by her own investigator's fake name and then fail to fire him.

Four. And this was the item that kept me up past midnight, staring at my ceiling like it owed me money: Rafael Dunmore had visited the foundry. The same foundry whose stamp, if the lawyer's cipher-work was half as good as I suspected, would be on whatever held Edmund Voss down at the bottom of the harbor. I was being paid by a woman who might be burying evidence, to watch a man who was apparently digging it up.

There's a moment in every investigation when you realize you're standing on the wrong side of the glass. You've been watching the specimen, and it turns out the specimen has been watching you, and one of you is in the wrong cage. I'd been hired to be the watcher. It was time to consider becoming the watched—or at least to compare notes with the other party.

* * *

Dunmore worked late. I'd learned that much. The lights in the estate office above the old bank building burned past eleven most nights, and through the frosted glass the silhouette bent over documents like a scholar over scripture. On Thursday night, with the rain coming down in that fine malicious drizzle the town specializes in, I climbed the stairs and knocked.

The typing stopped. A long pause—the pause of a person deciding whether the knock is danger or inconvenience. Then: "It's open."

Rafael Dunmore is not what I expected, though I couldn't tell you what I expected. Smaller than their reputation, maybe. Tired around the eyes in a way that isn't about sleep. They looked up from a desk buried in paper—actual paper, ledgers and photocopies and a magnifying glass, the tools of someone doing this the slow honest way—and they looked at me the way you look at a bill you were expecting but hoped would be smaller.

"Mr. Okafor," they said. "Mrs. Voss's investigator. Should I be flattered or alarmed?"

"Alarmed," I said. "Flattery comes later, if at all." I hung my wet coat on the hook they indicated without being asked, which told me something about how badly they wanted to hear what I'd come to say. "I need to show you something, and then I need to ask you a question, and then—depending on your answer—we may need to decide whether we're on the same side or opposite ones."

"That's a great deal of structure for ten o'clock at night."

"I'm a structured man."

I laid out my evidence the way I lay out everything: chronologically, without decoration. The fog versus the clear morning. The Ferro test. The trimmed contradiction about the two men on the dock. I watched Dunmore's face while I talked, and I'll tell you what I saw—not shock, not fear, but recognition. The look of someone confirming a suspicion they'd been carrying alone, the way you carry a heavy bag farther than you should because there's nowhere safe to set it down.

"The solicitor," Dunmore said quietly. "Prentiss handles all of Marisol's correspondence. He summarizes everything. He's done it for years—he did it for Edmund." A pause. "Or so I'm told."

"You don't sound sure."

"I'm not sure of anything anymore." Dunmore took off their glasses and rubbed the bridge of their nose, and for a moment looked less like a lawyer and more like what they probably were underneath: a tired person holding a secret too big for their hands. "Why are you telling me this? He pays you. She pays you. Your interest runs the other direction."

"My interest," I said, "runs toward the truth, same as yours apparently does. And here's my question, the one I warned you about." I leaned on the desk. "Did you go to the foundry?"

"Yes."

"What did you find?"

For a long moment they studied me, and I let them. I know what I look like—a big man, weathered, built along the lines of a filing cabinet—and I've learned to let people do their arithmetic while I wait. Whatever sum Dunmore arrived at, they must have liked it, because they turned a ledger page around and slid it across the desk.

"Forty pounds of chain-weight iron," they said. "Ordered the day before Edmund Voss sailed. Paid through the trust. Stamped with the foundry's mark." Their voice stayed level, professional, but underneath it I heard the thing that level voices are built to contain. "Someone bought enough iron to sink a man, Mr. Okafor. And the paperwork says who."

I read the entry twice. Numbers don't lie, but they also don't volunteer; somebody had made these numbers speak, and the somebody was sitting across from me with ink on their fingers at ten o'clock on a Thursday night.

"Why haven't you gone to the police?"

"Because everything I have is inference and cipher," Dunmore said. "Because the last lawyer who got close to this—" they stopped, and something crossed their face that I recognized as grief wearing work clothes, "—because I have reason to believe the police in this town have been managed for twenty years. Because whoever wrote me letters threatening me knows things only an insider could know. And because I wanted—" they hesitated, choosing the word with a lawyer's care, "—corroboration. From someone outside the estate. Someone whose loyalty hasn't been bought, because I understand everyone's has been bought at some point. Including mine, in a way. Including my mentor's."

"Halbrook," I said.

That landed. I'll admit I enjoyed it a little—watchers enjoy being right—but mostly I didn't, because Dunmore's face when I said the name was the face of a person hearing a diagnosis they'd already suspected.

"You know about Halbrook."

"I know he drew retainers from Voss Shipping for twenty years. I know he died the month after Voss disappeared. I know his files were boxed up by his firm and half of them never made it to storage." I shrugged. "I was hired to look into a drowning, Mr. Dunmore. Drownings are cheap. Everything attached to them is expensive."

We sat there in the lamplight, the two of us, the watcher and the watched, and I felt the strange vertigo of realizing we'd been circling the same truth from opposite sides for weeks, each assuming the other was the wall when we were both, in fact, the door.

"So," Dunmore said. "Where does that leave us?"

"It leaves us," I said, "with a problem. I can't keep reporting to Marisol through Prentiss. Every fact I give her gets laundered before it reaches her, and every fact I don't give her leaves her blind in a house full of people deciding what she sees. But if I quit, I lose my eyes inside the house. And if I stay and feed Prentiss garbage, I'm lying to a client, which I've never done and don't intend to start."

"And if you stay and feed me the truth instead?"

"Then I'm betraying a client," I said. "Unless."

"Unless?"

"Unless my client wants the truth. Which she claims to. Which she may even mean." I rubbed my jaw. "There's a version of this where I put everything in front of Marisol directly. No summaries. No Prentiss. Face to face, my words, her ears. If she takes it—if she lets me dig where the digging goes—then I'm still her man, and everything I find is hers. If she refuses..." I spread my hands. "Then she's chosen her side, and I'm free to choose mine."

Dunmore was quiet for a moment. Rain ticked against the window. Somewhere below, the town went about its small dark business.

"She won't take it," Dunmore said at last, and there was no cruelty in it, only a terrible gentleness. "Marisol saw something on that dock the morning Edmund sailed. I don't know what yet. But she's been carrying it a long time, and people who carry things that long stop wanting to put them down. The weight starts to feel like penance."

"You sound like you speak from experience."

"I sound," Dunmore said, with the ghost of a smile, "like I've been invisible for forty years and have developed opinions about the people who prefer it that way."

* * *

I gave Marisol forty-eight hours. I owe her that much, and more than she'll ever know I owe her.

I came to the house on Saturday morning, when the light off the harbor does its best to make the place look innocent, and I asked for ten minutes alone with her—no Prentiss, no housekeeper, no daughter drifting through with her charming questions. Marisol received me in the morning room with her hands folded and her face arranged, and I laid it all out. The altered reports. The Ferro test. The two men on the dock. The foundry order, which I attributed to "sources close to the estate investigation" because some confidences you keep even when you're breaking others.

"Mrs. Voss," I finished, "I'm your investigator. I work for you. But I can't work through filters. Either you want what I find—all of it, unedited—or you want what someone decides you should hear. Tell me which, and I'll serve you accordingly. I'll serve you either way. That's the job. But I need to hear it from you."

She looked at me for a long time. And I want to be fair to her, because history won't be, and because fairness is the only currency I deal in that doesn't devalue. Marisol Voss is not a monster. She is a woman who married a powerful man and learned, the way women in that position learn, that survival means knowing which truths are load-bearing and leaving them exactly where they stand. She loved him, probably. She feared him, definitely. And on some gray morning three years ago she stood somewhere near that dock and saw something she has been paying interest on ever since.

"Mr. Okafor," she said, "you've been thorough. I appreciate thoroughness. But my husband drowned. Boats capsize. Men drink. The sea doesn't need conspiracies to take what it takes." Her voice was smooth as poured cream. "I'd like you to conclude your engagement. The final payment will be wired Monday."

No anger. No denial. Just a door, closing softly, the way she does everything.

I stood, and I tipped my head, and I walked out through that beautiful house with its beautiful silence, and on the drive back into town I felt lighter than I had any business feeling. Fired. Professionally ruined, possibly—Voss money talked in this town, and I'd just told it to shut up. And underneath all that, steady as a keel: relief.

* * *

Monday evening I climbed the stairs above the old bank again. Dunmore opened the door before I knocked, which meant they'd been watching the street, which meant they understood the game as well as I did now.

"Well?" they asked.

"Fired," I said. "Politely. With severance." I stepped inside and set my bag on their desk—the bag with everything in it, my real ledger, my unaltered reports, Pell's statement about the two men, the photographs of the cleat and the tide line, three years of a drowning examined properly for the first time. "Which makes me a free agent. So I'm here to offer my services to the estate's executor, pro bono until the estate can afford me, which I understand is never."

Dunmore looked at the bag, then at me, and I watched them weigh it—the same arithmetic as before, but heavier now, because accepting me meant accepting a witness, and witnesses can't be unseen.

"You understand what this costs you," they said. "This town doesn't forgive people who choose the truth over belonging. I know. I chose invisibility instead, and even that had a price."

"I'm from Lagos by way of Portsmouth by way of six towns that forgot me the week I left," I said. "Belonging and I have an understanding. It stays away from me, I stay away from it." I unbuckled the bag. "Besides. Somebody in this story has to keep a clean ledger. Might as well be the two of us."

They almost smiled. It was a small thing, that almost-smile, but I'd seen enough of Dunmore's faces by then to know it was the equivalent of another man's handshake.

We worked until two in the morning, heads together over the decoded entries, my fieldwork braided into their cipher-work, and somewhere around midnight I stopped thinking of it as an alliance and started thinking of it as what it actually was: two people who'd each decided, separately and at cost, that the truth was worth more than their seat at the table. Trust between strangers isn't built. It's discovered—both parties reaching into the same pocket at the same time and finding the same thing there.

Near the end, Dunmore pulled out a sheet I hadn't seen: a page of the cipher, nearly complete, with a column of initials and dates running down the margin. "There's a final key," they said. "The letters were written by someone who knows this family intimately—who knew Edmund's secrets, who knew Halbrook, who knows my habits well enough to photograph me at a foundry. I've narrowed it to three people inside the circle." They tapped the page. "Prentiss. Imogen. Or Marisol herself."

"And your money's on?"

Dunmore was quiet a moment. Outside, the wind had come around, and it carried the smell of the harbor—that low-tide smell of weed and rust and old wood, the smell of a town keeping its secrets underwater.

"The handwriting on the letters," they said slowly, "is careful. Practiced. The hand of someone who writes for a living but disguises it." They looked up at me. "Okafor, tomorrow I want you to do what you do best. Follow someone. Follow Prentiss. Everywhere. I want to know where the family solicitor goes when he thinks no one is watching."

I picked up my coat. "And if he leads us somewhere ugly?"

"Then," Dunmore said, "we'll finally know who's been writing to me—and whether they wanted ransom," and here their voice dropped, quiet and certain, "or rescue."
