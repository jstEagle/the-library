## 3. Entries After the End

The diary sat on Elodie's desk for two days before she could bring herself to open it again.

She had told herself she would return it. That was the rule—her rule, the one that had kept her employed and unremarkable at St. Aldhelm's for eleven years: touch nothing that isn't yours, notice nothing you can't prove, and above all, never let anyone see you noticing. The archive was a country with one citizen, and Elodie Vane was its quiet queen, and queens who meddled in police matters lost their thrones.

But the diary was not hers, and it was not the police's either, really. It belonged to a girl who had sung alto in the north transept every Thursday and Sunday, whose voice had a small crack on high notes that she covered by leaning into them, and who had vanished from the face of Wexcombe on the second Tuesday of October as completely as if the cathedral floor had opened and swallowed her.

Elodie made tea she did not drink. She turned the lamp low. And she opened the diary to the last entry she had read—the one dated the day before Maren Osgood disappeared—and kept turning.

The handwriting changed.

Not dramatically. Maren wrote in a slanted, hurried cursive, letters leaning like choristers straining toward the processional exit. But three pages past the disappearance date, the hand grew smaller, more deliberate, the loops disciplined, the crossbars of the t's drawn straight as ledger lines. Then it drifted back toward Maren's natural scrawl, then tightened again, as though two people were taking turns—or as though one person were trying very hard to be someone else.

*That's absurd,* Elodie told herself. *People's hands change when they're frightened.*

She read on.

The entries continued for eleven days after the disappearance. Eleven days. The police had closed their file in four.

***

**October 3rd.** *Woke up early. Couldn't sleep. Went down to the close before anyone else was up. The mist was sitting in the gutters like it paid rent there. I counted the gargoyles on the south aisle again—nineteen, still nineteen, still ugly. If I'm gone a while, somebody should know I liked it here. Even the ugly parts. Especially the ugly parts.*

October 3rd was the morning after she vanished. Elodie checked her own records: the verger's log showed the south door unlocked at 6:14 a.m., which meant someone had been in the close at dawn. Callum had noted "early mist, no visitors" in his round book. No visitors. Except, apparently, one.

**October 5th.** *Evensong was thin tonight. Mr. Fenwick rushed the psalm again—he always rushes when he's nervous, and he's been nervous all week. I sat in the back where nobody looks. I've gotten good at being where nobody looks. It's a skill. Aunt Prue would say it's a waste of a good voice.*

Mr. Fenwick. Elodie knew him—a retired schoolmaster who substituted at evensong when the regular organist was away, and the regular organist *had* been away the first week of October, in Bath, visiting his sister. She verified it against the music schedule pinned in the vestry. The detail was correct. The detail was *verifiable*. Whoever wrote this entry had stood in the nave on the evening of October 5th and heard a nervous man rush a psalm.

Maren had been gone two days by then.

**October 7th.** *Someone left flowers at the Halloway brass. White ones, cheap ones, the kind you buy at the petrol station. I don't know why that made me cry. I didn't even know her well. I just keep thinking about how the brass is worn smooth where people touch it, all those fingers over all those years, and now someone's touching it for me while I'm still here to see it.*

Elodie set the pen down—she'd been taking notes without deciding to, her own hand copying dates and details in her small, precise script—and pressed her palms flat against the desk until they stopped trembling.

The Halloway brass. She knew it intimately: a memorial plate set into the floor of the ambulatory, 1847, commemorating Edmund Halloway, wool merchant and benefactor, his wife Constance, and their daughter Margaret. The brass was worn to a soft golden blur at its lower edge where generations of visitors had touched it—for luck, for grief, for habit. And on October 8th, the cleaning rota—which Elodie herself maintained—recorded that fresh flower stems had been found beside the Halloway brass and removed before the 10 a.m. opening.

White flowers. Petrol-station cheap. Removed by Mrs. Pargeter with a note in her round hand: *"again?? who keeps leaving these."*

Again. As in, more than once.

Elodie got up and walked, without quite deciding to, down the archive stairs, across the cold flagstones of the ambulatory, to the Halloway brass. She knelt. The stone around it was dry—it hadn't rained in nine days—but the brass itself held a faint smear, a print of some kind, half an oblong, too large for a child's thumb, too small for a man's. Someone had touched it recently. Someone had touched it *after* Maren Osgood stopped existing.

*I'm still here to see it.*

Elodie knelt on the cold floor of St. Aldhelm's Cathedral and understood, with the slow terrible clarity of cold water rising, that the diary was not a relic. It was a message. It was a girl writing to whoever might someday find it, telling them exactly where to look, because she could not tell anyone out loud.

And the place she kept circling—the place the entries returned to like a tongue returning to a broken tooth—was the crypt.

***

**October 9th.** *I went down again. I know I shouldn't. The steps are older than everything else, older than the tower, older than the name of the town probably. It smells like iron and old rain down there. There's a vault with our name on it. OUR NAME. I didn't even know we had a vault. Why does my family have a vault in a crypt under a cathedral? What did we do? What did somebody do?*

**October 11th.** *Down again. I brought a light this time. The Osgood vault has a plate but the letters are nearly gone. Someone scratched marks next to it. Little marks, like tally strokes or birds' feet. I copied them into here so I don't lose them. If anything happens to me, look at the marks. LOOK AT THE MARKS.*

There, in the margin of the page, in that careful second hand: a short row of angular symbols, seven of them, arranged in two groups of three and one standing apart. They meant nothing to Elodie—not yet—but something in her hindbrain snagged on them the way a sleeve snags on a nail. She had seen shapes like these before. Recently. In brass.

She did not go down to the crypt. Not yet. Her rule held, barely, by its fingernails. Instead she spent the evening in the archive pulling the memorial brass rubbings—herself, meticulously, over six years—and laid them across the desk like a spread of playing cards. The Halloway brass. The Chedzey brass. The little-known plate for a sexton named Ambrose Pell, 1791. And there, along the lower border of the Pell plate, worn nearly to invisibility: a decorative band of what the Victorian antiquaries had called "masons' marks."

Birds' feet.

Elodie sat very still in the lamplight while the cathedral settled around her, timbers ticking, the great building breathing its long stone breath. Somewhere overhead a door closed—Callum locking up, probably, doing his rounds, faithful Callum who had worked beside her for a decade and never once asked her a personal question, which was why she loved him.

The diary said: *look at the marks.* The marks matched the brasses. The brasses included the Halloway family—the same Halloway family whose name was currently all over the local paper because Bishop Halloway's cousin was contesting an inheritance, because old Edmund Halloway's estate had finally, after generations, come up for probate, and the will everyone was quoting looked, to Elodie's archivist eye, suspiciously clean. Too clean. Documents that have passed through many hands get smudged. This one gleamed.

And Maren Osgood—an orphaned chorister with an aunt who was, if Elodie remembered the parish gossip correctly, a solicitor—had a family vault in the crypt, and had written *why does my family have a vault* eleven days after the world decided she had run away.

Elodie looked at the seventh symbol, the one standing apart from the other six, and felt the itch behind her eyes that always preceded a pattern. She hated the itch. The itch had cost her friendships, once, long ago, in another city, in another life where she had noticed things about a fire that the fire brigade preferred unnoticed. The itch was why she had come to St. Aldhelm's, why she had built her small invisible kingdom among the ledgers and the dust. You did not follow the itch. You fed it quietly, on safe things—misdated folios, mislabeled relics—and you never, ever followed it out of the building.

She looked at the diary.

*If anything happens to me.*

Eleven days of entries. Places Elodie could verify: the close at dawn, the evensong, the flowers, the psalm rushed by a nervous substitute organist. All true. All confirmed against independent record. Which meant the writer had been *here*, in the building, moving through spaces Elodie knew better than her own flat, while the constabulary filed paperwork saying otherwise.

One place Elodie could not verify: the crypt. Because the crypt was locked, and only four keys existed, and Elodie held one of them, and the key log—which she also maintained—showed no checkout since September.

Either the diary was a fabrication, a hoax of astonishing patience and local knowledge—

Or someone was living beneath the cathedral, writing in a dead girl's voice, leaving white flowers at a brass, scratching cipher-marks beside a vault, waiting for someone with eyes to notice.

Or—and this was the possibility that made Elodie's tea go cold untouched in its cup—the girl herself was down there. Alive. Writing *I'm still here* in the only way left to her.

***

She broke her rule at 9:40 on Thursday morning.

Not by going to the crypt—she wasn't ready for that, wasn't ready for what she might find or what finding it would obligate her to do—but by walking out of the cathedral entirely, crossing the close in full daylight where anyone might see her, and presenting herself at Wexcombe Police Station without an appointment, without a reason anyone would believe, and without, for the first time in eleven years, a workable plan for being overlooked.

The desk sergeant knew her vaguely—"the archive lady"—and rang through, and sounded genuinely surprised that Detective Inspector Sorrel agreed to see her. Elodie heard the surprise in his tone and understood it. Sorrel had a reputation. People said she was difficult. People said she didn't play well with the closure-happy machinery of the force. People said, in the tones people reserve for women who ask questions, that she *didn't know when to let things go.*

Elodie climbed the stairs thinking: *good.*

The office was small and aggressively tidy, the way offices are tidy when tidiness is a weapon. A corkboard held three photographs connected by red string, which struck Elodie as almost endearingly literal. DI Sorrel sat behind the desk with the stillness of someone who had learned that stillness unnerves people more than aggression does. She was perhaps fifty, grey-shot hair cut short as a hedge, eyes the color of wet slate.

"Miss Vane." She did not offer to shake hands. "You're the first person to walk into my office about Maren Osgood in nineteen days. Sit down."

"I don't have an appointment."

"I noticed. I'm enjoying it." Sorrel leaned back. "Say your piece."

And Elodie, who had rehearsed nothing, who had walked across the close with her heart hammering like a novice bell-ringer's, discovered that she had no piece to say—or rather, that every version of her piece ended with *and I have a missing girl's diary hidden in my archive, which makes me either a witness or an accessory,* and she could not make her mouth form those words. Not yet. Not to a stranger. Not until she understood what the marks on the brasses meant, and what the Osgood vault held, and whether the hand in the diary belonged to a living girl or a very clever ghost.

So instead she said: "The diary. Maren's diary. I understand you recovered her effects from the choir loft."

"We recovered a satchel. Books, pencil case, a hymnal with her name in it. No diary." Sorrel's slate eyes sharpened by degrees, like weather turning. "Why do you ask?"

"Because I catalogued the loft shelving in August," Elodie said, which was true, "and I have an eye for displaced objects," which was also true, "and I believe a diary existed," which was truest of all, and a lie in its implications, and she felt it go through her like a swallowed stone.

Silence. Outside, a gull argued with the roof of the station.

"You believe," Sorrel repeated.

"The loft is dusty," Elodie said. "Dust remembers. There was a rectangular absence on the shelf beside her hymnal, and the absence was the right size, and the shelf below it had a scratch that looked recent, as though something heavy had been dragged off and put back carelessly. Someone took a book from that loft after the police finished searching it. Or someone put one back."

It was, Elodie reflected later, the most reckless sentence she had ever spoken: technically true, strategically insane, and delivered in the flat archival monotone that made people assume she was reciting facts rather than constructing a trapdoor beneath herself.

Sorrel studied her for a long moment. Then she reached into her desk drawer, produced a card, and wrote something on the back of it.

"My direct line," she said. "Not the station line. When you find that diary—and I've met archivists, Miss Vane, and you have the look of a woman who finds things—you'll call me. Same day. Before you do anything clever."

"And if I don't?"

"Then I'll start wondering why the cathedral's most invisible employee suddenly developed a conscience." Sorrel slid the card across the desk. "Invisible people are useful, you know. Nobody watches them. Doors open for them. Files get left where they can be read." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "I imagine it's tempting."

Elodie took the card. Her hand, she was pleased to note, did not shake.

"Detective Inspector," she said at the door, "the three accidents this autumn—the scaffolder, the churchwarden, the bell-hanger. Did any of the reports mention wet shoes?"

For the first time, Sorrel went still in a different way. Not the weaponized stillness of before. The stillness of a woman hearing a sound in an empty house.

"Why do you ask?"

"Because it hasn't rained on any of the nights in question," Elodie said, "and I catalogued the coroner's photographs for the inquest library, and all three of them are wearing wet shoes. On dry nights. In buildings with dry floors." She let the silence sit for exactly two seconds. "Dust remembers, Detective Inspector. So do I."

She left before Sorrel could answer, descended the station stairs into the grey Wexcombe morning, and stood for a moment on the pavement with the card in her pocket and her rule lying somewhere behind her, broken on the floor of a police office, irretrievable.

Behind her, through the propped door, she heard Sorrel pick up her phone and dial an internal extension, and heard her say, in a voice stripped of all its armor:

"Pull the files on the scaffolder, the churchwarden, and the bell-hanger. Yes, again. And find out everything we have on a girl named Maren Osgood—because I think somebody's been writing in her diary since the day she died."

*Since the day she died.*

Elodie walked back toward the cathedral with the words ringing in her skull like an unsilenced bell, and thought, with dread and something adjacent to hope: *No. You're wrong. That's the one thing you're wrong about.*

Because the diary was still in her archive bag, pressed between two folios of seventeenth-century deeds, and the last entry—the eleventh-day entry, the entry dated four days ago—had ended with a single line, in that careful second hand, in ink still faintly glossy:

*Whoever you are, you found it. Come down. Bring bread. And don't tell the policeman.*
