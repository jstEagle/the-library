## 5. Brasses and Buried Names

The reading room of the archive was Elodie's cathedral within the cathedral. Above her, the great church performed its daily theater—bells, tourists, the dry rustle of the vergers' brooms—but down here, in the lamplit quiet beneath the north cloister, the only congregation was paper. She had arranged Maren Osgood's diary on a foam cradle, weighted open with lead snakes, and she had not touched it for an hour. She was looking at the margins.

The marginal marks had seemed, at first, like the idle pen-strokes of a bored chorister: little hooks and dashes in the gutter of the pages, clusters of them, sometimes three, sometimes five, always small enough to be mistaken for smudges. But Maren had been a careful girl. Elodie had learned that much in the weeks since the disappearance—careful in her breath marks, careful in her ledger of vestments, careful in the way she had written her diary in two hands and never once explained why. Careful girls did not smudge. Careful girls *encoded*.

Elodie copied the marks onto a fresh sheet, in order, as they appeared through the diary: forty-one clusters in all. Then she did what she always did when a pattern refused to speak. She went upstairs to ask the dead.

* * *

The memorial brasses of St. Aldhelm's ran along the aisles like a ledger laid into the floor. Brass did not photograph well and did not wear well, and so the tourists walked over them without seeing, their trainers squeaking on the effigies of men and women who had bought their permanence in guineas. Elodie knew them the way other people knew the faces of their neighbors. There was Aldhelm himself, fourteenth-century, hands raised in blessing, the Latin worn to a shine at his feet where six centuries of processions had polished him. There was the Halloway brass—Bishop Edmund Halloway, 1841, kneeling beside his wife, the inscription in a black-letter hand so fine that Elodie kept a rubbing of it framed above her desk.

And there, third from the choir screen on the south side, was a smaller brass, a woman alone, no effigy, only a plate and a line of text: *MARGARET ASHLEY, BENEFACTRESS, OBIIT 1893, CUIUS ANIMA REQVIESCAT.* Margaret Ashley. Elodie's mother's maiden name had been Ashley. It was a common enough name in the county; Elodie had walked over that brass ten thousand times and thought nothing of it, the way one thinks nothing of one's own reflection in a shop window.

Tonight she knelt on it with a notebook and a raking lamp, and thought a great deal.

The cipher, if it was a cipher, had to be keyed to something. Diaries did not come with codebooks, but churches did. Every chorister learned the brasses the way they learned the psalms—by walking them, by singing the founders' names on the anniversary of their gifts. Maren had walked these aisles twice a day for four years. If a girl wanted to hide something in a cathedral, she would hide it in the one text no one read and everyone stepped on.

Elodie worked by elimination, which was the only honest method. The clusters of marks ran three to five symbols. The brass inscriptions ran long. But names—surnames alone—ran three to five letters. She began to map each cluster against the nearest brass to the position Maren had marked in her diary, and the diary, she realized with a small cold shock, was positional. The entries weren't dated only by day; the marginal marks sat in the margin at heights that varied—high, middle, low—and Elodie had spent enough years among architects' plans to read a nave elevation when she saw one. High: the triforium line. Middle: the arcade. Low: the aisle floor. Maren had been recording *where*.

The first cluster resolved at a quarter past midnight.

The marks were three: a hook, a dash, a cross. The brass nearest the position—south aisle, low, third bay—was *WILFRIC STANNARD, CHURCHWARDEN, 1877*. Wilfric. Six letters. But the *surname*—she counted the letters against the marks, and the marks were not letters at all, they were strokes, and the strokes were counts. Hook: three. Dash: one. Cross: four. *S-T-A-N-N-A-R-D* had eight letters, but *STANNARD* keyed against the alphabet by stroke-count gave her—she tried it three ways before the third way held—*S(3), T(1)... no.* She sat back on her heels. The lamp hummed. Somewhere above, a door boomed shut.

It was the donors' list that broke it open. In the archive there was a bound volume, *Catalogue of Benefactions, 1820–1960*, which Elodie had catalogued herself and which no living soul had consulted since. It listed, for each donor, not only the gift but the *form of the name as subscribed in the deed*—and deeds, unlike brasses, abbreviated. *W. Stannard. The Misses Peverel. Halloway, Edm.* Three to five characters, every one. Elodie laid the benefactions list beside the copied marks, and the marks lay down beside it like tired dogs, one after another after another, and the diary stopped being a diary and became a ledger of visits: names, positions in the building, and—when she cross-checked the dates against the sexton's interment register and the works ledger—dates. Maren had been recording meetings. Meetings in the aisles, in the triforium, at the brasses. Meetings with the people whose names she had written in a second hand.

The second hand. Elodie had spent three chapters of her own private investigation telling herself the second hand was Maren practicing calligraphy, or copying a favorite author, or any of the hundred harmless things a careful girl might do. Now she looked at the names the second hand had written—*Stannard. Peverel. Halloway.*—and understood that Maren had not been practicing anything. She had been keeping a second record, in another person's writing, of the people who had signed their names to something. A girl who witnessed a signature remembers it. A girl who fears the signature copies it, so that she will know it again—so that she can prove, later, that the hand on the document was never hers.

*The Halloway inheritance.* The aunt, the solicitor, the pressing to declare a dead girl dead. Elodie had thought the diary was a cry for help. It was worse. It was *evidence*.

* * *

She did not go home. She made tea in the archive's corner kettle and worked until the windows went from black to the color of wet slate, and when the verger's key turned in the outer door at six—Callum, early as always, whistling something from the morning psalm—she had the whole of it laid out on three tables like a surgery.

Forty-one clusters. Thirty-eight resolved cleanly to names in the benefactions list. The remaining three were damaged—Maren's marks smeared, one page torn—and Elodie flagged them rather than force them. Forcing a cipher was how you made it say what you wanted. She had a rule about that. She had rules about most things, and the rules had kept her invisible and safe for forty years, and she was about to break the largest of them, because the thirty-ninth cluster would not resolve, and would not resolve, and then resolved all at once, horribly, the way a face resolves in a crowd.

The position was south aisle, low, third bay. The name was *Ashley, Margt.* The benefactions list gave the subscription in full: *Ashley, Margt., of this city, widow, for the glazing of the south aisle, £40, 1891.* The diary entry it keyed to was dated nine days before Maren vanished, and in the second hand, in the body of the entry, in Maren's careful imitation of another person's writing, were the words: *the widow's name was put in after. She said the old lady never signed it. She said the old lady was dead before the deed was drawn.*

Elodie read it four times. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Her own reflection hung faint in the dark window, a woman bent over tables of paper, and she had the sudden vertiginous sense that the reflection was the true one and the woman was the copy.

Her mother. Margaret Ashley, of this city, widow. Elodie's mother had died eleven years ago in a care home outside Wells, having told her daughter, in forty years of Sunday phone calls and folded letters and one shared flat in the close, precisely nothing about a bequest to a cathedral. Elodie had catalogued her mother's effects herself—she had catalogued *everything*, it was what she was *for*—and there had been no deeds, no glazing, no forty pounds, nothing but a biscuit tin of ration books and a photograph of a man Elodie had never met. And yet here was her mother's name, subscribed to a benefaction in 1891, when her mother had been born in 1931.

The arithmetic was so simple it was almost gentle. It was not her mother. It was her mother's name—the name, the *Ashley*, the widow, the woman alone—reused. Names were reused; Elodie knew that better than anyone; a name was the cheapest thing in the world to forge because it was the only thing the dead could not defend. But the diary entry said *the old lady never signed it*, and the second hand belonged to someone who had been in the room when a name was put in after, and the brass under which Maren had crouched to record it was a brass that said *MARGARET ASHLEY, BENEFACTRESS*, and the cipher that Maren had built out of donors' names had been built, in part, out of Elodie's own inheritance—the name she had walked over ten thousand times without seeing, the name she had signed to library slips and delivery notes for forty years, the name that had made her invisible precisely because it was so ordinary.

*The past reaching up through the floorboards.* That was the phrase that came to her, uninvited, and she let it come, because it was exact. The brasses were the floor. Her mother's name was in the floor. And a girl had knelt on that floor with a pencil and written down the sound of it being stolen.

Elodie's hands were not shaking. She noted that with a kind of clinical interest, the way she noted water damage and foxing. Her hands were very still, and very cold, and she understood that this was what fury looked like in a person who had spent her life being careful. It did not shake. It *catalogued*.

* * *

Callum found her at half seven, standing in the south aisle with her raking lamp, looking down at the brass.

"You're here early," he said. "Or late. I can never tell with you, El." He was carrying two paper cups of tea from the refectory machine, and he handed her one without asking, because he had been handing her things without asking for nineteen years, since they were both young and underpaid and the cathedral was the only place either of them fit. "Bad night?"

"I was working."

"You're always working." He followed her gaze down. "Margaret Ashley. Funny. I've always liked that one. Little brass, big story, they used to say. There's a tale the glazing money never came." He smiled his easy smile, the one that made the tourists trust him with their coats. "Old dead people and their money. It's all this cathedral is, when you get down to it."

"Yes," Elodie said. "It is."

She heard her own voice and was alarmed by it—flat, and hard, and carrying. Callum looked at her properly then, and for a moment something moved behind his friendliness, some small animal of attention, and she thought: *he knows I've changed. He can tell. Nineteen years and he can tell in one sentence.* She made herself smile. It cost more than any lie she had ever told, and she had not yet told any.

"Get some sleep, El," he said gently, and went off down the aisle whistling, his keys singing at his hip, and Elodie stood over her mother's stolen name and listened to him fade into the nave like water into sand.

* * *

By nine she had done what she should have done at midnight, which was to stop looking at the names and start looking at the gaps.

Thirty-eight resolved clusters. But a cipher keyed to a donors' list was only as complete as the list, and Elodie had catalogued the benefactions volume herself, and she knew—she knew the way she knew the number of steps in the night stair—that the volume was incomplete. There was a second register. There was always a second register. The Chapter's *Liber Benefactorum*, the great medieval book, chained (formerly chained) to a desk in the treasury, with its illuminated initials and its gaps where pages had been cut out. Elodie had reported the cut pages in her first year, in writing, to the Chapter, and had received in reply a memo thanking her for her vigilance and noting that the losses predated the current administration. She had filed the memo. She had thought about it, over the years, perhaps twice.

She thought about it now.

Three clusters had refused to resolve against the printed list. She carried her copied marks through the waking cathedral—past the vergers setting out chairs, past the flower team and their buckets, past the boy on the hoover who nodded at her without seeing her, because no one ever saw her, and this morning, for the first time in her life, the invisibility felt less like a coat than like a wound—down into the treasury, and she took the *Liber Benefactorum* off its shelf, and she turned to the section for the 1890s, and she looked at the stubs where pages had been cut.

Three stubs. Three unresolved clusters. The cut pages would have held the subscriptions—the *original* subscriptions, in the donors' own hands, the very thing Maren's second-hand entries were built to compare against. Someone had cut them out. Someone had cut them out decades ago, or someone had cut them out recently and the memo had lied, and either way the pattern was the pattern she had spent her life refusing to see: things in this cathedral did not get lost. Things in this cathedral got *removed*, by people with keys, and the removals were always tidier than accidents, and the accidents were always tidier than murders, and three people had fallen on dry nights in dry shoes and everyone had signed the paperwork and gone home.

And one of the cut names—the one Maren had crouched over the brass to record, the one written in the second hand beside the words *the old lady was dead before the deed was drawn*—was *Ashley*.

Not her mother. Her mother's name. Which meant the name had been used *before*, in 1891, in a forgery old enough to have its own brass—and had been used *again*, recently, in the Halloway papers, because the forger had gone looking for a dead widow named Ashley and found one that fit, found one with a reclusive daughter who never read the newspapers and never opened her post and would never, ever notice. Her mother's name had been *chosen*. Out of a ledger. Out of the floor. The past had not reached up through the floorboards; it had been *sent* up, deliberately, by someone who knew the brasses as well as she did, who knew this building the way she did, who had walked these aisles at midnight with a lamp—

Elodie stood very still in the treasury with the great book open in her arms, and the morning light came through the high window and lay across the cut stubs like a bandage laid over a wound, and she thought of the one person besides herself who had known the benefactions list by heart, who had access after hours, who had been asking old Tom about her, who had smiled at her seven minutes ago and handed her tea.

No, she thought. Not Callum. Anyone but Callum.

But the thought had been thought, and it did not unthink itself, and she closed the *Liber* with a sound like a door.

She went back to the archive and took out the diary, and turned to the last resolved entry—the one dated two days before Maren vanished—and read, in Maren's own hand this time, the sentence she had read a dozen times and never understood: *If I'm wrong about the brass, then I'm only a silly girl who reads too much. But if I'm right, then the fourth name on the list is the one who'll come for me, and it's someone who knows where everyone sleeps.*

Elodie looked at the three unresolved clusters. Then she looked at her copied marks, and at the third one—the smeared one, the damaged one—and for the first time she let herself try to force it, rules be damned, and the strokes came apart under her pencil and reassembled, and the name they made was short, and ordinary, and signed to benefactions in three different centuries.

It was the name of someone still alive. It was a name she had said aloud, warm and unguarded, not an hour ago, over tea.

The archive door, at the top of the stair, clicked shut.
