## 7. A Death That Fits Too Well

Detective Inspector Sorrel had learned, over nineteen years of policing the city of St. Aldhelm's, that death kept office hours in this town. It preferred the hours when no one was watching—early morning, late evening, the long gray trough of a Sunday afternoon. So when the call came at 6:47 on a Thursday evening, just as the cathedral bells were ringing for evensong, she knew before she answered that it would be bad.

"Rood screen gallery," said Sergeant Ibbetson, his voice pitched low as though the stones themselves might overhear. "St. Aldhelm's. A woman's fallen. She's—she didn't make it, ma'am."

Sorrel was already reaching for her coat. "Who?"

"The name given is Osgood. Vivian Osgood."

The name landed in Sorrel's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She stood very still in her narrow hallway, one arm half into her coat sleeve, and listened to the bells rolling out over the rooftops—the same bells, she would think later, that had been ringing when Maren Osgood vanished six weeks ago.

"I'm ten minutes out," she said. "Don't let anyone move anything. And Ibbetson—find out who was in that gallery tonight. Everyone. Choir, vergers, cleaners, tourists. Everyone."

* * *

The cathedral at night was a different country. By day it belonged to tourists and worshippers, to the shuffling queues and the candle-smell and the echo of organ music; by night it belonged to the stone itself, to the cold breath rising from the crypt grates, to shadows that pooled in the transepts like standing water. Sorrel had been inside perhaps a dozen times—a funeral for a colleague's mother once, a carol service she'd left early—and each time she felt the building take her measure and find her wanting. Tonight it did not even bother to pretend welcome. Tonight it simply watched.

Vivian Osgood lay at the base of the rood screen on the north side, beneath the great painted beam where the crucifixion figures hung forty feet above. The paramedics had covered her; Sorrel asked them to wait while she looked, and then made herself look properly, because the dead deserved that much and because looking properly was the whole of her trade.

A woman in her fifties, smartly dressed in a charcoal suit that had not survived the fall with its dignity intact. One shoe off, lying a yard away. Her glasses had folded under her. There was blood, but less than Sorrel expected, and the angle of the body told its own quiet story: she had come over the gallery rail feet-first, or nearly so, and the stone floor had done the arithmetic.

"She was up there for the view," said the verger who'd found her—a young man, pale as tallow, his name tag read CALLUM. He kept wiping his hands on his trousers though they were clean. "We do open the triforium and gallery access some evenings, for the tower tours. She signed the visitors' book. We've got it. But nobody saw her go up. Evensong was starting, everyone's at their posts, and—" He stopped, swallowed. "She must have leaned. The rail's old. It's been flagged for repair twice."

"Twice," Sorrel said. "Flagged by whom?"

"By us. The fabric committee. It's all documented." He said *documented* the way other people said *help me*, and Sorrel noted it without mercy: this was a man rehearsing a story he hoped was true.

She left him to the paramedics' kindness and climbed the narrow spiral stair to the rood screen gallery herself, her torch beam skating up walls that had heard nine hundred years of prayers. At the top, the gallery ran along behind the screen's carved tracery, a walkway barely four feet wide, the rail waist-high and worn smooth by centuries of hands. From here the nave opened below like the inside of a bell—vast, hollow, resonant. A person could lean over that rail easily. A person could be leaned.

The visitors' book sat on a small lectern by the stair head. Sorrel photographed every page, then turned back through the recent entries with gloved fingers. Vivian Osgood, 6:12 p.m., printed neatly, address in Cheltenham. No other entries that evening. The page before was three days old.

Below the lectern, wedged in the gap between it and the rail post, someone had tucked a folded sheet of paper—or dropped it, or hidden it. Sorrel eased it out with a pencil. A printed email, single page, from an account she didn't recognize, addressed to V. Osgood. Most of the text was legal boilerplate about estate administration, but one line had been underlined twice in ballpoint:

***Before the declaration is filed, we should agree what is to be said about the girl.***

Sorrel read it four times. Around her the cathedral breathed its cold stone breath, and somewhere far below, doors closed, voices murmured, the machinery of a death investigation began to turn. She put the page in an evidence bag and thought: *the girl.* Maren Osgood, seventeen years old, missing since September, written off by half the force as a runaway. The aunt who wanted her declared dead quickly. The aunt who was now dead herself, having fallen from a height on a dry evening.

Sorrel stood at the rail and looked down at the covered shape on the nave floor, and let herself think the thought she was paid to think and hated thinking: *People don't fall from rood screens. They are helped.*

* * *

The incident room that night smelled of burnt coffee and wet wool. Sorrel pinned photographs to the board in the order that felt truest rather than the order that was tidy: Maren Osgood, school photo, gap-toothed grin. Vivian Osgood, pulled from the firm's website, smiling the careful smile of a solicitor who billed by the quarter-hour. The rood screen. The gallery rail. The email.

"Coroner'll want accident or suicide," Ibbetson said, coming up beside her with two coffees and offering her the less burnt one. "Falling from a height, middle-aged woman, family troubles—she'll get one of the two easy verdicts and we'll all go home."

"Her handbag was in the vestry," Sorrel said. "Locked. She asked the verger to mind it before going up. Who asks to have their handbag minded before they lean over a rail?"

"Someone careful."

"Someone expecting company," Sorrel corrected. "You don't safeguard your bag for a solo fall. You safeguard it so it can't be searched if you don't come down." She took the coffee. "And she wasn't suicidal. I've spent the day with her life. New coat, dental appointment Tuesday, a holiday deposit on a cottage in Wales, non-refundable. People planning to die don't put deposits on cottages."

"So it's murder."

"It's murder," Sorrel said, "and it's the fourth one, and I'm tired of being the only person in this building who can count."

Ibbetson didn't answer right away. He turned his coffee cup in his hands, and when he spoke it was with the gentleness he saved for things he thought might break her. "Guv. The three you keep flagging. The stonemason, the archivist's assistant, the bell-hanger. They're closed. The super signed off. You wrote your objections and they went into a drawer."

"They all died within sight of this cathedral," Sorrel said. "They all died on dry nights. And here's the detail nobody wants to hold onto, Ibbetson, so hold it for me: the mason was found with wet shoes. The bell-hanger too. Wet shoes, both of them, on nights when it hadn't rained for days. The pathologist shrugged—dew, condensation, who knows. But dew doesn't soak through to leave tide-marks on laces. Water does. Someone washed something off. Someone walked through water, or cleaned something with it, and then died, and everyone agreed it was an accident because agreeing was easier."

"And tonight?"

"No rain today either," Sorrel said quietly. "Check her shoes anyway. Check everything. If there's damp in the seams, I want to know before the coroner does."

He went. She stayed at the board a long time after the room emptied, the photographs watching her with their paper patience, and it was there, alone, that she finally opened Vivian Osgood's appointment book—recovered from the locked handbag, logged, photographed, and now, at midnight, hers to read.

Most of it was the ordinary sediment of a solicitor's life: client initials, court dates, a reminder about the Welsh cottage. But the last three weeks carried a rhythm, and rhythms were what Sorrel lived by. Three entries marked only with a small drawn key. Two entries marked with a letter she couldn't parse—an ornate *H*, looped like a bishop's crozier. And on the final page, dated the morning of her death, a single line:

**E.V. — archive — 4 p.m.**

No surname. No phone number. Just those initials, and the word *archive*, and a time that meant nothing until Sorrel remembered, with the small cold click of a lock turning, that St. Aldhelm's had an archivist. A woman. Reclusive, according to the parish secretary she'd phoned at one in the morning, apologetic but firm. *Miss Vane keeps to herself. Wonderful with the records. She wouldn't hurt a fly.*

Everyone always said that about someone.

* * *

Sorrel slept badly and arrived at the cathedral at eight the next morning, before the doors opened, and stood across the street in the thin gold light watching the staff arrive. She watched the young verger Callum unlock the west door and stand aside to let the cleaning women pass, touching each of their shoulders briefly, a small ritual of courtesy. She watched an old man in a canvas jacket—the steeplekeeper, someone had said—cross the close with the unhurried gait of a man who had walked it ten thousand times. And she watched, last, a slight figure in a gray cardigan come down the lane from the archivist's lodging, carrying a leather satchel, walking with her head slightly bowed as though apologizing to the pavement for existing.

Elodie Vane. E.V.

Sorrel crossed the street.

Up close the archivist was smaller than she'd seemed at distance, and younger than her stoop suggested—thirty-something, sharp-boned, with the translucent look of someone who worked by lamplight. Her eyes, when they lifted to Sorrel's warrant card, were the color of strong tea and entirely unafraid, which was itself interesting. Most people flinched at the warrant card. This woman examined it the way she probably examined everything: closely, silently, cataloguing.

"Detective Inspector Sorrel," Sorrel said. "I need to ask you about Vivian Osgood."

Something moved behind the tea-colored eyes—not guilt, Sorrel decided, though she kept the possibility open. Recognition. As though a shape long suspected had finally been named.

"You'd better come inside," Elodie Vane said. "Not my office. The reading room has better light, and better light makes for fewer lies."

It was such an odd thing to say that Sorrel almost smiled. She followed the archivist through cloisters that smelled of cold incense and old paper, past a door marked ARCHIVE that the woman unlocked with a key she wore on a chain around her neck like a relic, into a small high-windowed room where the morning light fell in clean bars across a scarred oak table.

"I never met her," Elodie said, before Sorrel could arrange her first question. "Vivian Osgood. I want that understood first, because it's true, and because you'll test it against everything else I say, and it should hold."

"But she had an appointment with you. Yesterday, four o'clock. In your archive."

The archivist's hands went still on the table. Not frozen—still, deliberately still, the stillness of someone choosing their next words with a jeweler's care. "I don't know anything about an appointment."

"It's in her book. Initials E.V., the word archive, four p.m., yesterday's date." Sorrel laid a photograph of the page on the table between them and watched the woman read it. Watched her lips part slightly. Watched the color leave her face in a slow tide, and return.

"That isn't mine," Elodie said at last. "I mean—the initials are mine. But I didn't write it, and I never agreed to see her." She looked up, and now there was something raw in her face, something that had been held carefully behind glass and was suddenly cracked. "Inspector, how well did you know Maren Osgood?"

The question came from nowhere and everywhere. Sorrel kept her voice level. "Missing persons isn't my case. Why?"

"Because I have her diary," Elodie said, "and it kept being written in after she disappeared, and I think whoever killed her aunt yesterday may have killed her too, and I think it started thirty years ago with a death in this building that everyone agreed to call an accident, and I think—" She stopped. Drew a breath. Started again, quieter, as though stepping back from a ledge. "I think I should show you rather than tell you. But I need to know something first."

"What?"

"Whether you're the kind of detective who closes cases," Elodie Vane said, "or the kind who opens them. Because I've spent my whole life among people who prefer things shut, Inspector, and I can tell within a minute, and yours is the only opinion in this city I currently need."

Sorrel sat back. Through the high window the light had shifted, laying a bright bar across the table between them, and beyond the glass she could see the tower rising into a sky scoured clean by wind—the tower where the bells rang, the gallery where Vivian Osgood had fallen, the crypt below whose grates breathed their cold air into the nave. A building full of locked rooms. A city full of closed cases. Nineteen years of watching this town agree with itself that terrible things were accidents.

Three deaths with wet shoes on dry nights. A fourth that fit too well. An appointment book with her own initials forged in it—which meant someone wanted the police looking at Elodie Vane, or wanted Vivian Osgood to believe she had a friend in the archive, or both.

"I reopened three cases last month that my superintendent had signed off," Sorrel said. "I'm not supposed to have. If it goes wrong, I lose more than my pension." She met the archivist's eyes. "So you'll understand when I say I don't trust easily either. Show me the diary."

For a moment Elodie simply looked at her, and Sorrel had the strange sensation of being weighed, measured, and—for the first time in years, perhaps for the first time ever—found sufficient.

Then the archivist rose, unlocked the chain at her neck, and reached for the key.

But before her fingers touched it, footsteps sounded in the cloister outside—quick, deliberate, stopping just short of the reading-room door. A shadow crossed the frosted glass panel. And a voice, smooth and warm and unmistakably accustomed to obedience, called through the wood:

"Miss Vane? Forgive the intrusion. Bishop Halloway wonders whether you might spare him a few minutes this morning—he's anxious to discuss the Osgood estate papers. He says you'll know why."

Elodie's hand closed around the key, and Sorrel saw her knuckles whiten, and understood in the same instant that the archivist did: whatever the diary held, the hunt had already begun—and it had just knocked politely on their door.
