## 12. Tom Knows More Than He Says

The bread was gone again.

Elodie stood in the cold breath of the crypt at half past six in the morning, staring at the stone shelf behind the Osgood vault where she had left a heel of brown loaf and a wedge of cheese the night before. Both were gone. In their place, folded with an exactness that made her chest ache, lay a square of paper — not torn from anything so careless as a notebook but cut cleanly from the endpapers of an old book, cream and foxed at the edges. On it, in pencil, in a hand she now knew as well as her own mother's: *Thank you. The cheese was too kind. Please stop.*

Please stop.

She read it four times. The words did not change. The crypt did not change either — the same green dark, the same smell of limestone and candle smoke and centuries, the same bones sleeping in their niches like a congregation that had heard one sermon too many. But something in Elodie shifted, some load-bearing assumption giving way with a sound only she could hear.

*Please stop.* Not *who are you*. Not *help me*. A request for continued silence, wrapped in courtesy. Whoever lived down here did not want rescue. They wanted to be left alone, which meant they had a reason, which meant they were afraid of precisely the thing Elodie had been planning to offer them: the light.

She climbed the crypt stairs slowly, the paper pressed flat between her palms like a moth she was afraid to crush.

* * *

Tom Pargeter was in the bell chamber, which was where Tom Pargeter always was when the world became too much for him, which was often. He sat on the upturned crate he'd claimed sometime during the reign of a previous dean, his back against the wall beneath the great tenor bell, mending a stay rope with fingers that moved on their own while his eyes looked at nothing. He was seventy-eight years old and had kept this tower through five ringers' associations, two lightning strikes, and one scandal involving the curate that everyone agreed never to mention. His hands were enormous, cracked like drought earth, gentle as a christening.

He didn't look up when Elodie's head appeared through the trapdoor. "You've got rope burn on your conscience," he said. "I can always tell."

"Can you."

"It's in how you hold your wrists." He bit the twine, tied it off, inspected his work. "Come up properly or go away, girl. I'm too old to talk to a floating head."

Elodie came up into the chamber. Morning light came through the louvres in dusty blades, laying gold across the sacking bags of bell-hangers' sand, the coiled ropes hanging slack in their long gallery like a row of hanged men waiting for Sunday. She loved this room. She loved it the way you love a place where you have never once had to explain yourself. Tom had never asked her why she spent her life below ground among the dead; she had never asked him why he spent his above it among bells. It was, she understood now, the same vocation. Keeping things. Keeping them safe. Keeping them quiet.

"Someone's been living in the crypt," she said.

The twine went still in Tom's hands. Just for a moment — a pause no longer than the space between two strokes of a bell — and then it resumed, steady as ever. But Elodie had spent thirty years reading silences the way other people read print, and that pause was a whole paragraph.

"That so," Tom said.

"You knew."

"I knew nothing." He set down the rope and finally looked at her, and his eyes under their white thatch were the pale grey of weathered lead. "I fed somebody, is what I did. Whether they're living there or just passing through hungry, that's their business and God's."

"Tom."

"Don't 'Tom' me in that archivist voice. I've buried better interrogators than you." But there was no bite in it. There never was. He sighed, a sound like wind moving through an empty tower, and patted the crate beside him. "Sit down, then. You're going to stand there looking at me with those eyes until I tell you everything anyway, so we may as well be comfortable."

She sat. The crate was cold through her skirt. Above them the tenor bell hung in its darkness, three tons of bronze cast before anyone alive had been born, and Elodie felt suddenly very small beneath it, very young, though she was forty-one years old and had catalogued eleven thousand documents and buried her mother alone.

"Who is it?" she asked quietly. "Is it Maren?"

Tom's hands stopped entirely this time.

* * *

It took him a long while to answer, and when he did, he didn't answer the question. He answered a different one, older, the way old men do when the truth has too many doors and they must choose which to open first.

"You know I was friends with Walter Osgood," he said. "Maren's grandfather. Forty years we rang together, him and me. He taught me Grandsire Triples when my own father wouldn't give me the time of day. When his lungs went, I sat with him every Thursday at the hospital. Four months of Thursdays." Tom's jaw worked. "Last Thursday, he wasn't making sense most of the time. But near the end he came clear as a bell, and he grabbed my wrist with what he had left, and he said: *Tom. If ever a child of mine comes to you hungry and afraid, you feed them and you ask nothing. Swear it on the bells.*"

"And you swore."

"I swore on the bells." He said it the way other men said *on my mother's grave*, and Elodie understood that for Tom the comparison ran the other direction — the bells came first, had always come first, would come last. "Walter's dead these three years. But a swear doesn't die with the man who hears it. That's the whole point of a swear."

"So you've been feeding someone in the crypt since—"

"Since November." He rubbed his face with both hands, and Elodie saw how the skin hung loose over the bones, how old he actually was, how tired. "Leftovers from the refectory, mostly. Bread. Sometimes Mrs. Chandos's soup when she makes too much, which is never, so sometimes I buy it myself and pretend. Candles — I keep candles down there, good ones, because it's black as a pocket if you don't. And a blanket off my own bed in January, before you say anything, because I could hear her shivering through the vault door and I'm not made of stone, whatever Callum tells the new ringers."

"*Her,*" Elodie repeated. Her heart was beating somewhere up near her collarbone. "You said *her*."

Tom closed his eyes. "I'm a bad liar and always have been. It's why I stayed a steeplekeeper instead of going into politics like my sister wanted."

"Tom." Elodie reached out and put her hand over his great cracked fist, and felt it trembling under hers like a bird deciding whether to stay. "Look at me. Three people have died in this cathedral in eight months, and every one of them was ruled an accident, and every one of them had wet shoes on a dry night, and I think whoever killed them thinks there's a witness buried in my crypt. If Maren Osgood is alive down there, she isn't safe. Being hidden isn't the same as being safe. You of all people should know what happens to things in this building that nobody checks on."

"She's not stupid," Tom said. "She's the cleverest of Walter's lot, and Walter was clever enough to frighten people who thought they were cleverer."

"Clever people die too. I catalogue their deaths."

For a moment neither of them spoke. Somewhere far above, a jackdaw complained against the louvres. The light blades had crawled across the floor toward Tom's boots.

Then Tom said, heavily, "How much do you know? Truly. Not guessing. Knowing."

And because lying to Tom Pargeter would have been like lying to the tower itself — possible, perhaps, but it would stand around you forever afterward — Elodie told him. The diary with its two hands. The entries past the end. The cipher in the memorial brasses, keyed to donors' names, spelling out a list. Her mother's name among the beneficiaries of an estate her mother had never once mentioned across twenty-nine years of careful, silent life. The aunt who had fallen from the rood screen gallery with a solicitor's appointment book carrying Elodie's own name in it. The detective inspector who believed her, more or less, and who was pulling threads Sorrel-style, patiently, from the wrong end.

When she finished, Tom was quiet for a long time.

"Your mother," he said at last. "Margaret Vane. Quiet woman. Used to sit in the north transept every Wednesday evening, never took communion, never spoke to anyone. I used to wonder about her." He shook his head slowly. "So it runs in families, then. Whatever it is — the seeing, the hiding."

"It appears to."

"And now you want to go down there and drag that girl into the light because it's the right thing." Tom turned his hand over under hers and gripped her fingers, surprisingly strong. "And I'm asking you not to. Not yet. Because I gave my word to a dying man, and because—" his voice caught, and he steadied it the way he'd steady a bell that swung a hair too hard, "—because she trusts me, Elodie. She's got nothing else. No family left that isn't trying to declare her dead and take the money. No name she can use. Nothing but an old fool and a blanket. And if I break that trust, even for good reasons, even for *your* good reasons, then I'm just another person in her life who decided what was best for her without asking."

The words landed harder than Elodie expected. She thought of her mother, sitting silent in the north transept, keeping whatever secret she'd kept until it went with her into the ground. Everyone in Elodie's life, it seemed, had decided what was best for her without asking. She had built her entire self out of the wreckage of that — an invisibility chosen freely because it was the only thing anyone had ever let her choose.

And here was Tom, doing it again, to someone else. For love. For loyalty. For a swear on the bells.

"The right thing," she said carefully, "and the loyal thing are not always the same thing. I learned that this month."

"Aye." Tom smiled, thin and sad. "Welcome to being seventy-eight. Took me fifty years to learn it and I still get it wrong weekly."

* * *

They talked for another hour, there in the bell chamber, and by degrees the shape of it emerged — not a plan, exactly, but the outline of one, drawn in pencil the way Maren drew her marginal marks, ready to be erased if the world required it.

Tom would not reveal where in the crypt the girl hid; he had promised, and besides, Elodie realized, he genuinely might not know the full labyrinth of it. He would carry a message. One message, written by Elodie, unsigned, offering not rescue but recognition: *I found the cipher. I know about the list. I know your aunt is dead and the police believe you are. I am not writing this to bring you out. I am writing it because whoever killed the others will find you eventually, and I would rather you heard the truth from someone who owes you nothing than from him.*

"And if she says no?" Tom asked. "If she sends back *please stop* again?"

"Then I stop." Elodie said it and found, examining the statement inside herself, that she meant it — and that meaning it cost something, some hot impatient part of her that wanted to march down those stairs with a lantern and a warrant. "I won't drag her. I'll leave the door open. That's all any of us can do for another person, in the end. Leave the door open and keep the kettle warm."

Tom looked at her for a long moment, and something in his face eased, some tension that had been living there since November, since the first stolen soup, since the swear.

"Walter would have liked you," he said gruffly. "He liked people who could wait. Said patience was just courage that had learned to read."

* * *

She wrote the note that afternoon at her desk in the archive, surrounded by the smell of vellum and dust, and rewrote it seven times. Too long. Too short. Too presuming. Too cold. The eighth version was nine sentences, plain as a parish notice, and she sealed it in an unmarked envelope and carried it down to the tower herself because she did not trust it to any messenger but her own hands.

Tom took it from her at the top of the ringing chamber stairs. "Thursday," he said. "I go down Thursdays. Give her time."

"Take all the time she needs."

But time, as Elodie knew better than almost anyone, was the one resource in this affair that was running out. The trial date was coming. Sorrel had said it plainly: without a witness, the forged Halloway will would stand, and the estate — and the cathedral's future, and the names on the brasses, and whatever her mother had witnessed thirty years ago — would be locked away behind a legal fiction forever. Someone was killing to keep it that way. Someone with keys, and access, and a talent for accidents.

She was halfway back across the cloister when she saw Callum.

He stood by the vestry door in his verger's cassock, talking to the dean's secretary, laughing at something, easy and warm in the low sun. He raised a hand to her as she passed — *evening, Ellie* — and she raised hers back, and smiled, and walked on, and it was only ten steps later that she stopped, struck by a small cold fact she couldn't immediately place.

Callum had called her *Ellie*.

No one called her Ellie. No one had called her Ellie since she was nine years old, since her mother, since a lifetime of being Miss Vane and the archivist and *that quiet woman from the cathedral*. She turned back toward the vestry door, meaning to say something — she didn't know what, a joke, a question, anything — but Callum had already gone inside, and the door stood shut, and the cloister was empty except for the jackdaws and the lengthening shadows.

Probably nothing, she told herself. Probably she'd misheard. Probably it was the sort of small warm slip that friends make, and Callum was her friend, had been her friend for fifteen years, had taught her to laugh again after her mother died.

Probably.

But she had spent her whole life learning that the archive never lies and that people lie constantly, in the smallest ways, in single letters of a name — and that the pattern no one else sees is usually the true one.

That night, Tom went down into the crypt with her note in his pocket. And Elodie sat up in her flat above the archive with all the lights burning, listening to the cathedral breathe around her, waiting for Thursday the way you wait for a bell to strike — knowing exactly when it must come, and unable, until it does, to make yourself believe it.
