## 14. Bishop's Gambit

The summons came the way weather comes to the coast—not announced so much as felt, a pressure behind the ears, a change in the light.

Elodie was elbow-deep in a crate of eighteenth-century vestment patterns when Callum appeared at the archive door, and she knew before he spoke. He had his hat in his hands. Callum only removed his hat for funerals and bad news.

"He wants to see you," he said. "The Bishop. In the morning. Nine o'clock."

"Me?" She kept her voice mild, though her pulse did not cooperate. "I'm not on any rota."

"No." Callum shifted his weight. "He asked for you by name, Elodie. Said—" and here he hesitated, and the hesitation told her more than the words would have, "—said he'd heard you'd been *finding things*."

She made herself smile, small and unremarkable, the smile of a woman who found dust and ledgers and nothing else. "That's what I'm paid for."

But that night she took Maren's diary from its hiding place—inside the hollowed spine of a water-damaged Breviarium no one had requested in forty years—and read it again by lamplight, and the marginal marks seemed to look back at her like eyes through slats. The brasses. The names. Her mother's name, sitting in a dead man's estate like a coin someone had slipped into her pocket without her noticing.

*He knows,* she thought. Or he suspected. And a man like Bishop Halloway did not summon suspicion to tea. He summoned it to be measured, weighed, and if necessary, buried.

She slept badly, which for Elodie Vane was itself an event worth recording.

* * *

Bishop Halloway's office occupied the southeast corner of the episcopal residence, and it was a room designed to make visitors feel like petitions rather than people. Books lined the walls but were clearly never read; they were architecture. Light came through leaded glass in amber panes, gilding everything equally—the silver inkstand, the portrait of his grandfather, the face of the woman who sat across from him with her hands folded and her mind running like a millrace beneath still water.

Elodie Vane was not what he had expected. He had expected either defiance or fear, and instead he got attention—that unnerving, total attention of hers, as though she were cataloguing him the way she catalogued everything: date, provenance, condition, value.

"Miss Vane. Thank you for coming." He gestured at the tea service between them, untouched. "You'll forgive the formality. I find that conversations about the cathedral's future go better over porcelain than over pews."

"The cathedral's future," she repeated pleasantly.

"Indeed." He leaned back, letting the chair take him, letting the room do its work. "You've served this institution quietly for eleven years. Cataloguing, conserving, keeping our memory alive. I want you to know that your work has been noticed. By me, particularly."

"I hadn't realized I was noticeable."

"That," said the Bishop, smiling, "is precisely my point—and precisely the problem. A talent like yours, hidden away in a crypt. It's a waste, Miss Vane. It's always struck me as a waste." He let that sit. Then, gently, as though offering her something warm: "I've secured funding. A proper archive. Climate control, conservation lab, a full-time staff under a director who reports directly to the diocese. Your name was the first one mentioned. *The* first."

For one treacherous moment, Elodie let herself see it. The long tables. The good light. The boxes opened and their contents honored instead of moldering. A room where nothing was lost, where every name was written down properly and kept—

Her mother's name, written down properly, in a forged will, in a dead man's hand.

"And what would the director be expected to provide," she said, "in return?"

Halloway's smile didn't move, but something behind it did—a recalibration, like a man adjusting his grip. "Continuity," he said. "Order. There are certain documents circulating—unofficial papers, private notes, materials that belong properly to the diocesan record. If such materials came into your custody, the correct procedure would be to deposit them with the diocese. With me. For safekeeping. For *context*." He spread his hands. "An archive is only as trustworthy as its chain of custody, Miss Vane. Loose documents, loose ends—they damage everyone. They damage the memory we're trying to protect."

"You mean the diary."

The word dropped into the room like a stone into a well, and Elodie watched the ripples cross the Bishop's face—surprise, quickly mastered, then something colder underneath. He had not expected her to say it aloud. Good. Let him learn what she already knew: that invisibility was not ignorance, and that quiet people heard everything.

"I don't know what you mean," he said.

"A young woman's diary," Elodie said, "belonging to a girl who may or may not be dead, whose aunt fell from the rood screen gallery three weeks ago, whose name appears in connection with an inheritance your family has been administering for thirty years. That diary. The one you'd like deposited 'for context.'" She picked up her teacup, sipped, set it down. Her hands were steady. She was almost proud of them. "I don't have it, Bishop. But if I did, I wouldn't give it to you. I'd give it to Detective Inspector Sorrel, because that's what a document is *for*. It's evidence. Evidence isn't context. It's testimony."

The silence that followed had texture. Halloway studied her for a long moment, and Elodie understood that she was watching a man decide something—not whether she was guilty of anything, but whether she was dangerous enough to matter.

"You sound like your mother," he said at last.

The words went through her like cold water through cloth. She did not flinch. She had spent thirty years learning not to flinch. "You knew my mother."

"I knew *of* her." He rose, signaling the interview's end, moving to the window with his hands clasped behind his back. "A difficult woman. Principled past the point of sense. She could have had a comfortable life here, Miss Vane. She chose otherwise. I'd hate to watch history repeat itself." He turned, and the amber light caught his face and made it kind, which was somehow worse. "The offer stands. Think about it. Think about what you're protecting, and whether it's worth what it will cost you. Doors close quietly in this city, Miss Vane. So do archives."

"Then I'll keep mine open," Elodie said, standing, collecting her coat. At the door she paused, and gave him the truth wrapped in courtesy, which was the only way she knew how to deliver it: "Thank you for the tea, Bishop. It was very good porcelain."

* * *

Sorrel's flat smelled of instant coffee and rain-damp wool, and the detective stood at her kitchen counter reading the same paragraph of the incident report for the fourth time because Elodie Vane was pacing her kitchen like a caged thing, and Sorrel had learned in three weeks of partnership that when Elodie paced, listening was the job.

"He offered me an archive," Elodie said. "Funded. Staffed. My name over the door."

"And all you had to do was hand over the diary."

"Not the diary. '*The documents.*' Plural. Unspecified. Which means he doesn't know exactly what I have—he only knows I have *something*, and he's fishing with a net wide enough to catch whatever falls out." She stopped pacing. "And Sorrel—he said my mother's name. Not her name, exactly. He said I *sounded like* her. Thirty years ago. He knew her. He knew what she found, or he knew what happened to her because of it, and he stood there in his amber light and offered me money to become him."

Sorrel set down the report. "What did you say?"

"I said thank you for the tea." Elodie's mouth twisted. "I've decided it's going to be my signature exit line. Very memorable. Intimidating, even."

"It needs work." But Sorrel was almost smiling, and then she wasn't, because the other thing was there, the thing they'd both been circling since the aunt's body came down off the rood screen. "Elodie. If he offered you a bribe today, it means today he concluded you're a threat. Men like Halloway don't conclude that and then wait politely. What else did he say? Anything about doors?"

"'Doors close quietly.'"

Sorrel was already moving, pulling her jacket off the back of a chair. "Then tonight you're not sleeping at yours."

"My flat is above the archive. It's the safest building in the city—"

"It's the most *watched* building in the city, which is different, and you know it." Sorrel looked at her, and Elodie saw the thing under the detective's dryness, the thing that had made them recognize each other from the start: two women who had built lives out of being underestimated, now both thoroughly estimated. "One night. My sofa. Tomorrow we move the diary somewhere he can't reach it and start building the case properly. Grand jury convenes in nineteen days, Elodie. We need a witness, not a martyr."

So Elodie went home anyway—because she was stubborn, because she needed the cipher page from her mother's effects, because some part of her still believed that being careful was the same as being safe—and she told herself the unease crawling up her spine was just the lateness of the hour.

The street outside her building was empty. The stairwell smelled of cabbage and old carpet, as always. Her door was locked, exactly as she'd left it.

Inside, everything was almost right.

That was how she knew. Eleven years of cataloguing had trained her eye to detect displacement the way sailors detect weather, and her flat was displaced by millimeters. The books on the shelf reshelved by height instead of by her system. The cushion on the chair bearing a crease that wasn't hers. The drawer of her desk closed a finger's width too far, its runners catching at the wrong point. Someone had been thorough. Someone had been patient. Someone had put everything back *almost* perfectly, and the almost was a signature.

She didn't turn on the lights. She stood in the dark of her own home, heart hammering against her ribs like a fist on a locked door, and made herself inventory the room the way she'd inventory a collection. The diary was safe—it had never lived here. The cipher page from her mother's box was gone from its envelope. In its place, on the desk, weighted under a stone from the cathedral precinct, lay a single sheet of cream paper.

Typed. No signature.

*Some inheritances are burdens. Yours needn't be. Last chance.*

Below the words, pressed into the paper hard enough to emboss it, someone had stamped a small image. Elodie tilted it toward the streetlight leaking through the curtains.

It was a memorial brass. A rubbing of one—the figure of a kneeling donor, hands folded, face worn smooth by centuries of reverent fingers.

And beneath the figure, where the inscription should have read a name, the brass had been altered. Freshly cut. Recently polished.

It bore her mother's initials.

Elodie stood very still in the dark, holding the page, and understood with perfect clarity that the game had changed. She had spent her whole life looking at other people through the safe end of the telescope. Now the telescope had turned around, and she was the small distant figure in the lens, and somewhere out in the wet night a man—or men—who moved bones and staged accidents and typed threats on cream paper knew her address, her mother's name, and the exact price of her silence.

They had searched her home while she drank tea with a bishop.

They had left her a message instead of taking everything, which meant they wanted her afraid, not destroyed. Yet.

Elodie set the page down with hands that shook only a little, took her coat back off its hook, and slipped out into the night toward Sorrel's flat, walking fast, walking quiet, a hunter's daughter learning to run.

Behind her, across the street, a cigarette tip glowed once in a doorway and went dark.
