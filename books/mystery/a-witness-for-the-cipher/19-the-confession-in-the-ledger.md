## 19. The Confession in the Ledger

The archive at three in the morning had a temperature all its own—a cool, mineral stillness, like the inside of a stone held too long in the hand. Elodie Vane worked by lamplight with her cardigan buttoned to the throat, and she told herself, as she had told herself for two days now, that what she was doing was ordinary. Verification. Cross-referencing. The bread and butter of any competent archivist.

She did not believe herself. She hadn't believed herself since Callum's alibi had come out of his mouth sounding like a lesson rehearsed for a schoolmaster.

The ledger lay open before her: the cathedral's maintenance record, volume after volume going back decades, each entry in the handwriting of whichever verger or steeplekeeper had made it. Beside it, stacked in careful towers, were the bell-rope logs—thin pamphlets, really, kept by the tower captain and countersigned by whoever performed the work. Ropes wore. Ropes frayed. Ropes were spliced, shortened, replaced. It was the most boring paperwork in the entire building, and Elodie had loved it once for exactly that reason. Boring things were honest things.

Or so she had believed.

She had begun with the dates. That was the natural place to begin—the three accidents, the wet shoes on dry nights, the deaths that Sorrel had reopened and that now hung over the cathedral like weather coming in. Verity Pell, the flower arranger, found at the foot of the north transept stair on the fourth of March. Dennis Ackroyd, the mason, dead beneath scaffolding he had helped erect himself on the nineteenth of June. And old Mrs. Tansy Wren, the sacristan, drowned in no more than six inches of water in the baptistry on the eleventh of September. Three deaths. Three rulings of misadventure. And one detail that had lived in Elodie's head like a splinter since the night she'd first noticed it: their shoes had been wet. All three of them. On nights when not a drop of rain had fallen within thirty miles.

Wet shoes meant water. Water meant somewhere water could be found on a dry night—and there were only four such places in St. Aldhelm's. The baptistry. The cistern room below the south cloister. The slop sink in the vestry. And the ringing chamber, where a bucket stood always by the door, because wet ropes gripped badly and every ringer knew you dampened your hands, never the rope itself—or rather, you *did* dampen the rope, deliberately, in controlled inches, when a rope needed stretching or settling before a heavy peal. Every bell-ringer knew this. Every ringer also knew that a soaked rope behaved strangely for hours afterward—heavy, sluggish, prone to catching in the pulley blocks.

Elodie pulled the rope log for March toward her and flattened it under her palm.

*4th March — Verity Pell.* The entry for that week was in Callum's hand. She knew his writing the way she knew the grain of the oak desk under her fingers: the forward slant, the way he crossed his t's with a single impatient stroke, the little hook he put on his g's. *Rope 3 (tenor) inspected, dressed with beeswax, sallies re-whipped.* Routine. Unremarkable. Countersigned by Tom Ashplant, whose shaky signature looked like a spider drowning in ink.

But here was the thing she had turned over and over for two nights, the thing that would not lie down: beeswax dressing was done to the *upper* length of a rope, the part that ran through the ceiling boss into the bell chamber. To dress a rope properly—to do it at all—you took the rope down. You unhooked it from the wheel, ran it through your hands from end to end, worked the wax in. Which meant that on the fourth of March, Rope 3 should have been *off its wheel*, coiled on the ringing-chamber floor, for the better part of an afternoon.

And Verity Pell had fallen down the north transept stair that same evening, her shoes wet, on a dry night.

Elodie sat back. The lamp hissed faintly. Somewhere above her, through nine floors of stone and timber, the tenor bell hung silent in its frame, seven hundred years of patience in bronze.

It proved nothing. She wrote that in her notebook, because she made herself write such things: *rope maintenance alone proves nothing*. A man might dress a rope and have nothing whatever to do with a woman falling down a stair across the cathedral. Coincidence was a real thing. She had built her whole life on the assumption that most strange patterns were coincidence wearing a costume.

But then she checked June.

*Nineteenth June — Dennis Ackroyd.* Rope log: *Rope 6 (treble) — sally worn, replaced with new worsted, rope stretched and settled.* Dated the eighteenth. And here—here was the detail that made her stomach turn over slowly, like something rolling in its sleep—the entry noted that the new sally required *wetting and weighting overnight* to set the whipping tight. Standard practice. Absolutely standard. Any ringer would confirm it. You wet the wool, you hang a weight on it, and by morning it is perfect.

Which meant that on the morning of the nineteenth of June, the treble rope—the lightest rope, the one nearest the tower stair, the one a man descending quickly in the dark might grab without thinking—had been hanging there freshly wetted, slick, heavier than it should be, behaving in ways even experienced ringers found surprising.

Dennis Ackroyd had not been a ringer. He was a mason. He used the tower stair because the scaffolding for the clerestory repairs went up through the belfry, and he crossed the ringing chamber twice a day, morning and dusk. His shoes had been wet when they pulled him out from under the fallen poles. Everyone had assumed he'd stepped in the puddle left by the recent rain—but the nineteenth of June had been dry as a bone. Elodie had checked the meteorological records herself, twice, with the grim thoroughness of a woman who hoped to be proven wrong.

A wet rope. A dark stair. A mason who didn't know the treble had been dressed the night before, who reached for the banister rail and found instead—or slipped past, or caught—a rope hanging where it shouldn't behave, dragging him off balance at the top of a stone spiral.

Or perhaps not. Perhaps he simply fell. People fell. She wrote it down again: *still proves nothing.*

September was worse.

*Eleventh September — Mrs. Tansy Wren.* The rope log for that week recorded work on Rope 5: *crown staple greased, rope run and re-seated, floor boards around rope hole lifted and refitted.* Floor boards. Lifted. In the ringing chamber, directly around the fifth rope hole, on the ninth of September—two days before Tansy Wren went into the baptistry and did not come out alive.

Lifted floorboards meant the boards were up. Meant the chamber floor had holes in it, temporarily, edges loose, nails proud. Meant anyone crossing that floor in the dark—even someone who knew it well, even *especially* someone who knew it well, because we trust the floors we know—was walking over traps.

And Tansy Wren had keys to everything. Tansy Wren had been the sacristan for thirty-one years, and if anyone had ever noticed something in the crypt, or the vestry, or the ledger—if anyone had seen a document, a signature, a name that didn't belong—it would have been Tansy, moving through the building's private hours like a small grey ghost with a feather duster.

Her shoes had been wet. The baptistry was forty feet from the ringing chamber stair. Forty feet, on a route that passed directly below the tower.

Elodie closed the September log and discovered her hands were not entirely steady.

* * *

She made tea she did not drink. That was the trouble with working in the crypt annex at three in the morning; the kettle was a museum piece and the milk was a gamble, and besides, her mouth had gone dry in a way no liquid would fix. She stood at the little sink window that looked out onto the cloister garth, at the wet grass silvered by the security lamp, and she made herself think about Callum Reeve.

Callum, who had taught her which of the vergers' storeroom keys stuck and which could be trusted. Callum, who had carried her mother's funeral wreath as though it were made of spun glass, who had stood beside her at the graveside not saying anything because he understood she didn't want anything said. Callum, who called her *El* though no one else dared, who brought his son Danny round at Christmas because he said the girl deserved to see somebody's family even if it wasn't her own. Twenty-two years. He had been assistant verger for twenty-two years, and her friend for longer than that, and last Tuesday, when Detective Inspector Sorrel had asked him where he was on the night Maren Osgood's aunt had died, he had given an alibi so smooth it squeaked.

*Ringing practice,* he'd said. *Full attendance. Then I stayed after to dress the tenor rope—ask Tom, ask anyone. I was in the tower until half eleven.*

Tom had confirmed it. The attendance book had confirmed it. Half the ringers would swear on a stack of Bibles that Callum Reeve was in the tower until half eleven, because Callum Reeve was always in the tower, because Callum Reeve was the man who did the ropes.

And that was precisely what had woken Elodie at two in the morning with her heart hammering: not a discrepancy in his story, but the absence of one. Because she had spent two nights in these logs now, and she knew something that Callum must have known she would know—that she, Elodie Vane, keeper of the cathedral's paper memory, would eventually pull the rope records the way she pulled everything, eventually, because paper remembers what people forget.

He had counted on the wrong thing. Or he had counted on the right thing and gotten sloppy, or desperate, or both. Because here was the flaw, the beautiful terrible flaw, sitting in front of her in black and white:

On the night of the aunt's death—the night Callum claimed to be dressing the tenor rope until half eleven—there was no entry for that work. Not in the rope log, not in the maintenance ledger, not anywhere. Callum Reeve logged *everything*. He was famous for it. He teased her about it: *you and me, El, we're the only ones who keep the books straight.* Twenty-two years of immaculate entries, and on the one night that mattered, nothing. As if the man who never forgot to write things down had suddenly forgotten—or as if the alibi had been constructed later, from habit, from the shape of a thousand true evenings, without checking whether the paper agreed.

Paper disagreed. Paper always disagreed, eventually, with anyone who lied to it.

And more than that. She went back to the desk and laid the pages side by side, March, June, September, and the missing October entry, and she saw the pattern whole for the first time, and it was worse than murder. It was method. Each accident had been preceded by legitimate, logged, routine maintenance work that placed him in the right place with the right tools and an innocent explanation for every oddity—a rope down here, a wetted sally there, floorboards up yonder. He hadn't evaded the logs. He had *used* them. He had made the cathedral's own honesty his accomplice, because he understood, better than anyone, that no one questions a written record. No one but her.

He had built his alibis out of ropes. And ropes, she now understood, could be sabotaged in both directions—not just to kill, but to account. A man who controls the maintenance schedule controls where he can be seen, and when, and why, and with witnesses. Ringing practice had full attendance. The hours after had Tom. But Tom was eighty-three and half-blind in the low light and adored Callum, and a man dressing a rope in the gloom above can be absent from the chamber below for twenty minutes at a stretch without anyone thinking twice. Twenty minutes. The rood screen gallery was ninety seconds' walk from the tower door. Ninety seconds there, ninety back, and the rest of the hour genuinely spent with wax and whipcord among friends.

Elodie pressed her knuckles to her mouth.

Because that was the final piece, wasn't it. The one she had been circling for two days like a bird over deep water. Callum's alibi for the night of the aunt's death hadn't just been rehearsed. It had been *built on the ropes*—on the assumption that the ropes would vouch for him, as they always had. But the ropes couldn't vouch for him that night, because the ropes hadn't been touched. And Elodie could prove it, because she had spent yesterday afternoon in the bell chamber with a magnifying glass and a soft brush, examining the tenor rope's sally under Sorrel's reluctant authorization, and the wax dressing on it was three months old at least. Old wax yellows. Fresh wax shines. This wax was dull as an old candle, worked deep into the flax, long past the week-old freshness his story required.

He had lied. Callum had stood in that interview room with his cap in his hands and lied to a police officer, calmly, kindly, in the voice he used for telling frightened children the bells wouldn't hurt them.

* * *

Dawn came grey through the high slit window, and with it came footsteps on the annex stair—she knew them instantly, that familiar uneven tread, right foot landing harder than the left, the hip that had bothered him since the fall of '09. Her chest tightened so hard it hurt. She slid the ledgers closed and laid her notebook face-down, an instinctive gesture, hiding evidence from a man she loved like family, and hated herself for the smoothness of it.

"El? You're down here at this hour?" Callum filled the doorway, big and rumpled and smelling of cold morning air and engine oil from the van. His face did the thing it always did when he found her working too late—the fond exasperation, the almost-fatherly frown. "Sorrel's got you burning midnight oil too, then."

"Cross-referencing," she said. Her voice came out level. She was almost proud of it. "Maintenance records. For the trial preparation."

"Ah." He nodded, easy, unsurprised. "You'll get it all sorted. You always do." He glanced at the closed ledgers, and something flickered across his face—quick, unreadable, gone. "Anything interesting?"

The question hung between them like a rope swinging gently in a tower where no one had rung.

"No," Elodie said, and lied to him for the second time in her life, and the first time to his face. "Nothing yet."

He smiled, warm as ever, and said he'd put the kettle on upstairs, and went. And Elodie Vane sat alone in the grey light with her hand flat on the cover of the ledger, over the page where his handwriting marched neat and faithful across twenty-two years, and understood at last what she was holding.

Not a confession. Ledgers don't confess. But a proof—the next best thing, the archivist's version: a pattern only visible to someone who loved the paper enough to read it properly, and damning enough to hang a man she'd have trusted with her life.

She thought of Danny, sixteen and grieving debts he'd inherited from a will that was forged. She thought of Tom, who had fed a hidden girl in the dark on a promise to a dying friend. She thought of Maren, alive, refusing to testify while the killer walked free above her head.

The killer had a name now. The killer had taught her which keys stuck.

Elodie picked up the telephone and dialed the number Sorrel had made her memorize, and when the detective answered on the second ring—awake, of course, detectives were always awake at the bad hours—Elodie heard her own voice come out steady and cold and entirely unfamiliar:

"It's Vane. I need you to come to the archive. Bring a warrant." A breath. "And bring it quietly. He's upstairs making tea."
