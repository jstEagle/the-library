## 20. Evensong Trap

The plan was made in the archive, after midnight, by lamplight, and it was made the way Elodie did everything: precisely, quietly, with an eye to what could be undone.

"We don't trap him," Sorrel said, pacing between the shelves of folios. "We *invite* him. There's a difference, and the difference is what keeps us out of prison."

Elodie sat with her hands folded on the ledger, the maintenance logs spread before her like a confession she had written in someone else's blood. She had spent three nights proving what her heart had refused for weeks: that the bell-rope inspections Callum signed off on were dated to hours when the tower was locked; that his alibi for the night of the aunt's death rested on a rope he himself had frayed; that every wet-shoed accident traced back through a keyholder's list to one set of hands. Callum's hands. The hands that had taught her to carry a folio properly. The hands that had fixed her radiator without being asked, twice, and refused payment both times.

"He'll come for Maren," Elodie said. "If he believes she's alive, and if he believes we've found something—"

"Then we make him believe it at a time and place of our choosing." Sorrel stopped pacing. "Evensong. Thursday. The cathedral will be full — choir, congregation, vergers moving through the shadows. He can't do anything obvious with two hundred witnesses."

"Which is why he'll do something that isn't obvious." Elodie looked up. "He knows the building better than anyone except Tom. He knows where the cameras aren't, because there aren't any. If we stage a discovery in the crypt — candles lit, Maren's things arranged, a note half-burned — he won't come during the service. He'll come *during* the service, because everyone's eyes are up front and the crypt stairs are behind the quire screen."

Sorrel studied her. "You've thought about this more than you've admitted."

"I've thought about everything," Elodie said, "for months. I just never said any of it aloud. That was always my mistake."

* * *

They told almost no one, which meant they told the people who mattered.

Tom took the news standing in the bell chamber, surrounded by ropes, his great weathered hands hanging at his sides. When Elodie finished — the cipher, the ledger, Callum — he said nothing for a long time. Above them the tenor bell hung silent in its frame, big as a chapel.

"I christened that boy," Tom said finally. "Held him in this very tower while his father rang rounds below. His mother cried when the bells went off over his head and he didn't cry at all. Just looked up." He turned away, toward the louvred opening and the grey evening beyond. "Looked up like he already knew what was up here waiting for him."

"Tom," Elodie said gently, "if you can't be part of this—"

"I'll be part of it." His voice came out flat as a paving stone. "But I'm telling you now, girl, so you can plan around it or not: nobody dies in my tower. Not him. Not anybody. Whatever else happens Thursday, that's the one thing I'll put my body against."

Maren, when they told her, went white and then stubbornly pink. "I want to be there."

"You'll be *in* it," Sorrel said. "That's different from being there. You'll be in the vestry with the door barred and Sorrel's constable outside it."

"And if he gets past the constable?"

"He won't get past me first," Elodie said, and heard her own voice sound like someone she hadn't met yet.

The bell-ringers were told only their piece: a full peal attempt Thursday evening, all eight bells, called changes throughout evensong — unusual, but the ringers loved an excuse, and old Fenwick had been angling for a quarter-peal for his seventieth. The noise would cover everything below. It would also mean the tower stair was occupied, ringing chamber sealed, every rope in motion. No one would come down from above. And no one would go up.

Except, if the plan held, Callum — who would come down from the triforium instead, along the passage only vergers used, into the crypt, following the candlelight they'd leave burning beside the Osgood vault.

* * *

Thursday came in low and gold, the light slanting through the west windows in long amber blades across the nave floor. Elodie watched it from the shadow of the quire screen and thought, absurdly, of all the Thursdays she had spent invisible among these stones, cataloguing, unnoticed, content. She had dressed carefully tonight — not to disappear but to be *placed*, a figure who belonged exactly where she stood, because tonight belonging was camouflage.

Below, in the crypt, the stage was set. Two candle stubs — real ones, taken from the rack Tom kept — burned beside the Osgood vault. Maren's scarf, the blue one, lay draped over the iron gate. A page torn from a modern notebook sat half-consumed in a dish of ash. To anyone who knew what to look for, it said: *she was here, and someone came for her.*

Above, the choir assembled. Maren's empty stall wore its dust sheet, as it had for months — the cathedral's quiet grief made linen. Bishop Halloway processed in vestments worth more than Elodie's yearly stipend, his face composed into its public benevolence. He caught her eye once, across the crossing, and gave her a small nod, and she gave him back nothing at all.

At seven minutes to six, the first bell went up.

The sound filled the tower like water filling a well — the treble, then the second, third, fourth, until all eight were swinging and the whole stone spine of St. Aldhelm's shivered with it. Elodie felt it in her teeth, in her sternum, in the floor under her shoes. In the ringing chamber overhead, eight ringers pulled in rounds, and the noise poured down through the tower shaft and drowned the world.

It also, she knew, drowned footsteps.

She took her position at the top of the crypt stair, behind the quire screen, where the candle-glow rose faintly through the open door below like breath from a throat. Sorrel waited in the dark of the south transept, plainclothes, a radio silent in her pocket. The constable stood at the vestry door where Maren sat barred inside, pale and furious and safe.

Evensong began. The introit rose. The congregation murmured into hymn 34.

And Elodie waited.

* * *

POV SORREL

Sorrel had been in enough ambushes to know that the worst part was not the violence. The worst part was the arithmetic of waiting — counting heads, counting exits, counting seconds since the last check. She counted the vergers' positions: two at the west end, one processing with the bishop. Which left Callum unaccounted for, and had been unaccounted for since six o'clock, when he'd told the head verger he was checking the undercroft heating.

The undercroft heating. Sorrel could have laughed, if laughing wouldn't have cost her the stillness she needed. The man had lied with the same tidy competence he brought to everything — the flower rotas, the brass polishing rota, the murders.

The bells thundered on. Through the stone she felt them more than heard them, a pulse in the masonry, and she thought: *he planned around these too.* Every accident, every wet-shoed death, timed to services, to ringing, to the roar that swallowed small sounds. He'd learned the building's acoustics the way other men learned golf swings.

Her radio crackled once — the constable at the vestry: *all quiet* — and then the transept was silence again, and hymn 34 rolled to its amen, and the choir began the responses, and Sorrel watched the dark mouth of the triforium passage above the quire screen and waited for a shape to fill it.

At 6:14, it did.

A shadow, brief, at the passage mouth. Then gone. Moving, she judged, toward the stair that let down behind the screen — the verger's stair, the one that bypassed the nave entirely.

She keyed the radio twice. Below, in the crypt, Elodie would have seen the candle flames bend in a draft that shouldn't exist, because the verger's stair door opened onto the crypt corridor, and the draft meant the door was open, and the door being open meant—

*He's coming down,* Sorrel thought. *Right on schedule. Right into it.*

And felt, despite twenty years of discipline, the cold slide of doubt: *or right into whatever he's decided it should be instead.*

* * *

POV ELODIE

The draft reached her before the sound did.

Elodie stood at the crypt stair with her hand flat against the cold wall, and the candle flames below bent sideways, steadied, bent again, and she knew. Her heart did not race; it did the opposite — it went slow and enormous, each beat a struck bell.

Footsteps on stone. Unhurried. Familiar. She had catalogued that gait for eleven years, the way she catalogued everything: Callum walked the way careful men walk, heel-first, weight even, no wasted motion.

He emerged from the corridor into the candlelight and stopped.

For a moment he simply looked — at the candles, at the blue scarf, at the burned page — and Elodie, pressed into the recess of the stair arch above him, saw his face in profile, and it was worse than rage or triumph. It was grief. He looked at the scarf the way Tom had described looking at the tenor bell: like a man seeing everything he'd tried to keep whole come apart in his hands.

"You're not here," he said softly, to the scarf, to the dark. "You were never here. I checked. I *checked*, Maren."

"She's not dead, Callum."

Elodie stepped out of the arch, and the sound of her voice in that stone throat was small and enormous at once. Callum turned. Above them, muffled through yards of masonry, the bells swung on, and the psalm was a vibration rather than a sound.

"Elodie." Something moved through his face — relief, horror, calculation, and then a terrible settling, like a man putting down a weight he'd carried too long. "You should have stayed in your archive."

"I know." She descended two steps, keeping the rail between them. "That's what I told myself for months. But you taught me better than that, didn't you? You taught me that a thing left unrecorded is a thing that happens again. You said that about the memorial books. Remember?"

"Don't." His voice cracked on it.

"The wet shoes," she said. "Dry nights, Callum. Three people, dry nights, wet shoes. I noticed it in week one and I hated myself for noticing, because I kept hoping I was wrong. I'm never wrong about details. You know that. You're the one who told me details are how the dead speak."

He moved then — fast, far faster than she'd imagined, and the rail was between them but rails are not walls, and his hand closed on her wrist and wrenched her down onto the crypt floor, and the pain was white and total, and his knee was on her chest and his face above hers was the face of a drowning man.

"There's no version of this where you walk away," he said, and his voice was shaking. "There isn't. I've done the arithmetic. I've done it a hundred times. My son—" and his voice broke clean through, "—my son owes money to men who got that debt from a forged will, and every road out of it goes through one girl who saw a meeting through a door, and I have carried this alone for a year, Elodie, *alone*, because there was no one—"

"There was me," Elodie gasped. "You never asked me."

His grip faltered. Just a fraction. A fraction was enough.

Because above them, the bells stopped.

Not the end of a touch — the wrongness of it was absolute, eight bells cut mid-swing, and into that ringing absence came a sound Elodie had known all her life and never expected to hear as rescue: footsteps on the tower stair, many feet, descending fast, and Tom Ashgrove's voice rolling down the shaft like the voice of the building itself:

"*CALLUM PRITCHARD.*"

* * *

POV SORREL

Sorrel came down the verger's stair at a run and burst into the crypt to find the geometry already wrong — Callum rising off Elodie, backing toward the Osgood vault, a heavy candle-iron wrenched from its bracket in his fist, and Elodie on the flags, breathing, alive, her eyes furious and wet.

"Police!" Sorrel's voice, and her warrant card out, and her heart going like a trip-hammer. "Callum Pritchard, I'm arresting you for the murders of Vera Osgood, Dennis Halloway, and Ruth Ellery—"

"You're arresting me for accidents," Callum said, and he was smiling, and the smile was the most frightening thing yet, because it was Callum's smile, the warm one, the one from the refectory. "Wet shoes, Inspector. Slippery floors. Old people fall. Ask anyone. Ask the *bishop*—"

"I'm asking a jury."

Behind her, boots on the stair — the ringers, eight of them, filing down into the torchlight with the stunned faces of men who had left a quarter-peal attempt and found a nightmare. Fenwick at their head, seventy years old and trembling with something that wasn't fear.

And Callum backed toward the crypt's inner door — the old charnel stair, the way out he alone knew — and found it blocked.

Tom stood in it.

The steeplekeeper filled the doorway the way the tenor bell fills its frame: wholly, immovably, a fact of the architecture. He had come down the tower stair ahead of his ringers, and he stood with his hands at his sides and his feet planted, and he looked at Callum with ninety years of love and loss stacked behind his eyes.

"Move, Tom." Callum's voice was ragged now. "Tom. *Move.*"

"No," Tom said.

"They'll hang it on you too. Harboring. Obstruction. Move, and I'm gone, and you never saw me—"

"I christened you." Tom's voice didn't rise. It didn't need to. In the stone silence where the bells had been, it carried like a sermon. "I held you in that tower and you looked up at the bells and you didn't cry. I've been asking myself for thirty years what you saw up there. And I think I know now. I think you saw that everything rings, boy, and nothing listens." He took one step forward. One. "So listen now. Nobody dies in my tower. Not that girl. Not Elodie. Not you. You want to leave, you put down that iron and you walk past me and you take what's coming, because what's coming is *justice*, and justice is the only bell in this building that's ever rung true."

Callum raised the candle-iron.

Every ringer in the crypt tensed. Sorrel's hand went inside her coat.

And Elodie, from the floor, said quietly: "Your son doesn't know, does he. What you did. Any of it."

The iron wavered.

"He thinks you're protecting him," she went on, still on the flags, her voice level, her wrist screaming. "He thinks his godfather is a good man having a hard year. If you swing that, he learns the truth at a trial. If you put it down, maybe — *maybe* — he only ever learns his father broke. People break, Callum. I break. Tom breaks. It's the carrying it alone that kills. You said it yourself. There was no one." She pushed herself up, slowly, against the vault, until she stood. "There was everyone. You just never looked up."

The candle-iron hit the flags with a sound like a single bell stroke.

Callum Pritchard sank down beside it, into the dust and the candlelight, and put his face in his hands, and wept — loudly, horribly, the way men weep who have not wept in years, and the ringers parted around him as Sorrel closed the distance and read him his rights in a voice that shook only at the edges.

Tom did not move from the doorway until it was done. Then he crossed the crypt, picked up the candle-iron, and set it back on its bracket, straightening it with two fingers, the way he straightened everything.

"Ropes need checking after all that swinging," he said to no one. "Service tomorrow starts at nine."

* * *

Later — much later, past midnight, the cathedral emptied and sealed — Elodie sat with Maren in the vestry where the girl had spent four hours listening to the whole catastrophe through a barred door. Sorrel had taken Callum away. The bishop had been informed, and had gone very still, and had asked one question — *"Does it touch the estate?"* — which told Elodie everything about the man and nothing she hadn't already decrypted from the brasses.

Maren was wrapped in Tom's coat, drinking tea that had gone cold twice.

"You came down the stairs," Maren said. "When he had you on the floor. Tom said you talked to him. What did you say?"

Elodie considered lying, and found she had lost the taste for it. "I told him the truth. That he was never alone, he just refused to look."

"That's not what saved you, though." Maren's eyes were sharp and young and had seen too much. "The bells stopping. That's what saved you. They stopped *exactly* when you needed them to. How?"

Elodie smiled, tired and strange, and reached into her bag, and drew out the diary — Maren's diary, the one with two hands in it, the one that had started everything.

"Because I sent the signal early," she said. "Three minutes early. The ringers weren't cutting the peal on my timing, Maren. They were cutting it on yours." She turned to the last written page, and the marginal mark there, the cipher initial that matched the brasses, and laid the book open between them. "Someone wrote in your diary eleven days after you vanished. Someone who knew the crypt, and the candle racks, and the verger's stair. I assumed it was you, hiding. I was wrong about the hand."

Maren stared at the page. At the ink. At the initials that were not quite her own.

"Then who—" she started.

And from the doorway, where he had appeared without either of them hearing, Tom said quietly:

"That'll be my doing, miss. And I think you'd best hear it from me before the trial, not after."
