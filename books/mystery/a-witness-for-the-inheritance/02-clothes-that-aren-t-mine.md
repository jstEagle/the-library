## 2. Clothes That Aren't Mine

The call came at 6:47 in the morning, which was the first wrong thing. Tamsin Amberdale had spent eleven years cultivating a very specific relationship with the telephone: it rang when they called out, and it rang when Wren needed something, and otherwise it kept a respectful silence, like a well-trained dog. A call before seven from an unknown number was either a death or a scoop, and Tamsin had learned long ago that the two were often the same animal wearing different collars.

"Amberdale." The voice on the other end was clipped, official, and doing its best to sound casual, which meant it was neither. "This is Detective Sergeant Okafor, Fenwick CID. We'd like you to come down to the towpath by Marleigh Cut this morning. As soon as you're able."

Tamsin sat up in the tangle of their unmade bed, the duvet falling away, the cold of the flat pressing in through the single-glazed windows. "Is this about the Vane file? Because if you're calling to tell me not to cover it—"

"It's about a body."

Three words. Tamsin had interviewed enough grieving people, enough liars, enough cops reading statements off cards, to know that three words delivered that flatly were never just three words. There was always a fourth word hiding underneath, waiting.

"Whose?"

"We'd rather show you than tell you." A pause, and then, almost reluctantly: "Bring your coat. The one with the pockets. Actually—" and here the voice did something strange, something that sounded almost like discomfort, "—bring identification. And don't touch anything when you get here."

* * *

Marleigh Cut was twenty minutes east of the city, where the canal gave up pretending to be picturesque and settled into its working middle age: rusted lock gates, a defunct coal yard converted into luxury flats nobody could afford, herons standing in the shallows with the patience of creditors. The police tape had already gone up by the time Tamsin arrived, fluttering in a wind that came straight off the water and smelled of silt and diesel and something else, something organic and wrong that Tamsin's hindbrain identified before their conscious mind caught up.

They had covered scenes like this before—from a distance, respectfully, through the lens of true crime rather than the lens of grief. They knew the choreography: the white-suited SOCOs moving with the unhurried precision of people who had all the time in the world because the man on the ground had none; the uniformed constables keeping gawkers back; the tent, blue and white, pitched over the worst of it like a bad joke about a picnic.

But knowing the choreography didn't prepare them for the moment a young constable lifted the tape and Tamsin ducked under it, and every head on the towpath turned at once, and they understood—with a lurch that started in the stomach and finished somewhere behind the sternum—that today they were not the observer. Today they were the story.

"You made good time." Detective Sergeant Idris Okafor was waiting for them just short of the tent. He was a tall man built along the lines of a door—broad, flat, immovable—with close-cropped hair going grey at the temples and eyes the colour of wet slate. He looked at Tamsin the way a vet looks at an animal brought in by an owner who has clearly been feeding it the wrong things: professional concern layered over a private conviction that this was going to be more trouble than it was worth.

"Tamsin Amberdale." They offered a hand. He looked at it for a moment too long, then shook it briefly, as if completing a formality he considered optional.

"I know who you are. My daughter listens to your show." He said *your show* the way some people said *your generation* or *your music*. "Forty-seven cases closed. Impressive numbers."

"They're not numbers to me."

"No," he agreed, in a tone that suggested he thought they absolutely were. "Before we go any further, Mr.—Ms.—"

"Tamsin is fine."

"*Tamsin.*" He tried the name out and visibly decided against it. "Before we go further, I want to be clear about why you're here. This is a courtesy. A professional courtesy, extended because your name came up in connection with certain items found at the scene, and because I would rather you hear it from me than from a press leak. It is not an invitation to podcast. If you record anything, photograph anything, or speak to any of my officers beyond what I permit, that courtesy ends and you become what you actually are, which is a member of the public standing at a cordon. Are we clear?"

"Crystal," Tamsin said, and meant it, and hated how much they meant it. There was a version of themselves—a younger version, the one who'd started the show in a bedsit with a secondhand mic—who would have bristled at being spoken to like that. The current version felt only a cold, spreading relief that someone official was finally taking the shape of things seriously. "Why am I here, Sergeant?"

Okafor studied them for a long moment. Whatever he was looking for, he apparently found enough of it, because he turned and led them toward the tent.

"I'm going to ask you to look at the deceased," he said. "Specifically, at what he's wearing. And then I'm going to ask you some questions, and I'd like you to answer them honestly, because I'll know if you don't. I've listened to forty-seven episodes in the last three days. You have tells."

"That's deeply unsettling."

"Yes," he said, without a flicker of humour. "It is."

He pulled back the flap of the tent.

* * *

The dead man lay on his side on the towpath, one arm flung forward as though reaching for something just past his fingertips, the other tucked beneath him. He was perhaps sixty, silver-haired, dressed in a good wool overcoat that had done nothing to keep the canal's damp out of him. His face was composed in the way faces sometimes were after drowning—not peaceful, exactly, but finished, a sentence ended with a full stop instead of a question mark.

And he was wearing Tamsin's cardigan.

Not a cardigan like Tamsin's cardigan. Not a similar cardigan, not a cardigan from the same high-street shop in a similar shade of moss green. It was *the* cardigan—the one with the dropped shoulder seams and the small burn mark near the left cuff from a candle incident during episode thirty-one, the one Tamsin had worn in the promotional photos for the anniversary special, the one that had vanished from their flat sometime in the last few months along with a grey lambswool scarf, two pairs of socks, and a feeling, growing ever since, that their home had developed a draught no surveyor could find.

The scarf was there too, wound once around the dead man's neck, tucked into the cardigan with a neatness that made Tamsin's skin crawl. Someone had dressed him. Someone had stood over this body—or arranged it before rigor set in—and put Tamsin's clothes on him the way a parent dresses a child for school. Carefully. Familiarly.

The world went narrow and loud and very far away, all at once. Tamsin heard Okafor saying something about time of death, about the canal, about hypothermia versus immersion, and the words arrived like radio through static. They were aware, distantly, of their own hands—of having put them in their coat pockets, of gripping the lining so hard the stitching complained—and of the fact that they were breathing in a controlled, deliberate rhythm that they recognized, with clinical detachment, as the breathing they coached interviewees through before difficult testimony.

"Amberdale." Okafor's voice cut through. "Look at me. Is that yours?"

"Yes." Their voice came out steadier than they felt. Good. Eleven years of practice. "That's my cardigan. That's my scarf. I reported nothing missing because I didn't know anything was missing—I noticed gaps in my wardrobe around February, assumed I'd mislaid things during a move of studio equipment, and let it go. Apparently I shouldn't have let it go."

"When did you last see these items?"

"In my flat. On my body. In recordings—I wear the cardigan on camera sometimes, listeners joke about it, there's a whole thread on the forum about 'the moss cardigan.' You can date it from the burn mark. Episode thirty-one, March of last year, candle, my own stupid fault."

He wrote that down. "Your flat. Who has access?"

"My producer, Wren Halloway. She has a key for emergencies. My building's super. And—" Tamsin stopped. Counted. Found the list shorter than comfort allowed. "That's it. That should be it."

"*Should* be."

"There's no *should* about locked doors, Sergeant. There's only locks, and people, and the space between them." They heard themselves say it and realized it was a line from the show, episode twelve, the Hollis case. Okafor's expression flickered—he'd heard it too, recently, probably more than once, probably at double speed while doing the washing up.

"Anyone else?" he pressed. "Cleaner? Partner? Family?"

"No partner. No family worth the word." Something moved through Tamsin then, quick and cold, a fish turning in dark water. "One uncle. Legal guardian, once upon a time. He has a key to the old house, not to my flat. Why? Do you think someone's been inside?"

Okafor didn't answer immediately. He walked them a few paces along the towpath, away from the tent, away from the ears of the young constable, and stopped where the light was flat and grey and showed nothing kindly.

"The deceased is Edmund Pryce," he said. "Solicitor, retired, lived alone in Fenwick. No next of kin local. We pulled his file this morning and your name is in it—not recently. Thirty years ago. You were a child. He acted for the estate of an R. Amberdale in probate matters dating from 1994."

The canal seemed to get louder. Or quieter. Tamsin couldn't have said which.

"Rosalind," they said. "Rosalind Amberdale. My aunt. She died the year I was nine. She fell—at Halloran House, the family place. Everyone said she fell."

"And Pryce handled the paperwork after." Okafor watched them with those slate eyes, missing nothing. "Here's where we are, Amberdale, and I'll be plain because I suspect you prefer plain. A man is dead, dressed in clothes taken from your home over what our forensics suggest was a period of months—which means someone had repeated access to your flat, or you're lying to me, and I don't think you're lying. The same man's files connect him to your family's estate. And you are, as of this morning, the most famous person in this county whose wardrobe is at a crime scene. So my questions are going to get less comfortable from here, and you're going to answer them anyway. First one: why does a solicitor who died wearing your cardigan have your name in a thirty-year-old probate file?"

Tamsin looked back toward the tent, toward the moss-green wool gone stiff and dark with canal water, toward the scarf wound with such terrible care around a stranger's throat.

Because the case wasn't cold, they thought. The case had been warm the whole time, warm as a body, warm as breath on the back of the neck. Someone had been in their flat, in their closet, in their life, folding themselves into the spaces between the hangers, and they had never once heard the door.

"Sergeant," Tamsin said slowly, "I think I need to tell you about a voicemail I got last week. About a witness who disappeared in 1994. And I think you need to tell me everything Pryce kept in those files. Because I have a feeling we've been working the same case from opposite ends, and only one of us knew it."

Okafor closed his notebook. For the first time since the towpath, something shifted behind the slate-grey eyes—not warmth, not yet, but the recognition of a fellow professional staring into the same dark room.

"Start talking," he said. "And Amberdale—walk me through every person who's ever had a key to your life. Every single one. Because somebody dressed that man this morning in clothes they took off your shelf, and until I know who, everyone who loves you is a suspect. Including whoever taught you that breathing trick."

Tamsin opened their mouth to answer—and stopped. Because across the water, on the far towpath where no police tape flew and no constable stood guard, a figure in a long dark coat stood watching them. Watching *them*, not the scene, not the body. Even at that distance, even across the grey ribbon of the canal, Tamsin could feel the attention of it like a hand laid flat against the back of the neck.

By the time they turned to point, the towpath was empty.

Only a single grey feather rocked on the water where the figure had stood, turning slowly, like a signature on a letter no one had signed.
