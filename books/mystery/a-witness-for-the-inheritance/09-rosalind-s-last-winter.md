## 9. Rosalind's Last Winter

The county archive smelled of cold paper and older secrets—a dry, mineral chill that settled into the lungs like the air of a place that had been holding its breath for decades. It lived in the basement of the old assize courts on Wexford Street, down a flight of stone steps worn concave by two centuries of clerks and solicitors and grieving widows come to read what the law had written about their dead.

Tamsin signed the visitors' ledger with a false name out of habit, then crossed it out and wrote their real one. *Amberdale.* If this case had taught them anything in the past fortnight, it was that hiding was no longer an option. Someone had been dressing a corpse in their clothes. Someone had been walking through their flat while they slept. Anonymity was a coat they'd already lost; there was no point mourning it.

The archivist was a small woman named Mrs. Pell with reading glasses on a chain and the unhurried manner of someone who had spent forty years watching people find things they wished they hadn't. She took Tamsin's request slip—*Rosalind Amberdale, personal papers, 1993–1994*—and studied it over the top of her spectacles.

"Deposited by the estate solicitors," she said. "Pryce & Halloran." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "Before Mr. Pryce's death, I should say."

"You knew him?"

"I knew his handwriting." She said it the way other people said *I knew his sins.* "Box fourteen. You'll have until four o'clock. Gloves are on the trolley. And—" she held the slip a moment longer, her eyes flicking up, sharp and quick as a wren's, "—the papers were catalogued twice. Once when they arrived, once last month, after the police came. Some items are noted as missing."

"Missing," Tamsin repeated.

"Letters, mostly. From the winter of '94." Mrs. Pell set the slip down and folded her hands. "I only mention it because you're the second person to ask for them this year, and the first one asked about the missing ones too."

"Who was the first?"

"I'm not permitted to say." But something in her face—something almost apologetic, almost kind—suggested that if Tamsin came back at closing time and bought her a cup of tea from the machine upstairs, permission might be found to bend.

The box waited on a scarred oak table beneath a green-shaded lamp, and Tamsin sat before it for a full minute without touching it, the way one sits outside a door one is afraid to open. This was the trouble with true crime, they thought—not the corpses, not the liars, but this: the moment when the archive stops being research and starts being family. When the file folder has your own surname on it in faded ink.

They pulled on the cotton gloves and lifted the lid.

* * *

Rosalind Amberdale had written letters the way she'd done everything, according to family legend—thoroughly, elegantly, and with an iron will disguised as good manners. The early ones were mundane enough to ache: notes to a seed merchant about rose cuttings, a complaint to the water board, a long affectionate scold to a cousin in Canada about the price of wool. Her handwriting slanted forward like a woman leaning into wind.

Then came November 1994, and the handwriting changed.

It tightened. The elegant slant straightened, as though she had gripped the pen too hard, or as though the wind she leaned into had turned against her. Tamsin spread the letters across the table in chronological order and read them twice through, and by the second reading their hands had gone cold inside the gloves.

*My dear Celia,* the first began, dated the ninth of November—three weeks before Aunt Rosalind fell from the east terrace, three weeks before Marlowe Vane walked out of history.

*I write this knowing you will burn it, as you burned the others, and I rely upon it absolutely.*

*There is a matter at the house which I cannot put in a telephone call and will not put in front of Edmund, who means well but whose discretion is a professional instrument rather than a virtue. He keeps confidences the way a bank keeps gold—for a fee, and against a rainy day.*

*A man has been coming to the gate. Not a beggar and not a reporter, whatever he says. He knows things about the family that are not in any newspaper. He knows about the child.*

Tamsin stopped. Read it again. *He knows about the child.*

Which child? Tamsin had been born in March of 1994. They would have been eight months old that November. Their mother, Lark Amberdale, had left the country the following spring—the official story was a teaching post abroad, then silence, then the quiet erasure that families perform on the inconveniently departed. Tamsin had grown up on Rosalind's knee and Uncle Ambrose's stories, told that their mother was flighty, restless, unsuited to motherhood. That she had chosen the wide world over her own daughter.

*The child,* Rosalind wrote, and Tamsin's pulse ticked in their throat.

*He says he saw something on the night of the twelfth of October. I do not believe him entirely—he is a man who trades in belief—but I believe enough. Enough that if he speaks to the wrong person, everything we have built to keep that little girl safe will come down around us like a shot rookery.*

That little girl. Tamsin set the letter down. Their gloves left faint damp prints on the acid-free tissue. Around them the archive hummed its fluorescent hum; somewhere above, a door slammed, and the whole building seemed to shudder and settle.

They picked the letter up again.

*Celia, you will ask me why I do not simply go to the police. I will tell you, since you cannot answer: because the police are the last thing I want near this. Because what he claims to have seen, if it is true, does not merely embarrass this family—it unmakes it. Because there is a version of events in which my sister-in-law did not abandon her child but was taken from it, and if that version reaches daylight before I am ready, the ones who arranged matters will make certain neither mother nor daughter lives to give evidence.*

*I am not being dramatic. I am being precise. There is a difference, and it is measured in funerals.*

Tamsin read that line four times. *Taken from it.* Not *left.* Not *chose elsewhere.* Taken.

Their whole childhood rearranged itself around the sentence like furniture in a dark room when the light comes on suddenly and everything is not where you thought. The flighty mother. The teaching post. The Christmas cards that stopped. Uncle Ambrose's careful, sorrowful explanations—*she loved the road more than us, Tamsin, some people are made that way*—delivered always with that particular gentleness he reserved for wounds he didn't want probed.

And underneath the gentleness, all these years, a lid.

* * *

The next letter was dated the twenty-third of November, and it was shorter, and it frightened Tamsin more.

*Celia —*

*He came again tonight. I met him at the gate myself, which Edmund advised against and which I therefore did. He is not mad. That is the worst of it. Madmen can be pitied and dismissed. This one is lucid and afraid, and fear makes men honest.*

*He says the person who went over the terrace wall in October was not the person everyone believes. He says he saw the face in the torchlight and it was not the face the coroner recorded. He says—and here I had to sit down on the cold stone, Celia, an old woman sitting on her own terrace steps like a chambermaid—that the wrong Amberdale died that night, or nearly died, and that the right one ran.*

*I have paid him. God forgive me, I have paid him, and I will pay him monthly, because a witness bought is a witness kept, and a witness kept is a child kept whole. Call it blackmail if you like. I call it a dowry paid to silence so that my brother's granddaughter may grow up believing her mother simply drove away.*

*Better a coward for a mother than a corpse for one. That is the arithmetic I have chosen. Do not write back. Burn this.*

Tamsin sat very still.

The lamp buzzed. Somewhere in the stacks a pipe ticked as the heating cycled. Outside, high windows showed a grey afternoon thickening toward rain, and Tamsin felt the thirty-year-old winter close around them like water.

*The wrong Amberdale died that night—or nearly died—and the right one ran.*

October the twelfth. Not November. The date was wrong—or rather, the date the world had been given was wrong. The coroner's record, the newspaper clippings, the family's own mournful anniversary—all of it fixed Rosalind's fatal fall in mid-November. But Rosalind herself, writing weeks before her death, spoke of a night in October when someone went over the terrace wall. When someone *nearly* died.

Two falls. Two dates. One story stitched over the other like a patch on a wound.

And Marlowe Vane—the witness who vanished in 1994, the name that had opened this whole case, the voice on the listener's voicemail saying *he never left town*—Marlowe Vane had stood at the estate gate and seen a face in torchlight that wasn't the face in the coroner's report. Had seen, perhaps, not Rosalind falling at all, but someone else. Someone running. Someone who was supposed to die and didn't.

Lark. Tamsin's mother.

The thought arrived not as a revelation but as a physical event—a pressure behind the sternum, a ringing in the ears, the archive tilting slightly on its foundations. Tamsin gripped the edge of the table and breathed, the way they'd learned to breathe in a hundred interview rooms with a hundred grieving families: slow, counted, deliberate. You could fall apart later. Falling apart was a scheduled activity. Right now there were letters.

* * *

There were eleven letters in the box from that winter. Mrs. Pell had said some were missing, and Tamsin counted the gaps by Rosalind's own references: a letter mentioned on the first of December that wasn't there; another referenced in January, absent; a third described in furious detail in February—*you will not tell me to go to the police again, Celia, I know exactly what the police would do with a dead witness and a living child and a codicil nobody was meant to read*—and gone.

A codicil. Tamsin's skin prickled. Okafor had let that word slip weeks ago, grudging as a man handing over a live coal: Pryce had been blackmailing heirs over a codicil that disinherited the Amberdale line. Tamsin had filed it away as motive. Now it glowed with a different light. A codicil nobody was meant to read. A beneficiary born the year Marlowe Vane vanished. A condition, according to the fragments Okafor had shared before shutting the drawer on the subject: that the beneficiary *come forward as witness.*

Come forward as witness. To what? To a death that hadn't happened the way the record said? To a mother who hadn't abandoned her child but fled a killer?

Every anonymous year of Tamsin's life—the pseudonym, the voice-only podcast, the flat with the good locks, the careful habit of never photographing well—rearranged itself again, less comfortably this time. Not just a personality. A design. Someone had built a hiding place around them brick by brick, and Rosalind's letters suggested the bricks had been mortared with hush money and fear.

The last letter in the box was dated the eleventh of November, 1994—no. Tamsin checked. The tenth of November, 1995. Nearly a year after the others. Rosalind's handwriting had loosened again, gone soft and tired, an old woman's hand at last.

*Celia,*

*I am tired in a way sleep does not touch. The man at the gate—I have learned his name at last, and I will not write it even here, even for you, because names are how these things get found—is asking for more than money now. He wants to testify. He says his conscience wakes him. I tell him conscience is a luxury of the safe and he is not safe, and he looks at me with such pity, Celia, such terrible pity, as if I am the one who needs saving.*

*Perhaps I am. I have spent a year building a fortress out of lies and calling it love. The child laughs at nothing, the way children do, and every time I hear her I think: this laughter stands on a buried thing, and someday the ground will give.*

*If it gives while I am alive, I will tell her everything. I owe her that. If it gives after—then whoever finds these words, find her. Tell her her mother did not leave. Tell her her mother was the bravest person at Amberdale, and that the fall everyone mourned was not the fall that mattered.*

*Burn this. But if you do not—if you keep them all, as I suspect you do, sentimental old bird—then keep them for her.*

*Rosalind*

Three days later, Rosalind Amberdale went over the east terrace wall. Or was helped. Or stepped. Or—Tamsin's mind supplied, unhelpfully, with the cold clarity of thirty years' hindsight—was pushed by someone who had finally decided the fortress was worth more standing than the woman who built it.

The archive clock said ten past four. Rain streaked the high windows. Tamsin photographed every letter, both sides, then sat a moment longer with the last one, gloved fingers hovering above the ink as if warmth might still rise off it, as if the woman who wrote it might feel herself finally, after three decades, being believed.

"You've found something," Mrs. Pell said from the end of the table. Not a question. She'd materialized with the soundless competence of her profession, a trolley at her elbow.

"Mrs. Pell. The first person who asked about the missing letters. The one you're not permitted to name." Tamsin looked up. "Was it an old man? Sixties, maybe seventies. Careful with his words. Eyes like he's been awake a long time."

Something moved behind the librarian's glasses—pity, or its close cousin. "It was a woman," she said. "Middle-aged. Said she was doing family history." She paused. "She signed the ledger *C. Vane.*"

The name landed like a stone dropped down a well. Tamsin listened for it to hit bottom, and heard instead—far below, faint as a memory of a memory—the click of a floorboard in an empty room.

"Celia Vane," Tamsin said slowly. "Marlowe Vane's wife?"

"That I couldn't say." Mrs. Pell began wheeling the trolley away, then stopped. "But I'll tell you what I can, since you've been polite about my gloves. That woman didn't look like family history. She looked like someone checking whether the fire was still burning." She glanced at the box. "And she left something in the return slot. We're obliged to hold deposited items for collection. Nobody's collected it."

She produced a small envelope, yellowed, sealed with a crackle of ancient glue, and laid it on the table between them. On the front, in Rosalind Amberdale's unmistakable forward-leaning hand:

*For the child, when she comes asking.*
