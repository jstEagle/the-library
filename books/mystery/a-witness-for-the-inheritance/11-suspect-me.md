## 11. Suspect: Me

The folder landed on Idris Okafor's desk at 7:42 in the morning, and he knew before he opened it that the day had already gone wrong.

It was the weight of it. A thin case was a whisper; this one had been bound with a binder clip thick as a thumb, tabbed, cross-referenced, annotated in the tidy hand of someone who had been awake all night building a cathedral of suspicion. Detective Inspector Hargreaves stood on the other side of the desk with her arms folded, watching him the way she watched suspects through one-way glass.

"Read it," she said. "Then we'll talk."

Okafor read it. He read it twice, because the first time through, his eyes kept snagging on the same three exhibits like a tongue returning to a broken tooth.

Exhibit A: the cardigan. Soft grey wool, moth-eaten at the left cuff, recovered from the body of Edmund Pryce where it had been fished out of the canal eleven days ago. The label was from a small shop in Camden that sold to maybe two hundred customers a year. The victim's own clothes were folded neatly on the towpath beside him — folded, which meant undressed and redressed, which meant ritual or message or both. And the cardigan belonged, by the shop's purchase records and three separate witness statements, to Tamsin Amberdale.

Exhibit B: the fingerprints. Pryce's office had been searched after his death — not ransacked, *curated*, Okafor thought now, looking at the crime scene photographs again with new eyes. One drawer emptied. One file removed. And on the surviving files, on the leather folio that had held them, on the brass handle of the drawer itself: prints belonging to Tamsin Amberdale, clear enough to run, fresh enough to date within days of the murder.

Exhibit C: the timeline. Amberdale had interviewed Bess Carnow on a Tuesday. Pryce had died on the following Saturday. Carnow's statement — the recanted one, the one that had started unraveling thirty years of careful stitching — existed only because a podcaster with a microphone and a grudge against closed doors had gone knocking.

Hargreaves let him sit with it. She was good at that. Twenty-two years in the job and she'd learned that silence did more work than any question.

"Well?" she said finally.

Okafor closed the folder. He was aware of his own face doing what faces do — settling into neutrality, arranging itself into the mask you wore when your private thoughts and your professional obligations had stopped speaking to each other.

"It's circumstantial," he said.

"It's a pattern."

"It's a story," he said. "Somebody's telling it very well."

"Somebody always is." Hargreaves pulled out the chair across from him and sat, which she almost never did, which meant this was not a conversation between colleagues but a conversation between ranks. "Idris. The Super wants Amberdale brought in. Formally. Not for a friendly chat — for an interview under caution. There's pressure from above the Super, if you want the truth of it, and the truth of it is that a dead solicitor wearing a celebrity's clothes is the kind of thing that sells papers, and the Chief Constable reads papers."

"And what do you want?"

She looked at him for a long moment. Whatever she saw there, she didn't flinch from it.

"I want you to tell me why I'm wrong," she said. "Because I've read this folder four times and I can't find the hole in it, and that worries me more than the folder does. You've spent three weeks closer to this case than anyone. So either you're too close to see it, or you're too close to say it. Which is it?"

* * *

He drove out to the canal afterward without deciding to. His hands made the decision somewhere around the second roundabout, and by the time his mind caught up he was parked in the gravel pull-off above the water, engine ticking, looking down at the spot where they'd found Edmund Pryce floating in his dead man's borrowed cardigan.

The canal was low and green and indifferent. A heron stood in the shallows with the patience of something that had never once doubted itself. Okafor envied it.

Here was the problem, laid out as plainly as he could lay it: the evidence said Tamsin Amberdale killed Edmund Pryce. The evidence said it in a clear voice, with footnotes. Clothes taken from Amberdale's flat over months — months, the forensics report said, not hours; someone with repeated access, someone patient. Prints on the missing files. Proximity to every witness who'd died or recanted. A podcast that had, functionally, published a hit list: here are the people holding up the wall of a thirty-year lie, and here is when I'll be talking to each of them.

And underneath all of it, quiet as silt, was the other thing. The thing that had been bothering him since the second week, since the gatehouse fire, since the scorched cassette he wasn't supposed to know about — the thing he had been carrying around like a stone in his shoe, telling himself he'd deal with it properly, soon, in the right way, through channels.

Tamsin Amberdale hadn't stolen those clothes. Someone had *given* them to the killer. Someone who wanted the world to look at Tamsin.

He'd known it the way you know a room has been rearranged — not by seeing the change but by feeling the wrongness of it. Killers dressed their victims in their own clothes, or in nothing, or in whatever was at hand. Nobody dressed a corpse in a specific stranger's wardrobe unless the stranger was the message. And messages required an audience that would understand them. Tamsin Amberdale understood them. That was the whole point.

Which cut both ways. It always did.

His phone buzzed. Wren Okonkwo — no relation, a joke between them that had stopped being funny sometime last week — texting him a single line: *Nightjar's IP came back to the gatehouse. You need to see what was on that wall before the fire took it.*

He stared at the message for a long time. Then he typed back: *Not on the phone.* And then, because loyalty was a muscle and it was getting its workout today: *Tomorrow. Bring everything.*

He put the phone face-down on the passenger seat and watched the heron strike the water and come up with nothing.

* * *

The interview room at the station smelled of old coffee and floor polish, and Tamsin Amberdale sat in it looking — Okafor searched for the word and settled, reluctantly, on *unarmored*. No studio lighting, no producer hovering with a recorder, no audience of forty thousand listeners to perform composure for. Just a person in a chair, hands wrapped around a paper cup of tea they hadn't touched, watching him with the flat, level attention of someone who had spent years reading other people's tells and knew theirs were being read in return.

"This isn't under caution," Okafor said, closing the door. "Yet. I argued it down to this. You get one hour of my discretion. Use it well."

"Understood." A pause. "Thank you. For arguing."

"Don't thank me yet." He sat, opened the folder, and turned it so the photographs faced them. "Tell me about the cardigan."

"I told the uniformed officers—"

"You told them what you tell everyone. Polished. Complete. Rehearsed." He held up a hand before they could bristle. "I'm not insulting you. I'm telling you it doesn't work on me. So let's try the version you haven't polished. When did you first notice things going missing?"

Tamsin set down the cup. Something shifted behind their eyes — not fear exactly. Recalculation.

"Months ago," they said. "Small things. A scarf in February. The cardigan in — I want to say April, but I honestly can't swear to it, because I assumed I'd lost it. Misplaced. You don't report a missing cardigan, Sergeant. You feel briefly annoyed and buy another one."

"But eventually you noticed a pattern."

"Eventually I noticed a pattern." They leaned forward slightly. "And here's the part that should be in your folder and isn't, because I didn't understand it until later. The things that went missing weren't random. They were things I *wore*. Things that photographed well. Things that were recognizably mine. Whoever took them wasn't stealing a wardrobe. They were collecting a costume."

"That's consistent with someone framing you."

"It's consistent with someone who knows how I think," Tamsin said quietly. "Who knows what I'd notice and when I'd notice it. Who has been in my flat, Idris. More than once. While I slept, probably. And who wanted me to spend weeks half-noticing, the way you half-notice a sound you've heard your whole life." Their hands tightened around the cup. "You don't do that out of hatred. Hatred is fast. This was — curatorial."

Okafor wrote nothing down. He watched them instead, and what he saw was exhaustion sitting on top of grief sitting on top of a ferocious, banked intelligence that refused, even now, even here, to stop working the problem.

"The fingerprints," he said.

"On Pryce's files. Yes." A short, humorless breath. "I handled those files. In his office, three weeks before he died. He showed them to me himself — dangled them, really, the way a man dangles bait when he wants to find out what kind of fish you are. I touched the folio. I touched the drawer. I was wearing gloves in my own memory of it, but memory lies, and apparently so do gloves, or I never wore them at all. Either way, they're mine. I won't insult you by denying it."

"Why would Pryce show you estate files? Your family's files?"

"Because he was selling something," Tamsin said. "He was always selling. And I think — I *think*, I can't prove it — that what he was selling, in the end, was me."

The clock on the wall ticked. Somewhere down the corridor a door slammed, and the sound of it went through the little room like a bullet through water.

"Here's my problem," Okafor said. "Everything you just said is plausible. None of it is provable. And meanwhile I have a folder this thick" — he tapped the binder clip — "that says you're our best suspect, and a Chief Constable who'd like this solved before it becomes a documentary about us instead of you. If I bring you in formally, the machine takes over. Custody. Solicitors. Press. You know how it goes."

"I know exactly how it goes. I've covered forty-seven of them."

"Forty-eight," Okafor said. "This one makes forty-eight. And here's what I need from you, and I need you to hear it as a copper saying it, not a friend, because I'm not sure anymore which one I am." He met their eyes. "Stay reachable. Don't leave the county. Don't record anything about the interview, don't broadcast anything about today, and don't go anywhere near that estate without telling me first. Can you do that?"

"Yes," Tamsin said. Then, softer: "Idris. Whoever built this frame knew I'd end up in a room like this someday. They've been rehearsing it longer than I have. Please tell me you're looking for the rehearsal."

He stood, gathered the folder, and paused at the door with his hand on the frame.

"One more thing," he said, and heard his own voice do something it had never done in an interview room: hesitate. "The forensics on the cardigan. Fibers, wear patterns, the works. There's a secondary analysis running. It'll take another week."

That much was true.

What he did not say — what he would carry home tonight like contraband in his coat pocket — was that the secondary analysis had come back yesterday morning. That the cardigan's left cuff, the moth-eaten one, had been *repaired*. Recently. With thread that matched nothing in Tamsin Amberdale's flat, mended by hands that were not theirs, by someone who had worn the garment long enough to care for it. A dead man's cardigan, pre-worn by his killer. And a detail like that — a detail that pointed away from the person in the folder and toward whoever had actually lived in those clothes — was sitting right now in an email attachment marked *pending review*, and Idris Okafor had marked it pending himself, with his own credentials, at 6:15 this morning, twenty minutes before Hargreaves walked into his office.

Duty said: disclose. Duty said: the truth doesn't belong to you, it belongs to the process, and the moment you start editing the process you're just a better-dressed liar than the ones you hunt.

Loyalty said: the process is currently being steered by pressure from above, toward the easiest suspect, on a timetable set by newspapers. Loyalty said: the moment that fiber analysis surfaces, whoever repaired that cuff will burn every trace of themselves, and the one person who can lead you to them — the one person the killer is *dressing corpses for* — will be locked in custody while it happens.

He had crossed lines before. Small ones. A hunch pursued off-book, a name withheld from a press briefing. Every copper had a drawer full of those. But this was different. This was him standing between the machine and a suspect, choosing with his own hands what the machine got to see, and there was no unseeing it, no undoing it, no version of tomorrow where he was the man he'd been yesterday.

He walked out of the interview room and down the corridor, and Hargreaves was waiting by the vending machines with two coffees and an expression that said she already knew.

"Well?" she said.

"Secondary analysis isn't back," he said. "Week, maybe more. Nothing conclusive yet."

She studied him for three full seconds — long enough for him to feel every degree of the temperature drop between them.

"You're a straight arrow, Okafor," she said at last, handing him a coffee. "It's why I trust you. Don't make me regret the coffee."

He took it. His hand was steady. He was almost impressed by that.

* * *

That night he sat in his kitchen with the case file spread across the table like a tarot spread, and he did the thing he'd been avoiding for a week: he listened to the podcast.

All of it. Back episodes, the whole archive, forty-seven closed cases narrated in that calm, deliberate voice — a voice built, he realized now, not for entertainment but for testimony. Tamsin Amberdale hadn't been making a show. They'd been building a witness stand, one episode at a time, waiting for the day someone would need it.

And then the current season. The Vane disappearance. Rosalind Amberdale's fall. The alibi network stitched together by three families around one estate. Episode by episode, Tamsin taking apart the lie with the patience of a surgeon and the tenderness of someone who'd grown up inside the wound.

Near midnight, he reached the episode recorded two days after Pryce's death. Two-thirds through, there it was: a sound in the background, faint, half a second long. A floorboard creaking in a room Tamsin had said, on air, was empty.

Okafor played it eleven times. He isolated it, amplified it, ran it against the acoustic profile of Tamsin's building that Wren had helpfully — recklessly — posted online. The creak came from the front room. The recording rig was in the studio. Between the two rooms: a hallway, and a window over the fire escape.

Someone had been in the flat while the microphone was live. Someone close enough to be caught, careless enough to creak, confident enough not to care.

Or confident enough to *want* to be caught — to plant the idea of an intruder, to build the alternative narrative, to give the eventual defense its reasonable doubt.

Okafor sat back. The kitchen was dark except for the laptop's glow, and in that glow his reflection looked like a stranger wearing his face: a man who had hidden evidence that morning and was now doing amateur audio forensics at midnight for a suspect he'd officially cautioned, and who could no longer draw the line between investigating a frame-up and becoming part of one.

His phone lit up on the table. Wren.

*Got the wall photos developed. You need to see the child's drawing. Tonight if possible.*

He looked at the message for a long time. Then at the folder. Then at the window, black and reflective, where his own face hung over the sleeping street like a watcher at a window of its own.

*Coming,* he typed.

And as he pulled on his coat, he understood that whatever was on that wall — whatever a child had drawn with a careful hand and signed with two letters — it had already decided something for him. Because there were two men he could be tomorrow. The one in the folder, straight and punctual and finished by Christmas.

Or the one walking out the door now, carrying a secret in each pocket, betting the whole case on a podcaster's innocence and a dead woman's drawing.

He locked his kitchen door behind him, and did not look back.
