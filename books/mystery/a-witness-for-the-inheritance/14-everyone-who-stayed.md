## 14. Everyone Who Stayed

Wren had always believed the show's true architecture wasn't sound. It was people.

The studio at 3 a.m. looked like a mission control built by insomniacs: three monitors, two cold cups of tea, a corkboard that had outgrown its cork and was now mostly pins holding pins. Wren sat with headphones around their neck, not listening to anything, listening to everything—the hum of the server rack, the tick of Tamsin's ancient kettle in the next room, the particular silence of a person upstairs who hadn't slept properly in nine days and was pretending otherwise.

Tamsin was up there now, in the flat above the studio, behind a door Wren had insisted on replacing with one that locked from the inside. A deadbolt, actually. Wren had installed it themselves on Tuesday, kneeling on the landing with a screwdriver while Tamsin watched from the doorway with an expression Wren couldn't file under any of their usual categories—not gratitude, not fear, something closer to bewilderment that anyone would bother.

"You didn't have to do that," Tamsin had said.

"I know," Wren said, and kept screwing.

That was the whole conversation, and it was one of the most important ones they'd ever had.

Now Wren opened the fan network's private channel—*The Night Desk*, twelve hundred verified members, every one of them vetted through a process Wren had designed over six years and guarded like a dragon over gold—and pinned a new message to the top of the board.

**AMBERDALE PROTOCOL. Effective immediately. Read all of it before you post.**

They typed for forty minutes without stopping. The rules were simple and absolute: no one contacted any person connected to the case directly. No one visited the estate. No one posted anything publicly, not even a hint, not even obliquely. All research happened here, in the dark, where the killer—if the killer was watching, and Wren was increasingly certain someone was—would see nothing but ordinary traffic. And one more rule, the one Wren wrote last and meant most:

**Nobody works alone. If you have a lead, you post it before you follow it. If you go anywhere, you tell two people where and when you'll be back. This is not a suggestion. This is how we keep everyone alive.**

They hesitated over the word *alive*. Then they left it.

The replies began within minutes, because the Night Desk never truly slept—it just changed shifts, like a hospital, like a lighthouse. Wren read them as they came in, and with each one the knot in their chest loosened by a single degree.

*Halloway-47* first, as always: retired Detective Inspector Margaret Halloway, seventy-one, of the old county force, who had joined the network three years ago after writing Wren a letter that began *I spent thirty years watching cases die of neglect and your show is the only coroner I trust.* She wrote now: **Understood. I'm requesting my old colleague's notes on the 1994 inquest through legitimate channels. I know which drawer they're in. Give me 48 hours.**

Then *ArchivistAnselm*: **The county records office opens at nine. I've already emailed about the planning permissions for the estate grounds, 1990–1996. Groundskeeping records tell you who had keys. Keys tell you who could move at night without breaking anything.**

*Quill-and-Vellum*, who transcribed every episode into accessible formats and had once corrected Wren's timeline of a 1978 poisoning with such gentle precision that Wren had hired them on the spot: **Starting a shared document for the 1994 timeline. Hour by hour. Every statement, every claimed sighting of Marlowe Vane, cross-referenced against weather records—we had snow that November, don't let anyone forget it, snow holds footprints and lies.**

And beneath them, dozens more. *Lanternist*, a night-shift nurse who listened on her breaks. *OldRoadsPete*, a delivery driver who knew every lane in the county the way other men knew football scores. *Moth-and-Candle*, who never gave a name or location and whose contributions were nonetheless so precise that Wren privately suspected a second career in intelligence. Twelve hundred people who had never met Tamsin Amberdale, who knew them only as a voice that refused to let the forgotten stay forgotten, and who would now, without being asked twice, stand between that voice and whatever was coming for it.

Wren sat back and let the warmth of it move through them like a tide. It was easy, in this work, to believe the world was made entirely of Bess Carnows—witnesses who recanted, neighbors who saw nothing, families who closed ranks around a lie until the lie became load-bearing. Thirty years of that. But then there was this: a retired inspector pulling favours at dawn, a nurse checking the channel between rounds, a stranger drawing a map of lanes on a screen at four in the morning because somewhere a person they'd never met was afraid in their own home.

The killer had dismantled an alibi network built on fear and money. Wren intended to build a better one out of the opposite materials.

* * *

By seven, the kitchen smelled of coffee and toast, and Tamsin came down the stairs looking like a document that had been left out in the rain. Wren pushed a mug across the table without comment and turned the laptop toward them.

"Sit down. You're going to want to see this."

Tamsin sat. They drank. They read. Wren watched their face move through it—the flicker of surprise at Halloway's message, the longer stillness at the timeline document, already populated with forty-one entries, each one sourced and timestamped.

"This is—" Tamsin stopped. Started again. "Wren, some of these people have jobs. Lives."

"They have a show," Wren said. "You built it. You think you made a podcast? You made a congregation." They tapped the screen. "Halloway thinks the 1994 inquest notes were never fully catalogued—she says the coroner's clerk that year was sloppy and half the witness statements went into a miscellany box. Anselm is pulling the estate's staff rosters. Quill has found three separate sightings of Marlowe Vane in the week before Rosalind died that were never followed up, including a bus ticket."

"A bus ticket?"

"Purchased at the station in Ashcombe, November the ninth, 1994. One way to the coast. Never used—or used and returned, we can't tell yet. But Pete remembers—" Wren allowed themselves a small smile. "Pete, who was a boy then, remembers a man matching Vane's description working the winter at the garden centre off the ring road. Under another name. He's going back through his father's old wage books."

Tamsin stared at the screen. Their hand, Wren noticed, was trembling slightly around the mug—not fear this time, or not only fear. Something rawer.

"Thirty years," Tamsin said quietly. "Thirty years I thought nobody cared about that night except me. And there are forty people awake right now counting hours against the weather."

"Fifty-two," Wren said. "It grew while you were asleep. Which, incidentally, you should do again at some point."

"I slept."

"Three hours, horizontal, fully clothed, on top of the duvet. I heard the floorboards." Wren regretted it the moment they said it—hearing the floorboards was exactly the wrong phrase to use these days—but Tamsin only huffed something that was almost a laugh.

"The floorboards in *this* house are honest," they said. "I checked."

It was meant lightly. It landed heavily. They both let it sit there, unexamined, like a stone in a shoe you agree not to mention.

* * *

The day organized itself around the timeline. Wren ran the channel like an air traffic controller, routing information, flagging contradictions, keeping the tone warm and the discipline iron. By noon the 1994 document had grown to ninety entries and its first genuine shape was emerging, and it was a shape that made Wren's mouth go dry.

Because the timeline didn't show chaos. It showed choreography.

Every witness statement from that night aligned too neatly. Bess Carnow placing Marlowe Vane at the gate at nine o'clock. The gardener confirming he'd seen lights in the east wing—where no one had any reason to be. The family solicitor, Edmund Pryce, arriving to take statements within hours, before the police had even finished their first walk-through. Each account slotted into the next like joinery. Like something built.

"Halloway," Wren said into the channel, "when you get those inquest notes, look for gaps. Not what's there—what isn't. Someone edited this night. I want to know the editing style."

**On it,** she replied. **And Wren—one thing. The coroner's clerk I mentioned? He didn't retire. He died in 1997. Fell down his own stairs.**

Wren read that twice, and then walked upstairs and knocked on Tamsin's door, because some things you didn't leave sitting in a chat log.

* * *

Evening brought the thing Wren had been dreading and preparing for in equal measure: proof that the enemy was watching them back.

*Moth-and-Candle* posted at 9:14 p.m.: **Heads up. A new account requested membership tonight. Application is thorough—too thorough. References check out on paper, but one of the referees listed died in March. Either a clerical error or a ghost. Recommend rejection and watch.**

Wren rejected the application, screenshotted everything, and added a line to the protocol: **We are being probed. Expect more attempts. Vet everything twice.** Then they pulled up the network's access logs and did what they did best, the quiet forensic work of reading patterns in metadata—and found that over the past week, someone had been viewing the public episode pages at the same hour every night. 11:40 p.m. Always 11:40. The old broadcast slot, from the show's first season, kept out of habit by ten thousand listeners and, apparently, by one person for whom the habit was not nostalgia but surveillance.

"They're listening," Wren told Tamsin, who had come down at midnight and stood in the kitchen doorway with the wary look of someone who no longer trusted rooms to be empty. "Every night. Same time. Our watcher watches us watch the past."

"Then let's give them something to hear," Tamsin said, and there was something in their voice Wren hadn't heard since before Pryce's body came out of the canal—something with edges. "Next episode. We announce we're reopening Marlowe Vane. Officially. We say the word *witness* on air. We say the word *codicil*."

"Tamsin—"

"No. Listen." They crossed the kitchen, and for a moment Wren saw them clearly in the overhead light: exhausted, frightened, and done with being hunted in the dark. "This person killed Edmund Pryce and dressed him in my clothes to send me a message. They drowned Bess Carnow because she talked to me. Everything I do quietly gets answered loudly. So we stop being quiet. We make noise so loud they can't tell who's a threat anymore. Fifty-two people become five hundred. Let them try to find the signal in that."

Wren wanted to argue. Wren had a whole argument prepared, in fact, complete with risk assessments. But they looked at Tamsin's face and understood that the argument wasn't really about safety. It was about whether Tamsin got to be a person who acted, or only a person who was acted upon. And Wren had built a career—a life—on the belief that people deserved their own voices back.

"Live mic in the gatehouse incident was my idea," Wren said slowly. "Fine. But we do it my way. We record tomorrow night, we air Thursday, and between now and then the Night Desk doubles down—nobody alone, everybody logged. And Tamsin?" They held their gaze. "If this flushes them out, things will move fast. When they move, I need you to promise me you'll do the boring thing. Lock the door. Call me. Wait."

Tamsin held the gaze for a long moment. Then, quietly: "I promise I'll try."

"That's the worst promise I've ever heard."

"It's the only kind I make honestly."

Despite everything—the dead solicitor, the drowned housekeeper, the burned gatehouse Wren still hadn't fully described, the cassette with a name on it that Tamsin wouldn't yet speak aloud—despite all of it, Wren laughed. A short, helpless sound, surprised out of them. Tamsin almost smiled back, and for a moment the kitchen was just a kitchen, and the two of them were just two tired people at the end of a very long day who had chosen, again and again, to be tired in the same room.

* * *

At 11:40 p.m., Wren sat alone in the studio with the board dimmed and the logs open, and watched the nightly visit arrive on schedule. One viewer. The episode pages. Fourteen minutes, as always. Wren imagined them somewhere in the dark of the county—perhaps in a warm house with photographs in drawers, perhaps somewhere colder—listening to the archive of a show that was closing in on them hour by hour, unaware that the archive was now also watching back.

Wren opened the channel and typed:

**Shift change. Timeline's at 112 entries. Halloway has the inquest box. Anselm found a second set of keys cut in 1991—cut for whom, we're finding out. Quill located the bus ticket's seller; she's alive, she's ninety, and she remembers faces. Tomorrow we go loud. Sleep if you can. Thank you—all of you—for staying.**

The replies came in like a tide coming home. **Still here.** **Still here.** **Wouldn't be anywhere else.** From a nurse on a break, an inspector with a box of dead men's papers, a driver mapping lanes in his head, a stranger with no name and perfect instincts. Twelve hundred small lights, and Wren sat in the glow of them and felt, for the first time since the phone call about the canal, something that was not fear.

Then a new message appeared at the bottom of the thread, from an account Wren didn't recognize—no application, no history, materializing inside the private channel like frost forming on glass.

No username. Just a single line:

**You're counting the wrong hours. Ask what happened at the well before Bess ever fell in.**

Wren stared at it. Refreshed. The account was gone—deleted, dissolved, as though it had never existed. Only the words remained, hanging in the channel among all those small loyal lights, cold at the center of the warmth.

Wren reached for the phone to call Tamsin, and stopped, and looked at the clock.

11:57 p.m.

Three minutes to the watcher's hour. Somewhere out there, someone had just spoken when they should have stayed silent—which meant either a mistake, or an invitation.

Wren picked up the phone anyway.
