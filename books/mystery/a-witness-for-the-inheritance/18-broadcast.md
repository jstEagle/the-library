## 18. Broadcast

Wren had always believed the studio was safest at night—the red light off, the faders down, the building humming its low electrical lullaby. Tonight the red light burned like a wound and every chair in the room felt occupied by ghosts.

They stood at the glass, watching Tamsin through it. Tamsin sat very still at the broadcast desk, headphones around their neck, both hands flat on the table as if holding down a lid that wanted to blow. The cassette—scorched at one corner, rescued from the gatehouse ash—lay beside the mixing board in an evidence bag Okafor had grudgingly released after copying it three times. Next to it, smaller and more terrible: the memory card from the lapel mic Wren had pressed into Tamsin's coat pocket eleven hours ago, with a shaking hand and a whispered *wear this or I'll never forgive you*.

Eleven hours ago, Tamsin had walked into Amberdale Mausoleum wearing Wren's microphone.

Now the file sat on the hard drive, 47 minutes and 12 seconds long, and nobody could agree on whether to let the world hear it.

"We're not airing it." Okafor stood by the door with his arms crossed, his coat still smelling of rain and old stone. He'd made the arrest two hours before dawn—Uncle Alistair, *Mr. Amberdale-Hurst*, the guardian, the protector—taken quietly at the estate's east lodge, no drama, just cuffs and a read-through of rights while fog curled off the lawns. "It's evidence. It's contaminated evidence, obtained in a situation where my suspect didn't know he was recorded, and if you put it on air before trial, I will personally—"

"You'll what?" Wren asked. Not cruelly. They were too tired for cruelty. "Arrest us? Add us to the file?"

Okafor's jaw worked. In the glass's reflection, Wren watched him look at Tamsin—at the set of their shoulders, the bruise blooming along their jaw where the crypt door had caught them—and saw something in the detective's face give way, the same slow structural failure Wren had been watching in him for weeks. Duty bending. Not breaking. Bending.

"I hid something," Okafor said abruptly.

The room went quiet. Even the servers seemed to hush.

"The fingerprints on Pryce's files. Yours, Tamsin—they matched, they were clean lifts, and the super wanted you charged the day Bess Carnow came out of that well. But the timestamps didn't work. Your prints were lifted from documents Pryce handled *after* your interview with him—the archive visit, the county records office. You touched those pages legally, days before he died. Someone transferred them. I knew that. I sat on it." He said it to the floor. "I crossed a line. I've been crossing lines since the canal. So don't tell me what evidence is worth. I know exactly what it's worth. I know what it costs."

Tamsin looked up at him through the glass. When they spoke, their voice was rough as gravel underfoot. "Then you understand why it has to go out."

"It'll end the prosecution."

"It might. Or it might be the only reason the CPS doesn't bury this in a plea deal and thirty years of sealed filings, like everything else in my family gets buried." Tamsin rose. Through the glass, Wren saw them press a palm flat against it, and Wren—because they were who they were, because eleven years of producing a show about truth had taught them that walls are just things you route sound around—came out of the booth and met the palm with their own.

"Forty-seven cases," Tamsin said softly. "Every single one, we put it out. Unedited. We never decided for the audience what they could handle. We never decided for the families, either—we asked them. So I'm asking."

Wren's throat closed. Because that was the thing about Tamsin Amberdale, the thing the listeners loved and the thing Wren had fallen for somewhere around case nineteen and never found the courage to name aloud: they didn't take. They asked. Every time. Even now, even hollowed out, even wearing the shape of a person whose whole life had turned out to be someone else's plot.

Wren turned to the room. The room was not empty—that was the point of it, that was the whole architecture of the last six years. Dev the archivist sat cross-legged on the couch with three laptops open, having driven four hours through the night when Wren's text came through. Marguerite, the retired detective inspector who'd been listening since episode one, stood by the kettle she'd refused to use because *this is not a kitchen, this is a newsroom*. And on the monitor mounted above the desk, a grid of forty small faces glowed: the core listener council, woken at 4 a.m., pixelated and bleary and absolutely present. The Nightjar thread veterans. The woman in Leeds who'd transcribed every episode into braille. The retired court stenographer who'd once caught a discrepancy in a 1991 inquest that cracked case thirty-one wide open.

The found family, assembled in pixels and pyjamas, waiting to be asked.

"We have the tape," Wren said to all of them. "Forty-seven minutes. A confession to two murders and to thirty years of engineering a child's life into hiding. We can air it tonight, unedited, before anyone can seal it, spin it, or make it disappear the way everything else in this story has disappeared. Or we can hold it, hand it to the lawyers, and trust a system that has failed every witness in this case so far." They took a breath. "We decide together. That's the rule. Say your piece."

Marguerite spoke first, arms folded, voice like a door closing. "Air it. Evidence that stays in a drawer is just paper. I spent thirty years watching drawers."

Dev, without looking up from a laptop: "I've already got the distribution mirrored across four servers in three jurisdictions. If anyone tries a takedown, it re-seeds faster than they can file. No offense, Detective."

"None taken," Okafor said grimly. "I'm being outvoted by infrastructure."

On the grid, face after face lit up. *Air it.* *Air it.* A man in his seventies, a former witness protection officer, leaned toward his camera: "I kept people hidden for a living. Hidden isn't safe. Hidden is just a slower kind of dead. Let her—" he corrected himself, gently, the way the community had learned to, "let *them* choose the light."

Tamsin laughed—a cracked, wet sound—and wiped their eyes with the heel of one hand.

"That's the vote," Wren said. Their heart was hammering against their ribs like something trying to get out. "Detective, you've heard it. You can arrest us after."

"Oh, I intend to stay," Okafor said, dragging a chair to the back wall, "and watch every second of it. If I'm going down with this broadcast, I'm going down informed."

* * *

They ran the tape once, all the way through, before air. Just the five of them in the room plus the silent grid of faces, lights down, because some things should only be heard in the dark first.

Alistair Amberdale-Hurst's voice filled the studio—cultivated, warm, the voice of a hundred family dinners Wren had never been invited to but Tamsin had survived. He'd talked for a long time before he understood he was being recorded; the mic had caught the scrape of the crypt door, Tamsin's breathing, the echo of stone. Then the lock turning. Then the conversation, circling, Tamsin asking questions with that terrible gentle steadiness they had, and Alistair answering, because he had come to gloat, or to explain, or perhaps simply because after thirty years of carrying it alone he was desperate for one person to know.

*"You were never supposed to find any of this,"* he said on the tape. *"Rosalind promised me. She drew up the codicil herself—the condition, the witness clause—and then she died before she could control what it did. Edmund Pryce found it. He was going to sell you to the highest bidder, Tamsin. Do you understand? Your name, your claim, your existence—he'd have auctioned you. And Bess... Bess was going to recant. She told me on the phone, after your little podcast went digging. Thirty years I kept that statement solid. One phone call and she was ready to unravel it all."*

*"So you killed her."*

*"I protected you."* The voice cracked then, and it was somehow worse than the cold parts. *"Your mother gave everything so you would never be an Amberdale. Never be hunted by that house, that money, those vultures. Rosalind swapped places with her that night—did you know that? Marlowe Vane saw it happen. Saw which sister walked out onto that terrace and which one stayed. Your mother ran, and Rosalind fell, and I have spent my entire life making sure the world never looked closely enough to see the difference. That was the duty. That was the love. And you—"*

A pause on the tape. Long enough that Wren had gripped the edge of the desk.

*"You keep digging holes in the ground I buried everything in. I'm sorry. I'm so sorry. But I can't let you dig."*

Then sounds Wren would carry to their grave: the scuffle, the shout, the crash of the mausoleum's iron furniture—and then, faint and fierce, Wren's own voice in their memory overlapping, because they'd been monitoring the feed from the car, screaming Tamsin's name into a phone while dialing Okafor with the other hand.

Forty-seven minutes. Twelve seconds. Wren stopped the playback.

Nobody spoke. On the grid, forty faces were wet-eyed and rigid.

"He confessed to Pryce too," Dev said finally, quietly. "And to paying Marlowe Vane's silence—not killing them. Marlowe's alive. He thinks." He looked up. "That's chapter nineteen's problem. This one's ours."

Wren nodded and reached for the fader.

* * *

They went live at 6:04 a.m.—the slot Wren had chosen deliberately, the grey hour, the hour shift-workers and insomniacs and new parents and the lost all share, the hour when *The Coldest File*'s audience was always at its most honest.

Wren opened the show themselves. Their hands shook on the script they'd written and abandoned; they spoke without it.

"This is Wren. If you're hearing this live, you're part of the largest audience we've ever had, and I need you to do the hardest thing this show has ever asked of you. I need you to listen to a human being confess to murder. We're not editing it. We're not summarizing it. We considered every alternative, and we voted, all of us—all of *you*—and here is why we chose this. Because for thirty years, the truth in this story has been locked in rooms. A well. A crypt. A gatehouse. A silence bought and maintained by a man who called it love. Tonight we open the last room. What you're about to hear was recorded eleven hours ago at Amberdale Mausoleum. The person wearing the microphone is our own Tamsin Amberdale. They are alive. They are safe. They are sitting ten feet away from me, and they asked to be here for this. Listener discretion won't help you. There is no discretion adequate to this. Listen anyway."

They hit play.

For forty-seven minutes, the studio held its breath. Wren watched the meters dance with a dead man's killer's voice and thought about all the hours they'd spent cleaning up audio—de-essing, denoising, trimming the ugly parts—and how there was nothing in the world they less wanted to polish than this. Raw was the point. Raw was the whole point. Some truths only survive unedited.

The chat—usually a river of emojis and timestamps—went almost silent. Then, slowly, as Alistair's voice broke over the words *that was the duty, that was the love*, it began to move again: a slow tide of messages from every timezone. *I'm here.* *We're here.* *Tamsin, if you can see this, we're all here.* A listener in Lagos wrote: *My brother disappeared in '09. Nobody ever aired anything for him. Thank you for airing this.* The braille transcriber posted: *Transcribing in real time. It will be readable within the hour. He doesn't get to hide in print either.*

When the tape ended, the studio was silent except for the hum of the meters settling to zero.

Then Tamsin pulled on the headphones, keyed the mic, and leaned in.

Wren had heard Tamsin's voice on thousands of hours of tape. Confident, wry, wounded-but-functioning, the voice of a person built carefully out of omissions. This was different. This was the voice underneath the voice, sanded down to the grain.

"You heard him," Tamsin said. "All of you. My uncle. My guardian. The man who signed my school forms and drove me to my first broadcasting gig and told me, every time I asked about my parents, that some doors were better left closed. He killed two people to keep me a secret, and he did it because he loved me, and I need you to sit with how horrifying that is, because I've been sitting with it for eleven hours and I'm not done yet." A breath, audible, unsteady. "He wasn't wrong about everything. The world *was* dangerous for me. The codicil, the claim, the name—it was a target painted on a child. Hiding me was a choice someone made out of love. But love that locks doors isn't protection. It's just a smaller prison with better lighting. I lived in it for thirty-four years and called it anonymity. Called it privacy. Called it a brand strategy." A broken laugh. "I called it a lot of things."

Wren's eyes blurred. They kept their hands on the board because the board was the only thing keeping them upright.

"So here's what I'm doing instead," Tamsin went on. "There's a name on my birth certificate. A name Alistair erased, that Rosalind buried in a codicil, that my mother carried out of that house the night her sister died for her. I've never said it. Not on air. Not anywhere. I told myself it was safety. It was fear wearing safety's coat." Another breath, longer. "But you heard the tape. You're witnesses now—all of you, tens of thousands of witnesses—and a witness deserves to know who they're witnessing for."

The pause stretched. In the glass, Wren saw Okafor lean forward. Saw Marguerite close her eyes. Saw Dev stop typing.

"My name," Tamsin said, "is Elowen Rosalind Amberdale. My mother was Sera Amberdale, and she is alive, and I am going to find her. My aunt died so my mother could live, and a man named Marlowe Vane saw it happen, and I am going to find him too, and when I do, this show will be here, with all of you, to hear it. That's the inheritance I'm claiming. Not the house. Not the money. This. You. The ones who stayed up all night. The ones who are here right now." Their voice frayed, held, steadied. "This is *The Coldest File*, case forty-eight, and for the first time in my life, I'm telling you exactly who's talking."

Wren faded in the outro theme—the old one, the one from episode one, the creaky piano loop Dev had dug out of the archive as a surprise—and watched the chat detonate. *Elowen.* *Elowen.* *Elowen.* It scrolled like snowfall, like applause, like a name being passed hand to hand across the world until it belonged to everyone and therefore, finally, safely, to Tamsin.

They went off air at 7:11. For a moment nobody moved.

Then Marguerite put the kettle on, declaring the newsroom a kitchen after all, and Okafor stood, buttoned his coat, and said, "I have to go write a report that explains all of this, and God help me, I'm going to tell the truth in it. Every line." At the door he paused. "Elowen," he said, testing it, giving it back like something borrowed. "When you find Marlowe Vane—I drive. I know the back roads."

Tamsin laughed, and cried, and let Wren fold them into a hug that neither of them prettied up or pulled back from.

It was Dev who broke the moment, laptop balanced on one knee, frowning at the screen in a way that made Wren's producer instincts prickle before he even spoke.

"Uh. Wren? You're going to want to see this." He turned the screen. "The voicemail line. It's lighting up. Hundreds of messages. But there's one that came in four minutes ago, during the broadcast, from a number our system flags as..." He swallowed. "As a landline. Registered in 1994. Still active. Still paid, somehow, all these years."

He hit play, and out of the speakers, over the fading piano, an old voice—cracked, careful, unmistakably afraid—said:

"You said your name. After thirty years, you said it out loud. I heard it. I heard everything." A long, shaking breath. "My name is Marlowe Vane. And I think it's time somebody finally heard what I saw."

Wren looked at Tamsin. Tamsin was already reaching for the headphones.

"Case forty-nine," Wren murmured, and hit record.
