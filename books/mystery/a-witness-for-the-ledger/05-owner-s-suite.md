## 5. Owner's Suite

The elevator to the sixth floor required a key that only three people in the building possessed. Silas Ashgrove had never been one of them.

He stood now in the service corridor behind the elevator shaft, holding a master key he had signed out of the security office under the pretense of a routine lock audit, and told himself—again—that this was not theft. It was diligence. Fifteen years of diligence had a way of wearing grooves in a man's conscience until questionable things rolled along them smoothly, like marbles down a banister.

Maren Kettle stood beside him in the dim light of the corridor's single caged bulb, her coat still damp from the fog outside. She had not asked how he got the key. She had only looked at it, then at him, with an expression that suggested she was adding it to some internal ledger of her own.

"You've been up here before," she said. Not a question either.

"Once a year. To deliver the owner's quarterly correspondence." He turned the key over in his palm. "Edmund Vane prefers to read his mail alone. I leave it on the console inside the door. I have never gone past the console."

"And tonight?"

"Tonight we go past the console."

The elevator hummed up through the building's spine like something digesting. Maren watched the floor indicator climb. "Tell me about him again. The owner."

"Edmund Vane. Forty-six years old. Inherited the Marlowe Grand from his mother, who inherited it from her father, who built it with money nobody in Port Ellery likes to discuss." Silas kept his voice low, though the corridor was empty. "He lives here year-round in the penthouse suite. He eats dinner alone in the Corner Room every Tuesday and Friday. He tips generously and remembers the names of every employee's children. He has donated the ambulance for the regatta every summer since I've worked here."

"That's the résumé," Maren said. "Give me the rest."

Silas hesitated. The elevator arrived with a chime too cheerful for what they were about to do.

"The rest is that he watches people," Silas said. "Not the way a hotelier watches guests. The way a man watches weather he's already decided is coming. And twice in fifteen years, when something has gone wrong in this town—something buried, something bought—I have found his handwriting in places it had no business being. A condolence note to a family whose daughter drowned, written before anyone reported the drowning."

The doors opened onto a short private landing. One door, black lacquer, brass numbers: 601. Beyond it, through a transom window, the fog pressed against the glass like a face.

* * *

The suite smelled of cold coffee and cedar. That was Silas's first observation, made in the two seconds it took his eyes to adjust—the smell of a room lived in by someone precise enough to store wool properly and restless enough to let cups go unwashed. His second observation was the piano, a baby grand positioned beneath the window with its lid closed, and on top of the lid, arranged with curatorial care, a collection of framed photographs.

Maren went left. Silas went right. They moved through the suite the way they'd agreed downstairs: no talking unless necessary, touch nothing without noting it first, photograph everything with the small camera Maren produced from her coat pocket—a real camera, film, purchased with cash, because Maren Kettle did not trust devices that could testify against you.

The sitting room was larger than Silas's entire apartment above the laundry. Two sofas, a fireplace laid but unlit, bookshelves filled with volumes whose spines showed actual wear. Tide tables, he noticed. Three different editions, their pages soft with use. A monograph on coastal erosion. A history of Port Ellery shipping that Silas happened to know was out of print and nearly unobtainable.

"He reads about the water," Maren said quietly.

"He reads about everything." But Silas felt the observation settle somewhere beneath his ribs. The letters had come from someone who knew the tide's turning the way a priest knows his office hours.

The bedroom door stood half open. Silas pushed it with one knuckle.

The desk was the thing. It sat facing the window—not the bed, not the television, the window—with a green-shaded lamp and a leather writing case, and when Silas switched on the lamp, the light fell across a sheet of cream stationery bearing the hotel's crest, blank except for the ghosted impression of writing on the page above it.

"Paper's been used recently," Maren said, appearing at his shoulder. She tilted the lamp. "See the indentation? Someone wrote something, tore the sheet off, left the next page to take the imprint."

Silas fetched the writing case's spare paper and, as he'd seen evidence technicians do in another life, rubbed the side of a pencil gently across the blank page. Letters surfaced like bodies rising in shallow water.

He read them aloud, slowly, because saying them made them more real:

"...the tide will do what the coroner would not..."

Maren said nothing for a long moment. Then: "Same hand as the letters?"

"Almost certainly. Look at the descender on the *d*. The little hook." Silas set the pencil down with exaggerated care, as if it might go off. "We need the original letters to compare properly, but—"

"But we don't have the originals. Because our mysterious correspondent mailed them and vanished." Maren was already moving back toward the sitting room. "So we're comparing handwriting to handwriting, which any decent lawyer will laugh out of court."

"We're not building a court case yet. We're building a direction."

The photographs on the piano stopped Maren mid-stride. Silas joined her, and together they looked at fifteen years of Edmund Vane's life arranged in silver frames.

A young Edmund at a christening, holding an infant. Edmund and a woman—handsome, dark-haired, her smile carrying the specific strain of someone performing happiness—at what appeared to be a wedding anniversary. Edmund's mother, iron-haired and imperious, seated on the very sofa behind them. And then, in the center frame, smaller than the others as if it had been demoted or promoted, a photograph of two children on a beach.

A boy and a girl. Perhaps ten and eight. The girl held a crab in cupped hands; the boy pointed at something beyond the frame, mouth open in a shout the camera had caught mid-syllable. Behind them, gray water, gray sky, and the black teeth of the cliffs to the north.

Silas knew those cliffs. Everyone in Port Ellery knew those cliffs. Below them lay the tide caves.

"The girl," Maren said.

"Greer," Silas said. The name came out of him before he'd decided to release it, fifteen years of ledger-keeping compressing into a single word. "Greer Vane. She drowned when she was seventeen. Officially, she slipped from the rocks at Cutter's Point while swimming after dark. Unofficially—" He stopped.

"Unofficially, your ledger says otherwise."

"My ledger says the body was found four miles south of where she supposedly slipped. My ledger says the search party was called off after six hours on the direct instruction of Police Chief Aldous Halloway, who received, eleven days later, a deed to a house he could never have afforded on a chief's salary. My ledger says the coroner's report lists cause of death as accidental drowning despite ligature marks on both wrists noted—and then struck through—in the examining physician's preliminary notes."

Maren turned from the photograph. Her face in the lamplight had gone very still, the stillness of a woman recalculating everything. "And the boy?"

"Edmund." Silas touched the frame's edge without lifting it. "Her older brother."

* * *

They found the safe behind the portrait of Edmund's mother, because of course they did; Maren found it, actually, running her fingertips along the wall's seam while Silas examined the desk drawers. A click, a swing of plasterboard, and there it was: a floor safe with a combination dial, old-fashioned, stubborn.

"I can't crack this," Maren said. "Not without time we don't have."

"Then we note it and move on."

"No." She crouched, studying the dial. "You don't understand what kind of man buys a safe like this. It's not for jewelry. It's for documents. Things you need to keep but can't bear to look at, or things you need to look at but can't bear to keep." She straightened. "Try birthdays."

"What?"

"You said he remembers every employee's children's names. A man like that organizes his life around other people's dates. Try the drowning date. September ninth."

Silas's hands were steady on the dial, which surprised him. Nine, zero, nine. Nothing. He tried the year format. Nothing. He tried—

"His mother's birthday," he said suddenly. "March third. The whole town sends flowers. He signs every card personally."

Three, zero, three. The dial resisted. Three, three, sixty-one—the year of her birth, which Silas knew because the obituary had passed across the front desk four years ago and Silas remembered everything that passed across the front desk.

The bolt drew back with a sound like a held breath released.

Inside: a metal cash box, a bundle of deeds tied with string, and a ledger.

Not *his* ledger. Silas's heart performed a complicated maneuver. This was a school exercise book, its cover furred with age, its corners rounded by handling. The label on the front, in a child's careful capitals, read: **GREER'S TIDE LOG — DO NOT TOUCH EDMUND**.

Maren looked at him. Silas looked at the book. Neither of them touched it for a full five seconds, which in Silas's experience was the natural interval between discovery and consequence.

"She kept a tide log," he said finally. "Children do that here. The maritime society gives them out at school. You record the tides, the weather, what you find on the beach." He lifted it out with both hands, the way one lifts a bird with a broken wing. "She was fourteen in this photograph. If she started logging at twelve, she'd have three years of entries before she died."

"Entries that would tell us exactly when the tide turned on September ninth, fifteen years ago." Maren's voice had dropped to almost nothing. "Which would tell us whether the official timeline is even possible."

Silas opened the cover. The early pages were charming—crayon annotations, a drawing of a seagull labeled THIS IS GERALD HE IS RUDE. But as the pages turned, the charm gave way to something else. Greer Vane had been a meticulous child, and then a meticulous adolescent, and her final months of entries were written in a tight, adult hand that recorded not just tides but times, and not just times but *who*.

*June 3. Low 4:12. R. came to the boathouse again. Said if I told, no one would believe me. He's right. That's the worst part.*

Silas's blood went cold and stayed there.

"Maren."

"I see it."

*R. came to the boathouse.* He turned pages faster now, hating himself for hurrying, needing to hurry. The entries continued through June, July, August. *R.* appeared again and again, always at the boathouse, always with the same refrain: *no one would believe me*. The final entry was dated September eighth, the day before she died:

*Low 11:47. Told E. everything. He didn't cry. He just got very quiet and asked me exactly what R. said, word for word. I wrote it all down for him so I wouldn't have to say it out loud. He took the page. He said, "Leave it with me, Greer. I'll make it right."*

Silas closed the book.

The suite was silent except for the fog pressing at the windows and, far below, the muffled percussion of the night porter locking the lounge doors. Silas became aware that his hands were shaking, and that Maren had taken the camera out and was photographing the open log, page by page, with the mechanical calm of someone who had learned long ago that shaking comes later or not at all.

"E is Edmund," she said. "R is—"

"Don't say it. Not yet. Not until we're sure." But even as he said it, Silas's mind was assembling the roster of Port Ellery men whose names began with R, fifteen years younger, with access to a boathouse and a belief in their own impunity. The list was short. It was horrifyingly short. And at the top of it, in letters that seemed to burn through the darkness of the suite, was a name attached to the most powerful family in town—the family whose verdict had been bought, whose silence had been maintained, whose patriarch still played golf with the police chief every Thursday morning as if the arrangement were a subscription to be renewed.

"We take the log," Maren said.

"We can't. If he notices it's missing—"

"If he notices it's missing, that tells us he doesn't already know we have it, which means he isn't our letter writer, which is information. And if he *doesn't* notice—" She met Silas's eyes. "Then he never looks at it anymore anyway. Silas. This is a dead girl's testimony. It's spent fifteen years in a safe while everyone in this town agreed to forget her. We are not leaving it here."

Silas thought of his own ledger, locked in the concierge office, page one recording a small sin on a rainy Tuesday fifteen years ago. He thought of the twin letters. *I know about the ledger. I have been reading you for years.*

Someone had built him. Someone had curated his knowledge, fed him secrets, polished him into an unimpeachable witness—and now, standing in the killer's—or the avenger's—penthouse, holding a murdered girl's tide log, Silas understood with terrible clarity that he might be looking at the workbench where the whole construction had begun.

Or he might be looking at the reason for it.

He wrapped the log in his scarf and put it inside his coat, against his chest, where he could feel it like a second heartbeat.

* * *

They were at the door—Maren's hand actually on the latch—when the telephone in the sitting room began to ring.

Neither of them moved. The ringing filled the suite, absurdly loud, each peal bouncing off the high ceiling. On the eleventh ring, the answering machine clicked on, and Edmund Vane's voice emerged from the speaker, smooth and warm, the voice of a man who greeted ten thousand strangers a year:

*"You've reached Edmund Vane. I'm unable to come to the phone, but if you leave your name and the nature of your call, I'll return it within the day. Thank you for calling the Marlowe Grand."*

The beep sounded. A pause. Then breathing—someone deciding whether to speak—and finally a voice Silas recognized with a lurch, because he had heard it through the front desk receiver a hundred times, giving orders, making requests, arranging discreet deliveries:

Chief Aldous Halloway. His whisper was ragged, urgent, stripped of all its daytime authority:

*"Edmund. Pick up. I know you're there. The physician's notes—the ones from the Greer file—they're missing from records. All copies. Somebody's been in the archive. If that's you, if you're doing what I think you're doing, you call me back tonight, because I did not spend fifteen years keeping my mouth shut to have it opened for me now. Do you hear me? Call me back."*

The machine clicked off.

In the silence that followed, Maren's hand came off the latch. Silas stood with the dead girl's tide log pressed against his ribs and the chief's whisper still hanging in the air, and understood that the board beneath their feet had just shifted—that whatever game the letter writer was playing, it had entered a new round, and the players were beginning to panic.

Down the service corridor, the elevator chimed.

Someone was coming up to 601.
