## 1. The Night Shift

The rain came down on the dispatch centre like a verdict — steady, impersonal, final. Edmund Amberdale watched it streak the tall windows of the Fenwick Road operations room and thought, as she did most nights, that weather was the only honest thing left in this town. It told you exactly what it intended to do to you, and then it did it.

The switchboard hummed beneath her fingers. Eleven lines, each one a small lit eye she could watch without looking directly at it. She had been watching them for nineteen years, first as a junior operator in the days when the console still had actual buttons that clicked, now as senior night dispatcher, the woman the day shift handed the town to at ten p.m. with the relief of men passing off something heavy.

At 11:47, she closed her last open thread of the evening.

"Mrs. Okonkwo," Edmund said into her headset, voice pitched to the register she'd spent years perfecting — calm but not cold, attentive but not eager. "I have Constable Reyes on the other line for you now. He'll take your statement about the shed."

"He says it's probably kids," Mrs. Okonkwo said, for the fourth time.

"It may well be. But you reported it, and reporting things matters. That's how cases get solved." Edmund transferred the line with two keystrokes and watched the call fold away into Reyes's queue like a letter sliding into an envelope.

It was, if she was honest with herself — and she made a discipline of honesty with herself precisely because she extended so little of it anywhere else — the thing she was proudest of. Not the solving. Anyone could solve, given enough patience; patience was just stubbornness wearing a cardigan. What she was proud of was the closing. The clean end of a thing. Three cold cases in six years, all of them cracked from this chair, from these eleven lines, from the particular music a town makes when it thinks no one is listening.

The Hartley break-in, 2019: solved because a caller complaining about a barking dog had mentioned, in passing, that the neighbour's van was back early. The Denny girl, missing since 2004: found because a man phoning about a blocked drain used the past tense for someone he claimed was alive. And the Whitcombe fire — the one that had made the regional papers, the one where Detective Inspector Halloway had come to the dispatch centre in person and shaken her hand and said, *You should be doing more than this, Miss Amberdale* — solved because an elderly insomniac had called three nights running about a car idling outside his window, and Edmund had written down the plate on all three calls and noticed the third digit changed.

She kept a notebook for such things. A real one, paper, unlined, bought from the stationery shop on Cordwell Street by a woman who paid cash and gave no name. The notebook lived in her handbag, which lived under the desk, which meant it never appeared on any inventory, any camera, any log. Edmund Amberdale had built her life the way some people build model ships inside bottles: painstakingly, privately, through a very small opening. No social media. No photographs of herself anywhere she couldn't control. Her flat on Tallow Lane held furniture chosen not because she liked it but because it expressed nothing. Her nameplate said AMBERDALE, E., and even that felt like one syllable too much of herself given away.

People mistook it for shyness. It wasn't. Shyness is a fear of being seen. What Edmund had was a preference for seeing — and a suspicion, old and well-fed, that being seen was how you ended up in someone else's story, in a paragraph you didn't write, in a sentence that ended badly.

The rain thickened. At midnight the motorway traffic thinned to nothing and the town's calls turned nocturnal and strange: a woman who'd heard singing near the canal (Edmund logged it; drunks sang near the canal every Friday), a man convinced his neighbour was digging tunnels (logged; the neighbour had a allotment), a child whispering that there was someone on the stairs before hanging up (not logged — Edmund traced the number, confirmed it was a landline belonging to the Pell family, and rang back until a mortified father answered).

Routine. The word itself was a kind of shelter. Edmund sat inside it the way other people sat inside churches.

At 2:14 a.m., line seven lit up.

She took it on the second ring, standard practice — fast enough to show attention, slow enough to suggest composure. "Fenwick Road emergency dispatch, this is Edmund. What's your emergency?"

Silence. But not dead air — the silence of someone breathing carefully, holding the phone close to their mouth the way you'd hold a candle in wind.

"Caller, can you hear me? This is a recorded line."

"I know," the woman said.

Her voice was low and roughened, the voice of someone who had been crying earlier or hadn't slept in days, or both. Younger than forty, older than twenty-five. There was water-sound behind her — not rain on glass but the hollow lap and echo of somewhere open and wet. Under a bridge, maybe. Or beside the canal.

"What's your location?" Edmund asked. "Are you injured?"

"No." A pause long enough that Edmund checked the line was still live. Then: "I need to say a name. I've been carrying it a long time and I need to say it out loud to someone who isn't—" The breath caught. Started again. "Someone who isn't going to write it down wrong."

"People here write things down correctly," Edmund said gently. "That's rather our whole profession. You can give me a name."

Another silence. Rain against whatever the woman stood under, drumming softly, patient as everything else in the dark.

"Wren," the woman whispered.

And the bottom dropped out of the night.

Edmund did not move. Nineteen years of training held her body perfectly still in her chair, hands light on the desk, voice level — but inside her chest something old and locked swung open on rusted hinges and let out a smell of summer, cut grass, hymnbooks, the specific heat of a church hall in July 1998, and a name being called across a crowded parish fete by a woman with flour on her hands.

*Wren! Wren, come away from the water.*

No one had called her that in thirty years. No one living knew it. It wasn't on any document; it had been retired before documents mattered, buried along with the rest of that summer, replaced at fourteen by a girl who decided, with a child's terrible clarity, that names could be doors and hers needed locking. Edmund Amberdale was a door with no handle. Wren was what was behind it.

"Hello?" The woman's voice sharpened. "Are you still there?"

"I'm here." Edmund heard her own voice and marvelled distantly at it — smooth, professional, untouched. Control was a muscle and hers was the strongest in her body. "You said a name. Can you tell me why that name matters to you?"

"It matters because it's going to be in the papers," the woman said. "Maybe tomorrow. Maybe the day after. And when it is, I need whoever answers this phone to remember I called. To remember I tried."

"Tried to do what? Are you in danger? Where are you calling from?"

"The canal path," the woman said. "By the old lock gate. You'll know it." A strange little laugh, wet at the edges. "Everyone here knows it. That's rather the whole problem, isn't it? Everyone knowing things."

"Ma'am, if you're near the water, I'd like to send someone to you—"

"Don't." The word cracked like ice. "Don't send anyone. If a car comes down that lane tonight, I'm gone, do you understand? I've been gone for years and I intend to stay gone. I just needed—" Another breath, ragged now. "I needed to say it to someone. Before. In case saying it out loud counts for anything, afterwards."

"After what?" Edmund asked. "After what?"

But the woman was talking over her, faster, quieter, the way people speak when they're spending courage they don't have much of. "Tell them the ledger was right. Tell them the columns balance. Whoever finds it — tell them the columns always balanced, that was the whole point, that was how they got away with it, because nobody checks a book that balances—"

"Whose ledger?" Edmund's voice had lost its polish; she heard it fray and didn't care. "What ledger? Who are you?"

A pause. When the woman spoke again, her voice had changed — steadied, almost tender, as though she were looking at something across the water that moved her.

"You sound like him," she said. "When you ask questions like that. Quick and quiet. He used to say the quiet ones hear the most." A breath. "He was right, you know. About me. I heard everything."

"Who? Who are you talking about? Give me your name."

"I gave you a name," the woman said softly. "It's yours."

The line went dead.

Edmund sat with the dial tone humming in her ear for four full seconds — an eternity in dispatch time — before her hand remembered its training and keyed the trace request. Line seven, 2:14 to 2:19. The system returned a sector, not an address: cellular, moving, signal degraded by weather. The canal corridor. Two miles of towpath, five bridges, the old lock gate.

She should flag it. Protocol said flag it: distressed female caller, possible self-harm risk, water proximity, dispatch a welfare check. Her finger hovered over the priority key.

*If a car comes down that lane tonight, I'm gone.*

Edmund looked at the rain. She thought about protocol, and then she thought about a woman who knew a name that had been dead for thirty years, who spoke of ledgers and balancing columns and hearing everything, who wanted a witness and refused rescue in the same breath. People like that — Edmund knew, because she was one — did not want to be found. They wanted to be believed. There was a difference, and it was the width of a life.

She logged the call. Time, duration, sector, content summarised in language so neutral it might have described a wrong number: *caller distressed, reference to local landmark, declined assistance, terminated contact.* She did not mention the name. Some instinct older than procedure folded that word — *Wren* — and put it away in the same interior drawer where the summer of '98 had lived undisturbed for three decades, and she pressed the drawer shut, and told herself the case would come to her if it was real, the way cases always did, through the switchboard, through the town's endless confessing.

At 3:40 a.m., the radio crackled.

"All units, all units. Reports of a body in the canal by the old lock gate. Fire and rescue en route. Dispatch, be advised—"

Edmund was already reaching for the incident log, pen moving, face composed, heart doing something slow and enormous behind her ribs.

"—female, recovered from the water. Ambulance attending. Not resuscitable."

Outside, the rain went on falling, telling the town exactly what it intended to do to it.

* * *

By dawn the canal path was strung with tape, and Edmund — off shift, sleepless, standing too close to the cordon in a grey coat that made her invisible among the gawping commuters — saw them bring the woman up the bank on a stretcher.

She was young. Dark hair plastered flat, skin grey-blue, one shoe missing. They'd covered her to the shoulders with a foil blanket that caught the early light and burned silver, and for one absurd moment she looked dressed for a party.

Then the stretcher tilted on the uneven ground, the blanket slipped, and Edmund saw what the woman was wearing.

A green wool jumper, oatmeal collar, cuffs slightly stretched. Buttons of dark horn. A small mend at the left elbow done in matching thread, neat as surgery.

Edmund owned that jumper. It hung in her wardrobe on Tallow Lane at this exact moment, she was nearly certain — except that certainty curdled as she counted backward through her week, through laundry days and rotation, and arrived at the truth: it had been there yesterday. She had seen it yesterday, hanging third from the left.

Which meant either her memory was failing, or her wardrobe had been opened by someone else's hands — studied, catalogued, worn.

The stretcher passed close. Close enough that Edmund, against every rule she had ever set for herself, stepped forward and looked down into the drowned woman's face — and the past, which had been tapping politely on the switchboard all night, kicked the door wide open.

Because she knew her. Not from life. From a photograph, sun-bleached and curling, of the St. Aldric's parish choir, summer 1998: two rows of children in white cassocks squinting into the camera, and in the back row, third from the left, a girl with dark hair and a gap between her front teeth, smiling like someone who has just been told a secret.

Edmund stood at the tape while the ambulance pulled away, and the rain stopped, and the morning came up pale and merciless over the water, and she understood — with the calm that arrives when dread finishes its work — that the woman on the stretcher had not stolen her clothes at random.

She had been dressing as Edmund Amberdale.

Or perhaps — and the thought landed like a stone in still water — she had been dressing as someone else entirely. Someone whose name Edmund had buried so deep that even she sometimes forgot it was hers.

Third from the left. Back row. Summer of '98.

The police were already asking who she was.
