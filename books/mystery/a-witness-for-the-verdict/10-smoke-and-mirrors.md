## 10. Smoke and Mirrors

The key to the study was in a drawer of her mother's old writing desk, and Isolde had walked past it for eleven days before she understood what she was looking at.

Her mother had been dead nine years. The desk had sat in the east sitting room since before Isolde could read, its green leather top worn pale at the corners where her mother's wrists had rested. Isolde used it now for correspondence, for the small administrative cruelties of grief—condolence replies, estate lawyers, the endless paper shed of a death. She had opened that drawer a hundred times. It held stamps and spare ribbon and a tin of lemon drops gone sugary with age.

It also, she now realized, held a key.

Not to anything obvious. Not to the study, which stood unlocked on the ground floor like a museum exhibit everyone was too polite to touch. Not to her father's bedroom, sealed now under dust sheets she kept meaning to remove. The key was small, brass, older than the others—older, she thought, than her father's tenure in this house—and it had no tag. Her mother had kept it beneath a false bottom in the stamp tray, wrapped in a scrap of velvet, as if it were something precious rather than something hidden.

Isolde sat with it in her palm for a long time that first evening, the fire down to embers, the house making its winter sounds around her. Then she put it back and closed the drawer and did not think about it again until three o'clock the next morning, when she woke with the certain knowledge that it fit the lock of the cabinet in her father's study—the low walnut cabinet behind his desk, the one he'd once caught her opening at fourteen and closed with a hand on her shoulder and a smile that hadn't reached his eyes.

*When you're older,* he'd said. *Some things you carry better after.*

She had never asked again. That was the bargain of being Edmund Voss's daughter: you learned early which doors stayed shut, and you loved him enough not to test them.

He was dead now. The doors belonged to her.

* * *

She went down at dawn, still in her dressing gown, hair unbrushed, feeling less like an heir than a burglar in her own house. The study smelled of cold ash and leather. She knelt at the cabinet the way she'd knelt there at fourteen, and the key turned on the first try, smooth as if it had been waiting.

Inside: ledgers. A dozen of them, cloth-bound, undated on their spines, arranged in perfect chronological order by a man who had never once in his life let a year go unfiled. Isolde pulled the most recent and carried it to the desk, where the light came up grey over the river.

It was not the company ledger. She knew those—she'd grown up with their columns, their tidy double-entry virtue. This was something else. Private accounts. Payments out, mostly, in her father's cramped hand, to names that meant nothing and then, page after page, to one name that meant everything.

**A. Pell.**

Ambrose Pell. Her father's lawyer. The man who had drafted her father's will with such tenderness, who had held Isolde's hand at the funeral and said, *he thought the world of you*, in a voice like polished stone.

She turned pages backward through the years. Pell, Pell, Pell. And then she saw the pattern, and the pattern was worse than the payments.

Every entry fell on the same date. The seventeenth of March.

She knew the date the way she knew her own birthday—not from any document but from the shape of her childhood. Every March seventeenth of her girlhood, her father had disappeared. A business trip, always. Always alone. And every year on his return, her mother would be quiet for a week, and there would be a night when Isolde, half-asleep, heard her parents' voices below her bedroom, low and urgent, her mother saying words like *enough* and *it's been long enough, Edmund*, and her father answering in a register she had never heard him use with anyone else—a voice stripped of all its charm, flat as slate.

Thirty years of payments. Every year, the anniversary of the fire.

Isolde set the ledger down because her hands had begun to shake, and she looked out at the river and made herself think it plainly, without ornament:

Either my father was paying a blackmailer, or he was paying penance.

And she could not tell which possibility frightened her more. Because blackmail meant someone had owned her father—Edmund Voss, who answered to no one—for thirty years. And penance meant there was something to do penance for.

The fire had killed him. That was the story she had been raised inside. He had nearly died in it; he had *been* dead in it, officially, for six hours while they fought the blaze, until they found him in the annex, burned and half-drowned in sprinkler water, and the doctors said no one could explain how he'd survived. He'd come back from it thinner, quieter, with a scar along his jaw he never discussed. The fire had made him gentle, her mother said. The fire had taught him what mattered.

What if the fire had taught him something else entirely?

* * *

She worked through the ledgers for two days, telling herself it was diligence, knowing it was excavation. She photographed every page. She built a timeline on the long table in the library, index cards fanned out like a hand of losing cards. Pell, annually, thirty years, amounts that grew modestly with inflation—too modest for extortion, she thought, too *orderly*. Blackmailers escalate. This read like a subscription. Like a standing obligation. Like a debt serviced faithfully.

There were other entries. Smaller ones, scattered. A payment to a firm of archivists in 2003. A payment to a private clinic in Lisbon—Lisbon, she noted, without knowing why the word snagged in her like a fishhook. A recurring sum marked only **R.H.**, beginning eight years ago and continuing to the present month. To the present *month*. Her father was four months dead. Someone was still paying R.H., or had been, right up until the ledgers stopped.

She called the family solicitor and asked, casually, who handled disbursements from her father's personal accounts now. The answer was Reginald. Of course it was Reginald. Uncle Reginald, keeper of the name, who had wept so beautifully at the funeral and who had told her last week, with warm finality, that the past was best left where her father had filed it.

She hung up and stood in the library among her index cards and felt the specific loneliness of a person who has seen something true in a house full of people insisting on a comfortable lie.

That was when she thought of Marguerite Nystrom.

* * *

He came at seven, and she watched him arrive from the library window—the tall, careful figure crossing the gravel with his collar up against the rain, moving like a man who counted his steps. The auditor. The liar. She had caught him within ten minutes of their first meeting, a fabricated records audit with a fabricated firm, and instead of showing him the door she had poured him coffee and watched him decide, visibly, whether to keep lying. He had chosen truth. She had liked him for it in a way that alarmed her, the way you like a knife you've decided to hold rather than be held by.

Now she needed him, and needing him felt like the line she couldn't uncross. Once she showed him the ledger, she would no longer be a grieving daughter conducting a private inventory. She would be a co-investigator. An accomplice to whatever truth lived in those pages. There was no version of this where she handed him the book and remained Edmund Voss's daughter in the old, uncomplicated sense.

She served tea anyway, because her hands wanted something to do.

"You found something," he said, before she'd even sat. Not a question. He had that way of reading a room's tension like text.

"Why do you say that?"

"Because you asked me here at dinner hour, alone, and you haven't touched your cup." His eyes went to the library door, then back. "And because you look the way people look when they've stopped hoping a thing isn't true."

Isolde laughed—short, involuntary, surprised out of her. "You always talk like that?"

"My mother was a housekeeper. Plain speech was the one luxury we had."

She let that sit between them, then rose and fetched the ledger and laid it open on the table between them, turned to face him. "My father paid Ambrose Pell every year on the seventeenth of March. For thirty years. Starting the year of the fire." She watched his face as he read. "Tell me what you see."

Marguerite read the way she imagined he did everything—completely, silently, once. When he looked up, something in his expression had changed. The auditor's neutral mask had slipped, and underneath was a man working very hard to stay calm.

"Where did you find this?"

"A locked cabinet. My mother kept the key."

"Your mother knew about it."

"My mother kept the key," Isolde repeated, and heard how it sounded, and hated how much it sounded like an accusation. "She may have known nothing else. Or—" She stopped. Started again. "Or she spent thirty years knowing exactly what this was, and chose to live beside it."

"And you want to know which."

"I want to know *what it is*. Blackmail or penance. Those are the only two readings I can find, and they can't both be true, and I—" Her voice betrayed her, thinned out. She steadied it. "I loved my father. I need you to understand that before you say whatever you're about to say. Whatever this is, I loved him, and I am going to find out what he was paying for, and I can't do it alone, because everyone else who could help me has a reason to stop me."

Marguerite was quiet for a long moment. Rain ticked against the window. When he spoke, his voice was lower than before.

"There's a third reading."

"Which is?"

"That he wasn't paying Pell at all. That Pell was the channel—that the money went onward, somewhere your father needed it to go, once a year, on a date he couldn't bring himself to choose any other day of the calendar." He tapped the column of entries gently, as if the numbers might bruise. "Guilt keeps anniversaries, Ms. Voss. Blackmail keeps invoices. These are anniversaries."

Something in her chest went tight and cold. "You think it's penance."

"I think," he said carefully, "that I've spent three weeks reconstructing a fire everyone wants forgotten, and every thread I pull runs through this house, and this family, and now through this book. I think you deserve to know that before you decide how far in you are." He met her eyes. "Because once I tell you what I have—once we start comparing what we know—we're not separate anymore. You'll know things I know. I'll know things you know. And whoever buried the truth the first time will treat us accordingly."

"Us," she repeated.

"If you want it to be."

Isolde looked at him across her father's secrets—at this stranger with his careful voice and his mother's plain-spoken poverty, this man who had lied to her once and chosen truth ever since—and felt the ground tilt in a way that had nothing to do with ledgers or fires. She was about to hand a man she'd known three weeks the keys to her family's grave. She was about to dig it up herself.

"Yes," she said. "I want it to be us."

He nodded slowly, and then he took a folded paper from his inside pocket—worn soft at the creases, handled many times—and laid it beside the ledger. Two documents side by side: her father's secret debts, and a letter in handwriting she almost recognized.

"Before we go further," Marguerite said, "you should see this. Because your father's payments started the year of the fire. And so did these letters—one confessing to arson and murder, one offering money for silence. Written in the same hand."

Isolde stared at the letters, at the looping, deliberate script, and felt the floor of her whole life shift an inch sideways. Because she *did* recognize the hand. She had seen it on birthday cards, on the margins of her school reports her father kept, on the sympathy note delivered last month with such grave kindness.

Ambrose Pell's handwriting. On a confession to murder.

"Marguerite," she said quietly, "when did the second letter promise its money?"

"The seventeenth of March."

Outside, the rain thickened against the glass, and somewhere in the walls the old house settled into the dark, keeping its counsel, keeping its dates. Isolde put her hand flat on the ledger—on thirty years of her father's faithful, terrible calendar—and understood that she had just crossed the line she'd been standing at, and that there was no walking back across it, and that she did not, in any part of herself that mattered, want to.

"Then we start with Pell," she said. "Tomorrow. Before anyone tells him we're coming."
