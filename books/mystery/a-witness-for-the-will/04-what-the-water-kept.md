## 4. What the Water Kept

The fog came off the water the way it always did at dusk, climbing the bluffs in slow gray hands, and Wren let it take them.

From here — from the flat shelf of granite where the gorse grew sideways and the wind came in off the strait with a salt edge that never quite wore down — the Harrow house looked like what it had spent thirty years pretending to be: a beautiful thing at rest. Three stories of gray stone and white trim, its windows catching the last copper light of the sun going down behind Marrow Bay's crooked skyline. The widow's walk on top. The chimneys, two of them breathing thin smoke into the evening. The long lawn falling away toward the water in terraces their mother had planted with lavender that never quite survived the winters, though she replanted it every spring with a stubbornness Wren had inherited without knowing it until now.

Wren knew every window by heart. That was the cruelty of it. You could burn a map of your own life, you could change your name twice and your face once and learn to answer to a stranger's name so thoroughly that strangers believed it — but the body kept its own records. The body still knew that the third window on the second floor, the one with the light burning amber behind its curtains, was the room that had been theirs. The body still flinched, after all these years, when the wind hit the bluff at exactly this angle, because the wind sounded like the night the water took everything.

*You're not supposed to be here,* Wren told themselves, the way they told themselves every evening for the past two weeks, and then stayed anyway.

Below, on the terrace, a figure moved with a tray. Marta. Even at this distance, even as a smudge of motion against the lit interior, Wren would have known her anywhere — the particular economy of her movements, nothing wasted, nothing hurried, a woman who had learned to carry heavy things quietly. She set the table on the terrace for two, adjusted a chair a precise inch, and went back inside. The household was already rehearsing. Wren could feel it from up here like heat off a stove: the dinner three weeks away, the anniversary of an anniversary, the whole house winding itself tight around a date like a watch spring.

They had come to Marrow Bay telling themselves it was simple. Come back, look at the place from a distance, hire someone competent to stand between them and whatever the letters promised, and leave. That was all. A transaction. A person who did not exist paying money to a coroner who asked no questions, in exchange for a guarantee that no one would drown them in the cove below the boathouse the way —

The way the letter said. The way the letter described, in detail that made Wren's hands shake even now, weeks later, folded in a coat pocket over their heart like a stone.

*On the night of the anniversary dinner, you will be drowned in the cove below the boathouse, as she was. Unless the truth of her death is proven first.*

Wren pulled their coat tighter and made themselves look at the house properly, because looking was the only penance available. The library light came on — ground floor, left side, the tall windows facing the lawn. Edmund. Their father — no. *Edmund.* Wren practiced the correction daily, the way other people practiced scales. He would be at his desk now, pouring the four-ounce measure of whisky he allowed himself before dinner, reading correspondence he'd already answered in his head. Wren knew the rhythm of his evenings the way sailors know tides. Knew that at nine he would climb the stairs slowly, one hand on the rail since the winter his hip began to fail him, and pause on the landing outside the room that had been Lorna's — sealed now, thirty years sealed, though the staff dusted it weekly, which meant someone gave the order to keep it, which meant something lived in that room that Edmund could not bring himself to kill.

Or couldn't afford to.

The thought arrived cold and unwelcome, the way the truest thoughts did. Wren pushed it down with the others.

There was a game they played with themselves on these evenings, standing in the fog with the gorse scratching at their ankles: *if I walked down there.* If they crossed the lawn right now, past the lavender that some patient gardener had finally coaxed into blooming — their mother's lavender, thirty years of failure and someone had done it, someone had finished her sentence — and knocked on the door with the brass knocker shaped like a heron. What would happen? Edmund would open the door himself; he always did when he was expecting no one. And Wren would say—

What? *Hello. I'm the child you buried in absentia eight years after you buried my mother. The sea declined the job. I've come about the dinner reservation.*

A laugh escaped them, small and wrong in the empty air, startling a gull off the rocks below. The sound of it broke something loose, and for a moment Wren let themselves feel the whole weight of it at once — not the fear, which had become furniture, but the longing, which was worse. Because the house was beautiful. Because the light in those windows was warm. Because somewhere inside there was a piano — the Bechstein, it had to be, Edmund would never have sold it — and Wren's hands remembered it, remembered the worn ivory of middle C and the sticky key above it that nobody ever fixed because Lorna liked it, said it gave the instrument character, said a piano with no flaws was just furniture.

*I could sit down at it,* Wren thought. *I could play the Chopin she taught me, the one in C minor, and whoever was in the house would come running, and for eleven seconds before anyone recognized anything I would be home.*

Eleven seconds. They'd done the arithmetic more than once. It was the kind of arithmetic that could get a person killed, and the letters suggested someone intended exactly that.

The fog thickened. The library light went out, replaced by the softer glow of the drawing room, and Wren knew — knew with the helpless intimacy of a ghost haunting its own life — that Edmund had moved to his chair by the fire, and that in twenty minutes Vivienne would come down to take her sherry standing, never sitting, and Aldous would not come down at all, and the house would settle into its nightly silence like an old dog circling three times before lying down.

Wren turned away. It was time. The text had come an hour ago, four words on a burner phone: *Boathouse. Eleven. Alone.*

Sable Eriksen kept their word about punctuality, at least. About everything else, Wren was beginning to suspect, keeping score would be dangerous.

* * *

The boathouse at night was a different country than the boathouse had been in childhood.

Wren came down the cliff path with a flashlight they didn't turn on, navigating by memory — the third step from the top still loose, the rope railing still gone, replaced by nothing, as if the town had simply agreed decades ago that no one should be walking this path at night and had legislated the darkness accordingly. Below, the cove breathed. The tide was coming in, and the water made its small conspiratorial sounds against the pilings, and Wren hated how well they knew those sounds, hated that their body relaxed at them, some animal part of them still believing this water was home water, safe water, *their* water.

The boathouse door stood open a hand's width. Inside, a lantern burned low on an overturned crate, and Sable Eriksen sat on the dock planks with their legs hanging over the black water, coat collar up, looking — infuriatingly — comfortable. As if waiting in a murder site at eleven at night were a thing a person did between errands.

"You found it," Sable said, without turning.

"I grew up here." The words were out before the discipline caught them. Wren froze, but Sable only nodded slowly, as if filing the slip in a drawer rather than opening it.

"Careful," Sable said mildly. "That's the second true thing you've said to me. At this rate we'll have a whole conversation by Thursday."

Wren closed the door behind them and stood with their back against it, which was not brave but was necessary. The lantern light did unflattering things to the boathouse — showed the rot in the far corner, the oilskin covers over shapes that might have been boats or might have been nothing, the chain hoist hanging from the beam like a question. Thirty years ago there had been rowboats here, and a canoe, and the smell of pine tar, and their mother's straw hat on a nail by the door. Now it smelled of mildew and cold iron.

"You wanted to meet," Wren said. "So talk."

"I wanted to show you something." Sable reached into their coat and produced a folder, held it out. "But first I want to say my piece, and I want you to listen without doing the thing where you go very still and rehearse your denials. I've seen you do it twice now. You're good at it. I'm better at watching."

Wren did not take the folder. "Say it."

"Three weeks from Saturday, twelve people sit down to dinner in that house. One extra place is already laid. I don't know yet who it's for, but I know the menu matches a menu served thirty years ago, seat for seat, course for course, and I know that the surviving Harrows recite their alibis from that night like liturgy — same words, same pauses, same little cough before the same lie." Sable's voice stayed level, almost gentle, which somehow made it worse. "And I know that my client hired me to prove a murder will not happen, which is a strange thing to buy, unless what you actually bought is insurance. Unless you believe the murder has your name on it."

The water slapped the pilings. Somewhere overhead, on the bluff, a night bird called once and got no answer.

"If that's your theory," Wren said carefully, "then you should return my retainer and forget my face."

"That's the problem with your face." Sable stood, unhurried, and crossed the dock planks until they were close enough that Wren could see the lantern reflected twice in their eyes. "I can't forget it. I've tried the professional distance thing. It doesn't take." A pause, and then, quieter, with something underneath it that Wren refused to examine: "Whoever you are — whatever name you're wearing this decade — you're in danger, and you hired me, and I don't leave a job half-finished. But I can't protect a client who lies to me by omission in a town where omission is the local industry. So. One condition."

"Which is?"

"No one learns who you are. Not the sheriff, not the family, not the town council, not God if he asks politely. Not until the dinner is over and you're standing on dry land." Sable held up a finger before Wren could speak. "That's not me granting you a favor. That's me telling you what I've already decided, because I've watched how this town handles people who remember things, and I'm not handing you to it. Whatever you were to this house — however you know which window was yours, why you flinched when I mentioned the boathouse, why your hands—" Sable stopped. Started again, softer. "Whatever it is, it stays between us until it can't hurt you anymore. That's the deal. Take it or fire me."

For a long moment Wren couldn't speak. This was the trap they hadn't anticipated — not exposure, not interrogation, but *kindness*, offered with conditions attached, from a stranger with tired eyes who sat on docks in the dark waiting for liars. They had prepared for suspicion. They had not prepared for the ache of being protected.

"You could lose everything," Wren said finally. "Your position. Your license. If it comes out that you knew—"

"I'm a coroner in a town that falsified a drowning," Sable said. "My relationship with everything is already complicated." They held out the folder again. "Now. Are you going to take this, or do I have to leave it on the dock like a cat leaving a dead bird?"

Wren took it. Opened it. The lantern light fell across a photocopied page — an old guest list, typed, the paper browned at the edges — and beside it, in Sable's narrow handwriting, a column of names. Twelve names. And next to each one, a seat number.

Their eyes went down the list and stopped, as Sable had known they would, at the eleventh line.

*Seat 11: Rowan Harrow (unconfirmed).*

The boathouse seemed to tilt. Wren heard their own breath go out of them, heard the water, heard the chain hoist creak faintly in a draft they couldn't feel.

"Someone's already laid the plate," Sable said quietly. "Has been for days, according to the staff schedule I photographed through the kitchen window. Seat eleven. Facing the head of the table. Same seat a certain person would have occupied thirty years ago, at the dinner party the night Lorna Harrow went into the water." They tilted their head. "Funny thing about seat eleven. Whoever's setting that table seems very confident the guest will confirm."

Wren's hands had gone numb around the folder. The fog pressed against the boathouse walls, and the cove below whispered its old, patient whisper — *we kept it, we kept it, we keep everything* — and Wren understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that the letters had never been a threat aimed at a stranger.

They had been an invitation addressed to a ghost. And someone in that house knew the ghost had answered.

"You asked for my condition," Wren said, and their voice came out steadier than they had any right to expect. "Here's mine. Whatever you find in that house — whoever set that place at that table — you come to me before you go to anyone else. Not the sheriff. Not the council. Me. Because I think we've both just realized the same thing."

"Which is?"

Wren looked up from the guest list, into the lantern light, and let Sable see — for the first time, deliberately — how afraid they were.

"The killer isn't planning a murder," they said. "The rehearsal started weeks ago. Someone in that house isn't waiting to see whether history repeats."
