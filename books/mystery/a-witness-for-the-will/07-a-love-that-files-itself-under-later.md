## 7. A Love That Files Itself Under Later

The rain came in off the bay around seven, the way it always did in Marrow Bay — not falling so much as arriving, a gray curtain drawn across the town as though someone behind the weather had decided the day was over and wanted privacy. Sable Eriksen watched it from the coroner's office window, coffee gone cold in one hand, Lorna Harrow's file open on the desk like a wound that wouldn't close.

The office smelled of old paper and formaldehyde's ghost, of the lemon polish Sable used on the filing cabinets because the cabinets were the closest thing to a congregation the town had. Thirty-one unsolved cases. Thirty-one folders in the bottom drawer, alphabetized with a care Sable gave to nothing else, because the dead deserved order even when the living refused to provide it.

The knock came at eight-fifteen. Two soft raps, apologetic, the knock of someone who had spent a lifetime trying not to be heard.

"Door's open, Wren."

Wren came in shaking rain from their coat the way a bird shakes off water — a quick, economical movement, as if even their body had learned not to take up more space than necessary. They carried a paper bag that smelled of the chowder house on Pier Lane, and they set it on the desk without being asked.

"You didn't eat," Wren said. "I could see you through the window. You had the face you get."

"The face I get."

"The one where you're arguing with a dead person."

Sable looked down at the file, then up. "I'm losing, for the record. Lorna Harrow was smarter than everyone in this town, including the man who killed her, and she still ended up in the water."

Wren flinched. It was small — a tightening around the eyes, a half-beat pause in the motion of unwinding their scarf — but Sable had spent twenty years reading bodies on slabs and in doorways, and a flinch was a flinch. Wren knew something about Lorna Harrow that went past sympathy. Wren flinched like a person hearing a family story told wrong.

"Sit," Sable said. "You brought chowder, you're staying."

"I shouldn't."

"You said that last time. You stayed four hours."

Wren sat. And that was the trouble, Sable thought, ladling chowder into two mismatched mugs because the office had never needed cups before Wren started showing up with food. That was the whole trouble, filed neatly under a heading Sable refused to write down: four hours had started feeling like a shift worth working.

* * *

Wren

The chowder was too hot and Wren drank it anyway, letting it scald a path down their throat because the pain was simple and the rest of the evening was not.

The office at night was different from the office by day. By day it belonged to Sable — the coroner, the pragmatist, the woman*—the person* who had looked at a triple payment for proving a negative and shrugged the way other people shrugged at rain. But at night, with the lamp turned low and the rain erasing the windows, the office belonged to the two of them, and the files spread across the desk like a séance, and Wren kept forgetting to be afraid.

That was the danger. Not Edmund. Not the letters, not the boathouse Wren knew was being prepared, not the thirty-year-old alibis recited in voices gone smooth as river stone. The danger was this: the lamp, the chowder, the way Sable pushed their hair back from their face with the back of a wrist still holding a pen, ink smearing across the temple, and Wren's whole chest going quiet with a feeling that had no name Wren was willing to give it.

"You're staring at the drowning report," Sable said, without looking up. "You've been staring at it for six minutes."

"It's wrong."

"It's falsified. Different thing, worse thing. The bruising on the forearms was noted and then noted over. Defensive wounds, Wren. She fought."

Wren's hands had gone cold around the mug. *She fought.* Thirty years Wren had built the memory of that night out of fragments — a scream heard through a child's window, the smell of the dock, a mother's coat gone from its hook by morning — and every reconstruction had ended in the same terrible arithmetic: *she was alone, and it was quick, and there was nothing she could do.* But no. She had fought. She had raised her arms against whatever came at her, and someone had written it down, and someone else had buried it, and the child had grown up believing the water took her gently.

"I knew her," Wren said. It came out before the caution could catch it. "Lorna. I mean — I've read everything about her. I know her the way you know someone from a file."

Sable looked up then, and their eyes in the lamplight were the gray of the bay in November, and they were not unkind and they were not fooled. "Nobody knows a dead woman the way you know her," Sable said quietly. "I've watched you with these files for two weeks. You don't read them. You *grieve* them."

The room got very small. Wren set down the mug because their hands were no longer to be trusted with hot liquids, and the truth rose in them like water in a locked room, pressing at the door — *my name is Rowan, she was my mother, I drowned when I was eleven and nobody bothered to find the body* — and Wren opened their mouth to say all of it, every word, to hand over the whole drowning of their life to this person who smelled of coffee and lemon polish and who had, last Tuesday, fallen asleep for ninety seconds in a chair with a folder over their face, and whom Wren trusted more than anyone breathing.

"I—" Wren said.

The phone rang.

Sable held up a finger, answered, listened. "No. No, Mr. Corliss, I understand. Yes. The records request is withdrawn. Understood." They set the receiver down with a gentleness that was worse than slamming it. "Exhumation's dead. Edmund leaned on the council. Corliss called to tell me the town finds my interest in the Harrow family 'historically morbid.'"

Wren laughed, or something adjacent to a laugh, and the moment broke like ice on a pond, and the truth sank back down into its cold room and locked itself in. *Later,* Wren told it. *Not yet. Not until I know what happens at that dinner. Not until keeping you can't be used against you.*

They worked until midnight. And if Wren's knee happened to rest against Sable's under the desk, and if neither of them moved it, that was a fact that filed itself, like everything else in this office, under later.

* * *

Sable

The trouble with Wren, Sable decided on the fourth night, was the hands.

It was a small thing to notice and a large thing to have noticed. They were working through the guest list from the dinner party held the night Lorna died — twelve names in Corliss's fountain-pen cursive, twelve seats — and Sable had the original floor plan of the Harrow house pulled from a property dispute file from 1989, a surveyor's drawing with the rooms labeled in faded ink. Sable laid it flat and read aloud, mostly to hear the shape of the house in the dark: parlor, dining room, conservatory, the long gallery with the piano.

"The piano," Sable said, tapping the drawing. "There's a piano in the gallery. A Bechstein, according to an insurance appraisal. Lorna's. It's still there — Aldous mentioned it, drunk, at the interview. Said nobody's allowed to touch it. Edmund keeps it locked."

"Mm," Wren said, in a voice that was doing too much work to be casual.

And then Sable, who was not watching Wren, who was absolutely not watching Wren, watched Wren's hands.

They were resting on the desk, and as Sable said *Bechstein* they moved — not much, just the fingers of the right hand, settling into a shape. A shape. Curved, thumb tucked under, the particular architecture of a hand that has found middle C ten thousand times. They held the shape for three seconds, four, and then Wren seemed to hear the silence of them, looked down, and flattened the hand against the desk as if killing a spider.

Sable said nothing. Sable was very good at saying nothing; it was most of the job. But the observation went into the drawer with the others, into the folder Sable was keeping in their head, the one that was growing heavier every night: *Wren knew the house. Wren flinched at Lorna's name. Wren's hands knew the piano that no one was allowed to touch.*

And still — this was the part that made Sable feel like a corrupt official of their own heart — still, when Wren reached across the desk at one in the morning to turn the drowning report right-side up for Sable, and their fingers brushed, and neither of them pulled back, Sable let the contact sit there like evidence of something, unexamined, unlogged, warming the room.

"You should go home," Sable said. "You look like a witness."

"I *am* a witness."

"You look like a *tired* witness. Worse. Tired witnesses confess to things."

Something crossed Wren's face then — something that in better light Sable would have sworn was temptation — and Wren stood, and gathered their coat, and at the door they stopped with their hand on the frame.

"Sable. If I told you something — something about who I am — would it change what you're doing?"

Sable considered lying. Considered the professional answer, the coroner's answer: *nothing changes the work.* But the lamp was low and the rain was loud and Wren was looking at them the way people look at the last lit window in a dark town.

"It would change how I do it," Sable said. "Not whether."

Wren nodded slowly, as if filing that away in some cabinet of their own, and then they were gone into the rain, and Sable stood alone in the office surrounded by the dead and their paperwork, and admitted it to the filing cabinets, which kept everyone's secrets: *I don't want it to change anything at all.*

* * *

Wren

The bluffs at two in the morning were the closest thing Wren had to a confessional, and the Harrow house below was the sin.

It sat in the rain with its windows lit — all of them lit, which was new, which was wrong — and Wren stood at the rail of the overlook with the wind coming off the water and did the thing they had done a thousand nights: mapped the house by memory. Gallery on the east side, second window. The piano behind it, locked, its key kept in Edmund's study in the third drawer of the desk that had been Lorna's before it was his, because Edmund Harrow had never in his life met a thing he didn't take.

Wren had learned that piano at six years old, standing on the pedal bench while their mother played with her arms around them, her chin on their head, her voice in their ear. *This one's yours, little wren. Learn it so it's yours even when I'm not here.* She used to call them her wren. It was the only name she chose for them that Edmund never managed to take, because he never knew about it, because it lived only in the gallery, in the music, in the small hours. And so when the child Rowan Harrow drowned — officially, bureaucratically, in a shipping manifest and a closed file — the survivor had needed a name, and there had only ever been one waiting.

Wren's hands ached with the shape of middle C.

Below, a door opened in the house — the service door, the one by the kitchen — and a figure came out with a lantern and walked, unhurried, toward the boathouse at the water's edge. Wren went very still. The figure unlocked the boathouse — Wren could see the lantern swing as the door opened — stood in the doorway a long moment, looking out at the dock, and then went back inside and locked it again.

Thirty years ago there had been a night like this. Rain, lantern light, a figure walking to the water. Wren had watched it through a child's window and had spent a lifetime telling themself the memory was a dream.

The figure tonight had been small. Careful. Moving the way people move when they are rehearsing.

Wren took out the latest letter — the fifth, received Tuesday, its block capitals pressed hard enough to tear the paper — and read it again by the light of the harbor beacon: *THE DINNER WILL BE LAID THE SAME. THE SEATS WILL BE THE SAME. AND AT THE END, THE WATER WILL BE THE SAME, UNLESS THE PAST IS PROVEN FIRST.*

*Unless the past is proven first.* Wren had hired the only coroner in Marrow Bay to do the proving, and the coroner was brilliant and stubborn and honest, and Wren had not counted on the way lamplight looked on their face, and had not counted on wanting to be known by them, and had not counted on the fact that every night Wren did not tell the truth was another night the truth grew teeth.

Tomorrow, Wren thought. Or the day after. Before the dinner, certainly. Before it was too late to matter.

Below, the house went dark window by window, gallery first, the piano's window going black like an eye closing. Wren turned up the collar of their coat and walked back toward town, toward the office they had no reason to be near at dawn except that Sable would be there, and the files would be there, and the truth would be one night closer to the surface of the water.

Behind them, in the boathouse at the edge of the dark, something was being prepared — laid out, arranged, set in order with a care that no one had yet explained.

And in Wren's coat pocket, folded small, was a photograph they had not yet shown Sable: a woman at a piano with a child on her lap, both of them laughing, the child's face unmistakable, the child's face the reason Wren could never again walk into that office and be anyone but who they were.

Tomorrow. Wren told themself tomorrow, the way you tell a drowning person tomorrow, and walked on through the rain.
