## 11. Thirty Years of Word-for-Word

The wall above Sable's filing cabinets had never been intended to hold anything but a calendar and, in the early years, a faded poster about rabies awareness that no one had ever taken down. Now it held index cards. Forty-one of them, pinned in rows, each one bearing a name, a date, and a fragment of testimony — because Sable Eriksen had spent three sleepless nights doing what coroners are not supposed to do: investigating the living instead of cataloguing the dead.

It had started with a feeling. Not the dramatic kind that novelists liked to put in their characters' chests — no thunderclap, no cold hand on the neck. Just a small mechanical wrongness, like a key that turns too smoothly in a lock it shouldn't fit. Sable had been transcribing the Harrow interviews from two weeks ago, typing up the "record updates" that had served as pretext for getting each family member alone, when the wrongness announced itself.

Edmund Harrow, asked where he was on the night of September 14th, thirty years ago: *"I was at the head of the table until nearly eleven. I remember because I'd told Cook to hold the soufflé until Lorna came down, and she never did. I went looking for her at half past. The dock lights were on."*

Vivienne Harrow, same question: *"I was at the table until nearly eleven. I remember because Father had told Marta to hold the soufflé until Mother came down, and she never did. He went looking for her at half past. The dock lights were on."*

Aldous Harrow: *"We were at the table until almost eleven. Dad said to hold the soufflé till Mom came down and she didn't. He went out at half past. Dock lights were on."*

Three interviews. Three separate rooms. Three people who had not sat across a dinner table together, by Sable's careful reckoning, in over four years — Vivienne wintered in Scottsdale, Aldous lived above the yacht club and rarely surfaced before noon, and Edmund took his meals alone in the east study with a tray and a newspaper folded into a barricade.

And yet they spoke as one mouth.

Sable read the transcripts again. Then again. Then printed them and laid them side by side on the desk under the good lamp, the one with the green glass shade that had belonged to the predecessor whose falsified autopsy notes still sat in the bottom drawer like a stone in a shoe.

The phrasing wasn't just similar. It was *identical*. Not the identicalness of shared memory — families do converge on stories, sand down the details, agree on a version the way water agrees on a channel. But memory erodes unevenly. One person remembers the soufflé, another remembers the rain, another remembers nothing but the brandy. Memory is a coastline; everyone's map differs.

These maps matched to the millimeter. Same clause order. Same rhythm. Even the hesitations fell in the same places — all three had paused after "eleven," all three had delivered "the dock lights were on" flatly, without inflection, like a line learned for a school play delivered one time too many.

Sable sat back in the chair until it creaked, and looked at the wall of cards, and understood something with the particular dread of a person who has found the flaw in a forgery: you don't coordinate testimony across decades by accident. You don't even do it by conspiracy, not really. Conspiracies drift. Liars forget which lie they told whom. Thirty years is long enough for any story to grow variations, unless —

Unless someone maintained it. Unless there had been an author.

* * *

Corliss Vane's office occupied the second floor of the old bank building on Harbor Street, above what had been a pharmacy, then a video rental store, then nothing. The brass letters on the door still read VANE & HARROW, ATTORNEYS AT LAW, though the "& HARROW" had gone green with tarnish sometime in the nineties and no one had paid to polish it. Sable climbed the stairs at nine in the morning, past the smell of old paper and radiator dust, and knocked on a door that had once been famous for how hard you had to knock.

"Come in before my tea goes cold, whoever you are," said a voice like gravel settling.

Corliss Vane had been Marrow Bay's lawyer for fifty years. He had drawn up wills for three generations, incorporated the cannery, defended the town against two lawsuits and married — in the loose legal sense — half its property disputes. He was also, according to the county medical society's grapevine, dying: pancreatic cancer, diagnosed in spring, given months, refusing treatment with the same iron courtesy he refused everything else. He sat behind a desk the size of a rowboat, thin as a coat hanger, his skin the color of the fog outside, wearing a cardigan buttoned wrong.

"Mr. Vane. Sable Eriksen. Town coroner."

"I know who you are. You signed my neighbor's death certificate in June. Nice penmanship." He waved at the chair across the desk. "Sit. And don't tell me this is a social call, because nobody visits a dying lawyer socially except other lawyers, and they bill for it."

Sable sat. "I'm updating records on the Harrow family."

Something moved behind Corliss's eyes — not surprise. Recognition. The look of a man watching a tide come in exactly where he'd predicted it would.

"Are you," he said.

"The anniversary dinner is coming up. Thirtieth."

"Is it." He poured tea from a thermos into a cup that had once, judging by the logo, belonged to a defunct marina. His hands shook, but his voice didn't. "You know, Ms. Eriksen — or is it Mr.? Forgive me, I've stopped keeping track of modern conventions—"

"Eriksen is fine."

"Eriksen, then. In fifty years of practice, I have learned that when an official walks in asking innocent questions about the Harrows, the official already knows the answer and wants to know if *I* know they know." He sipped. "So let's save the tea. What did you find?"

Sable had planned a slow approach. Pretext. Softening. But Corliss Vane was a man with no time left and less patience, and something in his directness made the indirect feel obscene.

"The alibis," Sable said. "For the night Lorna Harrow died. All three surviving family members recite them word for word. Identical phrasing. Identical pauses. That doesn't happen naturally."

"No," Corliss agreed. "It doesn't."

"They were drafted."

He set down the cup. Outside, a gull screamed once and gave up. "Drafted," he repeated, tasting the word. "That's a lawyer's word. You've been reading procedure manuals, Eriksen."

"Who drafted them?"

The silence lasted ten seconds. Sable counted them against the tick of a mantel clock that had stopped, apparently years ago, at 11:04 — and thought, absurdly, of dock lights going on at half past eleven.

"You understand," Corliss said finally, "that attorney-client privilege does not expire with the client or the counsel. It expires with neither. It's one of the few immortal things in this town besides the fog."

"I'm not asking you to violate privilege."

"What are you asking, then?"

"I'm asking you to tell me whether a document exists. A draft. Something in your files from the morning of September 15th, thirty years ago. I'm not asking what's in it. I'm asking if it's real."

Corliss looked at Sable for a long moment, and then he laughed — a dry, papery sound with real amusement buried somewhere in it, like a coin at the bottom of a well.

"You're learning," he said. "That's the correct question. That's the question a lawyer would ask." He pushed himself up from the chair, slowly, wincing, and crossed to a filing cabinet older than Sable, its drawers swollen with damp. He unlocked the top drawer with a key from his waistcoat and stood there, hand inside, not pulling anything out.

"There is a file," he said, to the cabinet rather than to Sable. "Matter number 88-0914. It contains a memorandum dated the morning after Lorna Harrow was pulled from the water. It contains notes of a conference held in this office at seven o'clock that morning, attended by myself and one client. It contains, in my handwriting, the narrative framework subsequently communicated to each member of the household — arrival times, seating positions, the matter of the soufflé, the dock lights. It contains the phrase 'the dock lights were on,' written once, and repeated thereafter by everyone who needed to say it, because I instructed that they say exactly that, in exactly those words, so that the account would be consistent under questioning."

Sable's heart was beating somewhere up near the teeth. "The sheriff questioned them within days."

"The sheriff questioned them with questions I helped shape," Corliss said. He turned around. His face was gray and calm, a man reporting the weather. "I was twenty-nine years old. Edmund Harrow was the firm's principal client and the town's principal citizen, and he walked into this office at seven in the morning smelling of brandy and lake water, and he said, *Corliss, my wife drowned, and here is what happened.* And then he told me what happened, and it was not what happened, and I wrote it down anyway, because I was twenty-nine and he was Edmund Harrow and the difference between those two things was the entire architecture of my life."

"Why?" Sable asked. It came out rougher than intended. "Why keep the file? Why not burn it?"

"Insurance," Corliss said simply. "Against him. A man in my position learns to keep one card for every hand he plays dirty. If Edmund ever turned on me, that file was my exit." He smiled thinly. "He never turned on me. We grew old together instead, like a bad marriage."

"Then give it to me. Sign a statement. Testify."

"Ah." Corliss returned to his chair and lowered himself into it with the care of a man handling his own bones. "There it is. The ask."

"It's murder, Mr. Vane. It's always been murder. Your predecessor at the morgue falsified the autopsy — defensive bruises, documented and buried. The law failed her once. You can un-fail it."

"And destroy the estate? Drag Vivienne through criminal court as a co-conspirator for a lie she was fed at nineteen? Put Aldous on a stand to admit he watched his father walk to a dock and said nothing for thirty years?" Corliss shook his head. "You think I haven't run this trial in my head a thousand times? I've cross-examined myself every night for three decades, Eriksen. I always lose."

"You're dying."

"Yes. Thank you for the update."

"You'll die with it. She'll stay in the ground marked *accident*, and the man who killed her will be eulogized at St. Brendan's by people who call him a pillar."

Corliss was quiet for a while. The stopped clock ticked anyway — some phantom mechanism, a habit of machinery. When he spoke again, his voice had changed, dropped its armor.

"Do you know what he said to me, that morning? At the end of it, after I'd written it all down and he was standing to leave? He put his hand on my shoulder — I can still feel the weight of it, I've carried it thirty years like a stone in a coat pocket — and he said, *Thank you, Corliss. This is what family means.*" He looked at his own hands, spotted and trembling. "Family means. As if he'd done me the honor of including me in it. As if complicity were a christening."

"Then take the honor back."

He closed his eyes. "Come back Thursday," he said. "I'll have the file out. I'll sign whatever statement you draft — accurate, complete, admissible. I'll sit in front of a camera if your sheriff has one that works. I'm past fear, Eriksen; the cancer saw to that. I just needed—" He stopped. Started again. "A day or two. To write to my daughter first. She lives in Portland. She should hear it from me before she hears it from a courthouse."

Hope is a stupid, stubborn organ. Sable felt it light anyway, warm and ridiculous, walking down the stairs of the old bank building with fog pressing at the windows like something trying to get in. A dying witness. A documentary trail. Matter number 88-0914. For the first time since Wren had laid that envelope of cash on the desk, the legal road — the clean road, the road where truth went through channels and came out the other end wearing a conviction — had opened ahead, paved and lit.

Sable went home and slept six hours without dreaming, which hadn't happened in weeks.

* * *

Thursday came in gray and colder, the first real breath of autumn off the water. Sable arrived at the bank building at nine sharp with a recorder, a notepad, and a statement drafted at two in the morning in language deliberately plain — no adjectives, no argument, just facts in a row like fence posts.

The stairs smelled different. Under the paper and radiator dust: bleach.

The door to Vane & Harrow stood ajar. Sable pushed it open and knew before seeing, the way coroners know, from the quality of the silence in a room.

Corliss Vane was at his desk, upright in his chair, cardigan buttoned correctly for perhaps the first time in years. On the blotter lay his teacup, rinsed clean and turned upside down to dry. Beside it, squared neatly to the desk's edge, lay a single sheet of paper in his own unmistakable hand:

*Resigned. Tired. No statement. C.V.*

No date. No witness signature. No mention of a file.

The filing cabinet's top drawer hung open and empty, its lock undamaged, opened with a key. The shelf where matter files had lived showed a clean rectangle in the dust, one folder wide.

Sable stood in the doorway for a long time, letting the room explain itself. There was no sign of struggle because there had been none. There was no note of accusation because the man who could have written one had chosen, in his last hours, to leave three lines that said nothing — or had been persuaded, gently, by someone with a key and a lifetime of standing in this office, that three lines saying nothing was the kindest ending available.

The sheriff's deputy who came to take the initial report was polite and unhurried. Natural causes, he said. Man had cancer, everybody knew. Sad, but a mercy, really.

Sable signed the coroner's portion of the paperwork with a steady hand and a mind running white-hot. *Natural causes.* Of course. Even his death would be filed in the town's tidy alphabet. Even his silence would be notarized.

Walking out past the brass letters — VANE & HARROW, the tarnish creeping letter by letter toward the ampersand, as if the name itself were being slowly eaten — Sable understood the shape of the thing at last. The legal road hadn't closed. It had never existed. Every door in Marrow Bay opened onto the same room, and Edmund Harrow had been standing in it for thirty years, holding the key to the filing cabinet, waiting for anyone who got close enough to need locking out.

But a man who cleans a dead lawyer's office the night before a statement is signed is a man who knows exactly what was coming. Which meant somewhere in that house, someone had talked — or someone had listened.

Sable stood on the sidewalk in the cold, and thought of the extra place setting laid for a guest who hadn't confirmed, and of Wren's face when asked about the letters, and of one question arriving now with terrible punctuality:

*How did Edmund know?*
