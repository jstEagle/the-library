## 17. Anniversary Eve

The boathouse smelled of fresh varnish and old lies.

Wren stood in the doorway of it at dusk, the last light coming off the water in long copper ribbons, and understood — finally, completely — that the room had been built for them. Not for a guest. For a body. The new planks on the floor were laid where the old dock boards had rotted through. The iron ring bolted to the wall was polished bright, though nothing had ever been tied to it. Someone had measured thirty years of memory and rebuilt it plank by plank, and they had done it carefully, lovingly, the way you prepare a nursery.

They had rehearsed today. Wren had watched from the treeline as Marta laid the table with the good china, as Aldous stumbled through his toast with a glass of water because Vivienne had confiscated the wine, as Edmund Harrow stood at the head of the table and practiced smiling at an empty chair. The chair at the end. The extra place setting. Wren's place, if Wren came. Wren's place, if Wren didn't.

*Either way,* the letters had promised, *the seat will be filled.*

Wren's hands were steady. That surprised them most. Thirty years of being no one — of flinching at the name Harrow, of practicing a stranger's signature until it fit their hand like a glove — and now, standing in the replica of the room where their mother had died, they felt only a terrible clarity. Fear was still there, yes, coiled somewhere beneath the sternum like a drowned thing waiting to surface. But above it sat something harder.

Tomorrow night, someone intended to finish what Edmund Harrow started.

And Wren was done running from a house that still, after everything, felt like home.

* * *

Sable found the office lights burning when they came back from the council chambers, where the last appeal on the exhumation order had died a quiet bureaucratic death — three votes against, one abstention, Edmund Harrow's lawyer smiling like a man watching a tide go out. Sable had stood in that wood-paneled room and listened to the law close its doors one by one, and had thought: *so this is how it feels when the map runs out.*

The office wasn't empty. Wren sat on the edge of the desk, not behind it — never behind it, they always left Sable's territory inviolate — turning the brass letter opener over in fingers that Sable had watched play a Harrow waltz from memory without once looking down.

"You're back late," Wren said.

"The council killed the exhumation." Sable set their coat over the chair. "Edmund's counsel filed a motion about 'desecration of remains' and 'family dignity.' The dignity of a man who beat his wife's bruises into the autopsy report." Sable heard their own voice come out flat and cold and did nothing to warm it. "Corliss is dead. Marta's testimony is hearsay without corroboration. The sheriff won't act on a thirty-year-old drowning based on the word of a cook and a drunk. Every legal road out of this town is washed out, Wren. I've checked them all twice."

"I know." Wren set down the letter opener. "That's why I'm going tomorrow."

Sable went very still. It was a coroner's stillness, the kind they'd learned over a hundred death scenes — the body's noise shutting off so the mind could work. "Going where."

"Dinner." Wren smiled, and it was the worst smile Sable had ever seen on them, bright and brittle as frost on a window. "I have an invitation. Well. A place setting. Marta confirmed the menu this morning — pheasant, the same as 1994. My mother's favorite. They're setting a place for me whether I accept or not, so I thought it was time I RSVP'd."

"No."

"You haven't heard the—"

"No." Sable crossed the room in four strides and took Wren by the shoulders, and felt them tremble under their hands — fine-boned, deliberate, terrified. "Listen to me. You walk into that house as Rowan Harrow, you are walking into a trap that has been *furnished*. There is a boathouse up there with a new iron ring in the wall. They built it for you. Do you understand? Someone measured out your death the way a carpenter measures a doorframe, and you want to walk through it wearing your real name?"

"If I don't," Wren said quietly, "they'll come for me anyway. That's what the letters said. *Unless the past is proven first.* And we couldn't prove it, Sable. We tried. The law wouldn't take the past, so the past is going to collect its debt itself, and it will send whoever it sends, and I would rather be standing in that dining room when it happens than hiding in some rented apartment waiting for footsteps on the stairs."

"Then we call the sheriff. Tonight. We put deputies at that dinner, we put a wire on you—"

"And Edmund's oldest friend in the world decides how many deputies show up, and which door they guard?" Wren shook their head. "You told me yourself. He offered you a promotion to look away. The sheriff's department isn't a road out of Marrow Bay. It's another wing of the house."

Sable let go of them and turned away, pressing both hands flat against the filing cabinet — Lorna Harrow's folder was in the second drawer, third from the top, and Sable knew its position the way other people knew their own heartbeat. Duty said: *call it in. Report the threat. Document your refusal to act on it. Protect yourself.* Duty had a form for this. Duty had always had a form.

But duty had also signed off on a falsified autopsy. Duty had buried Lorna Harrow under *accidental* for thirty years while her killer poured wine two streets away. Duty, in Marrow Bay, wore the family crest.

"Sable." Wren's voice came soft behind them. "I need to say something, and then you can hate me if you want."

"Don't be dramatic."

"I'm going either way. I'd just—" a breath, unsteady, "—I'd rather not go alone."

Sable closed their eyes. Behind their lids was the photograph of Rowan Harrow at eight years old, gap-toothed on a dock that no longer existed; behind that, the bruise-patterns in a dead woman's autopsy notes; behind that, the memory of Wren's hands finding middle C without looking, the way a child learns, the way a son or daughter learns in a house with a piano. Everything in Sable that had spent three weeks insisting this was a case — a file, a retainer, a problem with a procedure — finally, quietly, gave up the argument.

"If you go," Sable said, turning around, "you go as my guest. Official capacity. Coroner's office conducting a wellness check on a household with a documented threat history. I'll sign the paperwork tonight. If anything happens in that room, I am the one who certifies the scene, and no one — not Edmund, not the sheriff, not God himself — touches that dining room before I do."

Wren stared at them. "You'd put your name on it. Your license. Your whole life here."

"My whole life here," Sable said, "is currently standing in my office planning to get murdered at a dinner party. So yes. I'll put my name on it."

Something broke open in Wren's face then — not tears, not quite, but the thing underneath tears, the aquifer. They crossed the space between them and Sable met them halfway, and for a long moment neither of them said anything at all, because there was nothing left to decide. The choice had already been made, weeks ago probably, in a late-night office over cold coffee and a dead woman's file. Tonight was just the paperwork.

"They'll know what I am the moment I stand up," Wren murmured against Sable's shoulder. "Rowan Harrow, back from the dead at his mother's anniversary dinner. Whatever they've planned, it happens fast after that."

"Then we're faster." Sable pulled back and held their face between both hands — those hands that knew the piano, that forged a stranger's signature for twenty years, that were shaking now and didn't try to hide it. "You don't leave my sight. You don't eat, you don't drink anything you didn't pour. When Edmund gives his toast, you watch Vivienne — not him. He's the performance. She's the machinery. And if I stand up and say the word *tide*, you get behind me and you stay there, whatever happens, whatever you see. Swear it."

"I swear."

"Wren."

"*Rowan,*" they corrected softly, and almost smiled. "Tomorrow, you'd better practice."

Outside, the fog was coming in off the bay the way it did every October, swallowing the harbor lights one by one, and somewhere up the coast road the Harrow house burned yellow against the dark — twelve candles lit in the windows, Sable would learn later, one for each guest, and a thirteenth in the highest window that no one admitted to lighting.

* * *

Neither of them slept well, and neither pretended otherwise.

At four in the morning Sable found Wren in the office again, dressed already in dark clothes, sitting on the floor with their back against the filing cabinets and Lorna's folder open across their knees. Sable lowered themselves down beside them, shoulder to shoulder, and read the familiar pages upside down — the defensive bruises, the falsified timeline, the photograph of a woman who had smiled like her child.

"When I was small," Wren said into the dark, "she used to tell me the bay kept everything. Coins, keys, promises. She said nothing given to the water was ever really lost, it was just filed." A hollow laugh. "She'd have liked you. Terrible filing system, this town. But you keep everything."

"That's the job," Sable said. "Somebody has to remember the names."

Wren closed the folder. In the gray pre-dawn light their face looked young and old at once — eight years old and thirty-eight, the drowned boy and the living stranger, all of them showing. "When this is over. If I'm still—" They stopped. Started again. "I'm going to need a new name. Not Harrow. Not the false one either. Something that's actually mine."

Sable reached over and took their hand. "We'll pick it together."

The words hung there — *we* — plain as a signature, undeniable as a coroner's stamp. Neither of them took it back.

By seven, the fog had thickened until the world ended ten feet past every window. Sable typed up the wellness-check order and signed it, the pen scratching loud in the silence. Wren stood at the window with their arms crossed, watching nothing, waiting for the light to change.

At dusk, they would drive up the coast road together. At dusk, the thirteenth candle would be burning.

And in the boathouse, Wren thought, watching the fog erase the bay their mother had loved and died beside — in the boathouse, the water was already in.
