## 18. The Toast

The Harrow house had not changed.

Rowan had prepared for it to have changed — for the sea to have eaten a wing of it, for the wallpaper to have been replaced, for time to have done its ordinary work on the place the way it does on every house in every town that isn't Marrow Bay. But standing at the top of the gravel drive as the last light went amber over the water, Rowan understood with a lurch that this was the cruelest thing about the house: it had refused to change at all. The same shutters, the same salt-scabbed clapboard, the same lantern by the door burning behind green glass so that everyone who arrived looked slightly drowned already. Thirty years had passed through this house like weather through a window left open, and it had kept nothing of them.

Except, perhaps, the smell. Underneath the beeswax and the roast and the cut peonies Marta must have arranged that afternoon, there was still the faint mineral breath of the sea coming up through the floorboards, the way it always had, the way it had come up through the dock planks the night Lorna Harrow went into the water and did not come back out.

"You're doing the thing," Sable said quietly beside them. "Where you go somewhere else."

"I'm doing the thing where I stand outside my own house like a burglar."

"It's your house."

"It was never my house." Rowan smoothed the front of their jacket — Sable's suggestion, something unremarkable, something that would let them pass the first thirty seconds unnoticed — and found their hands were not entirely steady. "It was my mother's prison. Then it was my grave. I'm only here to haunt it properly."

Sable's hand found theirs, brief and warm and grounding, gone again before anyone could see. "We go in together. We leave together. Whatever happens between those two things—" They didn't finish it. There wasn't a version of *whatever happens* worth finishing tonight.

Inside, the dining room had been dressed for memory. Twelve places at the long table, white linen, the good silver that only came out of its felt wrappings for anniversaries and funerals, and in Marrow Bay the line between the two had always been drawn in pencil. Candles burned low and orange. A fire ticked in the grate. And at the head of the table, rising from his chair with the unhurried grace of a man who had never once been surprised by anything, stood Edmund Harrow.

He was eighty-one years old and he carried it like a tailored coat. His hair was white as gull feather, his hands folded neatly before him, and when his eyes swept the room and passed over the stranger at the door — the coroner's plus-one, nobody, furniture — they did not pause. Not even a flicker. Rowan felt the strange vertigo of being invisible inside their own name.

"Friends," Edmund said, lifting a glass of pale wine. "Family. Thirty years ago tonight, we lost a light in this house. We gather each year not to mourn her — Lorna would have hated mourning, she'd have called it self-indulgence with better china — but to remember her. To keep the table set for her." He gestured, gently, to the thirteenth place setting at the far end, laid and waiting, untouched all these years. "To love is to set a place that may stay empty. That is the whole of it. That is the whole of marriage, of family, of—"

Rowan rose.

They hadn't decided to. Or rather, they had decided it weeks ago, on the bluffs, in Sable's office, in a hundred small rehearsals against the ceiling of the dark — but in the moment it happened without decision, the way a wave happens, the way water finds the hole in the hull. Their chair scraped back against the floorboards and the sound went through the room like a shot.

"—of grief," Edmund finished, and turned.

For one full second, father and child looked at each other across thirty years and twelve feet of candlelit linen, and neither of them breathed.

Then Rowan said, "You always did give a beautiful toast, Father. Mother used to say you could make a eulogy feel like a compliment."

The room did not fracture all at once. It fractured the way ice fractures on a pond — first one hairline crack of silence, then a spreading web of small sounds: a fork set down too carefully, Vivienne's sharp intake of breath, Aldous's glass tipping and bleeding wine across the cloth while he stared without seeing it. At the far end of the table, Marta stood frozen in the doorway with the soup tureen in both hands, her knuckles white as the porcelain, her eyes fixed on Rowan with an expression that was half terror and half something older and fiercer — the look of a woman watching a bet she had placed with three decades of her life finally come due.

Edmund recovered first. Of course he did. He had been recovering from things for eighty-one years; it was the family trade.

"I'm afraid you have the advantage of us," he said, mild as milk. "You are?"

"My name is Wren." Rowan let the false name sit in the air for exactly as long as it deserved, which was no time at all. "But you knew that the moment I spoke. You've known since the letters started coming. You've known since the boathouse was stocked and the guest list was copied and the menu was printed from a card thirty years old." They took a step along the table, and the candles threw their shadow long and wavering up the wall, over the portraits, until it seemed to touch the smallest frame — Lorna, painted at twenty-six, smiling the way people smile when the painter insists. "You've known because you built this dinner around me. Every seat. Every dish. Every word of that toast. You've rehearsed me into this room, Father, the way you rehearsed everyone's alibi into the morning after she died."

"Sir," Edmund said to the room at large, with a small, regretful smile, "appears unwell. Perhaps someone might—"

"My name," Rowan said, louder now, letting it fill the whole house the way it should have filled it thirty years ago, "is Rowan Harrow. I am the son of Lorna Harrow, who is buried under a stone that says *beloved wife* as though that were the sum of her. I am the boy this family reported drowned off the point eight years after she died — a boy whose body was conveniently never found, because bodies that don't exist can't ask questions." Another step. The wine-dark stain on the cloth spread toward the candles like a tide. "I am the extra place setting. And I am here to tell you that the drowning you're all so faithfully commemorating tonight was not an accident, was not a tragedy, and was not — whatever you tell yourselves when the fog comes in and the house creaks — God's will."

Vivienne made a sound. Small, bitten-off, the sound of a hinge rusting shut. She sat very straight in her chair with her hands flat on the linen, and her face had gone the color of the tablecloth, and her eyes — her eyes were not on Rowan at all. They were fixed on her father with an intensity that had nothing of surprise in it. That was the thing Rowan would remember afterward, in the bad hours, in the retelling: Vivienne was not shocked. Vivienne had been waiting for this the way one waits for a diagnosis. Her brittle calm, all those interviews, all those word-for-word alibis recited like scripture — it had never been composure. It had been a woman holding a door closed with her whole body for thirty years, listening to something scratch on the other side.

Edmund set down his glass. The click of crystal on wood was very soft and very final.

"Young man," he said, and his voice had changed — dropped its warmth the way a stage drops its curtain, revealing the machinery behind, "you will leave this house."

"No," Rowan said. "I live here. I lived here before any of you learned to lie about it. And I'm not leaving until someone at this table says her name and means it. Lorna. Say it. Not *we lost a light*. Not *the table stays set*. Her name. Who put her in the water?"

Aldous was crying. Silently, sloppily, the tears cutting channels through the flush of drink, his mouth working around words that wouldn't come — and Rowan saw it happen, saw the exact moment thirty years of bought silence hit the limit of what money could hold down. Aldous opened his mouth. Looked at his father. Looked at the window, and beyond it the black water of the bay, and whatever he saw there made him close his mouth again and put his face in his hands.

Coward, Rowan thought, and then, immediately, forgive him, forgive him, he was nine years old —

"Enough."

Edmund's voice cracked across the room like a whip over water. And then he did the thing that chilled Rowan more than anything the night would offer — more than the accusations, more than the confession gathering itself in the corners of the room. He composed himself. Smoothed his jacket. Turned, deliberately, away from Rowan, dismissing them, erasing them with the oldest trick in the patriarch's book — and his eyes moved once, briefly, around the table.

Not to the door. Not to the phone. Not to the sheriff's empty chair.

To Vivienne.

It lasted less than a second. It was nothing — a glance, a flicker, the kind of look a man gives a clock or a weather report. But Rowan had spent a lifetime learning to read this family the way sailors read sky, and there was no mistaking it. It was not a plea. It was not fear. It was an instruction. *Handle this.* The same look, Rowan understood with a cold that started in the spine and radiated outward, that he must have given her thirty years ago, standing at the head of another table, after another dinner, before another trip down to the dock.

And Vivienne — Vivienne nodded.

Just barely. Just the smallest inclination of her chin, her hands still flat on the linen, her face still paper-white and perfectly still. But she nodded. And then she rose, smoothing her skirt, and said in a voice pitched for the whole room, bright and brittle as thin ice:

"This has all been very upsetting, and I think our guest needs air. Let me show him to the veranda. The fog's coming in — it's beautiful this time of year."

Her hand closed around Rowan's arm above the elbow. Her grip was gentle. Her grip was also, unmistakably, immovable — the grip of a woman who had spent thirty years practicing how to steer people, and had gotten very, very good at it.

Across the table, Sable was already on their feet, chair toppling backward, and Marta had set down the tureen with a crash that nobody acknowledged, and somewhere upstairs a door slammed in a house that had held its breath for three decades and could hold it no longer.

"The veranda's lovely," Vivienne murmured, close to Rowan's ear, her voice sweet as syrup poured over something with teeth. "But you should know — the boathouse path floods at high tide. It's why we stopped using the dock." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "You really oughtn't wander down there alone."

* * *

The veranda doors closed behind them, and the noise of the dining room cut off as though a lid had been set over it. Fog pressed against the railing, thick and moving, eating the garden, eating the lawn, eating the dark shape of the boathouse below — except that Rowan could still see it, or imagined they could, the new lock gleaming wetly where the old rotted latch used to hang.

Vivienne released their arm and stepped to the rail, folding her hands, looking out at nothing.

"He'll deny everything," she said conversationally. "He always has. It's remarkable, really — you could put a gun in his hand and he'd testify the gun acted alone." She turned her head, and in the fog-light her face was terrible in its calm. "I used to wonder if you'd come back. When the reports said the boat went down, I didn't believe it. I never believed anything this family said. That's my curse — I'm the only honest one, and honesty in this house is just another way of drowning."

"Vivienne." Rowan's voice came out steadier than they felt. "Thirty years ago. Were you at the dock?"

She smiled. It reached her eyes, which was the worst part.

"Sweet Rowan," she said. "You've read the file. You know the alibi. We *all* know the alibi." Below them, unseen, the boathouse door swung open on its new hinges, silent as a held breath, and the fog rolled in through the gap like something being invited home. "The question was never whether we were there. The question is whether anyone will ever say so — and darling, look around you. Nobody ever has."

From inside the house came the sound of raised voices — Sable's, low and hard; Edmund's, smooth as oil on water — and then, beneath both, a third sound that stopped Rowan's heart: the front door opening, footsteps on gravel, more than one pair, moving fast, moving down.

Moving toward the water.
