## 1. Postmark

The bell over the door of Okonkwo's Books had not rung in nine days, and I had begun to think of it less as a bell and more as a retired colleague—still technically employed, no longer expected to perform. So when it did ring, on a gray Tuesday in October, I looked up from my ledger with the particular suspicion of a man who has learned that good news rarely announces itself with a jingle.

It was the postman. Marcus, twenty-six years old, allergic to conversation, carrying a stack of mail for a shop that sold, on a good week, four paperbacks and a coffee. He dropped the stack on the counter, nodded once—the nod of a man acknowledging another man's continued existence—and left. The bell jingled him out. I envied him that.

I let the mail sit while I finished reconciling a column of numbers that refused, as usual, to reconcile. This is a habit I picked up in twenty-two years of police work: never approach a thing eagerly. Eagerness is how you miss the detail that matters. Also, I'll admit, it is how a coward buys himself another minute of ignorance, and I have never claimed to be anything but an efficient coward.

The stack was the usual archaeology. An invoice from the distributor, printed in a font that suggested the distributor was also struggling. A flyer for a harvest festival I would not attend, hosted by people who would not notice my absence, which was the entire point of my attendance policy. A utility bill with the word FINAL underlined twice, as if the first underline hadn't communicated the urgency. The electric company and I had a relationship built on mutual disappointment.

And at the bottom of the stack, an envelope.

I want to be precise here, because precision is the only religion I have ever kept, and I have kept it badly. The envelope was cream-colored, heavy stock, the kind you buy at a stationer's rather than a supermarket. My name and address were written in blue ink, in a hand that slanted slightly rightward, with a crossbar on the lowercase *t* that hooked like a question mark. The *R* of my name had a little flourish at its leg, an unnecessary elegance, a hand that had been taught cursive by someone who loved it.

I knew that hand.

I had spent eleven months of my life staring at that hand—on receipts, on birthday cards to her sister, on the guest register of the Harborside Inn, on a grocery list found in a coat pocket and logged as evidence, exhibit forty-one. I had testified, in a deposition that never mattered because the case closed itself before it could, that the handwriting on a note found in the Voss kitchen was consistent with Ada Voss's known writing samples. I had been very confident. Confidence was the thing I was best at, back then, and look what it got everyone.

The postmark read: HOLLOW CREEK, and the date was the fourteenth of March, seven years ago.

The day after Ada Voss's funeral.

* * *

Here is what you need to know about the shop, so you understand what I was risking by sitting there with that envelope in my hands. Okonkwo's Books occupies the ground floor of a building that should have been condemned twice and was condemned once, in 2011, before I bought it with the last of my pension and a loan from a bank that clearly wanted the collateral more than the repayment. It smells of mildew and old glue and the lemon oil I rub into the shelves every Sunday, a ritual that is equal parts maintenance and prayer. The mystery section is in the back, by the window that fogs, because I have a sense of humor about these things. The history section is in the middle, where the damp gets it first, which is a different kind of joke, one the building makes at my expense.

I bought the shop because I wanted to disappear. Not dramatically—not faked deaths, not bus tickets to inland towns, none of that, though I would learn later how much of that there was around me all along. I wanted the ordinary kind of disappearing, the kind where you become a fixture, a thing people stop seeing. A man who sells books. A man who waters the fern out front and never quite kills it. A man whose name, after enough years, gets shortened or mispronounced or forgotten entirely, until he is just *the bookshop*. I had spent two decades being seen, and it had gone badly for everyone involved, so I chose the opposite with the discipline of a convert.

Seven years. That's how long the trick had held. And now the past was at my counter, in cream-colored stock, with a hooked crossbar on its lowercase *t*, knocking politely.

* * *

I made tea. This is not a non sequitur; it is procedure. When a thing arrives that can change your life, you do not open it standing up with your hands shaking. You sit down. You do something small and repetitive with your hands. You give your body a job so your mind can do its work. I had learned this from Inspector Halloran, back when I was twenty-four and he was the sharpest instrument in the county constabulary, a man who could read a room the way I read a ledger—finding immediately the column that wouldn't reconcile. *The body lies before the mind does, Rafael,* he used to say. *So give the body something dull to hold.* He taught me everything. I want that on the record before what comes later, whatever later turns out to be. He taught me everything, and I believed all of it, for a very long time.

The tea was the cheap kind, in a mug that said WORLD'S OKAYEST FISHERMAN, a gift from Dot Calloway three Christmases ago. Dot lives next door, in the cottage with the blue door, and she was the county coroner for thirty years, which means she and I share a professional history I will come to, in time, the way you come to a bruise: eventually, wincing, because not touching it doesn't make it heal.

I sat at the counter with the tea and the envelope, and I did what I always do with evidence, even evidence that has no right to exist: I looked at it before I touched it further.

The stamp was ordinary. The postmark, as I said, was the fourteenth of March, seven years past. The ink had faded to that soft gray-blue that ink goes when it sits in a dark place for years—someone's drawer, someone's box of unfinished business. The envelope had not been recently posted. It had been *kept*. And then someone had put it in the mail now, or slipped it into a mailbag, or dropped it through the slot of the sorting office with the old stamp still on it, knowing—or not caring—that the machines would pass it through. Postmarks, I have learned, are a courtesy, not a guarantee.

Or. And this was the thought that made my tea go cold in the mug. Or it had been posted seven years ago and lost, and had only now found its way to me, the way water finds the crack in a foundation. The past doesn't knock politely, I told myself. It doesn't knock at all. It just seeps.

But my hands knew better. My hands, which had held that grocery list, exhibit forty-one, and turned it under the lamp for an hour, knew that handwriting the way you know a voice on the telephone. And my hands were not shaking because of a postal anomaly.

I opened it with a knife from the drawer, the way I'd open anything I might need to preserve, and the blade went through the paper with a sound like a small sigh.

* * *

One page. Cream stock matching the envelope. The same blue ink. The same hooked crossbars, the same flourished leg on the capital letters, the same slight rightward slant that handwriting analysts call, with a straight face, "forward-leaning," as if the writing were eager to get somewhere.

I read it standing up after all. Some things you cannot do sitting down.

*Rafael,* it began. No *Dear*, no *Detective*, no honorific. Just my name, in her hand, the way she'd written it on the checkout card of *Rebecca* the year before she died, the year before she stopped coming to the shop because her husband had started coming with her, standing too close, answering questions she hadn't been asked.

*You will want to burn this. Burn it or believe it, but do both quickly, because there isn't much time and I am so tired of being dead.*

*The body in the casket was not me. You always suspected. I saw it in your face at the funeral—you were the only one there doing arithmetic instead of grieving. I am sorry for what that has cost you. I am sorry for what it is about to cost you. If you are reading this, then someone has decided you should, and I have learned that when someone decides something in this town, there is usually an invoice attached.*

*Do not go to the prosecutor. Do not go to Halloran. Above all, do not go to the courthouse, because there is a man there now who did not kill me, and if you pull this thread the way you pull every thread, he will hang for a murder that never happened, and I will have to be dead forever, and I have found that I am very bad at it.*

*I was at your shop on a Tuesday in the autumn. You sold me a book about lighthouses and you told me the keeper's wife used to signal ships in code when the keeper was drunk. You said, "Everybody's hiding something, miss. The trick is picking what's worth the signal." I have thought about that every day for seven years. I am signaling now. I am sorry the code is so plain.*

*A.*

I read it three times. Then I did what any reasonable man would do with a document that unhinges seven years of settled history: I checked the details, because details are where liars live and where the truth keeps its spare keys.

The lighthouse book was real. *Sentinels of the Atlantic Coast*, a remaindered hardcover I'd sold for six dollars to a woman with a bruise she'd covered with a sleeve in July and stopped covering with a sleeve in September, because by then she'd stopped caring who saw. The story about the keeper's wife was real—I tell it to anyone who stands still near the maritime section. The phrasing, *picking what's worth the signal*, was mine, or close enough to mine that the difference didn't matter.

The funeral arithmetic was real too. I had done it. I had stood at the back of St. Brendan's with my hands folded like a good mourner and done the arithmetic: the closed casket, chosen by the family; the coroner's certificate signed with unseemly speed; the husband's note arriving four days later, typed, which had struck me then as odd and struck everyone else as nothing at all; the town's relief rolling off the church roof like heat off a road. Everyone had wanted it closed. Closure, I have come to believe, is a word invented by people who have never actually lost anything, or by people who have lost something and want very much for you to stop looking at the place where it used to be.

I had done the arithmetic, and I had said nothing, and I had sold books for seven years, and I had told myself that this was peace.

* * *

The bell rang again at four o'clock. Dot, carrying a casserole dish, because it was Tuesday and Tuesdays were the night she decided I was incapable of feeding myself, a theory with considerable supporting evidence.

"You've got a look," she said, setting the dish on the counter next to the ledger. She is seventy-one, built like a dock bollard, with reading glasses pushed up into white hair that she cuts herself, and her eyes are the pale gray of a winter harbor. Coroner's eyes. Eyes that have looked at everything and flinched at very little. "The look you used to get. The ledger look."

"I'm doing the ledger."

"You're doing the ledger *wrong*, is what you're doing. You've been on the same page since I could see you through the window twenty minutes ago." She peered at me, and I did what I have done for seven years, which was arrange my face into the shape of a retired man with nothing in his pockets but receipts. "Casserole's chicken. Don't let it sit."

"Thank you, Dot."

She paused at the door, and for a moment—half a moment, less—she looked at me the way you look at a word you've read a thousand times that has suddenly stopped making sense. Then the bell jingled her out, and she was gone up the walk to the blue door, and I was alone with a letter that had been written the day after a funeral and delivered the day before my life was supposed to stay quiet forever.

* * *

I locked up at six, which is early, which is always, and I took the letter into the back room and put it in a plastic sleeve from the box I keep for the first-edition dust jackets, because if I was going to lose my mind over a piece of paper, the paper was going to be preserved properly. Habit. Halloran again. *Respect the document, Rafael. Documents outlive the people who write them. That's their whole trick, and ours.*

Then I sat on the stool in the back room, under the bulb that flickers, and I let myself think the thing I had been not-thinking since Marcus dropped the mail on the counter.

The letter was either a forgery or it wasn't. If it was a forgery, it was a magnificent one, produced by someone with access to Ada Voss's writing samples, her biography, her reading history, my own conversational habits, and a supply of seven-year-old stationery—and produced for a purpose I couldn't yet see, though I could feel the shape of the purpose the way you feel a bruise through a coat. If it wasn't a forgery, then Ada Voss was alive, and the body in the casket belonged to some other daughter, some other sister, some other wife, and a man was sitting in a cell right now—because I'd heard the talk, the town talks, the town is a telephone exchange with a harbor—waiting to stand trial for her murder.

And if she was alive, then the letter was right about one more thing, the thing I liked least.

*You were the only one there doing arithmetic instead of grieving.*

She had seen me. At the funeral, in the back of St. Brendan's, seven years ago, she had been alive and watching and she had *seen me*, and she had known what I was doing, because she was the kind of person who did arithmetic too, and I had spent seven years believing I was the only one in the church who hadn't been fooled.

The bulb flickered. The fern in the front window, the one I never quite kill, stood in the dark glass like a witness.

I am a retired man, I told myself. I sell books. I water a fern. I eat Dot's casseroles and I do not reconcile my ledger and I have spent seven years building a small, damp, ordinary life on top of a case I helped close and never once believed was closed.

But I had also spent seven years being a man who notices things, and here is the thing I noticed, sitting on that stool with the plastic sleeve in my hands: the letter had said *do not go to the courthouse*, and it had said *do not go to Halloran*, and it had known enough to warn me off both.

Which meant that whoever wrote it—Ada, or the forger, or the past itself, seeping through the foundation—knew exactly where I would run first.

The bell over the door didn't ring. Nobody came in. But somewhere out in the dark, on the harbor road, the past had finished knocking politely, and I had the distinct, cold, familiar feeling that it was about to let itself in.
