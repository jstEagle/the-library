## 3. Trial Date

The courthouse in Marrow's Bend was built by men who believed justice should feel like a cold bath—invigorating, unpleasant, and over quickly if you kept your head down. Granite steps worn into shallow bowls by a century of nervous shoes. A rotunda that smelled of floor wax and old coffee and the particular despair of public institutions. I'd spent eleven years of my working life walking up those steps, and seven years of my retirement pretending they didn't exist.

The letter had been sitting on my kitchen table for two days now, under a ceramic dish shaped like a pheasant that Dot gave me for Christmas one year as a joke neither of us found funny anymore. I hadn't touched it since the second night, when I'd read it a third time and then put it down the way you put down something hot.

*You were right to doubt. I'm sorry it took this long to say so.*

That was the line that did it. Not the handwriting—I could have talked myself out of the handwriting, told myself some cruel prankster had practiced until their loops matched Ada Voss's loops. But *you were right to doubt.* Nobody knew I doubted. Not officially. Not in any way that survived the paperwork. The case closed itself, tidy as a book snapping shut, and everyone agreed to pretend the snap had been a verdict.

So on Monday morning I locked the shop, hung the sign that said BACK IN AN HOUR even though I had no intention of being back in an hour, and walked to the courthouse before I could talk myself out of it.

Retirement, I've learned, is mostly the art of talking yourself out of things. You get very good at it. It's the only skill the job develops reliably.

* * *

The clerk's office still smelled like toner and the particular brand of institutional lemon that seems to be manufactured exclusively for government buildings. The woman behind the counter was new—young enough that she'd have been in middle school when Ada Voss disappeared—and she looked at me with the polite blankness of someone who has been warned about strangers but not specifically about me.

"I need the docket information," I said. "State versus Marcus Voss."

Her fingers hovered over the keyboard. "Are you counsel?"

"No."

"Family?"

"No."

"Press?"

I considered lying. It would have been easy. But there's a version of yourself you have to live with at three in the morning, and mine is already crowded enough.

"Former police," I said. "Retired."

Something flickered behind her eyes—the small recalibration people do when a name or a face connects to a story they half-remember. She typed anyway. Whatever she'd heard, curiosity beat caution, or maybe she just didn't care. Young clerks never care. That's their great advantage.

"Trial's set for the fourteenth," she said, turning the monitor toward me. "Five weeks out. Judge Prentiss presiding."

Five weeks. I looked at the date on the screen and felt something in my chest that wasn't quite dread and wasn't quite excitement, though it wore the clothes of both. A clock, starting. Somewhere in the last forty-eight hours the letter had wound a spring inside me, and now here was the countdown it had been waiting for.

"Anything else?" she asked.

"The prosecution. Who's handling it?"

"Dana Whitfield. Her office is upstairs, but—" She hesitated, and the hesitation told me everything. "She's not really taking meetings on this one. High profile."

"Everything's high profile in a town this size," I said. "A lost dog makes the paper."

She almost smiled. Almost. Then she printed me a docket sheet with the solemnity of someone handing over state secrets, and I took it because taking it was free, and walked upstairs anyway.

* * *

Dana Whitfield's office was on the second floor, corner room, windows facing the square. I remembered her from years ago—a junior prosecutor back then, all elbows and highlighters, the kind of young lawyer who annotated her own annotations. Now her name was on the door in brass, and the woman behind the desk had the composed stillness of someone who had learned, expensively, to stop fidgeting.

"Rafael Okonkwo." She said it before I'd finished introducing myself, which meant she'd known who was coming the moment the clerk called up, which meant the clerk had called up, which meant the machine still worked exactly the way it used to. Some things about a town never change; they just change hands.

"Ms. Whitfield."

"Sit, please." She gestured at the chair across from her desk without getting up herself. On the wall behind her hung her bar admission certificate and, below it, a framed photograph of the courthouse steps at dawn, which struck me as the kind of thing you hang when you want visitors to know you love this building more than they ever will.

"I'm not here to cause trouble," I said.

"No?" She folded her hands. "Then you're here for the weather."

"I'm here because I received a letter."

The stillness didn't crack, but it shifted, the way ice shifts—not breaking, just redistributing its weight. "What kind of letter."

"The kind I'm not going to describe to you in an open office with a door that doesn't lock."

For a moment I thought she'd throw me out. I could see her considering it, running the arithmetic—old detective, dead case, five weeks to trial, press already circling like gulls over a fish market. Then she got up, crossed the room, and closed the door. Not locked. Closed. In Whitfield's world, apparently, that was a concession of enormous magnitude.

"Talk," she said.

"A letter postmarked the day after Ada Voss's funeral. Seven years ago. Arrived Saturday." I watched her face while I said it, the way I used to watch faces for a living, and what I saw was not surprise. That was the part that made my stomach drop. Surprise I could have worked with. What I saw instead was confirmation—someone hearing a thing they'd already braced for.

"Postmarked seven years ago," she repeated carefully, "and delivered Saturday."

"That's what I said."

"That's not possible."

"Neither is a dead woman writing letters, Ms. Whitfield, and yet here we both are, having this conversation instead of doing our jobs."

She sat back down. Picked up a pen, set it down again. When she spoke, her voice had dropped into a register I recognized from courtrooms—the one lawyers use when they're about to say something they don't want repeated.

"Do you know what happens if that letter becomes real? If it becomes evidence, or a leak, or a headline?" She didn't wait for me to answer. "Marcus Voss walks. Not because he's innocent—though between us, off the record, his defense is thinner than soup—but because the state's entire theory collapses. We convicted a dead man by suicide note and tried his brother for the murder nobody can prove happened. If Ada Voss is alive, there is no murder. There's no case. There's no closure for that family, no settlement of that estate, no—"

"There's an innocent man on trial," I said.

Whitfield stopped. Looked at me for a long moment, and something human moved behind the prosecutor's mask—briefly, like a fish under ice.

"You think I don't know that?" she said quietly. "I've read that file until I can recite it. I know the timeline has a hole in it you could drive a hearse through. I know the brother-in-law's alibi is weak and his motive is weaker and the only reason he's sitting in a cell right now is that somebody had to be charged and he was standing closest. I know all of it." She straightened a stack of papers that didn't need straightening. "And I also know that if I walk into that courtroom in five weeks and start asking questions I already know the answers to, the whole town comes apart. Do you understand what I'm saying? Not the case. The town."

"People aren't load-bearing walls," I said. "They can survive the truth."

"Can they?" She laughed, but there was nothing funny in it. "This town spent seven years building a story about what happened to Ada Voss, and the story is the only thing holding up the peace. Take it out and see what falls. Half the council donated to the reward fund. The other half signed the petition to close the case. Everyone's fingerprints are on this, Okonkwo. Including yours."

"Including mine," I admitted. "Which is why I'm here and not burning the letter."

"And what do you want?"

It was a fair question, and I noticed I didn't have a clean answer. What did I want? To be wrong. To be right. To sleep through a whole night without the pheasant dish on my kitchen table seeming to watch me. To find out whether the doubt I'd carried for seven years like a stone in my shoe had finally earned its keep.

"I want to know if the letter is real," I said. "Before the fourteenth. Because if it is, and you try that man with a murder that never happened, then whatever peace this town bought, it bought with a man's life. And I've seen what happens to towns that pay that bill. They don't get a receipt."

Whitfield was quiet for a while. Through the window behind her I could see the square, the war memorial, the bakery with its hand-lettered specials, all of it sunlit and ordinary and built, apparently, on top of something.

Here's what I'll offer," she said finally. "Nothing. Officially. Unofficially—if you find something real, something a judge won't laugh at, bring it to me and I'll listen. But if you go poking around trying to resurrect a dead woman, I will have you removed from every public building in this county, and I will enjoy it." She stood, which was my cue. "You were good at this once, they say. Retire again, Mr. Okonkwo. It suits the town better."

"It doesn't suit me," I said. "That was always the problem."

* * *

I came down the courthouse steps slower than I'd gone up, which I chose to attribute to the granite rather than to age. The square was doing its mid-morning performance: mothers with strollers, old men arguing amiably outside the hardware store, a kid dragging a wagon full of pumpkins toward the church. Normal. Staged-looking, almost, the way normal looks when you've started suspecting it's a set.

Five weeks. The number sat in me like a swallowed coin.

Because here's the thing Whitfield had said without meaning to, the thing that had slipped past all her careful lawyer language: *his defense is thinner than soup.* She knew. The prosecutor knew the case was rotten, and she was going to try it anyway, on schedule, because the schedule mattered more than the truth. The clock wasn't starting today. It had started seven years ago, and everyone had agreed to let it run out.

Except someone hadn't. Someone had written a letter in a dead woman's hand and mailed it into the past like a message in a bottle, addressed—to whom? Not to the prosecutor. Not to the courts. To me. To the one person in Marrow's Bend whose doubt had never made it into the file.

*I'm sorry it took this long to say so.*

I stood at the bottom of the courthouse steps and let the morning move around me, and I understood, with the clarity that only arrives after you've been refused, that I was already in it. There was no version of the next five weeks where I went back to the shop and alphabetized biographies and let a man I believed innocent walk into a courtroom alone. The letter hadn't asked me for anything. It hadn't needed to. It had only needed to arrive.

I turned toward home, and that was when I saw Nell Voss across the square, standing in front of the bakery window without looking at the bread, watching me with the fixed attention of someone who has been waiting a long time for a specific person to walk out of a specific building.

She didn't wave. She didn't cross the street.

She just kept watching, and I kept walking, and somewhere between us the whole quiet town seemed to hold its breath.
