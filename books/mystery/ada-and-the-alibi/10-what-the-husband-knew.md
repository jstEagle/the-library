## 10. What the Husband Knew

The dead are terrible correspondents. They never answer letters, they leave their affairs in the hands of people with agendas, and they expect you to do all the talking. Marcus Voss had been dead seven years and was already proving more forthcoming than half the living town of Marrow's Bend—I just had to learn how to read him.

I started where all honest research starts: in the back room of a failing bookshop, at a table I'd bought secondhand from a church that had no further use for it, surrounded by a box of files I'd sworn to Dot I'd thrown away years ago.

I hadn't thrown them away. That's the thing about men like me. We tell ourselves we've retired from the work, but we keep the tools oiled. A man who truly wanted to forget would not have kept a copy of every page of the Voss file in a banker's box labeled GARDENING — MISC. There is no gardening in that box. There has never been any gardening in that box. Dot has never once looked inside it, which tells me she understood exactly what it was, and loved me enough to let me lie to myself in peace.

Marcus Voss, then. Let me tell you what the town remembered of him, because the town's memory is a curated thing, pruned like the roses outside the courthouse.

The town remembered a grieving husband. Handsome in a weathered way, the kind of handsome that photographs well at funerals. He ran the marina, sat on two boards, coached little league until his voice got too loud for it and they gently retired him from that too. When Ada disappeared on the fourth of October, he gave interviews with red eyes and a steady jaw. When her body washed up three weeks later near the old tannery pilings, he identified her through the glass, wept once, beautifully, and thanked the community for its prayers.

And when he walked into the water off Miller's Point eleven months later, leaving a typed note on the seat of his truck that said *I did it. I can't live with what I did. Forgive me. M.V.* — the town closed the book, held a small service, and moved on with the relief of a jury that wants to go home.

That was the story. I'd helped write it, or at least failed to unwrite it, which amounts to the same thing on your conscience.

Here's what I remembered that the town preferred not to: Marcus Voss could barely type. I knew this because Ada used to come into the shop and complain about it, laughing, in the way people laugh when the thing they're describing is actually a wound. *He writes everything by hand,* she told me once, running her finger along the spines of my reference section without looking at any of the titles. *Little notes, lists, grocery things. His handwriting looks like a spider drowned in ink. He says typing is for secretaries.*

She said it lightly. But I was a detective for twenty-two years before I was a bookseller, and I noticed that she said it twice more over the following year, always lightly, always the same phrasing, the way a person returns to a loose tooth.

*I did it. I can't live with what I did. Forgive me. M.V.*

Typed. On the seat of his truck, folded under a stone, weighted against the wind off the point.

At the time, everyone read it as a man too ashamed to face his own hand. The prosecutor called it *the final admission of a broken conscience.* Halloran himself — and I'll get to Halloran, God help me, I'm getting there — Halloran stood at the press conference and said, *Sometimes the note tells you everything. This one does.*

I was standing ten feet away when he said it, and something cold walked down my spine and out through my shoes, and I ignored it, because ignoring things was becoming my profession.

Now, seven years later, with a letter in Ada's handwriting sitting in my safe like a lit fuse, I took the box marked GARDENING — MISC off the shelf and spread Marcus Voss's life across my back-room table.

* * *

Suicide notes are a genre. People think they're confessions, but mostly they're instructions — apologies, blame assignments, small domestic logistics. *Cancel the paper. Tell my mother it wasn't her fault. The insurance policy is in the blue folder.* They're written by hand almost always, even by people who type everything else, because a note like that comes from the body before it comes from the mind. Handwriting analysts will tell you the slant changes, the pressure drops, the baseline sags. A typed note is a document. A handwritten note is a wound.

Marcus left a document.

But here's what I hadn't known then, because I'd been shut out of the final review — here's what I pulled from the copies of the coroner's supplemental report that Dot had slipped me, years ago, in a manila envelope with no note, the way friends hand you grenades:

The note was typed on a machine that didn't match anything in the Voss house. The forensic examiner flagged it. The typewriter evidence — the spacing, the worn *e*, the slight vertical misalignment of the *t* — matched an IBM Selectric. The Voss household contained a laptop, a desktop, and a fountain pen collection. No typewriter. No Selectric. The examiner wrote, in dry bureaucratic prose that nonetheless manages to convey a raised eyebrow: *Source machine not located among effects.*

The response from upstairs, in the margin, in Halloran's handwriting — I recognized the heavy forward slant, the way he crossed his sevens: *Not material. Note content consistent. Close it.*

*Not material.* The source of a dead man's last words was not material. I had signed off on that closure myself, tired and grieving and wanting the town to stop bleeding, and I had never seen that marginal note, because Halloran reviewed the file before it reached me and after it left me, and somewhere in between, the question died quietly in a margin.

A typed note from an unknown machine. A confession written on equipment the confessor didn't own.

You don't need to be a detective to see where that points. You just need to be willing to look, and willingness was the thing this town had voted against, unanimously, seven years ago.

* * *

Money, though. Money was where Marcus finally started talking to me, and what he said made my tea go cold in the cup.

I spent two days at the county records office being pleasant to a clerk named Bernice who has hated me since 2011 over a disputed parking space, and whose hatred turned out to be negotiable at the price of my agreeing, explicitly and on the record, that she had been right about the parking space. I agreed. I have no shame left; I sold the last of it around chapter four of my life.

Bernice pulled the probate file, and I copied pages until the copier complained, and what emerged was a portrait of a man dismantling himself in careful increments.

In the week before Marcus Voss walked into the water at Miller's Point, four things happened to his finances.

One: he liquidated his marina shares. All of them. Sold at a discount to a holding company called Bluewater Holdings LLC, which, I want to stress, appeared nowhere else in his life, had no other transactions, and dissolved itself fourteen months later like a soap bubble.

Two: he emptied the joint account he shared with Ada — the account that should have been frozen or at least complicated by her death, except that death certificates move slowly and banks move fast when a living signature card holder asks nicely. Forty-one thousand dollars, withdrawn in cash over three days, in amounts just under the reporting thresholds. Somebody knew those thresholds intimately. Somebody with either banking experience or law enforcement experience, and I know which one keeps me up at night.

Three: he paid off the mortgage on the house outright. A cashier's check, drawn on an account I couldn't trace to any of his known funds. The house went to his brother — Daniel Voss, the man now sitting in a cell awaiting trial for a murder that apparently required a corpse to commit.

Four: he bought a life insurance policy rider eleven days before his death, naming Daniel as beneficiary. Two hundred thousand dollars. The insurer paid without contest. Suicides within the contestability window get scrutinized; this one sailed through with paperwork so clean it looked ironed.

Add it up. A man planning suicide does not sell his business at a discount, drain joint accounts in threshold-skimming cash withdrawals, settle debts, and buy his brother a quarter million dollars of insulation. A man planning suicide ties up loose ends. This man *paid* his loose ends. Generously. Systematically. Like a contractor settling accounts before leaving town.

Or like a man being paid to leave town, passing some of it along to the family he was about to gut.

Because that's the arithmetic nobody in Marrow's Bend had been willing to do: if Marcus killed Ada, why did his death make his brother rich? And if Marcus didn't kill Ada — if his confession was purchased, ghostwritten on a stranger's typewriter, delivered along with an envelope of cash — then the money trail runs backward, upstream, to whoever filled the envelope.

Confessions can be bought. I've seen it done with love, with fear, with leverage. It costs more than you'd think, buying a man's last words. You're not just paying for the note. You're paying for everything he won't say afterward, which is everything.

* * *

I sat with that thought for most of an evening, in the shop, with the CLOSED sign up and the lamp doing its yellow pool of light on the counter, and I did what I always do when a case turns ugly: I talked to the dead man out loud, because Dot isn't there to tell me it's morbid.

"All right, Marcus," I said. "Let's say someone came to you. Someone you trusted, or feared, or owed. Let's say they laid it out — your wife's gone, the town needs an ending, and you're the ending. Here's what happens: you sign a note, you take a drive to Miller's Point, and your brother gets taken care of. Your name goes in the ground instead of hers."

The dead man declined to correct me.

"Except," I said, tapping the probate copies, "you were a coward about some things and brave about others, and I think you were brave about this one, in the end. I think you did what they asked. I think you even believed maybe you deserved it — a man who put his hands on his wife doesn't need much convincing that the world would run smoother without him. But I think you left me a message anyway. Typed, because you couldn't bear to write it in your own hand. *Not my words. Not my machine. Look closer.*"

That's generous, probably. Dead men don't leave messages; they leave evidence, and evidence only speaks to people who ask. But the Selectric detail gnawed at me. Where does a man in Marrow's Bend find an IBM Selectric in 2018? Not a home. Not an office — the marina kept a laptop and a receipt spike. Libraries had phased theirs out. Law offices still had them, some of them, for wills and deeds, the documents where authenticity matters.

Police departments had them, too. Older precincts. Records rooms. The kind of machine that sits in a corner and types official statements, transcriptions, formal things.

I made a list of every Selectric in a fifteen-mile radius, which took me forty minutes and consisted of exactly three entries, because I'd spent thirty years in this county and I know where the old machines live. One belonged to a title company in Corbett Falls. One belonged to St. Ambrose parish office, Father Lundgren's ancient relic he refused to replace on principle.

And one belonged to the Marrow's Bend Police Department, retired from active use in 2009, never surplused, last logged in the property inventory as stored — per Inspector T. Halloran's personal authorization — in the records annex basement.

I looked at that line for a long time.

There's a particular sensation that arrives when an investigation stops being a puzzle and starts being a corridor, when every door you open reveals another door directly behind it, all of them leading the same direction. Detectives learn to dread it. The public thinks it's thrilling. It is not thrilling. It is the feeling of walking toward a house where you already know someone is waiting with the lights on, having heard your car coming up the drive.

Halloran taught me that feeling, actually. Twenty-six years ago, a rookie with good instincts and bad posture, and he put his hand on my shoulder in the records room and said, *Okonkwo, when the evidence all points one way, don't fight it. Walk straight at it. The truth doesn't ambush you — it waits politely. It's the lies that chase.*

Walk straight at it.

I looked across the shop at the wall where I keep the photographs — authors, mostly, and Dot at the Christmas party, and one faded picture of a graduating class of county detectives, 1999, young faces squinting into the sun. Halloran front and center, arm around my shoulders, grinning like a man who owns the future.

Ada's letter sat in the safe. The bus stub and its threat sat beside it. Somewhere inland, a woman with shaking hands pretended to prune hydrangeas. And in five weeks minus however many it had already cost me, Daniel Voss would stand in a courtroom and answer for a murder that had been solved before it was charged, purchased and packaged and gift-wrapped with a ribbon of typed remorse.

I turned off the lamp. In the dark, the shop smelled the way it always smells — paper, dust, the ghost of Dot's coffee — and I stood there a moment letting the last piece settle into place, the piece I'd been avoiding since the postmark.

Someone with money bought Marcus Voss's confession. Someone with access to a police typewriter produced it. Someone with the authority to wave away the mismatch, to write *not material* in the margin of a dead man's file, buried the question before it learned to walk.

There was exactly one person in Marrow's Bend who fit all three sentences, and he had taught me everything I knew about looking.

Tomorrow I would drive out to Cedar Hollow and knock on Inspector Thomas Halloran's door, and I would bring nothing but questions, because I was not yet ready to believe what the evidence was telling me.

I locked the safe twice. Old habit. New necessity.
