## 7. The Town That Keeps Secrets

The archive lived in Coral's head, but it had a filing system anyway.

That was the thing people never understood about a photographic memory. They assumed it was like a camera—point, click, done—and that the pictures just sat there gathering dust until something jogged them loose. But Coral had learned early that an unsorted memory was worse than no memory at all. It was a house where every drawer was open and every paper was blowing around in the wind. So at nine years old, sitting on Marisol's couch with a blanket over his legs and Hillcrest still smoking behind his eyes, he had begun to build rooms.

There was a room for weather—the hailstorm of April 1998, the summer the reservoir dropped so low they found a 1962 pickup truck with a fishing pole still in the bed. There was a room for meals—the exact order of pies at the Harvest Fair every year since 2001 (rhubarb first, always rhubarb, old Mrs. Tavares got there at dawn). There was a room for kindnesses and a smaller, colder room for cruelties, and he tried not to visit that one, but it kept excellent records.

And there was a room for secrets.

He hadn't meant to build that one. It had assembled itself, the way sediment becomes stone. People told Coral things because Coral listened the way other people prayed—with total attention and no judgment—and because everyone knew, somehow, that what went into Coral stayed in Coral. He never repeated a secret. He simply kept it, cross-indexed, dusted, waiting for a purpose.

For twenty-two years, the purpose had been nothing more than keeping. A librarian of other people's sins, shelving volumes nobody ever checked out.

Tonight, standing in his kitchen at one in the morning with a legal pad and a pot of coffee going cold beside him, he finally checked one out.

* * *

He started where any archivist would: with the dead man.

Dale Pruitt. Fire inspector, retired eleven years now, though he'd kept his badge on a lanyard around his neck right up until the reservoir took him. Coral pulled the file from memory the way you'd slide a folder off a shelf.

Dale Pruitt had inspected Hillcrest Home for Children four times between 1999 and 2003. Coral remembered the inspections themselves only as atmosphere—a heavy man in a short-sleeved dress shirt, sweat rings under his arms, clipboard, the smell of cigarettes and aftershave. But he remembered the aftermath perfectly, because children are natural archivists of adult moods. After every inspection, Miss Ruthie—the caretaker—would be different for days. Tighter. The good cereal would disappear and come back after a week. Once, Coral recalled with a small cold shock, she'd made them all practice saying "we love living here" in cheerful unison, like a choir.

He wrote on the pad: *Pruitt saw something. Or was paid not to.*

Then he did what he'd never once done in twenty-two years of keeping the town's secrets. He opened the cruelty room and started taking things out.

* * *

Item one came to him before he even finished his coffee, which was how he knew it had been waiting.

The social worker. Gloria Fenwick. Coral had her filed under *Kindnesses, Complicated*, because she was the one who'd brought him and Ada to Marisol's door after the fire, and she'd cried real tears doing it, and she'd sent birthday cards for three years until the cards stopped. But she was also the one who'd signed the placement paperwork putting them back at Hillcrest in the first place—Coral had seen her signature on his own file when he was sixteen and had talked his way into the county records office by claiming to be researching a school project. Her signature sat directly above a line certifying the home "meets all standards for habitation."

Two weeks before the fire.

He wrote her name down and stared at it. Gloria, who smelled like peppermint and drove a car with a cracked dashboard. Gloria, who had held Ada's hand in the back seat and told her the bad place was gone forever.

Somebody had to sign those papers. Somebody had to look at Hillcrest and say yes, this is fine, put the children here. And then when the place burned down with their caretaker inside, somebody had to make sure nobody asked why the inspection said it was fine.

Gloria's tears, Coral thought, might have been real. Tears usually are. That doesn't make them clean.

* * *

Item two took longer, and arrived by way of a barbecue.

In the summer of 2011, Coral had attended the Delgado family cookout because Marisol had catered it and needed someone to carry trays, and Coral carried trays the way he did everything—with total attention. He had stood near the garage for forty minutes holding a plate of ribs while two men talked on the other side of the half-open door, unaware or indifferent to being overheard.

One voice belonged to Ray Kowalczyk, who owned Kowalczyk Construction and had built half the new buildings in town, including, Coral happened to know, the county annex. The other voice was younger, nervous, wheedling. A subcontractor named Pete, complaining about a job.

"You want I should do it to code, Ray, it costs you. You want it cheap, don't ask me to sign off like it's code."

"Nobody's asking you to sign anything," Ray had said, easy, amused, the voice of a man who had never once been nervous in his life. "You think Dale Pruitt signed off on everything to code? You think that man ever climbed a ladder in his life? Half this town passed inspection on a handshake and a bottle of bourbon. Ask him about Hillcrest sometime. Oh wait." A laugh. "Can't. Sealed records. Isn't America great?"

Pete had laughed too, the nervous laugh of a man who wanted very badly to be on the right side of a joke.

Coral had filed it—*Kowalczyk, cuts corners; Pruitt, takes bribes; Hillcrest, sealed*—and moved on with the tray of ribs, because at the time it was just another secret, one of hundreds, weightless. Tonight it weighed like a headstone.

Ray Kowalczyk. The contractor who cut the fire code. If the wiring was cheap and the exits were blocked and the inspector was bought, then Hillcrest hadn't burned down. It had been built to burn.

His pen pressed so hard it nearly tore the page.

* * *

Item three he already had, and had carried for years without knowing its weight.

Judge Edwin Marsh. Coral had appeared before him exactly once, at seventeen, for the misdemeanor of climbing the water tower, and had spent the entire proceeding memorizing the judge's chambers out of boredom: the flag, the gavel with the crack in the handle, the framed photo of Marsh shaking hands with a governor, and—this was the detail that mattered tonight—the narrow door behind the bench, half-hidden by the flag, that led to a records room. Coral knew because the bailiff had stepped through it to fetch a file, and Coral's eyes had followed, and his memory had kept the room: floor-to-ceiling cabinets, brass locks, a green banker's lamp, and a single shelf set apart from the others, higher up, with gray boxes on it and a typed label he could still read letter by letter if he let himself.

*HILLCREST — SEALED PER ORDER — 11/03/03.*

November third. Nine weeks after the fire. Long enough for the smoke to clear and the questions to start, short enough that nobody had gotten answers yet. Judges didn't seal records because there was nothing to find. Judges sealed records so that finding became impossible.

Coral wrote *Marsh* at the bottom of the list and looked at the four names together for the first time.

Pruitt. Fenwick. Kowalczyk. Marsh.

Inspector. Social worker. Contractor. Judge.

It wasn't a list of suspects. It was an org chart.

* * *

He sat back and let the room spin slowly around him, the way he did when he wanted his memory to talk to itself instead of to him. This was the trick of it, the part nobody taught you: you couldn't force a connection. You could only lay the pieces out and wait for the pattern to notice you.

And the pattern said: these four men and women had never needed to conspire in any dramatic sense. No candlelit meetings, no signed oaths. That wasn't how towns worked. Towns ran on a gentler currency—favors, debts, the slow accumulation of *you scratch my back*. Dale Pruitt took bourbon money to pass inspections because everybody did. Ray Kowalczyk cut corners because everybody did. Gloria Fenwick signed placements because the alternative was leaving children with nowhere to go, and the system didn't pay her enough to be a hero. And when the worst thing possible happened, each of them protected not the conspiracy but themselves—one signature, one sealed file, one shrug—and the shrugs locked together like fingers and became a wall.

A cover-up didn't need a mastermind. It only needed four ordinary cowards who trusted each other's silence.

And somewhere in that silence, two children had almost died, and one caretaker had actually died, and nobody had ever been asked a hard question about any of it.

Until now.

* * *

Coral looked at his own name in his mind—not written anywhere, he hadn't dared—but present anyway, the way a word is present in a language you refuse to speak. Because here was the thought he'd been circling since the reservoir, since the jacket, since the second body:

Whoever was killing these people knew about Hillcrest. Knew the players. Knew the order of guilt, maybe. And was staging the scene with *his* belongings, item by item, like someone building an argument he'd be forced to read.

Someone who knew Coral kept secrets. Someone who knew he'd never told anyone about Hillcrest—not the inspections, not Miss Ruthie's tightness afterward, not the choir practice of *we love living here*. Twenty-two years of perfect silence, and whoever this was had bet everything on that silence continuing. Had bet that Coral would rather hang than open the archive.

The killer wasn't just framing him. The killer was *counting on* him.

That was either the cruelest thing he'd ever heard, or—

He stopped. Set down the pen. His heart was doing something strange, a slow double-beat, like a record skipping in a familiar song.

Or it was someone who loved him. Someone who knew that the only way to make Coral Quintana turn his gift outward—out of the archive, out of the polite silence, into the light where it could actually *do* something—was to make silence lethal. To make his own protection the trap.

Only one person had ever known about the rooms in his head. Only one person had helped him build them, back at Marisol's kitchen table, two kids sorting the wreckage of Hillcrest into piles so it wouldn't crush them. *This drawer is for the fire,* she'd said, age ten, deadly serious, drawing little tabs on index cards. *This one is for after. Don't mix them up, Coral. Mixing them up is how you drown.*

Ada.

He hadn't let himself think the name in full for six days. Now it arrived whole, unbidden, wearing her voice.

"No," he said aloud, to the kitchen, to the coffee, to nobody. "No, she wouldn't—"

But even as he said it, some deep filing clerk in his mind was already pulling records. Ada, who at ten had made a list of every adult who'd failed them and hidden it inside a hollowed-out dictionary, and when he'd found it she'd snatched it away and said, *Someday I'll finish it.* Ada, who had a memory too—not photographic like his, but photographic in the way that matters, the kind that keeps pain at original resolution, no fading, no mercy. Ada, who vanished at eighteen with one duffel bag and a note that said *Don't look for me. Live your life. One of us should get to.*

He looked down at the legal pad. Four names. Two of them already dead.

Pruitt, crossed out by the reservoir. Fenwick, crossed out by whatever had happened to her yesterday—he'd have to check, but the sheriff's face at the diner had told him enough.

His hand was shaking. He noticed it the way he noticed everything, catalogued it, filed it under *fear*, and then—deliberately, for the first time in his life—refused to file anything else. Some things shouldn't go in drawers.

Because there was one more record surfacing now, one he'd kept in the deepest room, the room he'd built at twelve and locked at twelve and never entered again: the night of the fire itself. Smoke. Screaming. Miss Ruthie's voice saying something he'd never been able to retrieve, something Ada had heard too, something that had made Ada grab his hand in the yard and say, *We saw nothing. We were asleep. Say it, Coral, say we were asleep.*

He had said it. He'd been saying it for twenty-two years.

And now somebody was killing the people who'd built the lie, one name at a time, and framing him for it, and the only person alive who knew why he'd spent two decades lying about a fire was the girl who'd made him promise to.

Coral stood up so fast the chair went over backward. He grabbed his keys, then stopped, staring at the hook by the door where his spare set should have been.

Empty. Of course. The killer had those too.

He laughed—a short, unhinged sound in the quiet kitchen—and took the bus instead, because apparently he was the kind of man who could lose his car keys to a murderer and still worry about parallel parking.

The county records office opened at eight. Judge Marsh's chambers, if Coral remembered the docket correctly—and he remembered everything correctly, that was the whole cursed point of him—held a motions hearing until noon, which meant the sealed room behind the flag would sit empty and unlocked for four hours.

Four hours to find out what November third had buried.

He was halfway down his front steps when Marisol's porch light snapped on across the yard, and her silhouette filled the doorway, arms crossed, and Coral understood with sinking certainty that his midnight absences had finally run out of explanations.

"Coral Quintana," she called into the dark. "Where exactly do you think you're going?"
