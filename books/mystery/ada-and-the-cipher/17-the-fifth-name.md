## 17. The Fifth Name

The kitchen at three in the morning had become Coral's second office, which was a strange thing to say about a room with one window, a humming refrigerator, and a table Marisol had given him because "a man can't decode his sister's murder notebook on the couch like some kind of animal."

He'd covered the table in index cards. Of course he had. There were forty-one of them now, arranged in a grid so precise that a surveyor could have used it to plat a subdivision. Each card held a fragment of Ada's cipher—the rotating alphabets, the number pairs, the little marginal doodles she'd used as nulls to punish anyone who tried to read the thing linearly. He knew, because he'd tried to read it linearly for six years and gotten nothing but headaches and one very convincing anagram of *pancakes*.

But tonight the layers were peeling.

It had happened the way it always happened with him—not in a flash, but in a slow settling, like sediment finding the bottom of a glass. The first layer had been names. The second, dates. The third, which he'd cracked only two nights ago over a pot of coffee he would later describe as "a decision," was the part that kept him up: her account of Hillcrest, written in the careful block letters of a nine-year-old who already understood that adults read children's notebooks when the children weren't looking.

*Mr. Voss locked the pantry again,* one entry said. *Coral cried and I told him the dark was just a room with the lights off. I don't think he believed me. I didn't believe me either.*

Coral had sat with that one for a long time. Not because it hurt—though it did, in the dull way old wounds ache before rain—but because he couldn't remember it. That was the joke of his life, the cosmic punchline he'd stopped telling at parties: a man with a memory like a courthouse vault, and the one file missing was the one that mattered. Everything before the fire was there in perfect detail—the smell of the linoleum, the way Ada chewed her pencil, the exact number of stairs from the second floor to the porch (fourteen, with the seventh one loose)—and then the night itself was a blank page, clean as snow.

He'd always assumed the fire had burned it out of him. Trauma did that; Marisol had told him so gently, years ago, her hand on his shoulder, and he'd accepted it the way you accept weather.

Tonight, decoding by the light of the stove, he wasn't sure he accepted anything anymore.

Because there was a fourth layer. He could feel it the way you feel a stair that isn't where your foot expects it. The third layer ended mid-page, in the middle of a sentence about the pantry, and below it the cipher changed—shifted key, shifted hand, the letters tightening from a child's roundness into something leaner. Ada had come back to this notebook as an adult. She'd built a lock inside a lock, and she'd built it recently, and she'd built it facing inward.

Which meant it was meant for someone who could open it.

Which meant it was meant for him.

"Okay," Coral said aloud, to the refrigerator, which hummed supportively. "Okay. Let's see what you've got."

He worked until the sky went from black to the color of dishwater, and somewhere around five-forty, with his hands cold and his heart doing something arrhythmic and unprofessional, the fourth layer opened like a door.

It was not a confession. He'd braced for a confession. It was not even a letter, exactly. It was a list.

Four names, each followed by a date, each date followed by a single word: *witnessed*. Voss the inspector. Delgado the social worker. Crane the contractor. Judge Ellery Marsh. Four names, four dates—and Coral checked them against his index cards with fingers that wanted to shake and mostly succeeded—four deaths. The drowned man in his jacket. The retired social worker with his car keys. The contractor whose confession had vanished along with his recorder. And the judge, found yesterday morning in a house full of sealed files, dead in his sleep with the peaceful face of a man whose conscience had finally been relieved of duty.

Four names, crossed out in four different inks, because Ada had done them at different times, in different places, and had never once owned more than one pen at a time. Even now, even after everything, that detail undid him a little. His sister had planned all of this with the logistics of a general and the stationery budget of a nine-year-old.

And then there was the fifth line.

There was no fifth name. There was a blank space where a name should be, and above it, in the adult hand, tight and deliberate, a heading:

**THE ONE WHO REMEMBERS EVERYTHING EXCEPT THE THING THAT MATTERS**

Below the heading, the cipher continued, and Coral decoded it line by line, and with every line the kitchen got colder, or he got colder, it was hard to say which.

*I tried for six years to reach him. Letters he never answered—I know now they were intercepted, or lost, or both. So I came back. I took his clothes because he would notice. He notices everything. That was always the point of him.*

*I wore his jacket into the water and left it on a dead man because grief makes people look closely, and looking closely is what Coral does instead of feeling.*

*I gave him my notebook's address in the only language we ever really spoke.*

*I am not framing my brother. I am assigning him homework.*

Coral set down the pencil.

He stood up. He sat back down. He looked at the page for a long time, the way you look at a medical report, hoping the words will rearrange themselves into something survivable.

She had staged the murders—no. He made himself be precise, because precision was the last solid floorboard in the house. She had committed the murders. Four men were dead, whatever they had done, whatever the fire had been, and no amount of childhood pantry-locks made that anything other than what it was. But the frame—the clothes, the keys, the recorder, the witness at the reservoir—that hadn't been designed to convict him. It had been designed to *activate* him. Every piece of evidence against Coral Quintana was also, if you turned it over and squinted, a clue addressed to Coral Quintana. A scavenger hunt with a body count. A message in a bottle, except the bottle was his own life, and she'd smashed it at his feet to make sure he'd pick up the pieces.

*He notices everything,* she'd written. *That was always the point of him.*

"That was always the point of me," Coral repeated, and heard how it sounded, and laughed—one short, awful syllable with no humor in it whatsoever.

Because here was the thing, the thing sitting in his chest like a swallowed stone: it had worked. He was furious, and he was frightened, and underneath both, shamefully, patiently, like a pilot light he'd thought went out years ago, something in him was almost *grateful*. Someone had finally taken his gift seriously. Not as a party trick, not as the town's filing cabinet, not as the weird kid who could recite the license plates of every car at his own mother's funeral. As a weapon pointed at the truth. Ada was the only person alive who had ever looked at Coral Quintana and seen not a curiosity but a *key*.

And she'd spent four lives getting his attention.

"No," he said, louder, to the refrigerator, to the index cards, to the ghost of a nine-year-old chewing her pencil. "No, you don't get to do that. You don't get to make me the plan."

But the page disagreed. The page said, in the next paragraph, in handwriting gone suddenly small, the way writing goes small when the writer is crying and trying to hide it:

*I need him to remember because I can't prove any of it alone. My word is worth nothing. I'm the girl who ran. But his memory—if he remembers what he saw that night—it isn't testimony, it's architecture. Juries believe buildings.*

*And if remembering breaks him, then I'm sorry, Coral. I'm sorry, I'm sorry, I'm sorry. But they broke us first, and nobody wrote *that* down anywhere official, so somebody has to write it down somewhere true.*

*Fifth name. Last witness. You were always going to be the ending. I just needed you to remember the beginning.*

Coral put his head down on the table, among the index cards, and stayed there while the sun came up over the town of Ada, illuminating, in order: the reservoir, the diner, the sheriff's office, the ruins of Hillcrest on its hill like a pulled tooth, and one kitchen window where a man was learning that he had never been the detective in this story at all.

He'd been the prize.

* * *

Marisol found him there at seven, still at the table, having achieved the particular dignity of a man who has slept sitting up against a salt shaker. She came in through the back door with her usual announcement—"I brought eggs, don't argue"—and then stopped, because Coral's face must have done something, because Marisol had spent thirty years reading faces across a diner counter and had never once needed a cipher to do it.

"Coral." She set the eggs down without setting them down, if that made sense—the carton hovering, undecided. "What happened?"

"I finished the notebook," he said.

Her face did something too. Something quick and closed, like a drawer being pushed shut with a hip. He saw it. Of course he saw it. Seeing things was the whole tragedy of him.

"There's a fifth name," he said. "Except it isn't a name. It's a job description." He slid the decoded page across the table. "It's me, Mari. The whole time, it was me. The frame, the bodies, the breadcrumbs—she wasn't building a case against me. She was building a case *through* me. I'm not the suspect." He heard his own voice go flat and strange. "I'm the star witness. She killed four people to subpoena me."

Marisol read the page. He watched her read it, and he watched the exact moment—the little catch of breath, the hand coming up to her mouth—when she reached the part about the pantry, about Mr. Voss, about a boy crying in the dark while his sister lied to him about lights.

"You knew," Coral said quietly. "About Hillcrest. You told me that. But you didn't know about *this* part. About what she wants from me."

"No," Marisol said. "I didn't know about this part." She sat down across from him, heavily, the chair complaining the way chairs do around honest women. "But Coral, listen to me. Listen very carefully, because I'm only going to say this once and then I'm going to deny it forever." She leaned forward. "Whatever she thinks you saw that night—whatever she's trying to dig up—you have to ask yourself why it's buried so deep. Trauma doesn't bury things that neatly. People bury things that neatly. Somebody helped you forget, and I want you to sit with who had access to a twelve-year-old boy in the weeks after that fire, and who loved him enough to do it."

The kitchen went very quiet. The refrigerator hummed. Somewhere outside, a bird sang the same four notes over and over, like it too was stuck on a loop it couldn't crack.

"Mari," Coral said slowly, "what are you saying?"

"I'm saying," Marisol said, standing up, gathering her keys, not looking at him, "that Ada thinks you're the fifth name on her list. But I count differently. I count a sixth person who was at Hillcrest that night, who signed papers, who drove a scared boy home in a truck that smelled like coffee, and who has been bringing him eggs every Sunday for thirty years." She paused at the door. Her voice, when it came, was steady, which somehow made it worse. "Go to the ruins, Coral. Go today, in daylight, and take the notebook. Whatever's waiting for you there—her, or the truth, or both—they've been waiting a lot longer than you have."

"And you?" he asked. "Where will you be?"

Marisol smiled at him, and it was the smile she gave customers who ordered the meatloaf on a Tuesday, warm and sad and slightly pitying.

"Honey," she said, "I've been at the ruins every day for thirty years. I just call it gardening."

The door clicked shut behind her. Coral sat alone among his forty-one index cards, holding a page that called him the ending, wondering—with a cold, rising, vertiginous certainty—whether he'd just been talking to the beginning.

Then he picked up the notebook, and his coat, and his keys, and headed for the hill.
