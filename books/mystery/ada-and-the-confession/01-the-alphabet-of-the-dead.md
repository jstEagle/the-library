## 1. The Alphabet of the Dead

There is an alphabet for the dead, and I have spent ten years learning to speak it.

It isn't the alphabet you're thinking of—no Latin, no Greek, none of the grand dead languages the seminarians used to boast about over coffee in the refectory. It's a humbler one. A is for *Acta*, the minute books. B is for *Baptismal registers*, water-stained and furred with mildew at the corners like old men's ears. C is for *Correspondence*, which sounds tidy until you've opened a drawer and found forty years of it knotted together with rubber bands that have surrendered their elasticity to history and become, functionally, part of the paper.

I know where everything is. That is my profession, my vocation if you want to be grand about it, and my entire personality if you want to be accurate. Canon Anselm Whitcombe—though everyone called him Father Anselm, and I called him nothing at all, because I have never been good at names out loud—used to say that an archive was a promise made to people who couldn't hold you to it. He said it the way he said most things: mildly, while doing something else with his hands, so that you weren't sure until later whether he'd handed you a blessing or a burden.

Ten years. Ten years of filing the cathedral's records, and I could tell you the exact shelf on which the parish's memory sleeps, down to the box, down to the folder, down to the individual sheet. What I could not tell you, until this week, was why that felt like enough.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Archives teach you chronology. Let me be obedient to my own training.

* * *

The morning of the funeral, I arrived at the cathedral at six-fifteen, an hour before anyone would reasonably expect me, because grief disguised as routine is still grief but at least it's punctual.

I let myself in through the archive door—the narrow one off the cloister walk, the one visitors mistake for a janitor's entrance, which has always suited me fine—and stood for a moment in the dark among the shelves, breathing the particular air of that room. People think archives smell of dust. They don't, not really. They smell of paper and time and the faint vanilla sweetness of lignin breaking down, which is the smell of books slowly becoming themselves. I have loved that smell since I was eleven years old and found my mother's old filing cabinet in a storage unit she'd stopped paying for, and understood, in the wordless way children understand things, that paper keeps what people cannot.

My mother left town when I was twelve. But that's another file, and I am not yet ready to open it. Even now. Especially now.

I turned on the lights, made coffee in the archive's ancient percolator—a machine the canon had christened "the reliquary," because it too held something sacred and no one knew quite how old it was—and began, without deciding to, on the day's work. There is always day's work. That is the mercy of an archive; it does not permit mourning, because there is always something misfiled.

That morning it was the 1987 restoration invoices, which someone—I had my suspicions, and the suspicions were shaped like the former treasurer—had filed under R for *Roof* instead of under the project number, as if roofs were a subject rather than a transaction. I reshelved them. I straightened the spines of the bound registers so they stood at attention. I ran my finger along the 1995 box—not looking for anything, just touching it, the way you touch a banister going downstairs—and moved on.

At seven-thirty the first mourners began arriving, and I heard them the way you hear weather: doors, murmurs, the organ warming up in fragments, a note held and released like a hand letting go.

* * *

The funeral filled the nave by nine.

I did not sit in the nave. I want to be clear about this, because later—when everything unravels and people ask me how much I saw, how long I'd known—I want the record to show that I watched from the choir loft, which is the one place in that entire stone building where I have ever belonged.

The loft sits above and behind the west end, tucked into the organ gallery, and from it the whole congregation is visible as a sea of dark wool and bowed heads, and the altar is distant and bright, and nobody can see you unless they crane around and up, which no one does. For ten years I have taken my lunch up there sometimes, eating sandwiches among the organ pipes, listening to the building breathe. The loft doesn't care that I'm quiet. The loft doesn't care that I'm odd. The loft just holds the music after the music stops, the way an archive holds words after the speakers are gone.

From up there, Canon Anselm's funeral looked almost orderly. Almost alphabetical.

The choir sang first—something Renaissance, all interlocking lines, grief braided into counterpoint. Then the bishop spoke, using the warm generalities bishops keep in stock like vestments: a faithful servant, a gentle scholar, a shepherd of this flock for four decades. All true, as far as it went. The trouble with eulogies is that they are indexes without the text underneath. *See: kindness. See: patience. See: devotion.* They never give you the page.

Then Father Anselm's own words were read—his instructions, written years ago, because of course he'd written his own funeral. He would have considered leaving it to others a failure of documentation. The reader's voice wavered through it: thanks to the parish, forgiveness asked of anyone he'd ever failed, and then a line that made me sit forward against the rail:

*"And to my archivist: put everything in order. Everything. You'll know what that means when the time comes."*

The congregation murmured politely. A few people smiled, thinking it a small joke about tidiness, about me, the person who alphabetizes the hymnals. I smiled too, mechanically, and then stopped smiling, because I had spent ten years learning how the canon communicated, and he did not joke in writing. Not once. Not in ten thousand pages of correspondence, not in his sermons, not in the marginalia he left in the reference books like breadcrumbs for whoever came after him. Anselm Whitcombe wrote exactly what he meant, and he meant exactly what he wrote, and he had just told two hundred mourners and one archivist that there was something to put in order.

*You'll know what that means when the time comes.*

I didn't. Not yet. But the sentence went into me the way a splinter goes into a thumb: small, clean, and destined to fester.

* * *

Afterward, the nave emptied in stages, the way water leaves a lock. First the congregation, flowing out into the bright cold morning, their voices suddenly loud and ordinary—casserole logistics, parking complaints, the price of flowers. Then the family, such as he had: the vestrymen and the old families pressing hands, the women in black hats nodding at each other like a jury reaching verdicts. I recognized them all from ten years of processing their records. The Hallorans, whose donations had built the north transept and whose name appeared in the ledgers more often than some saints'. The Okafors, older money than anyone admitted, though these days mostly represented by Wren, who did the flowers. The others—the Vances, the Merricks, the Delgados—scattered through the crowd like punctuation.

And last, when the nave was nearly empty, the staff. Rosa, the housekeeper, who had kept the canon's house for thirty-one years and who cried the way stone weeps—slowly, invisibly, only the dampness giving it away. She paused at the foot of the loft stairs on her way out and looked up, and for a moment I thought she was going to say something. Her mouth opened. Closed. She pressed her hand flat against the newel post, once, like a woman taking an oath or steadying herself, and walked out into the light.

I noted it. Of course I noted it. I note everything; it's the affliction that comes with the vocation. At the time I filed it under *grief makes people strange*, which is a real category in my head, cross-referenced and well populated.

Then it was just me and the verger dousing candles, and then just me.

* * *

I stayed in the loft until the afternoon light came through the west window and laid a stripe of colored glass across the floorboards—ruby and cobalt and a green the glaziers of four centuries ago got from copper shavings and spite. Below me the nave stood empty, the funeral flowers already wilting at the edges, lilies going soft and brown at the throat. Someone would have to clear them. Probably Wren Okafor would come back for the arrangements she'd made; she always reclaimed the containers. I'd catalogued her flower receipts for years without ever properly meeting her. That was the shape of my life: I knew this town by its paperwork, intimately and entirely, and it did not know me at all.

Here is what I have never said out loud, to anyone, and may as well say now, to this page, which keeps secrets better than I keep friends:

For ten years I believed I was content. I built my days the way I built the shelves—load-bearing, evenly spaced, nothing ornamental. Up at six. Coffee from the reliquary. The day's misfilings hunted down and corrected. Lunch in the loft. Home by six with a book and a frozen dinner and the deep, unglamorous satisfaction of a system maintained. On Sundays I came in anyway, because empty buildings are the honest ones.

Content. I had the word filed under C, along with *catalogue* and *custody* and *careful*, and I never once examined the folder.

But standing in the loft that afternoon, watching the light crawl across the floor where two hundred people had sat grieving a man who had employed me, taught me, fed me soup when I had the flu in 2019, remembered my birthday every single year with a card signed *A.W.* and nothing else—and realizing that I had no one to grieve *with*, that my mourning had no witnesses, that the closest thing I had to family was a coffin being lowered into consecrated ground by men who shared his God but not his handwriting—that was when the crack appeared. Small. Clean. Destined to fester.

Grief, it turns out, is a loneliness audit. Every loss takes inventory of who you have, and mine came back with a count of zero, and the zero had been sitting there for ten years wearing the costume of contentment, and I had shelved it and dusted it and never once read it closely.

I am an archivist. I should have known better than anyone: nothing stays filed forever. Sooner or later every document gets pulled, every box gets opened, every secret waits for its researcher. I just always assumed I'd be the one holding the key, not the one inside the box.

* * *

I went down eventually. I always go down eventually; the loft is a place you visit, not a place you live, though God knows I've tried.

In the archive I hung up my coat and stood before the shelves and did the thing I do when I don't know what else to do: I worked. I pulled the correspondence files for the current year, because the canon's papers would need processing soon—there'd be an estate, a will, lawyers, the slow bureaucratic digestion of a life—and I wanted to be ready. I wanted the folders labeled before grief could make me sloppy.

His last year of letters was thin. He'd been slowing down; we both knew it, though neither of us said it, because we were alike that way, communicating entirely through work performed rather than words spoken. I flipped through the folders—A through M, neat, expected—and reached N.

N was wrong.

Not dramatically. Nothing so useful as a missing folder. Just: the N drawer was a half-inch shallower than it should have been, the way a bookshelf looks when one volume has been turned face-down to hide it. And at the back, behind the folders, where the metal met the wood of the drawer's false bottom—because yes, my predecessor built false bottoms into the correspondence drawers, a habit I'd documented years ago and dismissed as eccentricity—there was a weight.

I stood there a long moment. Down the hall, the cathedral ticked and settled, the way big stone buildings do at dusk, cooling and contracting, speaking their slow language to themselves.

I should tell you I hesitated. Some part of me did—the part trained by ten years of protocol, of provenance and access rules and the sacred boundary between the record and the archivist's curiosity. But a larger part of me heard again that reading voice wavering over the pulpit: *put everything in order. Everything.*

I reached in and drew out a small brass key, old, unlabeled, cool as a coin from a wishing well, and beneath it a single envelope with no addressee, sealed, my name on it in the canon's upright, unhurried hand:

*Sebastian.*

He had never once, in ten years, written my name on anything. Initials on the payroll. "The archivist" in his memos. My name existed in his files only as a job title. And here it was, spelled out, in ink, addressed, deliberate—as if he had finally decided that whatever came next required a person, not a position.

I sat down on the step stool, in the aisle between C and D, in the room where I had spent a decade being invisible and content, and I broke the seal.

The letter inside was short. Three sentences. I will not reproduce them here—not yet, not until I understand them, and understanding, I have learned, is a slower process than reading. But the third sentence contained a date. 1995. And the second contained an instruction: *the crypt key opens more than the crypt.*

And the first—

The first said: *If you are reading this, then I have died before I found the courage, and I am sorry to leave you the mess.*

Outside, the light was failing. Somewhere in the nave the funeral lilies were rotting sweetly in the dark. And in my hand, in a room ordered down to the last acid-free folder, the first loose thread of my entire life had just come undone, and I could feel the whole careful alphabet beginning, very slowly, to fall.
