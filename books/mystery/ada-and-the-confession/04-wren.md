## 4. Wren

There is a particular kind of silence that lives inside a cathedral the morning after a funeral. It isn't peace. Peace is what people imagine when they've never had to sweep up after grief. This silence is more like the hush in a house where someone has just stopped crying — exhausted, embarrassed, waiting to see if anyone noticed.

I know this silence professionally. I have spent twenty-two years delivering flowers into it.

The nave smelled of lilies and cold stone and, faintly, of two hundred mourners who had sat shoulder to shoulder for ninety minutes pretending their grief was orderly. My van was backed up to the side door with the hazard lights blinking like a nervous heartbeat, and I was halfway through dismantling the arrangements I'd built three days ago — before he died, when they were still *celebration* flowers for an anniversary service that would now never happen — when I felt the eyes on me.

I didn't look up right away. That's a florist's trick. You learn to feel being watched the way you learn to feel frost coming: a pressure at the back of the neck, a stillness in the air that shouldn't be there. I kept stripping eucalyptus from its foam, letting the leaves fall into my apron pocket, and gave whoever it was another thirty seconds of my back.

Then I turned, secateurs in hand, and found Sebastian Sandoval standing at the end of the aisle like a question nobody had asked out loud.

"Archivist," I said. "You're blocking my light."

They didn't move. They never quite do — Sebastian has a way of occupying space as if they'd been filed there, spine aligned, hands folded, waiting for someone to request them. Ten years in this town and I could count on one hand the number of conversations we'd had, most of them about humidity levels in the record room. I knew their mother's name from the old parish rolls. I knew they took their coffee black and drank it standing up. I knew they watched everything, which until this week had struck me as harmless. Archivists watch. That's the whole job. Moths watch lamps.

"Ms. Okafor." Their voice was flat and careful, the voice of someone reading aloud from a card catalogue. "I wanted to ask you about the flowers."

"The flowers."

"For the canon's funeral. Who ordered them."

I let myself smile, because smiling is free and buys time. "Half the town ordered flowers. The other half ordered them through me without admitting it. Why?"

"They're beautiful," Sebastian said, in the tone of someone who has not come to compliment anyone. "The white ones by the crypt door. I haven't seen that variety in the cathedral records before."

*The cathedral records.* Of course. This person had spent a decade cataloguing every stem that ever wilted inside these walls, and I mean that almost literally — there is a ledger in the archive, I know because the canon showed me once, laughing, that lists floral donations going back to 1911. Somewhere in that ledger there is an entry for a funeral wreath made of ragweed during the war, because the war takes what it takes.

"Well," I said, snipping a dead head off a chrysanthemum with more force than strictly necessary, "you can't put everything in a ledger. Some things are just for the dead."

That landed somewhere. I saw it land — a small flinch behind the glasses, quickly filed away. And I thought, with the cold clarity that comes to me only in churches and cemeteries: *careful.*

Because here is what you need to understand about me, since I'm telling this part of the story and no one else can be trusted to: I am very good at lying. Not the way con artists are good at it, all flash and misdirection. I'm good the way long-married couples are good at it, the way caretakers are good at it — quietly, structurally, with load-bearing walls. I have lied to priests, to police officers, to my own reflection on bad mornings, and I have done it so smoothly for so long that the lies have grown bark. They are trees now. You could hang a swing on some of them.

And Sebastian Sandoval was looking at me the way you look at a tree you've suddenly realized has something buried under it.

* * *

Let me tell you about the canon instead, while my hands are busy and my mouth is safer.

Father Wilhelm — though no one living called him that, and if you'd said the name to his face he'd have waved it off like a fly — baptized me when I was six weeks old, stood godfather in a church that smelled of fresh paint and my mother's panic, and thereafter treated the role not as a formality but as a vocation. He came to my school plays. He came to my mother's second wedding and my father's funeral and the terrible year in between when I stopped speaking for four months and he simply... arrived, every Tuesday, with a book and a bag of oranges, and sat in our kitchen not requiring anything of me until words came back. He taught me that flowers have meanings — that rosemary is remembrance, that rue is regret, that you should never send white lilies to a widow unless you also send food, because lilies say *death* and widows need someone to say *dinner.*

He was the only adult who never once asked me what I saw.

Understand what that means. Everyone else in my life, from the age of seventeen onward, has been asking in one way or another — some with questions, most with silences shaped exactly like questions. The canon asked nothing. He built a wall around me out of Tuesdays and oranges and flower meanings and never once knocked on it, and I loved him for it with the fierce, unexamined love of someone who does not look too closely at gifts.

Now he was dead, and the wall was just a wall, and I was standing in front of it holding secateurs.

"You were close to him," Sebastian said. Not a question either, but closer to one than anything they'd managed so far.

"I was his goddaughter."

"Yes. I know. There's a record of the baptism." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "1990. St. Brigid's."

"Congratulations on your research."

Something moved across their face — not anger. Hurt, maybe, or the memory of hurt, the way a scar remembers. I felt a flicker of shame and set it aside; shame is a luxury for people whose secrets are small.

"He mentioned you," Sebastian said. "In his last weeks. He asked whether you were well. Whether you were — " and here they hesitated, and the hesitation told me more than the sentence did — "whether you were sleeping."

The chrysanthemum stem snapped in my fingers.

"He asked if I was sleeping," I repeated.

"He worried about you. For years, I think. There are — " Sebastian stopped themselves, visibly, the way you stop yourself on ice. "There are notes in his files. About people he looked after."

*I'll bet there are,* I did not say. *I'll bet your ten years of filing never once went near the drawer where he kept us.*

What I said was: "He was a priest. Looking after people was the job description." I hoisted the stripped arrangement off its stand and carried it toward the van, forcing Sebastian to either follow or stand alone in the aisle, and they followed, of course, because following is what archivists do. "If you're here to order flowers for the estate reading, the answer is yes, I'm doing them, and no, the family hasn't confirmed a color scheme, because the Hallorans have never once in my professional life confirmed a color scheme."

"Marguerite Halloran wants white roses again," Sebastian said. "She's requested them for every memorial service since 1995."

My hands did not shake. I want that on the record, wherever records go. My hands did not shake, because I trained them not to, because at seventeen years old I learned that shaking hands get noticed and noticed girls get asked questions, and I have not permitted myself a visible tremor in thirty years. Inside, something went off like a dropped tray of glassware.

"White roses," I said lightly. "Marguerite's a traditionalist."

"She is," Sebastian agreed. "It's in the file. Everything about her is in the file."

We had reached the van. I set the arrangement down among its brothers and turned around slowly, and I gave Sebastian Sandoval the full benefit of my composure — the thing people mistake for warmth, the smile that has opened more doors than any key I own.

"Is there something you actually want to ask me?" I said. "Because you've been circling for ten minutes, and I have four funerals this week, and one of them is a child, so forgive me if my patience is thinner than usual."

And here is the thing — the thing I keep turning over like a stone with something wet underneath: I think they believed me. I think the child funeral bought me the grace I didn't deserve. Sebastian's shoulders came down half an inch, and they said, "I'm sorry. That wasn't fair," and for one naked moment they looked less like an interrogator and more like what they actually were, which is a person who lost the only man who ever saved them a seat, and who has no idea how to grieve except by working.

Which I understood. God help me, I understood that completely.

"Ask me properly sometime," I said, gentler now, because cruelty toward the bereaved is a debt I don't collect. "When it's not the week of his funeral. Ask me anything you like."

"Did you know Tomas Reyes?"

The question came out quiet and level, dropped between us like a coin into a well, and I listened for the splash.

Everyone in Ashcombe knows the name Tomas Reyes. He is the town's beautiful wound — the organist with the hands like a blessing, dead at twenty-six, three days before his wedding, fallen from the loft on a night in October when the whole town swears it was raining, though I have since learned it was not. Ruled accidental. Mourned annually. The sort of death a town keeps like a relic, polished and passed around, everyone touching it briefly and putting it back.

"No," I said. "I was a child in 1995. I barely knew who he was."

Three lies in one breath. I was seventeen, not a child. I knew exactly who he was. And I had known him the way you know a person whose wedding flowers you are carrying down a dark corridor at eleven o'clock at night.

Sebastian nodded, wrote nothing down — they didn't need to; I could see the entry already typeset behind their eyes — and thanked me for my time, and walked back up the aisle with their spine straight and their shadow long, and I stood in the doorway of my van breathing through my mouth until the shaking started, safely below the elbows where no one could see.

* * *

That night I went home to the shop — the flat above the shop, technically, though I live mostly in the workroom among the buckets — and I pulled the loose brick out of the chimney breast, the way I have done perhaps forty times in thirty years, always at night, always alone.

Inside was a bundle tied with ribbon that had once been white. Letters, mostly. One photograph. A dried sprig of rosemary that had gone brown and brittle and still smelled, faintly, impossibly, of remembrance.

At the top of the bundle, a letter in the canon's hand, undated, unfinished. I have read it so many times I could recite it, but I read it again anyway, the way you touch a bruise:

*Wren — if you are reading this then I have run out of Tuesdays, and I am sorry for it, and sorrier for what I am about to ask of you, which is the same thing I asked of you at seventeen, which is silence, again, one more time, though God knows —*

It stops there. God knows what. The sentence hangs over my life like weather.

I sat on the floor of the workroom with the letter in my lap and the smell of tomorrow's funerals rising out of the buckets — stock, and statice, and the white roses I'd already conditioned for Marguerite Halloran, because she orders them for every memorial and I fill every order, because filling orders is what I do instead of screaming.

Here is what I told Sebastian today: I was a child. Here is what I will tell them tomorrow, or next week, when they come back, because they will come back — people like Sebastian always come back, they are patient the way water is patient: I will tell them whatever keeps the wall standing.

But I will tell you, since you've gotten this far and seem the trustworthy type, the true shape of it. Just the outline. Just enough.

On the night Tomas Reyes fell, I was in the cathedral. Not passing by. *In* it — down the north corridor, past the vestry, to the mouth of the crypt stairs, carrying a box of white roses for a wedding that would never happen. And I was not alone on those stairs. Someone was climbing ahead of me, someone I knew, someone whose face I have kept locked in the chimney breast of my own heart for thirty years because the canon asked me to, because he said it was for my protection, and because I was seventeen and loved him and obeyed.

I have spent three decades arranging other people's grief into beautiful shapes. I am very good at making things look peaceful.

Tomorrow, they say, the archivist has requested access to the 1995 accident file.

I think I should like to know what's in it before they do.
