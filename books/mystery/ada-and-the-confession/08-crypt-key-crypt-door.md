## 8. Crypt Key, Crypt Door

The key had been sitting in my desk drawer for six days before I worked up the nerve to touch it.

That's not entirely honest. It sat in the drawer for six days while I told myself I was waiting for the right time—early morning, when the cathedral would be empty, when no verger would come down the north stair and find the archivist standing where the archivist does not stand. But the truth is that I was waiting for courage, and courage, like everything else in this building, was filed somewhere I hadn't catalogued yet.

The key itself was a disappointment, at first. Small, iron, older than the lock it fit, with a bow worn smooth as a river stone. I'd found it in the canon's second drawer, beneath a false bottom I only discovered because I dropped a stapler—the universe's preferred method of revelation—and heard it rattle against something hollow. The letters to "W" had been folded on top of it like an afterthought, or maybe like a warning: *this goes with this; you'll understand later.*

I did not understand later. I understood nothing later. That was becoming the theme of my autumn.

* * *

Here is what I knew about the crypt, in ten years of working beneath the cathedral: almost nothing, which for me is a kind of professional humiliation. I knew its door—a slab of black oak banded in iron, set into the floor of the ambulatory behind the choir stalls, so unobtrusive that most visitors walked over it without ever looking down. I knew it was locked, because I had once, in my first year, tried it out of idle thoroughness and found it immovable, and Father Anselm—the canon, my canon—had appeared beside me so quietly I nearly jumped out of my skin.

"Curious, Sebastian?" he'd said, and smiled his smile, the one that made you feel both forgiven and gently managed.

"Just taking inventory," I'd said.

"Good man," he'd said. "Some things inventory themselves."

At the time I'd taken it for one of his small jokes, the kind he scattered like crumbs for the sparrows of the staff. Now, standing over that same door with his key in my hand, the sentence came back to me with all the warmth of a headstone.

*Some things inventory themselves.*

I knelt. The stone around the iron ring was worn in a way I hadn't noticed from standing height—worn *smooth*, a shallow crescent polished into the flagstone as if by decades of knees. Or feet. Or the repeated, careful dragging of a heavy door across it.

Someone had been opening this door often enough to polish the floor.

* * *

I want to be clear about what I felt in that moment, because it matters to what happened next. It wasn't fear. Fear would have been simpler. What I felt was the specific vertigo of the archivist who discovers a gap in the record—a hole in the alphabet, a folder missing from a complete run. For ten years I had believed I knew this building the way I knew my own handwriting. And here was a door I had knelt beside a thousand times, dusting, photographing, measuring, and I had never once seen that it was *worn*. The dead had been keeping a secret from me, and the living had been helping them keep it, and I had been so busy loving the order of things that I'd mistaken the catalogue for the world.

I put the key in the lock.

It turned the way old things turn—not smoothly, but with the grudging cooperation of metal that remembers being useful. There was a sound underneath the sound, a deep mechanical sigh, and then the door gave, and cold air rose up out of the dark and touched my face like a hand.

It smelled of stone and candle grease and, faintly, impossibly, of lilies.

* * *

I had a flashlight. Of course I had a flashlight; I am Sebastian Sandoval, and I do not enter unmapped territory without equipment. I also had my phone, a notebook, two pencils (never pens near documents), and a granola bar, because grief makes me practical and practicality makes me hungry. I stood at the top of the stairs—there were stairs, eleven of them, cut into the living rock below the foundation stones—and I said aloud, to no one, in the voice I use for reading inventories:

"I'm coming down. No objections recorded."

No objections. I went down.

The flashlight found stone first—vaulted ceilings, low and close, the architecture of people who expected to be visited by God and not by electricians. Then niches. Dozens of them, cut into the walls, each holding a coffin or an ossuary box, each labeled with a brass plate gone green with age. Halloran. Ashcombe. Vane. Merrick. Okafor—no, not Okafor; I checked twice, and Wren's family was not here, which surprised me more than it should have. The five founding families, generation stacked on generation, their names receding into the dark like a sentence being spoken very slowly.

And then, at the far end, apart from the rest, a single niche that did not match.

It was newer. The stone around it was paler, the mortar still sharp-edged. And it was empty—or rather, it held no coffin. It held a shelf. And on the shelf, arranged with a care that made my throat tighten, were objects: a folded surplice. A pair of wire-rimmed glasses. A hymnal with a ribbon marking page 214—"Now Thank We All Our God," though the pages fanned open more naturally to 87, and I noted that too, because noting things is the only prayer I know how to say.

There was no nameplate. There didn't need to be. Tomas Reyes had been the youngest person ever buried in this crypt, and the only one not born to any of the five families, and the file said his coffin lay in the public cemetery on the hill with his mother and his grandmother before her. So why was there a shelf for him down here? Why did someone build him a place among the families that had signed his alibis?

Unless the alibis weren't the whole story. Unless someone down here had loved him in a way the paperwork never captured.

I photographed everything. My hands were steady. I remember being proud of that, later, before I learned what steadiness can cost.

* * *

The reliquary was on the second shelf, tucked behind the hymnal, and I nearly missed it.

It was small—a brass box the size of a paperback book, the kind used for saints' bones, for fragments of the true cross, for the physical evidence of holiness. This one had been repurposed. The hinge was modern. The latch was a simple hook. And inside, on a bed of faded velvet that had once been white—

A ring.

A wedding ring. Gold, plain band, size eight or nine, engraved inside with letters too fine to read by flashlight. I did not touch it. I want that on the record, whoever ends up reading this record: I did not touch it. I crouched there with my flashlight beam trembling slightly despite my steady hands, and I looked at a wedding ring hidden in a saint's box in a crypt beneath my cathedral, and I thought: *Tomas Reyes died the night before his wedding.*

The accident file—I'd been through it three times now, cross-referencing, annotating—said the ring was never recovered. Lost in the fall, presumed lost in the dark of the organ loft, entered into evidence and then quietly de-accessioned, the way small tragic things get lost in institutional memory. His fiancée had married someone else two years later, in another town, and the ring had become one of those details that exists only in the margins, the archival equivalent of a footnote nobody follows.

Except somebody had followed it. Somebody had found it—up in the loft, presumably, in the dark, in the aftermath—and instead of returning it to the family, or burying it with him, or turning it over to the police, they had brought it *here*, down eleven stairs past five family vaults, and hidden it in a reliquary behind a hymnal, and built him a shelf to keep it company.

You don't hide a thing like that unless the thing means something. You don't hide a thing like that unless hiding it protects someone.

My awe curdled, right there in the cold, into something sharper. Because there was dust on these shelves—thin, even, undisturbed except in one place. One clean oval on the velvet where the ring had rested until recently. And the brass of the reliquary showed fingerprints in the gray film, recent ones, small ones, the size and spacing of a woman's hand.

Someone had been visiting. Recently. After the funeral. Possibly after the letter arrived.

I stood up too fast and my knee cracked like the door had. In the silence that followed, I became aware of a sound I should have noticed earlier: a faint scrape, high above, at the top of the stairs. The oak door, settling back into its frame.

Or closing.

I killed the flashlight and stood in the absolute dark listening to my own heart conduct its business, and I made myself count to sixty, because panic is just data arriving faster than you can file it. At forty-seven, I heard footsteps crossing the ambulatory above—unhurried, familiar-weighted, moving away toward the vestry. Not running. People who run are afraid of being caught. People who walk away slowly believe they were never in danger of it.

I gave them ten minutes. Then I climbed the eleven stairs, let myself out, locked the door behind me, and knelt to check the flagstone.

In the crescent of worn stone, half-hidden in the seam between flags, lay a single dried petal. White. From a lily.

Wren Okafor arranged the memorial flowers. Wren Okafor had lied to me, smoothly and kindly, about where she'd been in 1995. And Wren Okafor, or someone with her flowers and her small hands, had been down here visiting a dead man's wedding ring within the last week.

I put the petal in an envelope. I labeled it. Date, location, probable source. It's what I do. It's the only way I know how to love anything—by writing down exactly what it is.

Then I stood in the empty ambulatory with the canon's key warm in my pocket, and I understood, finally, the shape of what he'd left me. Not the key itself. The question under it. He had spent ten years telling me *some things inventory themselves*, and now, kneeling on a floor polished by thirty years of secret visits, I finally heard what he meant.

He hadn't been making a joke. He'd been giving me instructions.

The dead had been waiting down there in the dark, patient as paper, and one of them was missing a ring, and someone alive was still tending the absence. Tomorrow I would pull the weather records and the church schedule and start mapping alibis against minutes. Tonight I went home and did not sleep, and at 3 a.m. I got up and wrote in my notebook, in the dark, without looking:

*Who visits a grave they helped dig?*

Underneath it, after a long time, I added a second line, smaller, the handwriting less sure:

*And who taught the canon to be afraid?*

Because that was the thing I couldn't file away, the thing that kept the lamp burning in me all night: the letters to "W" had intensified in the weeks after Tomas Reyes fell. Whatever the canon knew, he had known it *then*. And whatever he had spent thirty years protecting, it was still walking around up here—wearing good shoes, walking unhurried, certain no one was watching.

They were wrong. I had always been watching. It was simply that, until now, no one had thought to tell me what I was looking at.
