## 9. The Alibi That Doesn't Add

There is a particular pleasure in a spreadsheet that no one warns you about. Archivists know it. Accountants know it. It is the pleasure of watching chaos resolve into columns, of taking a thing that has resisted every human attempt at understanding and forcing it, at last, to sit still and answer questions.

I had been at it for six hours when Marguerite Halloran's alibi broke.

But let me back up, because the breaking was the end of a long and strangely beautiful process, and I want to be fair to it. The process deserved that much. The truth usually does, even when it's ugly. Especially then.

* * *

It began, as most of my best work does, with a refusal. The county records office—housed in a building that smelled of wet carpet and municipal despair—would not release the 1995 police file to me. "Active privacy restrictions," said the clerk, a young man with the complexion of someone who had never been outside. "Next of kin only."

"Tomas Reyes has no next of kin," I said. "He has a grave, and it's in your cemetery, and someone has been lying about how he got into it for thirty years."

The clerk looked at me the way people look at dogs that have begun to talk. I did not get the file. But I got something better, or at least something more in my native language: paper. Lots of it. Weather records. Church schedules. The cathedral's own registers, which I could access because I had spent ten years earning the right to touch them, and because no one had ever thought to restrict the archivist from the archive. That is the quiet joke of my profession. We are so invisible that no one guards the door against us. We are the door.

I took everything home to my apartment above the bookshop—the one with the slanted floor and the radiator that clanked like a ghost with opinions—and I spread it across every flat surface I owned. Weather service monthly summaries for October 1995. The cathedral's sacristan logs, kept in Father Ambrose's immaculate hand. The parish calendar. The five families' statements, which I had photographed from the accident file's public summary before the clerk wised up, and which I now transcribed onto index cards, one family per card, the way you'd catalog specimens.

The Hallorans. The Ashcombes. The Vances. The Okafors. The Merricks.

Five families. Five statements. Every one of them signed, witnessed, and notarized within seventy-two hours of Tomas Reyes falling—or being helped—from the organ loft on the night of October 14, 1995.

I made a pot of coffee I would not drink and lined the cards up on my kitchen table like a firing squad.

* * *

Here is what the statements said, in the aggregate: everyone had been at the Halloran harvest dinner.

This was, on its face, a lovely alibi. Forty guests, catered, photographed—Mrs. Vance's sister had brought a camera, and the developed prints were attached to the file like exhibits at a garden show. Everyone smiling under string lights. Everyone accounted for.

Except.

An alibi is not a photograph. An alibi is a claim about time, and time can be audited. I had learned that from the canon, actually, though he'd been talking about liturgical calendars at the time—the way feast days drift, the way a date can be in two seasons at once depending on which rite you follow. "Dates lie, Sebastian," he'd said, tapping the calendar. "Hours don't. Get the hours."

So I got the hours.

The dinner began at six, per the invitation reproduced in the society page of the *Courier*—which I found on microfilm, squinting at a screen until my eyes ached, watching a younger town smile back at me from thirty years away. The cathedral's evensong that night was scheduled for seven-thirty. Tomas Reyes was the organist; the sacristan log confirmed he was expected in the loft by seven to prepare.

The cathedral is eleven minutes' walk from Halloran House. I know because I walked it twice, once in each direction, at dusk, timing myself with the same pocket watch the canon left me, because apparently I have become the kind of person who inherits pocket watches and uses them for forensics.

Eleven minutes. So if Tomas left the dinner at, say, six-forty, he could be in the loft by seven with time to spare. The statements all said he left "around half six" or "just before seven"—the witnesses were admirably consistent, which is itself a kind of tell. Real memories scatter. Rehearsed ones align.

And here is where the weather comes in, because the weather is the one witness nobody thinks to coach.

October 14, 1995. I pulled the hourly record from the state climatological archive, and there it was, in a font that looked like it had been typed by a machine that hated its job: rain beginning at 6:15 p.m., heavy by 6:45, 1.4 inches total before midnight. A proper autumn deluge. The kind of rain that soaks a wool coat in ninety seconds and turns an eleven-minute walk into a miserable, memorable ordeal.

Mrs. Vance's photographs, which I studied with a loupe like a forger's apprentice, showed the string lights dry, the lawn dry, guests arriving in the early shots with dry shoulders. The dinner started before the rain. Fine. But the rain was torrential by 6:45 and did not stop until nearly eleven.

Now. Four of the five statements mentioned the rain. The Ashcombes said they'd waited in the porte-cochère for their car. The Vances said the tent leaked. The Merricks said their son had been disappointed the bonfire was canceled. The Okafors—Wren's parents, and I read that card three times before I could read it calmly—said they'd left early because of the storm, driving home slow through it, and that they had seen nothing, heard nothing, noticed no one on the road. A careful nothing. A nothing with its shoes polished.

Marguerite Halloran's statement said none of that.

Marguerite Halloran's statement said: *"I was on the porch for much of the evening; I find crowds tiring. I did not see Mr. Reyes depart."*

On the porch. In 1.4 inches of rain. For much of an evening.

I sat back from the table. The radiator clanked. Somewhere below me, the bookshop's late customer—the one who always buys the same paperback of poems he has clearly already read—rang the bell and left.

Because here's the thing about Marguerite Halloran, and I say this with the respect of a man who has cataloged her family's papers: she is not a woman who endures discomfort for atmosphere. She is a woman who has her roses moved indoors for the winter by staff. She is a woman whose idea of roughing it is a hotel without a harpist. The Marguerite Halloran I know—and I have known her at a distance, the way you know a monument—would no more sit on a porch in a torrential downpour than she would eat soup with her hands.

So either she was lying about the porch, or she was lying about something else and the porch was the cover story, chosen precisely because it was unverifiable. Rain destroys witnesses. Nobody else was outside. The porch faces the garden, not the drive. She had built herself a room made of weather, and she had sat in it for thirty years, dry as a bone and silent as one.

Unless she hadn't been on the porch at all.

I got up and walked a lap around my apartment, which is what I do when my brain is ahead of my nerve. Past the shelves. Past the window where the streetlight does its one long exposure of the night. Past the corkboard where, without quite deciding to, I had begun pinning things: a photograph of the crypt door, the index cards, the key on its ribbon.

The canon's key. I still hadn't used it. I told myself I was being methodical. I told myself a lot of things that week.

* * *

The next morning I went back to the cathedral, because the second half of the audit lived there. If Marguerite's alibi was weatherproof, the canon's letters were not.

I had been avoiding the second drawer. That is a confession, and I make it here, in the privacy of my own head, where all my best confessions go to die. For two days I had cataloged everything around the drawer—rebinding a 1920s guestbook that did not need rebinding, rehousing a box of choir robes buttons with the tenderness of a nurse—because the drawer was where the letters to W lived, and opening it again meant sitting with what I'd found there: that the man who taught me everything about order had spent a decade maintaining an disorder so large it had a body in it.

But the hunt had me now. I want to be honest about that. There is a thrill in it, and the thrill is not noble. It is the same thrill that makes people read the last page first. I had spent ten years filing other people's stories, and here, finally, was one that needed a filing clerk with a grudge and a pocket watch. I could feel myself becoming someone new, and I could not tell yet whether I liked them.

I opened the drawer.

The letters were where I'd left them, in their bundle tied with the same green ribbon the canon used for Advent correspondence. I had read them before, quickly, guiltily. This time I read them as data. Dates first.

There were forty-one letters. I laid them out on the long table in the archive, in chronological order, and made a timeline on the back of a conservation report I was never going to finish anyway. The first letter was dated March 2009. The last, unfinished, was dated the week before he died.

For the first fourteen years after Tomas's death—1995 to 2009—the canon wrote nothing to W. Nothing. I checked his correspondence logs, because of course I did; the man logged his own letters like a customs officer. Fourteen years of silence on the subject, or near enough.

Then March 2009: the first letter. And then the pattern. One letter in 2009. Three in 2010. Five in 2011. By 2015 he was writing to W monthly. By 2019, weekly. The final year, he sometimes wrote twice a week, the letters shorter and more desperate, the handwriting tightening like a fist.

I made a little graph, because graphs are how I pray. A line climbing, year over year, from 2009 to the present.

And then I did the thing that made my hands go cold, the thing I have been circling ever since: I overlaid the timeline with the other dates. The dates I'd been collecting like a magpie without knowing why.

The letters to W began in March 2009. I went back to the parish records, to the sacristan logs, to the *Courier* microfilm, looking for what happened in March 2009, and I found it in the most boring possible place: the property ledger. In March 2009, Marguerite Halloran sold a parcel of land to the cathedral—the meadow behind the north wall, the one that became the memorial garden. A gift, the ledger called it. A generous gift, the *Courier* called it, with a photograph of Marguerite and the canon shaking hands on the steps, both of them smiling the way people smile when a transaction is complete.

Two weeks after the sale, the first letter to W.

Coincidence is the archivist's version of a creaky stair. You notice it, you note it, you keep climbing. But this wasn't a creak. This was a door opening in a wall I'd thought was solid.

I sat down on the rolling ladder, which is not designed for sitting and creaked its own opinion, and I thought it through. The canon began writing to W in 2009—fourteen years after the death. Why then? What happened in 2009? The land sale. And what does a land sale mean, in the grammar of this town, between the Hallorans and the church? It means a debt being paid. It means hush money with a deed attached. It means someone finally, finally bought the silence that had been on layaway since 1995.

Except the silence hadn't been bought. It had been *rented*. Because the letters kept coming, and they got worse, and the canon—the man who taught me that a record is a promise made to the future—was clearly drowning in whatever he knew, writing and writing to someone he trusted with a truth he could not say out loud.

And the person he trusted was W. Wren Okafor. The funeral florist who lied to me about 1995 with a smile like a closed gate.

Or—no. I stopped myself on the ladder. That was the easy answer, and the easy answer had been wrong all week. Wren had been a child in 1995. Wren's parents' statement was careful but not guilty. And the canon's letters, reread with cold eyes, didn't read like letters to a co-conspirator. They read like letters to a confessor. *You know what I cannot say. You know why I keep the door locked. Forgive me the length of this silence.*

Someone the canon trusted. Someone who knew. Someone who had known long enough that the guilt had fossilized into devotion.

I gathered the letters, retied the ribbon, and put them back in the drawer, and I stood there with my hand on the closed wood for a long moment, in the archive that smells of dust and beeswax and him, and I felt the two feelings that have been fighting for the steering wheel all week.

The thrill: I was close. Marguerite Halloran had built an alibi out of rain, and the canon had started paying for something in 2009, and the thread ran from the porch to the meadow to the locked crypt door, and I could follow it. I *wanted* to follow it. There is a version of me that has been waiting ten years for a mystery to need solving, and that version was singing.

And the fear, underneath, quiet as water in a basement: the canon had spent the last fifteen years of his life writing to someone about this, protecting someone, carrying something so heavy it bent his handwriting. And I was about to dig up the thing he'd carried. And whatever I found, I would have to live in the town where I found it. I would have to buy bread from people whose grandparents I'd implicated. I would have to pass Marguerite's roses every day.

He knew that. That's why he locked the door. Not to hide the truth from the town—from *me*. From the one person whose whole life was the truth, arranged and labeled and shelved.

I locked the office. I walked home in the dark, past the cathedral's east wall, past the memorial meadow that Marguerite Halloran bought in March 2009, and I stood at the gate for a while, looking at the bare winter beds where the roses would come up in spring.

Then I took out the pocket watch, and I held it, and I made myself a promise in the language I trust most: *hours, not dates. Follow the hours.*

And behind me, up the lane, a car slowed—too slow for the street, engine idling—and I did not turn around, because turning around is how you tell someone you've noticed.

But I wrote down the sound of it when I got home. Make, color, the particular rattle of a loose exhaust.

I filed it under H.

For Halloran.
