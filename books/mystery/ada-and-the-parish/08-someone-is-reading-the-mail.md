## 8. Someone Is Reading the Mail

The letter arrived on a Tuesday, and by Thursday Petra had begun to think of it as a living thing.

She kept it in the bottom drawer of her desk, beneath a stack of closed probate files from the Whitcombe estate, inside a manila folder labeled with the name of a client who had died peacefully in 2011. It was not a hiding place so much as a habit of concealment — she had learned, long ago, that the best place to keep a dangerous document was inside paperwork so dull no one would ever want to open it. Bureaucracy as camouflage. Her whole career had been built on the principle that no one reads what they believe they already understand.

She had not shown the letter to anyone. Not her clerk, Mr. Fenwick, who handled the post and noticed everything and said nothing. Not the junior associate who shared her floor and whose curiosity was, Petra suspected, the only genuine talent he possessed. She had photographed the confession twice — once with her phone, once with the old camera she kept for document verification — and stored the images in two places, and then she had sat at her desk late on Wednesday night, the office dark around her except for the green-shaded lamp, and read it again.

The handwriting matched the will. Not approximately. Exactly. The same narrow, upright script, the same peculiar way Edmund Parish crossed his t's with a single flat stroke that looked almost like a blade laid across the word. The same hesitation marks where the pen had rested too long and the ink had bled into a small dark bloom, like a bruise under the paper's skin.

*They will tell you I died on the fourth,* the letter began. *The fourth is a convenient date. I chose it myself. Ask instead what happened on the eleventh, and ask why a man who has spent sixty years in one village cannot find a single soul to say what he was doing that night.*

And then, further down, in a hand that had begun to shake:

*Her name is Ada. Whatever you have been told about Ada, un-tell it. I am sorry to put this on you. I chose you because you of all people will understand what it is to be saved by a lie.*

Petra had read that last line eleven times. She had read it the way one probes a tooth — again and again, wincing, unable to stop. *You of all people.* Edmund Parish, whom she had met exactly twice, both times in the flat professional context of estate planning for a client who referred him, had looked across the distance between a dead man and a living lawyer and seen something she had spent twenty years burying under invoices and filing systems and the careful gray language of trusts.

She did not know how he knew. That was the part that kept her awake. Not what he knew — the fact of it was undeniable — but *how*. Who had told him. What else he had told.

And now, on Thursday morning, standing in the doorway of her own office with her coat still on and her umbrella dripping a small dark circle onto the carpet, Petra understood that someone else had been reading her mail.

* * *

She noticed it the way she noticed most things: by the absence of disorder.

The office was exactly as she had left it. That was the problem. She was a woman of precise habits — the files on her desk aligned to the desk's edge, the blotter square, the chair pushed in at an angle she could have measured. When she had left the night before, the Whitcombe folder had been sitting on the stack of closed probate files, and she remembered, with the particular clarity that comes too late, that she had left it slightly askew, its corner overhanging the stack's edge by perhaps a centimeter, because she had been holding the confession letter in her other hand and had not cared enough to square it away.

Now it was square. Perfectly square. Aligned with the desk's edge as if it had been placed there by someone who understood her habits better than she articulated them — someone who had studied her, or who simply shared her own compulsions and had tidied without thinking.

Petra stood very still. Rain ran down the window behind her, and the radiator ticked, and somewhere below, in the street, a delivery van reversed with its shrill electronic complaint.

She did not touch anything. Twenty years of estate law had taught her that a scene is evidence, and evidence is destroyed by the first sentimental hand that reaches for it. She set down her umbrella, took out her phone, and photographed the office from the doorway — the desk, the shelves, the filing cabinets against the wall, the small sofa where clients sat and wept or lied or both. Then she walked the room slowly, looking down, looking at the things that lived below eye level.

The carpet showed nothing. The window was locked from the inside; the fire escape door at the corridor's end had a bolt that was, she confirmed with two fingers, still engaged. The building had a night porter, a stooped man named Hollis who watched television with the sound off and could describe every vehicle that entered the car park. She would ask him. But first —

First, the drawer.

She knelt, opened the bottom drawer, and slid out the stack of Whitcombe files. The manila folder was there. The letter was there.

She almost laughed with relief, and then she saw it.

The letter had been refolded. She knew its folds the way she knew her own signature — three creases, the first slightly off-center because Edmund had folded it in haste or in trembling, so that one edge of the paper protruded a millimeter beyond the others and had, over its days in the envelope, worn a faint soft line into itself. She had noticed that millimeter on the first night, had registered it without knowing she was registering it, the way one registers the exact weight of a key in one's pocket.

Now the folds were crisp. Even. The millimeter gone. Someone had unfolded the letter, read it — read all of it, there was no way to unfold a letter that size without reading all of it — and folded it back with a care that was almost a courtesy, and put it back, and closed the drawer, and squared the Whitcombe folder on the desk, and left.

Not stolen. Photographed. She was certain of that too, though she could not have said how — some instinct, some sense of the room's air, as though the office had been breathed in and breathed out again by lungs that were not hers. Whoever it was had wanted the contents, not the paper. Had wanted *her* to keep the paper. Had wanted her to find it exactly where she expected it and understand, in the slow cold way one understands such things, that it had been read.

*We know what you have,* the tidy folds said. *We are not taking it. Yet.*

* * *

Hollis, it turned out, had seen nothing.

"No one came in after eight," he said, in the porter's lodge that smelled of boiled tea and carpet cleaner. "I'd have logged them." He turned the ledger around so she could see the blank evening columns, the ruled lines waiting like empty roads. "There was a car, though. Sat in the car park from about half seven. Grey, maybe silver. Never saw anyone get out."

"Did you see it leave?"

"Wasn't there this morning." He said it comfortably, a man reporting weather. "These things happen. People park, people think better of it." He looked at her over his spectacles with mild, incurious eyes, and Petra had the sudden, unwelcome certainty that Hollis saw a great deal more than he logged, and that his discretion was for sale at a price she had not yet been asked to pay.

She thanked him, walked back up the stairs, and sat down at her desk, and did not touch the letter.

Instead she thought.

Whoever had entered had done so without forcing a lock, without tripping the porter, without leaving so much as a scuff. That meant either a key or a skill, and both meant money, and money meant that Edmund Parish's death — his tidy, certified, witnessed death — had unsettled someone enough to spend real resources on a solicitor's office in a city two hours from Marlow St. Cuthbert.

She made a list, because lists were how she kept the world from tilting.

One: whoever searched her office knew the letter existed. That narrowed the field to those who had access to her correspondence or her movements — or to Edmund himself, before his death, which raised the question of who Edmund had trusted, and the answer, increasingly, appeared to be: no one, except perhaps the vanished doctor and the woman named Ada.

Two: they had not taken the letter. A thief takes. This person had *read*, and left the reading behind like a calling card. That was not the behavior of someone gathering evidence for a court. That was the behavior of someone gathering leverage for a conversation.

Three: they had tidied the folder. The squareness of it, the alignment — that was a message in itself, or she was losing her mind, and she was no longer entirely certain those were different things. *We are careful. We are patient. We see how you see.*

There was a fourth item on the list, and she wrote it last, and her pen pressed hard enough to emboss the page.

Four: *You of all people.* If Edmund knew what she had done — or what had been done for her — twenty years ago, then whoever had taught him might know it too. The letter in her drawer was no longer only a dead man's confession. It was a key to a door she had spent two decades pretending was a wall.

* * *

She spent the afternoon doing what she did best: nothing visible.

She worked. She returned calls. She dictated a letter to the bank regarding the Whitcombe accounts in a voice so level that Fenwick, bringing in the post at four, remarked that she sounded better than she had all week, and she thanked him and meant nothing by it and everything.

The post held nothing from Marlow St. Cuthbert. It held a utility notice, a journal renewal, and a brown envelope of no interest whatsoever, which she opened anyway because she had begun, in the last two days, to open everything.

At six she locked the office, took the letter out of the drawer, and did not put it back. She sealed it inside a padded envelope, wrote no name on the outside, and carried it out of the building in her briefcase, past Hollis's window, past the empty space in the car park where a grey car had sat and thought better of itself.

She walked three streets in the rain before she found what she was looking for: a self-storage facility wedged between a tyre garage and a defunct bakery, the kind of place that rented by the month to people between houses, between marriages, between lives. The manager was indifferent in the way of the professionally incurious. She paid cash for a small unit, signed a name that was legally hers but that no one had used since her mother's death, and left the letter there, on a metal shelf, in the dark, in a building with cameras she did not trust and a padlock she did.

It was not safety. It was distance. There was a difference, and she had learned to live on it.

* * *

The call came at nine-forty that night, to her flat, to the number she gave to clients and to no one else.

She knew before she answered. The phone rang with a particular patience — four rings, a pause, four more — the rhythm of someone who knew she was home and was willing to wait.

"Miss Villalobos." A man's voice. Unhurried. Slightly nasal, the voice of someone who had spent a career in rooms where being liked mattered less than being believed. "I hope I'm not calling too late. I know how solicitors value their evenings."

"Who is this?"

"A well-wisher." A pause in which she could hear him breathing, and beneath the breathing, faintly, the sound of a television with the sound off — Hollis's lodge, she thought absurdly, and dismissed it. "I'm calling about Edmund Parish. I understand you're handling the estate."

"I'm not able to discuss client matters over the phone."

"Of course not. I'd never ask you to." The voice smiled; she could hear it, a small dry movement in the consonants. "I only wanted to say — and I say this as someone who admires thoroughness — that the letter was very well hidden. The Whitcombe folder. Under the closed files, in the bottom drawer. I'd never have thought of it. Dead clients from 2011. Very good."

The floor did not move, but Petra felt it move. She sat down slowly on the arm of the sofa, because her legs had opinions of their own.

"You've been in my office."

"I've been *in the vicinity* of your office," the voice corrected gently. "There's a difference. You'd appreciate that — a lawyer's whole trade is differences. Is versus was. Alive versus certified." He let that sit. "I'm not your enemy, Miss Villalobos. I'm a reader. I like documents. And I like to know where they sleep at night. The storage unit on Corben Street, for instance — unit forty-one, is it? The padlock is a good one. I'd have gone brass."

The rain against the window had stopped, and in the silence she could hear her own pulse, quick and small, like something trying to get out of a room.

"What do you want?"

"Nothing yet. Truly. I'm patient. But when I do want something — and I will, soon — I'd like you to remember that I've read everything you've read. Including, I suspect, the parts you haven't read yet." Another smile in the voice. "Edmund Parish was a careful man, but careful men leave the best trails. You'll find the doctor. Everyone finds the doctor eventually. The question is whether you find him before I do — and what you'll say to him when you do."

The line went dead. No click, no goodbye. Just the sudden flat tone of a call ended by someone who had said exactly as much as he intended and not a syllable more.

Petra sat in the dark of her flat for a long time. Down in the street a car passed, and its headlights swept the ceiling, and she thought of a grey car in a car park, sitting patiently from half seven, thinking better of nothing at all.

*You'll find the doctor.*

She got up, took out a fresh sheet of paper, and at the top of it wrote, in her small precise hand, the only thing she knew for certain: *He signed the certificate a week before the funeral. He retired the same week. He has vanished.*

Then, beneath it, the new knowledge, the one that turned the puzzle into a hunt with two sets of footprints: *Someone is asking the same questions I am. And he is ahead of me.*

She looked at the words for a long moment. Then she packed a bag, because two counties was a long way to drive at dawn, and because she had the sudden, animal conviction that whatever the retired Dr. Merrick knew — whatever he had been paid to kill on paper, and whatever he had told the man with the patient voice — he would not stay found for long.

The letter slept in its dark unit on Corben Street. The will sat squared and innocent in her office. And somewhere between the two, a grey car was already moving.

Petra turned off the lamp, and in the sudden dark she heard, quite clearly, the sentence the voice had left behind like a folded letter: *the parts you haven't read yet.*

She would drive through the night. She would find the doctor first.

She had to.
