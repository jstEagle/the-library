## 13. What Edmund Did

The vestry smelled of beeswax and old stone, and Ada Marsh had spent twenty years learning to breathe it like a native. She polished the same brass lectern her father had polished, dusted the same ledgers, wound the same stubborn clock in the tower stair that lost four minutes every week and was corrected every Sunday by the vicar's thumb. Routine was a country you could live in if you never looked at its borders.

But tonight she had the case open on the table before her, and routine had failed her entirely.

She had come down from the tower after locking the archive — after removing, for the second time that month, the folder with Petra Villalobos's name in the visitor log — and she had meant only to sit with the photographs for a while, the way one sits with a fire. Instead she had found, wedged behind the passport in her dead name, an envelope she had never seen before. Her father's handwriting on the front: *For Ada, when the lawyer comes.*

Not *if*. When.

Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Twenty years of steadiness had been drilled into her like a psalm: *you were never born, you were never born.* She opened the envelope with a churchwarden's letter knife and read what her father had written in the last weeks of his life, in a hand that grew shakier down each page, as though he were descending a staircase he could not see the bottom of.

*My darling girl. You will hate me, and you should, but not for the reasons you think.*

Ada read it once standing up. Then she sat down on the vestry step, because her knees informed her they were no longer part of the arrangement, and read it again.

* * *

She had been told a story all her life, and like most families' stories it had a shape worn smooth by repetition. Her mother, Miriam Parish, had drowned in the estuary in October, twenty years ago — a walker caught by the tide, said the inquest; a tragedy, said the village; an accident, said everyone, in the same gentle voice, until the word *accident* became a kind of wallpaper. And Ada, nineteen years old and drowning in her own way, had been sent away before the funeral. Before the gravestone. Before grief could be witnessed by anyone who might ask questions.

*You were never born,* her father had written in the letter she kept locked behind the vestry wall. *If anyone comes asking, you were never born.* She had understood it then as protection against whatever danger had taken her mother. She had built her whole life inside that sentence.

The new letter took the sentence apart.

*Miriam did not drown, Ada. I want you to hear that first, plainly, because everything else follows from it and you deserve one true sentence at the top of the page.*

*Your mother died in our house, on the night of the fourth of October, and I killed her. Not with my hands. With my slowness. With my disbelief. There is no kinder way to write it, so I will not try to find one.*

Ada's breath went thin. The clock in the tower stair ticked its four-minutes-lost tick above her, patient and wrong, and she let it count while she made herself keep reading.

*I have to begin further back, or none of it will make sense, and I have so little time left to make anything make sense.*

*You know — no. You don't know. You were a child when we told you your grandfather's name was a formality. Let me say it here, where no one can interrupt: Gerald Parish is not your blood. Your mother came to this marriage already carrying you, and I knew it, and I married her anyway, because I loved her more than I loved my own name, which I assure you is saying something, because the Parishes have always loved their name the way other men love God.*

*My brother Gerald knew it too. He has always known it. For years it was nothing — a family joke with teeth, the sort of thing said once at Christmas and never again. But three years ago Gerald's business began to fail, and Gerald discovered that shame, properly documented, is a commodity. He had letters. He had the testimony of a woman in Bristol who remembered your mother's situation in the spring of the year you were born. He had enough, assembled carefully, to prove not merely that you were illegitimate but that Edmund Parish had knowingly committed fraud upon his own marriage — and worse, that there were parties willing to pay for such proof. Men whose interests your mother's charity work had inconvenienced. Men who would pay not for the scandal itself but for the leverage it gave them over me, over the estate, over the parish lands I would not sell them.*

*Gerald was selling you, Ada. Piece by piece. A letter here, a memory there. Small transactions, deniable ones. Your mother found out in September of that last year. She confronted him, and he laughed at her, and she came home white as the churchyard lilies and told me everything, and I —*

Here the handwriting broke off mid-word and resumed on the next line, smaller, pressed hard into the paper.

*I did not believe her. That is the murder, Ada. Read it again. I did not believe her. I told her she was overwrought. I told her Gerald was a fool but not a monster. I told her I would look into it — after harvest, after the audit, after. I gave my brother the benefit of the doubt over my wife, because the doubt was comfortable and the truth would have required me to fight him, and fighting Gerald meant admitting what he was, and admitting what he was meant admitting what our name was, and I chose the name. Do you understand? In the space of one evening, without ever raising my voice, I chose the name over her.*

*She went back to him alone. Of course she did. She was braver than both of us put together. She went to take the letters back.*

*What happened in Gerald's study that night, I have pieced together across twenty years from a hundred small mercies of evidence, and I will set it down as best I can, because you should have it whole even if it breaks you. They argued. He struck her — there were witnesses later who heard the raised voices, though none came forward until it was too late to matter. She fell. Whether she was already dead when he carried her out to the car, I do not know, and God forgive me, I have never had the courage to decide which answer would be worse. He drove to the estuary and put her in the water where the tide does its work, and he came home and washed his hands and told the village his sister-in-law had gone walking.*

*And I helped him bury it.*

*Not with a spade. With paper. That was my part, and it was the greater part, and I have signed my name to enough documents in my life to know exactly how much a signature weighs. The doctor who attended us — a good man, a frightened man, a man who owed me — wrote the certificate I asked him to write. Drowned, accidental, October the fifth. The inquest was brief because the coroner was Gerald's oldest friend. The village grieved because grieving is what villages do, beautifully and without examination. And I stood at my wife's funeral with dry eyes and a face like a closed ledger, and every person who saw me thought: he cannot bear it. No one thought: he is bearing it because he built it.*

Ada stopped reading.

The vestry was very dark now; she had not noticed the light going. Somewhere outside, rain had begun again, the soft Marlow rain that fell so often it had become less weather than texture. She sat on the cold stone step with the pages trembling in her hands and felt the floor of her life open beneath her — not dramatically, not all at once, but the way a moorland path gives way: quietly, completely, with a long fall revealed where ground had been.

She had spent twenty years believing her father had hidden her to protect her from her mother's killer. It had even been true, in its way. But she had imagined a stranger, a menace from outside, some shadow with no face. She had not imagined that the killer ate Christmas dinner. That he called her *little niece* in a voice like oiled wood. That he still lived eleven miles away in a house with a good roof, aging comfortably, spending — she understood now, with a nausea that arrived like a tide — the money those small transactions had brought in, before her mother's death and after it, because a man who sells a secret once will sell it forever, and there had been buyers even for the story of a drowned woman.

*Why the confession, then,* she whispered to the dark. *Why now. Why her.*

Because the letter answered her, in her father's patient schoolmaster's hand, as though he had heard.

*You will ask why I am writing this instead of burning it, and why I sent it into the world at all. Here is why. Gerald is dying — slower than I am, but dying — and his son Marcus has found the letters. All of them. The boy is not clever, but he is greedy, and greed does the work of cleverness eventually. He believes the estate defrauded him of his inheritance, and he believes the proof of it lies in proving you dead — truly dead, legally dead, dead on paper the way your mother is dead in fact. He has hired people. I have watched them come and go in the village like crows counting sheep.*

*I could destroy these pages. I nearly did, a dozen times. But a lie kept by one person dies with that person, and I am dying quickly, and you would be left holding a secret with no key to it — hunted by a man with a grievance, protected by nothing but a fiction I would no longer be alive to maintain. So I am doing the thing I should have done twenty years ago: I am telling the truth to someone whose profession is truth, and whose discretion is purchasable, and whom I have chosen with more care than I chose anything else in my cowardly life.*

*Petra Villalobos. Look into her, Ada, before you judge me for the choice. A lawyer who survived, as a girl, what you survived — a record altered to save her, a life rebuilt on someone else's forgery. She knows what it costs. She knows what it protects. I read about her case years ago and I have followed her career since the way one follows a lighthouse one hopes never to need.*

*I am not asking her to save you. I am asking her to carry it, the way someone once carried hers. Some truths are too heavy to be spoken and too heavy to be buried. They need a bearer. That is all a confession ever is — a weight, handed on.*

*One more thing, and then I will stop torturing you with my penmanship. When they find my body — and they will find a body, Ada, because I have arranged for one, and I am sorry, I am so sorry, to make you mourn a coffin twice — the dates will not match. The certificate will precede the will. This is deliberate. It is the thread I am leaving trailing out of the pocket of the corpse, and it leads to Petra Villalobos, and through her to you. I could not simply mail her the truth; mailed things can be intercepted, and intercepted things can be used. But a lawyer given an impossible document will worry it like a dog with a bone. She will not rest. I am counting on her inability to rest.*

*I have made a great many calculations in my life and almost all of them were cowardice wearing arithmetic. This one is not. Or perhaps it is, and I am past the age where I can tell the difference. Either way: the weight is yours to accept or refuse. If you refuse it, burn this letter and stay Ada Marsh forever, and live, which is the only commandment I have any right to give you.*

*Live, Ada. Even if you hate me. Especially if you hate me.*

*Your father,*
*Edmund*

* * *

She did not know how long she sat there. The rain thickened against the vestry windows, and the candle she'd lit at some point without remembering had burned down to a stub, and her legs had gone numb beneath her, and still she sat, turning the pages as though rereading could rearrange them into something survivable.

Her mother had not drowned. Her mother had been carried, dead or dying, to the estuary, by a man whose photograph still hung in the Blue Boar, whose pew was the third from the front, whose condolences at the funeral had been described to Ada years later by a well-meaning neighbor as *so sincere, so broken up about poor Miriam.* Sincere. Broken up. The words rose in her now like silt disturbed from a riverbed, and she pressed the back of her hand against her mouth to keep the sound inside.

And her father — her gentle, punctual, hymn-quiet father — had known. Had known and chosen paper over justice, the parish's peace over his wife's name, his own comfort over everything, and then had the audacity, the unbearable audacity, to spend twenty years being beloved for his grief. *He cannot bear it*, the village had murmured. He bore it precisely. He bore it the way he bore everything: neatly, privately, with excellent penmanship.

She thought of the confession she had known existed — the half-finished pages Petra Villalobos had received, postmarked the day after the funeral. She had assumed it named the danger. She had not dreamed it named the crime. Somewhere in the world, in a solicitor's office or a hotel room, that woman was reading the same sentences Ada had just read, learning that Edmund Parish was not a murderer but something the law had no clean word for: a man who failed to prevent a murder and then forged the aftermath. An accessory after the fact, the statutes said, as if the fact were a place one could stand behind.

The moral floor was gone. She felt for it the way one's foot seeks a stair in the dark and finds air. Because here was the terrible arithmetic her father had left her: Gerald had killed her mother, and Gerald would die unpunished — was dying already, comfortably, in a house with a good roof — and the only person who could still be harmed by the truth was Ada herself. Justice, applied now, would land nowhere but on her. Expose Gerald, and Marcus's investigators would descend; declare her alive, and she became a legal person with a legal claim and a legal vulnerability, a target with a name. Bury it, and her mother stayed drowned in the official record forever, murdered twice — once in fact, once in paperwork.

*Some truths are kept, not told.* Her father had written that once, long ago, in the first letter. She had thought it wisdom. Now she recognized it for what it was: a man handing his daughter the shovel and calling it a keepsake.

She gathered the pages together with hands that had finally stopped shaking, or had passed through shaking into something colder and more useful. She would not burn them. Not tonight. Perhaps not ever — that was the trap of inheritance; you never got to choose only the parts you wanted. She slid them into the case beside the passport in her dead name, and the photographs of her mother laughing on the sea wall in a summer that predated all of it, and she locked the case, and she returned it to the cavity behind the vestry wall, and she set the panel back flush so that no one, looking, would ever know the wall was hollow.

Then she climbed the tower stairs in the dark, past the inaccurate clock, up to the archive where the records no one read slept in their acid-free boxes, and she stood at the narrow window with the rain running down the glass and the village lights scattered below her like embers that had refused to go out.

Eleven miles away, Gerald Parish was sleeping, or failing to sleep, in a house with a good roof. Two days ago a stranger had sat in the third pew from the front at Sunday service, asking the vicar gentle questions about parish records, and Ada had smiled her archivist's smile and performed her archivist's calm, and had gone home and been afraid in the ordinary, manageable way.

Now the fear had a shape, and the shape had a name, and the name was on a birth certificate that had never existed and a death certificate that someday might.

Below, at the edge of the churchyard, a figure crossed the lychgate and paused — a woman, coat collar up against the rain, standing a moment in the lamplight before the porch. Ada knew the walk. She had catalogued it, the way she catalogued everything: Petra Villalobos, arriving unannounced at nine o'clock at night, carrying a leather folder under her arm.

Coming, Ada understood, to finish what her father had started. Coming for the rest of the confession — or coming, God help them both, for her.

Ada stepped back from the window into the dark, and listened to footsteps on wet stone, and waited to see which of them would speak first.
