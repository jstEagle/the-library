## 14. The Man from the Probate Office

He came on a Tuesday, which Petra would remember afterward as the first honest thing about him. The rain had stopped overnight and the village lay rinsed and gleaming under a thin October sun, gutters still chuckling with the last of the storm, and the man from the probate office arrived at her temporary office — the borrowed front room of the old saddlery on Fenwick Lane, which Mrs. Aldous let out by the week to anyone with a briefcase and a reason — at nine-forty in the morning, carrying an umbrella he clearly hadn't needed.

He was not what she had expected. She had spent two days since the discovery of the photographed letter constructing him in her head: heavy, blunt, a man who leaned on doors. Instead he was slight and silvering, somewhere in his middle fifties, dressed in a good grey coat that had been expensive ten years ago and worn with the confidence of someone who no longer cared what it cost new. He carried a leather folder and a paper cup of coffee he never drank from. He introduced himself as Mr. Callum Reeve, of Reeve & Associates, probate investigations, London, and he set his card on the table between them with the gentle precision of a man laying down a trump.

"Miss Villalobos," he said. "Thank you for seeing me."

"You didn't give me much choice," Petra said. "Your letter said you'd come either way."

"I find it's better to say so. People imagine investigators enjoy ambushes. We don't. Ambushes are for people with something to hide." He smiled. It was a good smile, warm at the edges, and it did not reach anywhere near his eyes. "May I sit?"

She gestured to the chair across the trestle table she'd been using as a desk. The room smelled of saddle soap and cold plaster. Through the window behind him she could see the church tower rising over the rooftops, grey stone against grey sky, and she was aware — with the particular hyperawareness that had lived in her chest since the night she'd found her office searched — that the tower was where Ada was, right now, cataloguing something no one would ever read.

"Before we begin," Reeve said, settling himself, "I want to be transparent about my client. You'll have wondered who's paying for all this." He said *all this* lightly, as though the searched office, the stranger at Sunday service, the questions trailing her through two counties like cigarette smoke were simply weather. "My principal is Mr. Daniel Parish, of Cheltenham. Edmund Parish's nephew. His brother Gerald's son."

Petra kept her face still. Gerald Parish. The name surfaced from the will's supporting papers — the brother who had predeceased Edmund by four years, leaving one son and a quantity of unresolved resentment that the estate's correspondence made no effort to disguise.

"Mr. Daniel Parish believes," Reeve continued, unfolding his hands, "that his uncle's will defrauds him. He believes the estate — the house, the land, the investments, some four hundred thousand pounds after costs — has been diverted to a beneficiary who does not exist. And he has retained me to establish that fact."

"And what fact is that?"

"That 'Ada' — the named beneficiary, the so-called daughter — died twenty years ago. Drowned, off the Suffolk coast, August 1999. There is a death certificate. There is an inquest record. There is a memorial plaque in the church you can see out that window." He turned his head and looked at the tower himself, briefly, almost fondly. "A dead woman cannot inherit. If the will names a dead woman, it fails, and the estate passes to the next of kin. Which is my client."

"The will doesn't name Ada Parish," Petra said. "It names 'my daughter, should she live.' I've read every word of it, Mr. Reeve. The drafting is mine to interpret, not yours."

"Of course." He inclined his head, conceding the point the way a chess player concedes a pawn. "But interpretation requires evidence, doesn't it? Either the daughter lives — in which case someone must produce her, identify her, prove her existence to the Probate Registry — or she does not, in which case the clause fails. My client is content with either outcome, provided it's the true one." He paused. "Though I'll confess, between us, that he hopes rather strongly for the second."

*Between us.* As if they were colleagues. As if the last three weeks hadn't happened. Petra folded her own hands on the table and studied him. He had a small face, she decided, precise features in a soft frame, the kind of face that could go unnoticed in any room it chose. She thought of the photographs taken from her desk — copied, not stolen, left exactly in order, a message rather than a theft. She thought of the stranger in the back pew with the parish records question, and the retired doctor two counties away saying *someone else asked me last week the same questions.*

"That was you," she said. "At the service. At Dr. Halloway's cottage. In my office."

"Yes." No flinch, no denial. He picked up the untouched coffee, considered it, set it down again. "I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. You're better than most of the solicitors I deal with; you'd have caught me in a lie inside a minute, and then we'd have wasted the morning. So: yes. All of it. I photographed your documents because I needed to know what you knew, and I wanted you to know I knew it. Consider it professional courtesy."

"It was breaking and entering."

"It was entering," Reeve said mildly, "and the door was less secure than it should have been, which I'd suggest you raise with your landlord. But if it helps, nothing was removed, nothing was damaged, and I paid for the broken lock mechanism anonymously through a locksmith in Taunton. I'm not a criminal, Miss Villalobos. I'm thorough."

Outside, a delivery van ground past in low gear, and a woman crossed the lane with a wheeled shopping trolley, nodding to someone Petra couldn't see. The ordinary world going on its ordinary way, three feet of glass away.

"Let me tell you what I've established," Reeve went on, and now he opened the leather folder, and his voice took on the flat, unhurried cadence of a man reading from work he was proud of. "Edmund Parish certified his own death with the assistance of Dr. Martin Halloway, who signed a death certificate on the fourth of March stating cause of death as cardiac failure, and who retired within the week and has not practised since. The body buried in St. Cuthbert's churchyard on the eleventh is, according to the funeral director's records — which I have — a closed coffin, interment arranged by telephone, payment in cash. There is no coroner's report because none was required. The doctor's signature was genuine. The death was not."

Petra said nothing. Everything he was saying she had already found herself, alone, in the grey hours of the morning, and hearing it laid out in another man's tidy voice made it sound worse — made it sound like a scheme instead of a rescue.

"Now. Why would a wealthy recluse fake his own death?" Reeve turned a page. "My client's theory is tax, or spite, or both. Edmund Parish wanted to die free of the Inland Revenue and watch his family get nothing. Colourful, but it doesn't survive contact with the dates. Because here is the interesting part." He looked up at her, and for the first time his eyes were fully awake, fully interested, and Petra felt the temperature of the room drop. "The will is dated the seventh of March. Three days *after* the certificate. A man cannot execute a valid will while legally dead — or rather, he can, but it raises questions my client is very keen to have raised in court. Questions about capacity. About witnesses. About who actually drafted that document, and when, and why."

"The date was an error," Petra said. "Clerical. I've noted it for rectification."

"A clerical error," Reeve repeated softly, tasting it. "Three days. Signed by two witnesses whose statements I've taken — separately — and who each swear they signed on the seventh, in the presence of a living Edmund Parish, who was, they insist, in excellent health." He closed the folder. "You see the problem. Either the whole village is lying, or the death is. And the village, Miss Villalobos, is lying beautifully. Twenty years of practice, I suspect."

The silence stretched. Rain-washed light moved slowly across the floorboards. Somewhere above them a gull cried once and gave up.

"What do you actually want from me?" Petra asked.

"Cooperation. Nothing sinister." He spread his hands. "You're the executor's solicitor and the drafter of record. I need access to the original will, the witness statements, the estate accounts. I need you to confirm, formally, whether you believe the named beneficiary exists. That's all. If she exists — produce her. Identification, interview, registry forms. Done properly, she inherits, my client grumbles, everyone goes home. If she doesn't exist—" he shrugged, a small economical movement, "—then the will fails, and justice, of a sort, is served."

"And if she exists," Petra said quietly, "but doesn't wish to be produced?"

Reeve regarded her for a long moment. Then he did something unexpected: he sighed, and some of the polish dropped away, and underneath it there was a tired man doing a job he was good at and no longer loved.

"Then we arrive at the difficult conversation," he said. "So let's have it now, while we're being honest with each other. My client isn't only interested in the money. Four hundred thousand is real, but Daniel Parish is not poor. What he is, is humiliated. His uncle cut the family out — cut *him* out — for a daughter nobody has seen in twenty years, a daughter who is, on paper, at the bottom of the sea. Do you understand what that means to a man like him? It means his uncle preferred a ghost. It means the ghost gets everything. He wants her declared dead, Miss Villalobos. Formally, legally, permanently. He wants a court to look at the evidence and say: this person never survived, this inheritance is void, the record is corrected. And then it's finished. Not just the money. The *person*. Struck from the living world by order of the High Court. There are people in this country who exist only in filing cabinets, and my client intends to make sure that's where she stays — if she exists at all."

"You're assuming she exists."

"I'm assuming nothing," Reeve said. "I'm observing. I observe that the archivist in the tower took a file off the shelf the day after your name appeared in the visitor log, and put it in her coat, and walked out with it. I observe that she requested three weeks' leave beginning the first of November and then withdrew the request two days later. I observe that she has lived in this parish under her present name for nineteen years, employed by a vicarage that has never once advertised the post." He paused, letting each item settle between them like stones dropped in still water. "I observe that you met her for tea on Thursday, and that neither of you told the other anything true."

Petra's blood went cold and stayed cold. Not fear, exactly — something colder than fear, something with a shape. *He knows. He's known for weeks. This meeting isn't an opening move; it's the endgame, and he came to tell me so.*

"If you already know," she said, "why come here at all?"

"Because of what happens next." Reeve leaned forward, and his voice lowered, and it was somehow worse for being gentle. "If I take what I have to my client, he instructs counsel, counsel applies to the court, and the machinery starts. Subpoenas. Depositions. Exhumation orders — yes, they do those, even for coffins full of sand, especially for coffins full of sand. Within six months there will be journalists in Marlow St. Cuthbert, and a woman who has spent twenty years being no one will be on the front page of every paper in the country as the dead girl who wasn't. And my client — who is, I'll say this for him, not cruel, merely certain — will then do everything in his considerable power to have her committed for identity fraud, prosecuted for the false records, stripped of the name she's built. Permanently, Miss Villalobos. That's the word he uses. *Permanently.*"

He stood, buttoned his coat, and collected the coffee cup he'd never drunk from.

"But before I file my report, I'm instructed to make you one offer. One." He took an envelope from the folder and placed it on the table, squared to the edge, the way he'd squared his card. "Immunity. Full immunity, for you, for the doctor, for the witnesses, for anyone who cooperates — my client has no interest in prosecuting the living, only in correcting the record. And a fee, for your professional services in certifying the beneficiary's death. Fifty thousand pounds. The paperwork would be straightforward; you know better than anyone how straightforward. A declaration, a correction to the register, a quiet amendment. Nobody hurt. Nobody exposed. A woman who has been dead for twenty years simply... remains so. With dignity. With the past left buried, which I suspect — " and here his eyes flicked, once, deliberately, toward the tower and back — "is more than the truth would give her."

Petra looked at the envelope. It was thin. A single page, probably. Terms. Her name, probably, spelled correctly, in a lawyer's hand.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I file my report on Friday," Reeve said, "and the machinery starts, and neither of us controls it anymore. I've done this job thirty years, Miss Villalobos. The machinery doesn't care about anyone. Not her, not you, not the dead man in the sand. It just turns." He moved to the door, then stopped, hand on the latch, and glanced back with something that was almost kindness. "For what it's worth — and I know it isn't worth much — I read the confession letter you're keeping in the lining of your document case. I didn't photograph that page. Some things a man shouldn't have to carry twice."

The door clicked shut behind him. His footsteps faded down Fenwick Lane, unhurried, even, a man with nowhere to hide and nowhere to hurry to.

Petra sat for a long time in the saddle-smelling silence. Then she picked up the envelope, weighed it, and did not open it. Outside, the sun had gone behind the church tower, and the shadow of it lay across the lane like a finger pointing — at the saddlery, at her, at the woman in the tower who had until Friday.

She was already reaching for her coat.

* * *

In the archive at the top of the tower, Ada Marsh stood at the window with a box of baptismal registers in her arms she had no memory of picking up, watching a grey-coated man walk away down the lane toward the bus stop, and understanding, with the calm of a diver watching the surface close over her, that whatever he had come to say, the clock on her twenty years had just begun to run.
