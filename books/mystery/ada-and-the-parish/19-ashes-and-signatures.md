## 19. Ashes and Signatures

The fire had gone out of the parish office sometime before dawn, and what remained of it sat in the room like a guest who had overstayed its welcome — a smell of wet char, a ceiling stained the colour of old tea, and forty years of parish records reduced to a black drift that crunched underfoot like frost.

Petra stood in the doorway at seven in the morning, her coat still damp from the walk up from the village, and let herself look at it properly. The filing cabinets had been opened — every drawer, every one — and their contents pulled out and burned where they lay. Whoever had done this had not been careless. They had been thorough. The flames had been fed by hand, drawer by drawer, until there was nothing left to feed them. Then someone had thought to turn on the sprinkler system, or perhaps the fire brigade had arrived in time to save the walls. It hardly mattered. Paper was paper. Once it was ash, no subpoena in England could raise it.

Ada was not there. Of course she wasn't. Petra had not expected her to be, and yet some part of her — the part that had spent twenty years reading documents instead of people, because documents at least told you when they were lying — kept scanning the doorway, the vestry stairs, the shadowed corner behind the ruined cabinet where a person might stand if she wanted very badly not to be seen.

Nothing moved. Rain ticked against the broken window. Somewhere below, the vicar was on the telephone, his voice rising and falling in the cadence of a man explaining a catastrophe he did not understand.

Petra crouched and picked up a scrap from the edge of the burn line, where the heat had been gentler. A corner of a marriage register, 1974, the ink scorched brown but legible: *Edmund Parish to Margaret —*. She held it for a moment, then set it down again among the ashes. There would be time enough for grief later. Or there wouldn't. Grief, she had learned, was one of those things you scheduled around work, like lunch.

She heard the car before she saw it — tyres on wet gravel, unhurried, the arrival of a man who had never once needed to hurry in his life.

* * *

He came up the outside stair the way he seemed to do everything: openly, without apology, as though the world had been built to accommodate him and had simply failed to keep up. He was wearing a good coat today, charcoal wool, and he carried an umbrella furled but unused, as if rain were something that happened to other people. Behind him, at a respectful distance, came the younger man Petra had seen once before, loitering outside the post office with a camera bag he never opened.

"Miss Villalobos." The investigator's voice was warm gravel. "You're up early."

"So are you."

"I heard about the fire." He stepped over the threshold into the ruined room, and his eyes went around it slowly, cataloguing — the cabinets, the ash, the water stain spreading down the wall like a map of somewhere unpleasant. "Terrible thing. Old buildings. Wiring, I expect."

"Do you?"

"I'd want it confirmed, naturally. For my report." He smiled. It was a professional smile, well-maintained, like the coat. "You were here first, I think. Before the brigade even. The vicar says you were seen coming up the lane at half five."

"The vicar sees a great deal," Petra said. "It's why villages have them."

"Mm." He drifted toward the nearest cabinet, looked into its empty drawers with the mild interest of a man browsing a shop he didn't intend to buy from. "My client is going to be disappointed. He had such hopes for these records. Twenty years of baptisms, marriages, burials — all the little machinery of a life. And now—" he gestured at the ash with two fingers, delicately, "—nothing."

"Then your client has nothing to worry about," Petra said. "If there's nothing here, there's nothing to contest."

"That's one way of looking at it." He turned to face her fully then, and she saw that the warmth in his face was a surface treatment only, like veneer on cheap wood; underneath it his eyes were doing arithmetic. "Another way is that a fire which destroys exactly the records my client needs, on exactly the night before we intended to examine them, is not wiring. It's strategy. And strategy implies a player."

"Or bad luck."

"Bad luck," he repeated, tasting the word. "I've been in this business twenty-two years, Miss Villalobos. Do you know how often I've encountered bad luck? Never. Not once. Bad luck is what people call the things they've arranged and are ashamed of." He took a step closer, and the smell of the ash seemed to gather around him, or perhaps he carried it with him now, the way he seemed to carry everything else — insurance, leverage, patience. "But let's say I'm wrong. Let's say it's wiring. Then I go back to London, I tell my client the trail is cold, and everyone goes home poorer and wiser. That's the version where you're innocent. Would you like to hear the other version?"

"I imagine you'll tell me regardless."

The smile again. "The other version is that Edmund Parish faked his death, defrauded his estate, and left it — through a clause so transparently fraudulent that any first-year solicitor could tear it apart — to a daughter who has been legally dead for twenty years. The other version is that the woman cataloguing dust in that tower is Ada Parish, alive, breathing, and committing fraud every day she signs her name. The other version is that you know all of this, Miss Villalobos, and that last night you either watched a crime happen or committed one." He paused, letting the words settle into the wet air between them. "And the other version ends with me walking out of here with everything I need, and you walking into a disciplinary hearing with your name struck off the roll."

Outside, the rain thickened against the window. Downstairs, the vicar's phone conversation had stopped; the silence was the listening kind.

"You're forgetting something," Petra said.

"Am I?"

"Evidence." She said it the way she said everything in courtrooms and closing letters: flatly, precisely, without decoration. "You've just given me a beautiful speech. Speeches aren't evidence. You have no will — the original was in my office, and my office is intact, and the will names no one but the village trust. You have no death certificate irregularity on record, because the certificate was signed by a doctor who has since retired, and retired doctors' paperwork is famously unimpeachable. You have no confession —"

"No?" His eyebrows rose, gently, theatrically. "What about the letter? Postmarked the day after the funeral. Half-finished. In his hand. Naming a witness called Ada. I understand it arrived at your office in March. I understand you read it more than once. I understand—" and here his voice softened into something almost kind, which was worse than cruelty, "—that you've been carrying it around with you. In your briefcase. Which is, I'm told, a very fine briefcase."

Petra felt the floor tilt, very slightly, the way a ship tilts when you realise the water is already inside.

"You photographed it," she said. "In my office. When you searched it."

"We photographed *a* letter," he corrected. "Whether it was *the* letter depends entirely on whether you confirm it exists. Which brings us to the interesting part." He reached into his coat — slowly, showing her his hand was empty until it wasn't — and produced a slim leather folder, which he laid open on the one surviving surface, a windowsill scrubbed clean of ash. Inside were photographs. Her own handwriting on her own legal pad. The confession in Edmund Parish's slanted, patient script, page after page, each image sharp enough to read aloud.

"Here is what survives the fire, Miss Villalobos," he said. "This. My photographs. Taken lawfully — or near enough — in a room I had every right to enter, since my client retained me to investigate a suspected fraud and your office door lock is, frankly, an insult. These images establish that a confession existed. What they cannot establish is what happened to it. If it's been destroyed — and I notice your briefcase is rather lighter than it was in April — then destroying evidence in a probate matter is a criminal offence, and I become a witness to it. If it hasn't been destroyed, then I'd like to see it. Either way, I win. That's the architecture of a good case. Every door leads into the same room."

Petra looked at the photographs for a long moment. Long enough that he would read the pause however he wished to read it. She had learned that from her predecessor, the young lawyer in the flashback chapter of another woman's life — that silence was a document too, and everyone signed it with their own fears.

Then she reached past him and closed the folder.

"There was no letter," she said.

The investigator blinked. It was the first unguarded expression she had seen on him, and she treasured it, folded it away, knew she would take it out and look at it for years.

"I beg your pardon?"

"There was no letter." She met his eyes. "You've photographed blank pages, Mr.— I'm sorry, I don't believe I ever caught your name, and I find I don't care. Whatever your man saw in my briefcase was a draft of the estate inventory. My handwriting. Edmund Parish never wrote to me. He never wrote to anyone. He was a recluse who distrusted the post, which is a matter of record — ask the postmistress, ask the vicar, ask anyone in this village who ever tried to get a Christmas card out of him. Your photographs show pages you can't produce, describing a letter that doesn't exist, witnessed by a break-in you won't admit to. Take them to the Solicitors Regulation Authority. Take them to the police. Take them to your client and bill him for the train fare." She picked up the folder and held it out to him. "There is no case. There never was a case. There is a dead man, a valid will, and a village that received his money, and if you'd like to explain to a judge why any of that constitutes fraud, I'll see you in court and enjoy myself enormously."

For a moment — three seconds, four — neither of them moved. The rain filled the silence. She could hear her own heart, steady, and beneath it the older rhythm, the one from twenty years ago, the year someone had falsified a record to save her life and told her never to look back: *this is what it costs*, that memory said. *This is the price. You pay it every day and call it a career.*

The investigator took the folder. His fingers were perfectly steady; hers had been too, and that surprised her.

"You're lying," he said quietly. Not accusingly. Almost admiringly. "And you're good at it. Better than I expected."

"Yes," Petra said. "I am."

"And the archivist? The woman in the tower? Because my client has a photograph of her, aged nine, standing beside Edmund Parish at a regatta in 1998, and he has a photograph of the tower's tenant taken last month, and he has a face, Miss Villalobos, and faces are difficult to burn."

"Then your client should bring his photographs to the police," Petra said, "and explain how he came to be comparing them. Explain why a grieving nephew hired a private investigator before the funeral. Explain the questions he asked the doctor in Norfolk — yes, I know about that visit; Dr. Halloway keeps notes, and Dr. Halloway is frightened, and frightened men remember faces. Explain all of it, under oath, to a bench that will ask why a family that claims to have loved Edmund Parish spent twenty years trying to prove his daughter died." She stepped closer, close enough that the ash smell was all there was between them. "Your client doesn't want justice. He wants the tower flat, the trust income, and a woman declared dead so thoroughly that she stays that way. And I am telling you, as an officer of the court whose signature you will need on every document you ever file in this matter: he will not get it. Walk away. Bill him. Find a quieter fraud somewhere it doesn't rain."

The investigator studied her. She watched him weigh it — the case against the cost of the case, the certainty against the expense of proving it, the photograph of a child at a regatta against a lawyer who had just lied to his face without blinking and clearly would do it again, under oath, with pleasure. She watched him arrive, reluctantly, at the arithmetic she had done for him.

"People burn for less," he said at last.

"People burn for nothing, usually," Petra said. "That's the tragedy of fires."

He closed the folder. Tucked it away. Straightened his coat with two small tugs, a man restoring order to himself. At the doorway he paused, half-turned, the ruined room behind him and the grey morning ahead.

"For what it's worth," he said, "I don't think it was wiring either."

"No," Petra agreed. "I don't suppose you do."

"But I can't prove anything, and you can't prove I can't, and my client's money is finite, and mine is earned by the hour." He smiled the professional smile one last time, and this time she saw the tiredness underneath it, the whole career of doors that led into the same room, most of them empty. "Good morning, Miss Villalobos. I hope the village enjoys its inheritance."

His footsteps went down the iron stair, measured, unhurried. A car door. Gravel. Gone.

* * *

She stayed in the ruined office for a long while after that, long after the vicar had crept up to check on her and crept away again, satisfied that lawyers grieved in their own way. She picked through the ash at the edge of the burn line and found small things: the brass corner of a ledger, a hinge, a key fused to its own lock. She found the scorched corner of the 1974 register again and put it in her pocket without deciding to. Some documents deserved burial. This one would get it.

Victory, she thought, should feel different from this. It should feel like setting down weight, and instead it felt like picking it up — the confession that had never existed now lived inside her, in the place where her own falsified record lived, in the crowded dark where she kept the things she had chosen over the truth. Complicity, she understood now, was not a single act. It was a tenancy. You moved in, and you furnished it, and one morning you realised you had stopped thinking of it as temporary.

She was nearly at the door when she saw it.

On the windowsill, beneath the spot where the investigator's folder had lain, half-hidden under a film of ash: an envelope. Unburned. Dry, impossibly, in all that ruin — as though it had been placed there this morning, after the rain, by a hand that knew exactly which sill stayed sheltered.

Her name was on it. *P. Villalobos.* In Edmund Parish's slanted, patient hand.

Petra stood very still. Below, the village woke: a dog, a kettle, a bell practising for Sunday. Above, in the tower, a light she had not noticed before went out, quietly, the way a person steps back from a window.

She picked up the envelope. It was heavy — heavier than a letter should be. Something slid inside it with a soft papery whisper, and when she tilted it to the grey light, she caught the gleam of a lining, a second sheet folded within the first, sealed along its edges like a document hidden in the spine of a book.

Edmund Parish, it seemed, had not finished writing to her after all.

She tucked it inside her coat, against her heart, next to the register corner and twenty years of other people's secrets, and went down the stairs into the rain to find out what the dead man wanted now.
