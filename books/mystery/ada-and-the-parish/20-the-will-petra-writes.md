## 20. The Will Petra Writes

The office was cold when Petra let herself in on Monday morning — the particular cold of a room that has been watched and knows it. She stood in the doorway a long moment before touching anything, reading the space the way she had learned to read documents: not for what was there, but for what had been disturbed.

The confession letter was where she'd left it, in the second drawer of her desk beneath the tax files no one ever opened. The photograph of it — she was certain now there had been a photograph; the paper sat a half-degree off true against the drawer's alignment, and the dust along its top edge bore the faint interruption of fingertips — would already be in someone's hands. That was the point. The man from the probate office wanted her to know he could reach into her life whenever he liked, that her locks were courtesy rather than protection.

Good, she thought, switching on the desk lamp. Let him watch.

Because what she intended to do this morning required an audience of exactly one, and it might as well be him.

She made tea first. Ritual mattered in forgery — not the moral kind, which was simple cowardice or simple love depending on which side of it you woke up on, but the technical kind. Her hands needed to be warm. Her mind needed to be slow. She had spent twenty years learning how documents were born: the way ink settled differently on a page written quickly versus one written with care, the way genuine hesitation left tremors in the downstrokes that no forger ever thought to reproduce because forgers were always trying to look confident. Edmund Parish had understood this. His will — the impossible one, dated three days after his own death — had been written slowly, lovingly, with all the deliberate imperfection of a man who wanted it examined.

She took out a fresh legal pad and did not write on it. Instead she wrote on plain cream stock, the same weight as the paper in Edmund's personal stationery, which she had taken from the tower flat two days ago with Ada standing in the doorway behind her, arms crossed, watching Petra steal from a dead man with the tenderness of a thief lifting bread.

"You're sure about this," Ada had said. Not a question. A door held open, so that Petra could walk through it or not.

"I'm sure about nothing," Petra had answered. "That's why I'm the one who has to do it."

* * *

The problem, stated plainly, was this: Edmund Parish had died — actually died, heart stopped, lungs empty, the whole irreversible business — sometime in the week before his funeral. Of this Petra was now nearly certain, though she could prove none of it. The death certificate was real. The doctor who signed it was real, and frightened, and living under a name that wasn't his in a cottage full of orchids two counties east. And the will was dated after the death, which meant it was void, which meant the estate fell into intestacy, which meant it passed to the next of kin — and the only next of kin with a living pulse was a nephew in Manchester who had hired a polite man with a probate-office lanyard to make certain that "my daughter, should she live" remained forever a legal impossibility.

So the law offered Petra two doors. Through the first: declare the will invalid, let the estate flow to the nephew, and Ada Marsh — née Parish, dead these twenty years on every register that mattered — would remain dead, safe, and dispossessed. Through the second: prove Ada alive, execute the will, and hand her name to the investigator like a summons.

Petra had spent four nights choosing neither door. On the fifth night, somewhere between the third whisky and the first grey light, she had understood that there was a third option, and that it had been waiting for her all along, patient as paperwork: write a new will.

Not forge Edmund's. Forge his *intention*. There was a difference, and the difference was everything. A forged signature was a lie about a fact. What she meant to build was a lie about a document — a will dated correctly, witnessed properly, executed in accordance with every statute she had spent her career mastering, which happened never to have existed until Tuesday morning. It would say what Edmund had wanted said. It would leave what he had wanted left. And it would do it in language so clean, so unremarkable, so perfectly ordinary that no examiner in England would ever look twice.

The village would inherit. The church roof, the school fund, the footpath maintenance, the small bequests to people who had been kind to a recluse without knowing why he needed kindness. All of it flowing outward, generous and diffuse, leaving nothing concentrated enough to fight over.

And one clause — quiet, buried in the schedule of properties like a stone in a wall — transferring the tower flat above the parish archive to a tenant named Ada Marsh, lease of ninety-nine years, rent of one peppercorn per annum if demanded.

Peppercorn rents were old law. Ancient, respectable, beloved of solicitors precisely because they looked quaint. No one questioned a peppercorn. No one had questioned a peppercorn in three hundred years.

* * *

She began at nine o'clock with the date, because the date was the wound everything else had to close over. Fourteenth of March — eleven days before the death certificate, comfortably before any illness could have been argued to have clouded his capacity, late enough that it postdated the quarrel with his brother that Ada had finally described to her in the vestry, both of them sitting in the dark among the boxed registers like conspirators in a confessional with no priest.

Her pen moved. She had chosen a fountain pen, medium nib, blue-black ink — Edmund's ink, Edmund's pen, both recovered from the tower flat in a leather case that still smelled of his study, of pipe smoke and paper and the particular dust of a life lived alone on purpose. Writing with a dead man's pen felt less like desecration than like channeling. Her hand was not his hand. But she had spent three evenings practicing his script from the letters he'd sent his solicitor in the nineties, forty pages of correspondence she'd borrowed and photocopied and burned, and by the third evening something uncanny had begun to happen: her muscles had stopped fighting the letterforms and started remembering them. The slight forward lean. The open bowl of his *a*. The way his *P* in *Parish* broke its stem just below the loop, a flaw from some childhood injury to the penmanship no teacher ever corrected.

Capacity clause. Revocation clause — revoking all prior wills, which was legally necessary and privately exquisite, since it revoked the very document whose existence she was manufacturing the authority to revoke. Appointment of executor: Petra Villalobos, of Villalobos & Associates. She wrote her own name in a dead man's hand and felt the sentence settle around her shoulders like a coat cut for someone else.

Then the dispositions. This was the part that took the longest, and not because the drafting was hard. Because she kept stopping.

She kept stopping because she could hear him. Not literally — she was not a woman given to ghosts, and grief had taught her long ago the difference between presence and memory. But the voice was there anyway, dry and precise, the voice from the single time they'd met, when Edmund Parish had interviewed his own lawyer with more care than most lawyers interview clients. *You survived something,* he'd said to her, in a room that smelled of beeswax and cold ash. *I can always tell. You have the posture of a person who once read a document that saved her life.* And she had said nothing, and he had nodded as though she'd said everything, and hired her on the spot.

He had known. From the beginning, he had known about her — about the falsified record, the missing year, the stranger who had bent the law until it sheltered a frightened girl. He had chosen her not despite her compromise but because of it. The confession letter, postmarked the day after his funeral, addressed to her specifically: *because you once needed someone else to carry it for you, and they did, and you owe the debt forward.*

She owed the debt forward. Very well. She picked up the pen.

To the Parish Charitable Trust, established hereunder, the freehold of Church Farm together with its lands... To the Parochial Church Council of Marlow St. Cuthbert, the sum of... To Mrs. Iris Tallowin, in recognition of thirty years' friendship, my grandfather clock...

And then, in the Second Schedule, item seven, sandwiched between the boathouse and a strip of riverside meadow:

**7. The dwelling known as the Tower Flat, situate above the Parish Archive, Church Lane, together with a leasehold interest therein of ninety-nine years, unto ADA MARSH, of Church Lane aforesaid, at a peppercorn rent if demanded.**

She wrote the name slowly. Ada Marsh. A name that had never existed until ten days ago, invented over cold tea in the archive while Ada laughed for the first time since Petra had known her — a startled, rusty sound, like a hinge opening. *Marsh,* Ada had said. *Because I've spent twenty years standing in one.* The name was already on a tenancy application in Petra's briefcase, backdated six weeks, supported by a reference from a landlady in Wells-next-the-Sea who owed Petra a favor older than either of them cared to count.

Item seven. Perfectly ordinary. Utterly damning, if anyone ever traced the sequence of events. And invisible, if no one ever did.

* * *

The witnesses were the hardest part.

A will needed two. They had to be present, they had to sign in her presence and each other's, and they had to be people who would never, under any pressure, be asked the right questions — or who, if asked, would answer with the serene confidence of the genuinely deceived.

She drove to Norwich that afternoon and found them in a café near the cathedral: a young couple, tourists, sharing a plate of chips and arguing amiably about whether to visit the castle. She approached them with the apologetic smile she had perfected over two decades — the smile of a slightly flustered professional — and explained that she was executing a will for a client too ill to travel, that the witnesses needed to be unconnected persons, that it would take two minutes and she would buy their coffees.

They signed. Daniel Okafor and Maren Kjeldsen, their names now permanently woven into a fiction none of them would ever perceive. The young man joked that he hoped he wasn't inheriting anything unpleasant. Petra laughed, paid for the chips, and walked back to her car with her heart going like a trapped bird.

It was done, then. The instrument existed. Dated correctly, witnessed properly, drafted in a dead man's hand upon his own paper with his own pen. If she died tomorrow, it would pass any examination. If the investigator came tomorrow, it would — she ran the scenarios again, the way she ran them for clients facing audit — it would hold. The nephew could challenge capacity; but the date defeated him, being months before the documented decline. He could challenge execution; but the witnesses were clean, untraceable to any party, photographed by no one. He could demand handwriting analysis; and here Petra allowed herself the smallest, coldest satisfaction, because she had studied Edmund's hand for three evenings and the analysis would find what analysts always found in careful work: authenticity, or at least reasonable doubt, and reasonable doubt was a fortress.

She sealed the will in an envelope. She did not seal the original confession — Edmund's true confession, the one naming Ada, the one that had started all of this — because that document had one last journey to make, and it was not into any file.

* * *

She drove back to Marlow St. Cuthbert in the dark, wipers working against a rain that had turned fine and persistent, the kind that seemed less like weather than like the landscape breathing. The village appeared the way it always did: late, sudden, a scatter of lamplit windows around the grey bulk of the church. The tower rose above the rooftops, and one window in it was lit.

She parked by the lychgate and sat with the engine ticking down, holding the envelope on her knees.

This was the part no one taught you. Law schools taught you the statutes, the precedents, the architecture of validity. They did not teach you what it cost to hold a document in your hands that you had conjured out of love and fraud in equal measure, knowing that every word of it was false and every word of it was true — false in its making, true in its meaning, a forgery that told the truth more faithfully than any authentic record ever had. Somewhere in a drawer in Manchester there was presumably a certificate declaring Ada Parish dead. Somewhere in the parish registers there was a burial entry in her name, inked by a grieving father who had needed the world to believe a lie in order to keep his daughter breathing. Those documents were authentic. They were also murder, slow and bureaucratic, twenty years of it.

The will in her lap was a crime. It was also the only honest paper in the entire affair.

She thought of the person who had falsified a record for her, twenty years ago — the registrar whose face she had never quite been able to recall since, deliberately, because remembering would have obligated her to gratitude and gratitude would have obligated her to everything else. She had never asked what it cost them. She had simply accepted the shelter and built her life inside it, the way a bird builds inside a wall, never asking what the wall gave up to be hollowed.

Now she knew what it cost. It cost this: a cold car, a rain-blurred village, a dead man's pen drying in your pocket, and the permanent knowledge that you had crossed a line and found, on the other side, not ruin but a strange level ground — as though the line had been drawn on a map by someone who had never actually walked the territory.

She got out. She locked the car. She carried the envelope through the lychgate, past the graves — past Edmund's fresh stone, granite not yet weathered, the dates carved with the neat finality of a sentence properly closed — and around to the tower door, where the light in the upper window waited.

Ada opened the door before she knocked. She had a way of doing that, of being already aware, as though twenty years of hiding had tuned her to footsteps the way deaf people are said to tune to vibration. She looked at the envelope, then at Petra's face, and stepped aside without a word.

Inside, the archive smelled of paper and lamp oil and the ghost of smoke — the filing room had been scrubbed and repainted after the fire, but the smell clung, and probably always would. Ada had made tea. Two cups. She had known Petra was coming the way she knew everything: by reading the signs no one else thought to leave.

"It's done," Petra said.

"Say it properly." Ada's voice was steady, but her hands, wrapped around her cup, were not. "I want to hear it properly."

"The will of Edmund Parish, dated the fourteenth of March, leaves the estate to the village in trust." Petra set the envelope on the table between them, on top of a box of burial registers, which struck her as either blasphemy or benediction. "The tower flat passes to a tenant named Ada Marsh, ninety-nine years, peppercorn rent."

"A peppercorn." Something moved through Ada's face — not quite a smile, not yet, but the ground a smile grows from. "He'd have hated that. He'd have wanted it to be dramatic. A raven, maybe. Delivered annually."

"He'd have wanted you alive," Petra said. "The rest is decoration."

Ada looked at the envelope for a long time. Then she reached out and touched it, one finger, the way you touch a door you're afraid is painted onto the wall. "And the other one? His real confession?"

Petra drew it from her coat — the letter, postmarked the day after the funeral, the half-finished confession, the name *Ada* in a dying man's hand. She had promised herself she would burn it. She had bought a metal dish for the purpose, and matches, and had rehearsed the moment on the drive home until it felt almost routine.

But now, with Ada looking at it, she found she couldn't. Not tonight. Perhaps not alone.

"Not yet," she said. "Tomorrow. At the grave. There's something we should — I think he'd want us both there. When it goes."

Ada studied her, and whatever she saw made her nod slowly, and pour the tea, and pull a second chair to the table so that the two of them sat across from each other with the forged will and the true confession stacked between them like the two halves of a single sentence.

Outside, the rain thickened. And down in the village, at the corner of Church Lane, a car that had been parked there since dusk switched on its headlights, held them for a long moment — a signal, or a warning, or merely a man deciding — and then drove slowly away toward the coast road, leaving Petra staring through the dark glass after it, her tea going cold in her hands.
