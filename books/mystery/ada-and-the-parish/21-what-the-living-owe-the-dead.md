## 21. What the Living Owe the Dead

The frost had come to Marlow St. Cuthbert in the night, and by morning it lay over the churchyard like a sheet drawn up to the chins of the dead. Ada Parish — who had been Ada Marsh for eleven days now, who had signed the name four times and each time felt the pen hesitate in her fingers like an animal that sensed the trap — stood at the lychgate with her hands in the pockets of her father's old coat and did not go in.

She had been to the grave twice already since the fire. The first time, the week after, when the ash smell still clung to the tower and the village still spoke of the filing room in the lowered voices reserved for accidents and scandal. The second time at dusk, when no one could see her, when she could stand at the granite slab and say the things she had rehearsed for twenty years and never once said aloud: *I'm here. You did it. I'm here.*

But this morning was different. This morning, when she came down the tower stairs, Petra Villalobos was waiting in the porch, breath steaming, hands clasped behind her back in the posture of a woman who had been standing there long enough to grow roots, and who had decided — visibly, deliberately — not to pretend she was there for any other reason.

"The vicar said you'd be up early," Petra said. "He said the archivist keeps churchyard hours."

"The archivist keeps the hours the dead keep," Ada said. "They're very punctual."

Something moved across Petra's face — not quite a smile, not quite grief. "I was going to walk out to the grave. I've been putting it off. Eleven days of paperwork and I still haven't managed to stand in front of him."

"Eleven days," Ada said. "You count."

"I count everything. It's the affliction that made me a lawyer." Petra looked out at the white grass, the leaning stones, the yew tree that had been dropping its black berries onto Parish graves for three hundred years. "Come with me? Or don't. I'm not —" She stopped, and Ada watched her choose honesty the way a swimmer chooses cold water, all at once, no wading. "I don't know how to do this. I've lied to you for months and you've lied to me for months and now the lying's over and I find I don't have anything else."

Ada looked at her for a long moment. The frost was melting off the lychgate roof in slow bright drops. She thought of her father's letter, the one in the locked case behind the vestry wall — *If anyone comes asking, you were never born* — and she thought of the second letter, the one that had come out of the will's lining, the one Petra had shown her by lamplight in the archive with the fire's smoke still in their hair, the one that named not Ada but Petra: *She must decide. That is the whole of it. I am sorry to put the weight on her, but I have watched her carry things before, and she does not set them down.*

Her father had trusted a stranger with his daughter's life. And the stranger had burned a confession, doctored a will, stared down a man with a probate office badge and a polite voice, and walked away carrying it — carrying it the way Ada's father had carried it, the way the young lawyer twenty years ago had carried it, generation after generation of people deciding that some truths were not for telling.

"Come with me," Ada said. "I'll show you which stone to stand on. He liked people to stand on the left. He said the right side sank."

* * *

The grave was at the far corner, under the yew, where the ground rose slightly toward the wall and the village beyond it was visible in miniature — the roofs, the lane, the tower where Ada had lived as a ghost inside her own ghost story. EDMUND PARISH, 1948–2023. BELOVED FATHER. The stone was new enough that the cut letters still held shadow in their grooves, and someone — Ada knew who; she had watched Mrs. Tredwell do it, pretending not to — had left a jar of snowdrops against the base.

Petra stood on the left side. Ada noticed, and had to look away at the wall for a moment.

"He'd have hated this," Ada said. "All the ceremony. He hated ceremony. He used to say funerals were for people who hadn't finished their sentences."

"Did you finish yours?"

Ada considered. "I've been finishing them for twenty years. A few at a time. In the dark, mostly, to a stone." She crouched and brushed frost from the granite with her bare hand, and the cold of it went up her arm like a memory. "He never got to hear the important one, though. The one I've been saving."

"What's that?"

"That I forgive him." She stood. "No — that's not honest. That's the version I practiced." She looked at Petra, and it cost her something, this next part; she could feel it leaving her like blood leaving a wound. "The honest one is that I don't know if I forgive him. He let the whole village bury me. He let them grieve. He watched Mrs. Tredwell cry in the shop for a decade and never told her. He made me perform my own death every single day — not the dying, that was easy, the dying happened once — but the being dead. The being dead is a full-time occupation, Petra. You have no idea. You get very good at being furniture. You get so good at it that some mornings you wake up and you *are* furniture, and the only thing that tells you you're a person is that you hurt."

"And now?"

"And now I'm tired of being furniture." She said it plainly, and the plainness of it surprised her. "I sat up all night after we burned the archive thinking I'd run. I had the bag packed. I had three routes out of the village and a name that wasn't even a name yet. And then it got light, and I looked at the light, and I thought: I have run from one man my whole life, and the man is dead, and the man's brother is dead, and the man's son is sitting in some office with no case and no records and no proof that I ever existed — and I am still running. He's dead and I'm still running. That's not survival anymore. That's just a habit with a bag packed."

The frost was going off the graves in patches, the grass emerging dark and wet, the whole churchyard slowly undressing.

"So you stay," Petra said.

"I stay." Ada heard herself say it and felt the strangest sensation, as though the words had been waiting in her chest for twenty years and had gone stale in there, and came out dusty and true. "As Ada Marsh. Archivist. Tenant of the tower flat, per the will of Edmund Parish, which you wrote, which is a forgery, which is the kindest thing anyone has ever done for me." She turned to face Petra fully. "I've been trying to work out how to say thank you for that, and I can't, because thank you is too small and too clean. What you did — burning the confession, lying to that man, putting your name on a false document — you could lose everything. Your practice. Your license. Your whole self, the self that's built out of doing things properly. I watched you do it anyway. I watched you choose me over the law, and I don't think you've even let yourself understand what that cost you yet."

"It hasn't cost me yet," Petra said. "That's the trouble. The bill always comes later. I'm very good at reading documents, Ada. I'm terrible at reading myself."

* * *

They walked the long way back along the wall, where the village fell away below and the fields ran white and bare toward the river. Neither of them spoke for a while. It was not the silence of the archive, which had always been a silence full of listening — two women circling each other through months of careful, beautiful lying. This was a different silence. Ada found she could stand in it.

"I want to ask you something," she said finally, "and you can refuse to answer. I've had enough of people answering for me. But I want to ask."

"Ask."

"What does it cost? Carrying it." She stopped at the stile where the wall broke for the footpath, and put her hand on the frozen wood. "You've done it now. The way my father did, the way that young lawyer did twenty years ago — the one who falsified the record for my mother's inquest, the one whose name was in the file, the one whose name I recognized when I saw yours on the visitor log, because I've read that file four hundred times and I know the handwriting of every person who ever lied to keep me alive. You're in that line now. Petra Villalobos, keeper of the secret. So I'm asking you, honestly, because I need to know what I've done to you: what does it cost?"

Petra was quiet for a long time. Below them a pheasant broke from the hedge and went clattering over the white field, absurd and loud, and the sound filled the whole valley.

"Less than you'd think and more than I can say," she said at last. "That's the honest answer. The cost isn't the fear. I expected the fear, and the fear is — manageable. It's a professional risk. I've priced it. I know what I could lose and I've decided it's worth losing, and that part is almost easy, because it's a *decision*, and I'm good at decisions." She looked out at the river, which was the color of pewter. "The cost is the other thing. The cost is that you never get to put it down. Ever. Twenty years from now, some clerk will find a discrepancy in a death certificate, or some child of the investigator will come asking questions the way he did, and I will still be here, still carrying it, still deciding, every single day, to keep it. It's not a weight you carry once up a hill. It's a weight you agree to carry *forever*, and the agreeing never stops. You wake up every morning and you choose it again. And the strange thing —" her voice did something small and complicated — "the strange thing is that I know how to do that. I've been doing it my whole life. Because someone did it for me, once. Someone falsified a record to save a girl who'd survived something she couldn't survive being known, and that girl grew up and went into paperwork because paperwork was the only place that felt honest, and she has spent thirty years being invisible on purpose, and she never once let herself ask who that person was who saved her, because asking would mean admitting she needed saving."

Ada's breath caught. "The lawyer. The one from the file. That was —"

"That was the woman who raised me." Petra said it steadily, but her hand on the stile had gone white. "My aunt. I found out when she died. I found her copy of the inquest file in her desk, with her notes, and my name — my real name, the one I had before she took me in — written in the margin. She never told me. She carried it for thirty years and died with it, and the only reason I know is that she left it where I'd find it, which was the closest she could come to telling me. So when your father's letter came to my office — addressed to me, out of all the lawyers in England, addressed to *me* — I understood immediately. He'd done his research. He'd found the one person alive who knew what it was to be saved by a lie." She turned to Ada then, and her eyes were wet and furious and clear. "That's what it costs. It costs you your whole life, and you pay it gladly, and you never say so, and the only person who ever understands is the other one carrying it."

The wind came up the valley and moved the bare hedges, and somewhere below a dog barked twice and stopped.

"Then we're the only two," Ada said.

"For now. There'll be a third, someday, if you ever tell anyone. If you ever have —" Petra stopped. Recalibrated. "There's always a third. That's how it works. The secret gets handed on like a parish record. That's not a threat, Ada. That's — I think it might be a kind of hope. It means someday someone will love you enough to lie for you."

Ada laughed — a short, cracked sound, the first true laugh she had produced in twenty years, and it startled her so much she put her hand over her mouth as though to catch it back. But it was out. It was out in the cold morning air over her father's grave, and it was not going back in.

* * *

They ended up at the tower, because there was nowhere else, and because neither of them wanted the morning to end and neither would say so. Ada made tea on the single ring in the flat that had been her prison and was now, by forged deed, her home. Petra stood at the window looking at the churchyard below, at the small granite stone under the yew, and did not turn around when she spoke.

"I filed the doctored will on Tuesday. Probate accepted it without comment. The estate passes to the village — the hall, the land, the trust for the school roof. Your tenancy is in there, buried in a schedule. Ada Marsh, three-year lease, renewable. No one will ever look at a lease schedule. No one has ever looked at a lease schedule in the history of English law."

"You've thought of everything."

"I've thought of everything *legal*," Petra said. "That's the frightening part. It's all clean. It's all proper. On paper, nothing happened at all — no fire worth investigating, no investigator with a case, no confession, no Ada Parish. On paper you were never born and I was never here." She turned. "Which means the only place any of it exists now is in this room. In us. Two women and a teacup."

Ada looked at her — at this woman she had lied to beautifully for months, who had lied to her right back, who had burned a building's worth of truth rather than let a single page of it be used against her. The light came through the tower window the way it did at mid-morning, thin and gold, catching the dust that was all that remained of the archive, and Petra Villalobos stood in it with her coat still on and her hands around a chipped cup, looking less like a lawyer than like a woman standing at the edge of something.

"I keep thinking about the letter," Ada said. "The second one. In the will's lining. He named you. *She must decide.* He put it all on you."

"He put it all on both of us. He just only told me." Petra's mouth curved. "He was always one clause ahead. I've read enough of his documents now to know that. He probably knew, when he wrote it, that you and I would end up in this room."

"He couldn't have known that."

"Ada." Petra set the cup down. "The man faked his own death, dated the will three days after it, and mailed a confession to a stranger. He was a clause ahead of all of us. I wouldn't be surprised if there's a third letter. I've stopped being surprised by that man."

Ada laughed again — easier this time — and then stopped, because Petra was not quite smiling. Petra was watching her with an expression that Ada had seen only once before, across the archive table, the night they had finally told each other everything: an expression of careful, terrified attention, the look of a person standing very still so as not to frighten something wild.

"What?" Ada said.

"Nothing. I was going to say something and it isn't a legal matter and I don't have a clause for it." Petra picked the cup back up, which was so transparently a deflection that Ada almost laughed again. "I was going to say that I've been in this village four months and I've never once wanted to stay anywhere, and now I find I keep finding reasons to come back. To file things. The filing here is *endless*, Ada, it's a scandal, the diocesan records alone —"

"Petra."

"Yes."

"Stay for lunch. The filing can wait. The dead are very patient." Ada heard her own voice, and it was warm, and it was unguarded, and it was hers — not Ada Parish's, not the dead girl's, not the furniture's. Ada Marsh's, a name eleven days old, a voice with nothing rehearsed in it. "The dead are patient. I've decided the living shouldn't have to be."

Petra looked at her for a long moment over the rim of the cup. Outside, the frost was entirely gone now, and the churchyard lay green and wet and ordinary, and somewhere below them the village bell began to ring the quarter-hour, the way it had rung through every year of Ada's hidden life, marking time for a girl who did not exist.

"All right," Petra said quietly. "Lunch."

And it was there, in the small warm room at the top of the tower, with the tea going cold and the bell ringing and the whole impossible weight of twenty years finally, finally set down between them — it was there that Ada saw, on the table by the door, the envelope that had come with the morning post. Her name on it. *Ada Marsh.* In handwriting she knew better than her own.

Her father's handwriting.

Petra saw her face change, and followed her eyes to the envelope, and went very still.

"Ada," she said. "That's postmarked yesterday."
