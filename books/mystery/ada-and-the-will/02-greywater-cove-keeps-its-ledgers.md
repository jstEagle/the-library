## 2. Greywater Cove Keeps Its Ledgers

The will sat on my desk for an hour before I touched it again.

I made coffee I didn't drink. I watered a fern that had been dead since 2019 because watering it was something my hands knew how to do when my mind needed somewhere else to be. Then I unlocked the bottom drawer of the filing cabinet—the one with no label—and took out the folder I had promised myself, seven years ago, I would never open again.

VANCE, MARISOL E.

I keep my files alphabetized. It's not a quirk; it's a philosophy. In Greywater Cove, the truth doesn't get destroyed. It gets filed. And whoever holds the key to the cabinet holds the town.

I learned that from my father.

* * *

My father came to Greywater Cove in 1974 with a law degree, a pregnant wife, and a talent for being exactly where powerful men needed him. He wasn't corrupt—I want to say that clearly, even now, even alone in this office with nobody to perform for. He was something more common and more useful than corrupt. He was accommodating.

He probated the first Wren estate transaction in 1976 and the two-hundredth by 1990. He drew up the leases for the fish processing plant that everyone knew moved more product off the books than on them. He drafted the easements that let Aldous Wren's grandfather's company run a private road across public tideland, and he did it so cleanly that no auditor ever found the seam. When the state finally came sniffing around the harbor in the eighties—Operation Undertow, they called it, though you'd never have heard that name spoken aloud in this town—my father's records were immaculate. Immaculate because three days before the subpoena arrived, he'd spent a night in this office with a shredder and a bottle of rye, and what remained was immaculate the way a plucked bird is immaculate.

He never talked about that night. But I was eleven, and I woke up thirsty, and I saw him through the frosted glass of his study, feeding paper into the machine with the steady rhythm of a man bailing out a boat. He looked up at me once. He didn't stop. In the morning there was a new bicycle in the garage, and I understood, at eleven, without anyone explaining it, that bicycles come from somewhere.

That's the inheritance nobody puts in a will: the knowledge of what your name is worth, and to whom.

When he died, I took over the practice. Same office, same cabinets, same fern—my mother bought it at his funeral, which is why I can't throw it out. And within a year, the pattern reasserted itself, the way water finds the old channel. Judge Aldous Wren called me personally to welcome me back to "the family business," and I laughed at the joke, and we both pretended it was one.

* * *

Here is what people outside a small coastal town don't understand about places like Greywater Cove. They think corruption looks like bags of cash and men in dark rooms. Mostly it looks like paperwork done promptly. It looks like a deed recorded the same afternoon. It looks like a lawyer who returns calls, because the fastest way to bury a thing is to process it efficiently, with a stamp and a date, until it becomes history instead of news.

Greywater Cove keeps its ledgers. That's the old saying, older than the Wrens. The cove survived two centuries of wreckers and rum-runners and quiet fortunes built on other people's disasters, and it survived because nothing here is ever thrown away. Everything is kept—in boathouses, in church basements, in county annexes, in the heads of families who don't speak to each other but remember everything. The town doesn't forgive and it doesn't forget. It files.

And for twenty-two years, I was the file clerk.

* * *

Marisol Vance walked into my office on a Tuesday in March, seven years ago, with salt in her hair and a manila envelope under her arm, and she said, "I need you to hold onto something, Mr. Nakamura. Not to do anything. Just to hold it."

She was thirty-four then. She ran the charter boat her father had left her, the *Kindly Light*, and she wrote in a diary she carried everywhere in a zippered plastic bag, and she asked questions the way some people breathe—constantly, involuntarily, without caring whether it annoyed anyone. She'd been asking questions about a shipment that went wrong the previous fall. A boat had gone aground on the north jetty in fog, and the cargo that washed up wasn't lobster gear, and two of the crew stopped coming into the Rusty Anchor after that, and one of them left town entirely, and Marisol wanted to know why a coast guard report filed on the incident read like it had been written by someone describing a different night entirely.

"I'm not asking you to investigate," she said. "I know how this town works. I grew up here." Her half-sister Junie had left for the city by then; Marisol stayed, stubborn as ballast. "I just want copies of things to exist somewhere that isn't the county system."

"Why me?" I asked. "There are lawyers in Rockland. Portland."

"Because you're careful," she said, "and because you're scared of the same people I am, and scared people keep good secrets." She smiled when she said it—not cruel, just honest, the way she was honest about everything, which was the whole problem with Marisol Vance. "Also because you alphabetize. I've seen your shelves through the window. A man who alphabetizes won't lose my papers."

I should have sent her away. I knew exactly what holding those documents meant—that I would become a node in whatever she was building, and that nodes in Greywater Cove either get absorbed or get cut. But she put the envelope on my desk, and inside were photographs of the jetty at low tide, a fuel invoice from a marina owned by a Wren cousin, and a page torn from a tide log with Elias Boone's initials in the margin, and something in my chest—the part of me that was eleven years old watching my father feed the shredder—said: *this time, keep all of it.*

"Probate and estate work only," I told her, taking the envelope anyway. "I'm not a criminal attorney."

"Nobody in this town is anything they say they are," Marisol said. "That's kind of the point."

* * *

Four months later, she was gone.

Her boat was found adrift off Gull Rock, engine cold, a cup of coffee in the cockpit holder going flat. No body. No mayday. The search lasted nine days and was called off on a Friday, and by Monday the story in the *Cove Courier* had already softened from DISAPPEARANCE to TRAGEDY, the way language does its own quiet housekeeping here.

I went to the memorial service at the Congregational Church. Half the town came, which surprised me until I understood that half the town needed to be seen grieving, needed the record to show they'd been there, needed their names in the guest book the way alibis need witnesses. Aldous Wren sat in the third pew. He wore a dark suit and an expression of grave benevolence, and afterward he shook my hand in the vestibule and held it one beat longer than the handshake required.

"A fine woman," he said. "Headstrong. The sea doesn't forgive headstrong." Then, quieter, with a smile that reached his eyes the way frost reaches a window: "You'll handle the estate matters properly, I trust. For the family's sake. Closure is a gift, Cyrus. One of the few things lawyers can actually give people."

I said yes, judge. I said of course. I shook his hand and felt the shape of the instruction inside the courtesy, the way you feel a hook inside bait.

* * *

The presumed-death petition came through my office six months later, standard procedure once the statutory period allowed it. Junie signed everything remotely, faxed pages back from wherever she'd landed—a city I never learned, a life she never described. She sounded young on the phone and far away in every sense. "Is there any chance," she asked me once, near the end of the last call, "that she just... started over somewhere? She used to talk about it. Sailing south until the charts ran out."

"There's always a chance," I said, because it was true and because it was kinder than the number I actually gave it, privately, in my head: four percent. Maybe five. People vanish on purpose all the time. But Marisol hadn't taken her diary bag, and Marisol hadn't touched the emergency cash taped inside the *Kindly Light*'s chart table, and Marisol Vance did not leave a cup of coffee unfinished unless something interrupted her mid-sip.

The declaration was granted. Judge Wren signed the order himself—he handled most of the county's probate calendar, efficient man—and I filed the certified copy in the cabinet, under V, between a trailer dispute and a boundary survey, and I closed the drawer, and I told myself the case was over.

It wasn't over. Nothing here is ever over. It's just filed.

* * *

Here is the part I've never said out loud, the part that has lived in me for seven years like a splinter working toward the surface.

Three weeks after the death declaration was entered, I pulled the original case materials—the documents Marisol had given me, plus the police file copies I'd obtained through discovery on an unrelated matter—to make a complete archive copy for storage. And the police file was wrong.

Not wrong the way files get wrong, with coffee rings and misfiled pages. Wrong the way a ledger is wrong when someone has been in it. The marine unit's incident report was there, but the appendices weren't—no tide tables for the night in question, no fuel purchase records, no witness canvass sheets. The index listed fourteen exhibits. The folder held nine. And the certification stamp on the cover sheet had been renewed—re-stamped, dated five weeks after the file was assembled—which meant the file had been reopened, altered, and recertified by someone with authority to do both.

I checked the renewal signature. I won't pretend I was surprised. Aldous Wren's signature sits on half the official paper in this county like a watermark. There are innocent explanations for a judge recertifying a file. There are perhaps two innocent explanations, and neither of them involves removing five exhibits from a drowning case in a town where the drowned woman had been asking questions about the judge's family business.

I stood in my office holding that thin folder for a long time. Outside, the harbor did its evening glitter, and the *Kindly Light* sat at its mooring with a for-sale sign nobody answered, and somewhere across the water Aldous Wren was probably pouring a drink and feeling satisfied with a job well done.

I could have reported it. I want to be honest about this, even if honesty costs me: I could have driven to the state police barracks in Rockland, forty minutes north, and handed them the discrepancy, and let the machinery grind. Maybe it would have gone somewhere. Maybe Wren's reach didn't extend past the county line. Maybe.

Or maybe it would have gone nowhere, and I would have spent the rest of my career as the lawyer who accused Aldous Wren of tampering and produced, in the end, a folder with a legitimate-looking recertification stamp and no provable crime. My father's shredder night flashed through my mind—not the guilt of it, but the arithmetic. He'd calculated the odds and fed the machine. I calculated mine and closed the drawer.

I made a photocopy set instead. Three copies, hidden in three places, including one in a bank box under a client name that would take a court order to open. Then I put the thinned file back in the cabinet, exactly as I'd found it, and I became, formally and permanently, the keeper of a record whose purpose was no longer memory but evidence-in-waiting.

That's what I told myself, anyway. Evidence-in-waiting implies a day when you intend to use it. Some nights, in the years after, I wondered if I'd simply found a more comfortable word for cowardice. Keeping the papers safe is not the same as keeping faith with the person they're about. Marisol had asked me to hold onto something. I'd held it. Whether I'd ever have had the spine to hand it forward—if she hadn't come back to ask for it herself—I genuinely did not know.

Seven years is a long time to not know a thing about yourself.

* * *

I became useful, in the way my father had been useful. Wren sent me complicated estates and delicate transactions, the ones needing discretion, and I performed them flawlessly, and the town relaxed around me the way a body relaxes around a well-fitted prosthesis. I was the man who could be trusted with the inconvenient document. Every town has one. Ours was me.

People brought me things without quite knowing why. Old Harriet Cole brought me her husband's logs from the seventies before she burned the rest of his effects—"you keep things, Cyrus, you'll know if they matter." The Delgados brought me a lease they'd been pressured to sign and asked me, in the vaguest possible terms, whether signing it was the kind of mistake that couldn't be undone. I told them the truth, which cost me Wren's referral stream for a year, and then Wren forgave me, which was worse, because forgiveness from Aldous Wren was a line item. Everything with him was a ledger entry. Every kindness accrued interest.

I told myself I was playing a long game. Custody of the record. Biding. All the respectable words for waiting.

Then Tuesday happened. A courier, a sealed envelope, a will dated eleven months after the sea supposedly took Marisol Vance—and the impossible document sitting on my desk like a door standing open in a wall I'd papered over.

I put the VANCE file back in the bottom drawer. I locked it. I looked at the will again, at the confident black ink of a date that could not exist, and I understood that the long game had just been played on me. Someone had known about my cabinet. Someone had known about my habits, my alphabetizing, my seven years of careful custody. The will hadn't been sent to any lawyer in Greywater Cove.

It had been sent to the keeper of the ledgers.

Which meant that somewhere out there, past the breakwater, past the seven quiet years, someone believed I still had the spine to open a door I'd locked behind myself—and they wanted me to walk through it.

The envelope's second page, tucked behind the will itself, held a single typed line:

*Executor to contact J. Vance upon acceptance. Further materials to follow.*

Further materials. I sat very still in my office while the dead fern dropped a brown frond onto the carpet, and I thought: *what else is coming?*

The answer arrived three days later, smelling of brine and wrapped in kelp, delivered not by courier but by the tide itself.
