## 6. The Man Who Files Better Than Police

The county annex closed at four on Fridays, and Cyrus Nakamura had spent eleven years learning what that meant: that Greywater Cove's official memory had business hours, and everything that mattered happened after them.

He locked his office door at six, poured the last of the cold coffee rather than make fresh, and cleared his desk the way a surgeon clears a field. One legal pad. Two pens, because one always died at the wrong moment. And the box he kept in the bottom drawer, beneath the tax files nobody would ever steal—the box he'd told himself for seven years was just thoroughness.

It was not thoroughness. He'd known that for a while now. Thoroughness doesn't make your hands unsteady when you lift the lid.

Inside: his private copies of the Vance file. Not photocopies made from the official record—that would have been discoverable, and Cyrus had survived this town by never leaving fingerprints on anything that mattered. These were his reconstructions. Notes taken from memory within hours of each filing, cross-referenced against the documents he'd been allowed to see, flagged where the file had felt thin. He'd started the habit his second year in practice, after old Judge Merrick had asked him, mildly, to "misfile" a survey dispute that would have embarrassed the harborside development interests. Cyrus had misfiled it. And then, disgusted with himself in a way he hadn't yet learned to manage, he'd gone home and written down exactly what he'd been asked to hide, and where he'd hidden it.

It had become a discipline. A private ledger of the town's convenient gaps. He'd told himself it was insurance. He'd told himself a lot of things.

He flattened the first page and began.

* * *

Reconstructing a file from memory is like rebuilding a drowned boat from the shape of the hole it left in the mud. You work backward from absence. You ask not *what is here* but *what would have to be here, in any competent investigation, and isn't*.

The Vance presumed-death order. Cyrus had drafted the petition himself in the spring, seven years back—eleven months after Marisol Vance's boat was found drifting, engine off, fuel cap loose, no sign of struggle. He'd been retained by the estate's nominal administrator, a bank trust officer in Rockland who had never met Marisol and never would. Standard procedure. Declaration of death in absentia, so the modest assets could move: the house on Tide Row, the boat itself, the small accounts. Junie Vance had been notified by certified letter to an address in Ohio. The letter came back unclaimed. Cyrus had noted that at the time—*no family contact*—and filed it under the numb category of *sad, not suspicious*.

He wrote the date of the declaration on the legal pad. Then he wrote the name of the judge who signed it.

Aldous Wren.

Cyrus sat with that for a moment. It wasn't a revelation. Wren signed half the orders in this county; he'd been on the bench for thirty years, and before that he'd been whatever a man becomes in a harbor town when his family has owned half the waterfront since before the Coast Guard had a station here. Wren had signed the order the way he signed everything—efficiently, without ceremony, his fountain pen leaving that distinctive heavy stroke Cyrus had seen on a thousand documents.

But here was the thing Cyrus had never let himself think clearly: the Vance petition had not been assigned to Wren. Cyrus remembered the assignment notice. It had gone to Judge Prentiss, the rotating out-of-county judge who handled the overflow docket twice a month. And then, three days before the hearing, the notice had been amended. *Reassigned for docket management.* No explanation. Cyrus had been annoyed at the time—Prentiss was faster, and he'd had a closing scheduled against the hearing date—and then he'd been grateful, because Wren moved things along, and Cyrus had a closing.

He had been grateful to the man who signed a woman out of existence because it fit his calendar.

Cyrus wrote: *Who requests reassignment?* And under it: *Find out.*

* * *

The boat. He reconstructed the marine side from what he'd read in the file and what he'd heard, because the file's marine section had been the thinnest part of all, and he'd noticed that too, seven years ago, and done nothing about it, because noticing things and acting on them were, in Greywater Cove, entirely separate professions.

A found vessel should generate: a boarding report, a condition survey, photographs, a fuel system inspection, and a trace of the owner's last known movements. The Vance file contained a two-paragraph incident summary from the harbormaster's office—*vessel observed adrift, secured, towed to mooring*—and nothing else. No photographs. No condition survey. The incident summary wasn't even on the standard form. It was a memo, typed on plain paper, unsigned in the signature block. The name typed beneath the blank space was the deputy harbormaster, a man named Coyle who had, Cyrus now recalled with the slow cold turn of a combination lock finding its number, retired six weeks later and moved to Florida.

He wrote: *Coyle. Still alive? Still in Florida?*

And beneath that, because the thought arrived with the weight of something that had been waiting a long time to be thought: *Who tells a deputy harbormaster to skip the form?*

The answer, in a town like this, was never complicated. There was one voice that carried from the bench to the docks without ever seeming to raise itself. Wren didn't give orders. Wren had opinions, and men around him had careers, and the space between those two facts was where Greywater Cove did its real governing. Cyrus had watched it work for years the way you watch weather—peripherally, with a professional's appreciation, and with the settled understanding that his job was to be indoors when it broke.

He turned the page of his reconstruction and found his own handwriting from seven years ago, smaller than the rest, cramped into the margin: *D. file lighter than intake list. Count again.*

He'd counted again at the time. He'd counted three times. The intake log—the master index every filing generates, the one document too boring to lie about—listed eleven items for the Vance matter. The physical file had contained seven.

Four pages. Gone. And Cyrus had noted the discrepancy, and then he had closed the drawer, because the alternative to closing the drawer was asking who could reach into a county records annex and remove pages from a file, and he had not wanted to live in the world where he knew the answer.

He was going to live in that world now. He picked up the pen.

* * *

Item one of the missing four: the certified letter to Junie Vance. The intake log listed it. The file had contained only the unclaimed-return receipt, not the letter copy itself. At the time, Cyrus had assumed sloth. But a letter copy is generated automatically; removing it requires a decision. And here was the detail that had never fit, the splinter he'd worried at for years without drawing blood: the return receipt was stamped *unclaimed* on a date four days after mailing. Ohio is not four days from Maine by mail in the modern era. The receipt had been back-dated, or the mailing had never happened, or both. Which meant someone had wanted the record to show the family was notified and indifferent. Someone had wanted Junie Vance to look like she didn't care.

He wrote Junie's name and underlined it. The woman who had shown up at his office with a diary fished out of the tide, furious and grieving and sharper than anyone in this town had given her credit for. Someone had spent seven years making sure she'd never come looking. And the first thing she'd done on arriving was come looking.

Item two: the log of the last known sighting. The intake list referenced a witness statement from the night Marisol disappeared—*W., A., re: marina, 10:40 p.m.* Cyrus had never seen the statement itself. He'd assumed it was misfiled. W., A. Aldous Wren had given a witness statement the night Marisol Vance vanished, and it was one of the four pages someone had removed from the file.

Cyrus set the pen down. His hand was steady, which surprised him. He'd expected fear. What he felt instead was colder and more useful: the particular clarity of a man who has finally stopped pretending the building is sound. He'd spent his career around documents. He knew what documents did. They didn't forget, they didn't forgive, and they didn't disappear on their own. A missing page was not an absence. It was a presence, moving through the file like a hand through water, and it left a shape.

Four pages, one hand. A reassigned hearing, one hand. A memo instead of a marine survey, one hand. A letter never sent, made to look like a sister's indifference, one hand.

And above all of it, presiding, the fountain pen with the heavy stroke: *declared dead, A. Wren, J.*

The man had not just buried the investigation. He had signed the burial certificate himself. He had put his own name on the document that made Marisol Vance legally nonexistent, which meant he had never feared the file. He had feared nothing, because he owned the machinery that produced the file. The removal of pages wasn't concealment. It was housekeeping.

Cyrus thought about the diary in his safe, its entries continuing past the date of the disappearance, that fluent, faltering, wrong voice. *The lawyer who alphabetizes my absence.* He'd read that line four times now, and each time it had landed a little differently, and this time it landed as a message from the forger directly to him—or rather, a message the forger had expected someone like him to eventually read, written by someone who knew exactly how Cyrus's mind worked, because everyone in this town knew how Cyrus's mind worked. That was what he'd been for. The keeper of inconvenient documents. Useful precisely because he was careful, and careful precisely because he was afraid, and afraid precisely because he'd understood, earlier than most, who the building belonged to.

He poured the cold coffee and didn't drink it.

* * *

He worked until midnight. The reconstruction grew from three pages to nine. He built a timeline on the back of the intake log copy, and the timeline had a shape he didn't like, because the shape was a funnel, and everything funneled toward one name.

Seven years ago: Marisol Vance confronts someone about something—Cyrus didn't yet know what, though Elias Boone's face had been in his mind since their conversation, that old man's guilt worn like weather. Her boat is found adrift. The marine file is replaced by a memo. A witness statement from A. Wren is taken and then vanishes. Four pages leave a county file that no one without authority could touch. The hearing is reassigned to Wren. The death order issues in nine days—a speed, Cyrus noted, that he had once admired as efficiency. The estate settles quietly. The house on Tide Row is sold to a buyer Cyrus had looked up once, idly, and found to be a holding company, and had not looked up twice, because the holding company's registered agent was a law firm in Rockland, and the law firm did occasional work for Wren family interests, and Cyrus had understood the shape of that sentence and put it down.

The house. Marisol's house, the one Junie was moving into, the one with the freshly oiled hinges and the moved kettle. It hadn't scattered into the estate market. It had been collected. Someone had wanted it, and had used the death declaration to get it, and had kept it for seven years the way you keep a thing you might need again—or a thing you're not finished with.

And now the will had surfaced. A will dated after the death declaration, impossible on its face, naming Cyrus, leaving everything to Junie—everything, which would include, if it stood, the house on Tide Row. Which would put the house back in Vance hands for the first time in seven years.

He wrote at the bottom of the timeline, in capitals, because his hand wanted to shake and capitals kept it honest:

*WHO BENEFITS FROM MARISOL VANCE STAYING MISSING?*

And under it, the answer he'd been circling for eleven years of careful, useful, complicit silence:

*THE MAN WHO SIGNED THE PAPER SAYING SHE WAS.*

* * *

He was still sitting there when the phone buzzed. Junie. He almost didn't answer at this hour, then answered, because the old Cyrus screened his calls and the old Cyrus had filed a woman's life under *sad, not suspicious.*

"I couldn't sleep," she said. No preamble. She'd been in town four days and already talked like the harbor—direct, salted. "I was going through Marisol's things. The real things, in the attic, not the staged stuff downstairs. And I found a photograph."

"Of what?"

"Of a clambake. Town thing, years ago. Everyone's in it." A pause, and he heard her decide to say the next part plainly. "Judge Wren's in it. Standing on the dock at the marina. Cyrus, it's dated—the date's written on the back in Marisol's handwriting. It's the night she disappeared. The clambake was the night she disappeared. He told the county he was inland at a dinner in Camden that night. That's in the file, isn't it? That's the alibi?"

Cyrus looked down at his reconstruction, at the line he'd written an hour ago: *W., A., re: marina, 10:40 p.m.* The statement that had been taken and then removed. The statement that had placed Wren at the marina the same night he'd sworn he was two towns away—and someone had made it disappear, and then made the whole file lighter, and then signed the order that ended the case before the contradiction could ever be noticed.

"It was," Cyrus said. "It's not anymore. But I know it existed, and I know who took it, and Junie—" He stopped. He heard his own voice do something it hadn't done in eleven years: commit. "I know where the original intake log is. The master index. It's the one record in that annex nobody thinks to steal because nobody reads it. And tomorrow morning, before the annex opens, I'm going to read it. Because if I'm right, it doesn't just list what was removed. It lists who signed the removal slip."

The line was quiet. Then Junie said, "You're telling me the man who declared my sister dead took her out of the file himself."

"I'm telling you," Cyrus said, "that he filed better than the police did. And a file is a confession, if you know how to read the gaps."

Outside his window, the cove lay black and patient, holding everything it had ever been given. The spring tide was three weeks out. And somewhere across the water, in a house with a locked boathouse, an old man with a heavy fountain pen was sleeping the sleep of a man who believed his filing was perfect.

He was almost right. He'd missed one page. The one in Cyrus's bottom drawer.
