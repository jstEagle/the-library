## 9. A Visitor in the Night

The alarm on the back door of my office had been broken for three years. I knew this because I was the one who'd never called anyone to fix it. A lawyer in a town like Greywater Cove learns quickly that an alarm is a promise you make to yourself, and I had stopped making promises a long time ago. Invisibility didn't require security. Invisibility required nothing worth stealing.

I stood in the doorway at 6:40 in the morning, coffee still in hand from the drive in, and understood that something had changed.

Not the glass—none of it broken. Not the lock—the deadbolt sat in its frame like it had never been disturbed. But the air inside was wrong. Offices have a smell when they're closed up overnight: paper dust, toner, the faint vinegar of old binding glue. This air had been moved through. Breathed in. Someone had spent time in here after I left, and they had been careful, and careful people leave a different kind of mess than careless ones.

I set the coffee down on the windowsill without drinking it and walked the room the way I'd once walked crime scenes as a very young associate, back when I still believed documents could protect people.

My filing cabinets were open. Not ransacked—*read*. Every drawer closed again afterward, every hanging folder pushed back flush against its guide, but the order inside them was off by degrees only I would notice. The Henderson estate file sat a quarter inch forward. The Kowalski deed packet had been re-stapled with the wrong staple—a bright galvanized one where my dull brass should be. Whoever had gone through my office hadn't just searched it. They had *audited* it.

And then there was the desk.

The diary copy was gone.

I keep copies of everything—that's not paranoia, that's probate practice—but the photocopy of Marisol Vance's diary, the one Junie and I had worked through page by page two nights ago, had lived in the second drawer under a false folder labeled *MUNICIPAL WATER RIGHTS, 1998–2004*, because nobody in the history of Greywater Cove has ever wanted to steal water rights litigation from 2004. The folder was there. The copy wasn't.

In its place, weighted down by my own letter opener, was a single sheet of cream stationery.

I didn't touch it at first. I stood over it the way you stand over a snake you've found in your boot, and I read it three times before my hands would agree to pick it up.

The handwriting was Marisol's.

I want to be precise about what that sentence did to me, because precision is the only tool I've ever trusted. It was not handwriting that resembled hers. It was not a good imitation viewed from across a room. It was her hand—the slight forward lean of it, the way she crossed her t's with a flat, impatient stroke, the little hitch in the descender of her g's that I had studied for hours two nights ago while Junie drank cold tea and said, "That's her, that's really her." I had held pages written by a living woman and then by whatever came after, and now here was that same hand telling me:

*Stop reading what isn't yours. Some estates settle better unopened. — M.*

No signature flourish beyond the initial. No threat spelled out. Just the voice of a drowned woman, seven years dead, leaving me a note on my own desk in a room with an intact lock.

I sat down slowly in my chair, which someone else had adjusted—two inches higher than I leave it—and let the fear arrive properly. I think it's important to let fear arrive properly. Most people spend their lives outrunning it or dressing it up as anger or caution or professionalism. I had spent seven years doing exactly that, filing away the town's ugly truths in neat alphabetical drawers, telling myself that survival was a neutral act. Sitting there with a dead woman's handwriting warming in my fingers, I finally felt the full invoice come due.

Someone had been in my office. Someone had read my files well enough to know which one mattered. Someone had taken the copy and left the original will—not out of mercy, but arithmetic. The will had been filed with the county, entered into the record, witnessed by a courier service and a notary whose name was now attached to it in three databases. Stealing it would announce the theft to the world. Taking the diary copy, though—the copy existed nowhere official. Take the copy, leave a note, and the message lands clean: we know what you have, we know how you work, and we can reach the inside of your locked office without breaking anything you'd notice.

That last part was the part that frightened me most, so I made myself examine it first. My office sits above the old chandlery on Harbor Street. One exterior door, one stair shared with the building's rear entrance, windows facing the water that haven't opened since I replaced the sash cords in 2019. To enter without forcing anything, you needed either a key or the patience to pick a lock quietly, and either way you needed to know my habits—when I leave, when the street empties, which nights I don't come back after dinner. This wasn't a burglar. Burglars take electronics. This was someone who had studied me the way I had studied Marisol's case file: closely, patiently, with ownership.

With, I realized as the thought finished forming, the same intimacy the forged diary entries displayed.

I laid the note beside my legal pad and forced myself into analysis, because analysis was the rope I could climb out of the pit with. The forged entries in the diary weren't just fluent—they were *informed*. They referenced the harbor master's boat schedule. They referenced the interior of the Vance house. They referenced, in that line Junie couldn't stop repeating, "the lawyer who alphabetizes my absence"—a phrase that required knowing not just that I existed but *how* I worked, that I was the town's quiet repository, that I filed things other people buried. And now this note. *Some estates settle better unopened.* That was probate language. That was the vocabulary of a man—or woman—who had sat through enough estate closings to know exactly which phrase would land in a lawyer's chest like a hook.

There were perhaps four people in Greywater Cove who spoke that language fluently. Three of them were lawyers, retired or otherwise harmless. The fourth signed Marisol's death declaration.

I didn't write the name down. I've learned that much about myself: I don't commit the dangerous conclusions to paper anymore. But the name sat in my head with the weight of a verdict awaiting evidence, and beneath it, colder, sat a second thought I liked even less.

*They know the case file better than the investigators did.*

Because that was the thing, wasn't it? The original investigation into Marisol Vance's disappearance had been thin—thinner than thin, a coat of paint over a hole. Two deputies, three days, a presumption of voluntary departure that nobody questioned because nobody in Greywater Cove questions the Wren family's version of any event. The investigators hadn't known enough to forge convincing diary entries. They hadn't known about Elias Boone pulling her boat from the water. They hadn't known about the shipment, the confrontation, any of it. Whoever wrote those entries knew things the police never learned—which meant whoever wrote them wasn't reconstructing from the public record. They were working from memory. From presence. From having been *there*.

And they had just demonstrated they could walk into my office, read my entire filing system, identify a decoy folder, extract a specific document, and leave behind a forgery of a dead woman's handwriting—all without disturbing a lock.

Fear, properly arrived, does something strange if you let it finish its work. It stops being a wall and becomes a lens. Sitting in my too-high chair, I found that the fear had burned off the fog I'd been living in for seven years, and what remained was embarrassingly simple.

They took the copy because the copy was the only thing they could take. Which meant the original diary—the real one, in Junie's possession—was the thing they couldn't reach, or hadn't found, or believed was somewhere they couldn't get to without exposure. And they left the will because the will was bait-proof, record-anchored, untouchable. Which meant they were operating under constraint. Careful people are constrained people. Constrained people make mistakes when you change the board.

I got up, retrieved my coffee from the sill—it had gone cold, and I drank it anyway—and began to put my office back in order, drawer by drawer, folder by folder, restoring my own small alphabet the way you'd straighten a picture frame after an earthquake. It steadied my hands. It also gave me time to decide what I was going to do, and by the time the Henderson file slid home into its slot, I had decided.

First: Junie. She needed to know the diary she was keeping at Marisol's house was now, officially, the most valuable object in Greywater Cove, and that the person who valued it had already proven they could go anywhere. She needed to move it, or move herself, or both. She needed to hear it from me before she heard it from a broken window.

Second: Elias. If someone was willing to burgle a lawyer's office over a photocopied diary, then the harbor master who had pulled Marisol's boat from the water and kept his mouth shut for seven years was no longer sitting on a secret—he was sitting on evidence, and evidence was becoming a hazardous material in this town.

Third—and this was the one that surprised me—I needed to stop being invisible on purpose. For seven years my strategy had been to be so useful and so quiet that the powerful people of Greywater Cove forgot I was a man with a conscience and remembered only that I was a man with a filing system. The note on my desk ended that arrangement. You cannot be ignored by someone who adjusts your chair. The moment they touched my office, they converted me from furniture into a participant, and participants get to choose sides.

I chose.

But I'm getting ahead of myself, and the morning had one more thing to teach me before I could act on any of it. Because as I was locking up—actually locking up, testing the handle twice, a habit I resolved to acquire retroactively—Junie came up the stairs two at a time with her jacket half-on and her face drained white, and she thrust her phone at me before she said good morning.

"I went to the house," she said. "Marisol's house. To get the diary, bring it here, after what we talked about. And Cyrus—" Her breath caught. "The kettle was on the stove. Filled. Not boiled yet, but filled, and the burner knob turned to the right position, like somebody set it and got interrupted. And there's a cup out. One cup."

She swallowed.

"It's the cup from the photo. The one on the mantel, the one Marisol's holding in the picture from the harbor festival. Somebody's been drinking tea out of my sister's cup in my sister's kitchen."

I looked at the phone, at the photograph of the cheerful yellow kettle sitting on a cold burner, and I felt the last of my invisibility burn off like morning fog under a hard sun. They hadn't just been in my office. They'd been in her house, recently, comfortably, the way you visit a place that belongs to you.

"Junie," I said, and heard my own voice come out level, which surprised me, "pack a bag. You're not staying there tonight. And then I need you to tell me everything you know about Judge Aldous Wren's boathouse—because I think we've been reading the diary wrong. We've been asking who wrote it." I locked the office door behind us and pocketed the key. "The better question is who taught him to write it."

* * *

We drove out to Elias Boone's houseboat with the diary itself locked in the trunk under a blanket, because I had decided that if the original was going to travel, it would travel with all three of us in daylight, in public, where a careful man's constraints would bind him tightest. Junie rode shotgun holding the keys like a talisman. I drove past the Wren estate road without slowing, the way you pass a dog you know is chained—until you're no longer sure about the chain.

Elias was mending a net on his deck when we pulled alongside, and he read our faces before we said a word. Seventy years of reading weather will do that to a man.

"Somebody been in your office," he said. Not a question.

"They took the copy. Left a note in her handwriting." I watched his hands stop moving on the net. "Elias, the handwriting was perfect. Better than perfect. It was rehearsed."

He looked out at the cove for a long moment, at the gray water that had taken Marisol Vance and given back, seven years late, a diary and a will and a question. When he spoke, his voice had the flat calm of a man who has finally stopped hoping the storm will miss him.

"My grandfather ran rum for Aldous Wren's father," Elias said. "You know what the first thing he ever taught me was?" He folded the net down over his knees. "How to sign another man's name. Said in this cove, the pen drowns more people than the water does."

The tide was coming in. Somewhere under it, in the dark below the wreck of her own boat, the truth was waiting with more patience than any of us.

It was time to stop letting the patient one win.
