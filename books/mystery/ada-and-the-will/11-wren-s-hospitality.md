## 11. Wren's Hospitality

The invitation came on cream cardstock, the kind of paper that costs more per sheet than Junie used to spend on a week of groceries in Phoenix. *Aldous Wren requests the pleasure of your company for dinner, Friday next, seven o'clock.* Below it, in a different hand—smaller, fussier—someone had added: *He is an old friend of your sister's. He knew her well.*

Junie read that line four times.

*He knew her well.*

Nobody in Greywater Cove had known Marisol well. That was the whole point of Marisol—she'd kept herself like a locked drawer, and everyone in town had learned to stop trying the handle years before she disappeared. If Aldous Wren claimed intimacy with her sister, he was either lying or he was one of the people Marisol had kept secrets *from*, which amounted to the same thing with better manners.

She took the card to Cyrus's office that afternoon, setting it on his desk between a stapler and a stack of probate files he'd been pretending not to reorganize all week.

"He wants to feed me," she said.

Cyrus picked up the card the way he picked up everything—two fingers, like it might be evidence. Which, Junie was learning, everything in this town eventually was. "You don't have to go."

"I know I don't have to go." She sat down across from him without being asked. She'd stopped asking permission to exist in his office somewhere around the second week. "I'm going."

"Junie—"

"You said it yourself. The diary mentions him. The file has his fingerprints all over it—he signed her death declaration, he thinned the case records, whatever Elias told you about the night at the harbor. And now he invites me to dinner." She leaned forward. "Men like this don't invite you to dinner because they're lonely, Cyrus. They invite you because they want to look inside you and see how much you know."

Cyrus set the card down. For a moment he looked older than his years, the way he did when the lawyer fell away and something wearier showed through. "And what do you want him to see?"

Junie smiled. It wasn't a nice smile; she could feel that from the inside. "A grateful heir. A grieving little sister who's overwhelmed and out of her depth and so thankful that someone respectable is finally taking care of things." She shrugged. "I've been underestimated my whole life. Might as well make it useful."

Something shifted in Cyrus's face—not approval exactly, but recognition. Like he was watching someone pick up a tool he'd been carrying alone for too long.

"If you're going," he said slowly, "then we do this properly. You're not walking into that house blind."

* * *

They spent three evenings preparing, and Junie privately thought of them as rehearsals for a play only two people would ever see performed.

Cyrus spread what he knew across his desk like a surgeon laying out instruments: the timeline of Marisol's disappearance, the gaps in the police file, the phrase from the will—*the tide giving back what it took*—and everything Elias Boone had told them under the low yellow light of his harbor office. Junie memorized names, dates, boat registrations. She learned which shipments had moved through Greywater Cove in the smuggling decades, and which families had gotten comfortable, and whose comfort had depended on nobody asking questions.

But mostly, they rehearsed her.

"Tell me about your sister," Cyrus said, playing Wren, playing the sympathetic patriarch, hands folded, voice warm as banked coals. "You must have so many questions after all these years."

"I was twelve when she left home for good," Junie recited. "We weren't close. Different mothers, same father, and our father collected grudges the way other men collect stamps. I came back because she left me a house and a name, and I wanted—" she paused, caught herself performing even for Cyrus, then let the performance deepen deliberately, softened her voice, wet her eyes on command the way she'd learned to in a childhood full of adults who needed managing— "I wanted to understand her. That's all. I just want to understand her."

Cyrus watched her for a long moment. "That was very good," he said quietly. "It made me want to comfort you. Which means it would make him want to underestimate you." He hesitated. "It also made me feel like a fraud for sitting here coaching you through it."

"We're both frauds this week," Junie said. "At least we're frauds together."

He almost smiled. It was the first time she'd seen anything close to a real smile on him, and it changed his whole face—the careful, alphabetized face cracking open just enough to show the person who'd built all those filing systems to hide behind. She found, unexpectedly, that she liked that person. That she trusted him, which was a thing she hadn't felt toward anyone since Marisol's memorial service, when half the town had shown up to perform grief for a woman they'd spent seven years calling reckless.

On the last night of rehearsal, Cyrus walked her to her car in the rain and stood there getting soaked because he was constitutionally incapable of ending a conversation first.

"One rule," he said. "Whatever happens in that house—you're a guest, not an investigator. You look. You listen. You eat the food and admire the view and leave by ten. You don't touch anything, you don't open anything, and if he offers you anything to sign, you tell him your lawyer will review it." A beat. "Your lawyer being me."

"And if he offers me money?"

"Then be flattered and vague. Money offered at a first dinner isn't a gift, Junie. It's a price tag. He's checking whether you have one."

She drove home through the wet streets of Greywater Cove, past the shuttered cannery and the dark windows of the chandlery, and thought about price tags. About how the whole town ran on them—every silence bought, every question priced out of reach. Marisol had refused to be priced. Junie was beginning to understand that this was the family inheritance, the only thing her sister had actually left her.

* * *

The Wren estate sat on the headland above the cove like a sentence written in stone: *this belongs to me, and so does the view, and so does everything the view contains.* Junie's cab crunched up a gravel drive lined with wind-bent pines, and there was the house itself—gray shingle, white trim, three stories of old money weathered into respectability—and below it, past terraced gardens gone slightly wild, the boathouse.

She catalogued it before she was even out of the cab. That was the trick she'd decided on: treat the evening like inventory. Every room, every object, every pause in conversation went onto a mental shelf, labeled and dated. Grief was useless in that house. Fear was worse. But a woman taking stock—that was a woman nobody worried about.

Wren met her at the door himself. Up close he was smaller than his reputation, a compact man in his seventies with a sailor's weathered face and eyes the pale gray of the harbor in February. His handshake was dry and precise.

"Miss Vance." He said the surname like he was tasting it. "You favor her, you know. Around the eyes."

"Do I?" Junie let her voice wobble just slightly. "Everyone says I look like our father. I never know what to do with that."

"Come in. Let me feed you. Grief is easier on a full stomach—my wife used to say that." A flicker crossed his face at the mention of the late Mrs. Wren, gone or genuine, impossible to say. "God rest her."

The house smelled of beeswax and woodsmoke and, underneath it, faintly, of brine—as if the sea had gotten into the bones of the place and never left. A housekeeper showed Junie to a sitting room where a fire burned against the fog rolling in off the water, and Wren poured sherry from a decanter older than the state.

"To Marisol," he said, raising his glass.

"To Marisol," Junie echoed, and drank, and hated the taste, and smiled.

Dinner was sole and new potatoes and a wine Wren clearly expected to impress her, served in a dining room hung with paintings of ships. He talked, and Junie performed, and underneath the performance she worked.

He asked about her life in Arizona—she gave him Phoenix, waitressing, community college, a fiancé who hadn't survived the move east, all true enough to hold weight. He asked about the house on Harbor Lane—was she settling in, did she find it cold, the damp got into everything in winter. She said she loved it, that it felt like coming home to a sister she'd barely known, and watched his face for the reaction.

There was one. Small. A stillness around his mouth, like a man hearing a floorboard creak in a house he believed empty.

"You were close, then?" he asked. "You and Marisol?"

"Not close enough." Junie looked down at her plate, arranged her grief like a centerpiece. "That's why I came back. I keep thinking—if I sit in her kitchen long enough, maybe I'll finally hear what she was trying to tell me."

Silence, two seconds long. Then Wren laughed, warm and easy, and refilled her glass. "The dead are poor conversationalists. Take it from a man who's buried most of the people he ever loved." He lifted his wine. "This town remembers things too long, Miss Vance. It's a harbor habit. Harbors collect wreckage. Best thing a person can do is let the tide take what it takes."

*The tide giving back what it took.* Junie kept her face soft and grateful while something cold walked down her spine. He didn't know the will's phrasing—or he knew it too well. Either way, he'd just handed her a thread, and she wound it carefully around her mental shelf and tied it off.

* * *

After dinner he gave her the tour, and she understood it was a test as much as a courtesy: *look at what I own, little heir, and understand the difference in our weights.*

The library first—walls of books, a globe on a stand, a desk with nothing on it, which was its own kind of statement. Then the long gallery hallway, hung with photographs of the cove across a century: the fleet at anchor in 1954, the cannery at full roar, regattas, christenings, storms. Wren narrated each one with the ease of a man who had been the main character in his town's memory for sixty years.

Junie stopped walking so fast she nearly tripped over her own performance.

The photograph hung at eye level, in a heavy dark frame, third from the corner. Black-and-white, grainy, taken from the headland looking down at the harbor at dusk. The date penciled on the matting in faded ink: *October 12th* — seven years ago. The night Marisol vanished.

In the foreground, standing on the estate's own terrace steps, a small group raised glasses to the camera. Town faces she was learning to recognize—Elias Boone younger and heavier, the harbormaster's cap pushed back; a woman she thought might be the former postmistress; two men whose names she couldn't yet attach. And at the center, arm slung around a neighbor's shoulder, grinning like a man with nothing on his conscience and everything in his keeping:

Aldous Wren. Dry. In shirtsleeves. A drink in his hand. Sunset light on his face.

She knew the date the way she now knew her own heartbeat. She'd read the coast guard report eleven times. Wren had told investigators he'd been inland that day—at a land-use conference in Rockland, three hours north, unreachable, uninvolved. His testimony had anchored the timeline that ruled out foul play near the water. His testimony had closed the file.

And here he was, immortalized in silver gelatin, toasting the camera on his terrace while his town's harbor went dark and one of its boats went out and never came home.

"Ah." Wren had come up beside her. She hadn't heard him cross the hall, and that frightened her more than the photograph did. "The last good season. We were celebrating the harbor trust settlement—forty years of fighting, finally done." He studied the image with something she might have mistaken for nostalgia if she hadn't already started counting his lies. "Marisol would have been out that night, I expect. She loved the water best at dusk. Reckless girl."

"Was she?" Junie asked lightly. "Reckless?"

"She didn't know when to leave things alone." He said it fondly. Fondly, and flatly, both at once, and Junie felt the words settle into her inventory like a knife laid on a shelf. "It's a family trait, I suspect. Vance women." He turned from the photograph and smiled at her, and his eyes were the color of deep water over a wreck. "Don't inherit it."

* * *

He saved the boathouse for last, or she maneuvered him toward it—by then she honestly couldn't have said which, and that scared her a little too, how easily the performance and the person were braiding together.

It stood apart from the house, down a flagstone path slick with fog, a long low building with a peaked roof and doors tall enough for a fishing skiff. Newer than the house. Better maintained. The path lights along it were the only ones on the property that worked.

"That's the original Wren wharf foundation underneath," Wren said, keys already out. "My grandfather built the first version. This one's mine." He unlocked the near door and swung it wide, and Junie stepped into a cathedral of varnished wood and rope and engine oil.

Inside: a restored sloop on a cradle, gleaming. Workbenches, tools ranked by size. Crab traps stacked to the rafters. Everything orderly, everything cared for, everything *used*—except.

Except the far end. Past the sloop, past a hanging tarp, a second set of doors. Larger. Steel-cored, from the look of them, though painted to match the wood. And locked—she saw the hasp, the padlock, fresh brass, no salt corrosion, no rust. Locked in a building where the owner kept his boats behind a door he'd opened for a stranger within thirty seconds of meeting her.

"What's back there?" she asked, all innocent wonder, drifting toward it.

"Storage." Too fast. Half a beat too fast, and smooth again immediately. "Old gear. Nets. Things I keep meaning to throw out." He laughed. "Every harbor man's boathouse is half museum, half landfill. Come—look at the sloop. My father's design. I rebuilt her myself, took six years."

He talked about the sloop for a long time, and Junie admired it properly, running her fingers along mahogany rails, asking the right dumb-landlubber questions. But some part of her stayed pinned to those steel doors the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. Behind them: space enough for a skiff. Space enough for gear. Space enough, her mind whispered treacherously, for anything a man might need to keep off the water and out of sight until the right tide.

She thought of the diary's forged entries, patient as sediment, year after year, steering any future reader away from the harbor. She thought of *the tide giving back what it took*. She thought of a man inland at a conference who stood dry on his terrace at sunset, drinking to forty years of winning.

"Miss Vance?" Wren was watching her. "You went somewhere."

"Just tired," she said, and let her eyes shine, and let him believe it. "It's been a strange autumn. Finding out I had a sister's whole life to catch up on."

He walked her out personally, down the flagstone path, fog beading on his silver hair. At the top of the drive he took her hand in both of his—dry, strong, precise—and held it a moment longer than kindness required.

"You'll come again," he said. Not a question. "Greywater Cove takes care of its own, Junie. May I call you Junie? Good. The town will seem harsh to you at first—it's the weather, mostly—but under it, everyone here wants the same thing." He released her hand. "Peace. Quiet water. Nothing stirred up that's better left on the bottom."

"Of course," Junie said warmly. "I want that too."

She smiled at him from the cab window all the way down the drive, and held the smile until the pines swallowed the house, and then she let it drop and pressed her shaking hands flat against her knees and made herself breathe.

* * *

Cyrus answered on the first ring, though it was nearly midnight and he was a man who presumably slept.

"It's me. Are you alone?"

"Yes. What happened?"

"He was there." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "The night she disappeared. There's a photo in his gallery—dated October twelfth, him on his terrace, dry, toasting the camera. He told the coast guard he was in Rockland. Cyrus, he *lied in the official record*, and the record bent around him and closed."

A silence on the line, but a working silence; she could practically hear him reaching for a pen. "Describe the photo exactly. Everyone in it."

She did—Elias, the postmistress, the two men she couldn't name, the angle, the light, the pencil date on the matting. Then the boathouse: the steel doors, the new brass lock, the half-beat stall when she asked. Then the phrase about leaving things on the bottom, and the way he'd said *reckless girl* like a verdict he'd reached a long time ago and never needed to revisit.

When she finished, Cyrus was quiet for a long moment.

"You got all that out of one dinner," he said finally, and there was something in his voice she hadn't heard before—not the careful archivist, not the invisible man. Something warmer and more alarmed at once. "Junie, most witnesses give me that in three depositions and a lie detector."

"Well." She stared out the cab window at the fog swallowing Harbor Lane, at her sister's dark house waiting for her at the end of it. "Marisol always said the Vance women didn't know when to leave things alone. Turns out it's genetic." She paused. "Cyrus. Those steel doors. I need you to find out what a man like Wren would keep behind a lock like that."

Another silence. When he spoke again, his voice had dropped into a register she'd only heard once before—in his office, the day he'd traced *the tide giving back what it took* to Elias Boone and gone pale.

"I think I already know," he said. "And tomorrow morning, I'm going to show you why we may not have much time. There's something in the probate archive I should have looked at seven years ago—something the forged entries have been pointing me toward all along. Get some sleep if you can. Tomorrow we go digging."

The line went dead. Junie sat in the parked cab outside Marisol's house, fog crawling up the windows like the tide coming for the town itself, and realized her hands had finally stopped shaking.

Not because she was calm. Because she was done being afraid of the answer.
