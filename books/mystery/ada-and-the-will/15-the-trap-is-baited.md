## 15. The Trap Is Baited

The fire at the annex changed the arithmetic.

Cyrus had spent seven years doing math the town could live with—rounding down, carrying the one into a drawer, letting remainders vanish. Arson was not a remainder. Arson was someone standing in front of him with a lit match and saying *I know what you have, and I will burn anything to keep you from using it.*

So he stopped rounding.

He sat in his office at four in the morning, three days after the fire, with the blinds drawn and a single desk lamp burning, and he did what he had done his whole career: he drafted a document. Only this time, every word of it would be a lie.

He'd thought about it for two days before he let himself start. There were rules for this kind of thing—he had spent twenty years enforcing them on other people—and every one of them said no. You don't fabricate probate instruments. You don't forge the voice of the dead. You don't use the machinery of the law as a lure. He knew all of it by heart because he had recited it to clients, to opposing counsel, once, memorably, to Judge Aldous Wren himself, back when Wren still smiled at him across chambers like a man sharing a joke only they both understood.

*You file things away, Mr. Nakamura,* Wren had said, five years ago, sliding a thin folder across the desk. The Marisol Vance file, already lighter than it should have been. *That's your gift. Filing. Not finding.*

Cyrus took the pen out of its holder now—the good pen, the one he used for signatures that mattered—and uncapped it over a legal pad, because this document would never touch a computer. Computers left metadata. Metadata was testimony. This would exist in exactly two places: his hand, and the hands of the people he trusted, which currently numbered three, and one of them was dead.

He wrote the date first. Then the caption: *In re Estate of Marisol Vance. First Codicil to Last Will and Testament.*

Then he sat there, pen hovering, and made himself say it plainly, inside his own skull, the way he'd once made clients say their crimes aloud so they understood the weight of them:

*I am about to commit fraud. Knowingly. For a dead woman who cannot consent, and against a living man who has killed twice that I can prove and probably more.*

The lamp hummed. Somewhere below, the harbor slapped at its pilings, patient as ever. Greywater Cove kept its ledgers. Fine. He was going to enter something into them the town would never be able to erase, because you couldn't un-hear a thing read aloud in public, under oath, with witnesses.

He began to write.

---

The codicil ran four pages. He wrote it twice—once fast, to get the architecture right, and once slow, in the careful hand he reserved for instruments meant to survive scrutiny, because scrutiny was the entire point. If Aldous Wren's lawyers got their eyes on this thing, it had to look real enough to frighten them. And if Wren himself read it—and he would; Cyrus was counting on it—it had to do something worse than look real.

It had to make him desperate.

*First*, the codicil declared, *the Executor hereby confirms that remains consistent with those of Marisol Vance have been located offshore of Greywater Cove, at a depth and position known to the Executor and disclosed to no party prior to the reading of this instrument.*

A lie. No divers had been hired. Nothing had been found. But Elias Boone had spent forty years reading that cove the way other men read weather, and when Cyrus had laid the harbor chart on the table in Elias's wheelhouse and asked, quietly, *If you were going to hide a body where nobody would ever drag for it, where?* Elias hadn't even hesitated. He'd put one blunt finger on the water east of the old channel markers, where the wreck of a lobster boat named *Persephone* had sat on the bottom since the winter after Marisol vanished.

"Under her own boat," Elias had said. "Nobody drags a wreck. A wreck's already been searched. That's the whole trick of a wreck." Then he'd looked up, and his face had been the face of a man setting down something heavy he'd carried too long. "That's where I'd have put her. If I were the kind of man who puts people places."

*Second*, the codicil continued, *the decedent recorded a sealed audio confession on or about the date of her disappearance, which recording has come into the Executor's possession and shall be played in full at a public reading of this instrument, to occur at high water on the spring tide, upon the municipal pier at Greywater Harbor.*

There it was. The bait, whole and gleaming. A confession she recorded—before she died—which meant it existed somewhere outside Wren's control, which meant it might name him, which meant that every hour between now and the spring tide was an hour in which Aldous Wren did not know whether the rope around his neck was real.

It wasn't. There was no recording. There was nothing but a surveillance notebook, a handwriting analysis, and a burned archive—but Wren couldn't know how thin the evidence actually was, and that ignorance was the only weapon Cyrus had ever going to get. The man had spent seven years writing in a dead woman's voice. He believed documents. He believed paper could hold power. So Cyrus would give him paper.

*Third*: the reading would be public. Noticed in the county paper, posted at the harbor office, open to any resident of Greywater Cove who cared to attend. Because Wren could burn an annex in the night, could ransack an office, could threaten a half-sister on her own porch—but he could not shoot a confession out of the air in front of forty witnesses without confirming everything it said.

And *fourth*—Cyrus paused here, pen above the page, and allowed himself the smallest, coldest satisfaction of his professional life—*the Executor notes that copies of this instrument and of the recorded confession have been deposited with parties unknown to the Executor's correspondents, to be released in the event of the Executor's death, incapacity, or disappearance.*

*Disappearance.* He chose the word deliberately. Let Wren chew on it.

---

Junie came at seven, letting herself in with the key he'd given her, carrying coffee and a bakery bag and the particular set of her jaw that meant she'd already guessed why she was summoned.

"You're going to do something stupid," she said.

"I'm going to do something fraudulent," Cyrus said. "There's a difference, and it matters legally."

He slid the draft across the desk. She read it the way she'd learned to read in the last month—fast, then again slowly, hunting the seams. She'd gotten good at seams. Living in Marisol's house had taught her that; the house was all seams, fresh oil on old hinges, a kettle always two inches from where she'd left it.

"This says they found her," Junie said finally. Her voice had gone very quiet, which he'd learned was worse than loud. "This says divers found my sister, and it isn't true."

"It says remains *consistent with*. It says located offshore. It says I know where and won't tell until the tide." He kept his voice level, lawyer-flat, because if he let it soften they'd both drown in it. "None of it is true yet. But here's what happens next, and this part I need you to hear. After Wren moves—if he moves the way I think he'll move—we hire divers. Real ones. To that spot Elias marked. Because either she's there, and we've just found her honestly, or she isn't, and we've lost nothing but our dignity."

"And if she's not there?"

"Then we still win," Cyrus said. "Because by then he'll have confessed to something. Men like Wren don't chase ghosts for two weeks without leaving fingerprints on the ghost."

Junie set the codicil down. She looked out the window at the gray water, and when she spoke again her voice had that flinty edge he'd first heard on her third day in town, when a woman at the general store had called her *the other Vance girl* in a tone meant to warn her off.

"When he came to the house," she said, "he told me the cove takes people and keeps them. He said it like a kindness. Like he was explaining the weather." She turned back. "Read it at the pier. Read it loud. I want the whole town standing there when he finds out the ledger's open."

Cyrus nodded and picked up his pen. "One more thing. Your name goes in it. As witness to the deposit of the recording."

"There is no recording."

"There is now," he said. "You're holding it. Right now, in fact. In your bag. A small digital recorder, sealed in an envelope, signed across the flap by you and me and Elias. It doesn't matter that there's nothing on it. What matters is that from this moment forward, three people swear it exists, and none of us can be bought, and one of us—" he nodded at her, "—has already proven she doesn't scare."

Junie took the envelope out of the bag. Looked at it. Signed the flap in handwriting that was unmistakably, defiantly her own—no imitation, no faltering, nothing practiced. She handed the pen back.

"For Marisol," she said. "Write it in the instrument. First line. *For Marisol Vance, in her own words, finally.*"

He wrote it.

---

Elias heard the plan out in his wheelhouse with his arms folded, staring at the chart the way he stared at weather—looking past it, at what it was going to become.

"You understand what you're asking," he said. "Spring tide's nine days out. Nine days that man knows something's coming. Nine days of a cornered animal deciding whether to run or bite." He tapped the chart, east of the channel markers. "And you're telling him exactly where to look if he wants to check your story. He sends his own divers out there, they find nothing, your codicil's dead in the water."

"He won't send divers," Cyrus said. "Divers leave records. Dive shops log tanks. Boat launches are witnessed. Wren hasn't touched a shovel in seven years—he works through paper and through people. He'll come at the recording itself. At me. At the deposit. Which means he comes to ground I've chosen, on a schedule I've posted publicly, in front of a crowd." He met the old man's eyes. "Unless you think I'm wrong."

Elias was silent a long moment. Gulls argued over the pilings outside. When he finally spoke, his voice was low and rough, like something hauled up from depth.

"I pulled her boat out of the water that night," he said. "You know that. What you don't know is I checked the hull first. Standard thing—look for damage, look for why she went down. There was no damage. There was seawater in the bilge that hadn't had time to grow anything, and a scratch along the starboard rail, low, like something dragged." His jaw worked. "I told myself it was nothing. Told myself it for seven years, and every year it cost more to keep telling myself." He straightened. "Nine days. Post your notice. I'll stand on that pier and I'll swear to every word of it, and if he bites, Nakamura—" the old smile, the one with rust on it, "—I owe that man one last debt, and I intend to pay it in full, in public, with interest."

---

The notice went into the county paper on Thursday. By Friday it was thumbtacked to the harbor office board, and by Saturday afternoon the whole town had performed its favorite ritual: discussing it loudly while pretending not to look at each other.

*PUBLIC READING OF CODICIL — ESTATE OF MARISOL VANCE. Municipal Pier, Spring Tide, 6:12 a.m. All residents welcome. Remains located. Recording to be played.*

Cyrus walked the waterfront that evening and felt the town's attention slide across him like searchlight beams he refused to duck. The fish market went quiet as he passed. Old Mrs. Calloway crossed herself, though she was Episcopalian and everyone knew it. Two men outside the chandlery stopped talking mid-sentence, and one of them—Deke Harrow, who'd run errands for Wren since before Cyrus came to town—peeled off and walked quickly up toward the hill road, phone already at his ear.

Good, Cyrus thought. Run along. Tell him.

Seven years of invisibility, and it turned out the trick of stepping into the light was simply deciding the light couldn't hurt him anymore. It could, of course. It could hurt him terribly—disbarment, ruin, worse; Wren had already shown him the shape of worse in the ashes of the annex. But invisibility had never been safety. It had been drowning, slow and comfortable, and he was done being comfortable.

At the end of the pier, Junie stood at the rail looking east, toward the channel markers and the deep water beyond, where a wreck named *Persephone* held whatever it held. Elias stood beside her, saying nothing, the way harbor men say the most important things.

Cyrus joined them. Below their feet, the tide was already turning, beginning its long patient pull toward the spring.

"Six days," Junie said.

"Six days," Cyrus agreed.

And up the hill, in a house with lights burning in every window, Aldous Wren watched the pier through binoculars—and made a phone call of his own.
