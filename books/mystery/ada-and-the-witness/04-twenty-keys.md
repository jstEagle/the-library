## 4. Twenty Keys

By seven o'clock the next morning, Emrys Reyes had done something he had not done in twenty years of service at the Saltmere Grand: he had made a list about the hotel instead of for it.

The distinction mattered. Lists *for* the hotel were instruments of care — the linen rotation, the wine cellar's gaps, which radiator on the third floor had opinions about Tuesdays. This list was different. This list was a net.

He sat at his station behind the front desk with a legal pad angled away from the lobby's morning traffic, and he wrote down every person who could reach the tidal pool without being seen doing it. The pool itself lay below the seaward terrace, tucked into the rocks where the old bathhouse used to be, and it filled and emptied with the tide twice a day like a slow, patient lung. Aldous Crane had been found in it face-down at dawn, his porter's uniform still buttoned to the throat, as tidy in death as he had been in life.

The police were calling it an accident. Emrys had read enough confessions — two letters' worth, now, both addressed in the same careful hand — to know that accidents did not arrive by post in advance.

So: the list.

*Staff with pool access.* He wrote their names in his small, upright script. Marta and Devi, housekeeping, who carried keys to everything including, technically, the pool gate they'd been told never to lock because guests kept drowning themselves in seawater recreationally. Old Piet on maintenance, who had been patching the same section of terrace railing since the second Bush administration and knew the hotel's bones better than its architect ever had. The night porter, Callum, young enough that Emrys still thought of him as "the boy" despite the beard. The kitchen staff came and went through the terrace door during service — four of them, though only two had been on shift last night.

*Long-term residents.* This was the shorter list and somehow the heavier one. Eleven names. People who had lived at the Saltmere Grand so long they had stopped being guests and become, in some geological sense, features. Colonel Ashworth in 305, who complained about the lift the way other men complained about the government. The Fenwick sisters in 208, who had divided the room's duties decades ago — one handled the window, the other the door, and neither had spoken to the other since 2011 except through notes left on the shared dresser. Mr. Okafor, who took his breakfast at precisely 8:15 and tipped in exact change counted from a leather purse.

And Mrs. Sable. Room 412.

Emrys wrote her name and then, after a moment, drew a small line under it. He could not have said why. It was simply that when he thought of the tidal pool and of someone standing beside it in the dark, arranging a drowned man with the patience of a florist, the image arrived wearing Mrs. Sable's cardigan, and he disliked the image, and he distrusted himself for having it.

She had lived at the hotel eleven years. She paid quarterly, in advance, in cash, which no one questioned because she was elderly and English and cash was considered charming in the elderly and English. She took tea in the lounge at four. She returned library books. She remembered birthdays — not just the staff's, but their mothers', which Emrys had once found touching and now found, in the cold arithmetic of the morning, *logistically alarming*. To remember that many dates about other people required either love or surveillance, and the two looked identical from the outside.

He looked at his list. Twenty names, counting himself.

Twenty people who could walk to the water without anyone thinking twice.

Twenty keys, if you counted loosely — and Emrys counted loosely; it was a professional habit. The hotel's locks were old and sociable. A hairpin opened half of them, and the other half opened for anyone who smiled at Piet.

Which meant the real question was not *who had access*. Everyone had access. The Saltmere Grand was a hotel, not a vault; its whole business was letting strangers into private places. The real question was who among the twenty knew the hotel the way the letter-writer clearly did — knew that Aldous Crane walked the terrace at midnight, knew that the tide would be low enough at dawn to leave a body visible but not yet discovered, knew the rhythms of the place the way a musician knows a piece they've played a thousand times.

Knew it, in fact, the way *Emrys* knew it.

That thought arrived quietly, the way unwelcome guests do, and sat down in the corner of his mind and refused to leave. Because there was exactly one person whose knowledge of the hotel matched what the killer had demonstrated, and that person was currently holding the legal pad.

He put down the pen.

"Mr. Reyes." Devi was at the desk with a stack of towels nobody had asked for, which was how Devi announced she wanted to talk. "Is it true about Aldous?"

"It's true that he died," Emrys said. "Beyond that, I'd wait for the inspector."

"The girl detective?" Devi lowered her voice even though the lobby contained only the Fenwick sisters, one of whom was deaf in the ear that faced the other sister. "Marta says she carries her father's file around like a Bible."

"Marta should carry towels."

"These are towels."

"Then deliver them," Emrys said gently, "to 305. The Colonel has been ringing twice an hour since six, which means he has run out of complaints and is inventing new ones."

Devi went. Emrys watched her cross the lobby, and he watched Marta intercept her by the lifts, and he watched them both glance back at him — once, quickly, the way people glance at a clock when they suspect it's stopped. The lobby had always talked about him. That was fine; he had spent twenty years making sure it talked about nothing true. But this morning the glances had a new texture. Not gossip. Assessment. As if the hotel itself were re-reading him and finding marginalia.

He picked up the pen again and added a twenty-first name to the list, then crossed it out, then, feeling foolish, wrote it in again smaller:

*E. Reyes.*

Because here was the thing he had not said aloud to Inspector Halloway, the thing that had been sitting in his chest since the second envelope: whoever wrote those letters knew things about the Ilse Vane case that were never in any newspaper. Knew the register had been signed. Knew the porter's habits. Knew — and this was the part that made his hands go cold on the pad — knew phrasings that felt less like research than like *correction*, as if the writer had read the official account and found it sloppy.

There were perhaps three people alive who could find the official account sloppy. One was dead in the tidal pool. One was a disgraced detective's daughter. And one had spent thirty years not thinking about a night at the seawall, successfully, right up until two cream envelopes arrived at his desk.

* * *

At eleven, Wren Halloway commandeered the small sitting room off the lobby that the hotel optimistically called the Reading Room, on the grounds that it contained nine books and a lamp. She had spread her father's file across the card table like a surgeon laying out instruments, and when Emrys brought her the coffee she hadn't asked for, she didn't look up.

"You've made a list," she said.

It was not a question, which annoyed him pleasantly. "You can see the list?"

"I can see the pen behind your ear and the ink on your thumb and the fact that you've checked the terrace door three times since I sat down. You're a man with a list, Mr. Reyes. Show me the list."

He showed her the list. She read it the way she seemed to do everything — fast, unimpressed, thorough — and then tapped one finger on the long-term residents.

"Eleven years," she said. "Mrs. Sable."

"Eleven years, quarterly, cash."

"Cash isn't suspicious. Cash is *tidy*. My father always said—" She stopped. Her jaw did something brief and controlled. "People who pay cash are either hiding or organized. Sometimes both. What does she look like?"

"Like a grandmother in a biscuit advertisement," Emrys said. "Silver bun. Cardigans in seasonal colors. She knits."

"She *knits*."

"She knits things for the staff. Scarves, mostly. Devi has one in a color I'd call 'ambitious.' Last winter she made mittens for Callum. He wears them. He's twenty-six and he wears them on duty."

Wren looked at him over the rim of her coffee. "You're defending her."

"I'm describing her. There's a difference, and it's the difference between my job and yours." He straightened the pad's edge against the table, a small unnecessary act of order. "But I'll grant you one thing. Yesterday, after we found the second letter, I went through the morning register. Routine. And Mrs. Sable was in the lounge, and she was watching me do it. Not idly. Guests watch me all day — I'm the most interesting piece of furniture in the building. This was different. She watched me the way you watch someone open a safe."

"And did you open the safe?"

"I found Ilse Vane's name crossed out in my own handwriting," Emrys said, "from thirty years ago, in a hand I don't remember using, on a page I don't remember reading. So yes. Something opened."

Wren was quiet for a moment. Outside the Reading Room's tall window, the sea did its eternal impersonation of indifference.

"My father had a saying about hotels," she said finally. "He said a hotel is the only crime scene that cleans itself twice a day. Whatever happened here thirty years ago, the building's been laundering it ever since. Beds changed. Registers archived. Staff turned over." She tapped the list again. "Except the ones who didn't turn over. Except the ones who stayed."

"Mrs. Sable stayed."

"Mrs. Sable stayed," Wren agreed. "Eleven years. Which is either devotion or reconnaissance, and I've never met a difference I trusted."

* * *

The trouble with paranoia, Emrys reflected that afternoon, was that it was indistinguishable from attentiveness, and attentiveness was his entire personality.

All through the lunch service he caught himself cataloguing. Piet on the terrace ladder — had Piet ever mentioned the pool gate? The Colonel complaining about the lift — had the Colonel ever mentioned the *terrace*? The Fenwick sisters taking their constitutional past the seaward windows, moving in their silent orbit of mutual grievance. Every familiar gesture now arrived with a question mark attached, like a guest whose reservation couldn't be found.

It was exhausting. It was also, he admitted privately, a little thrilling, and he was ashamed of the thrill. Twenty years of watching other people's dramas from behind a desk, and now that a drama had finally chosen him, some traitorous part of him was *enjoying the seating*.

At four o'clock, Mrs. Sable took her tea in the lounge.

Emrys watched her from the desk without meaning to, the way you watch weather. She wore the grey cardigan — October, therefore grey — and she poured for herself and for no one, and she read a paperback with a cracked spine, and she was, by every observable measure, an elderly woman having tea. Then, at 4:17, without lifting her eyes from the page, she raised one hand — not waving, not beckoning, just *raising*, the way you'd signal across a crowded ballroom to someone you'd known for forty years — and held it there for three seconds, and lowered it.

Emrys looked behind him. There was no one. There was never anyone behind him; he had designed his station so there never would be.

When he looked back, Mrs. Sable had turned a page.

His heart was doing something unprofessional. He composed it. He crossed the lobby at the unhurried pace of a man attending to nothing, and paused by her table under the excellent pretext of refreshing the flower arrangement, which needed no refreshing, and which he refreshed anyway, badly.

"Lovely afternoon, Mrs. Sable."

"Is it? I hadn't noticed." She smiled at her book. "I've been reading."

"A good one?"

"A reread." She turned it so he could see the cover — some seaside mystery, a lighthouse, a body. "I always know the ending before I start. That's rather the point of rereading, isn't it? Knowing how it ends and watching everyone else be surprised." Her eyes came up then, mild and blue and perfectly kind. "How are you holding up, Mr. Reyes? Poor Mr. Crane. And poor *you*, having to manage it all. You take such care of everyone. Who takes care of you, I wonder?"

"The hotel does," Emrys said.

"Mm." She sipped her tea. "Yes. I expect it thinks so too."

He moved the flowers a quarter inch, accomplished nothing, and withdrew. Halfway across the lobby he realized his hands were not entirely steady, and he was unable to determine whether the cause was fear or the sudden, vertiginous suspicion that he had just been *reread* — that to Mrs. Sable he was not the concierge but a chapter, and she already knew the ending, and was watching him arrive at it page by page, surprised.

* * *

That evening, after the dinner rush had thinned and the lobby had settled into its amber hush, Emrys stood at the window behind the desk and looked out at the dark line of the seawall, where a child had once stood at a rail, and where a woman's hand had—

Nothing. The memory presented its bill and withheld the goods. Just the rail, just the shape of almost-remembering, salt and piano wax somewhere underneath it all like a stain beneath fresh paint.

He unfolded his legal pad and looked at the twenty-one names. Twenty keys. Somewhere among them was a person who knew the hotel's heart by rote, who had watched it for years with the patience of tide itself, waiting for the water to give back what it took.

And one name on the list was underlined, and he could not fully explain the underline, and the underline bothered him more than all the rest — because either it was instinct, and his instincts had never once failed him in twenty years, or it was memory, leaking up through the floorboards of the life he'd built over it.

The lift chimed. Callum emerged with the evening post, yawning, wearing — Emrys noted with a small, helpless warmth — the mittens.

"Just the one for you, Mr. Reyes," Callum said. "Hand-delivered, the courier said. No stamp."

Cream envelope. The same careful hand.

Emrys set it on the desk, squared its edges with the blotter, and did not open it. Across the lobby, in the lounge, Mrs. Sable turned another page.
