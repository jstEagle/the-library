## 6. The Boy at the Rail

The trouble with a memory you cannot access, Emrys Reyes had learned over twenty years of concierge work, is that it behaves exactly like a guest who has checked in under a false name. It pays its bill on time. It tips generously. It smiles at you in the corridor and wishes you good morning. And all the while it is doing something upstairs that you will not discover until the smoke alarm goes off.

He thought about this on Thursday morning while polishing the brass rail at the front desk, because thinking about inaccessible memories was marginally better than thinking about the two cream envelopes locked in his desk drawer, and considerably better than thinking about the way Inspector Halloway had looked at him yesterday — not with suspicion exactly, but with the particular patience of a woman holding a question she had decided not to ask yet.

The lobby was quiet in the way he loved best: the ten o'clock lull, when the early risers had gone out to walk the seawall and the late risers were still negotiating with their pillows. Mrs. Pettibone from 207 was doing her crossword by the window with the ferocious concentration of a woman defusing a bomb. The new porter, Teodor, was learning the ancient art of looking busy without moving anything.

And then the piano started.

It shouldn't have. The Saltmere Grand's piano — a Broadwood, older than the hotel's plumbing and considerably more temperamental — lived in the Palm Court, and no one was scheduled to play until evening. But someone had lifted the lid, and a guest Emrys didn't recognize, a round-faced man in a cardigan the color of weak tea, was picking out something slow and halting, the way people do when they half-remember a piece from childhood.

Emrys went to fetch the piano wax.

This was ritual. Every Thursday, after anyone played, he waxed the Broadwood himself. He did not trust it to the cleaning staff, not because they were careless but because the piano required a particular touch, a particular cloth, a particular beeswax-and-turpentine blend that Mr. Aldous Crane had taught him to mix thirty years ago, back when Crane was a young porter with strong opinions about wood and Emrys was a boy of nineteen learning the desk from a man named Fenn.

He opened the tin. The smell rose up to meet him — beeswax, turpentine, and beneath both, faintly, the salt tang that had worked itself into the grain of the tin lid across three decades of sea air.

Salt and piano wax.

Emrys stood very still with the tin in his hands.

It was nothing. It was a smell. He had smelled it four thousand times. He had smelled it last Thursday. But this morning it went through him like cold water through a wetsuit, sudden and total, and for one disorienting second he was not standing in the Palm Court of the Saltmere Grand at all.

* * *

A railing. Black iron, flaking white where the paint had blistered, and his hands wrapped around it — small hands, a child's hands, the knuckles still soft. Below the railing, rocks. Beyond the rocks, the sea, gray-green and muscular, hauling itself against the seawall in long slow pulls. Evening light, or morning — the kind of low gold light that made the water look like hammered metal.

His hand was holding another hand. A woman's. Long fingers, cool, a ring he could feel against his palm — not a wedding ring; something thinner, worn smooth. She was leaning over the rail toward the water, and she was laughing, or she had been laughing, and her fingers were slipping through his —

Not being pulled. Slipping. The difference mattered. He knew, even inside the fragment, that the difference mattered enormously, though he could not have said why.

Her fingers slid free. Not fast. Slowly, the way a rope pays out, the way a tide takes a mooring line, and there was a moment — a whole held breath of a moment — where her hand hung in the air between them, open, palm up, as if offering him something. As if waiting for him to take it back.

He didn't. He was a child and he didn't, and then she wasn't there anymore, and the sea kept hauling itself against the wall as if absolutely nothing had happened, which was either the most comforting thing about it or the most horrifying, and he —

"Mr. Reyes?"

Emrys came back to the Palm Court with the wax tin still clutched in both hands and the round-faced man in the tea-colored cardigan peering up at him with mild concern.

"You've gone rather pale," the man said. "Is it my playing? My wife says I murder Chopin, but I'd hoped the witnesses would be kinder."

"It's not the Chopin," Emrys heard himself say. "Your Chopin is fine. It's — forgive me. A draft."

There was no draft. The windows were shut. The man nodded anyway, satisfied, and returned to murdering Chopin, and Emrys walked — he made himself walk, unhurried, a man with nowhere urgent to be — back behind the desk, set down the wax tin, and sat in his chair with his hands flat on the blotter.

His hands were shaking.

* * *

Here is what everyone believed about Emrys Reyes: that he forgot nothing. It was said as a compliment, mostly. Guests marveled at it. *He remembered I took my coffee at nine and liked the window seat and that my daughter's name was Beatrice.* Twenty years of faces and preferences and small confidences, filed away in a head that never seemed to lose so much as a coat-check ticket.

Here is what Emrys knew about himself, and had known since boyhood, and had told no one: his memory was not an ocean. It was a hotel. Rooms upon rooms, all meticulously kept — and certain doors, certain entire corridors, locked. Not forgotten. Locked. He had discovered this at fourteen, when a schoolmaster asked him about his father and Emrys found, reaching for the answer, not grief or anger or even absence, but a door. A clean, closed, unremarkable door, with no handle on his side.

He had learned to work around it. You can run a hotel around a locked corridor. You can run a life around one, if the corridor is quiet and you don't rattle the handles. And Emrys had been very careful, for forty years, never to rattle the handles.

Until two cream envelopes had arrived at his desk, addressed in a hand that had made his skin prickle before he knew why.

* * *

At noon, when the lobby filled with returning walkers wanting lunch reservations and local gossip, Emrys did what he always did with a problem too large for his office: he carried it into the small rituals of the day, letting each task hold a piece of it. He confirmed the Harrow party's dinner. He settled a dispute about a lost umbrella with the diplomacy of a border negotiation. He complimented Mrs. Pettibone on finishing the crossword, knowing full well she had used the dictionary for 14-Across, and received in return the look of a woman who knows that he knows and respects him for pretending otherwise.

But underneath the rituals, the fragment kept replaying. The rail. The ring. The hand, palm up, waiting.

And one detail kept surfacing that he could not place anywhere sensible: music. Beneath the sound of the sea in the memory, faint and warped by wind, someone had been playing piano. Not Chopin. Something simpler, older — scales? No. A recital piece. The kind of thing a girl plays at a hotel recital while guests in evening dress sit with their programs folded on their knees.

*Ilse Vane.*

The name arrived in his head fully formed, the way names do when they've been waiting a long time in a room you never enter. Ilse Vane. He tested it silently, the way you test a key in a lock you're not supposed to open. It fit somewhere. Deep in the hotel of his mind, down some corridor he had never walked, a door swung an inch inward, and through the gap came cold air and the smell of salt and piano wax.

He crossed out her name. That was the next thing that surfaced, arriving with the terrible clarity of things that cannot be unknowable once known: he had crossed out her name. In the registry. In his own handwriting — younger, rounder, nineteen years old and trying very hard to write like a proper concierge. There had been a reason. There had been a reason and an instruction and a night, and the door at the end of the corridor was pushing against him now from the other side, and every instinct he owned told him to lean his weight against it and keep it shut.

Every instinct except one. Because two letters sat in his drawer, and a man named Aldous Crane — who had taught him to mix piano wax, who had called him "young sir" for thirty years without a trace of irony — was dead in the tidal pool, and Inspector Wren Halloway was somewhere in this building carrying her father's file like a stone in her chest, and the letters had been bait. He had understood that much already, the way you understand weather coming. Bait meant for him. Which meant whoever wrote them believed there was something in him worth catching.

They were right. God help him, they were right.

* * *

At four o'clock, unable to bear his own company any longer, Emrys did something he almost never did: he left the desk during hours. He handed Teodor the phone extension, endured the boy's expression of a man being given the keys to a nuclear submarine, and walked out through the service door onto the seawall path.

The afternoon was bright and indifferent. Gulls argued over something in the shallows. Joggers passed. An elderly couple stood at the rail taking photographs of nothing in particular, the way elderly couples do, photographing the fact of having come.

Emrys walked the wall slowly, north to south, reading the iron railing the way other men read inscriptions. It had been replaced twice in his tenure — storm damage, 2009 and again in 2016 — but the footings were old, Victorian, granite blocks worn round at the edges by a century of hands.

Halfway along, near the old boathouse steps, he stopped.

He couldn't have said why he stopped. His body simply declined to continue, the way a horse refuses a bridge. He put his hand on the rail — black iron, modern paint, but bolted to the same granite — and looked down at the rocks below, green-black and weed-slicked, and the sea hauled itself against the wall in long slow pulls, exactly as it had, exactly as it always had, patient as arithmetic.

*I was here.*

Not nearby. Here. This rail, these stones, this stretch of water. A child of — how old? Seven? Eight? — standing where he stood, holding a woman's hand, and losing it.

He tried to reach further and hit the door. Full weight against it, the way he hadn't leaned in forty years. The door held. But through it, faintly, came sounds: applause. Polite, scattered, evening-dress applause. A woman's voice saying something he couldn't make out — quick, low, urgent. And his own voice, a child's voice, thin with terror, saying words he could not hear but whose shape he suddenly dreaded more than anything on earth.

Because the shape of those words was a question. *Where did she go? Where did she go?* Asked over and over, to adults who did not answer, or answered wrong, or answered in ways that made the child understand — with the terrible animal clarity children have — that he must stop asking.

Someone had told him to forget. Someone had taken his face in their hands — he felt it suddenly, actual pressure on his cheeks, cool fingers, a voice above him saying *never mind, never mind, it was the sea, the sea took her, and you saw nothing* — and he had obeyed, because he was a child, and because the person saying it loved him, and love is the most effective lock ever devised.

Emrys took his hand off the rail. His legs felt unreliable. He walked back along the wall past the joggers and the gulls and the couple photographing their happiness, and no one looked at him twice, and that was somehow the worst of it — that a man could stand at the exact spot where a drowning began and be entirely invisible, which meant, which had always meant, that the person who drowned might have been invisible too, screaming into a sea that hauled itself against the wall as if absolutely nothing were happening.

* * *

He got back to the desk at quarter to five. Teodor had survived; the phones had not (three calls dropped, one guest booked into the wrong room, a taxi summoned to Bristol instead of Saltmere), and Emrys repaired everything with the mechanical calm of long practice, and all the while the knowledge sat in him like swallowed seawater.

He was the witness.

That was what the letters wanted. Not Crane — Crane was a means, a first domino, a confession planted to pull the police in and pull the file open. Not the registrar, not the porter, not whatever chain of custody the killer was walking backward through. Him. The boy at the rail. The only living soul who had seen what happened to Ilse Vane, and who had spent forty years not seeing it, and whom someone — someone patient, someone tender, someone who mixed kindness into cruelty like sugar into medicine — had decided it was time to remember.

*When the tide gives back what it took.* He had read the phrase a dozen times and taken it for menace. Now he heard it differently. It wasn't a threat. It was a promise. The tide had taken something from him too — a night, a name, a corridor of doors — and someone intended to give it back, whatever it cost him.

At six, Wren Halloway came through the revolving door with wind in her hair and her father's file under her arm, and stopped when she saw his face.

"What happened?" she said. No preamble. He was beginning to suspect she didn't own one.

"I remembered something," Emrys said.

Her eyes changed — sharpened and softened at once, which he would not have believed possible in a human face before this week. She glanced at the lobby, at Mrs. Pettibone's window, at Teodor's magnificent ears, and said, quietly, "Not here."

"No," Emrys agreed. "Not here." He reached for his coat, and paused, and looked back at the desk — at the drawer where two cream envelopes lay locked, at the brass rail he polished every morning, at the whole beautiful, orderly, carefully curated surface of the life he had built on top of a locked corridor.

"Inspector," he said, "when we find who wrote those letters — I think I need to tell you something first. About the night Ilse Vane died. I think—" and here his voice did something it almost never did, wavered, "—I think I was there."

Wren Halloway went very still. Then she said, in a voice stripped of everything except purpose:

"Then we'd better talk before whoever wrote them finds out you remember. Because if they sent those letters to flush you out, Mr. Reyes—"

She didn't finish. She didn't need to. Outside, far below the seawall, the tide was turning, and somewhere in the hotel, patient as arithmetic, someone was counting the dominoes still left to fall.
