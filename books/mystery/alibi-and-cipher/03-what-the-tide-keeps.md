## 3. What the Tide Keeps

Wren Ashgrove had a theory about grief that he never shared at dinner parties, mostly because he was never invited to any: grief kept books. It kept meticulous, vindictive books, and it always came back to collect.

Maren Voss had been dead nine days when her brother knocked on the door of the coroner's office with his coat still smelling of the funeral flowers and his eyes red-rimmed in a way that suggested he'd been crying on and off for a week and had gotten quite good at hiding it between bouts.

"Mr. Ashgrove." The man extended a hand. His grip was strong but careful, the grip of someone who'd learned to handle fragile things. "Denny Voss. Maren's brother."

"I know," Wren said, and then, because that sounded like something a stalker would say, added, "I mean — I read the family records. Occupational habit. Everyone who comes through here gets alphabetized eventually."

Something flickered across Denny Voss's face — not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, the way a fish ghosts under dock pilings. "She said you were funny. She said you were the only man in Saltmere who talked to her like she was a person instead of an institution."

"She was an institution," Wren said gently. "Forty years of Sunday school and a hymnbook collection the historical society would kill for. But yes. She was also a person."

Denny sat down without being asked, which Wren liked. People who asked permission to sit in a coroner's office were usually people with something rehearsed.

"I'm not leaving town," Denny announced.

"It's a nice town."

"It's a town that ate my sister alive for thirty years and then buried her like she was furniture being moved to storage." He leaned forward. "I came back for the funeral. I meant to stay three days. I've been here nine. I keep finding reasons."

"Grief is good at inventing reasons."

"This one isn't invented." Denny reached into his jacket and produced an envelope — old, soft-cornered, the paper gone the color of weak tea. "This came every year. First week of December, thirty-one of them. Cash inside. No return address. Postmarked here, Saltmere. My sister saved every one. She kept them in a biscuit tin under her bed like they were love letters from a secret admirer." His voice cracked on the last word. "I found the tin going through her things. There are letters in there addressed to my mother too. Going back further. Before I was born."

Wren took the envelope carefully. The postmark was faded but legible: SALTERE, DECEMBER 3RD. No year visible, but the stamp was old enough to have been licked by someone who remembered when stamps cost less than a cup of coffee.

Thirty-one envelopes. Thirty-one first weeks of Decembers. And the drowning — Tobias's file, Edith's certificate, the boy pulled from the harbor the night after the winter dance — had happened in the first week of December, thirty years ago.

The first payment would have come one year later. Almost to the day.

"Were you aware," Wren asked, keeping his voice level, "of where this money came from?"

"Maren thought it was guilt money. She wouldn't say whose guilt." Denny's jaw tightened. "When I asked her directly — two Christmases ago, I drove up special — she looked at me like I'd asked her to swear in church. She said, *'Some people pay their debts, Denny. Let them.'* Then she made me a ham sandwich and changed the subject to the weather, which is how Vosses say *drop it.*"

*Let them.* Wren turned the envelope over in his hands. Someone in Saltmere had been paying the Voss family a private annuity of remorse for three decades, and Maren Voss had known who, or at least known enough to protect them, and had taken that knowledge into the ground beside her brother.

And now, nine days after her burial, a confession letter had surfaced among her effects, postmarked the day after her funeral.

"You found something else in her effects," Denny said. It wasn't a question. "I saw you at the house. You went through that biscuit tin like a surgeon."

Wren weighed it. He was, by trade and temperament, a keeper of confidences — but Denny Voss had just handed him a thread, and threads only mattered if you followed them out loud.

"There was a letter," Wren said. "Unsigned. A confession to a drowning. Your brother's drowning."

The color left Denny's face so completely that Wren half-rose, ready to catch him.

"My brother didn't drown," Denny said finally. "My brother drowned. Both sentences are true and I hate both of them." He pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes. "I was four. I don't remember him. I remember a photograph and a ham sandwich's worth of stories. And now you're telling me someone confessed?"

"Not confessed to killing him. Confessed to — knowing. To what happened that night." Wren hesitated. "I believe the letter was written by Edith Crane."

"Your coroner. The one before you."

"The one who signed his death certificate."

Denny lowered his hands. He looked, suddenly, very tired and very much like his sister around the mouth. "Then somebody mailed it for her. She died two years ago. I sent flowers. Everybody sent flowers. Saltmere does flowers beautifully — it's cheaper than honesty."

Wren laughed before he could stop himself, and Denny laughed too, briefly, wetly, and for a moment the office felt less like an archive of the dead and more like what it occasionally managed to be: a place where the living could sit down.

"Mr. Voss," Wren said, "I need to ask you something, and I need you not to take offense, because I'm about to be rude in a professional capacity."

"That's my favorite kind of rude."

"The payments. Thirty-one years. That's not remorse. Remorse sends flowers. That is a *retainer*. Somebody bought your family's silence, and whatever they were buying silence *about*, they considered it worth more than a teacher's annual salary, every single year, through recessions and hurricanes and one memorable incident involving the church roof." He leaned forward. "Who in this town could afford that? Who in this town has been *that* comfortable, that consistently, for that long?"

Denny was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the gulls argued over something dead, as gulls do.

"Hale," he said at last. "Hale Fisheries. The Ellerys — Ruth Ellery owns half the waterfront rentals now. The Pikes have run the harbor since before the lighthouse had electricity." He ticked them off on his fingers like a man reading a menu he couldn't afford. "Take your pick. This town runs on maybe eight families, Mr. Ashgrove, and every one of them has a face for church and a ledger for everything else."

* * *

Denny Voss had left Saltmere at nineteen with two suitcases and no intention of looking back, and had spent twenty-six years proving the intention correct. He lived in Portland now, sold marine insurance, which he privately considered the funniest job a drowned boy's brother could hold — you spent all day pricing the odds of water winning.

He hadn't wanted to come back for the funeral. He'd come anyway, because Maren had raised him after the second loss, the quieter one, the one nobody counted because there wasn't a grave for it. You didn't skip Maren's funeral. Maren had skipped exactly nothing for him, ever.

What he hadn't expected was how the town would get under his skin again. Like a splinter you forget until it rains.

He stayed at the Anchor Inn, in the room over the kitchen, and every morning Mrs. Tully brought him breakfast and every morning she told him a little more than he'd asked for, which was the Saltmere way. By day five he'd learned that the Hale boy — August's son, the one who'd been at the dance that night, the one everyone went quiet around — was Dr. Hale now, respected, beloved, coach of the junior sailing club. By day six he'd learned that Colm Pike still ran the harbor and still told the same story about the storm of '94 at every opportunity. By day seven he'd learned that nobody, *nobody*, would say the name of the other boy who'd been on the dock.

Tobias.

Denny remembered him. Not well — he'd been four — but he remembered a tall skinny kid who used to let Denny hold the flashlight while he fixed things, bikes, crab traps, the loose step on the Voss porch. Foster kid. Lived with the Ellerys for a while, then somewhere else, then nowhere anyone would name. When Denny had mentioned him at the Anchor Inn's bar, conversationally, the way you mention weather, the whole room had gone so silent he could hear the ice melting.

Mrs. Tully had refilled his coffee without being asked and said, "Funny, the names that slip a body's mind," in a tone that meant *stop.*

So Denny did what Vosses did. He made lists.

He sat in the inn room that night with Maren's biscuit tin open on the bedspread, thirty-one envelopes fanned out like a losing hand of cards, and he wrote down everything he knew, which was almost nothing, arranged with great care. It was a family trait. Their griefs were tidy even when nothing else was.

The amounts had grown over the years — he could tell by the thickness of the envelopes, though Maren had banked most of it unopened, which said something about her that made his throat hurt. The handwriting on each envelope was different. Deliberately different, he suspected now, having spent one long evening comparing them. Block letters, then slanted script, then a spidery hand, cycling through disguises. But the postmark never changed. Saltmere. First week of December.

Someone local. Someone organized. Someone with thirty years of discipline and a conscience that wouldn't heal right.

There was a knock at the door. He opened it to find Wren Ashgrove standing in the hall with his coat collar up against the drizzle, holding a folder the way a priest holds a Bible.

"I've been reading tide charts," Wren said. "From thirty years ago. And I have a question that's going to ruin your evening."

"They're already ruined. Come in. Mind the envelopes."

Wren stepped in, glanced at the fan of tea-colored paper, and something in his face shifted — the look of a man whose suspicion had just stopped being a hypothesis and started being a floor plan.

"Here's my question," Wren said. "The night your brother died, the official record says he fell from the dock alone, at high water, during a squall. Except the squall peaked at eleven-forty, and high water wasn't until nearly one. And Edith Crane's certificate says the body was recovered at low tide." He held up the folder. "Those two facts cannot both be true, Mr. Voss. Which means someone adjusted the record to fit a story. And there are exactly three living witnesses from that night who could tell me which parts were true."

Denny felt the splinter shift under his skin.

"Pike," he said. "Ellery. Hale."

Wren raised an eyebrow. "You've been busy."

"I've been bored and orphaned twice over. It's amazing what a man can accomplish." Denny closed the biscuit tin. "One condition. Whatever those three tell you — I want to be in the room, or right outside it. My family paid for this story in the only currency we had. I'm done letting other people spend it."

For a moment Wren just looked at him, and Denny got the sense of being measured against some internal standard, some private alphabet of the trustworthy.

"All right," Wren said quietly. "But fair warning, Mr. Voss. I've sat across from a hundred people telling me stories about the dead. I can hear a rehearsed alibi the way you can hear a cracked bell." He tucked the folder under his arm. "And tomorrow morning, I'm going to go listen to three bells that I suspect were tuned by the same hand."

He paused at the door, rain silvering the window behind him.

"One more thing. The foster boy. Tobias. Every record of him in this town has a hole where he should be. Not missing — *removed*. Records don't remove themselves, Mr. Voss. Somebody with authority and a very steady hand took him out of the story entirely." Wren's eyes were dark and unhurried. "Which means whoever paid your family thirty years of hush money may have been paying for two silences. And I intend to find out what the second one cost."
