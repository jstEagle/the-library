## 7. Low Water

Edith Crane's notes were written in the same hand that had taught Wren to hold a pen over a body like it was a bird you might startle. He'd spent two days with them, spread across his kitchen table while the rain did its patient work on Saltmere's rooftops, and by the second evening he had a name.

Not *Tobias*. That name was a ghost, scrubbed from school registers and church rolls and even, as far as Wren could tell, from the memory of anyone willing to talk. But Edith had been careful in the way of people who expect to be searched: she never wrote the name he'd taken, only the road he'd taken it on. A bus line. A date. A notation in her small, upright script — *G. asks after him every few years. Still paying. Still afraid.*

Wren traced the letters with one finger. G. Hale. Thirty years of hush money, mailed like a subscription to silence.

The bus line still ran. Of course it did; coastal towns change slowly, the way driftwood changes — worn down but never gone. Wren drove out on a gray Thursday with the file on his passenger seat and a feeling in his chest like a tide going out, leaving things exposed he'd rather stayed covered.

* * *

Two towns over is a generous phrase for Merrow's Bend. It was one long street, a cannery that smelled of brine and diesel, and a boarding house with a porch sagging like a tired shoulder. The man Edith's notes pointed to — under the name *Toby Marsh* — worked the morning shift at the cannery and, according to the woman at the post office who was delighted to have something to talk about, "kept mostly to himself, poor thing, though he does fix the widow Ansel's radio without being asked."

Wren found him at the end of the pier behind the cannery, eating lunch out of a tin pail, watching the water with the expression of a man keeping an eye on an old enemy.

"Tobias Fenwick?" Wren said.

It was a gamble, using the name from the drowned boy's era. The man didn't flinch exactly. What he did was worse — he went very still, the way the dead go still, and Wren had seen enough stillness to know the living version when he saw it.

"That's not my name," Toby Marsh said. His voice was flat, unhurried, a voice that had practiced not mattering.

"No," Wren agreed. "I don't imagine it is." He sat down on the pier edge, a respectful six feet away, and opened his lunch — he'd bought a sandwich at the cannery canteen specifically so this wouldn't look like what it was. "Wren Ashgrove. I'm the coroner in Saltmere."

"I know who you are." A pause. "Everybody from there who comes through here knows who you are. They check."

"Check?"

"To see if you're one of theirs." Marsh looked at him then, and his eyes were pale gray, sea-worn, older than his face. "You're not. You've got outsider hands."

Wren glanced down at his own hands, resting on the sandwich he no longer wanted. "What are outsider hands?"

"Hands that haven't signed anything for them yet." Marsh went back to watching the water. "You'll want to ask about the Voss boy. Everyone does, eventually. Took thirty years, but here you are."

"Actually," Wren said, "I want to ask about you."

That landed. He saw it land — a small flinch around the mouth, quickly mastered. Marsh set down his fork and folded his hands, and Wren recognized the posture: it was how witnesses sat when they'd finally decided to tell the truth and wanted their bodies to behave while they did it.

"You have her handwriting," Marsh said. Not a question.

"Edith Crane's. Yes."

"She was kind to me. Did you know that? In a town full of people being kind to each other in a way that always costs somebody else, she was actually kind." He laughed, short and dry, no humor in it. "She used to bring me books. Said a boy shouldn't lose his whole library just because he lost his address."

"What happened that night, Tobias?"

The name again. This time Marsh let it sit, the way you let an old dog hear its old name.

"We were fourteen," he said. "Me, Danny Hale, and the Voss boy — Peter. Peter Voss was the only one who was ever nice to me, you understand? I was the foster kid. The county rotated us through Saltmere every eighteen months like weather. Nobody learned your name because why bother. But Peter called me Tobias like it was a real name. Like I was a real person. He said—" Marsh stopped. His jaw worked. "He said everybody deserved to be called what they were called at home."

The tide lapped against the pilings. Somewhere behind them the cannery clanked and hissed.

"There was a dare," Marsh went on. "There's always a dare. Danny Hale dared Peter to walk the dock rail at low tide, end to end, because Peter had done it before and Danny couldn't stand not being best at something. And Peter — Peter was showing off for Ruth Ellery's little sister, who was watching from the seawall. He got to the end. He made it to the end." Marsh's voice cracked on a rusted hinge. "Then Danny shoved him. Just — playing, he'd say. Just a shove between friends. And Peter went off the end into the water, and it was low tide, and the current under the dock at low tide is a rope pulling you out to sea. Everybody in Saltmere knows that. Everybody except a boy from inland who'd been there four months."

Wren kept his voice level. "And you?"

"I jumped in." Simple words, delivered like a verdict. "I was the strongest swimmer of the three of us — foster kids learn swimming the way they learn everything else, out of necessity. I got to him. I had him. I had his wrist." Marsh held up his own wrist, staring at it as if the ghost of another wrist might still be there. "And then Danny started screaming from the dock that if Peter drowned, we'd all say *I* pushed him. Because who would believe me? Who would believe the foster kid over the Hales? And I panicked. I heard myself in the future — police, courts, the Hales' lawyers — and I let go. Half a second. Maybe less. The current took him, and I let go, and I have been letting go ever since."

Wren found, to his surprise, that his eyes were burning. He thought of Maren Voss in her coffin, thirty years waiting beside a brother whose death certificate said *accidental drowning, no contributing factors*, and felt fury rise in him like floodwater — fury at Danny Hale, at August Hale, at the whole polished machinery of a town that could grind a child into a footnote.

But underneath the fury, pity, which was worse. Pity for a fourteen-year-old who'd been handed an impossible arithmetic: one boy's life against his own, weighted in advance.

"They told me the deal on the same day as the funeral," Marsh said. "August Hale came to the group home himself. Sat across from me with his hat on his knee like a deacon. He said Danny hadn't meant it, that a shove wasn't murder, that if it went to court I'd be charged too — negligent, maybe manslaughter, they had lawyers who could make it stick. And then he said the word he'd clearly come to say. *Emigrate.* That's what he called it. Emigrate. Like I was a bird flying somewhere warmer." Marsh smiled at the water. "They moved me to Merrow's Bend within the month. New name. New school. A monthly envelope, first from August, later from the son once the father's hands got bad. Thirty years of envelopes. You know what's in them besides money?"

"What?"

"A note. Every single one. Same sentence, thirty years, in Danny Hale's handwriting." Marsh reached into his coat and pulled out a worn leather wallet, and from it, carefully, a slip of paper gone soft at the folds. He handed it to Wren. "*We remember what you gave up.*" Wren read it twice. "Not *we're sorry*. Not *thank you*. *We remember what you gave up.* Like it was a gift. Like exile was a present they wrapped me in."

"Peter's sister died last week," Wren said quietly. "Maren. She was buried next to him."

"I know. I sent flowers. They won't know who they're from." He took the note back, folded it away with the tenderness of a man handling a relic. "Maren wrote to me, you know. Twice a year, Christmas and the anniversary. Never asked for anything. Just told me about her garden, her brother's grave, the weather. She was the only person who ever used my real name in thirty years. She'd sign it, *Your friend, Maren.*" His composure finally broke, just at the edges, a crack running through old concrete. "I couldn't go to the funeral. I stood at the edge of town until dark and then I drove back here. That's the whole of my courage, coroner. That's what's left of Tobias Fenwick."

Wren sat with it. The gulls argued overhead. The cannery bell rang the hour.

"The original inquest," Wren said at last. "Edith signed the certificate. Accidental drowning. She knew about you — her notes say so. Why would she falsify it?"

"Because August Hale showed her the same arithmetic he showed me," Marsh said. "One dead boy or two ruined ones. She chose the number she could live with. I think it nearly killed her anyway. I think that's why she kept the notes. People keep receipts when they know they've been robbed." He stood, brushing off his trousers, and looked down at Wren with those pale, tide-scoured eyes. "So now you know. The question is what you do with it. And I'll tell you for free, since everyone in Saltmere tells you things for free: whatever you decide, decide fast. Because the Hales will already know you're here. They always know. There's a reason I take my lunch on this pier instead of the canteen."

"Why's that?"

"Because from here," Marsh said, turning back toward the cannery, "you can see the whole parking lot. And I've spent thirty years watching cars come." He paused at the door. "Yours has been sitting there twenty minutes, Mr. Ashgrove. And that black sedan parked behind it — that's been here ten. I don't know whose it is. But it's got Saltmere mud on the wheel wells, and nobody drives to Merrow's Bend from Saltmere by accident."

Wren turned. The sedan sat in the gray light, empty, patient, its windows beaded with sea mist.

When he looked back, Tobias Fenwick was gone inside, and the tide was coming in.
