## 2. Thirty Years of Silence

Vila Serena smelled the way small towns do when they've decided to keep a secret: salt, diesel, and something sweeter underneath, like fruit going bad in a locked room.

I arrived on a Tuesday in October, when the tourist season had packed up its umbrellas and left the coast to itself. My rental car — a white compact with the personality of a filing cabinet — took the cliff road down into town while I rehearsed my opening line for Marta Esteves, executor of Tomas Reyes's estate and, according to three phone calls and one very terse email, the only person in Portugal who did not want me here.

*Ms. Almeida. Mr. Reyes is an old man who deserves peace. Whatever he is paying you for, it isn't worth what it will cost.*

I'd read that email eleven times. Not because it was threatening — it wasn't, not exactly — but because it was *specific*. People don't warn you off vague things. They warn you off things they can name.

The harbor came into view around the last bend: a crescent of grey water holding maybe forty boats, most of them older than my career. Nets hung like laundry. Men mended them with the unhurried hands of people whose work hadn't changed since their grandfathers' grandfathers. I parked near the fish market and sat for a moment, watching the water move.

Somewhere under that water, thirty years ago, Duarte Fonseca had drowned. Or hadn't. That was the entire problem, really — the or.

I found Marta Esteves where I expected to find her: behind the desk of a narrow office above the pharmacy, surrounded by the paper archaeology of one man's life. She was perhaps sixty-five, silver hair cut short as a verdict, reading glasses pushed up into it like a crown she resented wearing. She didn't stand when I entered. She looked at me the way you look at weather you've already decided not to enjoy.

"Ms. Almeida." Her Portuguese was accented by decades of English clients; mine was accented by two semesters and optimism. We settled into her language, which felt like a concession on both sides. "You found us."

"It wasn't hard."

"No," she said. "It never is. That's the tragedy of this town."

She poured coffee without asking, from a pot that had clearly been waiting for me, which meant she'd known my arrival time before I did. I filed that away. Executors who surveil their lawyers are executors with something to lose.

"I'll be direct," I said. "Mr. Reyes has retained me to—"

"To prove a murder didn't happen." She set the pot down with a click that ended sentences. "Yes. He told me."

"And your objection?"

"My objection," Marta said, folding her hands, "is that some questions are knives. You don't get to ask one and then act surprised when someone bleeds."

I let the silence work. It's the oldest tool I have and the only one that's never been disbarred. She lasted four seconds.

"Tomas Reyes is dying," she said. "He has perhaps months. He built something here — not just the cannery, not just the money. A family. Sofia Fonseca grew up in his house after her father drowned. The fishermen he employs have children who have children. And you arrive with a retainer and a question that could take all of it apart."

"A man confessed to murder."

"He confessed to *grief*," she said sharply. "You're a lawyer. You know the difference between what a man says and what is true."

Actually, I thought, most of my career rested on the assumption there was no difference at all. But I said, "Then give me the truth and save us both time."

Marta looked out the window at the harbor, and for a moment her face did something unguarded — a flinch, almost, aimed at the water.

"The record says Duarte Fonseca went out on the night of November third, 1994, in a storm, and never came back," she said. "The record says Tomas identified the body. The record says grief, insurance, probate, closure." She turned back to me. "The record is a document. Documents are written by survivors."

"And the harbor?"

Something moved behind her eyes — fear, or its well-dressed cousin, caution. "The harbor remembers whatever the harbor remembers. Ask the fishermen if you like. They'll tell you about the storm, the boat, the widow's crying. They will not tell you anything else, because there is nothing else." She stood, signaling an audience over. "Go home, Ms. Almeida. Take the fee. Some cases are better left unclosed."

"You mean unsolved."

"I know what I mean."

* * *

The town gave me nothing all afternoon, but it gave it generously.

At the maritime registry, a clerk with ink-stained fingers pulled the 1994 file and watched me read it like a man watching someone handle his grandmother's letters. Death certificate: Duarte Manuel Fonseca, cause of death drowning, body recovered November fifth, identified by business partner Tomás Reyes. Certifying physician: Dr. Anselmo Cordeiro. I wrote the name down without knowing yet why it would matter. The clerk mentioned, unprompted, that Dr. Cordeiro had died himself barely a year later — heart failure, they said, though he'd been fifty and swam every morning. Then he looked embarrassed, as if the town had rules about what got said to outsiders, and he'd just broken one.

At the café, when I asked about the Reyes-Fonseca partnership, conversation around me developed a sudden interest in its own coffee. At the church, the sexton remembered the funeral fondly and the aftermath not at all. By evening I had a folder full of dates and a head full of the distinct sensation of walking through a house where everyone had agreed to breathe quietly.

Sofia Fonseca, I learned, no longer lived in Vila Serena. Lisbon, someone thought. Or Porto. She'd left at eighteen and come back rarely, and when she did, she visited the cemetery alone. Even her grief was kept behind glass.

I walked the harbor at dusk. The boats knocked against each other like restless sleepers. An old man sat on a bollard near the seawall, hands still, watching the water with the patience of stone. I sat down at a respectful distance. For ten minutes neither of us spoke, and it was, strangely, the most honest conversation I'd had all day.

"They say you're the lawyer," he finally said, in Portuguese, not looking at me.

"They say correctly."

"There were two men in that boat." His voice was flat, factual, the voice of a man reporting tide tables. "Everyone knows it. Everyone says one came back." He turned his head then, and his eyes were pale and old and frightened in a way that had calcified into habit. "But you should ask yourself, senhora, which one the town thinks came back. And which one it was."

"Can you tell me—"

He was already standing, joints cracking like rigging. "Thirty years I've watched the water," he said. "Ask the water. It keeps better secrets than we do." And he walked away into the blue dark, leaving me with a sentence that would keep me up half the night, turning it over like a stone with something alive under it.

* * *

The note was waiting in my rental car.

I'd parked on the quay above the harbor, and when I clicked the door open, the interior light fell across a folded page on the passenger seat. White paper. No envelope. Someone had entered a locked car on a lit street in a town where, I now understood perfectly, everyone watched everything.

My first instinct — the lawyer's instinct — was evidence protocol. Don't touch more than necessary. Photograph it. But my second instinct, the human one, arrived faster: a cold slide of adrenaline down the spine, the animal knowledge of being *seen*.

I photographed it in place, then lifted it by the corner with a tissue from my bag.

Block capitals, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper:

**STOP ASKING WHO CAME BACK. SOME PEOPLE DROWNED FOR A REASON.**

No signature. The handwriting was careful, masculine, the letters formed by someone who'd taken trouble to disguise them and mostly succeeded. The paper was ordinary. The threat was not elaborate — but elaboration is for amateurs. The efficient ones just let you know they exist.

I sat in the driver's seat for a long time, engine off, watching the harbor lights shiver on black water. Somewhere out there was the spot where a boat had gone out in a storm thirty years ago with two men aboard. Somewhere in Porto or Lisbon or — the thought arrived unbidden and stayed — somewhere much closer than that, Duarte Fonseca was either dead or spectacularly alive, and I had just been told, in writing, which answer certain people preferred.

Here's what unsettled me most, and I'm a woman who is professionally unsettled for a living: the note wasn't protecting a secret. Read it again. *Some people drowned for a reason.* That's not a denial. That's a justification. Whoever wrote it wasn't telling me the drowning happened.

They were telling me it *should have*.

I drove back to my rental above the bakery, checked the locks twice, and laid the note beside the case file on the kitchen table. Two documents, side by side: a death certificate signed by a doctor who died too conveniently young, and a threat signed by nobody.

Tomas Reyes had paid my retainer in full, upfront, from an account I couldn't trace past a shell company in Madeira. He'd refused to meet. He'd given me one impossible instruction and then gone silent.

And now someone in this town of four thousand souls knew within twenty-four hours of my arrival exactly which questions I was asking.

Either Marta had reported our conversation — possible, likely even — or someone else was keeping closer watch on this case than I was. Someone who'd been watching for thirty years, waiting for exactly this kind of digging to start again.

I made tea I didn't drink and stared at the harbor through the window until the lights went out one by one, the way they do in towns that sleep early and dream carefully.

One thing I knew for certain, and it was the kind of certainty that makes a lawyer check the price of extending a car rental: everyone in Vila Serena was protecting someone. Marta protected Tomas. The clerk protected the memory of a convenient doctor. The old fisherman protected whatever he'd seen from the seawall in 1994 and had spent three decades wishing he hadn't.

The only question — the one that would follow me into the next morning and every morning after — was who was doing the protecting for whom.

And whether any of them were protecting the dead man from the living one.

My phone buzzed against the table. Unknown number. Local prefix.

A text, five words long:

*You should leave tonight, senhora.*
