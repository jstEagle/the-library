## 6. What the Harbor Kept

The fisherman's name was Anselmo, and he had been refusing to talk to me for six days.

I knew this because Marta kept a ledger of everyone who'd come to the house asking questions, and my name appeared in it four times, each entry annotated in her small, furious handwriting: *again. again. AGAIN.* Anselmo's name appeared once, dated the day after I arrived, with a single word beside it: *no.*

So when he waved me down from his porch on the seventh morning — a crooked finger, a face like driftwood left out too long — I understood I wasn't being invited. I was being collected. Some debts, Vila Serena had decided, were mine to pay whether I'd incurred them or not.

"You're the lawyer," he said, not as a question but as an accusation, the way you'd say *you're the weather* to a coming storm.

"I'm the lawyer."

"Tomas sent you."

"Tomas hired me. There's a difference."

He looked at me for a long time. His eyes were the pale gray of old ice, and they did the thing that eyes do here — they went past me, out toward the water, checking on it, the way other men check on children.

"Come inside," he said. "Not because I'll talk. Because it's cold, and you look like a woman who'll stand there all day out of spite."

"That's accurate."

Inside smelled of diesel and dried sardines and forty years of one man's living. He poured coffee that could have stripped paint and sat across from me with his hands flat on the table, as if pinning something down.

"I pulled bodies from that water for thirty years," he said. "Before your client's night, and after. You know what the sea gives back?"

"Whatever it wants."

"It gives back what it took," he said. "That's the trick of it. People think drowning is a disappearance. It isn't. The sea keeps nothing. It's the town that keeps things. The sea gives everything back, and then we decide what to do with it." He drank. "In 1994, the town decided."

"Decided what?"

"That Duarte Fonseca drowned." He set the cup down with a click. "And that Tomas Reyes came home alone, half-dead himself, crying a name into the rain. And that was the story, because the story fit. A storm. A boat. A rich man lost, a poor man grieving. Grief makes better neighbors than suspicion. Nobody wanted to ask the fisherman who'd been on the water that night what he saw."

"But you were on the water."

"I was always on the water." Something moved behind the gray eyes — not guilt exactly. Regret's older cousin. "And I saw two men go out in that boat. And I saw one man come back."

My heart did the thing it does when a case turns over in its sleep. "Tomas."

"No." He said it softly, and the softness was worse than shouting. "Not the one the town thinks. Not your crying Reyes. I was maybe three hundred meters off, running for harbor before the storm closed the mouth. I had lights on them for maybe ten seconds. Ten seconds is enough, at that distance, in that light, if you've spent your life reading boats." He turned his cup slowly, a full rotation, like a dial. "The man who came ashore that night walked up from the rocks below the point. Not from the harbor. Not from where Reyes's boat would have landed. From the north side, where a man could beach a dinghy and walk up into the pines and be gone before anyone thought to look."

"And Tomas?"

"Tomas came back later. Alone, in his own boat, wrecked half to pieces, screaming a dead man's name." Anselmo looked at me directly for the first time. "Your client confessed to a murder, senhora. But the man I saw walking up from the rocks that night was not a murdered man. He was a man leaving."

* * *

I drove back along the coast road with the windows down and the wind doing its best to rearrange my thinking. Two men in the boat. One man ashore by the north path. One man home late, wrecked, weeping. It didn't add up to murder. It added up to something more elaborate and more human: a disappearing act with an audience of one, and the audience had spent thirty years taking the blame.

Which raised the question I'd been circling since Porto: if Duarte Fonseca walked out of the sea alive, who had been paying whom ever since? And why did a town built on keeping things keep *this*?

Marta's car was in the drive when I got back to the guesthouse. That was wrong on two counts — she hadn't announced a visit, and Marta announced everything, the way weather announces itself. She was standing at my rental car.

She was holding a metal wastebasket.

Smoke rose from it in a thin gray rope. Even from twenty meters I could smell the burn — paper, ink, something sweet underneath, sealing wax maybe. She was feeding pages into it one at a time, unhurried, watching each one catch before reaching for the next. She'd taken off her rings. I don't know why that detail undid me more than the fire did, but it did. Bare-handed arson. The intimacy of it.

"Marta."

She didn't startle. "You're back early. Did the old man talk?"

"How did you know I went to Anselmo?"

"Because everyone talks eventually, and Anselmo has been waiting longest." She fed another page to the flames. "These are letters. Thirty years of letters. I'm deciding which ones the world needs."

"The world doesn't get to decide. They're evidence."

"They're *mine*," she said, and for the first time since I'd met her, her voice cracked down the middle like river ice. "Do you know what it is to keep a secret for someone, Lena Almeida? Do you know what it costs? Not the lying. The lying is easy. It's the *paying*. Every month, for twenty years, I have driven to the post office in Caxarias and mailed an envelope to a box number, and every month I have hated myself a little more, and every month I did it anyway, because the alternative was—" She stopped. Fed another letter to the fire. Watched it curl. "The alternative was worse."

"Blackmail," I said.

"Don't dress it up in Latin. Yes. Blackmail." She laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as the burning paper. "Two hundred escudos at first. Then euros. Then more. The amounts went up every few years, like rent. Like a subscription to silence. Do you know what the letters said?" She held one up, unburned, and read aloud in a voice gone flat and strange: "*Marta. The girl is getting older. Girls notice things. Girls ask about fathers. I would hate for Sofia to learn what her father really was. Send the usual. D.*"

The wind moved through the pines and the smoke leaned sideways between us.

"He never signed the whole name," she said. "Just the initial. As if a letter could be deniable. As if twenty years of envelopes could be anything else." She dropped it in. "I burned most of them over the years, you understand. These are just the ones I couldn't make myself destroy. The ones where he mentioned her. I told myself I'd need them someday, if I ever found the courage. Instead I found you."

"Marta. Who is D.? Say the name."

She looked at me then, and I saw it — not loyalty, which is what I'd assumed lived behind her flinty gatekeeping all week, but fear. Fear worn smooth by repetition, the way a stair tread wears hollow. She hadn't been protecting Tomas. She'd been protecting Sofia, and herself, and the whole rickety architecture of a life built on top of a grave that had no body in it.

"If I say the name," she said quietly, "then it's real, and then he's real, and then everything I paid for was for nothing, and I am an old woman who spent her life savings feeding a ghost." Her jaw worked. "But you're going to find him anyway. I've watched you work. You're like a dog with a bone, except the bone is a corpse and the corpse won't stay buried. So."

She reached into her handbag — not for another letter. For a photograph, creased white with folding, soft as cloth from handling.

It showed two young men on the dock at Vila Serena, arms around each other's shoulders, laughing at whatever young men laugh at when they own a boat and a future. On the left, unmistakably, a younger Tomas. On the right, a broad-shouldered man with dark hair and a smile that took up half his face.

And standing slightly apart, caught mid-turn as if he'd been walking past and glanced back, a third figure. Younger. Leaner. But I knew the set of the shoulders. I'd seen those shoulders across a table in Porto, denying his own name while offering me money.

"That's not possible," I said. "That photo is from the eighties. The man in Porto is—"

"Is what?" Marta said. "Thirty years older? So are we all." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "Duarte never left, Lena. Or rather — he left, and he came back, and he's been near us the whole time, close enough to watch, close enough to collect. Why do you think I paid without question? Why do you think no one in this town will say his name? He's not a memory. He's a neighbor."

The photograph trembled in my hand. Behind the two laughing men, blurred in the background of the frame, stood the cliffside house. And in its upstairs window, small and grainy and unmistakable to anyone who'd been staring at these people long enough —

A face, watching the camera. Watching the boat. Watching the future it intended to own.

"Marta," I said. "Where is Tomas right now?"

Her bare hands went still over the burning basket.

"He wouldn't tell me," she whispered. "He said if I knew, I'd be in danger. He said—" She stopped, and I watched thirty years of managed fear collapse into something raw. "He said goodbye, Lena. He said it like he meant it."

The last letter caught fire between us, and neither of us moved to stop it.
