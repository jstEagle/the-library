## 8. Alphabetized Suspects

Detective Brandt had a way of sitting in my lobby that made the furniture seem to apologize for existing. He'd claimed the same wingback chair three days running now — the one by the fireplace with the loose spring that had betrayed every guest who ever trusted it — and he sat there drinking coffee he hadn't paid for and asking me questions I'd already answered in triplicate.

"Walk me through it again," he said, not looking up from his notebook. "The night of the fourteenth."

"I walked you through it Tuesday. And Wednesday. And this morning, over your eggs."

"Humor me."

So I did. Again. The camera footage showed me entering the east wing at 10:47 p.m., forty minutes before Edmund Voss drank his last glass of port. It showed me leaving at 11:02. Fifteen minutes in the corridor of death, unaccounted for, and no witness alive or willing to say otherwise except Rosa Ferreira — who was currently sitting in county holding because her fingerprints were on the decanter and mine were on the hallway camera.

Brandt believed one of us killed Voss. He hadn't decided which yet, which I suppose was a kind of open-mindedness.

"Where were those fifteen minutes, Mr. Nakamura?"

"Delivering a letter to Room 409. Mrs. Okonkwo's arthritis medication came late; she doesn't sleep without it."

"The letter would have taken two minutes."

"Mrs. Okonkwo does not take her medication until she has told me about her grandson's soccer season. The grandson is nine. There are many games."

Brandt wrote something down. I doubted it was about soccer. He had the particular patience of a man who believes everyone lies eventually, given enough coffee and enough Tuesdays, and I had the particular patience of a man who knows exactly how long people can hold out, because I've kept records.

Twenty years of records. Which is why, when Brandt finally left — tipping an imaginary hat at June, who gave him nothing but the polite blankness of a woman who audits numbers for a living and has seen what people do to them — I went upstairs, locked my door, and began to do the thing I do best.

I built a file.

Not the official one. Brandt had the official one, and the official one was a blunt instrument being swung at the wrong throat. Mine would be finer work. Alphabetized.

* * *

I should explain something about grief, since I'm told novels require it: grief is unbearable in its natural state, so the mind converts it into tasks. This is why mourners scrub floors. Why they reorganize pantries. Why, in my case, I sat down at midnight with a fresh notebook and turned the murder of a man I'd known for twenty years into a filing problem.

Because here is what nobody tells you about watching people for two decades: eventually you love them. Not the guests — guests come and go like weather. But the town. Bellhaven itself. Every secret in my ledger belonged to someone whose coffee order I knew, whose children I'd watched grow up through the lobby windows, whose funerals I'd attended standing at the back with my hat in my hands. Edmund Voss had been a bastard of the first water, vain and cold and capable of ruining a man over a misfiled invoice. He had also, in 2009, quietly paid six months of rent for a housekeeper whose husband had left her with three kids and no warning, and had made me swear never to tell, and I never had, and now he was dead on a slab in the county morgue and someone was going to hang for it who didn't deserve to.

Rosa deserved to go home. That was the entire case, as far as I was concerned. Everything else was procedure.

But procedure wins trials. So: alphabetized suspects.

* * *

**A is for Adrian.**

The son arrived at the hotel two days before the murder, summoned — or so he claimed — by a telegram from his father that no one else saw. He was thirty-four, handsome in the way of men who have been told they're handsome often enough to stop hearing it, and he carried his resentment the way some men carry cigarettes: constantly, unconsciously, offering it around.

Motive: disinheritance. I'd watched the dinner where Voss informed Adrian, over the lamb, that the hotel would pass to a trust rather than to him. I'd watched Adrian set down his fork with exaggerated care, the way a man does when the alternative is throwing it. I'd heard him say, in the corridor afterward, to no one, to the wallpaper: *"He can't do this. He will not do this."*

Hole in the case: Adrian had left at 9 p.m. that night, or said he had, driving north to see his mother in Harwick. Except — and here my ledger earns its keep again — the toll booth on Route 9 logged a silver Alfa Romeo matching his at 11:31 p.m., heading *south*, back toward Bellhaven. I know because I pay the toll authority's clerk a modest retainer to send me interesting traffic summaries, a habit that sounds sinister and is merely thorough.

So Adrian lied about where he was. But liars are not automatically murderers; if they were, half of Bellhaven's founding families would be in the ground already, and the other half would be doing the burying. Adrian came back. Why? To beg? To threaten? To kill?

Or — and this possibility sat in my chest like a swallowed stone — to arrive too late, and spend the rest of his life knowing it.

* * *

**H is for Hale.**

Marguerite Hale, attorney-at-law, executor of the Voss estate, and the most composed person I have ever observed in a crisis, including myself, and I have practiced.

She had been Voss's lawyer for nineteen years. She drafted everything: the leases, the trusts, the contracts with the founding families that I could not read but could certainly *weigh* — thick documents, signed fast, filed away before anyone could study them. She was in the building the night of the murder, staying in Room 305, which put her ninety feet and one staircase from Room 412.

Motive: money, presumably, though lawyers' motives are always money wearing a suit. Or loyalty gone strange. Or fear. When I mentioned to her, delicately, that Voss had seemed agitated his final week — making calls, burning papers in his room's ashtray at hours when burning papers suggests panic — she looked at me with those level gray eyes and said, "Mr. Nakamura, you notice a great deal."

It wasn't a compliment. It was a measurement.

Hole in the case: none that I could find, which worried me more than any hole would have. Everyone has a hole. A person with no visible weakness is either innocent or very good, and Marguerite Hale did not strike me as innocent. Innocent people fidget.

* * *

**I is for Ilsa.**

And here my pen slowed.

Ilsa Voss — though she'd grown up as Ilsa Merrin, a name chosen by a mother who wanted no part of Edmund Voss, dead or alive. His illegitimate daughter. She'd walked into my lobby in the rain a month ago and paid me to prevent a murder, and I had taken her money and filed her under *curiosities* and done precisely nothing, because intervening in things was against my religion.

She had motive the way a knife has an edge: inheritance, again, if she could prove paternity, and she'd spent the last year trying to. She had opportunity — she was registered in Room 310, five doors down. She had knowledge of the poison, if you counted the fact that her mother had died slowly of a heart condition treated with digitalis, and digitalis was what killed Voss, and grief teaches people chemistry whether they want to learn it or not.

Hole in the case: she was the one who hired me. Killers don't generally hire witnesses to their own crime, unless the killer is playing a game so deep that hiring the town's best observer as a personal alibi-generator constitutes genius. Possible. But when Ilsa cried — and she cried once, briefly, in the stairwell, thinking I couldn't hear — it did not sound like performance. I have heard performed grief. It has better acoustics.

Still. I wrote her name down. Love is not evidence. I've spent twenty years learning that lesson and I resent having to learn it again at my age.

* * *

**V is for Vivian Ashcombe.**

The matriarch did not attend the funeral. Matriarchs of her caliber consider funerals beneath them — funerals are for the bereaved, and Vivian Ashcombe has never in eighty-one years been bereft of anything she couldn't replace.

But she had sent flowers. White lilies, extravagant, with a card reading only: *To an old colleague.* Colleague. As if Voss had been a fellow dentist.

My ledger holds eleven entries touching Ashcombe interests. Money moving through the hotel's accounts in patterns I never fully mapped — invoices for banquets that fed twelve, payments for renovations to rooms no guest occupied, a standing monthly transfer labeled *consulting* to a man I knew for a fact had never consulted anything but his own reflection. Voss laundered for them. I never knew how much, and I never asked, because asking questions is a form of intervention and I was, for twenty years, Switzerland with a front desk.

Motive: Voss had been agitated. Burning papers. Making calls. If Edmund Voss had decided, near the end, to confess — to clean the slate, settle accounts, maybe leave the hotel to a trust as penance — then every founding family in Bellhaven had motive, and Vivian Ashcombe was the founding family, capital letters, the rest merely subsidiaries.

Hole in the case: Vivian Ashcombe does not poison people herself. She has people for that, the way she has people for her gardens and her taxes. If she ordered it, then somewhere below her there is a pair of hands, and hands leave prints, and prints can be found. Also — and I grant this is thin — the lilies. You don't send lilies to a man you murdered. You send nothing. Sentiment is the one luxury the truly ruthless cannot afford.

Probably.

* * *

**D is for Dev.**

I almost didn't write his name. My hand actually hovered, pen aloft, like a diver deciding against the water.

Devraj Osei has tended the Grand Meridian's bar for fourteen years. He taught me to make an Old Fashioned properly, which is to say he mocked mine until I improved. He covers June's shifts when her migraines come. He named his cat after my ledger — called it "the Register" as a joke, years ago, and the joke stuck, and now there is a fat orange cat answering to Register sleeping on my radiator.

Motive: Dev owes money. I've known for two years — not because he told me, but because a man named Corrigan started drinking at the bar on Thursdays and never paying, and Dev comped his tabs with a tightness around his eyes that I catalogued and said nothing about. Corrigan works for people connected to the Ashcombe orbit. Gambling debts in Bellhaven do not go to collections; they go to employment.

Opportunity: Dev was working the night of the murder. The bar closes at midnight; the east wing is a straight shot past the service stairs. And the decanter — the poisoned decanter — came from the bar's inventory. Port, from a bottle only staff could access.

Hole in the case: Dev was behind the bar, in view of six guests, until 12:15 a.m. The medical examiner put Voss's death between 11:15 and 11:45. Unless Dev can be in two places — and I have audited stranger claims — the arithmetic protects him.

But arithmetic protected Rosa too, once upon a time, and look where she was sleeping tonight.

I wrote his name anyway. Then I sat for a long moment feeling like a man who has just pickpocketed his own family, and I reminded myself of the alternative: Rosa, in holding, twenty-three years old, protecting someone out of loyalty while the state sharpened its pencil for her.

Loyalty. There was that word again. It kept surfacing in this case like a body in a pond.

* * *

*

Three o'clock in the morning. The notebook held five names, seventeen motives between them, and nine holes, and none of the holes lined up with each other, which meant either that I was missing a sixth suspect or that the truth was some ugly composite — one person's lie covering another person's crime, the way a fence hides a grave.

I went downstairs for water and found June at the front desk, which was wrong, because June's shift ended at eleven and June does not linger. She was staring at the key cabinet with an expression I'd never seen on her face before, and June's face is a well-organized place.

"You're up late," I said.

"So are you." She didn't turn around. "Casper. The master keys. The log we keep for maintenance sign-outs."

"What about it?"

"There's an entry for October third. Key M-7, checked out at 2:40 a.m., returned 4:05. Signed out to—" she paused, and I watched her decide whether to say it, and I understood, in that suspended moment, exactly how it feels to be on the receiving end of information you're not ready for, "—to housekeeping. But Rosa wasn't on shift that night. I checked the schedule twice."

October third. Six days before Voss died. Someone had used a master key in the dead of night and written Rosa's name in the log to cover it — which meant whoever it was knew the schedules, knew the logs, knew the hotel's small machinery intimately.

Someone inside. Someone whose name might be on my list, or worse, someone whose name I hadn't thought to give a letter yet.

June finally turned around. In the low lamplight she looked older than her thirty years, and tired in a way that had nothing to do with shifts.

"Casper," she said quietly, "who else reads our maintenance log?"

And I stood there in my own lobby, in my own hotel, holding my glass of water like a man holding evidence, and realized I didn't have an answer I could bear.
