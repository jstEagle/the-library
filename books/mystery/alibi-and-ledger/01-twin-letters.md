## 1. Twin Letters

The rain had been falling on Marrow Bay since before dawn, the kind of patient, grey rain that seemed less like weather than like a decision the sky had made about the town. Freya Oyelaran stood in the doorway of the bookshop at seven-forty, keys in one hand, umbrella furled and useless against her hip, watching the water bead on the awning she kept meaning to replace. The canvas sagged in the middle where a gull had spent the winter dismantling it, thread by thread, with what she could only assume was malice.

The shop was called The Turning Page, which had been her husband's joke, and then, after he died, simply a fact she lived inside. Twelve years of salt air had eaten the gold leaf off the letters until they read more like a suggestion than a name. She unlocked the door, stepped over the threshold into the smell of old paper and damp wool, and did not yet know that everything she had buried was waiting for her in the post.

There were three items in the box that morning. A water bill, creased and apologetic. A catalogue from a distributor in Leeds offering forty percent off remainders nobody wanted. And two envelopes, both cream-coloured, both hand-addressed, lying together as if they'd been introduced.

Freya stood in the doorway with the mail fanned in her hand and felt the temperature of the room change.

She knew the handwriting on the first envelope. That was the obscene part—twelve years, and her body recognised it before her mind consented to. The *D* that started Daniel's name had always leaned forward, eager, as if the letter itself couldn't wait to be finished. He used to write her notes in the margins of library books when they were twenty-three, little arrows pointing at sentences he thought she'd love, and she had found them years later in secondhand shops across three counties, his handwriting surfacing like something the sea refused to keep down.

*F. Oyelaran,* the envelope said. No title. No *Ms.* Just her name, in ink gone slightly brown at the edges, written by a hand that had once known how her coffee took its milk.

The second envelope bore no name at all. Only the shop's address, block capitals, pressed hard enough to emboss the paper from behind. She turned it over. Sealed. No return address. The postmark was local—Marrow Bay, dated four days ago—and there was something wrong with the weight of it, a heaviness that a single sheet shouldn't have carried.

She set the water bill on the counter. She did not open either envelope. Instead she made tea she didn't drink, shelved a stack of returns she didn't see, and told herself, in the flat voice she used for frightened customers, that there was no reason a Tuesday should be any different from any other Tuesday.

At nine, when the first customer—a retired teacher named Mrs. Calloway who came in every week to buy nothing and complain about everything—had been safely installed in the history section, Freya took the envelopes into the back room and opened them with a knife she kept for breaking down boxes.

* * *

The first letter was two pages. The handwriting deteriorated between them, the confident lean of the opening lines giving way by the end to something cramped and trembling, as though the writer were fighting his own hand.

*Freya.*

*I have started this letter eleven times. I know because I kept them all, and then burned them, and then wrote it again, because some things don't stay burned and neither do I.*

*Nell did not drown the way they said she drowned. You knew that. I think you always knew that, and you let it go, and I have hated you a little for twelve years for being able to let anything go, and loved you the whole time for knowing it was a lie worth keeping.*

*I am writing now because I am dying, and dying men are owed one honest sentence, and here is mine: I was there. I put her in the water. Not alone—but I will not name the other name, because naming it would kill you faster than it kills me, and I have done enough killing.*

*The coroner called it tide and drink. I called it mercy, once, though God knows whose. I am calling it murder now, out loud, on paper, so that somewhere in the world there is a document that tells the truth even if no one ever reads it.*

*I'm sorry. Not for writing this. For everything the letter can't hold.*

*Yours, always more than I had any right to be,*

*Daniel Ashworth*

Freya read it twice. Then she laid it flat on the desk, aligned its edges with the edge of the blotter, and stood very still while the room performed its small betrayals—the tick of the radiator, the drip from the awning through a gap in the eaves, Mrs. Calloway's voice drifting faintly from the front, insisting to no one that the Napoleonic Wars had been mismanaged.

Nell Oyelaran had been twenty-nine the year she died. Marcus's younger sister, all collarbones and laughter, the kind of beautiful that made other women check their reflections without meaning to. Freya had liked her. Had, in the way of sisters-in-law, half-loved her. Nell had come round on Sundays with pastries and gossip and an appetite for Freya's company that never once felt like obligation, and when they pulled her out of the cove below Gullwrack Point, bloated and blue and officially drunk, Freya had grieved cleanly, the way you're allowed to grieve when the story holds.

Except it hadn't held. Even then. There had been the shoes—Nell's good leather boots, dry on the rocks above the tideline, placed side by side with a care that drunken women do not extend to footwear. There had been the bruise on her forearm the coroner waved away as a boating knock, though Nell hadn't been on a boat in two years. And there had been Daniel, standing at the back of the funeral with his collar up, looking at Freya across the grave with an expression she had spent twelve years refusing to translate.

She had filed those things away—not in a police file, because she'd handed her warrant card back by then, but in the grey ledger of the heart, under losses she chose not to audit. Closure protected everyone, she'd told herself. Including me. Especially me.

Now the ledger had learned to write back.

* * *

The second envelope she opened last, and slower, the way you approach a dog that hasn't decided about you.

Inside was a single sheet. Typed. Not printed from a computer—the letters had the uneven bite of an old machine, a ribbon running dry in places, an *e* that struck high. Someone had typed this on something with real keys, something that required commitment.

It said:

*You'll want to pull the file. You already have. You're reading it again tonight, the one you keep in the green box behind the accounting ledgers, third shelf, because you've never trusted yourself to throw anything away.*

*The confession is true. It is also incomplete.*

*The ledger has more pages to come.*

No signature. No greeting. Just that, sitting on the page like a handprint on clean glass.

Freya read it three times, and each reading stripped something else away. First the words themselves. Then the fact that whoever wrote them knew about the green box—knew the exact shelf, the exact arrangement, the small private taxonomy of a woman who had spent thirty years cataloguing other people's sins and her own. And finally, worst of all, the tone: unhurried, almost fond. This was not a threat shouted from a distance. This was someone leaning close, speaking quietly, enjoying the flinch.

She went to the shelf. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; she'd have expected them to shake, and their steadiness felt like a stranger's hands attached to her wrists. The green box sat where it had always sat, wedged behind the shop's actual accounting ledgers—her one piece of theatre, hiding the true ledger behind the false ones, because even her secrets needed camouflage.

She lifted the lid. Everything was there. The photographs of the cove she'd taken two days after the funeral, when she still had a friend at the constabulary willing to lend her a camera and look the other way. The coroner's report, photocopied at cost. Her own notes, in her own handwriting, the questions she'd never asked aloud:

*Why were the boots dry?*

*Who anchored a boat?*

*What did Marcus inherit?*

And beneath the notes, folded into quarters, a page torn from a tide table for the night Nell died, annotated in pencil.

Everything present. Nothing taken. Whoever wrote the second letter hadn't come for evidence. They'd come to prove they could stand in this room, at this shelf, and leave again untouched.

Freya closed the box. Sat down at the desk. Picked up Daniel's letter and read the closing line again—*I was there. I put her in the water.*—and heard, underneath it, the second letter's whisper: *it is also incomplete.*

Two letters. Two hands. One truth cut in half and dealt to two different people, or maybe the same person playing both sides, and no way to know which until she did the thing everyone in Marrow Bay had agreed, silently and permanently, not to do.

From the front of the shop came the chime of the door, Mrs. Calloway's departure, the wet slap of her shoes on the pavement outside. Then silence, except for the rain, which had settled in for the day, indifferent and thorough, the way it always was in Marrow Bay when something was about to surface.

Freya looked at the two letters lying side by side on her desk—Daniel's confession and the stranger's promise—and understood, with the cold clarity she used to bring to crime scenes, that the past she had buried had not stayed buried.

It had learned her address.
