## 7. Pages Missing

The redacted name had a shape.

Freya had learned that much in thirty years of reading documents the way other people read faces—that a blacked-out line still told you things. The width of it. The pressure of the pen beneath. She'd photographed the estate ledger page with her phone in Pryce's kitchen at two in the morning, both of them drinking tea neither wanted, and now she sat in the bookshop's back room with the image blown up on her laptop screen, measuring.

The redaction covered eleven characters. A surname of six or seven letters, most likely, with an initial before it. And the payments—quarterly, punctual as tide tables—had run for nine years after Nell Oyelaran drowned. £1,200 a quarter, then £1,500 when the will was probated, tapering off only when the family trust was restructured in 2019. Someone had been paid to keep quiet for nearly a decade, and whoever administered the ledger had decided, nine years on, that the arrangement was safe enough to leave a paper trail.

Or careless enough. Freya had known Marcus Oyelaran for twenty-two years and had never once thought of him as careless. Careful was his religion. He checked the weather twice before sailing, balanced his accounts to the penny, and wore grief like a well-cut coat—fitted, appropriate, never quite warm.

She set down her tea and looked out the rain-streaked window at the harbor, gray as dishwater, and let herself say the thing she'd been refusing to say since the ledger page came into her hands.

*It could be him.*

Nell had been set to inherit everything. That was the fact everyone in Marrow Bay knew and no one said aloud: old Ambrose Oyelaran had left the house, the boats, and the better part of two hundred thousand pounds to his daughter, passing over his son with a note that read, in its entirety, *Marcus has his own way of making money.* Nell died four months later, intestate, unmarried—and everything flowed to her brother. The family home Freya lived in now. The cottage Daniel had loved her in, once, before any of this had names.

Marcus had grieved beautifully. Freya remembered the funeral: Marcus in the front row, hand pressed flat over his heart, tears arriving exactly on cue during the eulogy. She'd been pregnant with a grief of her own by then—Emmanuel dead three years, Nell the last thread of his family—and she had leaned on Marcus's steady shoulder and called him brother and never once asked why a man who inherited a fortune on the back of his sister's drowning seemed so very little surprised by it.

The bell above the shop door rang. Freya closed the laptop without hurrying. Guilt, she'd learned, moved fastest when you flinched.

* * *

Pryce arrived at noon with fish and chips wrapped in newsprint and the expression of a woman who'd spent the morning losing an argument with a database.

"The trust records are sealed," she said, dropping onto the stool behind the counter, among the remainders and the water-damaged poetry. "Properly sealed—not lazy-sealed. Someone paid for the sealing."

"Can you unseal them?"

"With a court order, a sympathetic judge, and about six weeks." She pushed the chips across the counter. "Which is why I'm eating lunch in a bookshop instead of making an arrest. What have you got?"

Freya hesitated. It was an old hesitation, worn smooth from use—the instinct to hold information close, to work alone, to trust no one with the shape of a case until it was finished. But Pryce had seen her anonymous files. Pryce had offered alliance instead of handcuffs. And Freya was tired of being the only person awake in a town full of sleepwalkers.

"Who benefits if Nell's death stays accidental?" she said. "Not who killed her. Who profits."

"Marcus." Pryce didn't even pause to chew. "Everyone's always known that. Everyone's always decided it didn't matter, because Marcus Oyelaran would sooner forge a receipt than break a law."

"He forged something," Freya said quietly. "I just don't know what yet."

She opened the laptop and turned it toward Pryce: the ledger, the redaction, the quarterly rhythm of the payments. Pryce studied it for a long moment, chips forgotten.

"That's hush money," she said. "That's textbook hush money. Eleven characters—" She pulled a pen from her coat and wrote on the newsprint: *M. OYELARAN*. Counted. "Ten with the initial. Close enough, if the administrator padded it."

"It fits," Freya agreed. "But fitting isn't proof. I need to see how he reacts."

"You're going to confront him." Pryce said it flatly, not a question. "Alone."

"I'm going to ask my brother-in-law about family finances over Sunday dinner. Alone is the only way he'll talk."

"And if he's the one who burned your back room?"

Freya looked at the scorched doorway, the tarp nailed over it, the smell of wet char that no amount of airing had lifted. The fire had taken her files, Daniel's letters, twelve years of careful anonymity—all but the single ledger page she'd carried out pressed against her chest like a child.

"Then he'll know I know," she said. "And knowing is the one thing I can't take back."

* * *

Marcus's house sat above the cove—the same cove, though no one in the family ever called it that; they called it *the north shore*, as if renaming it could wash it. Freya had eaten a hundred dinners at that long oak table, had watched Marcus carve roasts with surgical precision, had listened to him tell the story of Nell's drowning at every gathering for a decade afterward, each telling polished smoother, until it sounded less like memory than liturgy.

He answered the door in a cardigan the color of oatmeal and looked genuinely pleased to see her.

"Freya. Thank God. I've been worried sick since the fire." He stepped back to let her in. "You should stay here until the shop's livable. Really. There's room."

"I'm fine at the shop."

"You're stubborn at the shop." He led her through to the kitchen, where a pot of something simmered and the radio murmured shipping forecasts. Domestic. Safe. Staged, she thought, and hated herself for thinking it. "Wine? I've got that Rioja you like."

"Just tea, thanks."

He made it without comment, and they sat at the table where she'd sat a thousand times, and for a while they talked about nothing—the fire, the insurance assessor, the price of heating oil—while Freya waited for the opening. It came when Marcus sighed and said, "They've released the remains, you know. Nell's. The coroner's office called. They want to reexamine everything." His hands were steady around his mug. Only his voice gave anything away—a thinness, like ice over moving water. "Twelve years, Freya. Can't they let her rest?"

"That's what I came to ask you about." She laid the photograph of the ledger page on the table between them. "Estate disbursements. Quarterly payments continuing after Nell died. To a name that's been redacted."

Marcus looked at it for perhaps three seconds. Then he laughed—a short, easy sound, entirely without strain.

"That'll be the boatyard settlement. After the accident with the Winchcombe boat, Dad paid out compensation for years. It was all tied up in the trust." He shook his head fondly. "You've been reading too many of your own case files. This is accounting, not conspiracy."

"Maybe." She kept her voice mild. "Where were you the night Nell died?"

And there it was. Not a flinch—Marcus didn't flinch—but a settling, the way a man settles into a chair he's sat in many times. He set down his mug, aligned it precisely with the table's edge, and began.

"I was at the Anchor that evening, early. Left around nine—Tom Ferrier will remember, he walked me partway home. I stopped at the north shore to check the moorings before the weather came in. Saw nothing, heard nothing. Nell had said she might walk out that way—she liked the water at night, you know how she was. I went home, slept badly, and found out in the morning." He spread his hands. "It's all in the original statement. You read it, surely."

"I read it," Freya said.

Her voice sounded distant to herself, as if someone else were speaking. Because she had read it—she'd read every word of the file last week, and again last night, and Daniel's confession lay open in her memory beside it, and the two statements lay against each other like pages of the same book.

*I left the Anchor around nine. Tom Ferrier saw me go. I stopped at the north shore to check the moorings before the weather. Nell said she might walk out that way—she liked the water at night. I went home and slept badly.*

Word for word. Phrase for phrase. The same sentences, the same cadence, the same odd specific detail—*slept badly*, repeated in both, the kind of humanizing touch a liar adds deliberately. Two men, twelve years apart, giving testimony in identical words.

Either they'd rehearsed together.

Or one of them had written the other's lines.

The floor did not drop so much as tilt, slowly, the whole solid house of her certainty leaning toward the sea. Freya looked at Marcus—at the cardigan, the steady hands, the grief worn like a well-cut coat—and understood that she was looking at a man who had prepared this answer the way he prepared his taxes: thoroughly, legally, and years in advance.

"You've told that story often," she managed.

"Tragedy does that." He smiled, gentle, brotherly. "You repeat it until it stops cutting."

"Yes," Freya said. "So I've heard."

She stood, thanked him for the tea, and let him walk her to the door with his arm warm around her shoulders, and it took everything she had learned in thirty years not to let her step falter—because there, hanging in the hall beside the photographs of Nell, was the old framed chart of the north shore, and pinned to its corner, half-hidden by the frame's edge, a corner of heavy cream paper.

The same stock as the second letter.

Marcus followed her gaze, and for one unguarded instant—less than a breath—his face was not a face at all but a door opening onto a dark room.

"Family history," he said lightly. "You know how I keep things."

"I know," Freya said. "You keep everything."

Outside, the wind had come around to the northeast, and far below, past the garden wall, the cove lay exposed at low tide—black rock, white foam, and the old mooring ring standing clear of the sand like a raised finger. Freya walked to her car on legs that felt borrowed, got in, and sat with her hands on the wheel, breathing.

Daniel hadn't written his confession. He'd copied it. Dictated, rehearsed, filed away—insurance, he'd said, though she hadn't understood until now whose insurance, or against whom. Marcus hadn't just inherited Nell's money. He'd inherited her death, curated it, maintained it, kept the paperwork current for twelve years like a man servicing a boat he intended to sail again someday.

And today he'd sailed it. He'd given her the alibi—the alibi, the one artifact that could hang him—because he believed she'd never compare it against a confession that had burned in her back room.

He didn't know she'd read it twice. He didn't know she remembered everything.

Freya started the engine. Tomorrow she would drive two towns over, to the hospice with the sea view, and put the two statements side by side in front of the dying man who'd signed his name to a murder he didn't commit—and ask him the only question left that mattered:

*Whose words were you given, Daniel—and who else knows Nell was alive when you left her?*
