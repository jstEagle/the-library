## 10. Closing the Ledger

The courtroom in Marrow Bay smelled of old varnish and cold radiators, and Freya Oyelaran sat in the witness box with her hands folded like a woman waiting for a bus that would never come.

Marcus Oyelaran's barrister was a tall man with a voice like wet gravel, and he had spent forty minutes trying to make her sound like a woman with a grudge. A jilted lover. A failed detective with a failing bookshop and a taste for reopening wounds because the alternative was looking at herself too closely.

"Mrs. Oyelaran—Ms. Oyelaran," he corrected himself, with a small smile meant to wound, "you'll admit your judgment where my client is concerned has been... compromised. By history."

"I'll admit," Freya said, "that I ignored evidence for twelve years because it was easier than following it. That's not a grudge. That's an apology I owe the dead."

The gravel-voice faltered. He hadn't prepared for honesty; most barristers never did. They prepared for pride, and pride could be picked apart like a gull working a mussel shell.

She walked them through it one more time, plainly, without ornament. The mooring ring exposed by erosion at Gullane Cove—the ring that proved a boat had been tied off on a night Nell supposedly wandered down alone and drunk and slipped beneath the water. The fractured scaphoid in Nell's right wrist, healed wrong years before her death, then re-fractured fresh: restraint injuries, the pathologist finally conceded, consistent with being held. The blue acrylic fibers from Marcus's boat cover, snagged in what remained of her cardigan. The estate ledger, with its payments continuing past the date of death to a name redacted by a hand everyone in the family had recognized once they were made to look.

And the alibi. She saved that for last, because it was the hinge.

"Marcus Oyelaran told me he was at the Anchor Inn until midnight on the fourth of October, twelve years ago. That he walked home along the cliff road and saw nothing." She let the silence sit. "Daniel Ashworth's confession—written years later, at Mr. Oyelaran's instruction—contains that same sentence. Word for word. *Walked home along the cliff road and saw nothing.* Two men do not independently produce identical phrasing for an alibi. One of them wrote it, and the other memorized it as insurance."

"And you believe Daniel Ashworth's account now? A dying man, confessing to a murder he says he didn't commit?"

"I believe him," Freya said, "because everything he lied about, he lied about to protect someone else, and everything he told the truth about cost him something. That's not how liars work. Liars spend truth freely and hoard the lies. Daniel did the opposite his whole life."

She looked, then, at Marcus in the dock. He had aged into a portrait of their father-in-law, silver at the temples, jaw set in the family expression she'd once mistaken for grief and now knew for arithmetic. He did not look away from her. She gave him nothing back—not hatred, not forgiveness. Just the ledger, balanced.

The tide had given up its dead. The least she could do was give up hers.

* * *

Daniel Ashworth's room at St. Brendan's Hospice overlooked a garden gone brown and patient with autumn. The sea was visible past the hedge line if you stood close to the glass, a grey seam between two greys, and Freya thought he had probably chosen this room for exactly that—a window onto the thing that had taken Nell and, in its slow way, was taking him too.

He was thinner than the man she'd loved at twenty-six. The illness had carved him down to essentials: bright eyes, long hands, the same crooked smile that used to undo her in the stacks of the county library.

"You came," he said.

"You knew I would. You counted on worse than that."

"I counted on you being better than me." He patted the chair beside the bed. "Sit. I've got maybe two good conversations left in me and I'd rather spend one on you."

She sat. For a while neither of them spoke, and the radiator ticked like a slow clock.

"I'm not here to absolve you," Freya said at last. "I need you to know that before anything else. Twelve years ago you watched a woman drown—or leave a cove toward her drowning—and you said nothing. You wrote a false confession and kept it in a drawer like a loaded gun pointed at yourself, and you let me marry into a house built on her money. I can't forgive that, Daniel. I don't have it in me, and I'm not going to pretend I do just because you're dying."

His eyes didn't waver. "Good," he said softly. "Don't. Absolution's cheap. It lets everybody off before the work is done." He turned his head against the pillow to face her fully. "Ask me what you actually came to ask."

Freya looked at her hands. "Did you love her? Nell?"

"No. I barely knew her. I loved—" he stopped, restarted. "I loved you. And when Marcus figured out I'd seen him at the cove that night, he didn't threaten me. He offered me you. Told me if I kept quiet, there'd be no scandal, no investigation, no questions about the detective's wife's family. He said, *You want a life with her? This is the price.* And I paid it." His breath caught, steadied. "Every year I told myself I'd tell you. Every year I found a reason. Then I got sick and realized the confession letter was the only honest thing I owned, so I sent it, and God forgive me, I sent it knowing it would break your life open, because I couldn't carry it into the ground with me."

"He sent the second letter," Freya said. "To see if you'd stay bought."

"To see if I'd stay quiet. There's a difference, and it's the whole distance between us." Daniel smiled, tired and crooked. "How is he?"

"In custody. Charged. Pryce says the CPS wants to try it in the spring."

"Good. Good." He closed his eyes. When he opened them again they were wet, but his voice was level. "Freya. I'm not asking you to forgive me. I'm asking you to stop carrying me like a weight. Whatever we were—that was real, whatever came after. But you've got a life standing in a bookshop full of other people's stories, hiding behind a till, and you're too good at invisible. Stop it. Be seen. Sign your name to things."

"That's twice now someone's told me that."

"Then it's probably true." He reached out and took her hand, and she let him, and it felt like closing a book gently rather than slamming it. "Tell me about the shop. Tell me something ordinary. I want to go out on ordinary."

So she told him about the damp in the poetry section, and the boy who came in Tuesdays for nautical charts he couldn't afford and read standing up, and Imogen Pryce's terrible habit of bending book spines. He laughed twice, which she suspected cost him more than he'd admit. When she rose to leave, he held her hand a moment longer.

"The ledger," he said. "Close it. Not the case—the ledger. Some pages are meant to end."

Outside, the light was going gold over the hedge line, and Freya stood in the car park a long time before she drove home.

* * *

They reopened the bookshop on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays were chart-boy days and she wanted witnesses.

The fire damage had been confined to the back room, but smoke had threaded through everything, and for six weeks the shop had smelled like a burnt offering. Freya had repainted the walls herself—a pale grey-green called, absurdly, Sea Glass—and replaced the ruined shelving with pine that still smelled of the sawmill. The new shelf went in along the north wall, under the window where the light fell best.

She stenciled the label herself, in letters small enough to require leaning in:

**CASES THE TOWN GOT WRONG**

Onto it went the books she'd been quietly collecting all autumn. A local history with the drowning at Gullane Cove footnoted as *misadventure*, annotated in pencil now: *no*. The inquest transcript, bound and typed by Imogen Pryce, who had driven over on her day off to help shelve and who pretended she hadn't brought biscuits. A slim folder, copied twice, containing everything Freya's private file had survived the fire to keep—the mooring ring photographs, the fiber analysis, the ledger entries, the alibi sentence underlined three times in red.

And on the shelf's end, at eye height, a single framed page. The first page of her first anonymous case file, the one about the fisherman lost off the Head in '09, ruled accident, reopened by a stranger's careful notes and closed again as negligence. At the bottom, where for fifteen years she had written only *A friend of the truth*, she had pasted a slip of paper over the pseudonym. In fresh ink, in her own unmistakable hand:

*F. Oyelaran.*

Pryce noticed it first, naturally. She stood in front of the frame with a custard cream halfway to her mouth and said, "About time," in the tone of a woman who had been waiting years and would deny it under oath.

The town came slowly at first, then in numbers. People who had never crossed the threshold came to stand at the new shelf and read. Mrs. Calloway, whose son's disappearance had been filed as *left of his own accord*, asked Freya, very quietly, whether she took requests. Freya said yes. She opened a fresh notebook while the woman watched, and at the top of the first page she wrote the young man's name, and beneath it her own, and dated it.

Word traveled, as word does in coastal towns, faster than buses. By December there were three notebooks. By the new year, seven. Pryce started leaving photocopied files in a canvas bag on the doorstep with no note, which was its own kind of correspondence. The chart-boy—his name was Ellis—asked if he could shelve things properly, and she hired him for Saturdays and taught him the ledger system her late husband had used, though she rewrote its rules: every entry signed, every entry dated, nothing redacted, nothing carried forward unexamined.

On a raw bright morning in February, a year almost to the week since the twin letters arrived, Freya stood at the counter with the shop's own accounting ledger open before her. The old one—Nell's inheritance, Marcus's mortgage, twelve years of quiet complicity—she had burned in a proper fire at the harbour, witnessed by Pryce and half the Tuesday regulars, and nobody had needed to say why.

This was a new ledger. Clean paper. She entered the day's takings, balanced the column, and then, out of habit older than the shop itself, moved to sign the pseudonym.

Her pen stopped.

She looked at the blank space for a long moment. Through the front window she could see the harbour, the grey seam of the sea beyond it, calm today, keeping what it keeps and giving back what it gives. Somewhere two towns over, Daniel was down to his last conversation or already past it; she had sent him a postcard of the shop with the new shelf visible in the corner, and no note, because some messages are complete without one.

Freya wrote her name. Freya Oyelaran, in full, in ink, where anyone could read it.

The bell above the door rang. A woman stood in the entrance, rain beaded on a coat that had travelled far, holding something wrapped in a plastic bag against her chest like a parcel she'd been carrying a long way.

"Are you the one who signs her name?" the woman asked. "They said—you're the one who looks at the cases nobody will touch."

Freya set down her pen and turned the ledger toward the light.

"Yes," she said. "Come in. Tell me everything."
