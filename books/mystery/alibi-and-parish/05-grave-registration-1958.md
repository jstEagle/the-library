## 5. Grave Registration 1958

The retired gravedigger lived in a narrow house at the end of Cobbler's Lane, where the fog came earliest and stayed longest, as if it had claimed the property decades ago and merely tolerated the tenant. His name was Emeric Sloane, and Jude found him because everyone in Dravenmoor Hollow was a door that opened onto another door—except Emeric, who turned out to be a window.

Jude had gotten his name from the barmaid at the Wakeful Crow, who'd mentioned it in the offhand way people here mentioned things, with the faint cadence of recitation: *Old Emeric dug half the parish before his hands gave out. He'd remember, if anyone would.* The same cadence Jude had been hearing all week, the town's memory passing through its residents like light through stained glass, arriving colored by whoever had installed it.

But Emeric Sloane answered his door at nine in the morning looking like a man who had never once rehearsed anything in eighty-one years. He was long and stooped, with forearms still corded from sixty years of spade work, and eyes the pale gray of weathered slate. He looked at Jude for a long moment without speaking.

"You're the podcast man," he said finally. "The one they're all whispering about."

"That depends on what they're whispering."

"They're whispering that you ask questions." Emeric stepped back from the doorway. "Come in before Mrs. Tulliver sees you. She reports to the records office by lunch."

*Reports to the records office.* Jude filed the phrase away as he crossed the threshold into a house that smelled of pipe smoke, coal dust, and something older—lamp oil, maybe, or the particular mustiness of a life kept deliberately small. The front room held two chairs, a stove, and a wall of photographs. Jude's eye went to them immediately, because walls of photographs were confessionals for men like Emeric Sloane. Men who had spent their lives watching other people's worst days and been paid to keep quiet about it.

There he was, younger, standing beside an open grave with three other men, shovels planted like rifles at a military funeral. And there—a grainy shot of a coffin being lowered, the date penciled on the border in a hand gone brown with age: *October 1958.*

Jude felt his pulse tick up. "May I?"

Emeric followed his gaze. He didn't answer right away. He lowered himself into one of the chairs with the careful economy of a man whose body had become a negotiation, and when he spoke, it was to the stove rather than to Jude.

"Forty years I dug graves in this parish. You want to know what I learned?" He didn't wait for an answer. "Nobody remembers a funeral. They remember the weather, and what somebody wore, and whether the widow cried proper. The grave itself—" he tapped his temple— "that lives here. In the diggers. Everybody else forgets the ground. We're the only ones who don't."

"I'm not here about the ground," Jude said. "I'm here about the paperwork."

Something moved behind the slate eyes. Not surprise. Recognition, maybe—the look of a man hearing a question he'd been waiting half a century for someone brave enough or foolish enough to ask.

"Sit down, then," Emeric said.

* * *

Jude sat, and Emeric talked, and what emerged over the next hour was a portrait of October 1958 that matched nothing in the official record—which was, Jude understood now, precisely the point.

"The burial of Aldous Parish," Emeric said, "was the biggest thing this town ever buried. Three hundred mourners. Bells. The council came down from their offices and stood at the graveside with their hats off like it was a coronation." He snorted. "And I'll tell you what nobody else will, because everybody else who could tell you is either dead or lying. That coffin was wrong."

"Wrong how?"

"Heavy." Emeric held up his ruined hands, fingers thick as fence posts. "I've lifted a thousand coffins. A man of Aldous Parish's size, embalmed, boxed in that oak they used—you know the weight in your shoulders before it ever leaves the trestles. This one took six of us, and two of us were straining. My cousin Bram slipped a disc carrying it and never dug again." He let that settle. "Six men for one coffin. And every one of us told ourselves the same story—rich man, double-lined casket, lead sealing, of course it's heavy. That's what we were told to think, so that's what we thought. That's how it works here. They tell you what to think, and thinking it feels like loyalty."

Jude's recorder lay in his pocket, running. He'd stopped asking permission after the third interview; the town's uniformity of speech had taught him that much. Now he said, carefully: "You're saying there might not have been a body in that coffin. Before yesterday, I mean. Before the stones."

"I'm saying I've wondered about it for sixty years, son, and I stopped wondering out loud around 1964." Emeric's gaze sharpened. "Because that's when they amended the record."

The room went very quiet. Outside, fog pressed against the windows like something listening.

"The record," Jude repeated.

"Burial register. Parish keeps it—the old volumes, pre-computer, the ones nobody's supposed to touch but everybody knows Maren Voss touches." Emeric leaned forward, and the chair creaked under him. "I wasn't supposed to see it. But in '64 I was asked to re-sink the headstone—frost heave, they said—and while I was waiting on the mason I was left alone in the records room with my tea. Twenty minutes, maybe. I read the entry for Parish. I'd earned the right; I'd dug the hole." His jaw worked. "There was ink on that page that hadn't been there in '58. Two amendments. Corrections, they'd call them. One dated March 1964. And the handwriting on the second one—it wasn't old. It wasn't faded. It looked like it had dried last week."

Jude kept his voice level, though his heart had begun to climb. "You saw this recently? The register?"

"I see nothing anymore. My eyes are gravel." Emeric smiled without humor. "But my niece works in the office. Cleaning, filing. She has eyes, and she has a temper, and she doesn't like Maren Voss any more than the rest of us like being told what our own grandfathers said. Last month she tells me—casual, over Sunday dinner—that the Parish volume was out on the conservation table. Being *conserved.* Alone. After hours." He spread his hands. "In sixty years, that volume was conserved twice. Once in '64. Once last month. Draw your own map, podcast man."

Jude drew it. It was a short map with a terrible destination.

*Someone altered the record recently. Not in some distant decade when memories softened and witnesses thinned. Last year. Which meant the empty coffin wasn't a historical mystery at all. It was ongoing work.*

* * *

He spent the walk back into town composing and discarding plans. The parish records office opened at ten; it closed at four; between those hours it was administered by Maren Voss with the serene totality of a lighthouse administering a coastline. Jude had already been refused once—politely, thoroughly, with a citation of heritage protocols he suspected she'd written herself. A second refusal would put her on alert. A third would make her certain.

So he wouldn't ask.

The problem was the permit. Research access to the restricted registers required a signed authorization from the county heritage board, and the county heritage board was forty miles away and famously slow, and Jude had neither the time nor the intention of being slow. What he had instead was a laptop, a scanner, a decent eye for official letterheads acquired during three investigations' worth of document forensics, and a growing conviction that the rules of Dravenmoor Hollow had been written by someone who broke them nightly.

He told himself it was proportionate. He told himself a forged research permit was a pebble against the mountain of falsification someone had already built. He told himself these things in the voice he used for justifying things, which was, he'd learned, the most dangerous voice he owned.

By midnight, sitting in his rented cottage above the harbor with the fog scratching at the glass, he had a permit. The letterhead was good. The signature was plausible. The reference number followed the format he'd extrapolated from two publicly posted authorizations. He printed it on paper stock he'd bought from the stationer—who had asked no questions, because in Dravenmoor Hollow questions flowed in exactly one direction—and he set it on the table under the lamp and looked at it for a long time.

Three accidents. Three families destroyed by the truth he'd dragged into daylight. Three sets of threats that had followed him across two states until hiding had started to feel less like cowardice and more like survival. He'd sworn, coming here, that he was done. Done digging, done pulling threads, done being the man who found the body everyone else needed not to exist.

And yet.

He picked up the permit. The lamp hummed. Somewhere below, the river moved through the dark toward the quarry country, patient as a debt.

*One look,* he told himself. *One page. Then I decide whether this town deserves my curiosity or my silence.*

Both statements were lies, and he knew it, and he went to bed anyway.

* * *

The records office occupied the ground floor of the old tithe barn, all stone and low beams and the smell of cold paper. At 10:04 the next morning, Jude pushed through the door with the forged permit in his breast pocket and his face arranged into the mild, slightly bored expression of academic bureaucracy.

Maren Voss was not at her desk.

Her desk was—he noted with the part of his mind that never stopped cataloging—immaculate. Ledger squared to the desk edge. Fountain pen capped and aligned beside it at a precise angle. A cup of tea, untouched, still steaming. She had left it minutes ago. Through the open door of the archive room beyond, he could hear the soft sounds of someone moving among shelves.

Jude stood in the empty office for the length of three heartbeats, weighing the ethics of simply walking back there, and decided that ship had sailed somewhere over the county line last night. He walked.

The archive room ran deep into the building, shelving receding into shadow, the air cooler by degrees. And there, at the far end, beneath a single hanging bulb, stood Maren Voss—with the Parish volume open on the conservation table before her, a sheet of archival tissue halfway laid over a page, her hands poised above it like a surgeon's.

She didn't startle. That was the first thing. He came around the shelving into her line of sight and she did not startle, did not flush, did not do anything so human as react. She simply looked up at him with those calm, deep-set eyes and said:

"You're early. The office doesn't open for visitors until ten."

"It's five past."

"Then you're late for everything else." She laid the tissue down over the page, completing the gesture she'd begun, and closed the volume with both hands. "Mr. Dravenmoor. You were declined access last Tuesday."

"And I've since obtained authorization." He produced the permit. "County heritage board. Restricted registers, supervised handling."

She took the paper. She read it the way she seemed to do everything—thoroughly, unhurriedly, as though time were a resource she owned outright. And Jude watched her read it and understood, with a cold spreading certainty, that she knew. That she recognized the letterhead's slight kerning error, or the reference number's impossible sequence, or simply the fact that no genuine authorization would arrive this fast. That she was letting him stand there holding his forgery while she decided—not whether to expose him, but what exposing him would cost *her*.

Then she handed it back.

"Supervised handling," she said. "Which means I remain in the room. Which means you may examine the register for thirty minutes, take no photographs, make no tracings, and remove nothing." She turned toward the conservation table and reopened the volume with the tenderness of someone uncovering a sleeping child. "The Parish register, I assume. Everyone wants the Parish register lately."

*Lately.* The word snagged in him. "Has someone else requested it?"

"People request many things." She drew on white cotton gloves and gestured to the stool across the table. "Sit, Mr. Dravenmoor. Thirty minutes begins now."

* * *

The volume was enormous—eleven by seventeen inches, bound in green leather gone brittle at the spine, its pages ruled in faded red and filled with the copperplate of successive registrars. Jude turned to October 1958 with hands that were not quite steady, and there it was: the burial entry for Aldous Parish, founder, aged sixty-one, interred the twenty-third of October in the presence of the parish council.

And beneath it, in the margin, two amendments.

The first was dated in a fine, controlled hand: *March 1964 — Correction to date of death per certification received from abroad. Original entry erroneous.* The ink had aged with the page, browned and settled into the paper's tooth. Six years after the burial. Someone had reached back into 1958 and adjusted where—and implicitly, *whether*—Parish had died.

The second amendment sat below it, and Jude's breath caught audibly enough that Maren Voss glanced over from her desk in the corner.

It was dated fourteen months ago. The ink was still faintly glossy, the hand modern, ballpoint rather than fountain pen—but the *letterforms*. Jude bent close, his forensic instinct firing on every cylinder. The distinctive open-topped *a*. The way the descender of the *g* curled left instead of right. The peculiar flat-topped *t* crossed with a single impatient stroke.

He had seen this handwriting once before—photographed through glass, two days ago, on the visitor log outside the exhumation site, where someone had signed in ahead of the ceremony. He'd taken the picture idly, the way he photographed everything, and hadn't thought about it since.

Now he thought about it very much indeed.

"Amendments require authorization," he said, keeping his voice casual, his eyes on the page. "Who signs off on corrections to a burial register?"

"The registrar. Historically, the parish council chairman." Maren's voice came evenly from the corner. "In practice, the records office prepares the correction documentation and submits it. It is a clerical process, Mr. Dravenmoor. Less dramatic than your profession prefers."

"Clerical." He traced the air above the 1964 amendment without touching it. "Correcting a death date six years after burial. That's an unusual clerical event."

"It happens when documents surface. Death certificates arrive late from foreign jurisdictions. Errors are discovered. Paper is imperfect." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat. "As you of all people know."

He looked up. Her face offered nothing but professional patience, and yet something underneath it—the faintest pressure behind the words, like water finding a crack. Did she know about the forgery in his pocket? Was this a warning, or a taunt, or simply his own paranoia reading texture into blankness?

His phone buzzed in his pocket. He ignored it. Then it buzzed again—twice, rapid, the pattern he'd set for messages from his one remaining contact in the true-crime world, a researcher named Dev who ran records requests for him from three states away.

Thirty minutes, Maren had said. Twenty-two remained. He could feel her attention on his back like a hand.

He memorized instead. The amendment dates. The letterforms. The certification language of the 1964 correction—*per certification received from abroad*—which implied a death certificate from overseas, which implied that in 1964 someone had produced documentary proof that Aldous Parish had died somewhere else than in the coffin Emeric Sloane had strained to lower into the ground. A dead man certified dead twice, in two places, six years apart.

And fourteen months ago, someone had touched this page again. Recently. Deliberately. While the town slept and the fog rolled in off the causeway.

Jude closed the volume gently and stood. "Thank you. That's all I need today."

"So soon." Maren rose from her desk, removing her gloves finger by finger. "Most researchers find the registers rewarding. Layers upon layers. This town keeps its history well."

"Yes," Jude said, meeting her eyes. "I'm starting to see exactly how well it's kept. And by whom."

For the first time, something flickered in Maren Voss's face—not guilt, not fear, but a kind of weary recognition, the look of a chess player watching an opponent finally notice the board. She walked him to the door. The bell above it chimed as he stepped out into the gray morning, and her voice followed him, pleasant and precise:

"Do give my regards to the county board, Mr. Dravenmoor. They're usually slower than this."

The door closed behind him.

Jude stood on the steps, the fog swallowing the street, his triumph at confirming the amendments curdling fast into something colder. Because the handwriting on that second amendment belonged to someone alive, active, and embedded—and fourteen months ago, long before any heritage ceremony was announced, long before anyone could have known the coffin would be opened—they had already begun preparing for the day the world looked inside it.

His phone buzzed again. He pulled it out. Dev's message loaded, and the bottom dropped out of the morning:

*Ran the name you sent on the visitor log. Jude—check the signature against the 1958 case file annotations when you get it. Same hand. I'd bet the farm. Also: the county heritage board says they've never issued your permit number. Whoever signed your authorization letter isn't real. Neither, apparently, is the department that countersigned it. Somebody's playing office inside your office. Call me.*

Jude read it twice, the fog beading cold on the screen, and understood that the permit he'd forged had been forged over—counterfeited by someone who had watched him counterfeit, or who had simply known, always, exactly what he would do next.

He looked back at the records office. Behind the darkened glass, a shape moved briefly past the window and was gone.

The killer wasn't history. The killer was working.
