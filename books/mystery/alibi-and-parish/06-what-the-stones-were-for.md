## 6. What the Stones Were For

The stones weighed eleven pounds each.

Maren Voss knew this because she had weighed one herself, three days after the exhumation, in the back room of the parish records office, with a brass scale that had been weighing christening records and deed boxes since before either of them had meaning. Eleven pounds, river-smoothed, gray-green, cold even in a warm room the way certain stones stay cold. She had turned it over in her hands and felt the faint striations of water on its face—decades of a river's patience pressed into mineral—and she had thought: *you were not supposed to be found.*

She set it down now on her desk, beside the ledger, and looked at it the way a doctor looks at an X-ray that confirms what she already feared.

Around her, the records office went about its morning as if nothing had happened. Mrs. Calloway was answering phones in the front room, her voice pitched to that particular register of civic grief the town had adopted all week—the exhumation had been a spectacle, and Dravenmoor Hollow loved a spectacle the way a drowning man loves driftwood. Two volunteers were re-shelving land surveys. The radiator ticked. Outside the window, fog lay against the glass like something waiting to be let in.

Maren opened the ledger.

It was not an official ledger. The official ledgers lived in the archive basement under lock and catalog numbers, and those were clean, those were *correct*, every entry typed or written in the careful hand of whatever clerk had held the pen that year. This ledger was hers alone—a marbled composition book she'd bought in 1981 for ninety cents, its spine cracked white with use. In it she kept what the official record could not hold: discrepancies. Margins. The places where the town's story and the paper's story failed to meet.

She had been keeping it for forty years. It had never once been this full.

*October 14.* She wrote in pencil, always pencil, because graphite did not fade and could be erased, though she almost never erased anything. Erasure was a sin she understood better than most, and she practiced it only when there was no other way.

*Coffin contents: 34 stones by count of witnesses (Hollis says 33; Hollis is 81 and counts like a man counting his own regrets). Average weight 11 lbs. Total approx. 374 lbs. A coffin built for a man of Aldous Parish's frame would carry such a load without complaint. Whoever filled it wanted it to feel occupied. Wanted the pallbearers to strain.*

She stopped. Read it back. Then added:

*Wanted the pallbearers to remember the weight. That was the point. Memory needs mass.*

Underneath, she wrote the question she had been circling for three days like a bird over open water:

*Where did they come from?*

She knew the answer already. That was the trouble with knowing things. You could spend years arranging your knowledge into neat shelves, catalogued and dusted, and then one morning a coffin comes up out of the ground full of river rock and every shelf falls over at once.

But knowing was not proving, and proving was not the same as controlling, and Maren Voss had spent her whole life learning the difference between the three.

* * *

The quarry sat four miles east of town, past the last of the founding families' fences, where the causeway road narrowed and the fog came off the water so thick that drivers learned to navigate by sound. The Halloway Quarry had closed in 1959. Everyone knew that the way everyone knew the date of the founding festival: it was one of the town's fixed stars, a fact that required no source because it had been repeated so long it had worn its own citation smooth.

*Closed in 1959,* Maren thought, driving out with the ledger on the passenger seat like a companion. *The same year Aldous Parish vanished from public life.*

Vanished was the wrong word. Vanished implied mystery, and there had never been any mystery, officially. Aldous Parish had gone abroad for his health—his lungs, the story went, ruined by the damp—and had died in 1961 in a clinic outside Geneva, where he was buried, where his grave remained to this day, unvisited, unverifiable, and very far away. The coffin in St. Brendan's churchyard was a cenotaph, a courtesy, a place for the town to put its flowers. Every schoolchild in Dravenmoor Hollow learned this before they learned their times tables. Maren had taught half of them herself, in the years when the historical society still ran school programs, before budget cuts and before she decided that children asked too many questions in the wrong order.

*A man who died abroad and was buried abroad does not need a coffin full of local stone.*

That sentence had arrived in her head at two in the morning, three nights ago, and had not left. It sat in her chest now like a swallowed coal.

The quarry road ended at a chain across two posts, the chain rusted to the color of dried blood. She parked and walked in. The fog was thinner here, pulled apart by wind coming off the ridge, and the quarry opened below her like a wound that had healed wrong: terraced walls of cut granite descending to a floor of pooled rainwater, green-black, still. Equipment skeletons rusted among the spoil heaps. A derrick leaned against nothing. Nature had begun the slow work of pretending men had never been here, and nature, Maren thought, was the greatest archivist of all—it destroyed everything eventually, without malice, without preference.

She descended the switchback path with the care of a woman who had not come this way in decades but whose feet remembered it anyway.

At the bottom, near the flooded workings, the ground changed. Granite gave way to rounded cobble—river stone, dredged and sorted here during the quarry's brief and disastrous experiment with aggregate, the one that had bankrupted old Halloway and shut the place down. The cobbles lay in a long pale spill along the eastern wall, exactly where the sorting beds had been, exactly where they had lain for sixty-five years, undisturbed.

Except not entirely undisturbed.

Maren knelt. The spill of stones thinned toward the wall, and there—at the edge, where the sorted pile met the raw scree—was a gap. Not a large one. A patch of bare earth perhaps two meters square, the soil beneath compressed, the moss around it torn in a way that rain and time had softened but not erased.

Someone had taken stones from here. Recently enough that the scar hadn't fully healed. Long ago enough that—

No. She wouldn't let herself finish that arithmetic. Some sums you did not do until you had no choice.

She took out her phone—she owned one, she used it grudgingly, the way she used the microwave—and photographed the gap from four angles. Then she took a stone from the outer edge of the remaining pile, one that matched the ones in her office drawer, and wrapped it in a handkerchief, and stood there in the bottom of the dead quarry with the fog lifting off the black water, and felt the truth settle onto her shoulders the way the fog settled onto the town: gradually, then completely.

Thirty-four stones. Carried out of here by someone who knew this place, who knew the quarry road was chained and forgotten, who knew that no one in Dravenmoor Hollow came east anymore because the east held nothing the town wanted to remember.

And the quarry had closed in 1959. And Aldous Parish had vanished from public life in 1959. And the coffin had been sealed, according to the burial record, in October of 1958—

Maren stopped walking.

She stood very still in the middle of the quarry floor, and the wind moved her hair, and somewhere above her a crow said one flat syllable and quit.

*Sealed in 1958. Filled with stones from a quarry that didn't process river aggregate until 1959.*

Unless the record was wrong. Unless the sealing date had been amended. Unless—

Her own handwriting swam up in her memory, an entry from years ago, one of the first in the marbled ledger: *Burial record amended twice. 1964. Reason unstated. Initials illegible.* And the second amendment, the recent one, the one that had made her blood go cold in a way she had not permitted herself to write down: *Amended again last year. By me. Authorized by no one. I signed my own name to a lie and told myself it was housekeeping.*

She had done that. She had done that eight months ago, in this office, at this desk, with the door locked, because a man had come to her with a request she could not refuse—or rather, with a request she could have refused, technically, legally, easily—but refusing him would have meant opening a door she had spent forty years keeping shut, and Maren Voss was many things but she was not a woman who opened doors.

She climbed out of the quarry. She drove back through the thinning fog with both hands on the wheel and the wrapped stone riding on the seat beside the ledger like a witness.

* * *

That evening she did not sleep.

This was not unusual. Sleep had been negotiating with her for years and losing. But tonight the insomnia had a shape, a gravity; it pulled her out of bed at one in the morning and down the stairs of the narrow house she'd lived in since 1979, past the framed photographs of a town she had curated the way other people curated gardens, into the kitchen, where she boiled water she didn't want and sat with the ledger open under the stove light.

She read back through the October entries. The exhumation. The crowd. The gasp—three hundred people inhaling at once, a sound she had heard live and then heard again on the true-crime man's recording, because of course Jude Dravenmoor had recorded it, of course he had, the man carried his recorder the way priests used to carry relics, and his voice on the audio had been steady while everyone else's cracked, and that steadiness frightened her more than any accusation could have.

*He is not like the others,* she wrote. *The others ask questions to hear themselves. He asks questions to hear the answer. There is a difference, and the difference is dangerous.*

She underlined it. Then she hated herself for underlining it, because underlining meant she believed it, and believing it meant she would have to decide what to do about it, and deciding things at one in the morning was how mistakes got made.

The kettle ticked as it cooled. The house creaked through its nightly liturgy of settling beams.

*Maren,* she wrote to herself, in the margin, the way she sometimes did when the ledger needed to hear her name spoken aloud, even by her own hand. *You are seventy-one years old. You have spent your entire adult life being the person who knows where everything is filed. You are the town's memory. And you are sitting in your kitchen at one in the morning trying to decide whether to erase yourself.*

Because that was what burning the page would be. Not destroying evidence—she was too good an archivist to pretend otherwise. Destroying evidence was what guilty people did, and she was not guilty, she had told herself that so many times the sentence had lost its texture, become smooth as river stone. What she was doing was *triage*. A historian's job was not to preserve everything; a historian's job was to preserve the right things, in the right order, so that the story survived intact. A surgeon cuts to save. An editor cuts to clarify. She had burned documents before. Twice. Both times for reasons she could defend in front of God, if not in front of a court.

The page she was considering now was different, and she knew it was different, and the knowing sat in her stomach like the stone itself.

It was the page where she had recorded the second amendment to the burial record. Date, time, her own signature, and beneath it, in her smallest hand, the reason—not the official reason, which was *clerical correction*, but the real one, the one that existed nowhere else in the world except in this kitchen and in the memory of a conversation that had happened in this very room, eight months ago, with a man whose name she had never once written down.

Not because she couldn't remember it. Because writing it would make him real on paper, and everything real on paper could someday be read.

She looked at the page for a long time.

Then she got up, took the iron pan from the hook by the stove—the one she used for cornbread, the one that had belonged to her mother, who had never known what her daughter became—and held the corner of the ledger page over the burner's blue flame until the fire took it, and carried it, burning, to the sink, and ran the tap, and watched the ash go down the drain in a gray spiral.

The smoke alarm did not go off. Small mercies.

She stood at the sink afterward with the water running over her wrists, colder than she'd expected, and she thought about the man's voice saying, gently, reasonably, the way he always said everything: *Maren. The town can survive a lot of things. It cannot survive the truth arriving all at once, unmanaged. Let me manage it. Let us manage it together. For the town.*

For the town. Forty years, and those three words still worked on her like a key in a well-oiled lock.

She dried her hands. She returned to the ledger and wrote, in fresh ink, on the page where the burned one had been, a replacement entry—clean, official, false:

*Oct 17. Reviewed burial record amendments. No irregularities found. File closed.*

Her pen hovered. Then she added one line more, small, at the bottom, where only she would look:

*The stones came from Halloway Quarry. The quarry closed in 1959. I am not ready to know what that means.*

She closed the ledger. She turned off the stove light.

In the dark of her kitchen, Maren Voss sat awake while the town slept its curated sleep, and she listened to the fog press against the windows, and she waited for morning the way you wait for a verdict—knowing already, in the place where you keep the things you cannot say, that the verdict was going to be guilty, and that the crime was not what she had done, but whom she had done it for.

Upstairs, on her nightstand, the unsent letter she had written in March—*Dear Papa, they are talking about opening the ground, and I don't know what to do*—waited with its stamp unlicked, addressed to a man dead these sixty-three years, in a grave that had never existed at all.
