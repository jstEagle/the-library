## 9. Letters to a Dead Man

The basement of the parish records office smelled the way Maren Voss imagined forgiveness would smell: cold stone, old paper, and the faint mineral breath of the river that ran somewhere beneath it all, patient as a debt.

She came down at seven, before the morning staff arrived, the way she had come down every weekday for forty years. The fluorescent tube above the stairwell flickered twice and steadied—she'd reported it to facilities eleven times, and each time they'd promised to fix it, and each time she'd privately hoped they wouldn't. A light that couldn't decide whether to shine was useful in a place like this. It kept people moving along.

Her keys made their small ceremonial sounds against her hip. Archive door. Inner cage. The climate-controlled room where the founding documents slept under glass and argon. And then—the last door, unmarked, painted the same gray as the wall behind it, so that anyone who didn't know it was there would walk past it forever.

She let herself into the narrow space between the shelving units and the foundation wall, a corridor barely wide enough for her shoulders, and knelt on the padded mat she'd placed there decades ago, when her knees were younger and her grief was louder.

The letters lived in a cedar chest that had once held christening gowns. She knew this because she'd bought it herself, secondhand, from a widow on Fenwick Street who had no idea what she was selling. Forty years of letters, bundled by decade with cotton tape, each envelope addressed in her own hand to a man who could not receive them:

*Aldous Parish, Esq.*
*Dravenmoor Hollow*

No street. No postal code. There had never needed to be one. He was the town; the town was him; and she had spent her whole life writing to a place and calling it a father.

She untied the oldest bundle—1979 to 1984—and drew out the first letter, though she did not need to read it. She knew every word the way other people knew prayers.

*Dear Father. I saw your photograph in the library today, the one where you're standing at the quarry gate with your hand on the iron. I put my hand where yours is. The metal was cold. I hope your hand was warm that day. I hope someone made sure of it.*

She had been nine years old when she wrote that. Nine, and already careful, because her mother had told her—once, only once, in the kitchen with the radio turned up loud—that the name Parish was not to be spoken above a whisper, that it was a door best kept shut, that some doors opened onto weather no house could survive.

Maren had spent her childhood learning which doors those were.

* * *

The trouble with forty years of letters was that they formed a record, and Maren Voss was, above all things, a keeper of records. She could chart her own life through them the way she charted the town's through its ledgers: the angry years of the eighties, when the ink pressed hard enough to tear the page. *You chose them. You chose the town over me, and then you chose death over both of us, and even your death wasn't real.* The desperate years of the nineties, when the handwriting loosened, went soft, went round. *I don't want the money. I want to know if you ever drove past the house on Corben Lane. I want to know if you ever saw me.*

And then the years after her mother died, when the letters changed again—not questions now, but reports. Dispatches from a country of one.

*I've taken the archivist position. They gave me the keys to everything. You would laugh—you always said records were just gossip that learned to behave. I'm good at it, Father. I'm better than good. I can make a town remember whatever it needs to remember, and forget the rest, and everyone thanks me for it. Is that a gift or is it what you left me instead of a name?*

She set that one down with hands that were perfectly steady, because she had trained them to be, and looked at the newest bundle—the current decade, still growing—and felt the familiar arithmetic begin in her chest.

Forty years of letters. One hundred and ninety-three envelopes. She had counted them last night, lying awake in the blue hour before dawn, counting the way she counted everything when the thinking got too large: ledgers, gravestones, lies.

One hundred and ninety-three letters to a dead man who was not dead.

That was the sentence she could never finish. Her mind approached it the way a tongue approaches a broken tooth, again and again, unable to leave it alone, unable to bear the contact. He was not dead. She had known it since she was twenty-six, since the night her mother finally broke—not loudly, not dramatically, but the way old dams break, all at once and without apology—and told her the truth in the kitchen on Corben Lane while the radio played something cheerful about harvest festivals.

*He's alive, Maren. He's been alive the whole time. He wrote to me once a year, unsigned, postmarked from places he'd never been. He asked about you. Every year he asked about you. And every year I burned the letter, because he asked us to burn it, because knowing was the one thing we couldn't afford.*

Maren had sat with that for a long time. Then she had asked the only question that mattered: *Where does he write from?*

And her mother, who had guarded the lie for thirty years and was tired down to the marrow of it, had said: *From nowhere. That's the point. He writes from nowhere so that no one can ever go there.*

* * *

By eight-fifteen she had restored the bundles to the chest, locked the gray door, and climbed the stairs into the ordinary morning, where Mrs. Tallow from the heritage committee was waiting at the front desk with the particular smile of a woman carrying a complaint dressed as a compliment.

"Maren! Just the person. We're finalizing the ceremony program, and there's some question about the order of proceedings. Aldous Parish's descendant line—isn't there supposed to be a reading from the family?"

"The family line ended," Maren said. "There are no direct descendants."

"No descendants at all? A man like that?" Mrs. Tallow's eyebrows performed their small theater of sympathy. "It seems sad, somehow. All that history and nobody to inherit it."

"History doesn't need heirs," Maren said pleasantly. "It needs custodians."

Mrs. Tallow laughed, thanked her, and left with her program notes, satisfied, and Maren stood at the desk watching the door swing shut and thought: *custodian*. Yes. That was the word she had built her whole life inside, the word that made duty out of damage. A custodian keeps what others would lose. A custodian is trusted precisely because she wants nothing.

Except that it wasn't true anymore, was it. It hadn't been true for three weeks, since the coffin came up out of the ground full of river stones and three hundred mouths fell open at once, and Jude Dravenmoor raised his little recorder like a man offering a toast.

She walked back through the archive hall, past the glass cases, past the framed photograph of Aldous Parish at the quarry gate—her father's face, the face she had pressed her nine-year-old palm against in another building, another century—and stopped in front of it the way she did most mornings, briefly, formally, a daughter paying respects to a portrait that did not know her.

"You should have stayed buried," she said softly. "Whoever you were in that box. You should have stayed buried, and I should have let you."

But that was the flaw in her, the crack running through the custodian, and she knew it: she had never once been able to let anything stay buried. Not paper, not names, not the truth. She collected it all, catalogued it all, sealed it in acid-free folders in the dark—and keeping a thing was only loving it slowly, over years, until the love became indistinguishable from the hoarding.

Somewhere upstairs, the phone was ringing. Somewhere across town, Jude Dravenmoor was asking his questions in that gentle, terrible voice of his, the voice of a man who had learned that people confess to kindness faster than to force. She had watched him work the wake two weeks ago, moving through the crowd like water finding cracks, and she had recognized him instantly—not as an investigator but as a fellow archivist. He kept records too. His just happened to be made of sound.

He would find the burial register amendments eventually. She had amended them herself—1964, to establish the exhumation protocol; last year, to prepare the ground for the ceremony—and a man like Dravenmoor would read those amendments the way she read them, as fingerprints. Fresh ones.

Which meant the letters had to move.

* * *

She waited until lunchtime, when the office emptied to the sandwich hour, and went down again with a canvas document case under her arm. In the gray-door room she knelt and began transferring the bundles, decade by decade, into the case, and it was during the transfer—during the handling of 1991, the year of the soft handwriting—that she found the letter she had forgotten.

It was not in her hand.

It was unsigned, postmarked from nowhere—or rather from a forwarding office in a port city four thousand miles away, a place chosen for its anonymity the way a stage is chosen for its lighting. Dated fourteen years ago. The only letter that had ever come the other way.

*Maren. If you are reading this, then your mother is gone, and I am sorry for the order of things. I will not sign this, and you will not answer it, and we will both understand why. But I want you to know one thing, and then I will stop writing, because stopping is the only protection I have left to give you. What I did at the quarry, I did to survive. What your mother did after, she did to survive. What you do now—you get to choose. That is more than either of us had. Choose carefully. Some stories keep a person alive, and some stories bury her. —A friend of the town*

She had read it perhaps fifty times over fourteen years, and it had never once said what she wanted it to say. No apology. No tenderness. Only that terrible gift, *you get to choose*, delivered by a man who had chosen murder and called it survival, and who had then spent sixty years letting a stranger rot in his grave while the town sang hymns over the wrong bones.

And yet.

And yet she had chosen too, hadn't she? She had chosen the ledger over the truth, the seal over the story, the curated memory over the raw wound. She had amended the register. She had rehearsed the town's grief until three hundred people gasped in unison. She had done it all believing she was protecting something—but protecting what? Him? The town? Or the one small country where she belonged, the archive itself, the only room in the world where she had ever been necessary?

The canvas case was nearly full. One bundle remained on the mat: the current decade, the letters of the last five years, written to a man she now knew was alive somewhere, aging somewhere, dying maybe, in a place called nowhere.

She looked at them for a long moment.

Then she took the top letter from the bundle—the one she'd finished at two in the morning, three days after the exhumation—and unfolded it, and read it standing in the dark between the shelves, in the smell of stone and river, in the flicker of the light that couldn't decide whether to shine.

*Dear Father. They found the stones today. Three hundred people watched, and I watched them watch, and I have never felt so much like your daughter as I did in that moment—because you taught me, without ever teaching me, that the truth is a thing you dig up only when you're prepared to kill whoever's holding the shovel. A podcaster has arrived. His name is Dravenmoor. He asks questions gently. I think he may be the shovel. I don't yet know whether I'm going to hand him the truth or bury him with it. Forgive me. Or don't. You never forgave anyone, including yourself, and look how well it worked.*

She refolded it along its worn creases and slid it into the case with the rest, and zipped the canvas closed, and stood there in the dark holding forty years of her own heart in archival-grade storage.

The plan had been simple: move the letters to the safe deposit box in Harrowgate, off-site, beyond any subpoena, beyond any podcaster with a forged research permit and gentle eyes. Simple. Clean. Custodial.

Instead she found herself kneeling again, opening the case, taking out only the newest bundle—the last five years—and carrying it alone to the far corner of the room, where the foundation wall met the shelving unit, where a loose panel she had installed herself concealed a cavity in the old stonework wide enough for a cedar box, a bundle of letters, and a wooden crate the length of a man's forearm that had not been opened since the night she'd carried it down here, twenty-two years ago, wrapped in a quilt, from a house on Corben Lane that no longer existed.

She set the letters beside the crate. She did not touch the crate. She never touched the crate.

Through the floor, faintly, she heard the front door of the office open and close, footsteps crossing the reception hall, and a voice she had begun to hear in her sleep asking Mrs. Ellery at the desk, in that gentle terrible tone, whether the archive kept any personal papers from the founding families. Correspondence, maybe. Diaries. Letters.

Letters.

Maren Voss stood in the dark with her hand flat against the loose panel, feeling the cold of the stone come up through her palm like a message from the river below, and understood that within a week—perhaps within days—he would be standing exactly where she was standing.

She pressed the panel home until it clicked, and turned off the light, and went up the stairs to answer his questions herself, wearing the face she wore for strangers: helpful, precise, and closed as a ledger at year's end.
