## 14. Trial Date Set

The announcement came on a Tuesday, which felt wrong. Catastrophes should arrive on Fridays, Jude thought—something about the end of a week made ruin feel earned. But the District Attorney of Graywater County chose a Tuesday morning, chose the steps of the courthouse in Graywater proper, forty minutes down the coast road from Dravenmoor Hollow, and chose to stand behind a podium with a forest of microphones while helicopters circled like gulls over a fish market.

Jude watched it from the back of the crowd, hood up, recorder in his coat pocket running hot.

He'd driven down alone. He'd told himself it was research, that he needed the press conference the way a cartographer needed a coastline, but the truth was simpler and uglier: he needed to see the machinery start. For three weeks the empty coffin had been a local wound, a thing the town bled over in private—at the wake, in the market, in the low voices outside the parish records office that went quiet when he passed. Now it was about to become a public organ, beating on the evening news, and everything that had been slow and fog-bound and deniable was about to accelerate.

"—and I want to be clear with the people of this county," DA Eleanor Marsh was saying. She was sixty-ish, silver-haired, with the kind of voice that had spent decades making juries lean in. "The exhumation of Aldous Parish was ordered in good faith as part of our heritage commemoration. What we found instead—" she paused, and Jude recognized the pause, had used it himself a hundred times in a hundred episodes, the pause that lets the audience fill in the horror— "was a coffin filled with river stones, and a grave that has been lying to this town for sixty-six years."

A ripple through the crowd. Someone near Jude whispered *stones* to someone else as if the word were new.

"Therefore, effective today, I am convening a formal inquest into the death—or non-death—of Aldous Parish, and into the circumstances surrounding the interment of 1958. The inquest will begin in six weeks." She let that land. "Sworn testimony. Subpoenaed records. And if the evidence warrants—and I believe it will—a criminal trial to follow."

Jude's thumb found the recorder through the fabric of his coat. Six weeks.

Marsh kept talking—something about cooperation from the parish council, something about the historical significance, something carefully vague about persons of interest—but Jude had stopped listening to the words and started listening to the structure. He'd built enough episodes to know a load-bearing sentence when he heard one. *Six weeks* was load-bearing. Everything else was decoration.

He slipped out of the crowd before the questions started and walked to where he'd parked, at the end of a side street where the courthouse's stone face gave way to shuttered storefronts and a diner with a hand-painted sign: EAT. He sat in the car with the engine off and the fog pressing against the windows like something that wanted in, and he did what he always did when the picture got too big to hold in his head.

He made a list. Out loud. For the recorder.

"Six weeks," he said. "Inquest. Sworn testimony. Which means—" he ticked it off on his fingers against the steering wheel— "subpoenas. The parish council's financial records. The burial register. The stipend ledgers. The 1958 investigation file, if it still exists in any form the county recognizes. Everything I've been digging for with a forged permit and a flashlight, the state is about to dig for with a court order."

He stopped. The fog ran down the windshield in slow gray ropes.

"And whoever rewrote that burial record—1964, and again last year—knows that too. Better than I do. Better than the DA does. They've had sixty-six years to think about this day, and now it has a date on it."

He turned the recorder off. Sat with it.

Because here was the thing he couldn't say out loud, not even to a machine: a deadline doesn't just organize the investigators. It organizes the killer. And a killer who had spent decades curating the town's memory—who had amended records, rehearsed witnesses, burned pages—was not going to spend six weeks waiting to be subpoenaed.

They were going to spend six weeks making sure there was nothing left to testify about.

* * *

The drive back to Dravenmoor Hollow took longer than it should have, because the fog on the causeway had thickened into something you could lean against, and because Jude kept pulling over at the pull-offs to check his mirrors out of a habit that was three weeks old and already felt lifelong.

The causeway road ran the last four miles into town on a spine of rock and fill with black water on both sides. In clear weather it was beautiful. In fog it was a corridor with no walls, and Jude drove it now at twenty miles an hour with his window cracked, listening to the water, listening for a second engine that wasn't there. Not today. But the habit stayed.

He got into town around two and found the square transformed. The heritage banners that had hung for the commemoration—the ones with Aldous Parish's engraving, all jaw and conviction—had been taken down overnight. In their place, someone from the county had hung official notices: *INQUEST INTO THE PARISH INTERMENT — PUBLIC SESSIONS COMMENCE [DATE]*. Six weeks out. The date sat there in bold type like a nail waiting to be hit.

Outside the parish records office, a small crowd had gathered—not a crowd, Jude corrected himself, an audience. A dozen townspeople, standing in the drizzle, looking up at the office's second-floor window, where a light burned behind the frosted glass. As if the window were a stage and the light a promise.

Maren Voss was up there.

Jude knew the shape of her silhouette now, the way she stood slightly forward of her shoulders like a woman perpetually about to lean into a conversation. He watched the watchers watching her window, and he understood something with a clarity that felt like cold water: the town wasn't angry at her. The town was *waiting* on her. She had spent her career as the keeper of Dravenmoor Hollow's story, and now that the story had cracked open like a dropped plate, the town looked to her the way a congregation looks to a church in a storm.

She was the immune system, he'd written in his notes once, before he'd known what that would come to mean. And the body trusts its immune system even when the immune system is the disease.

He crossed the square. He hadn't planned to. His feet decided.

* * *

The records office smelled the way it always did—paper, dust, lemon polish, and beneath it something older, a mineral smell like the inside of a stone. Maren was at her desk on the second floor with the county's press release spread out in front of her, and she did not look surprised to see him. He was beginning to suspect she was constitutionally incapable of looking surprised.

"Mr. Dravenmoor." She set down her pen. "You've been to Graywater, I take it. The courthouse steps. I saw a man in a gray hood on the news footage who had your shoulders."

"My shoulders are flattered."

"Six weeks," she said, tapping the release. "That's what everyone keeps saying. Six weeks. As though a calendar were a solution." Her eyes came up, and they were calm the way deep water is calm. "Do you know what I was doing when the announcement aired, Mr. Dravenmoor? I was pulling the 1958 coroner's inquest file. The real one. Because in six weeks, a court is going to ask this office for it, and I wanted to know what condition it's in."

"And what condition is it in?"

"Tragic," she said, without a flicker. "Water damage. 1974 flood. Pages missing. The county's copy is worse." She folded her hands. "You'll want to know that before you waste six weeks on it."

Jude studied her. He had spent three weeks studying her, in the way he studied everyone—recording the tells, the cadences, the small economies of truth—and Maren Voss remained the most disciplined subject he had ever faced. She gave him exactly as much as she chose to give and not a syllable more, and she did it while looking him in the eye, which most liars couldn't manage.

"Water damage in '74," he said. "That's convenient."

"It's a flood," she said. "Floods aren't convenient. They're floods." A pause, and then—almost gently—"You look tired, Mr. Dravenmoor. Have you been sleeping?"

"Not since my car went into the shallows on the causeway."

Something moved behind her eyes then. Not guilt—he'd have bet the podcast's entire back catalog it wasn't guilt. Something more like *attention*, the way a chess player's face changes when an opponent makes a move that wasn't in the book.

"Yes," she said quietly. "I heard about that. The police think it was a drunk driver who fled." She said it flatly, a fact being filed. But she held his gaze a half second longer than the sentence needed, and Jude walked back out into the drizzle with the half second burning in his pocket like a coin.

*She knows it wasn't a drunk driver. And she knows I know.*

* * *

That night he spread his kitchen table with everything he had, which was less than it looked. The forged permit. The photograph of the amended burial register—1958, amended 1964, amended again fourteen months ago. The stipend ledger pages he'd photographed in the court archive before the clerk got nervous. The leaked scan of the 1958 investigation file with its annotations in that careful, upright hand—the same hand, he was now almost certain, as the register amendments. He'd compared the letterforms for two nights running, the particular way the writer crossed their t's with a flat stroke, the way the numeral 4 stood open-topped like a birdcage.

He played the annotations back in his head. *No witnesses at the quarry after dusk—check the quarry foreman's shift log, it was destroyed in the '61 fire.* Every dead end, pre-annotated. Every investigative path, pre-blocked. Whoever had written those notes hadn't been investigating the file. They'd been *editing the investigation*—leaving a trail designed to be found, designed to satisfy, designed to end.

And now the state was coming with subpoenas, and the annotations were going to be read aloud in a courtroom by people who didn't know they were reading a script.

He pressed record.

"Okay," he said to the kitchen, to the fog at the window, to whoever might someday listen. "Let's think like her. Like him. Like *them*—I still don't know who I'm thinking like. Six weeks. Sworn testimony. What has to be true when the inquest opens, and what can't be?"

He laid it out in the air with his hands, the way he did when an episode was assembling itself.

"What *can't* be true: that the burial record was falsified in a way that can be proven. What can't be true: that the 1958 file's annotations are traceable to a living hand. What can't be true: that anyone can place Aldous Parish alive after the official death date of 1959, or place anyone else in that coffin. What can't be true—" and here his voice dropped without his deciding to— "that the stipend ledgers survive in a form that shows payments conditioned on silence. Because that's not just a crime, that's the *town's* crime. That's sixty years of founding families cashing checks to forget."

He stopped the recorder. The kitchen was very quiet.

Because that was the realization, the one that had been circling him all day like the helicopters over Graywater: the killer didn't have to destroy *evidence* in the narrow sense. The killer had to destroy the town's willingness to look. Sixty-six years of curated memory had been built on a simple architecture—the founding families were paid to forget, and everyone else had been taught a story so consistent, so rehearsed, that the truth would sound like slander. The inquest threatened that architecture. And the most dangerous thing an architect can do when the building is about to be inspected is renovate under cover of night.

"Six weeks," he said aloud, to no one. "They don't need to beat the subpoena. They just need the paper to be ash before it's served."

He thought about the quarry. Closed in '59—the same year Parish vanished from public life, the same year the official death certificate was issued for a man whose coffin held river stones. He thought about the unmarked grave on Parish land that the retired gravedigger, Tom Ellery, had described to him over two fingers of whiskey, hands shaking, eyes not meeting his: *There's a second one up there. Always has been. Nobody ever asks who's in it, and I learned real young not to be the one who asks.*

If the truth had a body, it was in that grave. And if Maren Voss—or whoever held the pen—was going to make the truth unprovable before a court could reach it, then that grave was the last physical evidence in the county that couldn't be water-damaged or burned or amended in a ledger.

You can't subpoena ash. But you can exhume a body.

He wrote it on the back of the press release, under the bold inquest date: *THE SECOND GRAVE. If they're going to move against it, they have to do it in the next six weeks. They have to come to it. And if they come to it—*

He underlined it twice, then sat looking at the words until the ink dried.

The fog pressed at the kitchen window. Somewhere up the hill, behind the frosted glass of the records office, a light was burning late, the way it burned most nights now, and Jude found himself wondering—not for the first time—what a woman looked like when she was deciding what a town would be allowed to remember.

He didn't know yet that he'd already decided what he was going to do about the second grave. That decision was six days away, and it would arrive dressed as a leak, and it would work better than he dreamed and cost more than he imagined.

But the clock had started. He could feel it in the town itself—in the banners coming down, in the notices going up, in the way people in the square had begun speaking of the inquest date the way sailors speak of a storm season. Six weeks. Sworn testimony. Subpoenaed paper.

Somewhere above the square, the light in the records office went out.

Jude sat in the dark of his kitchen and listened to the fog, and thought: *whatever she's going to do, she just finished deciding it.*
