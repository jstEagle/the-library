## 6. The Widow's Portrait

Selma Voss still lived in the same house on Kirkegata that she had lived in when Marek was alive — a narrow yellow townhouse with a blue door and a garden that had gone feral in a way that looked deliberate, foxgloves crowding the fence line like they'd been invited. Ulyana stood across the street for four minutes before she crossed it. She counted the minutes because counting was what she did instead of breathing.

She had rehearsed nothing. That was unusual for her. Normally she arrived at a door with three versions of herself ready to deploy: the bookseller, the old colleague, the mourner. Today she had only the last one, and she wasn't sure it still fit.

The doorbell made a sound like a struck glass. Footsteps, unhurried. Then the blue door opened and Selma Voss looked down at her from one step up, and eleven years collapsed into nothing.

"You," Selma said.

"Me," Ulyana said.

Selma was sixty now, or near it. Her hair had gone fully silver, cut short and practical, and she wore paint-stained linen as though it were a uniform she'd earned. Her hands were the hands of someone who worked — knuckles broad, nails short, a crescent of dried ochre in the crease of her left thumb. But her eyes were unchanged. They were the eyes of a woman who painted portraits, which meant they were the eyes of a woman who had learned to look at people so hard and so long that looking became a kind of possession.

"I wondered how long it would take you," Selma said, and stepped aside to let her in.

* * *

The hallway smelled of turpentine and coffee and, underneath both, the particular dust of a house where one room is never opened. Selma took Ulyana's coat without asking, hung it on a hook that already held two others, and led her through to the kitchen, talking as she went in a low, steady voice that seemed designed to fill space so that neither of them would have to.

"The kettle's on. It's always on. I've become one of those women. You'll take tea."

It wasn't a question. Ulyana sat at the kitchen table, at the chair nearest the window, and noticed — because she couldn't help noticing, because noticing was the only prayer she'd ever known — that there were two cups already out. Not fresh. Used, rinsed, set upside down on the rack by the sink. Selma drank alone but set out two cups anyway. Or she'd been expecting someone else today. Or she simply could not stop setting places for the dead.

The kitchen walls held small paintings — harbor studies, mostly, done fast and loose, the water always gray-green, the boats always riding too high as if relieved to be empty. On the refrigerator, held by a magnet shaped like a herring, was a photograph of Marek Voss that Ulyana had never seen before. He was laughing in it, caught mid-something, his hand raised as if warding off a joke rather than greeting one. He looked young. He looked like a man who did not yet know he was going to die, which of course he hadn't, then. Nobody ever does.

"He hated that photo," Selma said, following her gaze as she poured. "Said it made him look like an idiot. I said it made him look like himself, and he didn't speak to me for an hour." She smiled at the memory, and the smile cost her something; Ulyana watched her pay for it. "Eleven years. Do you know what's strange? I've now been without him longer than I was with him. The arithmetic turns against you eventually. For the first few years the marriage is still the bigger number. Then one morning it isn't, and nobody warns you."

"Selma." Ulyana wrapped her hands around the cup Selma pushed toward her. The tea was black, no milk, the way Marek had taken it. Ulyana wondered if Selma knew she'd done that. She decided she did. "I'm not here for tea."

"No," Selma agreed. "But you'll drink it while you're not here for it."

She sat down opposite, folded her paint-stained hands on the table, and waited. It was, Ulyana thought, exactly the technique she herself used — silence as an open door, patience as a net. Widows and detectives learned the same trade from opposite ends: how to make other people tell you things.

"A stranger came to my shop," Ulyana said. "Paid me to prove a murder won't happen. A murder tied to a date — the first day of the trial next month. The trial of the man convicted of killing Marek."

Something moved behind Selma's face. Not surprise. Recognition, maybe — the look of a person who has been carrying a package for a long time and finally sees someone else reach for it.

"And you came to me because?"

"Because your husband's name was in the file. Because I've spent eleven years not thinking about him, and the file wouldn't let me keep doing that. And because—" Ulyana stopped. Started again, more honestly than she'd planned. "Because I owed him a testimony I never gave. And I don't know where else to put that."

Selma nodded slowly, once, like a judge accepting a plea. Then she stood, carried her cup to the counter, and stood with her back turned, looking out the window at the feral garden.

"Do you know why I kept painting harbors?" she said. "After. Everyone told me to paint something else. Flowers. Faces. Anything but water. But I kept painting the harbor, over and over, hundreds of them, and I told myself it was grief, and maybe it was. But it was also—" She turned around. "It was also that I kept getting the light wrong. In my memory the night he died was bright. Streetlamps, moonlight, that flat harbor glare. And every painting came out too dark, and I thought, well, grief darkens everything. Except the paintings weren't wrong, Ulyana. My memory was. It wasn't bright that night. There was fog off the water so thick the lamps were just smears. I checked the weather records later. I don't know why I checked. A widow does strange things. The night was black, and my mind had been quietly repainting it for years, and I let it, because a black night meant he died alone in the dark, and a bright night meant—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. *A bright night meant someone might have seen.*

Ulyana's tea went cold in her hands.

* * *

They moved to the front room after that, the room Ulyana had been silently cataloguing since she arrived — the room with the good chairs and the piano nobody played and, above the fireplace, the portrait.

Marek Voss looked down from the canvas the way he had looked at crime scenes: patient, skeptical, faintly amused by the pretensions of the world. Selma had painted him in his coat, collar up, standing before a background of unmodulated gray, and she had gotten him exactly — the heavy brow, the scar through his left eyebrow from a bicycle accident he'd lied about being a knife fight, the way his mouth turned up on one side only, as if humor were something he permitted himself in strict moderation.

The portrait hung crooked.

Not badly. Two degrees, perhaps three — the kind of tilt that a guest notices and says nothing about out of politeness. But Ulyana had spent twenty years reading rooms the way priests read scripture, and a crooked portrait above a fireplace in a painter's own house was not a detail. Painters did not hang their own work crooked. Painters adjusted frames the way other people straightened their collars; it was reflex, vanity, care.

Which meant the frame had been moved recently, and not by Selma.

"When did you last touch that?" Ulyana asked.

Selma followed her eyes upward. Ulyana saw the exact moment she understood what was being asked — a small tightening around her mouth, a flicker of something that was almost fear and almost anger and chose, finally, to be neither.

"Last month," Selma said. "Someone took it down."

* * *

It came out in pieces, the way truth always did with Selma — not withheld, exactly, but rationed, each piece offered only when the previous one had been properly received.

A man had come to the house five weeks ago. Youngish, soft-spoken, well-dressed in the anonymous way of men who wanted to be forgotten on sight. He said he was writing a book about the harbor district's history. He said he'd heard Selma Voss was the finest portraitist on the coast and he hoped to commission her — a portrait of his father, who had worked the docks all his life. He brought photographs. He was charming in a careful way, rehearsed in a careful way, and Selma, who was lonely in the specific way of people whose houses are too quiet, had let him in and made him coffee and talked to him for nearly two hours.

"He was good," she said. "You'd have liked him, or you'd have seen through him instantly, I'm not sure which. He asked all the right questions about painting. About light. About how long a sitting takes. And then, somewhere in the middle — very casually, the way you'd ask about the weather — he asked if the house still felt haunted. If I still felt Marek in it. And I said yes, of course, in the paintings most of all. And he looked up at that portrait for a long time and said, 'He looks like a man who knew things.'"

Ulyana's skin prickled. "And then?"

"And then he asked to see it closer. Whether the varnish had yellowed, whether I'd consider reworking it. He took it down — asked permission first, I'll grant him that — and held it under the window light and looked at the back of the canvas for what felt like a full minute. Then he thanked me, said his father's face wasn't quite right for a commission after all, some nonsense about scheduling, and left. He paid for the coffee with cash. Who pays for coffee with cash?"

"What was he looking for on the back?"

"That's the question, isn't it." Selma's hands had gone still in her lap. "I turned the portrait over myself that evening, after he left. And I found this."

She rose, took the framed portrait down — it was heavier than it looked, Ulyana noted, the stretcher bars thick, old-growth pine — and laid it face-down on the piano bench. On the back of the canvas, in the lower corner, half-hidden beneath a paper dust cover that had been peeled back and pressed down again, there was a rectangle of discoloration. Faded paint around a missing patch. Something had been affixed there once — taped, glued, pinned — and removed, and not recently. The ghost of it remained: a pale square roughly fifteen centimeters by ten.

"Paper," Ulyana said. "Something paper was mounted there for years. Long enough to block the light and leave a shadow-line."

"Marek put it there. I remember now — I'd forgotten until that man made me remember. Years ago, before he died. He came home late one night, and I was awake, and he was in this room with the portrait off the wall and something in his hands, and he said—" Selma closed her eyes, retrieving it, and her voice changed, dropped into the cadence of quotation, of a sentence kept alive by repetition. "He said, 'If anything happens to me, Selma, look behind my face.' And I laughed at him. God help me, I laughed. I said, *you watch too many films.* And he smiled that half-smile and hung it back up and kissed my hair and went to bed. Three weeks later he was in the harbor."

The room was very quiet. Somewhere upstairs a clock ticked with the patience of furniture.

"And the thing itself?" Ulyana asked, though she already knew.

"Gone. Taken off the backing sometime between that night and now. I used to think I'd dreamed the whole exchange — grief does that, it edits. Until that man tilted the portrait toward the window and I watched him find the same empty patch I found, and I understood he knew it had been there. He wasn't looking for it. He was confirming it was gone." Selma opened her eyes. "Or confirming that *I* had it."

"Do you?"

"No." And then, softer, with the terrible honesty of the long-bereaved: "I've torn this house apart twice looking. Whatever Marek hid behind his own face, someone else got to it first. Before the funeral, I think. While I was sleeping in my sister's guest room because I couldn't bear our bed."

Ulyana stared at the faded rectangle. Fifteen by ten centimeters. Notebook-sized. Ledger-sized. The word surfaced in her mind from nowhere and everywhere — *the ledger* — and she had no idea why, and the not-knowing frightened her more than anything the stranger's file had contained.

* * *

They sat for a while afterward with the tea Selma insisted on refreshing, and the conversation drifted into smaller waters — Priya, whom Selma had met at the shop; Ove's hip; the foxgloves; whether the bakery on the corner had always been mediocre or had only recently achieved it. This was its own kind of tenderness, Ulyana thought. Two women who had been widowed by the same night, sitting in the wreckage of a shared grief, discussing bread. Neither of them said *we should have done this years ago*, but the unsaid sentence sat between them like a third guest, and both of them fed it.

At the door, Selma held Ulyana's coat but didn't give it to her.

"One more thing," she said. "That man. When he left, he paused on the step, and he said — and I've turned it over a hundred times since — he said, 'You're the second person to ask about Marek this year. The first was a woman. Dark hair, bookshop, I believe.'" Selma's eyes were level and sad. "Except I never told him I knew you. And I haven't spoken your name aloud in this house in eleven years. So either he was lying to flatter me—"

"—or he'd already been to me," Ulyana finished. "Before he came here. Working backward through everyone Marek loved."

"Yes." Selma handed over the coat at last, and her hand lingered on the wool. "Ulyana. Whoever sent that man knows what Marek hid. Knows it's missing. And is checking whether the widow has it." She glanced back into the dim hall, at the empty hook-shaped absence above the fireplace. "Which means whatever it was, it isn't history to them. It's still dangerous. Be careful what you go looking for behind people's faces."

Outside, the afternoon had thickened toward rain. Ulyana walked to the end of Kirkegata and stopped, and took out her notebook, and wrote down the dimensions of the faded rectangle before they could blur: 15 × 10 cm. Paper. Removed post-mortem. Visitor confirmed knowledge.

Then, below it, without deciding to, her hand wrote a single word:

*Ledger.*

She stared at it. She had never used that word in connection with Marek Voss in her life. It had simply arrived, the way the memory of fog had arrived in Selma's paintings — something true surfacing through layers of false varnish.

She looked up. Across the street, parked badly outside the shuttered chandlery, sat a gray sedan with a driver who was very carefully not looking at her.

Ulyana closed the notebook and began, unhurried, to walk home — taking, for the first time in eleven years, a route with no cameras on it.
