## 12. Ove Picks a Lock He Shouldn't

Ove Lindqvist had spent forty years opening things that other people wanted kept shut, and in all that time he had learned one rule above every other rule: the lock never tells you who is on the other side.

He was thinking about that rule now, sitting in the passenger seat of Ulyana's ancient Volvo at eleven minutes past midnight, a canvas tool roll open on his knees like a surgeon's tray, watching the municipal records annex across the street. It was an ugly building — poured concrete, salt-stained, three stories of fluorescent-lit bureaucracy by day and nothing at all by night except one security lamp over the loading door that buzzed like a trapped insect. The harbor was two blocks east. He could smell it through the cracked window: brine and diesel and old rope, the smell of this city's whole long memory.

"You're doing the thing," Ulyana said from the driver's seat.

"What thing."

"The thing where you narrate your own funeral."

Ove grunted. At seventy-three, he considered his own funeral a solved problem — cremation, no speeches, someone responsible for watering his tomatoes — but he let her have it. She needed to be sharp tonight, not soft, and arguing with Ulyana Nystrom was how you sharpened her. He'd learned that much in the year since she'd hired him to fix the bookshop's back door and then, without ever quite asking, made him family.

"Third patrol passed at ten-forty," he said instead. "Second one's due around twelve-thirty. That gives us—" he checked his watch, an ugly steel thing with a face like a manhole cover— "seventeen minutes inside, if we don't dawdle."

"We won't dawdle."

"And if we get caught?"

Ulyana looked at him. In the dark of the car her face was mostly shadow and the pale smear of the security lamp, and Ove thought, not for the first time, that she had the kind of face you could read only if you already knew the language. He knew the language now. What she was saying with it was: *I'd rather be caught alone.*

That was the trouble with her. That was the whole trouble with her, wrapped up neat as a pick gun in oiled leather.

"Biscuit's with Priya," she said. "Priya has my number, Halvard's number, and instructions to call neither unless we miss check-in twice."

"Priya will call both numbers simultaneously out of spite."

"Yes," Ulyana said. "Probably."

They sat another moment. Somewhere down the block a gull complained about the century. Then Ulyana exhaled through her nose — her version of a prayer, Ove suspected — and killed the engine, and they crossed the street together, two shadows moving through the wet cold, and Ove felt something in his chest he hadn't felt since he was a young man with more hair and fewer scruples.

It wasn't fear. He'd been afraid plenty; fear was just the body doing arithmetic. This was older than fear. This was the feeling of being *useful* — of standing next to someone who needed exactly what your hands could do, no more and no less.

The loading door was steel, fire-rated, fitted with a push-bar and a cheap alarm contact that hadn't worked since the last budget cycle — Ove knew because he'd fixed doors for the city for six years before retirement and remembered which buildings got maintenance and which got memos. The real problem was the interior door to the evidence archive, second floor, and beyond it the cage itself: chain-link and angle iron, a padlock older than Priya.

He went to work on the loading door first. Not picking — there was nothing to pick — but persuading. A shim of spring steel between the strike and the latch tongue, a quarter-turn of wrist, the particular patience of a man who understood that every lock is just a small stubborn animal that can be talked down. The bar clicked. The door swung on hinges that shrieked once until he thumbed them quiet.

Inside smelled of paper dust and floor wax. Their footsteps were very loud. Everything was very loud when you were somewhere you shouldn't be.

* * *

The archive room was on the second floor, behind a door marked RECORDS RETENTION — AUTHORIZED PERSONNEL, which in Ove's experience meant *authorized personnel and anyone with twenty minutes and a steady hand.* This lock was better. Six-pin, security bitting, probably installed by someone who cared. He crouched before it with the tension wrench seated and a pick that he'd ground himself from a bicycle spoke, and he closed his eyes, because that was how he did it — reading the pins through his fingers like Braille, feeling each one give or refuse, the little conversations between metal and metal.

"Nineteen minutes," Ulyana murmured, watching the stairwell.

"Hush. You'll make me drop a pin."

"Can you do that?"

"I once dropped four," Ove said. "In a bank in nineteen eighty-nine. Worst night of my life." He found the third pin binding, eased it, set it. Found the fifth binding hard — a spool, the sneaky kind — overshot it, cursed under his breath, came back. "Your partner Marek," he said quietly, because his hands worked better when his mouth was busy and because he'd decided on the drive over that somebody ought to say the name aloud in a place like this. "Was he careful?"

Ulyana was silent long enough that Ove opened one eye.

"He was careful about everything except people," she said. "He trusted the wrong ones beautifully."

"That's most men." The spool gave. The fourth pin. The first. "My wife used to say I trusted locks too much and people exactly right, and that someday the two would cancel out." The core turned. The bolt slid home with a sound like a knuckle cracking. "She was right, incidentally. Just took thirty years."

The door opened onto rows of gray shelving, boxed and labeled, and at the far end the evidence cage — chain-link to the ceiling, lit by its own caged bulb, humming faintly. Cold cases. Boxes with dates going back decades, each one a life interrupted and filed alphabetically by some clerk who never thought about what the letters meant.

Ulyana moved down the aisle with a slip of paper in her hand. Ove watched her find the row, find the box — VOSS, M. / HOMICIDE / 11-YR — and lift it down, and he saw her hands shake. Just once. Just enough.

"Ove," she said. Her voice was level. Her hands were not. "Stand where you are for a second."

He stood where he was. He was good at that too.

She opened the box on top of a filing cabinet, and Ove came close enough to see: bagged clothing, photographs in envelopes, a ledger of chain-of-custody signatures, and — wrapped in an evidence sleeve, sealed with tape gone amber with age — a black notebook, its cover stamped with the harbor police crest.

"His personal notebook," Ulyana said. "It was logged into evidence the week after he died and never touched again. Halvard confirmed it yesterday. If Voss was investigating anything — the ledger, the payments, whatever got him killed — it started here."

"And if someone got to it first?"

"Then we'll know that too." She peeled the tape. Her hands had steadied. "Somebody always gets to it first. That's the shape of this whole case. Somebody always arrives before the truth does."

The notebook came out stiff, swollen slightly from years of damp storage. Ulyana opened it flat on the cabinet under the cage light, and Ove leaned in beside her, and for a moment neither of them breathed.

The middle of the notebook was gone.

Not torn — razored. Three clean excisions, maybe fifteen pages' worth, cut so close to the spine that the blade had kissed the stitching. Whoever did it had been unhurried and thorough. Around the wounds, the surviving pages showed Voss's cramped, slanting hand: tide times, license plates, a name — *R.* — circled twice and then, in different ink, pressed so hard the pen had nearly torn the paper, the word *STOP* written over it, though whether by Voss or someone else, Ove couldn't say.

"They took it," Ulyana whispered. "Eleven years ago, someone walked into this cage and took the heart out of his notebook, and everyone swore the case was closed."

Ove didn't answer right away. He was looking at the razored stubs of pages, at the way the cuts had compressed the paper beneath them, and a very old feeling was climbing up his arms.

"Ulyana," he said. "Get me the lamp closer. And find me something soft. A cloth. Anything."

"Why?"

"Because whoever held that razor was holding it against a stack," Ove said. "You don't cut loose pages one at a time — you press the block flat and slice through, and every stroke prints the page underneath." He looked up at her, and he allowed himself the smallest smile, the one his granddaughter — no, not his granddaughter, the shop girl, Priya, he corrected himself, though the correction never stuck — called his *burglar's grin*. "Paper remembers pressure the way skin remembers a scar. There may be nothing left to read. But there may be ghosts."

* * *

It took an hour. Seventeen minutes became forty became an hour, because Ulyana made two calls — one to Priya, one, after a silence Ove pretended not to witness, to Inspector Halvard — and because the work could not be hurried. Hurry was how you tore the page. And these pages had survived enough tearing.

Ove worked with a graphite stick and a strip of microfiber cloth and, eventually, a jar of Ulyana's hand cream, which she surrendered without comment when he explained that wax could lift an impression where dry rubbing couldn't. He laid each razored stub flat, weighted the neighboring page with a steel ruler, and ghosted the graphite across the surface of the page *beneath* where the missing leaves had lain compressed for a decade — the writing of the destroyed pages, pressed into their neighbors like fossils into shale.

Mostly there was nothing. Shadow-ghosts, noise, the memory of handwriting without the handwriting. Twice he found half a word and lost it. Ulyana stood over him the entire time, breathing carefully, the way people breathe when they're trying not to hope.

On the eleventh page, the ghosts began to speak.

A column. Numbers. And beneath the numbers, names — shallow, incomplete, letters missing where the pressure had failed, but names. Ove wrote them out on the back of a parking receipt as they surfaced, Ulyana filling gaps from context, both of them whispering like men in a confessional.

*H——en, 4,000.*
*Bakk——, 6,500.*
*R——olt, 12,000. R——olt, 9,000. R——olt, 15,000.*

Three entries. One name, recurring, growing.

Ulyana went very still.

"Reinholt," she said. The word came out flat, stripped of everything. "Anders Reinholt. The prosecutor who closed Marek's case. The judge presiding over the trial in nine days." She looked at the page, at the ghost-numbers marching down it like a confession written in invisible ink. "He wasn't just in the ledger. He was the largest line item in it."

"Payments for what?" Ove asked, though he knew. You didn't pay a prosecutor twelve thousand crowns for his signature on a birthday card.

"For buying a confession," Ulyana said. "For closing a murder. For making sure a dockworker with a bad lawyer and a worse story went to prison for eleven years for a killing he didn't commit." She straightened. Something had changed in her face — Ove had watched faces for forty years, reading them through peepholes and keyholes and the little windows of safes, and he knew transformation when he saw it. Grief was still in her, but grief had been overtaken. Purpose had arrived like weather. "Ove. Photograph it. Every page, every ghost, before we move anything."

"I don't photograph. I have a phone for calls and complaints."

"Then describe it. Memorize it. Both of us memorize it." She was already copying the recovered columns into her own notebook, letter by letter, number by number, her pen moving fast and certain. "If we lose this, we lose Marek twice."

So Ove memorized. He was good at that too — a lifetime of remembering combinations and keyways and the exact turn-count of a client's safe had left him with a mind like a well-oiled tumbler. *Halvorsen, four thousand. Bakken, six-five. Reinholt, twelve, nine, fifteen.* He repeated it silently, folded it into himself, and thought: *Marek Voss, I never met you, but I know your handwriting now, and I know what it cost you to write those numbers down, and I promise you the old man with the picks is going to carry them out of here if it's the last thing his knees agree to.*

* * *

They were repacking the box when the radio on the loading dock crackled.

Both of them froze. The voice was tinny, bored, reciting a patrol check-in — *unit twelve, annex and harbor loop, all secure* — and then fading, and then gone. But beneath the radio voice there had been another sound, and Ove's ears, which had lied to him about dinner conversation for a decade but never about danger, caught it clearly.

A car door. Outside. Closing softly.

He looked at Ulyana. She had heard it too. They stood in the aisle of dead files with a murdered man's notebook between them, and Ove watched her do the arithmetic — front stairs, rear window, the loading door they'd come through — and watched her arrive, as he did, at the same conclusion: whichever way they chose, they would be choosing it blind.

"Back shelf," Ove breathed. "Behind the retention boxes. Now."

They folded themselves into the gap between shelving units, among boxes marked WATER DAMAGE 2003, and Ove pulled a carton in front of their knees and put his hand, without deciding to, over Ulyana's. She didn't pull away. In the dark her pulse was quick against his palm, and his own, he noticed with detached professional interest, was not quick at all. Old men's hearts know when panic is a luxury.

Footsteps in the corridor. Unhurried. Familiar with the building — no hesitation at the turns, no fumbling for switches, though the intruder didn't switch any lights on either, which told Ove something else: whoever this was, they preferred the dark, and they knew the dark wouldn't slow them.

The archive door opened. The beam of a flashlight swept the shelving, low and methodical, starting at the far end.

Moving toward the cage.

Toward the box.

Ove felt Ulyana tense beside him — felt her weigh the same terrible option he was weighing, that the notebook was sitting on the filing cabinet in plain sight, that if the flashlight reached it, everything they'd just clawed out of the dark would be taken back into the dark, and Marek Voss would die a second time, quieter than the first.

Her hand slipped free of his. He knew what she was going to do before she did it. It was written in her the way the dead man's writing was written in the page — pressed deep, impossible to erase.

So Ove did the thing he'd spent seventy-three years getting good at.

He reached into his tool roll, found the heavy steel tension wrench he'd ground from a bicycle spoke, and threw it — not at the intruder, but past him, down the corridor, where it rang off a radiator like a struck bell.

The flashlight whipped toward the sound. The footsteps broke into a run — away from the cage, away from the notebook, toward the noise, toward the old locksmith crouched in the dark with nothing left in his hands but a jar of hand cream and no regrets whatsoever.

Ulyana was already moving, notebook in her coat, pulling him up by the elbow, and they went out the way ghosts leave rooms — through the dark, down the service stairs, into the wet cold of the alley where the Volvo waited with its engine off and its secrets intact.

Only when they were three streets away, windows fogged, Biscuit's leash and Priya's five missed calls waiting on the dashboard, did Ulyana speak.

"You threw your best wrench."

"It was a good wrench," Ove agreed. "It deserved a dramatic death."

She laughed — a short, cracked, real laugh, the first he'd heard from her in weeks — and then it died in her throat, because her phone lit up in the cupholder, and the name on the screen was Priya, and Priya never called twice unless something was wrong.

Ulyana answered. Ove watched her face in the green light of the screen, and watched the color leave it.

"Priya," she said slowly. "Say that again."
