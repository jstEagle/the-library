## 9. Ruth's Last Letter

Ruth Calloway kept her lies the way she kept her tea things—organized, dusted, and never discussed.

I'd known that about her for twenty years and had somehow failed to understand what it meant until now. Standing in her sister's guest room in Marlowe Falls, forty minutes east of Halloway's Crossing, I understood it completely. The room was too clean. Not tidy—clean. The kind of clean that happens after someone goes through everything with a fine-toothed comb and then puts it all back exactly where they think you'd expect to find it.

"You can take whatever you like," Carol had said at the door, wringing a dish towel like it owed her money. "Ruth would've wanted—" She stopped. Started again. "She didn't leave much."

Carol was lying, but kindly, which is the worst kind of lying to be on the receiving end of. Ruth had left plenty. She'd left me a career, a method, and a voice in my head that still said *check the dates twice, Theo, once for the file and once for your conscience*. And apparently—judging by the freshly vacuumed carpet and the closet organized by someone who wasn't Ruth, because Ruth's idea of organizing a closet was "shove it in there before company comes"—she'd also left something worth killing Mr. Pell over.

I stood in the doorway a moment longer, letting my memory do what it does. Photograph. File. Index.

The bed: made with hospital corners Ruth never used. The nightstand: lamp, phone charger, a paperback of Tennyson placed face-down at page 112. Ruth hated Tennyson. She called him "a man who wrote three good lines and spent sixty years hiding them." The dresser: jewelry box open, empty of anything valuable, a hairbrush with strands of silver-blonde that were not Carol's color. The window: latch locked from inside, but the paint around it scuffed, recently, by someone careful enough to relock it.

Someone had searched this room within the last week. Someone patient. Someone who'd read the same books I read about how investigators think, because they'd put everything back one inch off true, betting I wouldn't notice.

The trouble with betting against my memory is that the house always wins.

* * *

Let me back up, because I've been accused—by Dana Okafor, mostly, in the tone she uses when she wants to say something unkind and is restraining herself—of skipping the parts where I explain myself.

Three days ago, Mr. Pell fell from a ladder in his own archive. I stood in that room afterward and saw the rigging the way you see a face in a crowd: instantly, totally, unwillingly. A quarter-inch of fresh scrape on the ladder's third rung where a shim had been seated and removed. A ceiling hook, brass, decorative, installed sometime in the last year—the screw bright where the rest of the fixture was green with age. Weight tested, load calculated, timing arranged so that Pell would climb, reach, and drop. It was elegant. It was the third such elegance of my career, and the first one aimed at something I loved.

Pell had gone pale over Vera Ashcombe's signature and asked me to forget it. He hadn't forgotten it himself. He'd gone home and done what archivists do—checked his records, cross-referenced, maybe pulled the original claim file—and someone had decided he'd checked one layer too deep.

Which meant whoever killed him knew what he'd find. Which meant they knew about the 1984 policy. Which meant they knew about Ruth, because Ruth had approved that claim, and if they were willing to kill an old man over a signature, then Ruth's effects weren't safe either.

I called Carol from the road. I told her I was coming to help sort Ruth's papers for the estate. That was even mostly true. What I didn't tell her was that I expected to find the room already picked over, because the alternative—that I was wrong about the search, wrong about the danger, wrong about all of it—was a door I've learned not to knock on. Hope is a lovely thing but a terrible investigative tool.

* * *

I started with the obvious places anyway. You always check the obvious places first, partly out of diligence and partly because the person who hid something well is usually counting on you to waste an hour on the obvious places, and there's a certain courtesy in obliging them.

Ruth's desk was in the corner under the window, a rolltop she'd bought at an estate sale in 1998 and restored herself. I knew every scratch on it. I knew the drawer that stuck unless you lifted the left side. I opened it now and found it sticking in a new way—lifted left side or not—which told me it had been removed entirely and reinstalled by hands less familiar than mine.

Inside: stationery, stamps, a rubber band ball, pens sorted by ink color because Ruth believed organization was a moral virtue. Under the tray liner, nothing. Under the drawer itself—I ran my fingers along the underside, feeling for tape, for an envelope, for the flat rectangle of a photograph—nothing but old glue and a dead spider.

The bookshelf next. Ruth's library was small and opinionated: mysteries she read to mock, insurance law she read to win, poetry she read to survive. I tipped each volume and fanned the pages. Nothing. I checked the hollow beneath the bottom shelf, the gap behind the row of binders, the false space where a shelf met the case's side panel. Nothing, nothing, and a very indignant mouse skeleton.

Two hours in, I sat down on the bed that wasn't made the way Ruth made it and let myself feel the thing I'd been holding at arm's length since I walked in.

She was gone. That was still true. I kept having to re-learn it, the way you keep reaching for a tooth that isn't there. Six weeks dead—heart, sudden, at her kitchen table with a crossword half-finished, 14 Across, *one who covers for another*, six letters. I remember thinking at the funeral that the puzzle had a sense of humor. I remember thinking a lot of things at the funeral that I'm only now understanding.

And she had been afraid. Or if not afraid, then busy in the particular way people are busy when they're putting their affairs in order ahead of a deadline they haven't announced. In her last year she'd called me more often. Asked odd questions. *Theo, do you still have that memory of yours, or has age finally taken pity on you?* *If you needed to hide something from everyone, where would you put it?* I'd answered the second one flippantly—in plain sight, obviously—and she'd laughed and said *yes, that's what I thought too* and changed the subject to my cholesterol.

I had filed those calls under *Ruth being Ruth.* My filing system, it turns out, has gaps.

* * *

It was the coat that did it. Of course it was the coat.

Here's the thing about searching a room: you search objects. Desks, drawers, shelves, mattresses, floorboards. You search containers, because containers are where things go. But Ruth didn't think in containers. Ruth thought in *wearing*. When we worked surveillance together in my first year, she taught me that the best place to keep something you need is on your body, because a room can be searched but a person has to be caught. She used to carry her backup pen clipped inside her waistband. She used to say, *Theo, the filing cabinet you can't lose is the one you're standing up in.*

So I went to the closet, and I took down her winter coat—the heavy wool one, charcoal gray, the one she wore to every claims inspection for fifteen years until the cuffs went shiny—and I turned it inside out under the lamp.

The lining had been resewn. Not professionally. Professionally means even stitches, matching thread. This was uneven stitches in thread a shade too dark, done by someone working by lamplight with a thimble and more determination than skill, and I recognized the stitch pattern because I'd seen it once before, years ago, when Ruth split her coat seam on a site visit and repaired it herself in the car while lecturing me about liability waivers.

My hands were not steady. I'll admit that here, in the privacy of my own narration, where no one can use it against me.

I unpicked the seam along the lower left panel. It took four minutes. Inside the lining, folded into eighths and pressed flat as a pressed flower, was a single sheet of paper, and on it, in Ruth's handwriting—her real handwriting, the sharp slanted hand, not the rounder script she used for condolence cards—was the beginning of a letter that stopped mid-sentence and never started again.

* * *

This is what it said. I have it memorized, which is fortunate, because you'll see why I couldn't keep the paper.

---

*Dear Theo,*

*If you're reading this, then either I'm dead and you've finally gone through my things without asking, which I taught you better than, or something has happened and you've come looking. Either way: sit down. No—you won't sit down. You never sit down. Fine.*

*I approved the Halloway claim in 1984. I want that sentence to exist somewhere outside Meridian's files, because Meridian's files have a way of losing sentences. The file says the death was verified. It was not. I verified what I was told to verify, and I was told by men whose names I will not write here because paper is evidence and evidence gets people hurt, as you may have noticed if you're reading this after the way things ended for Mr. Pell, whose archive request crossed my desk in March and frightened me more than any claim in forty years.*

*Edmund Halloway did not die in 1984. I don't know where he went. I know that the payout was authorized from above my head and that my signature was the last door it had to pass through, and I signed, and I have regretted it in the specific way one regrets a kindness done at the wrong time for the wrong reasons. There was a woman. Vera Ashcombe. If you've gotten this far, you've probably met her ghost already. She was not what the file says she was. Neither, it turns out, was I.*

*There is a lake house. North shore, past the old mill road, held in the name of a trust I was never supposed to connect to anything. I connected it. I'm telling you because I am tired, Theo, and because E.H.—*

---

And there it ended. Mid-name. Mid-sentence. The pen had pressed hard on that final dash, tearing slightly, as though her hand had stopped not gradually but all at once, the way a clock stops.

I sat on the bed that wasn't made the way Ruth made it, holding a dead woman's unfinished sentence, and I did not cry, because I am constitutionally opposed to crying in houses belonging to other people's sisters, but it was a near-run thing.

E.H. Edmund Halloway. She'd known. She'd known at the end—not just about the claim, not just about the fraud, but about *him*, that he was alive, that he was somewhere, that the lake house mattered. And she'd written to me instead of going to the police, instead of going to Meridian, instead of doing any of the ten sensible things available to a woman of her standing, because she'd understood something I was only now catching up to: that the sensible doors were watched, and that the only courier she trusted with the truth was a mind that couldn't lose it.

She hadn't mailed the letter. Why? Because mailing it meant it existed outside her body, and Ruth kept her truths the way she kept her tea things. Because she'd meant to finish it. Because she'd died at a kitchen table with a crossword half-done and a letter sewn into her coat, planning to add one more paragraph, one more name, one more fact—and the paragraph never came.

Or because she'd finished it in her head and decided I should earn the ending myself. That was Ruth too. God, that was so much Ruth.

* * *

I put the coat back together. All of it—the lining, the hangers, the angle of the shoulders on the rod, the two-inch gap between her coat and Carol's parka. If the searcher came back, they'd find what they'd left: a room already emptied of its secret, none the wiser that the secret had been sitting in their hands the whole time, wearing a charcoal-gray coat.

Then I sat on the floor with my notebook and did what Ruth trained me to do, which is think on paper.

Fact: the lake house is north shore, past the old mill road. Fact: held in a trust. Fact: Ruth connected the trust to something—or someone—and the connection frightened her enough to sew it into her clothing. Fact: Pell requested archived materials in March, Ruth saw the request, and within five months both of them were dead, one by ladder and one, possibly, by coincidence. I don't believe in that possibility anymore, but I record it, because a good investigator records even the possibilities she's abandoned, in case future-her turns out to be smarter than present-her, which has happened twice and humiliated me both times.

Question: who searched this room? Not Carol—Carol's grief is genuine and her dusting is not. Someone with access, patience, and a reason to believe Ruth kept documents. Someone who'd already killed once to keep those documents buried. Martin Halloway fit the profile the way a key fits a lock it was cut for: estate lawyer, grandson, custodian of the family myth, and—as far as I could tell—the only person in Halloway's Crossing with both the motive and the legal training to make a murder look like maintenance.

But I wrote *Martin?* with a question mark, because I've been wrong before, and because the man I was actually hunting had spent forty years teaching a town that he was dead, and dead men make excellent question marks.

Last item in the notebook, underlined twice, because some facts deserve emphasis:

*Ruth knew where the lake house was. Ruth is dead. The knowledge survived her. It is currently located in my head, which makes me, as of approximately four o'clock this afternoon, the most dangerous document in the state.*

I closed the notebook. Somewhere downstairs, Carol dropped a pan and swore with a fluency that would have delighted Ruth, and the ordinary sounds of an ordinary house went on around me, and I understood that I had just crossed a line I couldn't uncross. Pell's death had made this a case. Ruth's letter made it a promise.

North shore. Past the old mill road. A trust with a name I didn't know yet—but trusts leave paper trails, and paper trails are my native habitat, and tomorrow I would walk into the county records office and do the one thing no one, in forty years of careful murder and careful myth, had managed to stop me from doing.

Read.

I was halfway down the stairs when my phone buzzed. Okafor. I almost didn't answer—it was late, and whatever she had would keep until morning.

Except it wouldn't. I knew that the way I know things, instantly and totally and unwillingly.

"Theo," she said, when I picked up, and her voice had a flatness I'd heard only once before, at a ladder scene in a dead archivist's archive. "You need to get back to the Crossing tonight. We found something in Pell's effects. Something he was holding for someone else."

I stood in Carol's hallway, one hand on the banister, Ruth's letter folded against my ribs like a second heartbeat.

"What kind of something?" I asked.

"A photograph," Dana said. "Of a lake house. On the back, in Pell's handwriting, there's an address—and one word."

"Which word?"

She paused long enough that I heard her choose.

"*Witness,*" she said.
