## 12. Forty Years of Sundays

They buried Mr. Pell on a Tuesday, which was wrong, because Pell was a Sunday kind of man. He'd have hated a Tuesday funeral—too efficient, no time for the town to linger over the coffee and the casseroles and the careful lies people tell at funerals so they don't have to tell the true ones.

I sat in the third row of St. Brigid's with my hands folded in my lap like a woman who had never once thought about running. Behind me, the whole of Halloway's Landing murmured its grief: *terrible thing, that ladder, he was up there every day, his own fault really, should've retired.* Forty years of Sundays in this church and I could sort a lie from a prayer by the second syllable. Every one of those murmurs was a lie wearing its good coat.

I'd known Martin Halloway would come to the funeral. I watched him work the room after the service, shaking hands, face arranged into something solemn and generous, the family jaw softened by performance. He shook my hand too. Held it one beat longer than necessary.

"Ida," he said. "You're looking well."

"I'm eighty-one, Martin. Well is a relative term."

He laughed the way his grandfather used to laugh—warm as a bank lobby—and moved on to the next widow. And I stood there with his handshake still sitting in my palm like something dead I couldn't put down, and I understood two things at once, the way you understand things when you're old enough that understanding has stopped being useful.

The first thing: Mr. Pell did not fall off any ladder.

The second thing: I was next, unless I opened my mouth first.

* * *

Theo Lockridge came to my door on Thursday morning, and I'll say this for them—they didn't pretend it was a social call. No casserole, no condolences about Pell, none of the small theater of neighborliness. They just stood on my porch with their coat collar up against the drizzle and said, "Mrs. Voss. I think you've been waiting forty years for someone to ask you the right question. May I come in?"

Well. That's not precisely what they said. What they said was, "I found Vera Ashcombe's signature on a policy dated three days after Edmund Halloway died," and then they watched my face the way a man watches a dam in flood season.

But I heard the other sentence underneath it. People who spend their lives keeping secrets learn to hear the sentence underneath the sentence.

"Come in before the whole street sees you," I said. "This town gossips like it's paid to."

My kitchen smells the way it has smelled since 1979—coffee, lemon polish, and underneath it all, faintly, the lake. You can take the girl off the water but the water stays in her sinuses. Theo sat at my table with their notebook closed, which I took as a courtesy or a trap, and I poured two cups of coffee because my mother raised me to be hospitable even to the people who might get me killed.

Especially to them, maybe. Mama always said the dangerous ones are the polite ones.

"You were at the exhumation," Theo said.

"Half the town was."

"You left early. Before the lid came off."

"I saw enough." I wrapped my hands around my cup. "A hundred years of that man's legend, and they open the box expecting a saint and find sandbags. I didn't need to see the sandbags. I helped pick out the coffin."

That got their attention. Good. I was tired of being the only one paying attention.

"Mrs. Voss—"

"Ida." I said it sharp, before I could lose my nerve. "If I'm going to tell you what I'm going to tell you, you'll call me Ida. I've been Mrs. Voss for fifty years and it's the name I hid behind. The one before it was Ida Marsh, and Ida Marsh is the one who knew things."

Theo didn't write anything down. Didn't reach for the notebook. Just looked at me with those gray eyes that miss nothing—I'd noticed that about them back at the vigil, how they catalogued a room without moving their head, like a camera with a conscience—and said, "Then tell me, Ida. Whenever you're ready. Or never. Your choice entirely."

And that—that *your choice entirely*—is what broke me open. Not the questions. Not the evidence. Forty years, and nobody ever once gave me the choice. They gave me instructions, warnings, looks across parking lots. But never once did anyone say: this is yours to give or keep.

I looked into my coffee like it was a scrying bowl, and I started talking, and I did not stop for two hours.

* * *

Here is what I told them, and I will set it down here as plainly as I said it aloud, because if I'm going to be a dead woman either way I'd rather be a truthful one.

In 1984, Vera Ashcombe was twenty-six years old and she was going to die. Not might. Was. She'd found the ledger books—the real ones, not the ones Edmund showed the trust board—and she'd done the arithmetic, and Edmund Halloway had been skimming the founding trust since before I was old enough to vote. Nearly a million dollars by then, moved through shell companies with names so boring nobody ever looked twice. Meridian Timber Holdings. The Ashfield Land Company. He was clever the way ice is clever: smooth, and it kills you slow.

Vera went to him. That's the part people never understand about young women like Vera—she wasn't trying to destroy him. She liked him. We all liked him; liking Edmund Halloway was practically a civic duty. She went to him privately, thinking he'd fix it quietly, restore the money, let her keep her dignity and him keep his legend.

Instead he smiled at her—that warm bank-lobby smile—and asked her to stay for dinner, and while she was hanging up her coat he told her, conversationally, the way you'd discuss weather, that the lake was deep in October and that drowned girls stayed found only if someone wanted them found.

She came to me that night. Shaking. She'd known I kept books for half the businesses in town, that I saw where the money went, that I'd noticed the same arithmetic she had. And she said, "Ida, he's going to kill me, and everyone will help him do it, because everyone loves him."

And here is the thing I have carried under my ribs for forty years like a swallowed stone:

She was right. Everyone would have helped. If Edmund Halloway had walked into this kitchen in October of 1984 and told me Vera Ashcombe had to drown, some part of me—a part I am not proud of—would have helped hold her under. Because he was the founder. Because he'd paid for my son's surgery. Because the whole town was built on his generosity the way a house is built on a foundation, and you don't question the foundation, you just live in the house and thank God for the roof.

So yes. I helped her fake it.

It was easier than it should have been. The coroner, Walt Grimes, owed Edmund money from a bad season and hated him for it, which made him exactly the right kind of corrupt—a man who'd falsify a drowning report partly out of fear and partly out of spite, and sleep fine afterward. We used a body from the county morgue, an unclaimed woman, and Walt wrote it up as Vera. I identified the remains. I cried real tears, which surprised me. Grief for a living woman is a strange animal; it doesn't know which direction to run.

We put her on a bus to Spokane with five hundred dollars of Edmund's own embezzled money, which I have always considered the single most satisfying theft-by-proxy in American history. And I went home and told my husband I had a headache, and I lay awake listening to the lake, and I waited for the knock on the door that never came, because Edmund believed it. That's what saved us—not the plan, not Walt's paperwork. Edmund believed it, because Edmund believed that everyone loved him more than they loved the truth.

For eleven years, I slept.

* * *

"And then?" Theo asked, when I finally stopped to breathe. Their voice was very quiet. "What changed?"

"The coffin," I said. "Or rather, what wasn't in it."

I told them about the spring of 1995, when Edmund—old then, seventy-four, his heart failing, his hands spotted like autumn leaves—summoned me to the estate. Not the house. The boathouse, where we wouldn't be seen. And he sat there across from me in a wheelchair he didn't need yet but soon would, and he said, "Ida, I'm dying, and I intend to do it properly."

I didn't understand. So he explained, in that patient tone he used on slow children and slow towns: he had read his own obituary drafts for decades—he commissioned a new one every few years, vanity, pure vanity—and he had decided that the version written in 1984 was the best one. Younger. Cleaner. A man struck down at the height of his powers, mourned by thousands, his legend sealed in marble before age could chip at it.

"He wanted to die in 1984 forever," I said. "Do you understand? He didn't fake his death to escape the embezzlement charges. That was Vera's escape, not his. He faked his death thirty years later because he couldn't stand the idea of dying *late*. Of the papers saying he was eighty-four instead of sixty-three. Of the legend getting a wrinkled ending."

Theo sat very still. "The empty coffin. The centennial exhumation. He planned for it to be found?"

"No." I laughed, and it came out ugly. "No, that's the joke of it, the terrible little joke. He arranged everything—Walt's successor signed the transfer papers, the cemetery plot was bought, the funeral was beautiful, half the state sent flowers. He was buried in 1995 with full honors, in a coffin I personally helped select, because by then I was the town's designated keeper of sad occasions. But his grandson Martin—" I had to stop and drink my coffee, because my hand wasn't steady. "Martin worshipped him. Still does. And somewhere along the way, Martin decided his grandfather deserved better than a grave that time could touch. Or Edmund asked him—I don't know, I swear to you I don't know which. But sometime in the last year, that coffin came up out of the ground and came back empty, weighted with sand, waiting for the centennial like a stage prop waiting for its cue."

"So the exhumation—"

"Was supposed to find him. Bones, dust, a plaque, a ceremony, a hundred years of glory. Instead it found sandbags, because somebody—" I looked at Theo straight on. "Somebody in that family couldn't resist making the miracle perfect. And perfection is the one thing that always gives a lie away. I could have told them that in 1984. I nearly did."

* * *

Theo was silent for a long moment. Outside, the rain had thickened, drumming on the porch roof like impatient fingers.

"Mr. Pell," they said finally. "Did he know? About Vera?"

"Pell knew everything in those archives. He knew about the policy with her signature on it—she signed it three days after her own death, Theo, because Edmund insisted she keep her insurance current, as a taunt, as insurance of his own. Leverage. If she ever surfaced, he had a document proving she'd committed fraud, and he'd have buried her in it." My voice cracked and I let it. "Pell came to see me two weeks ago. First time in twenty years he'd spoken to me outside of church. He said, 'Ida, there's a young investigator asking questions, and I'm frightened.' I told him he was an old fool. I told him nothing gets past the surface in this town, nothing has for forty years." I pressed my knuckles to my mouth. "He climbed that ladder because he always climbed that ladder. And somebody made sure it was his turn to fall."

"Do you believe Martin did it?"

"I believe Martin believes he's finishing his grandfather's work." I said it carefully, because it was the truest and worst thing I knew. "Edmund spent forty years teaching this town that some truths are too heavy to carry. Martin learned the lesson better than any of us. He's not protecting a crime, Theo. He's protecting a *religion*. And religions bury their heretics."

Theo finally opened the notebook—but didn't write, just rested a hand flat on it, like a promise or a restraint. "Why now? Why tell me?"

I thought about it honestly, because I owed myself honesty after four decades of lying by omission.

"Because Pell is dead, and I'm next, and I'd rather choose my hour than have it chosen for me. Because I'm eighty-one and the stone I swallowed in 1984 weighs more every year. And because—" I gestured at the window, at the gray street beyond, at the whole damp devoted town. "Because I watched you at the vigil. You were the only person there listening instead of performing. And I thought: there's someone who might actually carry it. Who might not put it down when it gets heavy."

They held my gaze. "It will get heavy, Ida. It may get dangerous. If I publish what you've told me, your name will be in it. You'd be confessing to obstruction, falsification—"

"I confessed to worse in 1984, child. I just haven't said it out loud until today." I reached across the table and closed my hand over theirs, and their skin was cold, and mine was not, which felt like some kind of victory. "One condition. Vera's name comes out of it however the law requires—but you find her first, and you tell her I'm sorry, and you tell her the stone is out. She carried it too. She shouldn't carry it alone anymore."

Something moved behind those gray eyes—recognition, maybe, or grief. "I may already know where she is," Theo said quietly.

"Then don't lose her again. This town's very good at losing women."

They stood to go, and at the door they paused. "There's one more thing, Ida. When you say Halloway arranged his own burial in '95—you're certain he's dead? Certain there's no chance he—"

I almost laughed. Almost. Then I remembered the photograph Cass's grandmother's album, the tall figure at the edge of the parade crowd, and my laugh died somewhere in my chest.

"Child," I said, "that man has cheated death twice that I know of. I stopped assuming anything about Edmund Halloway in 1984, and I'd advise you to do the same. Check your photographs. Check them all." I pulled my cardigan tight. "And check your locks tonight. Because if Ida Marsh can find the truth after forty years, then whoever killed Walt Grimes' successor knows exactly what I just told you—and they know I told *you*."

Theo left into the rain. I watched them from the window until they turned the corner, and then I did something I hadn't done since 1984.

I took the loose brick out from behind the flour tin, and I took out the letter I'd been keeping dry all these years—Vera's handwriting, shaky, dated the night before her drowning: *If anything happens to me, Ida, it wasn't an accident. Look at the ledgers. —V.*

And beneath it, folded smaller, newer, delivered to my mailbox just yesterday with no return address, in handwriting I would know anywhere, aged but unmistakable, in ink the color of dried blood:

*Ida. Some stones are better left swallowed. Come see me before you do something we'll both regret. You remember the boathouse. —E.H.*

I sat down hard at my kitchen table, the letter trembling in my hand, and understood that the dead man had been reading my mail for a very long time.

Then I picked up the telephone and dialed the number Theo had pressed into my palm at the door, and my hands were shaking so badly it took three tries.
