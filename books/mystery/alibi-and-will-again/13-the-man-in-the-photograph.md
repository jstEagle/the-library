## 13. The Man in the Photograph

My memory has never been a gift so much as a filing system with no off switch. Most people look at an old photograph and see their grandmother squinting into the sun. I see everything: the crooked hem on the banner, the brand of soda in the cooler, the exact number of pigeons on the courthouse roof (fourteen, July 4th, 1987). It's exhausting to be me, frankly, and I say that without self-pity—only as a warning about what happens when a mind like mine finally notices something it should have noticed decades ago.

It happened on a Tuesday, in the basement of the *Halloway Gazette*, surrounded by forty years of parade coverage.

* * *

I'd gone to the newspaper office ostensibly to research centennial coverage for the insurance file—the exhumation was still officially "vandalism," though Chief Okafor and I both knew better, and we'd agreed to keep pretending while she quietly pulled Pell's ladder into evidence. The editor, a harried woman named Dot Ferrell who smoked like a chimney in defiance of three separate ordinances, waved me toward the archive room and told me not to touch anything with my bare hands.

The archive room was a tomb of gray boxes. Every parade, every festival, every ribbon-cutting since 1952, photographed by a succession of Gazette stringers who all had the same instinct: shoot wide, get the crowd, capture the town being itself.

I started with Founders' Day, because of course I did. Edmund Halloway's ghost presided over every frame whether he was in it or not. There he was in 1971, alive, cutting the ribbon at the new library annex, silver-haired and square-jawed, the kind of face that gets carved into courthouse friezes. There he was in '73, dedicating the fire station. In '78, handing over a comically large check to the school board.

Then 1985. The first Founders' Day after his death. The whole town in black bunting. A wreath where the man had been.

And there—at the edge of the frame, half-hidden behind the feed store float, a man in a dark coat watching the procession.

I wouldn't have flagged him. One man at the edge of one photograph is nothing; towns are full of men. But my memory doesn't work in single frames. It works in patterns, cross-referenced, indexed, and something about the set of those shoulders itched like a label sewn wrong-side-out.

So I pulled the next year. 1986. Founders' Day again. And there he was again—not behind the feed store this time but across the street, near the pharmacy, same coat, same posture, hands clasped behind his back like a man inspecting troops. Face turned three-quarters away from the camera.

1987. Same man. Closer to the reviewing stand this time.

I sat back on the metal stool and let the itch become a hypothesis, which is how all my worst discoveries begin.

* * *

Here is what you have to understand about Edmund Halloway's face: I had spent two weeks steeped in it. The estate archives, the Gazette's own retrospectives, the bronze plaque outside the bank, the oil portrait in the library that everyone pretended wasn't slightly flattering. He had a scar through his left eyebrow—a childhood accident, per the legend—and a habit of tilting his chin down so his eyes looked up from under the brow, a pose that photographs as gravitas and probably was, if you were the sort of person who found gravitas useful for embezzling a founding trust.

The man in the parade photos had the tilt. And when I got the 1991 photo under the loupe—Dot's loupe, borrowed without asking, because asking takes time—the scar was there too. Faint. White with age. But present.

Edmund Halloway died in June of 1984. His coffin, exhumed last month, contained sandbags and a lie. I had already accepted that much. What I had not yet accepted—what my mind was now presenting to me with the gentle insistence of a process server—was that the dead founder might not have gone anywhere at all.

* * *

I worked until the light through the basement window went amber and then gone. Dot came down twice, once to complain about the smoking ordinance and once to bring me coffee she claimed was decaf and almost certainly wasn't. By the second cup I had built a timeline.

The man appeared in twenty-three photographs between 1985 and 2009. Always public events. Always parades, memorials, the Fourth of July, the Christmas tree lighting. Never close to the camera. Never looking at it. He aged, technically—but wrongly. Between 1985 and 2009, twenty-four years, he aged perhaps ten. Gray crept into the hair slowly, grudgingly, like frost on a window someone kept wiping. The coat changed twice: wool, then a heavier wool, then—in the last photos—a quilted jacket that made him look like every other retired man in the county, which I suspect was the point.

In 2010, he stopped appearing. Or I stopped finding him. I pulled every event photo from 2010 through the present and came up empty, and I do not come up empty. My memory does not permit it. Either he'd gotten better at hiding, or he'd gotten sicker, or he'd finally done what the town believed he'd done in 1984.

He had been here. Watching parades. Watching the town he'd founded go on loving him, year after year, while he stood at the edge of its joy like a landlord counting his rent.

* * *

Okafor came by at nine that night, because I'd called her at eight and said only, "You need to see this, and I need you to not tell me I'm crazy before you've seen it."

She stood over the spread of photographs on Dot's archive table for a long time. She's good at silence, Okafor. It's one of the things I trust about her—she doesn't fill quiet with reassurance.

"Say it," she said finally.

"Facial reconstruction," I said. "Not the CSI kind. The boring kind. I've been measuring." I laid out my notes: interpupillary distance consistent across frames, ear geometry—I'd traced the right ear from six photos, and ears are as individual as fingerprints—the scar, the chin tilt, the way he stood with his weight on his left leg in every single image, like a man favoring an old injury. "This is the same man across twenty-four years. And this—" I tapped the 1978 ribbon-cutting, Halloway in his prime, "—is the same man eleven years earlier."

"You're saying Edmund Halloway attended his own memorial vigils."

"I'm saying someone did. Someone who shared his skeleton."

"Could be a relative. A brother."

"Halloway was an only child. I've read the centennial biography four times; it says so twice, because the biographer couldn't believe it either. Could be a son nobody knew about. But Martin Halloway is the grandson, and Martin has no brothers, and if there were another branch of that family tree, this town would have erected a statue to it by now."

Okafor picked up the 1996 photo—the Christmas tree lighting, snow coming down, the man in the heavy wool coat standing near the gazebo with his hands clasped behind him. "He looks like he's supervising," she said.

"That's exactly what he looks like."

"Forty years of Sundays," she murmured, echoing what Ida had told us, and then she frowned at her own words like they'd arrived from somewhere else. "Theo. If he's been here the whole time—if he's been *watching*—then Pell didn't die because someone panicked. He died because someone was keeping house. Pruning."

"Yes."

"And Vera Ashcombe, hiding in a lake cabin an hour away, isn't hiding from the law."

"No. She's hiding from a man who never left."

* * *

I want to describe the vertigo properly, because people use that word loosely. Vertigo isn't fear. Fear has an object—you're afraid *of* something. Vertigo is the floor turning out to be a painting of a floor. For two months I had operated on a simple model: Halloway staged his death in 1984, fled, and the town's conspiracy was a machine for maintaining the fiction of his absence. Distance. That was the comfort in it. Whatever else was true, the monster was elsewhere, and the people protecting the lie were doing it out of loyalty to a ghost.

But the ghost had been buying groceries. The ghost had stood thirty feet from the reviewing stand in 1993 while the mayor read a proclamation about his legacy. The ghost had watched children he'd never met lay flowers on his empty grave, and he had come back the next year, and the next, the way you return to a garden you planted.

He hadn't fled Halloway Falls. He had refused to leave it. He'd traded a name for a view.

And worse—much worse, this is the part I keep circling—if he was here, then Ruth Calloway knew. Ruth, who approved the payout, who left me a letter naming E.H., who died in a car accident I have privately reclassified as murder number four. If Halloway stayed, then Ruth's letter wasn't a confession about helping a fugitive escape. It was a confession about knowing he never left. About watching him watch us. About deciding, late, to say something—and paying for it.

I sat in that basement after Okafor left, under the hum of the fluorescent tube that flickers at exactly 1.2-second intervals (I have counted), and I felt the full weight of what my memory had done. Everyone in this town had seen that man at the parades. Hundreds of people, dozens of cameras, twenty-three photographs sitting in open boxes for decades. Nobody saw him because nobody was looking for a dead man among the living. I saw him because I can't stop seeing anything.

There are days I'd give a great deal to be able to forget. Tuesday was not one of them. Tuesday, for the first time since the coffin opened, I was glad to be the filing system with no off switch. Because somewhere in this town—or possibly not far outside it—was an old man who had murdered at least one person to protect a legend, whose grandson was murdering on his instructions, and who believed himself invisible.

He was wrong. I had twenty-three photographs and a memory like ledger paper, and I was going to find him.

* * *

One more thing, and I record it here because it matters, because details matter, because the detail is always where the truth hides.

At the bottom of the 2009 box—the last year he appears, the Founders' Day centennial-eve celebration, the whole town turned out, bunting and brass band and Cass Halloway's generation marching in costumes their grandparents would have recognized—there was a photograph I hadn't catalogued with the others. It showed the crowd from above, shot from the courthouse steps, a sea of faces tilted toward the bandstand.

And at the very edge, partially cropped, the man in the quilted jacket. Not watching the bandstand.

Looking up. Looking directly at the camera on the courthouse steps. Directly, as far as I could measure the geometry, at the photographer.

I checked the Gazette's records for who shot that event. The stringer credit read *M. Halloway*.

Martin had taken the picture. And Edmund—because I am past calling him anyone else—had looked straight into his grandson's lens, and I could swear, magnified and enhanced and blown up until the grain swam, that the old man was smiling.

He knew he was being photographed. He had known for years. Maybe he liked it. Maybe, for a man who had erased himself, being seen even obliquely—even anonymously, just one more gray face in a crowd that adored his myth—was the only pleasure he had left.

Or maybe it was simpler than that. Maybe he was posing for family.

I put the photograph face-down on the table, the way you'd close a casket, and I reached for my phone to call Vera Ashcombe's lake house, because if Edmund Halloway had been home all along, then the woman who faked her drowning to escape him needed to know that the water had never been between them at all.

The line rang four times. Then a voice I didn't recognize answered—male, calm, faintly amused—and said:

"He told me you'd call tonight."
