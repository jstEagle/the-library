## 22. Alibi and Will Again

A year is a strange unit of measurement for a town. It's long enough for the grass to grow back over a scandal and short enough that nobody's quite finished talking about it.

I stood at the edge of Halloway Cemetery on a bright October morning—exactly one year to the day since the centennial exhumation had opened a coffin full of sandbags and forty years of lies—and watched them lower an honest empty box into the ground.

That was the phrase the stonemason had used when I'd commissioned it, back when the town council finally stopped arguing and let me handle the arrangements. *An honest empty coffin.* He'd said it without irony, which in this town counted as progress.

The new stone read only:

**EDMUND HALLOWAY
1921–1984
and whatever remained**

Cass had suggested the last line. She was nineteen now, editor of the college paper two counties over, and she'd driven back for the ceremony wearing a press lanyard like armor. When she'd pitched the epitaph at the planning meeting, half the room flinched and old Mrs. Tarrow had walked out. But Cass had looked at me across the folding tables with that flat, unimpressed stare teenagers reserve for adults who've disappointed them, and said: "It's the only true sentence anyone's proposed all year."

She wasn't wrong. The whole saga had taught me that a town can survive almost anything except an incomplete story. Give them the whole thing—the embezzlement, the staged drowning, the letters from a dead man directing his grandson like a puppeteer with arthritic hands—and they'll grumble, but they'll stand up straighter. Leave them a myth with holes in it, and they'll spend decades quietly stuffing their own secrets into the gaps.

So we gave them everything. My account ran eleven thousand words in the *Meridian Courier*, published under my byline—my real name, my photograph, my face attached to my work for the first time in fifteen years. Theo Lockridge, insurance investigator, formerly a rumor with a briefcase.

The morning of the reburial, people kept coming up to me anyway, as if checking whether the byline had been a typo.

"You're taller than I imagined," said the woman who ran the bakery, which was a lie, because everyone in this town had seen me buy rye bread every Tuesday for a decade. They just hadn't *seen* me. There's a difference, and I'd spent most of my life on the wrong side of it.

"Thank you," I said, because what else do you say to someone admitting they'd looked through you for ten years?

The crowd was smaller than the exhumation crowd had been. Grief draws a bigger audience than truth; that's not cynicism, it's arithmetic. But the ones who came came deliberately. Chief Dana Okafor stood near the front in her dress uniform, hands clasped behind her back, watching the coffin descend with the expression of a woman auditing the ground itself.

She'd run unopposed for sheriff in the spring. Unopposed, after twenty years of the office being passed around like a family heirloom among men who believed law enforcement meant keeping the peace between the powerful and everyone else. Her opponent had withdrawn three days after announcing, citing "family reasons," and Dana had called me that night, furious, certain someone had gotten to him.

"Nobody got to him," I'd told her. "I checked. His wife took a job in Spokane. It's genuinely just Spokane."

"That's somehow worse," she'd said. "I wanted a fight. I wanted to win something."

"You won," I said. "You won by the town deciding it didn't want to fight you. That's harder than beating somebody."

She'd been quiet a moment. Then: "You know what my mother used to say? Trust arrives on foot and leaves in a car." A pause. "This town's walking toward me. Slowly. On bad knees. But it's walking."

Now, at the graveside, Reverend Amos kept his remarks to four minutes—a personal record, I suspected, and possibly a denominational one. He did not call Edmund Halloway a founder or a benefactor or a pillar. He said, "We are here to bury what we know, and to make peace with what we don't," and then he stepped back, and the wind moved through the maples, and that was the eulogy.

It was better than anything I could have written, and I write for a living now. More on that later.

* * *

After the service, I drove out to Ida's place. The house had been repainted—soft blue where the old paint had been gray with neglect—and the porch steps no longer announced themselves as a liability claim. But the garden was the real transformation.

Vera Ashcombe knelt in the middle of it, gloves off, dirt to her elbows, dividing a clump of irises with a knife that had probably seen things I didn't want to catalog. She was seventy-eight and moved like sixty, and when she heard my footsteps on the gravel she didn't turn around.

"Theo Lockridge," she said. "The only man in this county who calls before visiting, and still shows up unannounced."

"I called Ida."

"Ida tells me everything and remembers none of it. You're lucky I'm the one with the knife." She sat back on her heels and squinted up at me. Sunlight caught the lines around her eyes, the ones she'd earned honestly, one year at a time, instead of the ones she'd once faked with a death certificate.

"How is she?" I asked.

"Confused some mornings. Sharp as a tack the rest. Yesterday she told me the story of helping me fake my drowning like it happened to someone else, then cried when she remembered it was her." Vera wiped her hands on her jeans. "Then she made me lunch and told me I planted the tomatoes too close together. Both things were true. That's being ninety-one for you."

"And you? How does it feel, being a free woman in the town that buried you?"

She considered this with the seriousness she brought to everything, which I'd learned was her substitute for sentiment.

"Strange," she said finally. "For forty years I was a ghost haunting other towns. Now I'm a living woman haunting the one place I actually died." She smiled, thin and real. "Halloway took my name, my face, my future. And your confession recording gave them all back. So I figured the least I could do was give this town some tomatoes."

She nodded toward the far bed, where Ida sat in a wheelchair under the maple, supervising with a blanket over her knees and the imperious calm of a retired general.

"He'd have hated this," Vera said. "Edmund. Hated that the stone says *whatever remained*. Hated that the truth got printed. Hated most of all that nobody's afraid anymore." She picked up another iris clump, split it with one clean motion. "Fear was his fertilizer. Everything he grew came out of it."

"Do you forgive him?" I asked. I hadn't planned to. The question just fell out, the way questions do around Vera, who had a way of making silence into a door left open.

"No," she said. "But I've stopped waiting for him to apologize, and that turned out to be the same thing." She glanced at me sideways. "What about Ruth Calloway? Do you forgive her?"

Ruth. My mentor. The woman who'd approved the fraudulent payout in 1984, then spent thirty years trying to undo it, and died with the evidence sewn into a coat lining because she knew exactly what the Halloway machine did to people who talked.

"I'm writing a book about her," I said. "Chapter one is called *Betrayal*. Chapter thirty is called *Amends*. I haven't decided yet if forgiveness gets its own chapter or if it's just the margins."

Vera laughed—a dry, surprised sound, like she'd forgotten the machinery worked. "Margins," she repeated. "Yes. That's where most of the important stuff lives." She pointed the knife at the house. "Go say hello to Ida. She's been practicing a speech."

Ida's speech, delivered from beneath the maple with tremendous dignity, consisted of grabbing my wrist with surprising strength and saying: "You found her. Forty years, everybody said drowned, gone, dead—and you found her. You're a good boy, Theo Lockridge. Don't argue. I knew your mother."

I hadn't known my mother had lived here. Ida refused to elaborate. Some mysteries, I decided, could stay mysteries. Not everything needed to be exhumed.

* * *

The book comes out in March. *Alibi and Will Again.* My publisher wanted a punchier title; I told them the title was the thesis, and they eventually agreed, mostly because I threatened to make the subtitle even longer.

Writing it was the strangest work of my life. Fifteen years of investigating other people's lives had taught me to observe without appearing; writing required the opposite discipline—to appear, fully, on every page, including the parts I'd rather have skipped. The chapter where Martin cornered me in the boathouse and offered me erasure. The chapter where I came home and found everything displaced by exactly one inch, and understood that my invisibility hadn't been a personality trait but a product, manufactured and maintained by people who found me useful. The chapter where I admitted I'd been tempted. God, how I'd been tempted. A clean slate. No history, no enemies, no name. Just a man and a road.

Martin is serving twenty-five years. He wrote to me once, from Fairview Correctional, four pages in a lawyer's careful hand. He didn't apologize. He explained—which, in his world, was the same gesture. *My grandfather built a town on a story,* the letter said. *Stories need maintenance. I was maintenance.* I keep the letter in the same drawer as Ruth's unfinished one, the two documents leaning against each other like arguments that never met.

Dana read the manuscript before anyone. She returned it with sticky notes on forty-one pages, thirty-nine of which said some variation of *"Is this legal to print?"* and two of which just said *"Oof."* Then she called me at midnight and said, "Print all of it. If we're going to be a town that tells the truth, we should start with the ugly parts."

The ugly parts sold well. The honest parts sold better. Readers write to me weekly—investigators, mostly, and the quietly haunted, and always a surprising number of librarians—asking how I remember everything, whether it's a gift, whether it hurts.

It's both, I tell them. It's both, and yes.

* * *

Which brings me to Tuesday.

Dana called me at 7:14 in the morning, which for Dana constituted either an emergency or a social call, and there was no way to know until you answered.

"Lockridge. You busy?"

"I'm drinking coffee and watching a woodpecker assault my gutter. Extremely busy."

"There's a case." A pause, the kind she used when she was deciding how much to admit. "Fella named Corliss, out past the quarry. Fell from his own roof. Ladder's sitting there looking innocent as a church pew."

Something cold moved through my chest—not dread, exactly. Recognition. "Third accident of my career started with a ladder too," I said.

"I know. I was there for the second one." Another pause. "Theo, I'm not asking you to consult. Consult means I hand you a file and you vanish for three weeks and send back a memo. I'm asking you to come down to the station. In person. Where people can see you. Sign the log. Drink the terrible coffee. Be *known*."

I looked out my kitchen window. The woodpecker had given up on the gutter and moved to the fence post, hammering away at nothing in particular, loud and visible and utterly unconcerned with who might be watching.

A year ago, that request would have felt like a threat. Being known meant being targeted. Being targeted meant being erased. I'd spent my whole adult life perfecting the art of the footnote—present in every record, absent from every memory.

But that was before. Before the grave. Before Vera's tomatoes and Ida's grip and Dana's midnight phone calls. Before I learned that invisibility isn't safety; it's just danger with better lighting.

"What time?" I said.

"Nine. Wear the good coat. The one with—" She stopped herself. We both knew which coat. Ruth's coat, the one with the letter sewn into the lining, which I'd had the lining replaced but kept the coat, because some ghosts you keep close on purpose.

"I'll wear it," I said.

I hung up and refilled my coffee and stood at the window a while longer, letting the morning be ordinary. Somewhere past the quarry, a man named Corliss was dead, and someone had arranged him that way, and somewhere in the arrangement was a signature, a habit, a fingerprint of intent that only I would catch—because catching those things was what I did, publicly now, memorably, under my own name.

An alibi, I've come to believe, is just a will to be elsewhere. Edmund Halloway had one for forty years. Vera had one for forty more. I'd carried one my entire life without ever committing a crime—alibied away from my own existence, sworn out against being seen.

I set down the cup, picked up the coat, and went to be seen.
