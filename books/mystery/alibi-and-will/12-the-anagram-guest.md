## 12. The Anagram Guest

The name had been sitting in front of me for three days before I finally saw it.

That was the humiliation of my profession, if I was honest with myself—the thing I never said aloud at the desk. I could hold four hundred guests in my head like a deck of cards: faces, aliases, allergies, lies. I knew which senator paid cash and which opera singer didn't. But sometimes the truth didn't hide from me. It hid *in* me, folded so neatly into the ordinary that my own gift became its camouflage.

I sat in the back office after midnight with the old registration ledgers spread open under a green lamp, the week after Elodie Voss's drowning laid out like an autopsy. Room 412. Checked in on the fourteenth of October, three years ago, under the name **Dolive Vossen**.

Say it fast. Say it slow. Move the letters around your tongue until they rearrange themselves the way grief rearranges a family.

*Dolive Vossen.* Anagram of *Elodie Voss*—every letter accounted for, nothing added, nothing taken away. The dead woman's name, shuffled like a deck by someone with steady hands and thirty seconds to spare at a front desk at two in the morning.

And there was only one person who worked that desk at two in the morning. There had only ever been one person.

Ivo Marchetti had run the Meridian's nights for nineteen years. He was the reason insomniac dowagers requested him by name, the reason a lost child once slept peacefully in a lobby armchair while we searched the building, the reason the night staff called him *il faro*—the lighthouse—because he stood at his post and everything dark eventually came toward him. He had taught me, my first winter, how to read a guest's shoes before their face. He had covered for me twice when I made mistakes that should have cost me the job.

He had also, apparently, checked a drowned woman into room 412 under her own scrambled name, taken her cash without blinking, and entered into the register a departure date for a guest who never left through any door the hotel admitted to having.

I closed the ledger. My hands were not entirely steady, and I resented them for it.

There were two ways to do this. The way that kept me safe: say nothing, note it down, let the knowledge sit in my chest like a swallowed stone. Or the way that kept me human.

At 3:40 in the morning, I took the service elevator down to the cellar.

* * *

The Meridian's wine cellar is the oldest room in the building, older than the lobby's marble, older than the frieze I'd spent two weeks photographing panel by panel when no one was looking. It predates Augustin Voss by eighty years—a vaulted brick throat beneath the east wing where the temperature never changes and the silence has weight. Bottles sleep in iron cages behind gates older than most nations. The staff call it *the crypt*, half as a joke.

Ivo was there because Ivo was always somewhere, and at 3:40 he did inventory. I heard him before I saw him: the soft squeak of his cart wheel, the tick of his pen against a clipboard, and then—faintly, because he thought he was alone—he was humming. Something Neapolitan, off-key, contented.

It occurred to me, standing in the doorway with the shadows pooling around my shoulders, that I might be about to destroy the last uncomplicated affection in my life. Nineteen years of trust, balanced against one anagram.

"Inventory can wait," I said.

The humming stopped. In the dark, I watched him go very still—that particular stillness of a man who has spent decades listening for trouble and has just heard it arrive wearing a familiar voice. Then the lamp over his cart clicked on, and his face came up out of the gloom, weathered and watchful, the eyes doing all the work while the mouth stayed neutral.

"Sable." He said my name carefully, the way you set down something fragile. "You're up late."

"So are you."

"I'm always up late." A small smile, automatic, professional. "That's the job."

"That's the alibi," I said. "The job is what we tell people."

The smile died by degrees. He set down his pen with exaggerated care, aligning it parallel to the clipboard's edge, buying himself three seconds. I gave them to him. I owed him that much, whatever else I owed.

"You've been in the archives," he said. Not a question.

"I've been everywhere," I said. "It's what I do. You taught me that. Read the shoes before the face." I stepped forward into the lamplight. "Room 412. October, three years ago. Dolive Vossen."

For a long moment the only sound was the building itself breathing overhead—the great HVAC lungs of the Meridian, the distant freight-train rumble of the laundry chutes. Ivo looked at me, and I watched something happen in his face that I had seen perhaps five times in ten years behind that desk: the complete abandonment of performance. The mask coming off not because it was pulled, but because the man underneath was too tired to hold it.

"She spelled it wrong first," he said quietly. "Did you know that? She wrote it out herself, shaking so hard she dropped the pen. *Voslen.* And I looked at it and I thought, no, this won't survive even a lazy audit. So I fixed it. Made it elegant." His mouth twisted. "Elegant. That's what I told myself. I made her disappearance *elegant*."

"I need to hear all of it."

"Do you?" He straightened, and there was a flash of the old steel, the night manager who had stared down drunk celebrities and worse. "Do you actually, Sable? Because hearing it costs something. Once you know what I did, you're holding it too. That's how it works. That's how it's always worked in this house." He gestured upward, at the whole sleeping weight of the hotel above us. "Augustin built this place on held things. Every one of us is carrying a bag we can't put down."

"Then put yours down," I said. "Before someone takes it from you."

Something moved behind his eyes—something quick and frightened—and I understood, with the cold click of a lock turning, that I had guessed right about more than the register.

* * *

He poured us both a glass of Barolo from an open bottle he claimed was past its peak, though it tasted like velvet to me, and we sat on upturned crates between the iron cages like two conspirators in a play neither of us had auditioned for.

"She came to me on the Tuesday," he began. "Three days after they pulled the empty boat out of the lake. Everyone thought she was dead. There was a memorial card in the staff room—Marta cried. And then at two in the morning there's a knock at the night desk, and it's Elodie Voss, alive, soaked to the knees, with a bag over her shoulder and her father's checkbook in her coat pocket." He drank. "She'd cut her hair. She looked—God, she looked like a ghost who'd decided to keep going anyway."

"Why you?" I asked. "Why not run to anyone else?"

"Because I was the only one awake," he said simply. "And because—" he hesitated, and I saw him decide to give me the true answer instead of the kind one, "—because she knew I hated him. She'd seen it. We think we're invisible, those of us at the desk. We're not. They see us. She'd watched me take his abuse for years, take it and file it away, and she calculated—I was angry enough to help and disciplined enough not to ask questions. She was right on both counts."

"What did you do?"

"What you already found. Registered her under the anagram, took her cash, walked her down to the loading dock at dawn and put her in a produce truck headed south. Gave her Marta's cousin's number in Trieste." He set the glass down. "She asked me for one more thing. She said, *Ivo, someday someone will come asking questions, and when they do, I want there to be proof I was here.* So I started copying."

"Copying what?"

"The ledger." He said it flatly, watching me. "Don't look surprised. You've been photographing the frieze for two weeks. Did you think the cameras in the corridors are decorative? I installed half of them myself."

The chill that went through me then had nothing to do with the cellar's temperature. "How long have you known what I was doing?"

"Since the second night. You photographed panels forty through sixty-eight and skipped thirty-nine and sixty-nine, which is what a tourist does, but not what a careful person does. A careful person photographs everything so no one knows which part matters." He almost smiled. "You're getting better, though."

"The frieze," I pressed. "You knew what it was. All these years."

"I suspected. Then Elodie confirmed it before she ran. Dates, rooms, initials. Twenty years of payments and threats carved into the decoration everyone admires." He shook his head slowly. "*He kept his sins where everyone could admire them.* Her words. Augustin's joke on the whole world—if anyone ever decoded it, well, it would be their word against the architecture. Who accuses a building?"

"But you copied it. For insurance, you said."

"For insurance," he agreed. "Every year, after inventory, I'd spend a week transcribing the panels into a notebook. Dates, initials, amounts where I could infer them. Three notebooks, by the end. Kept them—" he paused, and I watched the color leave his face in real time, watched the memory arrive like bad news, "—kept them in my flat. In a locked box. Under the floorboards in the bedroom, because I'm a romantic idiot who watches too many films."

The silence stretched. I let it.

"Ivo," I said gently. "Where are the notebooks now?"

"They were there last month." His voice had gone thin. "I check every Sunday. It's ritual. Sunday mornings, coffee, check the box. Four weeks ago I opened the floor and the box was there and the lock was intact and the notebooks were gone. All three. Nothing else touched. Not the cash I keep there, not the papers, not the photographs of my sister. Just the notebooks."

"Who else knows about the box?"

"No one. No one living." He rubbed his face with both hands, and for the first time I saw how old he had gotten while I wasn't watching—how many nights at that desk accumulate in a man's knuckles. "Sable, whoever took them either watched me for months or—"

"Or they didn't need to watch," I finished. "They just needed to know how you think. Where a careful man would hide things. Someone who's known you a long time."

"Hale," he said immediately. "Hale's been going through the archives since the funeral. My cousin works at the records office; she told me someone pulled the estate's full correspondence file under a court seal request. That's Hale's firm's signature move."

"Maybe. Hale is a lawyer; lawyers work in daylight." I turned my glass slowly. "But stealing from you required knowing you. Knowing you kept copies at all. Knowing you well enough to predict the hiding place." I looked up at him. "Who knew, Ivo? Think. Not who you told—who *saw*. Who noticed the weeks every autumn when you vanished into the cellar with a notebook?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it. And I watched the terrible arithmetic happen behind his eyes as he counted the same people I counted: Marta, who cleaned the offices and noticed everything. Dev, who heard everything. Me.

"Us," he said hoarsely. "It was one of us."

"Or someone who used to be one of us," I said. "Someone who learned how this house thinks before she left it. Someone clever enough to fake her own death and patient enough to come back for the evidence."

"You think Elodie stole them? She *asked* me to make copies—"

"She asked you to make copies three years ago," I said. "People change. People get frightened. If she's alive, if she's the one who sent that letter to the funeral mail—and I believe she is—then she needs that ledger more than anyone. And she couldn't exactly ask you for it, could she? Not without revealing herself. Not without trusting you twice."

He stared at me. "You're saying the woman I saved may have robbed me blind."

"I'm saying someone did. And until we know who, we assume the worst about everyone, including each other, including me." I stood, setting the glass atop his cart, next to the aligned pen. "Which brings me to why I really came down here. Because I don't want to assume the worst about you, Ivo. I've spent ten years assuming the best, and it's the only luxury this job ever gave me. So I'm offering a trade instead of an accusation."

"What trade?"

"You help me finish decoding the frieze—all of it, tonight if we have to—and together we figure out who took your notebooks. And when this ends, however it ends, you and I walk out of it on the same side. No bags held over each other's heads. Everything held *between* us instead. Openly."

He was quiet for a long time. Somewhere above us, the freight elevator groaned through its midnight maintenance cycle, and the bottles in their cages seemed to lean in.

"You know what happens if Hale finds out we're talking," he said finally. "If anyone finds out. He's already counting heads, Sable. I've seen him in the lobby at odd hours, doing that thing lawyers do—smiling at people while his eyes move across the room like a man taking inventory. He's deciding who's a problem."

"Then we'd best become a bigger problem," I said, "before he finishes counting."

Ivo laughed—a short, startled bark, the first honest sound I'd heard from him all night. He wiped his eyes with the heel of his hand, stood, and pulled a ring of keys from his belt. At the back of the cellar, behind a rack of vintage port, was a door I had walked past a hundred times and never questioned, because questioning doors was something I'd trained myself out of years ago.

Inside was a folding table, a lamp, and twenty years of the Meridian's secrets stacked in neat, obsessive piles.

"Panel thirty-nine," he said, pulling a chair out for me. "Start there. That's where Augustin got sloppy."

I had barely sat down, barely unclipped my phone to compare the first photographs, when his mobile buzzed against the table. He glanced at the screen out of habit—and went white.

"It's the corridor camera feed," he whispered. "Someone's in the archive room. Right now. And Sable—" he turned the screen toward me, and the grainy image resolved into a figure in a hooded jacket, kneeling beside the shelf where the old reservation registers lived, a bottle in one hand and a rag in the other, "—they're carrying accelerant."
