## 1. Alphabetized Secrets

The microphone in my spare room cost more than my first car, which is not saying much, since my first car was a 1998 Corolla with a passenger seat that reclined without permission. But the microphone is the one extravagance I allow myself, because sound is a kind of honesty. You can lie with your face. You can lie in print. But sit close enough to a good condenser mic and it will catch every tremor, every swallowed breath, every place where your voice decides, on its own, to tell the truth.

I had been sitting close to it for two hours and forty minutes.

"Season finale," I said, and heard my own voice do the thing it does when I say that word — a small lift at the end, like a hand raised in a classroom. "Episode twenty-six. Seven years ago this October, Maren Kowalczyk walked out of the Blue Heron Diner on Route 9 at approximately nine-forty p.m., wearing a green canvas jacket, carrying a paperback copy of *Jane Eyre* with a bus transfer tucked inside as a bookmark, and she has never been seen again."

I paused there, because I always pause there. The pause is for her. It is also, if I am honest with myself — and filing systems are excellent at forcing honesty, everything having exactly one correct place — for me.

"For those of you joining us for the first time: welcome, and I'm sorry. This is not a comfortable story to arrive at late. Maren was nineteen. She worked the dinner shift at the Blue Heron, she saved her tips in a coffee can labeled *ESCAPE FUND* in silver marker, and she had told at least four people — we've confirmed four, possibly five — that she intended to be on a Greyhound to Portland by the end of that week."

I reached for my tea. Off-mic, the studio — spare room, egg-carton acoustic panels my neighbor Dale installed as a favor and then stayed to gossip about — went quiet enough that I could hear the rain starting against the window. October rain. Seven Octobers of it now.

"Here is what we know," I continued, settling back into the chair that had molded itself to my spine over seven years of episodes. "At nine-forty-one p.m., the diner's security camera caught Maren stepping out the side door into the parking lot. At nine-fifty-two, her bicycle was found upright against the marina fence, front wheel still spinning. Spinning, listeners. Someone had recently ridden it or recently moved it, and no one has ever come forward to say which. At ten-oh-three, a witness called in a report of a vehicle idling near the boat ramp. The call lasted eleven seconds. The dispatcher logged it as a possible drunk. The witness's name was redacted from every copy of the log I have obtained through public records requests — and I have obtained four copies, from four different offices, because redundancy is not paranoia, friends, redundancy is *hygiene*."

I smiled at that, alone in the room, because nobody was there to see me smile and that is rather the point of a smile like that one. The private kind. The kind you file away.

What I did not say, what I have never said in two hundred and sixty episodes' worth of recording sessions, is that the redacted name on that eleven-second call belongs to me. That I was the witness. That I was at the marina that night, seventeen years old and somewhere I had promised my mother I would not be, and that I saw something — or believed I saw something, the memory has been folded and refolded so many times it has gone soft at the creases — and that I dialed 911 with shaking hands and hung up after eleven seconds because I heard a car door slam behind me and chose, with the flawless survival instinct of a cornered animal, to become a rumor instead of a person.

That part goes in the files. The files know. The files keep everything.

"Over the past season," I said, "we've covered the marina's ownership dispute, the diner's missing shift ledger, the coroner's office response time on the night of the search — forty minutes, listeners, for a town where Dr. Pell lives eleven minutes from the water — and the curious fact that three separate witnesses described the same gray van and none of them were ever interviewed together. We've done all of that. And tonight, I want to end the season by doing the thing I find hardest, which is to stop talking."

I stopped talking. For a full thirty seconds, which in podcast time is a geological era. Rain on glass. The hum of the mic preamp. Somewhere down the street, Dale's dog registering an opinion about the mail carrier.

"Maren Kowalczyk was a person," I said finally. "Not a case number. Not a season arc. She laughed too loud at her own jokes and she bit her nails during horror movies and she once gave her entire tip jar to a family whose car died in the lot, and then walked home six miles in the dark, complaining about it cheerfully the whole way. If you knew her, the tip line is open. If you were there that night —" and here my voice did the tremor thing, the honest thing, the thing the microphone catches whether I invite it or not — "the tip line is open for you, too. Especially for you. There is no statute of limitations on doing the right thing. There is only the weathering of it. Call me. Let me carry some of it."

I let the last line breathe, then: "This has been *Cold Harbor*, episode twenty-six. I'm Ulyana Nystrom. Stay curious, stay kind, and lock up your secrets — someone always has a key."

I hit stop. Saved the file as CH26_FINAL_v3, because v1 and v2 live in the archive folder where all my abandoned courage goes, alphabetized between CH26_FINAL_v1 and CH27_DRAFT_NOTES.

Then I did what I do every night. I filed.

The Nystrom system is, I will admit, a masterpiece. Every document scanned, named, dated, cross-referenced. Physical originals in acid-free sleeves in banker's boxes labeled by year and subject. KOWALCZYK, M. — DISAPPEARANCE takes up four full boxes now, sub-divided: WITNESS STATEMENTS, MEDIA COVERAGE, FINANCIAL RECORDS, THE MARINA, THE DINER, CORONER'S OFFICE, and — thinnest box, lightest box, the one I handle least — NYSTROM, U. (PERSONAL/SEALED). That last one sits on the highest shelf, behind a row of Dale's old true-crime paperbacks he lent me and never asked for back, which serve as camouflage and, I suppose, as a warning from the universe about lending things you want returned.

Everything in its place. Everything knowable, sortable, containable. People think I organize the case because I'm thorough. What I actually organize is myself. A life arranged alphabetically cannot ambush you. A secret in a sealed box on a high shelf is a secret under guard.

This is what I told myself, anyway, at eleven-forty that night, standing barefoot in the spare room in my flannel robe, resleeving a photocopy of the diner's tax records, when my phone lit up on the desk and made me jump hard enough to scatter sleeve protectors across the floor like startled birds.

Voicemail. Unknown number. Local prefix.

I almost didn't listen. Unknown numbers at midnight are how horror movies begin, and I of all people know how horror movies end — badly, and with poor filing afterward. But curiosity is the engine that runs this whole operation, so I pressed play and held the phone to my ear.

Static first. Wind, maybe. Then a voice. Male, low, unhurried, and strange in a way I couldn't immediately name — until I could. It was strange because it was *calm*. Not drunk-calm or wrong-number-calm. Calm the way a person is calm when they have rehearsed.

"You said tonight there's no statute of limitations on doing the right thing." A pause, long enough that I checked the screen to make sure the message hadn't ended. "You're right, Ulyana. I've thought about it for seven years. Almost every night. And here's what I keep coming back to." Another pause. Rain on his end too, I realized. He was outside. Or near a window. Near water? "You told them everything you know. Except the part that matters. Except what you *saw*."

My knees found the edge of the desk chair without my permission.

"So here's my question for the great Ulyana Nystrom, keeper of files." The voice softened, almost gentle, almost fond, and that was the worst part, worse than anything — "If you won't say it out loud... would you at least *file* it?"

Click.

I stood in my tidy room with my tidy boxes and listened to the rain, and for the first time in seven years the alphabet felt less like a fortress and more like an index — a neat, comprehensive list of everything someone else might already know how to find.

On the high shelf, behind Dale's paperbacks, the thin box marked NYSTROM, U. seemed suddenly very visible. Very reachable. As if it had been waiting all along for someone besides me to come looking.

* * *

I did not sleep. I am telling you this plainly because everything afterward depends on it: I did not sleep, and sleeplessness makes archivists reckless.

By three a.m. I had pulled the sealed box down and gone through it page by page on the floor, wearing gloves I did not need, because ritual is its own comfort. My original handwritten notes from that autumn. The printout of the 911 log with my name blacked out — though the blackout bar, I noticed again with a fresh chill, was thinner than the other redactions, as if whoever redacted it had hesitated. And my witness statement. The statement I gave, eventually, weeks later, to a detective named Ansel Voss, in a room that smelled like burnt coffee and pencil shavings, in which I told the truth about being near the marina and a carefully curated half-truth about what I'd seen.

Page one. Page two. Page three.

There should have been a fourth page. I remembered signing a fourth page — remembered the specific scratch of that particular pen, the cheap ballpoint chained to the table, remembered Detective Voss turning the pages face-down as he collected them, one, two, three —

Three.

I counted twice. Three pages, and a staple hole in the upper left corner of page three where a fourth sheet had been attached and then removed. Torn out cleanly, recently, the fibers still bright against the yellowed paper.

I sat on the floor of my fortress at three-seventeen in the morning, holding evidence that someone had been inside my files — inside *my* files, inside the one system on earth I trusted — and I thought about the voicemail. *Would you at least file it?*

Someone had a key.

And whoever it was had taken exactly one page: the page that described what I saw at the marina the night Maren Kowalczyk's bicycle wheel was still spinning.
