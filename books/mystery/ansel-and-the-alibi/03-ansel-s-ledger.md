## 3. Ansel's Ledger

The ledger lived in the bottom drawer of Ansel Voss's desk, beneath a dead flashlight, three years of unpaid parking tickets he'd kept out of sentiment, and a tin of pipe tobacco he no longer smoked because his daughter had staged an intervention involving a laminated chart of his lungs.

He had not opened the drawer in seven years. He told himself this every time he opened it, which was roughly once a month.

Tonight the house was quiet in the particular way houses get when you've stopped filling them with noise. His wife's photograph on the mantel. The clock in the kitchen that ran four minutes fast and had run four minutes fast since the Clinton administration, because fixing it would mean admitting time was something that happened to him. Ansel poured two fingers of rye, carried it to the desk, and pulled the drawer open with the resigned tenderness of a man greeting an old dog he'd once kicked.

The ledger was black, cloth-bound, its corners soft as bread. *Kowalczyk, Maren E.* — the label still legible in his own careful hand. He set it beside the glass and did not touch it for a long moment. Somewhere outside, a neighbor's wind chimes were having opinions about the weather.

"Fine," he said to the empty room, which had heard worse from him. "Let's have it out."

* * *

He read the way he used to work: slowly, twice, with a pencil for notes and a second pencil in case the first one lied.

The early pages were all procedure and weather. October ninth, seven years ago. Maren Kowalczyk, nineteen, last seen at the Blue Heron Diner at 9:40 p.m., tip jar untouched, cherry pie slice paid for and never collected — a detail that had haunted him more than any blood ever had. A girl who ordered dessert and then walked out into the dark without eating it was a girl who expected to come back. Or who was made to leave faster than pie could be served.

His notes were good. He could say that about himself without blushing, which was rare. Witness interviews cross-referenced. Tide tables for the reservoir printed and annotated. A map of the marina with grease-pencil circles like bruises.

Then page forty-one.

Ansel turned to it the way you turn toward a sound in another room. There it was, in his handwriting, dated October tenth:

*2:15 a.m. — canvass of Shoreline Rd. negative. No vehicles observed. No persons observed.*

It was a lie. It was the only lie in four hundred pages of truth, and it sat there among the honest entries like a counterfeit bill in a church collection plate.

Because at 2:15 that morning, Ansel Voss had been standing at the end of Shoreline Road, and he had seen a vehicle. And near the vehicle, half-hidden by the boathouse shadow, he had seen a person — slight, dark-haired, frozen in the headlights like a deer that had read too many true crime books and knew exactly what headlights meant.

A girl. Seventeen, maybe eighteen. Not Maren. Someone else entirely, someone who had no business being at the marina at two in the morning except that she had been there earlier, she had seen something, and now she stood in the spill of his high beams looking at him with an expression he had never forgotten and never learned the name for. Terror, yes. But also something underneath it. Recognition, almost. As if she'd already decided what he was going to do to her and was bracing for it.

He had gotten out of the car. She had backed away. And he — twenty-two years on the job, a man who lectured rookies about the sanctity of the report — had looked at this frightened kid and understood, in one cold flash, exactly what would happen if he wrote her name down.

She'd be interviewed. Re-interviewed. Subpoenaed. Her name would travel from his notebook to the sheriff's log to the county attorney's file to some defense attorney's discovery packet, and from there into the town's mouth, where names went to be chewed. She was a witness to something at the marina the night Maren Kowalczyk vanished, and this town had never once protected a witness it could ruin instead.

So he had written *negative. No persons observed.* And he had driven her home instead — she wouldn't tell him her address, so he'd followed her walking, at funeral speed, until she let him turn his lights off and disappear.

He never got her name. That was the part that still amazed him. Seven years, and he had protected an anonymous girl so thoroughly that he'd protected her right out of his own case file. All he had was the memory: dark hair, a green coat, and eyes that had looked at him like he was either her salvation or her executioner and hadn't decided which.

The decision had cost him his badge eventually, though not directly — nothing is ever direct in small towns. When the case went cold and the state review board came sniffing, they found his canvass entry didn't match the diner's security timestamp, found gaps, found a detective who'd gone strangely quiet about one stretch of road. They didn't find the girl. They just found enough irregularity to make *resignation pending investigation* sound like mercy. He'd taken it. His protégé Dale Merrick — bright, ambitious, a young man who alphabetized his feelings — had watched him pack his desk and said nothing, which Ansel had always counted as a kindness.

Now Dale was sheriff. And Ansel was a consultant, which was a word meaning *old man we call when we feel guilty.*

Ansel closed the ledger and drank.

* * *

Here was the thing he could not stop turning over, the way you turn over a stone to check what's underneath even though you know: the body they'd pulled from the reservoir yesterday had been dressed in clothes described — precisely, lovingly, horribly — in episode twelve of a podcast called *The Cold File*. Ulyana Nystrom's podcast. Ulyana Nystrom, who had spent seven years taking Maren Kowalczyk apart and putting her back together on air, week after week, with the patience of a woman rebuilding a birdcage around herself.

He'd listened to every episode. Of course he had. He'd listened the way a man reads letters from someone he wronged — hungrily, wincing, unable to stop. And somewhere around episode nine he had begun to suspect what he now could not un-suspect: that Ulyana Nystrom knew things about October ninth that were not in any public file. Things that were not in *any* file but his.

The blouse. The scarf tied left over right. The victim arranged facing the water, hands folded — details Ulyana had described on air as *reconstruction*, as *imagination*, as *what I believe Maren's last moments might have held.* Everyone had praised her intuition. Ansel had sat in his kitchen with the volume low and felt the hair rise on his arms, because that wasn't imagination. That was memory wearing a costume.

And now a dead woman had been dressed out of that memory and floated in the reservoir like a message in a bottle, addressed in handwriting only two people in the world could read.

One of them was him.

The other, God help him, might be the girl in the green coat.

Unless — and here his mind went to a place it hated going — unless the girl in the green coat and the podcaster were the same person, and had been all along, and the whole seven-year broadcast had been a confession delivered sideways to an audience of strangers because she couldn't deliver it to him.

He thought of her eyes across the tape line yesterday. Seven years, and she still looked at him like he was a verdict pending.

"Oh, you *idiot*," he told himself gently, and refilled the glass.

* * *

He worked past midnight, because guilt in Ansel Voss metabolized into paperwork the way other men's guilt metabolized into whiskey or prayer. He made lists. He was, he admitted, a man whose idea of passion was a well-ruled margin.

List one: everyone who could know about the staging details. The coroner's office — Dr. Pell, who had signed off on the autopsy with a speed that struck Ansel as either efficiency or eagerness. The deputies first on scene. The podcast audience, thousands strong, but the staging details weren't in the episodes themselves, only their *shadows* — you'd need the drafts, the unreleased notes, the mind behind the microphone.

List two: everyone who benefited from Maren staying missing. This list was longer than it should have been, and that was the town's real confession. The diner owner whose true-crime pilgrims tripled her summer takings. The marina owner whose insurance settlement required no body. The county officials who'd converted a tragedy into a budget line. Grief, in Harrow Creek, was a renewable resource, and everyone had staked a claim.

List three, written smaller, in the back of the notebook where he kept things he wasn't ready to look at directly: *Find the girl in the green coat. Ask her forgiveness. Ask her help. In whichever order she can bear.*

Because here was what the staging told him, plain as a ransom note: whoever did this knew about that night. Knew about the marina at 2:15 a.m. Knew there had been a witness. Which meant either the killer had been there too, watching the watcher —

— or the killer had gotten inside whatever channel connected that night to that podcast, and was using it to speak to the one person who'd understand.

Either way, the message had a recipient. Either way, the girl in the green coat was in danger, whether she was a stranger he'd failed or a woman he'd loved from across seven years of silence and one crime-scene tape.

Ansel looked at the ledger, at his one beautiful lie, and felt the old guilt change shape. Guilt that sits still rots. Guilt that moves becomes something else. Resolve, maybe. Or recklessness. At his age the two wore the same coat.

He took a fresh sheet, wrote *NYSTROM, U.* at the top, and underlined it twice.

Then the phone rang, and the clock — four minutes fast, faithful as ever — said it was far too late for anything good.

Merrick's voice, when Ansel answered, was carefully level, which was how Dale sounded when he was terrified. "Ansel. We found something at the Nystrom woman's place. Somebody left a letter in her mailbox." A pause, the kind that has a body in it. "You're going to want to see what's quoted in it."

Ansel was already reaching for the ledger, and for his coat, and for the name he'd protected so well that protecting it had become the only prayer he knew.

"Don't let her talk to anyone," he said. "I'm coming."
