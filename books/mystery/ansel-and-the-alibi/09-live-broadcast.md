## 9. Live Broadcast

Ulyana had always believed the truth was a thing you filed away, alphabetized, kept dry and dark until it stopped bleeding. Tonight she was going to do the opposite, and her hands were shaking so badly she had to retake the microphone three times before the levels looked right.

The attic studio smelled of cedar and dust and the ghost of a hundred recording sessions. Below her, the house sat dark on purpose — every lamp off, the porch light unscrewed, her car parked two streets away at the diner where Rosa had agreed, for the price of one exclusive interview, to say nothing and leave the pie case light on as a beacon.

"Emergency episode," she said, and her voice steadied the way it always did when the red light came on. That was the strange mercy of broadcasting: the voice knew the job even when the woman didn't. "No music, no sponsor reads, no gentle wind-down. If you're hearing this, it's because I couldn't wait until Thursday."

She had written the script herself, every word, and every word was a lie dressed in truth's clothing.

"For seven years I've told you what I knew about Maren Kowalczyk. Tonight I'm going to tell you what I *have*." She let the pause breathe. "Three days ago, I found something in my house. Something I've kept hidden, even from you, even from the sheriff's department. A piece of evidence from the night Maren disappeared. I'm not saying what it is on air. But it's real, it's physical, and it's been sitting in my closet — the cedar one, the one with the broken latch — inside a hatbox, under the winter sweaters. And I believe the person who took Maren from us knows it exists."

There. Planted. The fabricated detail sat in the airwaves now like a hook in dark water.

She kept going, because a trap that sounded thin was a trap that failed. "I've thought about going to the sheriff. I've thought about burning it. But evidence belongs to the living, and Maren deserves —"

The floorboard in the hallway below creaked.

Ulyana went silent, hand hovering over the fader. The house had a vocabulary; she knew it the way she knew her files. That creak was the third board from the stairs, the one that only spoke under a foot that didn't live there.

She did not stop recording. That was the whole point. She leaned closer to the microphone and let her fear become broadcast.

"Someone's here," she whispered. "If anything happens to me, everything you've heard tonight is true, and the hatbox is —"

She killed the mic, killed the light, and climbed into the crawlspace behind the water tank with her phone in her fist and her heart going like a trapped bird. Through the gap in the attic floor she could see the hallway below, a gray rectangle of moonlit nothing.

Then, softly, from downstairs, a voice she knew from a hundred town meetings, a hundred gentle condolences: "Miss Nystrom? It's Dr. Pell. I saw your porch light was out. I just wanted to make sure you were all right."

* * *

Ansel Voss had taken worse beatings, but never from a man he'd once shared coffee with at the marina, and never while the whole town watched the sheriff's department escort him home like a criminal in his own story.

His ribs sang. His left eye had swelled to a sliver. But when Ulyana's text came through — *attic. now. bring Delgado* — he was already moving.

"You're supposed to be under house arrest," Sheriff Delgado said in the passenger seat, driving too fast down Hollow Pine Road, her siren unlit.

"I'm under *suspicion*," Ansel said. "Different animal. House arrest requires paperwork, and you've been too busy leaking my file to the *Courier* to file any."

"I did not leak your file."

"You leaked my *character*. Close enough." He pressed his palm against his ribs. "She baited him, Marta. She put a lie on the radio and waited to see who came to steal it. Seven years ago I would have told her that was reckless."

"And now?"

"Now I think it's the bravest thing I've ever heard." He looked out at the dark houses sliding past, porch lights off, dogs silent. "Turn here. If Pell's already inside, we do this quiet. I want him talking, not running."

Delgado glanced at him. "You've been listening to her podcast."

"Every episode. Twice." He almost smiled, and it hurt his face. "I had seven years of catching up to do."

* * *

From the crawlspace, Ulyana watched Dr. Pell move through her bedroom with the unhurried competence of a man who had spent thirty years in rooms where nobody argued with him.

He was not what the town's imagination had made him. He was small, tidy, silver-haired, with the kind of face people trusted at funerals. He wore a rain jacket despite the clear sky. In his gloved hands he carried a flashlight with a red filter, and he used it the way she imagined he used his instruments — precisely, without sentiment.

He opened the closet. The broken latch gave its little complaint. He lifted the winter sweaters, found the hatbox, and set it on the bed.

Ulyana had filled it with newspaper and one of Ansel's old case photographs, printed from the podcast archives. Bait inside the bait. She wanted him to open it. She wanted his gloves on it. She wanted his fingerprints in her bedroom at one in the morning with a red-filtered flashlight and a rain jacket on a dry night.

Pell stood over the box for a long moment. Then, without touching it, he said, in a conversational murmur, "You can come down now, Miss Nystrom. I know you're in the attic. I heard the floorboard."

Her blood went to ice water.

"I also know the box is empty of anything real," he continued, "because I was at the marina that night, and I know exactly what was and wasn't taken from that scene. Which means you're recording this." He tilted his head back, addressing the ceiling, addressing her. "Clever girl. Maren was clever too. Did you know that? She noticed things. That was her whole problem."

Ulyana's thumb hovered over her phone. She pressed record again. If he was going to confess to her ceiling, her ceiling was going to keep the receipt.

"Why her?" she called down, before she could stop herself. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "She was sixteen."

Pell considered the hatbox. "She saw my van," he said. "At the marina, at eleven-forty at night, when I was supposed to be home. Do you know what a small town does with that? A coroner who visits the water before there's a body? I'd have been finished. Ruined over nothing — over a *meeting*, Miss Nystrom, a private arrangement, nothing worse." He shook his head. "But then she went in the water, and suddenly it was everything. And then there was a witness who saw the van and said nothing for seven years. Until she started a podcast."

"I never said —"

"You said everything," Pell said gently. "Episode four. The angle of the moon. Episode nine. The smell of diesel. Episode twelve, where you dressed that poor drowned woman in your own clothes' description, detail for detail — you were telling *me*, weren't you? All these years, you were telling me you remembered." He picked up the hatbox at last, weighing it. "I've listened to every episode. Twice."

Footsteps below — not Pell's. Heavy, uneven, a man favoring bruised ribs. Then the porch, then the stairs, and Ansel Voss filled the bedroom doorway with Sheriff Delgado behind him, weapon drawn, and the whole tableau froze like a photograph someone would study for years.

"Dr. Pell," Ansel said. His voice was wrecked and calm at once. "Put the box down. You're holding evidence."

"Am I?" Pell smiled, and it was the smile from the funerals. "Or am I holding nothing at all? Because your witness fabricated it. Fabrication, Detective. I believe that's a crime. I believe *you* know all about those."

"I do," Ansel said. "One falsified entry, seven years ago, to protect a terrified girl. I lost my badge for it and I'd do it again. What's your excuse?"

Pell's smile thinned. He looked at the box, at the window, at the door — Ulyana could see the arithmetic running behind his eyes, and she could see it failing.

"Dr. Pell," came Delgado's voice, low and even. "The house is surrounded. The broadcast is live. Everything you've said in the last four minutes is on a recording in this attic, and I'd very much like to hear the rest of it in an interview room, with the tape running, where it counts."

For a moment — one long, suspended moment — Ulyana thought he might run, might lunge, might make them tackle a seventy-year-old man among her winter sweaters. Instead he set the hatbox down with terrible gentleness, the way he must have set down a hundred things in his careful career, and folded his hands.

"She was a clever girl," he said, to no one, to everyone. "It was never supposed to —" He stopped himself, but it was too late; the sentence had already been said into a live microphone and a dead girl's name.

* * *

Later — much later, after the cruiser doors, after the deputies, after Delgado had personally carried the hatbox out as if it were made of glass — Ulyana came down from the attic on legs that barely worked and found Ansel sitting on her kitchen floor, of all places, his back against the cabinets, eating the emergency granola bar she kept behind the flour.

"You're bleeding on my linoleum," she said.

"It's mostly bruise." He looked up at her, one eye swollen nearly shut, and smiled with the whole of the other half of his face. "Episode thirteen. Live from the attic. You know the listeners are going to say you planned the whole thing."

Ulyana slid down the cabinets until she was sitting beside him, shoulder to shoulder, the granola bar wrapper crackling between them.

"Let them," she said. Then, quieter: "Ansel. The recording caught him. But it caught me too. Everything I said on air tonight — the hatbox, the evidence — it was all fabricated. And tomorrow, when I give my real statement, the real one, the one from that night at the marina..." She turned to look at him. "It clears you. And it ends me. The anonymous witness. The whole seven years."

Ansel was quiet for a moment. Outside, a deputy's radio crackled; somewhere in town, a podcast was already downloading.

"Ulyana," he said, "I falsified a federal case file to keep you safe. You think I'm going to talk you out of the truth now?" He reached over and, very carefully, turned her phone face-up on the floor between them. The recording app was still running. Red light on.

"Besides," he said, "your microphone's live. You might as well start talking."
