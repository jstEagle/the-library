## 11. Ansel's Secret Name

The mortuary at night was a different building than the mortuary by day.

By day it was fluorescent and procedural — clipboards, glove sizes, the low hum of the cooler compressors, Tobias Merrow's voice carrying from the front office the way his father's voice had carried before him. By night it was all wood and shadow, the smell of formalin gone sweet and old in the walls, and the bodies in their drawers quiet in a way the living never managed. Ansel Voss had worked the night shift for six years because the night shift didn't ask questions, and because the quiet let him think, and because — though he'd never have said it aloud — he liked being the last person to keep a body company before the world took it back.

Tonight he wasn't thinking about the bodies. He was thinking about the envelope in his locker.

It had been there since morning. He'd opened it, read it twice, and put it back, and then he'd gone through the motions of his day — intake paperwork, a cosmetics consult with a widow who kept apologizing for crying — while the words sat in his chest like a swallowed stone.

*If you are reading this, then Elodie Halloran has found you, or you have found her. Either way, it's time. I'm sorry I didn't have the courage to say it while I was alive. I was a coward in life and I am trying not to be one in death. Ask her to come. Tell her everything. She deserves to hear it from you, not from a folder.*

Corwin Ashe's handwriting. Ansel knew it well by now — two years of letters, delivered quarterly to a post office box Ansel had never told anyone about, each one a little longer, a little more apologetic, each one stopping just short of the thing. Ashe had been a man who could only confess in installments. The final installment had been the will itself, and now this, the postscript, the instruction manual for the detonation.

Ansel checked the clock. Eleven forty. Elodie Halloran was sitting in the arrangement room, because he'd called her an hour ago and said, *I need to tell you something, and I'd rather do it here than anywhere else,* and she had said, *I'll be there in twenty minutes,* without asking what. That was the thing about Elodie. She didn't ask what. She just came, with her lawyer's coat and her lawyer's face and something underneath both that was less lawyerly than she probably believed.

He made coffee because his hands needed a job. The machine in the break room was ancient and produced something that tasted like hot cardboard, but Elodie had drunk two cups of it the night they'd first traded truths over the intake records, so he made it the same way, and he carried two cups down the corridor to the arrangement room where she sat with her coat folded over her knees, looking at the framed certificates on the wall like she was auditing them.

"You called it bad coffee last time," Ansel said, setting the cup beside her.

"It is bad coffee. I'm developing a taste for it. It's honest about what it is." She took the cup. "You look terrible."

"Thank you."

"That wasn't a compliment. It was an observation. You look like a man who's been carrying something for a long time and has just decided where to set it down." She turned the cup in her hands. "Or dropped it. Those aren't always the same thing."

Ansel sat down across from her, in the chair meant for grieving families, and understood that this was exactly what it was for. He was about to grieve something. He'd just never done it in front of another person before.

"Do you know why I work nights?" he said.

"Because the dead are better company than the living. You said that the first night. I thought it was a joke. Then I decided it wasn't."

"It's half a joke. The other half is that I've spent my whole life not being looked at. Nights are where the not-being-looked-at is easiest." He wrapped his hands around his own cup, not drinking. "My name isn't the one I was born with. That's where this starts."

Elodie went very still. He'd learned to read her in the weeks since the partnership — the stillness was attention, not alarm. Her stillness was the sound of a lawyer's mind opening a drawer.

"Voss is an adoption name," he said. "I was adopted at six weeks old. Closed records. I grew up in Duluth with a couple named Voss who were decent people and are both dead now, and I want to say that clearly, because what I'm about to tell you isn't a complaint about them. They were kind. They were just kind to a boy who always felt like a letter that had been delivered to the wrong address."

"You searched," Elodie said quietly. "When they died."

"When my mother died. Eleven years ago. I petitioned, I hired someone, I got nowhere — closed records in this state might as well be sealed tombs. So I did it the way I do everything. I got a job near death." He almost smiled. "You think that's a strange career choice. It isn't. It's the most logical career choice a person like me could make. Dead people don't lie and dead people leave paper. You taught me that, actually, before I ever met you. I'd been doing it for years. You just do it with wills and I do it with intake forms."

"Ansel." Her voice was careful. "What does this have to do with Maren?"

And there it was. Her sister-in-law's name, spoken plainly in the arrangement room of the only funeral home in a county that had ruled Maren Halloran's drowning an accident, and Ansel felt the stone in his chest shift, and he understood that he could still stop — that he could stand up, apologize, invent something, and walk back into the quiet of the night shift and never be looked at. That door was right there. It had been right there his whole life.

Corwin Ashe had taken it away from him in the end, the old coward. Or given it to him. Both.

"Her name was Maren Halloran," Ansel said, "and she was twenty-four years old, and she was found in the water off Harrow Point on the fourteenth of September, two years ago, and the ruling was accidental drowning, and everyone accepted it, including — I'm sorry, Elodie — including you, for a while."

"Not including me. Not entirely. I just had nothing." Elodie's knuckles had gone white around the cup. "Go on."

"I know about her because of a photograph." He reached into his shirt pocket and took out a folded newspaper clipping, soft at the creases from two years of handling, and laid it on the table between them. It was the anniversary piece the *Herald* had run — a small photo of a woman on a dock, laughing at someone out of frame, the caption: *MAREN HALLORAN, 1989–2021.* "My mother kept a box. Adoption mothers aren't supposed to keep boxes, but she did, and after she died I found it. There were two photographs in it. One was of a baby. Me. The other was of a young woman holding that baby on a porch, and she was laughing, and on the back, in my mother's handwriting, was a name. Not mine. Hers. *Maren.*"

The cooler hummed. Somewhere down the corridor a door eased shut on its own weight.

"I didn't know who she was," Ansel said. "There was no surname, nothing to search. I carried the photograph for eleven years like a coin in my pocket. And then two years ago I'm reading the paper on my break and there she is. Older. On a dock. Dead." His voice did something he didn't authorize — it cracked, and he let it, because he'd decided, coming down the corridor with the coffee, that tonight he wasn't going to manage himself. "Do you know what it's like to find your mother in the obituaries? To learn she existed, that she had a face, that she laughed at someone out of frame — and that she'd been dead for three days before you saw it? That the whole time I was petitioning the state, she was alive, forty miles from Duluth, and neither of us knew?"

"Ansel—"

"Let me finish. If I stop now I won't start again." He breathed. "I came here. To this county. I told myself it was to see where she'd lived. Then I told myself it was to see where she died. Then Corwin Ashe walked into the front office to arrange a service for a cousin of his, and I recognized his name from the article, and he looked at me — Elodie, he *looked* at me, the way you look at a photograph you already own — and three days later Tobias offered me a job I hadn't applied for. Nights. No questions. Above-market pay. I took it because I needed to be near her, and because I was twenty-six and stupid with grief for a woman I'd never met."

"And Ashe," Elodie said. "He knew."

"He knew." Ansel laughed, and it came out wrong. "He knew the whole time. He hired me, Elodie. He arranged it — I found that out a year in, when he started writing to me. Quarterly letters to a box I'd opened under my own name because I'm a careful man and even careful men are idiots. The first letter said, *I know who you are. I'm sorry. Please don't leave.* And I should have left. I should have gone to the police, or to you, or anywhere. But he wrote that he'd loved her. That he'd been trying to find out what happened to her for two years and getting the same wall everyone gets. And he wrote — this is the part I've read forty times — he wrote, *I put you where I could keep an eye on the body. Forgive me for saying it that way. It's the only honest way to say it.*"

"The body," Elodie repeated.

"Maren's case. The body of the lie. He wanted someone watching it from the inside — someone with a reason never to stop watching — and he decided that someone was her son, and he was right, and I hate him for it and I'm grateful to him and I don't know how to hold both of those at once." Ansel set down the cup because his hands had started to shake and he didn't want to spill it. "He paid the mortuary through a consulting arrangement — I found the ledger, you've seen the ledger, the one with the monthly disbursements that don't match any vendor. That was me. That was my salary for being a son who didn't know he was a son, keeping watch over a closed file. And then two months ago he got sick — he told me in the last letter, pancreatic, fast — and he stopped being able to wait for the truth to surface on its own. So he wrote the will. The will that shouldn't exist. The will that put you on my doorstep like a delivered letter."

Elodie had not moved. But her face had changed — the lawyer's face had gone, and what was under it was the thing he'd guessed at the first night over the bad coffee: a person who had also lost someone to a ruling of *accidental*, and had also spent years being told to accept paper over truth.

"Maren had a son," Elodie said slowly, "and Daniel never told me. Vivienne never told me. The family never—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "I married into that family, Ansel. I sat at their table. I stood at Maren's memorial service and held my wife's hand while her brother said she was taken by the water, and there was a child, and no one said a word."

"Because the child was the word they couldn't say." Ansel took the clipping back, and his thumb found the edge of it, the worn place. "Maren was pregnant when she died. Not visibly — six weeks, maybe. I know that because Ashe told me, in a letter, that he'd learned it from her doctor's records after the fact, and that it was the thing that made him stop believing the drowning was an accident. A woman planning a future doesn't swim alone at night off Harrow Point. A woman planning a future doesn't—"

He stopped. The room tilted slightly, the way it did when he stood up too fast, except he was sitting.

"Wait," he said. "No. That's not right. That's not—"

"Ansel. Slowly."

"The baby." His own voice sounded far away. "I was adopted at six weeks. Six weeks. She died two years ago and I'm thirty-one, so the timeline doesn't—" He pressed his palms against his eyes. "I've had this wrong. I've had this wrong for eleven years and I need to say it out loud before it eats me."

"Say it."

"Maren isn't my mother." He lowered his hands. The clipping lay on the table, the woman laughing on the dock, and it looked different now, or he looked at it differently. "Maren was going to have a baby. Two years ago. And I was born thirty-one years ago. Which means the photograph in the box — the woman on the porch — that's not her. Or it is her, but younger, and the baby isn't me, or—" He was spiraling and he knew it, the intake-form mind that never failed him failing him now. "Elodie, I have built two years of my life on being her son and the arithmetic just fell apart in front of me."

"No," Elodie said. And then, with the particular gentleness of a person correcting a client on the record: "No, I don't think it has. Listen to me. The birth certificate I found in Ashe's sealed accounts — the one with the redacted mother — I assumed it was Maren's own birth record, redacted to hide her connection to the Ashe family. But what if it isn't? What if it's a *child's* birth certificate, and the redaction isn't hiding who the mother was from the child. It's hiding who the child became from everyone else."

"A second child," Ansel said. "Maren had a child before. Years before. And that child was placed for adoption, and that child is—"

He couldn't finish it. He didn't need to. Elodie finished it for him, in the flat, careful voice of a lawyer reading a clause that changes everything after it:

"You're not her son, Ansel. You're her *grandson*."

The cooler cycled off. In the new silence, Ansel heard his own breathing, and the small sound of the newspaper clipping under his thumb, and nothing else, and it was the loudest nothing of his life.

Maren Halloran, laughing on a dock, at someone out of frame. He'd spent two years thinking the someone was him. It wasn't him. It was a baby she'd already given away — a baby who had grown up in Duluth as a letter delivered to the wrong address, and who was now sitting in a funeral home his grandfather had bought him a job in, keeping watch over the body of a lie, while the woman who had given him away drowned with her second child inside her.

"I need air," he said, and stood, and made it four steps before his knees informed him they had other plans, and Elodie was there — faster than he'd have credited her — with her hands on his arms, steering him into the chair, crouching in front of him the way you'd steady a grieving widow at a cosmetics consult, except she wasn't performing it. She was just doing it.

"Breathe," she said. "You're a mortuary assistant. You've seen a hundred people get this exact news. You know how it goes. Breathe through it."

"That's different. I'm on the other side of the table."

"Tonight you're on this side. It's fine. It's terrible and it's fine." She stayed crouched there, her hands on his forearms, and after a while she said, "Why did you really call me here tonight? Not for the adoption story. You could have told me that any time. You called because of the envelope."

He looked at her. She didn't even know about the envelope and she'd already seen it.

"Ashe left me a letter," Ansel said. "Delivered this morning. It says the will is his confession — that he learned, before he died, who moved Maren's body, and that he couldn't prove it, so he did the only thing he could: he named me in the will, knowing it would drag you in, knowing you'd never let a will that shouldn't exist go unexamined. He used you, Elodie. He used both of us. He said — I memorized this part — he said, *Tell her everything. She deserves to hear it from you, not from a folder.*" Ansel exhaled. "There's one more thing in the letter. A name. The name of the person he believed put Maren in the water. He wouldn't write it plainly — he wrote it as initials, and a date. The date is the anniversary dinner. Three weeks from now. He believed that's when it would happen again, whatever *it* is, because it happened the first time at the dinner. Maren died the night of the anniversary dinner, Elodie. The toast. Everyone raises a glass to her every year, and the first year they raised it, she was already in the water."

Elodie rose slowly. Her face had gone back to being a lawyer's face, but he could see the other thing burning under it now, and it wasn't grief anymore. It was the itch. He'd watched it wake in her the day the will arrived, and he'd watched it sharpen through the staged bodies and Brandt's suspicions and the place card reading ANSEL, and now it was fully awake, and he understood with a strange, settling calm that he had just handed the hunter her scent.

"Three weeks," she said.

"Three weeks. And Brandt thinks I'm a suspect, and Tobias watches me at work like he's waiting for me to break, and there is a man or a woman out there dressing bodies in clothes from your closet, and I just found out the woman I've been mourning as my mother is my mother's daughter, which means—" He stopped, and laughed, wet and unsteady. "Which means whoever killed her is my family too. One way or another. Everyone in this story is my family. That's the joke. That's the whole joke."

"It's not a joke," Elodie said. "It's a will. And I'm the executor." She picked up her coat, and her cup of bad coffee, and drained it like a woman taking a shot before surgery. "Tomorrow we pull every record Ashe's accounts touched — the disbursements, the consulting arrangement, the birth certificate with the redaction. If there's a second child, there's a second file, and if there's a second file, Ruth Okafor has touched it, because that woman has her hands in every drawer in that house. And Ansel—"

She paused at the door, and for a moment she wasn't the executor or the lawyer or the sister-in-law of the drowned. She was just a person who had, an hour ago, drunk bad coffee with a stranger and was now leaving as something else.

"You said you've spent your whole life not being looked at," she said. "Well. I'm looking. And I'm not going anywhere."

The door closed behind her. Ansel sat alone in the arrangement room with the clipping and the cold cup and the hum of the building, and he did not put the photograph back in his pocket. He stood it upright against the coffee pot, facing the empty chair across the table, the way you'd set a place for someone you were expecting.

Three weeks. Somewhere out there, the anniversary dinner was being planned, and a chair with his name on it was waiting, and the person who had built that table had no idea yet that the boy from the wrong address had finally learned his own.

* * *

He didn't sleep. He went back to the office instead, and pulled the ledger, and worked the disbursements line by line under the desk lamp until four in the morning, and that was where he found it — not in Ashe's records at all, but in the mortuary's own files, in a drawer Tobias's father had kept locked and Tobias kept merely closed.

A folder, thin, unlabeled. Inside: a photograph of a young woman on a porch holding a baby, the same photograph, the same laugh, the same someone out of frame. And on the back, in an older hand than Ashe's, in ink gone brown:

*M. and the boy. Last summer before everything. — R.*

R.

Ansel sat very still in the dark office, the lamp buzzing, the folder open on his knees, and understood that the letter had been delivered to the wrong address after all — not to Duluth, not to the Vosses, but to him, here, now, by a hand that had been in the room the whole time.

He reached for his phone to call Elodie, and stopped, because on the screen, glowing in the dark office, was a message from a number with no name:

*You have her laugh. Did anyone ever tell you that? Come to the dinner. Bring the photograph. — R.*
