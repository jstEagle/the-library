## 12. The Third Staging

The call came at 4:17 in the morning, and it did not come from the police.

"Mr. Halloran." A woman's voice, flat and careful, the kind of voice people use when they've rehearsed a sentence until all the feeling has been squeezed out of it. "This is Ruth Okafor. I keep house for Vivienne Ashe. You need to come to Harrow Point. Now. Before anyone else does."

Elodie sat up in the dark, his hand already finding the lamp. "Is she alive?"

A pause. Long enough to be an answer. "She's breathing. I don't know for how long. And Mr. Halloran — you should know what she's wearing before you see her. Because you'll want to sit down first, and there's no time for that."

He was dressed and out the door in six minutes.

* * *

Ansel heard about it from the scanner he wasn't supposed to own — a police-band radio tucked under the embalming counter like a guilty conscience. The dispatch crackled through the empty mortuary at half past four: *medical emergency, Harrow Point Road, possible poisoning, female, elderly.* Then, eleven minutes later, a second call that made him set down the trocar he'd been cleaning so hard his knuckles went white.

*Requesting detective to scene. Victim found in clothing not consistent with residence.*

He knew what that meant. He'd known since the second body, since the raincoat, since the moment he'd recognized the subclavian work on the harbor master and understood with sickening clarity that whoever was doing this had been trained by the same dead men who trained him — Merrow & Sons, two generations of it, the same needles, the same angles, the same terrible tenderness toward the dead.

But this one was different. This one was still alive.

He locked up the mortuary and drove toward Harrow Point with his headlights cutting through river fog, telling himself he was going because Elodie would need him, because someone had to be there who understood what the staging meant. He told himself that the whole way. It was almost true.

* * *

Harrow Point was a house that had been built to be looked at from the water — three stories of gray shingle and white trim, widow's walk on top, the whole facade turned toward the harbor as if waiting for ships that stopped coming decades ago. Elodie had been inside once, years ago, at some family function he'd attended out of obligation to his late wife's connections. He remembered canapés and a piano and Vivienne Ashe holding court in a chair positioned precisely where she could watch everyone enter.

Now the front door stood open, and Ruth Okafor stood in it like a gatekeeper.

"You came fast," she said. She was a compact woman in her sixties, silver hair cropped close, wearing a cardigan buttoned wrong. Her hands were steady but her eyes weren't. "Upstairs. Second door on the left. The ambulance is twelve minutes out — I called them after I called you. I wanted you to have time."

"Time for what?"

"To understand what you're walking into before Brandt's people turn it into a crime scene and start asking why a probate lawyer got here first."

Elodie climbed the stairs. The hallway smelled of lavender polish and, underneath it, something else — faintly sweet, faintly chemical, a smell his memory reached for and couldn't quite name.

Vivienne Ashe lay on her back on the floor beside her bed, and Elodie's stomach dropped through the floorboards.

She was wearing Maren's dress.

He knew it instantly, the way you know your own handwriting. He'd seen it in photographs a hundred times over the past weeks — Maren Halloran at the harbor picnic, Maren at Corwin Ashe's arm, the blue cotton dress with the small white anchors printed along the hem, a joke dress, a harbor-town dress, the kind of thing a young woman wears without thinking it will someday matter. Police evidence photos had shown it folded in a storage box, recovered from the Ashe estate, catalogued and released. It should have been in an archive drawer.

It was on Vivienne Ashe, buttoned to the throat, arranged with care, and the old woman's lips were the color of slate.

"She took something," Ruth said from the doorway, her voice stripped to wire. "I found the teacup. I don't know what was in it. I found her twenty minutes ago. She was conscious then. She kept trying to say something."

Elodie knelt. Up close, Vivienne's skin had gone waxy, her breathing shallow and rattling, each exhale a small surrender. But her eyes were open, and they fixed on him with a ferocity that had nothing to do with dying and everything to do with a lifetime of winning arguments.

"Mrs. Ashe. It's Elodie Halloran. Help is coming."

Her mouth worked. He leaned close, close enough to feel the heat coming off her skin — fever-heat, wrong for poison or right for it, he didn't know. Her hand lifted, trembling, and caught his lapel with a grip that surprised him with its strength.

"Tobias," she whispered.

The word came out wet and broken, but unmistakable.

"Tobias— he—"

Her eyes rolled. Her grip slackened. Downstairs, faint through the walls, came the sound of tires on gravel — the ambulance, early, or the police, or both. Elodie pressed her hand between both of his.

"Who? Tobias what? Mrs. Ashe — *what about Tobias?*"

But Vivienne Ashe had gone somewhere the words couldn't follow, her breath thinning to threads, and then the paramedics were pushing past him, and then the hallway was full of uniforms, and Detective Ilsa Brandt arrived last, took one look at the dress, one look at Elodie, and said, "Outside. Now."

* * *

Ansel reached Harrow Point just as they were loading the gurney. He stayed across the road, in the fog line, watching the blue dress rise into the ambulance like something being offered up to the sky. He saw Elodie on the lawn, hands in his hair, talking to a tall woman with a detective's posture. He saw the way Brandt kept glancing at the ambulance, then at Elodie, then at the house, assembling something.

He also saw Ruth Okafor standing on the porch, apart from everyone, watching Ansel.

Not looking at him. *Watching* him. With an expression he couldn't read — grief, maybe, or recognition, or the particular patience of someone who has been waiting a very long time for a specific person to arrive at a specific place.

When he crossed the road, she didn't move.

"You're the boy from the will," she said quietly. Not a question.

"I'm the mortuary assistant."

"Yes," she said. "That too."

Before he could answer, a uniformed officer waved her toward the house for questioning, and she went, but at the door she paused and looked back at him, and said, "She'll live. They got there in time. Remember that — whatever happens next, remember that *she lived*. That matters."

Then she was gone inside, and Ansel stood in the fog wondering how a caretaker knew he was in a will, and why it mattered to her whether Vivienne lived, and why every instinct he owned was suddenly screaming that the woman on the porch was not a witness to any of this.

She was an author.

* * *

Brandt interrogated Elodie in his own kitchen at seven in the morning, which he supposed was efficient if nothing else. She sat across from him with a coffee he hadn't offered and a notebook he wished she'd lose.

"Walk me through it again."

"I got a call at 4:17. Ruth Okafor. She asked me to come before the scene got processed."

"Why you? Vivienne Ashe has four living relatives within twenty miles. Why call the estate lawyer?"

"Because the dress was Maren's, and Maren is my sister-in-law, and Ruth apparently thought I'd understand what I was looking at."

"And do you? Understand it?"

Elodie turned his coffee cup slowly on its saucer. "Someone is staging scenes from the night Maren died. First the clothes from my closet — Maren's brother-in-law, the family connection. Then the harbor master in Corwin's raincoat — the man who pulled Corwin's boat out of the water that October. Now Vivienne in Maren's dress. Each one is a piece of that night. Each one is a witness, or a stand-in for one."

"You've had this theory for how long?"

"Since the second body."

"And you didn't share it."

"I shared it with the one other person working the same trail."

"Voss." Brandt wrote something. "Your mortuary friend. Who was witnessed destroying intake records. Who is named in a will dated after the testator's death. Whose name appears on a place card at an anniversary dinner neither of you were invited to." She looked up. "You know how this looks from where I sit, Elodie? It looks like you found yourself a partner."

"He's not my partner."

"No. Partners tell each other things." She closed the notebook. "Here's what I have. Teacup in Vivienne's room, residue consistent with something glycoside-based — foxglove, possibly oleander. Old-fashioned. Garden variety, literally; there's foxglove all over that property. No forced entry, no sign of struggle. Whoever poisoned her was let in, or lives there, or was expected." She let that hang. "And here's what I don't have. I don't have a single print. I don't have a single fiber that isn't accounted for. Three staged scenes now, and this person cleans up like a professional. Like somebody who's spent their life around—" she paused, choosing the word deliberately, "—bodies."

Elodie said nothing.

"One more thing." Brandt stood, draining the coffee. "Vivienne's sedated at St. Alder's. When she wakes up, I get the first interview. But the paramedic told me she was saying a name when they arrived. Saying it over and over to some man kneeling next to her." Brandt's eyes were flat and patient. "You wouldn't know anything about that, would you?"

"She said 'Tobias,'" Elodie said. "That's all I heard. Tobias, and then she went under."

Brandt studied him for a long moment. "Tobias Merrow," she said. "The funeral home heir. Who, I'm told, was engaged to Maren Halloran before Corwin Ashe ever entered the picture." She put on her coat. "Funny thing, Elodie. Every time I pull a thread in this case, it runs through either a funeral home or a lawyer's office. Sleep on which one you'd rather I pull harder."

* * *

They met at the mortuary at noon, because it was the only place left that felt like theirs — the one building in town where death was honest work instead of theater. Ansel had made coffee that tasted like scorched earth, and Elodie drank it anyway, sitting on a steel prep table because there were no chairs and neither of them cared.

"Tobias," Elodie said. "It fits. Motive — she chose Corwin over him. Access — he'd have known the house, known the family, known Maren. Skills—" he gestured vaguely at the room around them, at the table, the instruments, the whole quiet economy of the dead. "Skills like yours."

"But?" Ansel said. He could hear the *but* in Elodie's voice the way he could hear a lie in a eulogy.

"But it fits too well. Tobias Merrow has been the obvious suspect since chapter one of this story, and obvious suspects are either guilty or bait." Elodie set down the cup. "There's something else. On the lawn this morning, I watched Ruth Okafor. While everyone else was watching the ambulance, she was watching the house. Watching the windows. Counting them, almost. And when I asked her how long Vivienne had been unconscious, she gave me a timeline to the minute. 'I found her at ten to four. She was conscious for fifteen minutes.' Nobody times a discovery like that unless they already knew the schedule."

"You think the caretaker—"

"I think someone fed her a timeline. Or wrote one for her." Elodie rubbed his eyes. "Vivienne said 'Tobias' and then 'he' and then nothing. What if she wasn't naming her attacker? What if she was naming the man she thought would save her? Or the man she was afraid for? Or—" he stopped.

"Or the name she was given," Ansel said quietly. "To say. To you specifically."

The prep room was silent except for the hum of the refrigeration units, that low constant note like a held breath.

"Ruth called me at 4:17," Elodie said. "She called me before the ambulance. She wanted me to see the dress first. She wanted me to hear the name first. Everything about this morning was *arranged*, Ansel. The staging isn't just the body anymore. It's the whole morning. We're being moved around a board by someone who knows exactly which squares we'll go to."

Ansel looked down at his hands. They were steady — they were always steady, that was the job — but underneath the steadiness something was shaking. "Then we stop going to the squares," he said. "We stop reacting. Whatever invitation comes next, whatever name gets whispered, whatever body turns up dressed in somebody's memory — we look past it. At who's standing behind the curtain, holding the strings."

"That's easier said than—"

His phone buzzed on the table between them. Unknown number. A photograph, no text.

Elodie picked it up, and felt the floor tilt.

It was an image of a formal place card, cream stock, gold lettering, resting on white linen. The anniversary dinner table. Eight settings visible, nine chairs.

One card read ANSEL.

One card read ELODIE.

And at the head of the table, where Vivienne Ashe should have sat, where the hostess always sat —

the ninth card read RUTH.

Below the photograph, a single line of text appeared:

*She survived. The toast cannot wait another year. Friday, eight o'clock. Come hungry for the truth.*

Ansel read it twice. When he spoke, his voice was very soft.

"It was never Tobias," he said. "Tobias is the name she *gave* us. We've been reading the menu while the cook watches from the kitchen." He looked up at Elodie, and for the first time since Elodie had known him, the mortuary assistant looked afraid — not of death, which he handled daily, but of the living. "Friday. Four days. She's setting our places, Elodie. She expects us at the table."

Outside, the fog was burning off the river, and somewhere across the water, at St. Alder's Hospital, Vivienne Ashe slept on under sedation, the only soul alive who might say whether the name she'd whispered had been a confession — or a message written by someone else's hand.
