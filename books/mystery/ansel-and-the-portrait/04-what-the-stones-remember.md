## 4. What the Stones Remember

The police station smelled like burnt coffee and old carpet, and Detective Okafor's desk lamp buzzed at a frequency that made Quentin's teeth ache. They had rehearsed this a dozen times in the cab on the way over—the exact pages, the exact story, the exact shape of the truth they were willing to hand over.

"Dravenmoor." Okafor didn't look up from her notes. "You said you found something in your archives."

Quentin set the folder on her desk. Inside: a single photocopied page from the 1994 dispatch log, the entry for October 14th circled in red. The fare logged at 11:47 PM, pickup at Halloway Cemetery gates, destination left blank. And beneath it, in the margin where dispatchers sometimes scrawled notes when the night got strange, the words that had started everything: *Passenger refused name. Paid triple. Said tell no one E.H. was ever here.*

But that wasn't what mattered. What mattered was the line above it—another fare, logged three hours earlier, picked up at the river dock by a driver named Sal Petrakis. The passenger note read: *V. Crane, agitated. Kept asking if the body would surface.*

Victor Crane. The man Edmund Halloway had been accused of killing thirty years ago. Except Crane hadn't drowned in the river that night—he'd walked out of the water, climbed into a cab, and vanished with a satchel full of the town founder's forged debts. Edmund hadn't killed him. He'd caught him. And then he'd realized that the only way to prove his innocence was to produce a dead man who wasn't dead—and the only way to survive the scandal of *that* was to become one himself.

"The log shows Crane alive after the alleged murder," Quentin said. Their voice came out steadier than they felt. "He took a cab to the state line. I traced the driver. He kept a personal journal—his daughter let me see it before she moved to Ohio. He wrote that Crane confessed to him. Forgery, embezzlement, the whole scheme. He framed Halloway for his own death."

Okafor turned the page over twice, as if the back might hold something different. "And the man in the cemetery cab? The one you've been chasing for three weeks?"

"A ghost," Quentin said. It was almost true. "Dead now. Natural causes. There's nothing to charge anyone with."

The detective studied them for a long moment. Quentin held still under the look, the way they'd learned to hold still their whole life—invisible, unremarkable, a voice on a radio. Finally Okafor closed the folder.

"Crane's been dead since 2011. His son runs the hardware store." She tapped the photocopy. "This closes the historical file. It doesn't explain an empty coffin."

"No," Quentin agreed. "It doesn't. Some things don't get explained. They just get reburied."

Okafor's eyes narrowed, but she let it go. Maybe she suspected. Maybe she'd always known more than she said. But she stamped the log page into evidence, and the case became what the town needed it to be: a solved mystery about a long-dead fraudster, a founder posthumously cleared, a coffin empty because grief makes people do strange things.

Outside, the rain had stopped. Quentin stood on the station steps and breathed.

* * *

The call came through dispatch at 4:12 AM, two days later.

"One-way fare," said the voice—Marisol Halloway's voice, low and even, though Quentin could hear the exhaustion underneath it, the way a tuning fork holds the memory of being struck. "Long distance. No return."

Quentin looked at the board, at the little lights representing cabs scattered across the sleeping town, and did something against every regulation they'd followed for eleven years. They assigned themselves.

"I'll take it," they said. "Where to?"

A pause. Then, softly: "You know, don't you?"

"I know enough."

Another pause, longer. "The bus depot in Corvane. Forty miles east."

Quentin drove through streets still slick with rain, past the cemetery where the renovation crew had resealed the vault that afternoon—Edmund Halloway's coffin going back into the earth filled with river stones, smooth and gray and honest in a way the town would never understand. The coroner's report had called it an administrative error decades old. The stones stayed. Nobody wanted to dig again.

Marisol was waiting at the curb of the estate house with a single suitcase, dressed in a plain wool coat, hair pinned back, looking smaller than Quentin had ever seen her—looking, in fact, like exactly what she was: an old man wearing sixty years of someone else's life like a coat that never quite fit.

They didn't speak for the first ten miles. The wipers kept their patient rhythm. Streetlights strobed past, painting Marisol's face gold, then dark, then gold again.

"You gave them Crane," Marisol finally said. "Not me."

"He was the crime. You were just the sentence."

A dry sound, almost a laugh. "Thirty years I told myself I'd turn myself in eventually. When it was safe. When it wouldn't matter anymore." Marisol looked out the window at the dark fields sliding past. "It never gets safe. That's the trick of it. You just get tired."

"Why keep paying?" Quentin asked. "The hush money. Thirty years of payments through the estate. To whom?"

"To the people who remembered. Sal Petrakis first, then his daughter after him. A few others along the way. Not blackmail—" a pause, self-aware, rueful— "no, that's a lie. It was blackmail. I paid because I was afraid. And then I paid because it was easier than facing what I'd done, even the parts that weren't crimes. Running becomes a kind of living, if you do it long enough. A small one."

The depot appeared out of the dark like something surfacing from deep water—a squat brick building, sodium lights, a single bus idling with its door open and its exhaust curling white into the cold.

Quentin pulled to the curb and put the meter on pause. Neither of them moved for a moment.

"When I was nineteen," Quentin said, "I worked a summer job where everyone forgot I existed. Literally. Payroll skipped me for six weeks. I ate from vending machines and slept in my car and not one person noticed." They watched the bus door hang open, breathing its warm light onto the pavement. "I learned something that summer. Being unseen isn't the worst thing. Being unseen *by choice*, on your own terms—that's different. That can be a kind of freedom."

Marisol turned to look at them fully, and for a moment the mask slipped entirely, and there was Edmund Halloway—ninety-one years old, eyes bright with tears he'd probably been carrying since 1994.

"You're a strange one, Dispatcher Dravenmoor."

"So I'm told." Quentin reached over and opened the meter. "Fare's paid. Company policy—one complimentary ride per lifetime, for passengers who've waited long enough."

"That's not company policy."

"It is tonight."

Marisol gathered the suitcase, paused with a hand on the door. "The stones," they said. "In the coffin. Do you know why I asked for river stones?"

Quentin shook their head.

"Because the river is where the truth of me went under. Every stone in that box came from the bank below the old dock. I collected them myself, the week after the funeral, before I left." A breath. "Let them remember what I couldn't say."

The door shut. Quentin watched through the windshield as the figure crossed the lot, slow and straight-backed, and climbed onto the bus bound for somewhere no ledger had ever recorded. The doors hissed closed. The taillights shrank down the east road, two red points fading into the dark like a signal going off the air.

Then the lot was empty, and the night was ordinary again.

* * *

Quentin filed the trip as a no-show. Unbilled, unlogged, unremembered—except by them.

Back at the depot office, dawn was coming up gray over the water tower. The new dispatcher, a kid named Theo with ink-stained fingers and too many questions, glanced up from the overnight board.

"Quiet night?"

"Very," Quentin said, hanging up their coat. "One cancellation."

Theo nodded and went back to his crossword. Outside, the first shift crew was arriving, and somewhere across town the cemetery groundskeeper was locking the Halloway vault gate, thirty pounds of river stones resting in the dark, holding their silence the way stones do—completely, and forever.

Quentin sat down at the console, pulled up the morning schedule, and began, quietly, to assign the day's fares.

For the first time in eleven years, invisibility felt less like a sentence than a seat they'd chosen—warm, window-side, going somewhere.
