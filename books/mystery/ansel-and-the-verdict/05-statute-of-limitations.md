## 5. Statute of Limitations

The county courthouse smelled the way Wren remembered it: wet wool, old paper, and the ghost of a thousand bad coffees. They stood in the lobby for a long moment before crossing it, hat in hand, feeling like a man returning to a house he'd sold years ago. The security guard at the magnetometer glanced up, looked twice, and said nothing. That was the thing about leaving a career—people remembered your face longer than they remembered your name.

Third floor. Office of the District Attorney. Wren asked for Priya Raghavan the way you ask for someone you once argued with daily and never quite stopped trusting.

She came out herself, which surprised them. She was grayer now, her hair cut short and practical, reading glasses pushed up into it like a soldier's spare magazine. She wore a blazer that had seen better decades and an expression that had seen worse ones. When she saw Wren standing there among the potted ficus and the laminated notices about jury duty, she stopped walking entirely.

"No," she said.

"Good morning to you too."

"No. Absolutely not." But she was already waving them past the reception desk, past the cubicles where young prosecutors hunched over case files like monks over scripture, into her office—a small room with one window overlooking the parking structure and walls lined with binders whose spines had faded from use rather than age. She shut the door. "Twelve years, Oyelaran. Twelve years since you retired, and I have enjoyed twelve years of not seeing your face, because every time I see your face it means something is wrong."

"Something is wrong."

Priya sat down behind her desk with the deliberate weariness of a woman who had been sitting down to bad news for thirty years. She laced her fingers. "Tell me."

Wren told her. Not everything—not yet—but enough. A letter, unsigned, confessing to the drowning of Edmund Hale in March of 2019. Details no newspaper ever printed: the weight of the man's coat when they pulled him from the shallows, the position of his hands, the fact that his boots were on the wrong feet. The second letter promising more. And then—the part Wren almost didn't say, and said anyway, because lying to Priya Raghavan had never worked and never would—the private file. Three deaths. Three accidents that weren't. Solved quietly, annotated in their own hand, reported to no one.

When Wren finished, the office was very quiet. Somewhere down the hall a phone rang four times and gave up.

Priya took off her glasses, cleaned them on her sleeve, put them back on. It was what she did instead of shouting. Wren had watched her do it before juries, before judges, once before a defense attorney who made the mistake of implying she couldn't read a toxicology report.

"Let me make sure I understand," she said. "You investigated three deaths. You concluded—privately, alone, in the privacy of your own head—that they were homicides. And you told no one. No report. No referral. Nothing."

"They were ruled accidental. There was no evidence that would survive a courtroom. Only certainty, and certainty isn't admissible."

"That is not how this works." Her voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "That has *never* been how this works. You don't get to be the sole custodian of the truth, Wren. Truth that lives in one person's head dies with that person. Or worse—" she leaned forward, "—it gets *used*. By whoever finds out about it. Which, apparently, someone has."

"I know."

"Do you? Because from where I'm sitting, it looks like you spent years building a private museum of unsolved murders, and now some stranger has broken in and is rearranging the exhibits. And you came here—what, for permission? Absolution? A warrant?"

"For information." Wren set the two letters on her desk, flattened them with one hand. "I need to know how much time is left."

Priya looked at the letters for a long moment without touching them. Then she pulled a pair of latex gloves from her desk drawer—the reflexes of a career that never fully lets go—and picked up the confession letter by its corner, angling it toward the window light.

"Hale," she murmured. "Edmund Hale. March 2019. Drowning off the north shore." She recited it from memory, and Wren felt something cold move through their chest, because that meant she remembered the case too. Everyone had. It had been a two-column story on page six: local man, evening swim, alcohol likely a factor. Case closed by the end of the week.

"The statute of limitations for manslaughter in this state," Priya said, setting the letter down, "is five years."

Wren did the arithmetic they had already done three times that morning, standing at the shop counter with the ledger open and unattended. March 2019. It was February now. "Six weeks."

"Five weeks and change." Priya removed her gloves. "If the confession is true. If the writer is the killer. If any of it is provable—which it is not."

"It's a confession."

"It's a *letter*." She said it the way a surgeon says *benign*, wanting it to be true but refusing to build anything on it. "Anonymous. Unsworn. No chain of custody, no authentication, no signature. Any first-year defense attorney would have it excluded before lunch. For all we know, you wrote it yourself."

"Why would I write it myself?"

"To force the system to look at a case you've been carrying around for six years. To make someone else share the weight." She held up a hand before Wren could object. "I'm not saying you did. I'm saying a judge will think of it in under ten seconds, and I will have no answer for him. Anonymous letters aren't evidence, Wren. They're invitations. And right now you're RSVPing."

Wren stood at the window, looking down at the parking structure, at the gray cars in gray rows. "Then tell me what is evidence."

"A body that wasn't drowned accidentally. A witness who saw something. A document. A thread connecting a suspect to the scene. What you have is a piece of paper that arrived in your mail slot, addressed to a retired bookseller, describing details that—" Priya paused, and Wren heard her arrive at the same thought Wren had been circling for two days. "How *did* the writer know those details?"

"Only the killer could know them. Or someone the killer told."

"Or someone who read your notes."

The words landed exactly where Wren feared they would. "My notes are in a locked box under my floorboards."

"Were they always?" Priya spread her hands. "Think back. Twenty years of habit doesn't vanish because you changed professions. Did you ever keep files at the precinct? In a car? In a house you don't live in anymore? Did you ever talk in your sleep, tell a story at a dinner party, leave a notebook on a beach towel?" She shook her head slowly. "Somebody has been reading over your shoulder, Wren. For years, maybe. And whatever else that means, it means this letter-writer knows you. Knows what you found. Knows what you didn't do about it."

"And is punishing me for it."

"Is that what you think this is? Punishment?"

"What else would you call it? Someone sends me a murder confession and a promise of more, and waits to see what I'll do."

Priya was quiet for a moment. Outside, a siren rose and faded on the avenue below, going somewhere with jurisdiction. "I'd call it leverage," she said finally. "Someone wants you to do something. Confessions don't come with sequels unless the author wants an audience. So the question isn't just who killed Edmund Hale. It's what does this person want *you* to do—and what happens if you refuse."

* * *

They walked out together, because Priya claimed she needed coffee and everyone in the building knew she hadn't drunk coffee since 2011. They stood on the courthouse steps in the thin February light, breath steaming, and she spoke without looking at Wren, eyes fixed on the traffic.

"I'm going to say something now, and you're going to listen, because I only have the energy to say it once." She turned. "Do not investigate this."

"Priya—"

"Do not knock on doors. Do not walk beaches. Do not pull records, tail witnesses, or play the brilliant retired detective in a trench coat. If you touch this case and it ever surfaces—and things like this always surface—you will burn whatever credibility you have left, and worse, you'll burn mine for having talked to you. Worse than that: if the letters are bait, then every step you take is a step someone else has choreographed. You'd be dancing to their music and calling it detection."

"And if I do nothing, Hale stays a page-six accident forever. Five weeks, Priya. After that, no one can ever be charged. The truth expires like milk."

"The truth doesn't expire. Only the prosecution does." Her voice softened, barely. "I know what you're carrying. I've known since the day you handed me your badge and wouldn't tell me why. You think staying silent all these years makes you clean. It doesn't. It makes you quiet. Those are different things, and one of them is eating you alive—I can see it from here."

Wren said nothing. The wind came off the harbor and carried the smell of salt and diesel, the smell of the water that had taken Edmund Hale and kept its mouth shut.

"But," Priya said, and the word hung there like a hook, "if real evidence falls into your lap—if something physical, verifiable, documented arrives at that bookshop of yours—you bring it to me. Same day. Not next week, not after you've followed it around town for a fortnight playing Columbo. Same day. And I will move mountains with it. That's the deal. That's the only deal."

"That's not much of a deal."

"It's the only kind there is." She jammed her hands into her coat pockets. "One more thing. Whoever wrote that letter chose you. Out of every cop, every reporter, every grieving family member connected to that drowning—they chose the one person who solved it and said nothing. Ask yourself why. People don't send confessions to strangers. They send them to someone they trust to understand. Or someone they want to hurt. Or both." She started down the steps, then paused. "And Wren—get a lawyer's advice on that statute question. Because if the writer knows the clock is running, then the deadline isn't theirs. It's yours. They're counting your weeks for you."

She descended into the crowd, small and straight-backed, and was gone.

* * *

Wren walked back to the shop the long way, along the seawall, because the alternative was the bus and the bus meant sitting still. The tide was low, dragging the waterline far out and exposing the dark ribbed sand that in summer belonged to tourists and in winter belonged to gulls. Somewhere out past the breakwater, the water lay flat and gray and patient, keeping whatever it kept.

Five weeks.

The number had a physical weight, like a stone in a coat pocket. Five weeks until Edmund Hale's death became legally permanent—until the accident hardened into history and no court in the state could touch it. And the letter-writer knew. Had counted the days alongside Wren, perhaps, or before Wren, had chosen this morning, this Tuesday, this failing ledger and this quiet shop, with the precision of someone setting a clock.

*Unless the writer wants the clock to run out,* Wren thought, stopping at the seawall rail. *Unless the confession isn't a key but a door slamming shut.*

Because there was another reading, and it had been waiting all day for Wren to look at it. A confession arriving weeks before the statute expired might be a gift—or it might be theater. Proof that the matter was settled, that justice had been given its chance and declined it. A way of closing the book in front of the one person who'd kept it open. *Here is your truth, detective. Here it is, in writing, delivered to your door. And here it dies, legally, in five weeks, and you can do nothing, because you did nothing.*

Or—third possibility, the one that sat coldest in the stomach—the letters weren't about Hale at all. Hale was the opening move. *More will follow.* Someone was walking Wren backward through their own private museum, exhibit by exhibit, and Hale was simply the first door they wanted opened.

Wren pushed off the rail and walked on. The shop appeared at the end of the street, its sign swinging, its windows reflecting the gray sky, looking exactly as harmless as Wren had spent seven years pretending to be.

Inside, the mail had come again.

One envelope, same heavy cream stock, same careful hand. Wren stood in the doorway holding it, hearing Priya's voice—*same day, not after you've played Columbo*—and knowing, with terrible clarity, that they were going to open it here, alone, first, because some habits outlast every resolution ever made against them.

The postmark was local. Dated yesterday.

Inside, a single line:

*Three weeks is plenty of time, if you start today. She's still waiting where the water gave her back.*

Wren read it twice. Then a third time, while the shop bell chimed and a customer came in shaking rain from her coat, browsing mysteries, unaware that the genre had just stepped off the shelves and into the room.

*Where the water gave her back.*

Not Hale. Not the lighthouse. Not the garage.

Someone else. Someone the sea had returned—and Wren had a sick, rising certainty that they knew exactly which stretch of shoreline the writer meant, because they had walked it a hundred times, and never once thought to dig.
