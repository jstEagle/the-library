## 30. Safehouse

The cabin announced itself the way Priya did—plainly, without apology. A gray-shingled box at the end of a gravel track, two hours north of the city, sitting on a lake so still it looked poured rather than gathered. Pine. Cold water smell. A porch swing that had not been swung in years, its chains furred with rust.

Wren stood in the driveway with a single duffel and felt the silence press against their eardrums like water.

"You're staring," Priya said, unlocking the door.

"I'm listening."

"To what?"

"Nothing." Wren turned a slow circle. "That's the point. I haven't heard nothing in eleven years."

Priya held the door open and didn't comment on that, which was its own kind of kindness. She had driven up separately from the others, having collected Marisol Verne from the chapel ruins and Elena Ashcombe—Elena Marchand now, though the name sat strangely on her—from the airport in two careful, unconnected trips. Theo had ridden with Wren, silent for most of the drive, his knees drawn up against the dashboard of the old Volvo like a boy half his age.

Inside, the cabin smelled of cedar and disuse. Four bedrooms, a stone fireplace, a kitchen with copper pots hanging like question marks. Priya had stocked it before any of this began—she'd been planning the extraction for days, Wren realized, longer than she'd admitted—and there was bread on the counter and coffee in a canister and a stack of firewood under the eaves.

"Rules," Priya said, setting her keys down. "No phones except mine and Wren's burner. Nobody leaves alone. Nobody tells anyone where they are—not family, not friends, not priests, not dogs." She looked around at the four of them, assembled awkwardly in her living room like strangers at a funeral. "I know this feels like hiding. It isn't. It's staging. There's a difference, and in about three weeks you'll see it."

Marisol Verne stood by the window with her arms crossed, watching the lake as if it might produce witnesses. She was smaller than Wren had imagined from the journal—twenty-six years of running had compressed her somehow, made her economical, a woman who took up only the space she needed. Her hair was iron-gray at the temples. Her hands, when she finally took them off her elbows, were the hands of someone who had typed a million words and trusted none of them.

Elena sat on the edge of the sofa, coat still buttoned, as though she expected to be asked to leave at any moment. She was fifty-one and looked younger until you caught her eyes, which had the flat shine of a person who had rehearsed being nobody for so long that the rehearsal had eaten the role.

Theo hovered near the bookshelf. Of course he did. He'd been doing it all week—touching spines, reading titles, anchoring himself to printed things the way other people anchored to walls.

Wren set the duffel down and thought: *I have spent my whole life learning how to be alone, and now I am responsible for three frightened people and a lawyer.*

It should have felt like drowning. Instead, it felt like surfacing.

* * *

The first friction arrived with dinner, as frictions do.

Marisol cooked—insisted on it, waving Priya's takeout menus away with a look that suggested menus were an insult to a woman who had survived on canned goods and cash for a quarter century. She moved through the kitchen with startling authority, and within an hour the cabin filled with the smell of garlic and rosemary and something with paprika that made Theo drift in from the bookshelf twice under transparent pretenses.

Elena set the table. Neither woman spoke to the other. The silence between them had architecture—it went around corners, avoided doorways, occupied the exact center of the room while pretending to be elsewhere.

It broke over the pasta.

"You knew," Elena said. Not loudly. She was laying forks down with terrible precision, one at each place, aligning them. "In 1998. When you disappeared. You knew what they'd done to the will, and you knew I was still alive somewhere, and you never—" The fork in her hand trembled. "Twenty-five years, Ms. Verne. I thought *you* were complicit. I thought you took the money and ran and left me holding a name no one would believe."

Marisol turned off the burner. She did it slowly, deliberately, the way Wren had seen her do everything—as if speed were a luxury she'd trained out of herself.

"And I thought you were dead," Marisol said. "Because that is what I was told. That is what everyone was told, because that is what *I helped them tell*." She set the spoon down. "You think I ran with money? I ran with a go-bag and a fake name and the knowledge that the last stenographer who flagged an irregularity in an Ashcombe document drowned in a lake with her car. I stayed dead because staying dead was the only testimony I could give that couldn't be forged."

"You could have found me."

"With what?" For the first time, Marisol's voice cracked, and beneath the crack was something raw and very tired. "I spent nine years in Thunder Bay before I could afford a phone. I checked obituaries, Mrs.—Elena. I checked them for your name every week for nine years. Do you know what it does to a person, hoping to find a stranger's name in the death notices because the alternative is hoping she's alive, and if she's alive then you owe her twenty-five years of explanation?"

The kitchen was very quiet. Theo had stopped breathing somewhere near the doorway. Wren kept their eyes on their plate and listened the way they used to listen to interrogations—fully, without moving, letting the truth come out on its own schedule.

"I believed you were complicit," Elena said again, but softer now, the accusation losing its skeleton. "Because believing that was easier than believing you were afraid. If you were complicit, then the world was corrupt but legible. If you were afraid—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eye. "If you were afraid, then I spent my whole life in exile over something that could have been *fixed*, and I can't—"

"It couldn't have been fixed." Marisol came around the counter, slowly, giving Elena time to step back. Elena didn't. "Not then. Not without a detective willing to look, and there wasn't one. There wasn't one for twenty-five years." She stopped an arm's length away. "And then there was."

Both women turned to look at Wren.

Wren, who had spent decades perfecting invisibility, found it unbearable to be seen by two pairs of grieving eyes at once. "Eat your dinner," they said gruffly. "Both of you. It's getting cold, and I've been living on tinned soup since the shop—" They stopped. Started again. "Since a while."

Something loosened in the room. Not forgiveness—that would take longer, would need more nights exactly like this one—but the beginning of the shape of it. Elena laughed, a small wet sound, and pulled out her chair. Marisol served the pasta with hands that had steadied.

Later, washing dishes side by side—an arrangement neither proposed and neither refused—they spoke in low voices about Thunder Bay, about Montreal, about the grammar of false names. Wren dried plates at the counter and understood that something was being built here that had nothing to do with probate law.

* * *

Theo couldn't sleep.

Wren found him on the porch at one in the morning, wrapped in a blanket, watching the lake go silver under a half moon. They sat down beside him without asking. That was the language they'd developed—presence instead of questions.

"My uncle used to bring me here," Theo said. "Not here-here. Lakes generally. He said cities made you forget how big quiet was." He pulled the blanket tighter. "Edmund Hale was my uncle. Sometimes I say his name just to hear it. Everyone else says 'the victim' or 'the deceased' or 'Hale.' Like he was a file."

"He was a man who fixed boats badly and argued about tide tables," Wren said. "And he called you Teddy, and you hated it, and let him do it anyway."

Theo's breath caught. "How did you—"

"You told me. In the shop, the night you showed me the texts. You don't remember telling me because you told me sideways, the way people tell the truest things." Wren watched the water. "I remember everything people tell me sideways. It's the only filing system I trust."

They sat with that. A loon called somewhere out on the black water, a sound so ancient and lonely it seemed to come from before the lake had a name.

"Are we going to win?" Theo asked finally. "Actually win? Or are we going to be the part of the story where the good people almost make it?"

Wren considered lying. Considered the warm, easy answer, the one that belonged on a greeting card. But this boy had been hired to watch a stranger and had instead become a witness; he had slept in the back room of a ransacked shop because Wren asked him to; he had earned the truth the hard way, same as everyone in this cabin.

"I don't know," Wren said. "But I know that Julian Ashcombe has spent his whole life buying outcomes, and for the first time there are things in play he can't buy. A transcript with duplicate plates. A woman legally dead who types faster than his lawyers talk. Another woman whose signature was never on that will, and whose absence has been the load-bearing wall of his entire fortune." They turned to look at him. "He's not afraid of us yet. That's his mistake. He thinks we're hiding."

"Aren't we?"

"We're *loading*," Wren said. "There's a difference, and in about three weeks you'll see it."

Theo smiled despite himself. "That's what Priya said."

"Priya and I steal from each other constantly. It's how you know a friendship is real." Wren stood, joints announcing themselves. "Come inside. Sleep. Tomorrow we start working on your statement, and I warn you, I'm a merciless editor."

At the door, Theo paused. "Wren? Why are you doing this? Really. You spent years not doing this. You had a whole system."

Wren looked back at the lake, at the silver dark, at the boy silhouetted against it.

"The system worked," they said, "right up until it required me to watch other people drown in my place. Turns out I'm not built for that. Took me fifty-eight years to find out." They held the door. "Bed."

* * *

Elena woke before dawn and found Marisol already at the kitchen table with a legal pad, writing in a small, precise hand—the stenographer's hand, built for verbatim, incapable of approximation.

"What are you writing?" Elena asked.

"Everything. From the beginning. The hearing, the forgery, the day I understood what the Ashcombe money had bought." Marisol didn't look up. "Priya needs it in affidavit form by Friday. And I need—" She stopped. Set down the pen. "I need it to exist outside my head. Twenty-five years is a long time to be the only witness to your own life."

Elena hesitated, then sat across from her. "Read it to me," she said. "As you write. I want—I need to hear it. All of it. Even the parts that hurt."

"They mostly hurt."

"Good," Elena said. "Then we'll both be done flinching by Friday."

Outside the window, mist was coming off the lake in slow ropes. Somewhere down the road, unseen, the world was turning—courts and lawyers and a charming man in a beautiful house discovering, hour by hour, that his paper empire had developed cracks. But in the kitchen there was only the scratch of a pen and a woman's steady voice saying *On the seventeenth of March, 1998, I transcribed the following pages, and I knew before I finished the first paragraph that I was typing my own death warrant*—

and Wren, awake in the hallway, listening the way they always listened, thought: *This is what belonging sounds like. It sounds like testimony.*

* * *

It was Priya's phone that ended the peace, as phones do.

She came out to the porch on the second evening, where Wren was teaching Theo to split kindling—badly, on both their parts—and her face had the particular gray set that Wren had learned, over thirty years, to read before she said a word.

"What," Wren said.

"Corliss's replacement surfaced today. New fixer, out of the city. And Julian's counsel filed motions this morning—harassment allegations, a complaint to the bar about me, and—" Priya exhaled. "A story's going to run tomorrow. *Disgraced Detective Stashes Witnesses in Lake Cabin.* Someone talked to a reporter. Someone with access to the extraction plan."

The axe handle creaked in Wren's grip.

"There's a leak," Theo said quietly.

"There's a leak," Priya confirmed. "Small. Contained, probably. But Wren—" She met their eyes. "He knows where we are. Or he will by breakfast."

Behind them, through the window, the lamplight of the kitchen fell across Marisol and Elena bent together over the legal pad, two ghosts building a case out of ink and memory. Wren looked at them for a long moment.

"No," they said.

"Wren—"

"We don't move. Moving is what he wants—scattered witnesses, panicked witnesses, witnesses who look guilty." Wren handed the axe back to Theo, and their voice was calm in the way that made Priya's spine prickle, the calm of a person stepping back into a shape they'd sworn off. "We stay. We finish the affidavits. And tomorrow morning, you and I are going to find out who leaked—because whoever it is just made themselves the most dangerous person in this story, and I have spent my whole life finding dangerous people."

Priya stared at them. Then, slowly, she nodded.

"God help me," she said, "you're enjoying this."

Wren looked out at the darkening lake, at the cabin glowing behind them like a lantern someone had finally lit after years of darkness.

"No," they said. "I'm *home*. There's a difference."

But in the morning, the newspaper would arrive with its headline, and the leak would have a name, and it would be someone closer than any of them had imagined.
