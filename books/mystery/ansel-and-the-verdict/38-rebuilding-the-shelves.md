## 38. Rebuilding the Shelves

The carpenter's name was Hollis, and he smelled of sawdust and pipe tobacco, and he treated the ruined bookshop like a patient he'd grown fond of.

"Good bones," he said for the fourth time that morning, running a palm along a joist blackened at one end but sound at its heart. "Fire took the front wall and the shelving, but she held. Most of her held."

Wren stood in the doorway with two coffees and let him work. Three weeks had passed since the night the shop burned—three weeks of insurance forms and police interviews and sleeping badly in Priya's guest room while Theo slept worse on the couch below—and Wren still could not walk past the corner where the mystery section used to be without feeling something catch in their chest like a splinter worked loose.

The fire marshal had ruled it arson within a day. accelerant poured along the baseboards, lit from the alley door. Julian Ashcombe's network, the prosecutors said, sending a message to witnesses. The message had arrived too late to matter; by then Marisol Verne's affidavit was signed, Elena Ashcombe's testimony was transcribed, and the criminal complaints against six members of Julian's apparatus were already filed. But the fire had taken the first editions, the reading chairs, the ladder that groaned on its brass rail, and forty years of marginalia in books Wren had sold to no one because they couldn't bear to part with them.

"You can stand there all morning," Hollis said without turning around, "but the coffee's getting cold and the wall isn't going to rebuild itself."

Wren crossed the scorched threshold and handed him his cup. Outside, the town moved past the boarded storefront the way it always had—briskly, incuriously, the way it had moved past Wren for eleven years behind the counter. Except that now they didn't. Now they slowed. Now a woman from the bakery pressed a paper bag of rolls into Wren's hands and refused payment. Now a man who had bought exactly three paperbacks in a decade stopped to say his wife wanted to know when they'd reopen, because she'd been meaning to come in for years.

*For years.* Everyone had been meaning to come in for years.

Wren drank their coffee and did not say what they were thinking, which was that it had taken a dead stenographer, an erased heiress, a burning bookshop, and a confession written by a lighthouse keeper's daughter to make this town see them. They were not sure whether the thought was bitter or merely true. Probably both. Most honest things were.

* * *

The letter from Marisol Verne arrived on a Thursday, in handwriting that slanted leftward like something braced against wind.

*Wren —*

*I have spent thirty years being careful with money because I never knew when I would need to run again. I find I am tired of running and tired of the money both. Take some of it. Rebuild the shop. Buy better locks.*

*I know you will refuse. Refuse anyway, and then accept, because Elena tells me you are constitutionally incapable of charity and will only take it if I call it something else. So: consider it back pay. You were my investigator for twenty-five years without knowing it. No one works that long without wages.*

*— M.V.*

There was a check enclosed. It was not modest, whatever the premise of it claimed. Wren sat with it for a long time in the back room, which was the only room, now, and finally did the thing they had never once done in eleven years of quiet, invisible, self-sufficient solitude: they asked for advice.

Theo was in the alley splitting kindling from the salvage, because he had decided that grief was best processed with an axe, and Wren leaned in the doorway and held out the letter.

He read it twice. His hands had healed from the rope burns Julian's man had given him, mostly; there was still a stiffness in the left ring finger when it was cold.

"She's right," he said. "You'll only take it if it's called something else."

"That's not the question."

"It's absolutely the question." He set the letter down on the chopping block and looked at Wren with the directness he'd learned somewhere between his uncle's drowning and the texts that had terrified him into watching a stranger's bookshop. "You pulled me off a bus stop bench and gave me your floor to sleep on before you even knew my name. You didn't ask what it cost you. So don't tell me what it costs you to take this."

Wren looked at the check. At the boy who was not really a boy anymore, though he was still young enough that the word *boy* fit him in the way a coat fits someone still growing into it.

"I want a partner," Wren said. "Not an employee. The shop can't carry two salaries yet, maybe not for a year. But if we rebuild it together—"

"Yes," Theo said, before Wren finished the sentence, and then went red, and then pretended to be very interested in the kindling.

"The paperwork will be tedious."

"I don't care."

"There will be inventory at six in the morning."

"I've been up since five every day since the fire anyway," he said. "I sleep better when I'm doing something."

Wren nodded, and turned back inside, and stood for a moment in the empty shell of the shop with the light coming through the new plywood over the front window in pale dusty bars, and allowed themselves—briefly, privately, in the manner of a person testing ice—to feel something that might have been happiness.

* * *

They rebuilt through October.

Hollis framed the new front wall with taller windows than the old one, because Wren said, surprising themself, that they were done with places that hid. The new shelves went up in pine instead of oak—cheaper, lighter, and Theo stained them himself over four evenings until his hands were the color of strong tea. The reading chairs came secondhand from an estate sale, and Wren chose them deliberately ugly and deliberately comfortable, the kind of chairs people fell asleep in, because a shop should forgive you for staying.

Priya came by on a Saturday with a bottle of wine and an expression she probably thought was neutral.

"The DA's office is calling it the Greywater case," she said. "Six indictments. Corliss's replacement is cooperating in exchange for immunity, which is disgusting and useful. And the probate order came through final yesterday—the forged will is void, the 1994 instrument controls, and Elena Ashcombe is restored as beneficiary." She turned her glass by the stem. "She's taking the name Ansel, by the way. Did she tell you? Legally changing it. Said the family name belonged to the house, and the house belonged to the lie."

"She mentioned it." Wren had. Over tea, in the lake cabin, in the strange gentle week after the verdict when none of them quite knew how to be ordinary people again. "And Marisol?"

"Testifying in the criminal trials, spring docket. Her lawyer got her death certificate vacated last month—there was apparently a great deal of ceremony about it. She said—" Priya smiled into her wine. "She said the strangest part wasn't being declared alive. It was signing her own name in public for the first time in twenty-five years, and having the clerk just... stamp it. Like it was nothing."

"To her it was everything."

"To her it was everything," Priya agreed. She looked around the half-built shop—at the raw pine, the plastic sheeting, the salvaged counter with its burn scar sanded down but not gone. "You know, when you first came to me with those letters, I told you anonymous correspondence wasn't evidence and a retired detective playing vigilante would burn whatever credibility you had left."

"You did."

"I was wrong about the second part." She said it plainly, the way she said most hard things. "You weren't playing vigilante. You were doing what you always did—quietly, alone, without credit—and the difference is that this time you let people in. That's not a small thing, Wren. For you, I think it was the hardest thing in the whole case."

Wren looked at the counter. "It was harder than the docks," they admitted. "The docks, at least, I understood."

* * *

The shop reopened on a bright cold Tuesday in November, and Wren had expected perhaps a dozen curious people and a slow afternoon.

They had underestimated.

By ten there was a line. By noon it reached the bakery. People Wren had sold books to in silence for a decade came in and stayed—an hour, two hours—touching the new pine shelves as if checking they were real, asking after Theo's finger, asking after *the stenographer*, because everyone in town knew the story now, or thought they did, and wanted to see the place where it had started. A woman bought seven mysteries and said her mother had known Edmund Hale slightly, from church, and wasn't it terrible, and wasn't it wonderful, the truth coming out. The mayor came. The mayor! Wren, who had once watched the same man walk past the shop's window every day for three years without once looking in, shook his hand and found the gesture cost less than feared.

Theo worked the register and glowed. He had a talent for it Wren hadn't suspected—remembering names, remembering what people's daughters liked to read, steering the uncertain toward exactly the right book with a confidence that made Wren feel, obscurely, proud in a way they had no template for. Fatherhood, or something adjacent to it, arriving at sixty-one by way of a bus stop bench.

At closing time the bell over the door rang once more, and Marisol Verne came in out of the cold.

She was smaller than Wren had pictured from the chapel, bundled in a gray coat, her hair gone fully silver now and cut short and practical. She stood in the middle of the floor and turned slowly, taking in the shelves, the chairs, the lamp already lit in the window.

"Well," she said. "You spent it."

"Every sensible cent."

"Liar. Elena says you haggled with the electrician."

"He overcharged."

"He did," Marisol agreed. "Everyone does." She walked the length of the mystery section and stopped, and touched the spine of a book at random, and Wren saw her hand was steady and knew what it had cost to make it so. "I brought something. For the local history shelf—Elena tells me you're starting one."

She set a flat archival box on the counter and opened it. Inside, under acid-free tissue, lay a transcript. Typewritten, ribboned, the pages soft with age. The caption page read, in faded ink:

*In re Estate of ANSEL, Deceased. Probate Division, 1994. Transcript of proceedings. Stenographically recorded by M. Verne, Official Reporter.*

"I kept the original plates in my machine," Marisol said. "And I kept this copy everywhere I went for twenty-five years. It's been in four countries and one boat I won't discuss. It should live here now. Where people can read it."

Wren looked at the box. At the woman who had died on paper in 1998 and walked into a bookshop in November breathing cold air and alive.

"Thank you," Wren said, and found the words insufficient, and said them anyway, because sometimes insufficient words are the only honest ones.

Marisol smiled—a real one, unguarded, the first Wren had seen from her that wasn't armor. "Don't thank me. Bill me. I understand you're very reasonable about rates."

* * *

That night, after Theo had gone home to the apartment above the hardware store and the shop was dark except for the lamp, Wren sat in one of the ugly comfortable chairs with the transcript open on their knees.

They read the proceedings slowly. The testimony of servants. The accounting of the estate. And near the end, entered into evidence, the deposition of a housemaid named Adunola Oyelaran—Oyelaran, Wren's own name, their grandmother's name—who had testified in 1994 that she had seen Mr. Ashcombe senior sign the original instrument, and that she had witnessed the date, and that no one in the household had ever asked her to swear to anything before and she hoped she said it right.

*She hoped she said it right.*

Wren read the line four times. Their grandmother, whom they had loved and barely known, who had died when Wren was nineteen, who had never once spoken of the great house on the hill or the years she'd spent in service there—she had been the beginning. The overlooked maid whose plain, careful testimony had anchored the original will. The thread that ran from her mouth in a courtroom in 1994 through a forged document and three staged accidents and a drowned stenographer and two letters in a mail slot on a gray Tuesday, all the way to a burned shop rebuilt in pine with a boy at the register and a lamp in the window.

Wren closed the transcript and sat in the lamplight for a long time.

Then they took out a pen, and on the flyleaf, beneath the case caption, wrote the note that would stay with the book forever:

*Every verdict has a first witness. This one hoped she said it right. She did.*

Outside, the tide was coming in over the shoals where Edmund Hale had drowned, where a boat had fled into fog, where the sea kept what it kept and gave back what it chose. Tomorrow the trials would begin to move, and spring would bring Marisol to the stand, and there would be more nights like this one—hard ones, unfinished ones. Wren was done pretending otherwise.

But the lamp was lit. The door would open at nine. And for the first time in longer than Wren could measure, invisible was nowhere on the premises.

They switched off the light, and stayed.
