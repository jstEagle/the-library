## 7. Ruth Remembers Falling

The house on Delmore Street still smells of formalin.

Forty years in the coroner's office and the smell follows you home, settles into the curtains, the upholstery, the grain of the wooden spoon you stir your tea with. People think it leaves when you retire. It doesn't. It waits. It sits in the rooms where you sleep and reminds you of every body you ever lifted, every jaw you ever wired shut, every lie you signed your name beneath in a neat, legible hand.

I am seventy-three years old. I have buried a husband, outlived two sisters, and watched my granddaughter grow from a colicky infant into a young woman studying biochemistry at a university three hundred miles away. I have made my peace with most things. Death, I made my peace with decades ago — it is the honest one, the only appointment none of us reschedules.

It is the lying I never got good at. And now a young woman with gray eyes and a canvas satchel is standing on my porch, holding a photograph of a dead woman, and I understand that my forty years of lying have finally come due.

"Mrs. Okafor," she says. "Hazel Iversen. My father was Thomas Iversen."

"I know who your father was." I open the door wider than I intend to. That is the first betrayal, though not the last. "Come in before the neighbors see you. They'll invent something worse than the truth, and the truth is bad enough."

* * *

I make tea because my hands need something to do that isn't shaking. She sits at my kitchen table — the same table where I laid out the Marlowe paperwork twelve years ago, the same table where I told myself the story until I believed it — and she does not touch her cup. She just looks at me with those eyes that make me feel like a slide under a microscope, like something already fixed and stained and ready for examination.

"Edith Marlowe," she says. "October ninth, 2013. Found at the foot of her cellar stairs by her housekeeper. Ruled accidental. Cause of death: blunt force trauma consistent with a fall, compounded by cardiac arrest. You were the deputy coroner on scene. You signed the preliminary findings."

"You've read the file."

"I've *memorized* the file." She says it without pride, the way you'd say *I've read the weather report*. "Page eleven is missing from the county copy. I photographed it once, years ago, and I can't find my photograph of it, and I can't remember whether I never took one or whether someone took it from me. That's never happened before. Things don't leave my head, Mrs. Okafor. Things don't leave my files. Except this."

I wrap my hands around my own cup because the warmth is the only honest thing left in the room.

"You were seventeen when she died," I say.

"I was seventeen, and I was delivering the evening paper on Cordwainer Lane, and I saw a car I shouldn't have seen. But that's not what I came to ask you about." She leans forward, and her voice drops into something almost gentle, which is worse than if she'd shouted. "I came to ask you about her neck."

The kettle ticks as it cools. Somewhere down the street a dog is barking at nothing, the way dogs do, the way they always know.

"Her neck," I repeat.

"You wrote in your own hand — and then struck through, and then initialed the strike-through, which is a strange thing to do unless you wanted the strike-through to be legible — a note about 'discoloration at the anterior cervical region, consistent with ligature pressure, pre-mortem.' Pre-mortem, Mrs. Okafor. Before she died. That's not a fall. That's a rope, or a cord, or a man's hands, and you knew it, and then you buried your own knowledge in plain sight and hoped nobody would ever read it closely enough."

I have prepared for this conversation for twelve years. I have rehearsed it in the shower, on my knees at the bedside, in the long gray hours before dawn when the formalin smell is strongest. I had a whole speech. *The evidence was ambiguous. Falls can produce remarkable bruising. I deferred to Dr. Prewitt, the coroner of record. I am an old woman and my memory is not what it was.*

The speech dissolves in my throat like sugar in hot water.

"Sit still, child," I say, "and let an old fool tell it in order, because if I tell it out of order I will never finish."

* * *

Edith Marlowe was not a kind woman. I want that on the record, whatever record remains after tonight. She was sharp-tongued and solitary and she kept her house like a museum and her heart like a locked drawer, and half the town was afraid of her and the other half owed her money. But she was *ours*. She was the last of the old Marlowes, the family that built the mill that built the town, and when she died the whole community exhaled at once — grief and relief in the same breath, which is a breath I have smelled before, on the faces of the living standing over the recently dead.

I got the call at 6:40 in the morning. The housekeeper, a soft little woman named Ada Pell, had found her at the foot of the cellar stairs, folded like laundry. I remember the light — that thin, metallic October light that makes everything look already embalmed. I remember the smell of lavender and dust and, beneath it, something faintly wrong, the way a room is faintly wrong when a secret has been kept in it overnight.

I knelt beside the body and I did what I had done ten thousand times. I looked.

And her neck looked back at me.

There was a bruise across the front of her throat. A broad, dark band, high up, just beneath the jaw, and it was not the color of a fall. I have examined a hundred bodies at the bottoms of a hundred staircases, and a fall bruises you where you land — the shoulder, the hip, the back of the skull, the heels. It does not draw a neat dark ribbon across the front of your windpipe. That bruise had a *shape*. It had the shape of something that had been pulled tight.

I pressed my gloved finger to the skin — gently, always gently; the dead have given enough — and the tissue told me what tissue always tells me: this happened while the heart was still beating. Pre-mortem. Ante-mortem. Before.

I sat back on my heels in that lavender-and-dust room and I understood, with the terrible clarity that comes maybe three times in a working life, that Edith Marlowe had been strangled and then rolled down her own cellar stairs, and that whoever did it had been standing in that house not four hours earlier, and that I was the only person in the world positioned to say so.

And then the family arrived.

* * *

They didn't come all at once, the way mourners do. They came *strategically*, within the hour, before I'd finished my preliminary notes. Aldous Vane — young then, already smooth, already wearing authority like a coat that fit him — arrived first, with the family attorney. Then two cousins I will not name here, though I have written their names on a piece of paper and put the paper in the drawer with my husband's watch, because some part of me has been waiting twelve years for someone to ask.

They did not cry. That is the detail I keep returning to, the one that has kept me up on the bad nights. Mourners cry, or they go rigid, or they rage. These people *negotiated*. They stood in Edith's parlor among her dead lavender and spoke in the low, reasonable voices of men settling an estate, and Aldous Vane put his hand on my shoulder — I can still feel it, the weight of it, five fingers of warm authority through my uniform — and he said:

"Ruth. Edith was elderly. She was unsteady. She had refused a stair rail for years out of pure stubbornness. Nobody gains anything from a long investigation. The town has been through enough."

And then the attorney said, even more quietly: "Dr. Prewitt will be signing the final report. Your preliminary notes are, of course, *preliminary*. Preliminary notes are frequently revised in light of the complete picture."

And then one of the cousins — I will not say which; the paper in the drawer can say — said, in a voice like a door closing: "Your granddaughter is what, twelve now? Bright girl, I hear. It's expensive, the good schools."

I did not answer him. I want that understood. I did not answer, I did not nod, I did not shake his hand. I gathered my instruments and I walked out of that house and I sat in my car in the driveway for eleven minutes — I know it was eleven because I watched the clock — hating every one of them and hating myself more, because I already knew what I was going to do.

I went back inside. I struck through my own sentence. I initialed the strike-through so that it would be legible — so that *somebody*, someday, might read my cowardice the way you read a confession scratched into a prison wall. And I wrote *blunt force trauma consistent with a fall*, and I signed it, and I drove home, and I vomited into the hydrangeas, and I slept for fourteen hours as though sleep were a country I could emigrate to.

The anonymous gift came in March.

Five thousand dollars into a college fund I had opened for Amara when she was born, an account that had grown at the speed of a schoolteacher's pension — slowly, faithfully, never fast enough. Five thousand dollars, wired through a foundation with a name so generic I have never been able to find its offices. A card came with it. *For a promising young scientist. From a friend of the community.*

I called the number on the card. It had been disconnected before I finished dialing.

I never spent a dollar of it. Do you understand? I let it sit. I let it grow. I told Amara it was there for her, and she used it — of course she used it, she's brilliant, she deserved it, she deserved it *without* that bruise on a dead woman's throat — and every semester when the tuition cleared, I sat at this table and I felt the money pass through me like something swallowed that will not digest.

Complicity is not a single act. It is a diet. You eat a little every day for twelve years and one morning you look down and you do not recognize your own body.

* * *

Hazel Iversen has not interrupted me once. She listens the way I imagine a court stenographer listens, except her machine is behind her eyes. When I finish, she is quiet for a long moment, and then she asks the question I have been dreading more than any other, the question I have not let myself answer in twelve years:

"Did you tell my father?"

The tea in my cup has gone cold. I hold it anyway.

"Thomas Iversen came to see me," I say. "Three weeks after the funeral. He was notary for half this town; everyone's secrets passed through his stamp eventually. He came to my office with a folder under his arm, and he sat where you're sitting, and he said, 'Ruth, I need you to tell me what you saw at the Marlowe house.' And I looked at him — at that honest, tired face — and I lied to him. I gave him the fall. The whole fall, the stairs, the stubborn old woman, the tragedy of it. And he looked at me for a long time, the way you're looking at me now, and he said, 'All right, Ruth. Thank you for your time.' And he left."

"Did he believe you?"

"I don't know." My voice cracks on it, and I let it. "I have asked myself that question every day for twelve years. Because three days later, the county copy of my preliminary file came back from records with page eleven missing — the page with my note, the strike-through, the initials. And Thomas Iversen was the man who notarized half the documents that crossed that records office. And I have spent twelve years not knowing whether he stole that page to protect the liars, or stole it to protect the truth, or stole it because I lied to his face and he knew it and he wanted to spare me — spare *me*, do you understand, spare the woman who strangled her own integrity with a strike-through and called it procedure."

Hazel's face does something I have never seen a face do. It is not shock. It is not grief. It is recognition — the look of a woman finding a piece of a map she has been assembling in the dark.

"Page eleven was in his storage unit," she says quietly. "He sealed it. A witness statement, notarized the night Edith died. He sealed it, Mrs. Okafor. He didn't destroy it. He *sealed* it."

I close my eyes. Twelve years of calcified guilt shift in my chest like a stone rolling in a landslide.

"Then he lied to me too," I say. "Or I lied to him and he chose the truth anyway, and either way—" I stop. The dog down the street has gone silent. The house is very still. "Either way, child, there is a page in this world that says what I saw, and there is an old woman sitting in front of you who is done eating complicity for breakfast."

I go to the drawer with my husband's watch. I take out the paper with the names. I set it on the table between us, and I turn it face up.

"Two things before you go," I say. "The cousins on that paper were not the only visitors that morning. There was a third car in the drive — a small gray sedan, and a woman sat in it the whole time and never came inside. I saw her through the parlor window. Dark hair, gray at the temples, hands folded on the wheel like a woman at a funeral that hadn't started yet. I never learned her name. But Hazel Iversen — I saw her again, once, two years ago, at the pharmacy on Marsh Street. She was standing at the counter, and she was buying—"

My hands begin to shake, and this time I let them.

"—she was buying paper. Cream-colored paper. And ink. And sealing wax. The kind you use for letters. And she paid in cash, and when she left, she looked at me across the store, and she *smiled*, as if she knew exactly what I'd seen at that window twelve years ago, and exactly what I'd never said."

Hazel is already on her feet, satchel over her shoulder, and there is something in her gray eyes now that I recognize from mirrors in my working years — the look of a person about to open a body to find out what killed it.

"What was her name at the pharmacy," Hazel says. "The name on anything. A prescription, a loyalty card. Think."

"Think," I repeat, and I laugh, a dry terrible sound. "Child, I *never* stop thinking. Her card said M. Ashe. Miriam Ashe. And I have been carrying that name in my mouth for two years like a stone, waiting for someone to ask me to spit it out."

She is at the door. She pauses with her hand on the frame, and for a moment she is not an investigator at all, just a young woman standing in an old liar's kitchen, and she says, softly: "Thank you, Mrs. Okafor. For the names. And for finally saying it."

"Saying it doesn't raise the dead," I tell her. "But it might keep the next one from falling."

The door closes behind her. The formalin smell settles back over the kitchen like snow.

And I sit at my table, in the house where the smell has lived for forty years, and I pick up the telephone, and I dial the number for the state attorney's office, because an old woman who has lied for twelve years has exactly one honest act left in her — and I intend to spend it before whoever is writing letters in a dead woman's hand finishes writing mine.
