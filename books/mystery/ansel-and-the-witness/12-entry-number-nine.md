## 12. Entry Number Nine

The candle had burned to a stub by the time Noor finished the Vane correspondence, and she was reaching for the snuffer when she saw it.

The ledger lay open on the reading table where she had left it — she was certain of that, certain the way one is only certain of things one has arranged ten thousand times. Left-hand page, right-hand page, ribbon marker draped across the gutter like a sleeping snake. She had closed it. Or she had meant to close it. The distinction mattered now, in the way all small distinctions matter once the ground begins to tilt.

She did not touch it at first. Twenty years of archival discipline lived in her hands, and her hands knew what to do with evidence: nothing. She stood very still and let the candle flame lean in the draft from the window casement, and she looked.

The entry sat at the bottom of the right-hand page, below the last death she had catalogued — old Mr. Fenwick, organist emeritus, died of his heart in March, filed under *natural causes, verified*. Below Fenwick, in ink that had not been there yesterday, in a hand she knew better than her own face:

*E. — verger's boy. Thursday next. Drowned.*

Noor read it three times. The letters were hers. Not similar — hers. The particular forward slant of her lowercase e, the way she crossed her t's low and quick, the tiny hesitation she always made before a capital letter, a faint thickening of ink where the nib paused. Whoever had forged her hand before had done it again, and this time they had written a name she had spoken aloud only hours ago, in this room, to no one but Marginalia and the dark.

*Tam.*

She had said it out loud. She remembered now — standing at the shelf of recent accessions, telling the cat about the boy who had wept into the flour and sworn he never saw a face, only heard a voice. *Tam,* she had said. *Poor frightened Tam.* And the archive had heard her. The archive, or whatever moved through it at night like water through limestone.

Marginalia appeared from behind the map chests, tail up, and wound once around her ankles. Noor did not pick him up. Her hands stayed folded, pressed flat against her sternum, as if holding shut a door.

Thursday next. Four days. Three, if you counted from midnight, and the ink suggested midnight — the entry had the freshness of something made in the dark hours, when the cathedral's stones gave back the day's heat and every sound traveled down the nave like a confession.

She thought: I will burn it. She thought: I will take the ledger to the river and let the Thames have it, let the whole archive go, let them find nothing but ash and my absence. She had rehearsed disappearances for twenty years; she could perform one now, tonight, with the boy in tow. There were trains. There were ferries. There was a version of her life that began again under another name, third name, fourth name, and Tam could grow up never knowing he had been a line item in someone else's book.

But the flour trap had already told her what flight would cost. The killer — the forger, the voice, whoever wore her handwriting like a stolen coat — had come inside. Had stood where she stood. Had read what she read. Flight would not end the ledger; it would only move its keeping.

And beneath all of it, quieter than the draft through the casement, was the thing she could not say even to herself: *E.* The entry said *E.* Elias was E. She was E. The boy was neither, except that someone had decided he was close enough — a placeholder, a rehearsal, a message addressed to whichever Vane still drew breath.

Her hands came apart. She picked up the ledger, carried it to the light, and examined the entry under the magnifier she kept for watermark work. The ink was iron gall, properly mixed, properly aged — aged, she realized with a coldness that started in her teeth, because the forger mixed it weeks ago and let it oxidize, so that no chemical test would ever prove the entry new. This was not vandalism. This was craft. This was patience wearing her face.

The candle guttered and went out. In the sudden grey of pre-dawn, the archive looked exactly as it had looked for twenty years, and entirely different.

Noor set the ledger down, took off her white gloves finger by finger, and reached for the telephone.

* * *

There was a telephone in the archive because the chapter insisted on one — an emergency line, cream-colored, bolted to the wall beside the vestry door, its cord coiled like something waiting. In twenty years Noor had dialed it perhaps six times. Twice for burst pipes. Once for a bishop's funeral. Never, not once, for herself.

Detective Brandt's card sat in the third drawer of her desk, beneath the blotter, beneath a false bottom of receipts, weighted under a paperweight shaped like a cathedral she had never visited. She had kept the card for eleven days without deciding anything about it. Now she lifted it out and read the number twice, and her heart performed a slow, traitorous percussion against her ribs.

Cardinal rule: never call attention. Never be findable. Never let the institution look too closely at the woman who knew where everything was buried, literally and otherwise. She had built her life inside that rule the way a snail builds inside its shell — not comfortably, but completely.

She dialed anyway.

It rang four times. On the fifth ring, a voice answered, unguarded, roughened by sleep or by the hour: "Brandt."

"It's Noor Vane." Even saying the name aloud felt like opening a window in a house sealed for winter. "From the archive. At Saint Aldhelm's."

A pause. She could hear the detective thinking — hear the rustle of sheets, the click of a lamp. "Do you know what time it is?"

"Half past four." Noor's voice came out steadier than she deserved. "I need to report a crime."

"A crime." Flat. Awake now. "What kind?"

"The kind that hasn't happened yet." She closed her eyes. There was no elegant way to say it, so she said it plainly, the way she catalogued deaths — date, cause, location, verified. "Someone wrote an entry in my ledger overnight. It predicts a murder. A boy named Tam — Thomas Wrenley, the junior verger. Thursday. Drowned. And Detective — the last three entries in that hand have all come true."

Silence stretched long enough that she checked whether the line had gone dead.

"You're telling me," Brandt said finally, each word placed like a stone in a wall, "that you've had predictive entries in your possession. Plural. For some time. And you're calling me now."

"I'm calling you now," Noor said, "because now it's someone I know."

Another silence, but a different quality of silence — the sound of a decision being made on the far end of the line. When Brandt spoke again, the sleep was gone from her voice entirely, replaced by something dry and tireless.

"Don't touch the ledger. Don't clean anything. Don't let anyone else in — including clergy, including your bishop, especially anyone who asks to see it. I'll be there in forty minutes. And Vane—" a breath, "—the fact that you called at half past four instead of running tells me more than you think it does. Forty minutes."

The line went dead. Noor lowered the receiver onto its cradle and stood in the grey light with her palm still resting on it, as though the telephone were a rail and she a swimmer who had just caught hold after a long time in deep water.

Marginalia jumped onto the reading table and sat down directly on the open ledger, over the forged entry, as if he could smother it with his weight. Noor almost smiled. Almost.

Then she went to the crypt door and checked the lock, and found it exactly as she had left it — and understood, with a clarity that arrived like cold water, that the lock had never been the point. Whatever came and went in her archive did not use doors.

* * *

Brandt arrived in thirty-two minutes, which meant she had driven fast through streets still blue with night. She came down the archive stairs without ceremony, coat over her arm, hair pinned up in a way that suggested she had dressed in the dark, and stopped two steps from the bottom to look around — at the candle stub, the ledgers ranked in their cases, the map chests, the cat.

"So this is where you live," she said.

"This is where I work."

"At half past four in the morning." Brandt came the rest of the way down. Up close she smelled of rain and cheap coffee and something medicinal, lozenges maybe. Her eyes went straight to the reading table, to the ledger with the cat still sitting on it like a paperweight with opinions. "Move the animal."

"He won't—"

"Move the animal, Vane."

Noor lifted Marginalia down. He protested with a single indignant note and retired to the map chests, tail lashing. Brandt pulled on nitrile gloves from her coat pocket — she had come prepared, Noor realized; she had expected to find a body, or the beginnings of one — and bent over the ledger without touching it.

"Show me the true entries first. The ones that came true."

Noor opened the earlier pages with a gloved fingertip, angling the lamp. Brandt read the way she did everything, Noor was beginning to understand — quickly, thoroughly, with a flat expression that concealed the machinery underneath. Her eyes tracked down the columns: dates, names, causes. The alderman. The solicitor. The woman from the parish records office who had fallen from the triforium gallery in February while everyone said *ladder* and no ladder was ever found.

"All in this hand?" Brandt asked.

"All in my hand."

"And you didn't write them."

"No."

Brandt straightened. For a moment she just looked at Noor, and the look was long enough to be uncomfortable, long enough to be a question asked without words. Then: "Walk me through last night. Everything you touched, everyone who came near, every sound you can't account for."

So Noor walked her through it. The closing ritual — candles, checks, the ledger squared to the table edge. The hour she spent with the Vane correspondence. Telling the cat about Tam. Sleeping in the alcove bed behind the stacks, as she did three nights in five, because the archive slept better with someone in it. Waking at four to the sense — not a sound, only a sense — that the air had been rearranged.

"You sleep here," Brandt said. "In the same room as these books."

"They're safer with me."

"That's not what I asked." Brandt turned back to the ledger. "You sleep here, and someone entered anyway, wrote a death warrant in your own handwriting, and left without waking you. Either they're very good, or—" she tapped the page, once, above the word *drowned*, "—they wanted you to find it. Wanted you awake. Wanted you exactly where you are, doing exactly what you're doing."

"Which is calling you."

"Which is calling me." Brandt removed her gloves, folded them into a pocket. "Congratulations, Vane. You may be the bait."

The word landed in the quiet room and sat there. Noor found she was not surprised — only tired, in the marrow-deep way of someone who has spent twenty years being careful and has just learned that careful was never the same as safe.

"Then we should talk about the boy," she said. "Tam. He's sixteen. He copies music for the choir and feeds the verger's cat and he cried when I questioned him, and he is not going to spend Thursday anywhere near water if I have anything to say about it."

"Where does he live?"

"The verger's lodging, east range. With his aunt."

"Then today he develops a reason to be somewhere else." Brandt was already moving toward the stairs, already halfway into her coat. "I'll arrange it quietly — a school trip, a sick relative, something boring. Boring keeps boys alive better than drama does." She paused at the foot of the steps and looked back, and in the lamplight her face was unreadable and yet not entirely closed. "One more thing. The entry says *E.*"

Noor said nothing.

"Elias Vane drowned in 1998," Brandt said. "Or the record says he did. And you are Noor Vane, which is also an E-name if a person squinted at it. Someone writing in your hand, using initials that belong to your family—" she let it hang, then finished it gently, which was somehow worse, "—that's not a coincidence, Vane. That's a conversation. Somebody is talking to you, and they're using deaths as punctuation."

She climbed the stairs. At the top, her silhouette broke the grey light from the clerestory windows, and her voice came down one last time, level as a ruled line:

"Thursday, I'll be back. Between now and then, don't be alone with any book you didn't write."

Her footsteps faded up the stairwell. The outer door boomed shut.

Noor stood alone in the archive with the ledger open between her hands, and the forged entry, and the cat, and the four days stretching ahead of her like a corridor with no windows. For twenty years her cardinal rule had been *never call attention*, and she had broken it before dawn, and the sky outside had not fallen, and the detective had come, and the world had continued turning.

But the entry remained. *Drowned.* And beneath the fear, rising slowly through it like a bubble through dark water, was a thought she could not put down:

Whoever held the pen had chosen the word *drowned* — and only two people alive knew why that word, of all words, was aimed at her.
