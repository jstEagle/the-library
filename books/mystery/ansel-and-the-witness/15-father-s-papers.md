## 15. Father's Papers

The sealed records lived in the third bay of the archive, behind a door I had opened perhaps six times in twenty years.

It was not locked — that was the joke of it, the small blasphemy of it. The diocesan seal on the door was wax and ribbon and the assumption that no one would dare. The chapter trusted to shame where other institutions used steel. And for two decades I had honored that trust, or rather I had honored my own cowardice, which wore trust's clothing so well that even I sometimes mistook them.

Tonight I dared.

I had brought the lamp with the green glass shade because its light did not flicker, and flicker was a thing I could not afford. I had brought Marginalia's saucer of water because she insisted on supervising anything done after compline, perching on the shelf-ends like a small gray gargoyle with opinions. And I had brought the forged entry — E. Vane, dated next week — folded into my breast pocket, against my heart, as though paper could be armed.

*Prove it hasn't happened yet,* Mr. Sallow had said, in that voice like a drawer closing. *You of all people.*

I had spent three nights failing to prove any such thing, and one night succeeding at something else entirely: discovering that every recent death near the cathedral square — the solicitor Hargreaves, the retired coroner Mrs. Pell, the organist's widow Constance Airey — appeared in my ledgers before it occurred, in handwriting indistinguishable from mine, in ink I did not own. The next name was mine, or nearly. The date was four days out.

Which meant the killer was not merely reading my archive.

He was continuing it.

And there was only one collection in this building old enough, poisoned enough, to teach someone how a Vane thought.

* * *

The sealed bay smelled of tallow and dust and something faintly sweet, like fruit going over in a locked room. The air itself seemed older here, unbreathed, kept the way a wound is kept under gauze. My lamp threw a greenish pool across the shelves, and in that light the boxes looked less like records than like reliquaries — each one holding some sainted piece of scandal, bones of reputations long since buried elsewhere.

Aldous Vane had been the cathedral's benefactor for eleven years. His name was cut into the donor boards by the west door, gold leaf on walnut, letters tall enough to read from the back pew. *Given by Aldous Vane, in gratitude.* I had walked past those letters ten thousand times. As a child I had traced them with my finger when no one was looking, the way other children touched saints' toes for luck.

I had never once wondered what he was grateful *for*.

The first box I pulled was labeled in his own hand — that upright, patient script I knew better than my own face, having spent my girlhood copying it out as penance for slovenly capitals. *Correspondence, 1996–1998.* The string tying it had been knotted by someone who expected never to return. I cut it with the bone folder I keep in my sleeve, an archivist's small violence, and lifted the lid.

Letters. Dozens of them. Not drafts — *received*. Letters written to my father by judges, aldermen, a police superintendent whose name still appeared on the plaque outside the station house. I unfolded the topmost one with hands that had gone strangely cold, though the archive was warm enough that sweat pricked at my hairline.

*My dear Aldous — You will understand that I cannot put what we discussed in writing, and yet here I am writing it, which tells you everything about my position. The sum is possible if paid quarterly. It is not possible if you continue to—*

The rest was torn away. Torn cleanly, deliberately, by someone who wanted the sentence finished but not legible. I turned the page toward the lamp. The tear was old; the fibers had yellowed evenly. Whoever had mutilated these letters had done it decades ago, carefully, keeping the threats and discarding the specifics — or keeping the specifics somewhere else.

Somewhere else. My eyes went, unwillingly, down the length of the sealed bay. Forty boxes. Fifty. All in his hand. All knotted shut.

Marginalia leapt from the shelf-end onto the open box, landing with the particular insolence cats reserve for sacred ground, and began washing her face directly over a letter from a man who had since been made a knight. I let her. Some part of me wanted her weight there, wanted anything alive between me and the paper.

Because I understood now what I was looking at, and understanding came not as a shock but as a slow settling, like silt finding the bottom of a jar.

My father had not given money to the cathedral.

He had taken money *from* it — from everyone in it, around it, above it. He had sat in the crypt below my feet with his papers and his patience and he had made the city pay him rent on its own sins. The benefactor boards were not gratitude. They were tribute. Gold leaf as protection money, cut and gilded by craftsmen who never asked why a private man's generosity ran through the diocese like a river through stolen land.

I had spent twenty years believing I had hidden from my father's disgrace.

I had hidden inside his machine.

* * *

I worked until the lamp oil burned low, and then I worked past that, feeding it from the reserve can I keep for emergencies, telling myself this qualified. The green light moved across the boxes as the hours turned, and I moved with it, box by box, and the shape of the thing assembled itself in me the way a negative develops — nothing added, only revealed.

There were letters from the dean's office. From two members of the city council. From the editor of the *Herald*, whose paper had run the 2004 trial coverage with such scrupulous balance. There was a photograph, stiff with age, of men standing on the crypt steps — my father among them, younger than I remembered him, smiling the way he smiled when a deal closed, and beside him a young policeman I recognized from the trial gallery, and beside *him*, half-turned from the camera as though he had already begun to regret the afternoon, a woman whose face I knew from funeral notices: Mrs. Pell.

The coroner. Dead last month. Dead exactly as predicted, four days after the entry appeared in my ledger in my own stolen hand.

I set the photograph down flat, as one does with a thing that might bite, and pressed my palms against the table until the wood's grain printed itself into my skin. Sensation to hold onto. Archivist's trick. When the mind floods, give the body a task.

Think it through, Noor. In order. Like cataloguing.

Item: my father blackmailed perhaps thirty people over eleven years. Item: in 2004 he was tried — publicly, spectacularly — for fraud and extortion, and the case collapsed when key witnesses recanted or died or simply failed to appear, and he walked free into the cathedral close to die privately eight months later. Item: the witnesses who *did* testify, who did not recant — Hargreaves, Pell, Airey, and others — were the ones whose names now marched through my ledgers ahead of their deaths, each entry arriving days early like a bill sent before the service.

Item, and this was the item that made me stand up and walk once around the sealed bay because sitting still had become unbearable:

The witness list was not a list of people who had testified *against* him.

I found the proof in the eleventh box, in a folder marked only with a date — the fourteenth of March, 2004, the week the trial collapsed. Inside was a single sheet in my father's hand, no salutation, no signature, just names in a column, nine of them, each with a figure beside it and a word. I read the words twice before they stopped swimming.

*Paid. Paid. Paid. Refused. Refused. Paid. Refused. Paid. Refused.*

Six paid. Three refused.

Hargreaves had refused. Pell had refused. Airey had refused.

They were not being punished for testifying. They were being erased for *having something left to say* — the only three people alive who had stood in front of my father, refused his money, and carried out of that room the full inventory of what he'd held over whom. Everyone else had sold their silence twice over: once to him, once to the court. Their testimony was worthless because it was purchased. But three stubborn, unfrightened people had walked away owing him nothing, and for twenty years they had been the loose thread in the garment.

Someone was unpicking the seam.

I sat down again, slowly, on the archivist's stool that has held my weight through ten thousand quiet hours, and I felt the past reorganize itself around me — not crumble, nothing so dramatic, but *turn*, the way a specimen turns under glass, showing the side that was always there while everyone admired the front.

For twenty years I had believed the story the city told: disgraced benefactor, merciful collapse of the trial, tragic death, grieving children, one drowned son, one daughter vanished into holy work. A story with a moral, tidily bound. And all the while the true ledger had lain forty feet beneath my reading desk, knotted shut, patient as bone, waiting for someone with my hands and my name to come down and read it aloud.

The killer wasn't working from my files.

He was working from *these* — or from copies, or from memory, or from instruction. He was finishing a job my father had begun and the court had interrupted. He was not hiding the truth of 2004.

He was completing it.

* * *

Near dawn — I knew it was near dawn because the cathedral's stones change color before the light does, drinking the dark upward through their grain — I found the last thing, and it was the smallest, and it undid me more than all the blackmail together.

It was tucked inside the folder with the list of names: a child's exercise book, cheap paper, cover gone soft as cloth from handling. Schoolwork, at first glance. Sums. A copied psalm in a round, careful hand, each letter formed with the anxious precision of someone who has been corrected often. And then, halfway through, pages of practice — the alphabet over and over, then phrases, then whole paragraphs, and the handwriting changing as the pages turned, shedding its roundness, straightening, learning its ascenders, until on the final page it was—

Mine.

Not similar. Mine. My slant, my cramped g, my habit of crossing the t with a single impatient stroke that dips below the line. Paragraphs in my hand that I had never written, dated years ago, practicing my name — my *current* name, the name I chose at nineteen and told no living soul — over and over, *Noor Vane, Noor Vane, Noor Vane*, as though rehearsing it. As though wearing it in.

The book slipped from my fingers and lay open on the table, breathing its small terrible secret up at me in the green light.

No one knew that name. I had built it myself, brick by brick, out of nothing, in a registrar's office in a town where nobody knew my face. It was the one possession I had ever believed was truly mine — the self I made instead of the self I fled.

And someone had practiced it. Years ago. Had learned it the way I had learned my father's hand as a child, under correction, under threat, letter by obedient letter.

The forger's hand was not imitating me.

It had been *raised* on me.

Marginalia jumped down onto the table and put one paw flat on the exercise book, deliberately, the way she steps on whatever page I am trying to read, and I looked at her and heard myself speak aloud in the empty bay, my voice strange and rusted with disuse:

"There was someone else down there."

The words fell into the dark and did not echo. Stone absorbs. That is the first lesson of any crypt.

I stood in the sealed room with my father's crimes spread around me like a tide line — the letters, the list, the photograph of the smiling men, the child's book learning to be me — and I understood, finally, with the clarity that comes only at the end of a night that has dismantled your life, why Mr. Sallow had chosen *me*. Not for my access. Not for my discretion. Any competent researcher could pull these files. He had chosen me because the forger wrote in my hand, and only I could read the difference between imitation and inheritance — only I would recognize, in the killer's script, the handwriting of someone who had learned it from the same teacher I had.

From the same papers. In the same dark. Under the same roof.

Four days. The entry gave me four days, and tomorrow — today — Detective Brandt would come back through my door with her tired eyes and her questions, and I would have to decide which lies to stop telling, and to whom, and how much of my father's ledger to drag up into the light before it dragged me down instead.

I closed the exercise book. I tied the boxes back with new string, neat knots, an archivist's courtesy to whoever came next. At the door I paused with my hand on the wax-sealed wood and looked back at the bay — at the fifty boxes sitting in the dark like a congregation asleep, keeping my father's voice between them.

"Who taught you?" I asked the dark, softly, in the voice I use for fragile books.

The stone absorbed it. But somewhere above me, faint through the floor, the cathedral bell counted five, and beneath the bell, thinner, stranger, I could have sworn I heard footsteps crossing the nave — unhurried, familiar with the route, walking the way a person walks in a house they grew up in.

I blew out the lamp and went to find out.
