## 17. The Date Arrives

### Noor

The night before the date, Noor did not sleep. She sat in the archive with Marginalia curled on the ledger like a paperweight of fur and bone, and she watched the candle burn down through its hours the way other people watched a patient die.

The entry said Thursday. *E. Tammas — verger — found in the north transept, between the hours of vespers and matins.* The ink was her own hand. Not her handwriting exactly — her *archival* hand, the one she used only for the ledgers, the small severe script she had spent twenty years perfecting until it was less writing than engraving. Whoever had forged it knew that distinction. That was the part that kept her awake. Anyone could copy a signature. Only someone who had studied her for years could copy her *conventions*.

She had moved Tam to the crypt on Monday. He slept on a camp cot between the tombs of two medieval abbots, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of stone and myrrh, and he thanked her every time she brought him food as though she were doing him an enormous kindness rather than hiding him from his own murder. He was nineteen. He hummed when he was nervous. By Wednesday night he had stopped humming.

"Will you stay down there tonight?" he'd asked, catching her wrist at the crypt door. His fingers were cold. "Just — I know it's silly. But the dark down here has a sound to it. Like breathing."

"The dark everywhere has a sound," Noor said, which was not comfort, but he smiled anyway, because apparently anything said in a low voice by candlelight counted as comfort when you were nineteen and frightened.

"I'll be above," she told him. "One floor of stone between us. If anything happens, pull the cord."

There was no cord. There was nothing in the crypt but tombs and darkness and the original key around her neck. But he nodded as if she'd promised him an army, and she went up, and she hated herself a little for how easily he believed her.

At midnight she climbed to the triforium gallery, the narrow stone walkway above the north transept, where the cathedral kept its pigeons and its dust and its view of everything below. From there she could see the whole sweep of the transept floor, pale as a held breath in the emergency lighting. If anyone came for Tam, they would have to cross that floor first. They would have to pass under her.

She brought the ledger. She told herself it was to review the entry again, to search for some detail she'd missed — a pressure of pen, a hesitation in the downstroke of the *T*. But really she brought it because it was the only thing in her life that had never lied to her, and she wanted it near while it betrayed her.

Marginalia followed her up the spiral stair, complaining in a thread of a voice, and settled against her hip on the cold stone.

Below, the transept waited.

Above, through the clerestory, the sky went from black to iron to the color of old pearl. Noor watched Thursday arrive the way she imagined people watch a tide come in over something they love: knowing exactly what is coming, unable to stop it, counting the seconds anyway.

Vespers came and went at four in the afternoon — she had forgotten, absurdly, that the day would be full of ordinary holiness, choristers rehearsing, tourists photographing the saints. Matins was at eleven that night. Between those two hours, according to the forged entry, Tam was supposed to die.

At eleven o'clock the bells rang for the office, and no one in the building below her stirred except the verger on duty, who was not Tam, who yawned and shuffled toward the choir stalls with his keys jangling.

Midnight. One o'clock. Two.

At half past two, Marginalia stood, stretched, and looked at her with the flat golden patience cats reserve for humans who have failed to notice something obvious.

Noor looked down at the ledger open across her knees.

And saw that she had been reading the wrong thing all night.

### Brandt

Ilsa Brandt spent the predicted date parked across the close from Saint Aldhelm's in an unmarked car with a thermos of coffee gone bitter and a view of three doors, and she spent it thinking about how much she hated churches.

It wasn't the theology. Theology was fine; theology never asked her to work weekends. It was the architecture. Churches were buildings designed to make you feel small, and Brandt had spent thirty years in a job that made her feel small professionally and had developed a firm policy against volunteering for more of it. Also they were acoustically impossible for surveillance, full of echoing stone and hidden passages, and every single person who worked in one moved like they had somewhere sacred to be and resented being observed on the way.

At nine p.m., Detective Constable Ferro slid into the passenger seat with two bags of chips and the expression of a man about to deliver news he'd rehearsed.

"Say it fast," Brandt said.

"We ran the Sallow footage again. All of it." Ferro opened his chips. "The man enters the cathedral on the fourth of March at 10:14 a.m. We've got him on the west door camera, clear as anything. Grey coat, hat, gloves. And then—" He paused for effect, which she allowed because he'd earned it. "Nothing. Not the west door, not the cloister door, not the staff entrance, not the fire exits. Fourteen cameras. The building's like a sieve and we've got every hole covered since the second murder, and he walks in and just—"

"Dissolves."

"Becomes incorporeal, yeah. Either that or someone's been wiping footage, and the wipe jobs are clean. Professional clean."

Brandt chewed a chip and watched the cathedral's west front loom against the streetlights, all carved saints and soot, a wall of watching faces. Somewhere inside that building, a nineteen-year-old boy was sleeping among tombs because a woman who catalogued dead people had decided he was worth more alive than filed.

"And the archivist?" she asked.

"Vane. Sorry — Noor. Nothing. Woman's a ghost. Buys her groceries at the same shop same day every week, pays cash, talks to nobody except the cat. Our tech says she hasn't made a call from her landline in six years except to the fire brigade, once, about a blocked flue." Ferro crumpled the empty bag. "You think she's involved?"

"I think," Brandt said slowly, "that everyone in this case is either hiding or hunting, and I haven't worked out which one she is. Possibly both. Possibly the same thing."

The night wore on. At 11:00 the bells rang matins and Ferro dozed and woke and dozed again. At midnight a fox crossed the close with the insolent confidence of a creature that pays no rates. At 1:40 a.m. Brandt's radio crackled: the plainclothes pair covering the north transept door reported all quiet, the verger on duty was the regular one, no sign of any Tam.

At 3:00 a.m., Brandt made the decision she'd been circling all night.

"We're going in," she said. "Not a raid. A welfare check. If that kid's alive tomorrow morning, the prediction's wrong, and I want to know it's wrong *from inside*, not from a doorstep."

Ferro looked at her. "You think it'll be wrong?"

"I think," Brandt said, starting the engine, "that a killer this careful doesn't miss. So either the entry was never meant to be kept — or it was meant to be kept and something changed their mind. And both of those options end with me asking questions in a church, which I hate."

### Noor

What she had been reading wrong was the date.

Not the day — the day was right, Thursday, circled in the entry's own grim certainty. But the entry gave times: *between the hours of vespers and matins.* Vespers, in the archive's convention — her convention, her *cataloguing standard*, the system she herself had devised and documented in the front matter of every ledger — did not mean the evening service.

In her system, times of death were recorded against the canonical hours as the old parish registers had used them, and the old registers reckoned the day from sunset. Vespers began the day. Matins ended it. Which meant the window named in the entry was not the twelve hours she had guarded.

It was the next twelve. Friday's night. The night after the one she'd survived.

Noor sat in the triforium gallery with the dawn coming up grey behind the saints and felt the realization move through her like cold water finding a crack. She had spent the wrong night watching. She had put Tam in the crypt and stationed herself above the transept and counted the hours of a deadline that hadn't arrived yet — and whoever wrote that entry knew she would misread it. Knew it the way you know a friend's habits. Because the convention was hers. Because only she read dates that way.

Unless—

Unless the point had never been the killing at all.

She went down the spiral stair too fast, Marginalia abandoned, and crossed to the crypt door with the key already in her hand, and her hands were shaking, which annoyed her, because fear was supposed to be useful and hers was just loud.

Tam was asleep. She stood in the tomb-dark and listened to him breathe — the dark down here really did have a sound to it, a slow stone breathing, and beneath it the smaller human sound of a boy alive — and she let herself stand there for one full minute, doing nothing but listening, before she woke him.

"Get your shoes on," she said quietly. "We're moving you."

He surfaced from sleep the way young people do, all at once, eyes wide in the candlelight. "Is it — did something happen? Is it today? Is he—"

"It's tonight. I got the hours wrong. The window starts at sundown." She heard her own voice, flat and precise, the archivist's voice, and hated how much it sounded like her father's. "And I'm not keeping you here. Here is where anyone who knows me would look. Here is where I would look."

"Where else is there?"

"That," Noor said, "is the problem. There isn't one. So we're going to do something worse than hide."

### Brandt

The welfare check went sideways in the most church-like way possible: politely, and then not.

They entered through the west door at 3:20 a.m., waking a verger who nearly ascended on the spot, and were escorted by candle and flashlight through corridors that smelled of wax and cold brass to the crypt door — locked, naturally, because everything in this building was locked — where the verger spread his hands and said, with genuine regret, that he couldn't let them in, that the crypt was the archivist's domain, that even the Dean needed her permission, that honestly, officers, she was very particular.

"Then wake her," Brandt said.

"She sleeps in the archive, sometimes, but you can't just—"

"Ferro. Wake her."

But the archive, when they reached it — up stairs and along a gallery hung with plans of the building's bones — was empty. Candle burned down to a pool. Ledger open on the desk. Cat sitting on the open pages like a gargoyle with opinions, regarding Brandt with an expression she chose to interpret as *you're late.*

"Noor Vane?" Brandt said to the cat, and the cat blinked once, slowly, and said nothing, which was more cooperation than Brandt had gotten from anyone else in this building.

They found the crypt open and empty. Cot, blanket folded, a mug washed and inverted to dry on the tomb of Abbot Osric. A boy's trainers gone. In their place, weighted with a pebble, a page torn from a notebook, written in a careful, upright, unpracticed hand:

*Gone with Miss Vane. Safe. Don't look for us. Please don't tell anyone I was ever here. — T.*

"Please don't tell anyone I was ever here," Ferro read aloud. "From a kid hiding in a crypt. That's heartbreaking, that is."

"That's coached," Brandt said. She picked up the note by its edge and looked at it without touching the ink. "Look at the phrasing. *Don't look for us.* Plural. He's telling us she's with him, which we'd have guessed anyway, and *please don't tell anyone I was ever here,* which is not how a scared teenager writes. That's how a woman who has spent twenty years not being written about teaches a teenager to write."

"You think she took him?"

"I think," Brandt said, "that at some point last night our archivist re-read her own catalogue conventions, realized the murder window hadn't closed, and moved the witness somewhere even she couldn't be followed to. And left us a note designed to close the file." She set the page down. "Which means she's smart, and scared, and knows exactly how police think. Which means she's either our best witness or our best suspect, and I am done guessing."

The sun was coming up over the close, gilding the saints on the west front, making the whole obscene beautiful façade glow like something innocent. Brandt stood on the steps and looked at the building that had swallowed a man without spitting out footage, that hid boys in crypts, that kept archivists like specimens in amber.

"Get me the employment rolls," she said. "Everyone on the cathedral payroll, everyone with keys, going back ten years. And Ferro — pull the visitor logs from the archive. If our Mr. Sallow dissolved inside this building, somebody let him in. Doors don't open themselves."

### Noor

The safe house was a lie, and Noor had built it out of truth, which was the only material she trusted.

There was a room above the ossuary passage — a disused charnel chapel, deconsecrated a century ago, reachable by a stair that appeared on none of the modern plans because Noor had removed it from them herself, years ago, in one of the small quiet acts of self-burial that had constituted her life. She had kept it stocked the way other people kept go-bags: water, candles, a blanket, a first-aid tin, forty pounds in coins. She had never expected to use it for anyone but herself.

Tam sat on the step of the little altar with his knees drawn up, watching her light candles with the fixed attention of someone memorizing her face in case he needed to describe it later.

"You knew about this place," he said. "Before. You've known about it for ages."

"I've known about it for longer than you've been alive."

"Why?" Simple question. Nineteen-year-old question. The kind adults forget to ask because they've learned to assume the worst reasons.

Noor considered lying. It came easily, the way breathing came easily, and she was tired of how easily it came.

"Because I planned to disappear," she said. "Years ago. Before you were born, probably. I had a whole route mapped. This room was the first night of it." She set the last candle in its niche. "I never took the route. I stayed. But I kept the room ready, the way you keep a coat for weather that never comes."

"And now you're using it for me instead."

"Now I'm using it for you instead." She sat down on the step beside him, not too close. Below them, through the floor, the ossuary breathed its dry breath — shelves of skulls, orderly as files. Tam didn't know what was under the floor yet. She would tell him eventually, or she wouldn't; there was a mercy in not cataloguing everything at once.

"Miss Vane." He was picking at the blanket seam. "The entry. The one that says — tonight. You're sure you read it right now? You're sure it's—"

"I'm sure I read it the way I always read dates, which means whoever wrote it knew I would misread it first." She watched the candleflame steady. "That's what I keep turning over, Tam. A forger who wants a death to happen writes a clear entry. A forger who wants something *else* writes an entry with a trap in it. And the trap isn't for you."

He was quiet a moment. "It's for you."

"It's for me. The whole commission was for me. Prove the death hasn't happened yet, he said. Find the file, he said. Every step of it walked me deeper into my own archive, my own past, my own—" She stopped. Her father's name sat behind her teeth like a stone she'd swallowed at nine years old and had never fully digested. "Someone wants me looking. Someone wants me *moving*. And the question I can't answer is whether they want me moving toward them or away from something."

Outside, far off, the city was waking — a bus sighing at its stop, a gull arguing with the dawn. Up here in the dead chapel, the light through the bricked-up east window came in red as an ember through the gap where a brick had slipped.

Tam said, very softly: "Whoever it is. Do you think they're watching us right now?"

Noor looked at the slipped brick. At the thin blade of morning light lying across the floor between them like a drawn line.

"Yes," she said. "I think they've been watching me for twenty years. I think last night proved it. And I think—" She stopped, because the thought arriving was worse than the ones before it, and it arrived with total calm, the way true things do. "I think the entry was never a schedule. It was a message. *I know how you read. I know where you'd hide him. I know you better than you know yourself.*"

The ember-light crept another inch across the floor.

"And there's only one person alive who reads me that well," Noor said, "and I buried him myself, in absentia, twenty years ago, in a river."

Tam looked at her. "Then who's writing the entries?"

Down in the ossuary, faint through the floor, something shifted — a small dry sound, like a page turning, like bone settling, like a skull adjusting itself on its shelf to listen better.

Neither of them moved. The candleflame leaned, though there was no draught.

Noor rose without a word, took the candle, and lifted the trapdoor to the ossuary stair.

The room below was exactly as she had left it. Except that on the top shelf, centered with terrible neatness among the anonymous dead, someone had placed a single white flower — fresh, dew still on it — the kind sold in bunches outside the station, the kind that had been replaced every week for twenty years on a riverbank where a boy was supposed to have drowned.

A card was tucked into the stems. On it, in her own archival hand, five words:

*You always did count wrong.*
