## 18. Sallow Unmasked

The flowers were the thing that undid me.

I had walked past the riverbank a hundred times in twenty years and never once stopped, because stopping would have meant looking, and looking would have meant remembering, and I had built a whole life on the principle that some things are better catalogued than contemplated. But the flowers — white chrysanthemums, browning at the edges, replaced so recently that dew still clung to them — were impossible to ignore. They sat at the base of the old ferry piling where the police had dragged the river in October of 1998 and found nothing but a shoe.

One shoe. I had always hated that detail. Drowned boys, in my experience of ledgers, surrendered both.

I crouched beside them, careful not to touch, the way I would crouch beside a fragile manuscript, and I did what I do best: I read. The stems were cut at an angle, cleanly, by someone with a steady hand. The wrapping was brown paper, unbleached, the kind sold at the florist on Chandler's Row. And tucked into the twine, half-hidden, a slip of card with a single word in pencil:

*Thursday.*

No name. No time. Just the day, written in a hand that made my stomach turn over — because it was almost mine. The same forward slant, the same cramped ascenders, the same little hook on the *y* that I had never seen in anyone else's writing until three weeks ago, when I found it in my own archive, on pages I had never written, dated for deaths that had not yet happened.

The forger's hand. The hand that had predicted eight murders. The hand that had named Tam as entry number nine.

And now it was inviting me to a meeting.

* * *

I told myself I would not go. I said it aloud, in the archive, to Marginalia, who was asleep in the ledger tray and therefore no witness at all. "I will not go." The cat's ear twitched, the only dissent in the room.

But I had already begun, in the way I always begin — with the pencil, with the card, with the paper. I sat down at my table and I interrogated the slip like a document, because documents are the only witnesses that never lie, or at least never lie without leaving a scar you can find. The pencil was soft, 2B, the graphite slightly waxy — the same pencil that had annotated the forged entries. The card stock was old, cut from the back of a ledger board; I could see the ghost of a ruled line where a label had once been pasted and peeled. Ledger board from Saint Aldhelm's. From my own archive, or from somewhere very like it.

And the hand — I spread the slip beside the forged entry for E. Vane, dated next week, the entry that had started all of this, the entry that had brought Mr. Sallow and his fortune in a plain envelope — and I looked at them the way I had looked at nothing else in twenty years of cataloguing the dead.

They were not the same hand.

Everyone else who had seen them — Brandt, in her one grudging consultation — had called them identical. But I had spent a decade teaching myself to read hands the way other people read faces, and I could see it now, the way you can see, once you've seen it, that a painting is a copy: the forger's letters leaned a degree too far, as if the writer was leaning into a wind that wasn't there. The *y*-hook was right. The *y*-hook was perfect. But the *h* was wrong — my *h*, the real one, the one in the ledgers I had kept since I was nineteen, had a small hesitation at the crown, a tiny thickening where the pen paused, a stutter I'd had since childhood and could not train out of myself. The forger's *h* was fluent. Too fluent. Fluent the way a person is fluent in a language learned from books rather than from living.

Someone had learned to write like me from studying me. And someone else — someone writing *Thursday* on a card left with flowers on a riverbank — had learned from studying *them*.

Two hands. One descended from the other. A lineage of forgery, like a family tree.

I put the slip down. My hands were not steady. Marginalia woke, stretched, and regarded me with the flat golden patience of a creature who has watched humans lie to themselves for centuries and expects nothing better.

"Thursday," I said again.

The day after tomorrow.

* * *

I did not go to the riverbank on Thursday. I went on Wednesday night, because if twenty years of reading other people's endings had taught me anything, it is that the person who sets a meeting expects you at the stated hour, and the person who arrives early owns the ground.

The fog came off the river around ten, the way it does in October, rolling up the mudflats in slow grey ropes until the far bank dissolved and the city's lights became smears of amber, like candle flames behind vellum. I wore my oldest coat, the one that makes me invisible — grey on grey, the colour of weather — and I stood in the shadow of the coal shed by the disused ferry steps, and I waited.

I am good at waiting. It is, if I am honest, most of what I am. Twenty years of sitting still among the dead has made stillness my native language. The river talked to itself. A night heron said its one dry word from the piling. Somewhere behind me, a train crossed the viaduct, and the ground hummed like a struck bell.

At eleven minutes past midnight — not Thursday yet, not quite, the day still an hour from existing — I heard footsteps on the gravel path. Unhurried. Even. The footsteps of someone who had also decided that the stated hour was for other people.

I did not turn. I watched him in the fog's grey mirror, the way he would see me: a shape by the coal shed, small, still, unremarkable. He stopped perhaps ten feet away. He was tall. He wore a long dark coat, and his hands were pushed into its pockets, and the fog moved between us like a curtain being drawn by a careful stagehand.

"You came early," he said.

The voice went through me like cold water through cloth.

I knew that voice. Not as it was — it had deepened, roughened, acquired the gravel of years and of cigarettes or of shouting, I couldn't tell which — but underneath it, in its rhythm, in the particular way it let the last word of a sentence fall away rather than finish it, I knew it the way you know a hymn heard once in childhood and never since. You don't remember the words. Your mouth remembers the tune.

"Turn around," I said. My voice came out flat. Archival. I was proud of that, later. At the time I was not aware of having any voice at all.

He turned. The fog thinned for a moment, a boat's light out on the water catching his face, and I saw him.

He was forty-one years old. He was nineteen the last time I saw him, the last time anyone saw him, a boy in a soaked coat running down the ferry steps with the river roaring behind him. The boy had been all edges — sharp chin, sharp elbows, sharp grin. The man had softened and settled, like a house that has been lived in. There was grey in his dark hair at the temples. There was a scar through one eyebrow that I did not know. His eyes were my mother's eyes, which were my eyes, which was a thing I had spent twenty years not thinking about.

"Elias," I said.

And the world, which had held its shape for twenty years through sheer archival discipline, tilted.

* * *

He did not come closer. That was the first thing I noticed, the detail my mind seized on because it could not bear the larger ones: he stayed where he was, ten feet of fog between us, hands in his pockets, like a man at a viewing distance from something that might go off.

"Noor," he said. My old name. My true name. The name that existed nowhere in the cathedral's records, nowhere in the city's ledgers, nowhere but in the mouths of two dead people and one man who had been dead for twenty years. Hearing it was like hearing a floorboard creak in a house you had convinced yourself was empty.

"You're dead," I said. "You drowned. There's a file. I've read the file a hundred times. I *catalogued* the file." And even as I said it I heard how it sounded — how the whole architecture of my life, the ledgers and the locks and the candlelight, was suddenly revealed to be what it had always been: a shrine built over an absence, tended by a woman who had chosen the company of the dead because the dead could not walk out of the fog and contradict her.

"One shoe," he said.

The words went through me. He knew. He knew the detail I had hated, the detail that had itched at the back of my skull for two decades like a splinter under a nail.

"I left one shoe on the piling," he said. "I was nineteen and it was the best I could do. I've had twenty years to think of better." He took a hand out of his pocket, slowly, and I flinched — I flinched, at my own brother, and I saw him see it, and something in his face closed like a door. He turned his hand over. On the third finger sat a signet ring, heavy, old gold, worn smooth at the shoulders by a century of other fingers.

Our father's ring. The Vane signet. The one the estate records listed as destroyed, the one Brandt had traced from a terrified boy's pencil sketch, the one that had made her captain order her off the case — the ring that sat at the centre of the whole dark knot like a stone at the centre of a fruit.

"You're Sallow," I said.

"I'm Sallow." A ghost of a smile, there and gone. "It was the name on the first false passport I ever bought. I kept it. It seemed fitting — a man made of shallow things." He said it lightly, but his eyes did not lighten. "You want to hit me. Or run. I'd advise against the riverbank for both."

"Why?" It came out of me with twenty years of pressure behind it, and it was not the question I had rehearsed on the walk down, the questions about the forger and the murders and the entries dated for deaths that hadn't happened yet. It was the question underneath all of them, the one I had buried so deep I hadn't known it was a question until it came out of my mouth in the fog. "Why did you let me think — for twenty *years* —"

"Because you saw."

The river said something. The heron said nothing.

"That night," Elias said, and his voice had changed, the lightness gone, the gravel showing through. "In the crypt. You were asleep on the steps — you used to fall asleep down there, do you remember, among the tombs, because it was the only place Father's voice couldn't reach. And you woke up. And you saw."

"I saw a shape," I whispered. "I saw a shape and I heard the sound and I was a child and I told myself it was a dream, and then Elias was gone, and the shape was gone, and there was a shoe—"

"And if you had ever been sure," he said, "really sure, sure enough to say it out loud — you'd have been the witness who destroyed whatever was left of this family. Or it would have destroyed you. So I did the only thing I could think of. I made sure there was no witness. I made sure there was no body either. I drowned Elias Vane in the river and sent a girl named Noor into a church, and I told myself it was to protect her." He looked down at the ring. "I've had twenty years to decide whether I believe that."

* * *

I should have screamed at him. I should have wept, or struck him, or turned and walked into the fog and back up the hill to my archive and locked the door on the whole century. Later, in the hospital, in the courtroom, in the long accounting of what we did to one another, there would be time for all of it. But standing there, in the cold, with the river breathing beside us, what I did was what I always do.

I asked about the documents.

"The forged entries," I said. "The predictions. Eight people dead, exactly as written, exactly on the dates. Tam — the boy — named for Thursday. You wrote to me as Sallow. You paid me to prove a death hadn't happened yet. Why? Why me? Why now? Why the theatre?"

"Because the forgeries are in your hand," Elias said, "and only you would see what I saw — that they're not quite your hand. That they're a *copy* of your hand. And that means whoever is doing this learned to write like you without ever meeting you. From samples. From the ledgers. From twenty years of your catalogues." He paused. "And because the first forged entry I ever received was dated the day after my own death. E. Vane. Next week. Someone is going to kill Elias Vane, Noor. Someone has scheduled it, in your handwriting, in your archive, the way our father scheduled everything — and I have spent three months trying to find out who, and every road leads back to that cathedral, and every road leads back to you."

"The witnesses," I said. The cold was no longer in the air; it was in me. "The dead men. They were all—"

"Witnesses. From the trial. From the trial that was supposed to bury Father's name and didn't, quite, because trials never bury anything, they just file it." He took a step closer, and I did not flinch this time, and I think he noticed. "Someone is finishing the job, Noor. Someone is executing the witness list. And when they reach the end of it, there are only three names left. A detective who won't stop. A brother who's been dead for twenty years." His eyes held mine, and in the fog-light they were not my mother's eyes at all; they were older, and wearier, and afraid. "And the archivist. The sole witness. The one who saw everything and said nothing, and whose silence has kept the whole crypt sealed for two decades."

The fog closed. The boat light passed. For a moment he was only a voice and a shape, and I understood that this was how he had lived — a voice and a shape, a man who wasn't there — and that he had chosen it, and that some part of him had chosen it *for* me, and that I would never entirely forgive him, and that I had already, in some cellar of myself, begun to.

"The flowers," I said. My voice was steadier than I had any right to. "You've been leaving them. Every week. For twenty years."

He was quiet for a long moment. When he spoke, the gravel was gone, and what was underneath it was nineteen years old.

"Someone had to," he said. "You weren't coming."

* * *

We stood there while the fog moved and the river counted its slow money, and everything that needed to be said was said, and everything that could not be said yet sat between us like a third person, waiting its turn. He told me he had a room across the river. He told me he had watched the cathedral from the far bank more times than he could count, and had never once come up the hill, because he had decided long ago that his sister was safer as a memory than as a witness. He told me the forgeries kept coming — slipped under doors, left in dead drops, always in my hand, always dated — and that the latest one had named a date four days out, and a place.

"Where?" I asked.

He looked at me, and for the first time since the fog had given him back to me, my brother — the boy with the sharp grin, the man with the scarred eyebrow, the ghost in the long coat wearing our father's ring — looked afraid.

"The crypt," he said. "Entry number ten. The crypt. Dated for the night after next." He turned his hand over, and the signet caught the far light, and I saw that the seal on its face was not whole — that it had been scored through, once, deliberately, by someone who had wanted the name on it broken. "And Noor — I didn't write it. I swear to you, on whatever's left of our family, I didn't write it. But it's in your hand. It's in your hand, and it's dated, and it's *waiting* — and I think whoever wrote it knows I'm alive."

He said it, and the river kept moving, and the fog kept moving, and somewhere up the hill behind me the cathedral stood dark against the sky, holding its locked doors and its locked crypt and its twenty years of my silence, and I understood, standing on the mudflats where my brother had died and refused to stay dead, that whatever came through that crypt door on the night after next, it would not be a murder.

It would be a homecoming.

And someone had already written the date.
