## 6. What the Harbor Remembers

The Anchor & Chain smelled of salt, spilled stout, and forty years of the same wood polish, and Nell Pryce had long ago decided that was the correct smell for a harbor pub—honest, unglamorous, the smell of a place that knew what it was. She pulled the tap on the Harbourside Bitter with one hand and kept her eyes on the door with the other, because it was nearly six, and at nearly six on a Thursday, Ines Coldharbour came in.

She did, too. Right on time, the way she was right on time for everything, slipping through the door like she was trying not to disturb the air around it. Grey coat—the grey coat, the one that had been all over the police tape this week—and that scarf wound twice around her neck even though the pub's heat hit you like a wall the moment you crossed the threshold.

"The usual?" Nell called.

"Please." Ines took her corner stool at the end of the bar, the one under the mounted life ring nobody had ever needed, and unwound the scarf with careful fingers, folding it over her knee like it might be evidence someday.

Nell drew the cider slow, watching her through the taps. Everyone in Ashcombe was talking about the body below the sea wall. Everyone except Ines, who talked about nothing at all, which from Ines meant everything.

*Two men aboard,* said the voice in the back of her head, the one she'd been drowning in beer and routine for twelve years. *You saw it. You've always seen it.*

She set the glass down harder than she meant to. Foam slopped over the rim.

"You all right?" Ines asked. Of course she noticed. The woman noticed the grain direction of the bar top if you let her.

"Tap's playing up," Nell said, and wiped the spill with a rag that had wiped up worse.

* * *

That night, after last call, after Marr's lot had come and gone again—they'd been in three times this week, buying pints they barely touched, asking questions shaped like small talk—Nell locked the front door and stood in the empty pub and let herself remember.

She was good at not remembering. Twelve years of practice. You'd be amazed what a person can not-remember when remembering would cost something. She'd built a whole life out of it: the pub, the regulars, the rota on the corkboard, the crossword she never finished because she always got stuck on the same kind of clue—the ones where the answer is a thing you're trying not to say.

But the memory didn't live in her head. That was the trouble. It lived in her hands, in the specific weight of pulling a pint at eleven-forty on a Tuesday night in October 2012, when Thomas Reeve had come in off the *Kestrel* smelling of diesel and bad decisions, and drunk two whiskies standing up, and said, "Nell, love, if I'm not back by Friday, feed my cat."

And she'd laughed. God help her, she'd laughed, and said, "Where are you going that needs a Friday deadline?"

He'd looked at her then—a look she could still see, clear as the brass rail in front of her, because some things don't fade no matter how hard you scrub—and he'd said, "Just the water," and gone out into the rain.

She'd closed up at midnight. And because the keg delivery hadn't come and she'd been in a mood about it, she'd gone round the back to check whether the drayman had left it at the slip instead, and from the shadow of the fish store she'd seen the *Kestrel* moving out of the harbor, running dark, no navigation lights, which was wrong, which was already wrong before she counted heads.

Two men aboard.

One at the tiller. One forward, handling lines.

Thomas Reeve went out alone. Everybody knew that. The inquest said so: single-handed sailor, foul weather, swept overboard, body never recovered. Open verdict, drowned presumed. The whole town nodded along like sheep in a field, because the whole town wanted the story tidy, and a man alone in the sea is a tidy story.

But Nell had stood behind the fish store with rain running down her neck and counted two.

She'd told herself a hundred explanations since. Her eyes playing tricks in the dark. A fender on the foredeck that looked like a man. Reeve himself plus his reflection in the black water. She'd gotten so good at the explanations that some nights she almost believed them, and then she'd wake at three a.m. with the image behind her eyelids, sharp as the night it happened, and know she was lying to herself the way the town had been lied to—gently, repeatedly, until the lie wore grooves deep enough to look like truth.

She'd never told anyone. Not the police who canvassed the harbor the next week. Not the coroner's officer who bought a brandy and asked, so softly, if she'd seen anything unusual that October. Not her late husband, God rest him, because telling him would have made it real, and real things require action, and action required courage she'd discovered she didn't have.

Twelve years. She'd fed no cat—Reeve's cat had vanished within the month, rehomed or worse—but she'd fed the silence every single day, and it had grown fat and comfortable and lived in the pub with her like another regular.

Until a drowned man washed up wearing a dead man's face, wearing a living girl's coat, and the silence started choking on its own fat.

* * *

Thursday nights were quiet, which was why Ines came then. Nell understood that without ever discussing it. Ines didn't dislike people; she catalogued them, and cataloguing was exhausting, and Thursday was the one night the pub held only a handful of souls too old or too loyal to be anywhere else. Old Tam in his usual corner doing the Telegraph. The Wetherby sisters splitting a bottle of cheap prosecco and finishing each other's sentences. Room to breathe. Room to be still.

Tonight Ines wasn't still. Tonight she turned her glass a quarter-turn every few minutes, precise as clockwork, and her eyes kept going to the window where the harbor lay black beyond the glass, and Nell thought: *she knows something.* Or she suspects. Or she's frightened. With Ines it was hard to tell fear from concentration, both of them wearing the same flat, watchful expression, like a lighthouse doing sums.

"Penny for them," Nell said, wiping down the bar though it didn't need it.

"They're not worth a penny." But Ines smiled, a small dry thing. "I keep thinking about filing systems."

"Oh aye?"

"How a record is only as good as whoever keeps it." The quarter-turn of the glass. "If someone controls the files, they control what's true. Everything else is just... testimony."

Nell's hands went still on the cloth.

"That sounds like the sort of thing that keeps a person up at night," she managed.

"I sleep fine," Ines said, which was a lie, and both of them knew it, and neither of them said so, and that was the shape of their friendship mostly—two women agreeing not to say things, each assuming the other's unsaid things were smaller than her own.

Tam called for a refill. Nell went and pulled it, grateful for the interruption, and when she came back Ines was looking at the wall behind the bar—at the photographs, actually, the ones Nell's father had hung and Nell had never taken down. Regatta day, 1998. The lifeboat crew, 2004. And there, third row, slightly crooked: the harbor committee, 2011, a dozen men in blazers squinting into the sun, and among them a lean dark-haired man with a grin like a folded knife.

Thomas Reeve. Alive, sunlit, twelve years before the sea gave him back.

Ines was staring at it with that terrible total attention of hers, the look that made people feel photographed.

"He used to drink here," Nell heard herself say. "Before your time."

"Reeve?" Ines said. "Thomas Reeve?"

The name landed in Nell's chest like a dropped glass. "You know it?"

"It's in the files." Ines turned back to her cider. "Everyone in this town is in the files somewhere."

*Everyone in this town is in the files somewhere.* Said lightly, but Nell felt the weight of it settle over the bar like weather. Because that was the thing about Ines Coldharbour—she read things once and kept them forever, whole pages, whole ledgers, whole years, filed away in that uncanny head of hers. Nell had watched her recite insurance policy numbers from memory, dates of loss, claim references, the middle names of claimants' mothers. The girl was an archive with a pulse.

Which meant—if Nell ever found the courage to open her mouth about that October night—it wouldn't matter that she'd waited twelve years. Ines would hold whatever she said, exactly, forever. There'd be no taking it back. No pretending afterward that she'd misremembered, that the light had been bad, that it was a fender on the foredeck.

That was the terrifying arithmetic of telling Ines Coldharbour anything: it became permanent.

Nell topped up Tam's glass he hadn't asked about, and said nothing, and hated herself efficiently, professionally, the way she'd been doing for twelve years.

* * *

It was near closing when the Wetherby sisters left, arguing amiably about whether the new sea defenses were ugly or merely necessary, and Tam folded his paper and shuffled out into the cold, and then it was just the two of them and the hum of the beer cooler and the tick of the clock over the optics that had run four minutes fast since 2009 because Nell liked it that way—liked knowing the pub kept its own time, slightly wrong, stubbornly its own.

Ines stood to go, winding the scarf.

"Nell." She paused, hand on the scarf's end. "Can I ask you something? Off the books, as it were."

"Off the books," Nell repeated. "From you. That'll be a first."

"When Thomas Reeve drowned—" Ines's voice was level, careful, each word placed like a stone in a stream. "October 2012. Did you know him well?"

The clock ticked. Four minutes fast. The cooler hummed. Somewhere out in the black harbor, water moved against pilings, patient as a debt collector.

"Why do you ask?"

"Because his file says he drank at the Shipwrights Arms, up the coast. Exclusively, according to the statement his sister gave the coroner. But I have a photograph in front of me of him in this room, and photographs don't usually lie about rooms." A beat. "Files lie. Photographs rarely get the chance."

Nell set down the cloth. Picked it up. Set it down again.

"He drank here," she said. "Regular as tide tables. Tuesdays and Fridays, whisky, no ice. Sat right there." She pointed at the stool, third from the end, and heard her own voice go strange and far away, like it was coming up from underwater. "His boat was moored on the north wall. The *Kestrel*. He kept her like other men keep wives."

"And the night he drowned? Was he in?"

Here it was. The question she'd rehearsed answers to for twelve years, arriving on an ordinary Thursday from a girl in a grey coat, and every rehearsed answer evaporated at once.

"No," Nell said. "He came in earlier. Early evening. Had two whiskies standing up and left before eight." Her heart was going like the engine of the *Kestrel*, full throttle, running dark. "Said if he wasn't back by Friday, I should feed his cat."

"That's odd," Ines said quietly. "The coroner's timeline has him leaving the Shipwrights at half ten."

"Well, the coroner's timeline is wrong."

The words were out before Nell could weigh them, and once out, they couldn't be stuffed back, and she stood there in her own pub feeling the floor tilt beneath her like a deck in weather.

Ines had gone very still. Stillness, on Ines, was louder than shouting. "Wrong how?"

"Just wrong." Nell turned away, busied herself with glasses that were already clean. "It was twelve years ago, love. Nobody's memory of that year is worth much."

"Mine would be," Ines said, "if I'd been here. But I only arrived after. That's the strange part, isn't it? I came to Ashcombe the spring after he drowned, and everything I know about this town, I know from paper. Paper that somebody keeps." She picked up her cider, didn't drink it. "You were here, though. Before. During."

*Nobody's memory of that year is worth much.* Even as she said it, Nell knew it was the coward's line, and worse—it wasn't even true. Her memory of that year was perfect. That was the curse of it. Other people got to blur and soften and forget, and Nell Pryce remembered the rain ticking on the fish-store roof and the count of two, always two, two men aboard, one at the tiller and one forward handling lines, and the way the second man moved—quick, sure, familiar with the boat, not a stranger, not a passenger, someone who knew the *Kestrel*'s ropes the way she knew her own taps—

And she remembered, too, the morning after, when Edmund Voss had come into the pub before opening hours, which he never did, and sat where Ines was sitting now, more or less, and ordered tea instead of whisky, and said in his mild, reasonable, archival voice: *Sad business, Thomas Reeve. These things are best recorded simply. A man, a storm, the sea. Anything else we might add would only be speculation, and speculation doesn't belong in the town's history, does it, Mrs. Pryce?*

She'd said no. It didn't. And she'd carried the speculation alone ever since, sealed like a document in a basement, and Edmund Voss had raised an orphaned girl and taught that girl that records were safer than people, and Nell had sometimes wondered, in the small hours, what else the archivist had taught her—or kept from her.

"Nell?" Ines said. "You've gone white."

"Tired," Nell said. "Long week. Bodies washing up does that to a harbor."

"Yes," Ines said. "It does."

They looked at each other across the bar, across twelve years of unsaid things, and Nell saw—in the way Ines's eyes flicked, quick as a card sharp, to the photograph on the wall, to the third stool, to Nell's own hands gripping the cloth—that the girl had already filed tonight. Every word. Every hesitation. Filed and cross-referenced and kept.

And Nell understood suddenly that the silence she'd fed for twelve years was about to be taken off her, one way or another. Either she'd find the courage to speak it, or it would be dragged out of her by people with badges, or it would rot her from the inside like water in a hull. There was no fourth option anymore. The sea had returned what it took, and the sea did not return things to leave them buried.

"One more thing," Ines said at the door, scarf already on, grey coat buttoned to the throat. "The night Reeve's boat went out. The twelfth of October. Were you open late?"

The clock ticked, four minutes fast, keeping its own stubborn time.

"No," Nell said. "I closed at midnight."

A pause, thin as wire.

"But you were at the harbor," Ines said—not a question, never a question, Ines didn't need question marks, she just laid facts out like cards and waited to see which ones you picked up. "Midnight. The twelfth of October, 2012. I checked the keg delivery records for the Anchor & Chain this afternoon. There's no delivery logged for that week at all. But your statement to the coroner's officer—you gave one, it's in the file, one line, 'nothing unusual'—you signed it at 9:40 the next morning. Which means between closing and signing, you had a whole night in which something could have been unusual." She tilted her head, gentle as a bird. "I'm not the police, Nell. I'm just a woman who reads files. And I think you saw something."

The pub was very quiet. Outside, the harbor breathed against the sea wall, in and out, in and out, like something sleeping that was very close to waking.

"I closed at midnight," Nell said again, uselessly, and heard it for what it was: the last plank of a deck going under.

Ines nodded slowly, without disappointment, without triumph—just acknowledgment, the way you'd log a fact. "Whenever you're ready," she said. "I keep things. That's the one thing I can promise anybody." And she let herself out into the cold, and the door swung shut behind her, and Nell Pryce stood alone in the pub her father built, surrounded by forty years of honest smells and dishonest silence, and reached under the bar for the bottle she kept for emergencies.

This, she supposed, qualified.

Because tomorrow—she'd heard it from the postman, who heard it from the coroner's clerk—someone was reopening the old files. The 2012 inquest papers had been requested, formally, by name. Requested from the town archive, where every page of Ashcombe's history lived under the care of Edmund Voss, who had told her once, kindly, over a cup of tea, that speculation didn't belong in the record.

Nell poured two fingers, drank them neat, and stared at the photograph on the wall—Reeve grinning in the sun, two men short of the truth.

Whatever Ines Coldharbour found in those files, Nell thought, it wouldn't be everything. Because she herself was the only witness left who'd counted heads on the water that night, and the count was two, and it had always been two, and tomorrow the past was going to start demanding its accounts.

She owed it twelve years of interest.
