## 10. Night Shift

The headset fit the same way it always had, a weight behind the left ear like a hand resting there. Oscar had been back three nights now. The first night they'd said nothing to anyone, just signed in at 22:00 and sat down in the chair with the torn vinyl seat that had been torn for as long as Oscar had worked there, maybe longer, maybe since before the drowning, before the letter, before Ashcombe became a word that meant something other than home.

The console glowed its familiar colors. Channel one, county sheriff. Channel two, fire and rescue. Channel three, the quiet line where the town's small catastrophes came to die: cats in trees, disputes over driveways, old men who'd fallen and were too proud to say how badly.

Nobody had asked Oscar to come back. That was the thing nobody told you about doing what Oscar had done — the memorial, the recorder, the name spoken aloud into thirty years of held breath. Nobody fired you for it. They just stopped talking when you walked into the diner. They just let your shifts sit unfilled until you asked for them yourself.

Deputy Marisol Quintero had driven them in the first night, unasked, her cruiser idling outside the dispatch center until she saw the interior light come on.

"You don't have to do this alone," she'd said through the window, rain beading on her hat brim.

"Nobody's going to call me," Oscar said. "That's kind of the point."

She'd almost smiled. "Somebody will. Somebody always does."

* * *

The trials were set for March. Gerald Ashcombe's lawyers had already filed eleven motions, and the county prosecutor said there would be more; Gerald had money the way his family had always had money, deep and patient and willing to wait. His father was dead and beyond the reach of any court, but the cover-up — the letters bought, the alibis rehearsed, the silence maintained across three decades like a load-bearing wall — that part still had living architects, and some of them were testifying in exchange for immunity, their voices flat on the recorded depositions, saying *we agreed* and *we never spoke of it again* as if describing a zoning dispute.

Oscar testified twice. The second time, Gerald's attorney had leaned across the table and asked, gently, whether it wasn't true that Oscar's mother had accepted payment, had taken money to stay silent, had been, in the attorney's word, *complicit herself.*

"Yes," Oscar had said. "She was."

The courtroom had gone very still.

"She was nineteen years old, she was terrified, and she spent the rest of her life paying for it in ways none of you ever saw." Oscar had looked at Gerald then, really looked at him, the good gray suit and the good gray face. "The difference between my mother and your client is that she never made anyone else carry it."

Gerald hadn't flinched. He'd learned not to, over thirty years of memorial services and scholarship funds and ribbon-cuttings. But something behind his eyes had moved, some small animal turning over in its den, and Oscar had thought: *he knows I saw it.*

* * *

The town was fractured. That was the phrase the regional paper used — ASHCOMBE FRACTURED BY REOPENED CASE — as if the town had been whole before, as if fracture weren't just another word for finally telling the truth.

Some people thanked Oscar. A woman from the Methodist church pressed an envelope of cash into their hand outside the courthouse and wouldn't take it back. Daniel Ashcombe's surviving sister, eighty-one and sharp as broken glass, had called the dispatch center once, asked for Oscar by name, and said only: "Danny liked the water. He wasn't afraid of anything. Thank you for being afraid for him." Then she hung up.

Most people didn't thank anyone. Most people went on buying groceries and arguing about the school levy and pretending the memorial hadn't happened, though the memorial was all anybody thought about. Ruth Calloway's grave got fresh flowers every week from someone who never signed the card. The old Ashcombe mill, slated for demolition, now had a historical marker application pending, and the application mentioned the drowning in language so careful it was almost another kind of lie.

Oscar's mother's house sold in November, to a young couple from out of state who didn't know what had happened there and probably never would. Oscar kept only the locked box, and inside it the unsent letters, and inside those, folded into the last one, a photograph Oscar hadn't known existed: their mother at nineteen, standing beside a girl Oscar recognized as young Ruth Calloway, both of them squinting into a summer sun that fell on a world where Daniel Ashcombe was still alive.

On the back, in pencil, in their mother's handwriting: *R.C. & me, the summer everything was fine.*

Oscar kept the photograph in the breast pocket of their uniform shirt, over the heart, where the headset cord crossed. Some nights they touched it through the fabric without deciding to.

* * *

At 03:12 on the fourth night back, channel three lit up.

"Dispatch."

Silence first. Then breathing, quick and shallow, and beneath it a sound Oscar knew from a thousand calls: someone trying to decide whether speaking would make things worse.

"This is dispatch, I've got you. What's your emergency?"

"I—" A woman's voice, young. "I don't— is this—"

"You've reached county dispatch. You're safe to talk. Take your time."

"He's outside." The words came out in a rush, tumbling. "My—I got out, I took the car, but he followed, he's been following, I'm on Route 9 and I can't—my hands are shaking, I can't—"

"Okay. Listen to me. You're doing fine. What's your location on Route 9? Nearest mile marker or crossroad?"

"I don't—I passed the church, the old white church—"

"The Ashcombe Methodist, that's mile marker fourteen. Good. That's good. Are you moving or pulled over?"

"Pulled over. I couldn't—he was right behind me with his high beams and I couldn't see and I pulled over and he slowed down and now he's—" Her voice cracked. "He's parked up the road. Just sitting there."

Oscar's hands were steady. That surprised them, a little. Thirty years of listening to other people's emergencies, and here was the truth of it: the steadiness wasn't the absence of fear. It was fear with somewhere to go.

"What's your name?"

"Hannah."

"Hannah, I'm sending help right now. Stay on the line with me. Can you lock your doors and keep your engine running?"

Click of locks. "Done."

"Good. Units are en route, about six minutes out. Six minutes, Hannah. That's nothing. We'll talk until they get there. Tell me — is this someone you know?"

A pause that contained a whole history. "My husband."

"Okay, Hannah. You did the right thing calling. That's the hardest call there is, and you made it."

"How do you know?" she whispered. "How do you know it's the right thing? Everyone says I'm overreacting. Everyone says he'd never actually—"

"Because I know what happens when people stay quiet," Oscar said. "I know exactly what it costs, and I know it compounds interest for decades. You're not overreacting. You're the only one reacting."

On the line, Hannah breathed. Up Route 9, the headlights stayed where they were, and Oscar watched the CAD screen, watched the unit markers crawl toward mile fourteen, six minutes becoming five.

"Are you still there?" Hannah asked, small.

"Right here. Not going anywhere."

"Can I ask you something? You sound like—you sound like you understand. Like something happened to you too."

Oscar looked at the photograph's outline against their chest. Outside the dispatch center windows, the dark lay over the fields the way it always had, indifferent and enormous, and somewhere out in it was a town full of people who would never forgive Oscar for making them look at what they'd buried, and somewhere beyond the town was a grave with fresh flowers and no signature, and somewhere under all of it was the truth, which turned out to be the only thing that held.

"Yeah," Oscar said. "Something happened. And somebody stayed on the line for me, in their way. So I'm staying on the line for you."

Five minutes. Four.

"They're moving," Hannah said suddenly, voice spiking. "The car, it's—it's coming toward me—"

"Stay calm. Deputy Quintero is two minutes out, Hannah. Two minutes. She knows exactly where you are."

"Is she—is she good? Is she one of the good ones?"

Oscar watched the unit marker cross onto Route 9, lights running, and felt something in their chest that wasn't quite peace and wasn't quite hope but lived in the same neighborhood.

"She's the best there is," Oscar said. "Now keep talking to me. Tell me anything. Tell me what the sky looks like."

"It's clearing," Hannah said. "After the rain. There's stars coming out."

"Good," Oscar said, and stayed on the line, and didn't let go.
