## 4. The Accused

The Harrowgate County Detention Facility smelled of industrial lemon and old despair — a smell Priya Anand had come to know intimately in her three years of practice, though she had never stopped hating it. It clung to the cinderblock walls the way damp clung to a cellar, no amount of bleach or bureaucracy ever quite scouring it out.

She signed the visitor log with a hand that wasn't quite steady. *Priya Anand, counsel for Daniel Ferro.* Her signature looked smaller than usual on the line, as if the pen itself understood the weight of what she was about to do.

Because here was the thing she hadn't told anyone yet, not even Odile Winterbourne, not even herself in the honest hours after midnight: she had taken Danny Ferro's case for free because nobody else would take it at all, and now the empty coffin had turned her pro bono long shot into the most dangerous case in the county. The state was going to want its conviction back. And the people who had put river stones in Edmund Vale's grave — if there were people, if there had been a plan and not just a miracle — were going to want her client back in the box they'd built for him twenty years ago.

"Anand." The guard at the security door barely glanced up. "Ferro's in two. You've got an hour."

*Two* was the visiting room with the window that faced the parking lot, which meant it faced the highway beyond the parking lot, which meant that for one hour Danny Ferro could watch cars going places he hadn't been allowed to go since he was nineteen years old. Priya knew this because she'd asked him about it once, early on, when she'd still been feeling her way toward who he was beneath the file. He'd said, in his flat, careful way: "It's better than facing the wall."

She gathered her folder — the folder, the one that had grown fat enough to need a rubber band around it — and followed the guard down the corridor, past the cells where men watched her pass with the particular hunger of people who never got good news.

* * *

Danny Ferro was already seated when she came in, hands folded on the metal table like a man in a waiting room rather than a man in a cage. That was the thing about Danny that unsettled people who only knew him from the newspaper clippings — *GARDENER CONVICTED IN VALE MURDER*, the *Harrowgate Herald* had run, above a photograph of a sullen teenager with dirt under his fingernails — he didn't perform innocence. He didn't weep or protest or clutch at hope like a drowning man. He simply sat, and waited, and told the truth when asked, in the unadorned voice of someone who had learned long ago that nobody believed him anyway.

He was thirty-nine now. The sullenness had burned off him somewhere in the intervening years, leaving something quieter and harder to read. His hair was going gray at the temples. His forearms still carried the tan lines of a man who worked outdoors, though there wasn't much outdoor left in his life.

"Priya." He almost smiled. Almost. "You look like you slept in your car."

"I slept in my office. There's a difference. Marginally." She dropped into the chair across from him and set the folder down between them like a card player laying out her hand. "Danny, I need you to listen to me very carefully, because things have changed."

"They said on the TV they dug him up." His voice stayed level, but his hands came apart and reassembled, fingers lacing and unlacing — the only tell he had, and she'd learned to watch for it. "They said the coffin was empty."

"The coffin was empty."

"Stones?"

"River stones. And part of a suit that rotted before I was born."

Something moved through his face then — not joy, nothing so simple as joy. It was closer to vertigo, the look of a man who has spent twenty years standing on a floor that everyone told him was solid and has just been told it was never there at all. He looked down at his folded hands.

"Twenty years," he said. "Twenty years I told them. I told the first lawyer — Rourke, the one who quit — I told the second one, the one who drank. I told the judge. I stood up in that courtroom when I was nineteen years old and I said, *I saw him, I saw Edmund Vale alive, three days after he was supposed to be dead,* and the judge looked at me like I was something he'd scraped off his shoe, and the jury took forty minutes." He exhaled slowly through his nose. "Forty minutes. For a man's whole life."

"I know."

"You don't know." Not angry. Just tired, the way only a man serving a life sentence for a murder that never happened can be tired. "You weren't there."

"No," Priya admitted. "I wasn't there. But I'm here now, and I'm telling you that the ground just gave us back the one piece of evidence nobody could argue with. You can't cross-examine an empty coffin, Danny. The prosecution can't explain it away. Either Edmund Vale is alive, or somebody went to extraordinary lengths to make the world believe he was — and either way, the story they told about you falls apart."

"And if they find him?" Danny's eyes came up, gray-green and steady. "If Vale's alive somewhere, drinking rum on a beach — does that free me? Or does it just mean everybody in this town spends the rest of their lives pretending they don't remember what they did to me?"

It was the question she'd been turning over since the exhumation, the one that kept her awake in her office chair with her blazer balled under her head. She'd hoped he wouldn't ask it. She should have known better. Twenty years in a cell gives a man plenty of time to think past the obvious questions to the ones underneath.

"It frees you," she said. "That's all I can promise, and I won't promise more than I can deliver. What happens to the town afterward — that's not our fight."

"Isn't it?" He tilted his head. "You're the one who dragged that retired detective into it. Winterbourne. I heard the guards talking. Everybody in this building talks, you know that? Walls are thin and days are long." A ghost of something that might have been humor crossed his face. "They say she's the one who put half the people in here. They say she doesn't lose."

"She lost once," Priya said quietly. "Badly enough that she quit. But yes. She's working on it."

Danny studied her for a long moment. Outside the window behind him, a pickup truck pulled out of the visitor lot and merged onto the highway, heading east toward the coast, toward anywhere. He didn't turn to watch it. That, too, was new; the first year of her representation, he'd tracked every departing car with his eyes like a dog watching a door.

"Tell me what you need from me," he said.

* * *

What she needed, she told him, was everything. Again. From the beginning, in detail, on the record, because the retrial meant a new prosecutor — Assistant DA Marcus Webb, young, ambitious, and not personally invested in the original verdict, which made him both more dangerous and more reachable than his predecessor — and Webb would build his case from the transcript unless she gave him a reason to doubt it.

So Danny gave her the night again. The third time she'd heard it, and every time it came out the same, in the same flat order, with the same small details that no liar bothers to invent because they serve no purpose.

October fourteenth, twenty years ago. He'd been working late at the Vale estate — the Ashcombes' party had run long, and Mrs. Ashcombe wanted the grounds cleared of bottles and cigarette ends before morning, and Danny was nineteen and needed the overtime because his mother's furnace had died. He'd been hauling a bag of empties down to the compost shed behind the boathouse when he saw the two figures on the dock.

One was Edmund Vale. Danny had known the man's silhouette since childhood — the broad shoulders, the way he stood with his weight on his heels like a man surveying land he owned, which he did. The other figure was shorter, turned away, wearing a dark coat, and Danny had never seen the face. He'd ducked behind the shed because Vale didn't pay gardeners to eavesdrop, and he'd heard maybe thirty seconds of argument — Vale's voice rising, saying something about *"my money built this town and I'll take it with me if I have to"* — and then Vale had walked up the dock alone, gotten into a car Danny couldn't identify in the dark, and driven away.

Three days later, Edmund Vale was dead. Heart failure, according to Dr. Emory Pell's certificate. Buried in the cemetery on Founders' Hill with half the town weeping. And when Danny — stupid, nineteen-year-old Danny, who thought the truth was a thing you could hand to people and they'd take it — told the police he'd seen Vale alive after the death date, he'd discovered that the truth was instead a thing that could be handed to *you*, wrapped in chains.

A witness came forward — a woman Danny had never met, who swore she'd seen him burning something in a barrel behind the boathouse the night Vale died. His fingerprints, naturally, were everywhere on the estate; he'd worked there six years. A receipt for accelerant — kerosene for the greenhouse lamps, bought with cash by the estate's foreman, entered into evidence as though Danny had bought it himself. His lawyer, Rourke, had been paid for by the same founding families who employed half the county's legal establishment, and had done exactly what he was paid to do, which was nothing much.

Convicted in absentia, technically — Danny had fled before sentencing, a decision he still defended with a stubbornness that broke Priya's heart a little every time. "They were going to put me away anyway," he always said. "At least I got eight months of being a person first." They'd caught him in Spokane working under a false name, and he'd been in Harrowgate County Detention ever since, serving a life sentence for the murder of a man whose grave, as of last Tuesday, contained sixty pounds of river rock.

"So the man on the dock," Priya said, pen moving. "You're certain it was Vale. Not someone who resembled him."

"Priya." Danny leaned forward, and for the first time in three years of visits, she saw anger in him — not hot, but banked, like coals that had been waiting two decades for air. "I trimmed his hedges. I drove him to the airport. I knew the sound of his cough across a field. It was him. And he was *alive*, and he was *angry*, and whatever he was arguing about, it was about money, and then he got in that car and drove off, and eleven months later two detectives sat where you're sitting and told me I killed him." He sat back. "I don't need you to believe me. I need you to make twelve strangers believe me. Can you do that?"

The question hung in the lemon-and-despair air between them.

"I found a retired detective who thinks the coffin was empty on purpose," Priya said. "I found a groundskeeper who's been putting fresh lilies on an empty grave for twenty years without knowing why the family pays him to. I found—" She stopped herself. She'd been about to say *a book with handwriting in it that rewrites the town's history*, and something in her, some instinct older than her law degree, said *not yet*. Not until she understood it herself. "I found threads, Danny. Enough of them that important people are getting nervous. Nervous people make mistakes."

"Nervous people also make sure problems go away." He said it without drama, the way you'd note that rain makes roads slick. "You should be careful. You and the detective both. The people who buried those stones — they buried them for a reason, and reasons don't rot like suits do."

"I can take care of myself."

"Yeah." He almost smiled again, and this time it arrived fully, and it transformed his whole face into something younger and sadder. "My mom used to say that too. Right up until the week after my arrest, when she stopped coming to visit, and then stopped answering letters, and then the return address started coming back." He shrugged, a small economical motion. "Everybody can take care of themselves right up until they can't. Just — be careful. Please. You're the first person in twenty years who's fighting like she means it."

Priya looked down at her legal pad so he wouldn't see what his voice did to her. *Desperate hope tempered by fear* — she'd written that phrase once in law school margins about some appellate case, and she'd understood it intellectually then, the way you understand a word in a foreign language. Now she understood it viscerally. Danny Ferro had been rationing hope for twenty years, a teaspoon at a time, and every spoonful cost him something, and he kept paying anyway.

"One more thing," she said, when she trusted her voice. "The woman who testified against you. The one who saw you burning things. Have you ever wondered—"

"Every day." No hesitation. "For twenty years. I've gone through every face I knew back then, trying to figure out who hated me enough to lie, or who got paid enough. And I keep coming back to the fact that I don't know. That's the worst part, Priya. Not the cell. The not-knowing. Somebody looked at a jury and described a crime I never committed, in detail, and I have spent twenty years not knowing who was standing there doing it." His hands had gone still on the table. "Find her. If you find anything else — find her."

Priya wrote it down even though she didn't need to: *Identify the witness.* Underneath, in smaller letters she angled away from the guard's sightline: *Who benefits? Who lies well? Who was close to Vale AND to the families?*

And then, unbidden, her own mind supplied the answer it had been circling for two days, the name from the probate files she'd pulled at midnight, the secretary who drowned in 2003 with a death certificate signed by the same doctor who had signed Edmund Vale's:

*Lena Marsh.*

She closed the folder.

* * *

The corridor back to the entrance seemed longer than before, the hungry faces in the cells more numerous. At the security door, the guard buzzed her through and said, without looking up from his magazine, "Word of advice, counselor."

Priya paused.

"Ferro's a quiet one. Never causes trouble. But there was a man here yesterday — suit, no badge, wouldn't give a name — paid for a private visit slot and sat with one of the trustees in holding." The guard turned a page. "Don't know what it was about. Don't much care. But funny timing, is all. Funny timing, with the trial coming."

Priya stood very still in the buzzing doorway, the folder heavy against her ribs, and outside the windows the afternoon light lay flat and golden over the parking lot, over the highway, over the whole shining, lying town of Harrowgate — and she understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, that the other side had already begun to move.

She pushed through the door and went to find a phone.
