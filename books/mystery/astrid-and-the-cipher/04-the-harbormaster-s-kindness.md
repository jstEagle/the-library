## 4. The Harbormaster's Kindness

Soren Voss had a theory about kindness, and the theory was this: it worked best when it cost you something visible.

A free cup of coffee meant nothing. A free cup of coffee poured from your own thermos, carried down to the dock at six in the morning because you'd heard a fisherman's wife was in the hospital—that meant something. That got remembered. That got repeated over eggs and hash at the Salt & Anchor until the whole town had absorbed the lesson: Soren Voss sees you. Soren Voss takes care of things.

He'd been pouring that coffee for thirty years, and Greywater had drunk every drop.

He stood now at the window of his office above the harbormaster's station, watching Ada Nystrom come up the boardwalk with her coat buttoned wrong—one hole off, so the whole line of buttons sat crooked like teeth after a bar fight. He noted it the way he noted everything: automatically, without judgment, filing it where it might be useful. The woman who noticed everything hadn't noticed her own coat. Good. That meant she was rattled. Rattled people came to you.

The knock came at 9:14. Punctual even in distress. He liked that about her, in the way he liked good weather and well-maintained engines—appreciation for a thing functioning as designed.

"Come in."

Ada Nystrom filled his doorway looking exactly as he remembered her father: same narrow face, same eyes that went still when they were working, like a camera's shutter deciding whether to open. Ten years since he'd stood across a desk from Halvard Nystrom and watched those same eyes decide not to see what was in front of them. Genetics was a kind of loyalty, Voss thought. The most reliable kind.

"Harbormaster." She didn't sit when he gestured. Of course she didn't.

"Miss Nystrom. Please—Soren." He smiled the smile that had been photographed for the chamber of commerce brochure, the one where the warmth reached everything but the measurement. "Can I offer you coffee? Real coffee, not the swill in the station pot. My sister sends it from Bergen."

"No, thank you."

"You sure? It's the one luxury I allow myself." He poured a cup anyway, set it on the desk between them—an offering, or a test. People who refused small gifts refused them again later, when the gifts were larger, and by then refusal had become a habit he could predict. "I heard about the unpleasantness at the cove. Terrible business."

"That's actually why I'm here."

"I assumed." He settled into his chair, hands folded over his stomach—a big man gone soft in the way that made people underestimate the strength still in him, which was considerable. Thirty years of hauling lines and lifting engines didn't leave a body entirely. "You have questions about the harbor. About the tides, the currents, what comes in and what goes out. That's my job. Ask me anything."

Her eyes did the still thing. He watched her decide how much to show him, and he enjoyed watching it, the way a man enjoys watching someone count cards badly.

"The body," she said. "The man from the cove. You knew him?"

"Never saw him before in my life." True, as far as it went. He'd seen him twice, but never *known* him, and Voss had built a life on the difference between those words. "Stray dog of a thing, washes up wearing stolen clothes. The whole town's talking about it. Talking about you, too, if I'm honest."

"I imagine they are."

"Don't let it get under your skin. Small towns need a story the way boats need ballast. Right now you're the story. In three weeks you'll be old news and somebody's divorce will be the story instead." He sipped his coffee. "But I'll tell you what worries me, since you came all this way."

She waited. Patient. Her father's daughter.

"A stranger drowns in our cove wearing clothes out of *your* closet, and the first thing everyone asks is what you did. Nobody asks the better question." He leaned forward, letting his voice drop into the register he used for confidences—the one that made people feel they were being let inside something. "Which is: why would anyone want to hurt *you*, Miss Nystrom? You file insurance papers. You go to Mira's tavern on Thursdays. You keep to yourself. You're nobody's enemy."

"Are you asking if I have enemies?"

"I'm asking if you've *made* any lately." He held her gaze, mild as milk. "In my experience, that's the only kind worth worrying about. The enemies you've had for years, you know their shapes. It's the new ones that get close enough to do damage."

Something moved behind her eyes—not fear, he was pleased to note, but calculation. She was cataloguing him. He could practically hear the alphabetizing. Let her. Every fact she filed about Soren Voss was a fact he'd spent thirty years making boring.

"I'm not here about enemies," she said. "I'm here because the police pulled fingerprints on the body and ran them against an old case file. A drowning. Ten years ago."

The room stayed exactly as warm as it had been. Voss's heartbeat didn't change—he'd trained that out of himself decades ago, back when a changed heartbeat could cost you fingers—but some older part of him, some animal part below the training, lay its ears flat.

"Elias," he said gently.

"You remember him."

"Everybody remembers Elias. Best deckhand this harbor ever produced, and then—" He spread his hands, letting the sentence drown itself. "That was a bad year. Bad year for everybody. Your father most of all."

Something flickered there. Quick, quickly buried. But Voss had spent his life reading faces the way other men read water, and he caught it: not grief. *Recognition.* She'd gone somewhere when he said her father's name, somewhere specific, and she'd come back with something in her pocket.

Interesting.

"My father's death was five years ago," she said. "It wasn't connected to—"

"No, no. Of course not." He waved it away, generous, dismissing the thing before it could become a thing. "I only mean he took it hard. Losing a son does something to a man. Halvard was never quite the same after. Kept to himself. Started drinking more than a man should, though don't quote me on that—I won't speak ill of the dead, especially not a man who kept my books straight for twenty years."

"He kept your books?"

"Harbor accounts. Dues, mooring fees, cargo manifests. Boring work, but he had a head for numbers." Voss smiled. "Must run in the family."

She didn't smile back. She was quiet a moment, and he let the quiet stretch, because quiet was a tool too—people rushed to fill silence, and in filling it, they handed you things.

"The case file from ten years ago," she said finally. "Elias's file. I've requested it from county records."

"Have you." Not a question. A door, closing softly. "And what do you expect to find in a ten-year-old drowning report?"

"I don't know yet."

"May I give you some advice? Unasked-for, I know, but I'm an old man who's watched a lot of smart people make the same mistake." He rose, crossed to the window, looked out at his harbor—his, whatever the deed said—and spoke to the glass. "Greywater is a town that survives on forgetting. That sounds ugly when I say it plain, but think about what it means. A fishing town buries its dead at sea. The sea doesn't give them back, and we don't ask it to. We hold a service, we say the words, we go home and gut the next day's catch. Forgetting isn't a failure here, Miss Nystrom. It's a discipline. It's how we stay standing."

"And if the dead come back anyway?" Her voice was level. "Wearing my clothes?"

"Then you ask yourself what the sea is trying to tell you." He turned from the window. "And sometimes the answer is: nothing. Sometimes a stranger drowns and it means nothing at all, and the cruelest thing you can do—to yourself, to his family, to this town—is build a mystery out of an accident. Mysteries have a way of growing. They pull in people who never asked to be pulled in." He let that hang just long enough. "People you care about. People who've been very good to you. Mira Lindqvist, for instance."

Now he had her. He watched it land—the slight stiffening, the recalibration. Everyone in Greywater knew Ada Nystrom drank her Thursday beer at the Salt & Anchor and laughed at exactly two of Mira's jokes and left by eleven. Take away the tavern and the woman had nothing holding her to this town except a house full of alphabetized files. He wasn't threatening Mira. He was simply observing, warmly, that mysteries had a radius, and radii could be measured.

"Mira has nothing to do with any of this," Ada said.

"Of course not. Nobody ever does. That's what makes it so sad when they end up involved anyway." He sat back down, opened a drawer, and produced a folder—thin, official-looking, stamped with the harbor seal. He slid it across the desk. "Current tide tables. Current regulations on salvage—you'll want those if the police release the victim's effects through maritime channels, which they will, eventually. And my card, with my home number on the back. If anything else washes up, if anyone bothers you, if you just need a sane voice in this circus—call me. Day or night."

She looked at the folder without touching it. "Why are you being so helpful?"

The question hung there, and Voss gave it the pause it deserved. Honesty, deployed correctly, was the sharpest knife in the drawer.

"Because your father was my friend," he said. "Because when your brother drowned, I was the one who organized the search, and I was the one who told your father we were calling it off, and I watched a grown man come apart in this office, and I have thought about that day more times than I can count. And because I'm sixty-one years old and this town is the only thing I've ever built, and I don't like watching it tear at itself over a drowned stranger and a cardigan." He spread his hands. "That's the whole of it, Miss Nystrom. No mystery. Just an old man's affections."

Every word true. Every word arranged. That was the craft of it—lies were for amateurs; the professional's art was selecting truths and setting them in order, like flowers in a wreath laid over a grave.

Ada took the folder. Standing, she said, "Thank you for your time, Harbormaster."

"Soren."

"Harbormaster." Almost a smile. Her father's mouth, doing her father's stubborn thing. At the door she paused. "One more question. Ten years ago—when the search for Elias was called off. Who made the final call?"

Voss met her eyes across the office, across thirty years of poured coffee and held secrets, and gave her the truth one more time, arranged just so.

"The coroner ruled. The insurance company concurred. The family accepted." He shrugged, vast and harmless. "I just rowed the boat."

She left. Through the window he watched her go down the boardwalk, coat still buttoned wrong, folder under her arm, spine straight as a mast. She stopped once at the rail overlooking the slips and stood there a long moment, looking at the water, and Voss read her posture the way he read weather: a front coming in. Pressure dropping. Something building out past the point where the charts ended.

He picked up the phone and dialed a number he knew better than his own birthday.

"It's me," he said, when it answered. "She came to see me. She's pulling the old files." A pause. Listening. "No. No, I don't think she knows anything yet. But she's her father's daughter, and her father taught himself to stop seeing things, and she hasn't learned that trick." Another pause, longer. His eyes went to the harbor, to the grey water moving in its ancient patient way. "Because she's going to keep digging. That's not a guess. I watched Halvard do the same walk twenty years ago—curious first, careful second, and by the time he got to scared, it was too late to turn him around." He listened again, and something in his jaw tightened. "Then we do it the clean way. We give her a reason to leave town that looks like her own idea. I'll handle it. I've handled Nystroms before."

He hung up and sat for a while in the warm quiet of his office, listening to the gulls and the halyards clinking against masts, the sound of a town going about its innocent morning.

On his desk, beside the untouched second cup of coffee he'd poured for her out of habit, lay his copy of the harbor log, open to the current week. He turned back through the pages—past this week, past last month, past the neat entries in his own hand—until he reached a date ten years old, and the entry there, brief as a prayer:

*Search suspended. Body not recovered. Sea keeps what it's given.*

He'd written that himself, all those years ago, and signed it, and believed none of it, because he'd been standing on the dock that night. He'd been standing on the dock, and so had someone else—someone small, someone half-hidden behind a stack of crab pots, someone whose testimony had needed to become fog, and had, thanks to a chemist's patience and a father's terrible love.

*Sea keeps what it's given,* he thought. *Until it doesn't.*

Outside, the tide was coming in, carrying God knew what toward his shore.

Voss closed the log, locked it in the drawer, and went down to pour coffee for the fishermen, smiling, beloved, keeping count of everything.
