## 14. The Man in the Vent

They released me at 9:47 on a Thursday morning, which I know precisely because I wrote it down.

Okafor met me on the courthouse steps with a coffee she'd bought from a cart, still steaming, and the particular expression of a lawyer who has won a point she considers beneath her. "Technicality," she said, handing me the cup. "The warrant listed the wrong room number. Your ledgers were seized from 3B, not 3C. Reyes is furious. Reyes is also, I suspect, quietly relieved."

"Relieved?"

"She's been reading your notebooks. All of them. She told me—" Okafor paused, choosing the words the way I choose keys off a hookboard, "—she told me that whoever wrote these notices things no innocent person notices and no guilty person bothers to record. She said it's the most honest document she's ever read and she doesn't know what to do with that."

I drank the coffee. It was terrible. I have opinions about coffee the way I have opinions about everything, filed and cross-referenced, and this cup would have gone in the drawer marked *evidence of civic neglect*.

"Where are your ledgers now?" I asked.

"Property room. Photocopied, catalogued, and—here's the interesting part—sealed at my request. The originals, I mean. Reyes agreed. Which tells you she's not finished with you, Astrid. She just can't use you as a suspect anymore without the whole case falling over."

"And the forged page?"

"Being analyzed. The handwriting is very good." Okafor's eyes did something complicated. "Almost too good. Like someone who'd studied your hand for a long time."

I had been thinking about that phrase for two days, in the holding cell, on the thin mattress, in the fluorescent hour between lights-out and nothing. *Studied.* It kept returning the way a word returns when it's the wrong word for something and your mind knows it.

I thanked her. She told me not to thank her until the trial she was hoping never to have. Then she got into a car that cost more than my annual salary and left me standing on the steps with my terrible coffee and my freedom, which felt, in that moment, less like a door opening than a door left ajar by someone who intended to come back through it.

* * *

I did not go home.

This requires some explanation, because a woman released from custody might reasonably be expected to want her own bed, her own kettle, the small ceremonies by which a person reassembles herself. But my apartment is above the Marlowe Grand. My apartment has always been above the Marlowe Grand. And I had spent two nights in a cell learning the specific flavor of being watched—how it gets under the skin, how it reorganizes your relationship with every ceiling and every wall—and I could not shake the thought that whoever had forged that page had not needed to break into my room to learn my handwriting.

They could have simply *lived near it*.

So instead I went to the county records office, which opens at ten and smells of toner and the slow decay of civic ambition, and I asked the clerk—a young man named Dev with the unguarded enthusiasm of someone who still believes paperwork is a form of truth—for the maintenance files on the Marlowe Grand.

"All of them?" he said.

"All of them," I said. "Going back as far as you have."

What came up was four boxes and a digital archive that had been migrated twice and half-ruined both times, which is the usual biography of a digital archive. I signed the access log—of course I signed it; I sign everything; I am a creature of the register—and carried the boxes to a table by the window where the light was honest.

Here is what I knew going in. Every commercial building in Marlowe County files maintenance records for its mechanical systems, its elevators, its fire equipment. The Marlowe Grand is old enough—1911, Jonah Thackeray's pride and monument—that its systems are less *maintained* than *negotiated with*, and the county has been documenting that negotiation for decades. Boiler inspections. Elevator certifications. Fire-suppression tests. The unglamorous liturgy of a building staying alive.

I was looking for a pattern, though I couldn't have told you what pattern when I sat down. I only knew that the forged page had come from somewhere, and that the someone who made it had access to my room, my hand, and my habits, and that the hotel was the one place all three of those things overlapped for years.

I started with the fire inspections, because fire inspections are the most regular thing in any building's life. Annual, semi-annual in a hotel of our classification, always logged with date, time, and the name of the technician or contractor performing the work.

I made a list. I am very good at lists. It is, if I'm honest, the only thing I've ever been praised for, and I have built an entire life inside that praise like a hermit crab inside a borrowed shell.

The fire inspections were clean. The elevator certifications were clean. The boiler records were a small epic of neglect that I read with the fond horror of a long-term employee, but nothing there. It was when I got to the attic access logs—the records for the crawlspace and service areas above the third floor, the spaces that hold ductwork, wiring, the old pneumatic tube system from Thackeray's day, and, in the corner above the lobby, the housing for the lobby camera's original mount—that the pattern surfaced.

It surfaced the way things always surface in my ledgers: not dramatically, but as a repetition.

Access to the attic above the lobby required a master key and a log entry. The log was kept on a clipboard mounted inside the attic hatch—old system, never updated—and transcribed monthly by whichever maintenance contractor was on the books. I found the transcriptions going back to 2011.

2011. The year Eleanor Thackeray's husband came back from the dead to argue with her. The year the anonymous letters, per Okafor, claimed Jonah was still alive. I wrote the date in my margin and felt the small cold thrill that I have learned, in the last two weeks, to distrust: the thrill of a thing clicking into place before you know what the thing is.

Then I read the entries.

Every month. Every single month for thirteen years, there was an attic access entry. *Pest control inspection. Wiring check. Insulation survey. Duct cleaning.* The stated reasons rotated with the seasons, plausible as weather. But the signature was the same. Not the same name—the names changed, because the names were fictional or borrowed or rotated among a list of shell contractors the way Room 412 rotated among shell companies—but the same *hand*. I am not a handwriting analyst, but I have spent fifteen years reading the signatures of strangers, and hands are like walks: you know your neighbor's from a block away.

The same person had gone into the attic above my lobby every month since 2011.

I sat back. Dev, sensing something, drifted over with the anxious helpfulness of the young. "Everything okay, Ms. Vane?"

"Do you know," I said, "what the average interval is for a pest inspection in an attic that has shown no infestation in thirteen years?"

"Uh. Quarterly? Maybe?"

"Monthly," I said, "is not a pest inspection. Monthly is a *commute*."

* * *

I took the copies home—home meaning the one place I now trusted less than anywhere, which tells you what my week had been like—and spread them across my kitchen table under the good lamp, the one with the green glass shade that Gerald Moss gave me for my tenth anniversary at the hotel.

Gerald Moss. I want you to hold that name for a moment, the way I held it that night without knowing I was holding it. Gerald who trained me, my first winter, teaching me the register, the key board, the art of looking at a guest twice—once with your eyes and once with your memory. Gerald who fixed the radiator in my apartment every October before the cold came. Gerald who retired two years ago with a cake in the staff room and a speech about how the Marlowe was in my bones now, and everyone had laughed, and I had written in that night's ledger: *G. Moss retirement dinner. 34 staff present. He said the hotel was in my bones. I said I hoped that was a compliment. He said it was the truest thing he'd ever said to anyone.*

I had written that. I write everything down. It is my gift and, I was beginning to understand, my exposure.

Because here is what the maintenance logs said, once I cross-referenced them against payroll—payroll records Dev had pulled for me with the blithe generosity of a man who had not yet learned that helpfulness has consequences.

The Marlowe Grand's maintenance contract, the one covering the attic and service spaces, had been held since 2011 by a sole-proprietor operation: M. & S. Building Services. Registered to one Martin Selby. Martin Selby, I discovered after twenty minutes with the county business registry, did not exist. The address on the registration was a mailbox. The license number belonged to a man who had died in 2009. And the signature on thirteen years of monthly attic entries—the same hand, the same patient, unhurried hand—

I had seen it before. I see it every week. I have seen it on work orders, on radiator repair tickets, on the card Gerald Moss pressed into my hand at his retirement dinner with the words *call me if the boiler ever acts up, Astrid, you know I'll always come.*

Gerald Moss had signed into the attic above my lobby once a month, every month, under three different names, for thirteen years.

I put down the page. My hands were steady, which surprised me; I had expected them to shake, and their steadiness felt like a stranger's hands, like the hands of a woman in a photograph.

Let me be careful here. Let me do what I do. Let me lay out the entries the way I'd lay out a guest's movements, without adjectives, without conclusions, and see what the facts do on their own.

Monthly attic access, 2011 to present. The attic contains the original camera mount for the lobby camera—the camera whose disc "existed once," per Eleanor, and per the hotel's own records was decommissioned in 2010 and its archive "lost." The attic contains, I now remembered with the particular nausea of a late realization, the ventilation grilles. The lobby has four decorative grilles set high in the cornice work, brass, ornamental, the kind of thing guests photograph and employees forget. Behind them, ductwork. Behind the ductwork, the attic. And from the attic, through the grille above the front desk, a person could see the entire lobby. The desk. The register. The chair where I sit.

The chair where I have sat every night for fifteen years.

I thought about the forged ledger page. The handwriting, *almost too good, like someone who'd studied your hand.* I thought about the torn pages from October 2009, removed cleanly from notebooks kept in a locked drawer in my room—removed by someone with a master key, someone who had access to my rooms the way a landlord does, or a maintenance man, or a mentor. I thought about Room 412, paid annually by a shell, occupied one week each October, always empty when I checked—empty because the occupancy was never the point. The point was the *booking*. The point was a reason, renewed every year, for the hotel's systems to keep a room in a state of quiet, unquestioned readiness. A room that could be entered. A room that could be used.

And I thought about the photographs in 412. Fifteen years of me at the desk. Taken from angles I had never been able to place. High angles. Slightly left of center.

I had assumed a camera hidden somewhere in the lobby. I had spent one entire night of my life, years ago, on a ladder with a flashlight, hunting for it, and found nothing, and written in my ledger: *no camera located; conclude the angles were misjudged.*

They weren't misjudged. I had been looking for a device, and there was no device. There was a *person*. There was a grille, and behind the grille, a man with a camera and a master key and thirteen years of monthly visits and all the patience of someone for whom watching was not a hobby but a sentence he had chosen for himself.

The hotel became a suspect that night. I mean that literally. I sat in my kitchen with the green lamp burning and I looked at the walls of my own apartment—walls Gerald had plastered, pipes Gerald had insulated, a radiator Gerald serviced every October—and I understood that I had never lived alone. I had lived inside an instrument. The Marlowe Grand was not a building with a man hiding in it. The Marlowe Grand was a man who had arranged a building around himself, the way a spider is arranged around a web, and I had been the fly that thought it was the concierge.

I did not sleep. I made tea at 2 a.m. and did not drink it. At 3 a.m.—the hour, always the hour; the hour of the duffel bag in the service corridor, the hour of lime and moved cars—I found myself standing in my apartment's service closet, looking up at my own ceiling hatch, the little painted-shut panel that leads to the crawlspace between my rooms and the attic proper.

The paint was not shut. The paint had been scored, carefully, around the edges, with a blade. Scored and repainted, scored and repainted, the way you'd maintain a door you needed to open quietly but didn't want anyone to notice was a door at all.

I stood under it for a long time. I did not open it. Some animal part of me—the part that has survived fifteen years of night shifts in a building full of strangers—said very clearly: *not alone, and not tonight, and not without a witness who carries a badge.*

So instead I did the thing I do. I went back to the table, I opened a fresh notebook, and I wrote it all down. The names. The dates. The hand. The scored hatch. The grilles. The angles of the photographs and where they must have been taken from, which I calculated with a protractor like a madwoman and which put the camera, every time, at the cornice above the front desk, twelve feet from where I make my entries in the register every night of my life.

Then I wrote one more line, and I underlined it, and I have never underlined anything in a ledger before or since:

*He trained me to look up at guests. He never once looked up at me. He didn't need to. He was already above me.*

* * *

I called Reyes at seven in the morning, which I knew would annoy her and did it anyway, because a woman who has spent two nights in a cell stops being polite about other people's sleep.

"Vane." Not a greeting. A diagnosis.

"I need you to look at maintenance records," I said. "M. & S. Building Services. The attic access logs for the Marlowe Grand. And I need you to get a handwriting comparison on thirteen years of attic entries against one Gerald Moss."

Silence. The particular silence of a detective recalibrating. "Moss," she said finally. "The porter. The one who—"

"Trained me. Yes. Retired two years ago. Lives on Delano Street. Still holds a master key that was never returned—I checked the key log myself this morning before I called you. Entry 447, issued 2011, marked 'returned' in a hand that is not the key clerk's. I know the key clerk's hand. I've read it for fifteen years."

Another silence, longer. "You've been busy for a woman who got out of holding yesterday."

"I've been busy for fifteen years," I said. "I just didn't know what I was busy *doing*."

"Stay there. Don't go into the attic. Don't confront anyone. Don't—Astrid, I can hear you thinking. Whatever you're thinking, put it down."

I looked at the notebook. At the underlined line. At the scored hatch above my closet, patient as a held breath.

"I'm not thinking anything," I said, which is the first lie I have ever told a police officer, and I recorded it, because I record everything.

She came at nine with a warrant and two uniforms and a forensic tech who looked like he'd rather be anywhere. They opened my closet hatch with a paint scraper and a flashlight, and the tech went up into the crawlspace, and the building held its breath around us—I swear I felt it, the old Marlowe holding its breath, every pipe and duct listening.

He was up there four minutes. Then he called down, in a voice carefully stripped of everything, the way voices are stripped when what they contain is bad: "Detective. You're going to want to see this."

They brought it down in an evidence bag, and I looked at it, and the floor of my fifteen years tilted gently beneath me.

A folding camp stool. A battery lamp. A spiral notebook—*another* notebook, a shadow ledger to mirror mine, kept by someone who also recorded everything, who had also spent years writing the nights down. And taped inside its cover, a strip of black-and-white photographs, and at the top of the stack, the oldest one, faded and creased:

Me at the desk. October 2009. Young, unaware, my pen moving across the register.

And standing at my shoulder, half in frame, smiling down at my page with an expression I had never once seen from the front—because I had never once seen him from anywhere but the side—

Gerald Moss.

Reyes looked at the photograph for a long time. Then at me. Then at the notebook, which the tech had already begun to leaf through, and whose most recent entry, dated three days ago—while I was in holding—read:

*She knows about the logs. She called the county. She'll come to the attic next. She always comes to the source.*

And beneath that, in the same patient hand, a single line that I have now read perhaps two hundred times, and which I will carry out of this story if I carry nothing else:

*Time to close the register.*
