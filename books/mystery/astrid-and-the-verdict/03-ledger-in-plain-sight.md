## 3. Ledger in Plain Sight

The will had been read on a Tuesday, and by Wednesday morning the house had already begun to treat me like furniture that might be valuable.

I woke in the east guest room to gulls arguing about something and the particular silence of a house where three people were being very careful not to talk about me. I could hear them anyway. Marisol's footsteps in the kitchen had the rhythm of someone who'd cooked in that room for thirty years—no hesitation at the stove, no searching for the good knife. Dane's tread was heavier, deliberate, the walk of a man who wanted you to know he was coming. And Wren didn't seem to sleep so much as take shifts at it; I'd heard her door open twice before dawn.

I lay there and let my memory do what it does, which is everything, whether I ask it to or not.

The reading of the will, replayed: Voss's lawyer, a dry man named Pell with dandruff on his shoulders like weather, reading aloud that the estate would remain in trust until "the executor has decoded and verified the ledger." The beneficiaries' faces when my name was spoken. Marisol's hands going still in her lap—not surprise, I'd noted at the time, but something closer to confirmation. Dane's smile arriving a half-second late, like a man remembering he was supposed to be pleased. Wren looking out the window at the sea as if the will were a formality happening to other people.

And Edmund Voss himself, dead eleven days now, present only as a portrait above the study fireplace—a man painted in his sixties with the expression of someone who had just heard a joke he intended never to repeat.

"The ledger," Pell had said, when I asked where it was.

"In plain sight," he'd said, and declined to elaborate, because lawyers are paid to say things that sound like answers.

* * *

The study was on the ground floor's seaward side, and it smelled of pipe tobacco that hadn't been smoked in years but refused to surrender the territory. I went in after breakfast, alone, because I'd learned in twenty years of insurance investigation that witnesses cluster around an examination like moths around a flame, and moths are terrible at letting you work.

Plain sight. I stood in the middle of the room and did what I always do first: nothing. You can't find what you're looking for if you're busy looking. Instead I let the room photograph itself into me.

Desk, oak, scarred. Two chairs, one worn, one stiff—the worn one facing the window, the stiff one facing the desk. A man who kept visitors uncomfortable. Bookshelves, mostly maritime law and shipping registries, spines uncracked enough to suggest they were set dressing. A barometer. A brass telescope aimed at the harbor. On the walls: charts, a ship's wheel that had clearly never been on a ship, and—

There.

Between the window and the bookshelves, hung at exactly eye height, in a frame of dark walnut that cost more than most people's cars: an embroidery sampler.

It was the kind of thing a Victorian child might have made under a governess's disapproving eye. Alphabet along the top in cross-stitch, then rows of small motifs—flowers, birds, a little house—and beneath each motif, stitched dates and pairs of initials. Someone had gone to enormous trouble to make it look innocent. That was the first thing wrong with it. Nobody hangs a sampler in a shipping magnate's study unless they want the sampler seen, and nobody wants a sampler seen unless it isn't really a sampler.

I stepped closer. My memory, which has never once done me the courtesy of forgetting anything, began its work without being asked.

**March 14, 1987 — E.M.**

A date and initials. Stitched in blue thread, small and neat, beneath a motif of a burning candle. I stood very still while the archive behind my eyes turned over its files.

March 14, 1987. The Harrow Point cannery fire. Three dead, insured through Sterling & Associates—my firm, though I'd have been nineteen years old and memorizing bus schedules at the time. Claim filed by Elena Marsh, widow. Claim denied. Grounds cited: "non-disclosure of prior structural violations."

Elena Marsh. E.M.

I moved down the row. **June 2, 1991 — T.A.**, beneath a motif of a small boat on stitched waves.

June 2, 1991. The Aldous trawler, lost off the shoals with two hands. Tomas Aldous, owner-operator, claim denied—"improper maintenance," per the adjuster's report. Except I remembered the report itself, because I'd read it during my first year at Sterling, back when I read everything, back before I learned that some files are better left unread. The maintenance records had been forged. I'd noticed the ink on the inspection certificate was newer than the certificate's stated date. I'd been told, gently, by my supervisor, that noticing such things was not the same as acting on them.

Tomas Aldous drowned believing his own insurer thought he was a cheat.

My hands had gone cold. I noted this the way I note everything—with detachment that fools no one, least of all me—and kept moving.

**September 9, 1994 — R.C.**, beneath a motif of a bird falling from a stitched sky.

Ruth Calloway. The warehouse collapse on Pier Street. Four injured, one killed—her husband, Daniel. Claim denied. "Acts of God" clause invoked, which is the insurance industry's way of saying we believe in God exclusively when He saves us money.

Three denials. Three dates. Three sets of initials, stitched in thread by hands that must have trembled, or must not have—I couldn't decide which possibility disturbed me more.

But here was the thing, the thing that made the cold spread from my hands up my forearms: these weren't just denials. I knew, from the estate papers Pell had given me, that Voss's company had later made payments on all three claims. Quiet ones. Structured settlements routed through a subsidiary called Meridian Coastal Holdings, which appeared nowhere in the sampler but appeared forty-one times in the financial statements.

Denied publicly. Paid privately. And the dates of the private payments—

I closed my eyes and pulled the Meridian ledger pages up behind my eyelids, the way you'd consult a wall of filing cabinets that happened to be installed inside your skull.

Payment to E. Marsh: initiated March 14, 1988. One year to the day after the fire.

Payment to the Aldous estate: June 2, 1992. One year to the day.

Payment to R. Calloway: September 9, 1995.

Every anniversary. Every single one, stitched into cloth and paid out in secret, like a man keeping score against himself and refusing to let anyone watch the game.

* * *

I don't know how long I stood there. Long enough that the light through the window moved from the desk to the floor. Long enough that when I finally heard footsteps in the hall, I recognized them as Marisol's before they reached the doorway.

She stopped when she saw me standing in front of the sampler. She didn't look surprised. She looked like a woman watching weather she'd been promised arrive.

"You found it," she said.

"Pell said plain sight. He wasn't lying."

"He rarely does. It's his worst quality." She came into the room carrying a tray I hadn't asked for—coffee, bread, butter, the universal diplomat's kit of people who want to soften you before a conversation. She set it on the desk. "Edmund stitched it himself, you know. Learned from his mother. His hands were too big for the needle and he did it anyway, every anniversary, until his eyes went."

"Until his eyes went," I repeated. "So there are more entries than—"

"There are thirty-one." She said it evenly, but her thumb moved over her wedding ring, around and around, a small orbit of distress. "He stopped stitching five years ago. Said his hands couldn't hold the needle steady anymore. He had me write the last four dates in ink instead." Her chin lifted slightly. "In the corner. Under the house."

I looked. There, beneath the little stitched cottage, in faded brown ink in a hand I recognized instantly—the same hand that had written both letters sitting on my kitchen table two hundred miles away—four dates and four sets of initials.

The same tremor. The same ink. The same pressure on the downstrokes.

Marisol watched me see it. I'll give her this: she didn't flinch, and she didn't lie, and she didn't fill the silence with noise the way most people do when they're afraid. She simply waited, the way you wait for a tide.

"These aren't gifts," I said. "These are debts. He denied their claims and then paid them in secret, year after year, and stitched the whole confession where anyone could see it and no one would ever look."

"That's Edmund exactly," she said softly. "He wanted to be forgiven without ever having to ask."

"And the initials? The last four?"

Her thumb stopped moving on the ring.

"You'll want to sit down for those," she said. "Or maybe you won't. Men like you never do."

"I'm not a man like any kind," I said, "but I'll stand."

Something flickered across her face—not quite a smile, but adjacent to one. "The first three you found are the old ones. Fires, wrecks, collapses. Accidents Edmund's company denied to protect its rates." She paused. "The last four aren't accidents at all. And the last one—" her voice did something complicated and came out level anyway, "—the last one is dated the night of the ferry."

The ferry. I didn't yet know why that word landed in my chest like a dropped anchor. I wouldn't know for several days. But I wrote it down anyway, in the notebook I keep for the benefit of people who assume memory needs paper, and underlined it twice, and felt the fascination that had carried me all morning quietly change its chemical composition into something colder and much less comfortable.

Dread, I'd learn to call it later. At the time I just noticed my coffee had gone untouched and my hands had gone unsteady, and that for the first time in my adult life, I resented my own memory—because it had already shown me the ferry file, years ago, in a box marked CLOSED, and it was going to make me look at it again whether I wanted to or not.

"Who are the last four?" I asked.

Marisol picked up the tray as if our conversation had been about breakfast all along.

"Ask Wren," she said. "One of the initials is hers."
